# Entire function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Entire function"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), an **entire function**, also called an **integral function**, is a complex-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that is [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) on the whole [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). Typical examples of entire functions are [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) and the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678), and any finite sums, products and compositions of these, such as the [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) and [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) and their [hyperbolic counterparts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) [sinh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_sine) and [cosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_cosine), as well as [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) and [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) of entire functions such as the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111). If an entire function $f(z)$ has a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) at $w$, then $f(z)/(z-w)$, taking the limit value at $w$, is an entire function. On the other hand, the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), the [reciprocal function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_function), and the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) are all not entire functions, nor can they be [continued analytically](wikipedia://en/80842) to an entire function.

:::

A **[transcendental](wikipedia://en/297021) entire function** is an entire function that is not a polynomial.

Just as [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) can be viewed as a generalization of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210), entire functions can be viewed as a generalization of polynomials. In particular, if for meromorphic functions one can generalize the factorization into simple fractions (the [Mittag-Leffler theorem](wikipedia://en/2466640) on the decomposition of a meromorphic function), then for entire functions there is a generalization of the factorization – the [Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/1585155) on entire functions.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Every entire function $f(z)$ can be represented as a single [power series](wikipedia://en/59958):

$$\ f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n z^n\$$

that [converges](wikipedia://en/1743431) everywhere in the complex plane, hence [uniformly on compact sets](wikipedia://en/7442564). The [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476) is infinite, which implies that

$$\ \lim_{n\to\infty} |a_n|^{\frac{1}{n}} = 0\$$

or, equivalently,

$$\ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\ln|a_n|}n = -\infty ~.$$

Any power series satisfying this criterion will represent an entire function.

If (and only if) the coefficients of the power series are all real then the function evidently takes real values for real arguments, and the value of the function at the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $z$ will be the complex conjugate of the value at $z$. Such functions are sometimes called self-conjugate (the conjugate function, $F^*(z)$, being given by $\bar F(\bar z)$).[^n1]

If the real part of an entire function is known in a (complex) neighborhood of a point then both the real and imaginary parts are known for the whole complex plane, [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an imaginary constant. For instance, if the real part is known in a [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of zero, then we can find the coefficients for $n>0$ from the following derivatives with respect to a real variable $r$:

$$\begin{aligned} \Re(a_n) &= \frac{1}{n!} \left.\frac{\textrm d^n}{\textrm dr^n}\ \Re(f(r))\right|_{r=0} \\ \Im(a_n) &= \frac{1}{n!} \left.\frac{\textrm d^n}{\textrm dr^n} \Re\Bigl(f\Bigl(re^{-\frac{i\pi}{2n}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\right|_{r=0} \end{aligned}$$

(Likewise, if the imaginary part is known in such a neighborhood then the function is determined up to a real constant.) In fact, if the real part is known just on an arc of a circle, then the function is determined up to an imaginary constant. Note however that an entire function is ***not*** necessarily determined by its real part on some other curves. In particular, if the real part is given on any curve in the complex plane where the real part of some other entire function is zero, then any multiple of that function can be added to the function we are trying to determine. For example, if the curve where the real part is known is the real line, then we can add $i$ times any self-conjugate function. If the curve forms a loop, then it is determined by the real part of the function on the loop since the only functions whose real part is zero on the curve are those that are everywhere equal to some [imaginary number](wikipedia://en/14554).

The [Weierstrass factorization theorem](wikipedia://en/1585155) asserts that any entire function can be represented by a product involving its [zeroes](wikipedia://en/264210) (or "roots").

The entire functions on the complex plane form an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) (in fact a [Prüfer domain](wikipedia://en/12367835)). They also form a [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).

[Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312293) states that any [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) entire function must be constant.

As a consequence of Liouville's theorem, any function that is entire on the whole [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) is constant. Thus any non-constant entire function must have a [singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) at the complex [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139), either a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) for a polynomial or an [essential singularity](wikipedia://en/80862) for a [transcendental](wikipedia://en/297021) entire function. Specifically, by the [Casorati–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/33868), for any transcendental entire function $f$ and any complex $w$ there is a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(z_m)_{m\in\mathbb{N}}$ such that

$$\ \lim_{m\to\infty} |z_m| = \infty, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \lim_{m\to\infty} f(z_m) = w ~.$$

[Picard's little theorem](wikipedia://en/674050) is a much stronger result: Any non-constant entire function takes on every complex number as value, possibly with a single exception. When an exception exists, it is called a [lacunary value](wikipedia://en/4827464) of the function. The possibility of a lacunary value is illustrated by the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678), which never takes on the value $0$. One can take a suitable branch of the logarithm of an entire function that never hits $0$, so that this will also be an entire function (according to the [Weierstrass factorization theorem](wikipedia://en/1585155)). The logarithm hits every complex number except possibly one number, which implies that the first function will hit any value other than $0$ an infinite number of times. Similarly, a non-constant, entire function that does not hit a particular value will hit every other value an infinite number of times.

Liouville's theorem is a special case of the following statement:

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Assume $M$, $R$ are positive constants and $n$ is a non-negative integer. An entire function $f$ satisfying the inequality $|f(z)| \le M |z|^n$ for all $z$ with $\vert z$, is necessarily a polynomial, of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) at most $n$. Similarly, an entire function $f$ satisfying the inequality $M |z|^n \le |f(z)|$ for all $z$ with $\vert z \vert \ge R$, is necessarily a polynomial, of degree at least $n$.

:::

## Growth {#Growth}

Entire functions may grow as fast as any increasing function: for any increasing function $g:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ there exists an entire function $f$ such that $f(x)>g(|x|)$ for all real $x$. Such a function $f$ may be easily found of the form:

$$f(z)=c+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{z}{k}\right)^{n_k}$$

for a constant $c$ and a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers $n_k$. Any such sequence defines an entire function $f(z)$, and if the powers are chosen appropriately we may satisfy the inequality $f(x)>g(|x|)$ for all real $x$. (For instance, it certainly holds if one chooses $c:=g(2)$ and, for any integer $k \ge 1$ one chooses an even exponent $n_k$ such that $\left(\frac{k+1}{k}\right)^{n_k} \ge g(k+2)$).

## Order and type {#Order_and_type}

The **order** (at infinity) of an entire function $f(z)$ is defined using the [limit superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_superior) as:

$$\rho = \limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln \left (\ln\| f \|_{\infty, B_r} \right ) }{\ln r},$$

where $B_r$ is the disk of radius $r$ and $\|f \|_{\infty, B_r}$ denotes the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm) of $f(z)$ on $B_r$. The order is a non-negative [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) or infinity (except when $f(z) = 0$ for all $z$). In other words, the order of $f(z)$ is the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all $m$ such that:

$$f(z) = O \left (\exp \left (|z|^m \right ) \right ), \quad \text{as } z \to \infty.$$

The example of $f(z) = \exp(2z^2)$ shows that this does not mean $f(z)=O(\exp(|z|^m))$ if $f(z)$ is of order $m$.

If $0 < \rho < \infty$, one can also define the ***type***:

$$\sigma=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\ln \| f\|_{\infty, B_r}} {r^\rho}.$$

If the order is 1 and the type is $\sigma$, the function is said to be "of [exponential type](wikipedia://en/4081509) $\sigma$". If it is of order less than 1 it is said to be of exponential type 0.

If

$$f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n z^n,$$

then the order and type can be found by the formulas

$$\begin{aligned} \rho &=\limsup_{n\to\infty} \frac{n\ln n}{-\ln|a_n|} \\[6pt] (e\rho\sigma)^{\frac{1}{\rho}} &= \limsup_{n\to\infty} n^{\frac{1}{\rho}} |a_n|^{\frac{1}{n}} \end{aligned}$$

Let $f^{(n)}$ denote the $n$th derivative of $f$. Then we may restate these formulas in terms of the derivatives at any arbitrary point $z_0$:

$$\begin{aligned} \rho &=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\ln n}{n\ln n-\ln|f^{(n)}(z_0)|}=\left(1-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ln|f^{(n)}(z_0)|}{n\ln n}\right)^{-1} \\[6pt] (\rho\sigma)^{\frac{1}{\rho}} &=e^{1-\frac{1}{\rho}} \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|f^{(n)}(z_0)|^{\frac{1}{n}}}{n^{1-\frac{1}{\rho}}} \end{aligned}$$

The type may be infinite, as in the case of the [reciprocal gamma function](wikipedia://en/4017902), or zero (see example below under ).

Another way to find out the order and type is [Matsaev's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsaev's_theorem).

### Examples {#Examples}

Here are some examples of functions of various orders:

#### Order *ρ* {#Order_ρ}

For arbitrary positive numbers $\rho$ and $\sigma$ one can construct an example of an entire function of order $\rho$ and type $\sigma$ using:

$$f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left (\frac{e\rho\sigma}{n} \right )^{\frac{n}{\rho}} z^n$$

#### Order 0 {#Order_0}

- Non-zero polynomials
- $$\sum_{n=0}^\infty 2^{-n^2} z^n$$

#### Order 1/4 {#Order_1/4}

$$f(\sqrt[4]z),$$

where

$$f(u)=\cos(u)+\cosh(u)$$

#### Order 1/3 {#Order_1/3}

$$f(\sqrt[3]z),$$

where

$$f(u)=e^u+e^{\omega u}+e^{\omega^2 u} = e^u+2e^{-\frac{u}{2}}\cos \left (\frac{\sqrt 3u}{2} \right ), \quad \text{with } \omega \text{ a complex cube root of 1}.$$

#### Order 1/2 {#Order_1/2}

$$\cos \left (a\sqrt z \right )$$

with $a\neq 0$ (for which the type is given by $\sigma=\vert a \vert$)

#### Order 1 {#Order_1}

- $\exp(az)$ with $a\neq 0$ ($\sigma=\vert a \vert$)
- $$\sin(z)$$
- $$\cosh(z)$$
- the [Bessel functions](wikipedia://en/4700) $J_n(z)$ and spherical Bessel functions $j_n(z)$ for integer values of $n$[^o16]
- the [reciprocal gamma function](wikipedia://en/4017902) $1/\Gamma(z)$ ($\sigma$ is infinite)
- $$\sum_{n=2}^\infty \frac{z^n}{(n\ln n)^n} \quad (\sigma=0)$$

#### Order 3/2 {#Order_3/2}

- [Airy function](wikipedia://en/406896) $Ai(z)$

#### Order 2 {#Order_2}

- $\exp(az^2)$ with $a\neq 0$ ($\sigma=\vert a \vert$)
- The [Barnes G-function](wikipedia://en/2060183) ($\sigma$ is infinite)

#### Order infinity {#Order_infinity}

- $$\exp(\exp(z))$$

## Genus {#Genus}

Entire functions of finite order have [Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675)'s canonical representation ([Hadamard factorization theorem](wikipedia://en/2369798)):

$$f(z)=z^me^{P(z)}\prod_{n=1}^\infty\left(1-\frac{z}{z_n}\right)\exp\left(\frac{z}{z_n}+\cdots+\frac{1}{p} \left(\frac{z}{z_n}\right)^p\right),$$

where $z_k$ are those [roots](wikipedia://en/264210) of $f$ that are not zero ($z_k \neq 0$), $m$ is the order of the zero of $f$ at $z = 0$ (the case $m = 0$ being taken to mean $f(0) \neq 0$), $P$ a polynomial (whose degree we shall call $q$), and $p$ is the smallest non-negative integer such that the series

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{|z_n|^{p+1}}$$

converges. The non-negative integer $g=\max\{p, q\}$ is called the genus of the entire function $f$.

If the order $\rho$ is not an integer, then $g = \lfloor\rho\rfloor$ is the integer part of $\rho$. If the order is a positive integer, then there are two possibilities: $g = \rho-1$ or $g = \rho$.

For example, $\sin$, $\cos$ and $\exp$ are entire functions of genus $g = \rho = 1$.

## Other examples {#Other_examples}

According to [J. E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._E._Littlewood), the [Weierstrass sigma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_sigma_function) is a 'typical' entire function. This statement can be made precise in the theory of random entire functions: the asymptotic behavior of almost all entire functions is similar to that of the sigma function. Other examples include the [Fresnel integrals](wikipedia://en/271143), the [Jacobi theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_theta_function), and the [reciprocal Gamma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_Gamma_function). The exponential function and the error function are special cases of the [Mittag-Leffler function](wikipedia://en/1603459). According to the fundamental [theorem of Paley and Wiener](wikipedia://en/632992), [Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/52247) of functions (or distributions) with bounded support are entire functions of order $1$ and finite type.

Other examples are solutions of linear differential equations with polynomial coefficients. If the coefficient at the highest derivative is constant, then all solutions of such equations are entire functions. For example, the exponential function, sine, cosine, [Airy functions](wikipedia://en/406896) and [Parabolic cylinder functions](wikipedia://en/1762360) arise in this way. The class of entire functions is closed with respect to compositions. This makes it possible to study [dynamics of entire functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_dynamics).

An entire function of the square root of a complex number is entire if the original function is [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_function), for example $\cos(\sqrt{z})$.

If a sequence of polynomials all of whose roots are real converges in a neighborhood of the origin to a limit which is not identically equal to zero, then this limit is an entire function. Such entire functions form the [Laguerre–Pólya class](wikipedia://en/38133627), which can also be characterized in terms of the Hadamard product, namely, $f$ belongs to this class [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) in the Hadamard representation all $z_n$ are real, $\rho\leq 1$, and $P(z)=a+bz+cz^2$, where $b$ and $c$ are real, and $c\leq 0$. For example, the sequence of polynomials

$$\left (1-\frac{(z-d)^2}{n} \right )^n$$

converges, as $n$ increases, to $\exp(-(z-d)^2)$. The polynomials

$$\frac{1}{2}\left ( \left (1+\frac{iz}{n} \right )^n+ \left (1-\frac{iz}{n} \right )^n \right )$$

have all real roots, and converge to $\cos(z)$. The polynomials

$$\prod_{m=1}^n \left(1-\frac{z^2}{\left ( \left (m-\frac{1}{2} \right )\pi \right )^2}\right)$$

also converge to $\cos(z)$, showing the buildup of the Hadamard product for cosine.
