# Field extension

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Field extension"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), a **field extension** is a pair of [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) $K \subseteq L$, such that the operations of *K* are those of *L* [restricted](wikipedia://en/1589032) to *K*. In this case, *L* is an **extension field** of *K*, and *K* is a **subfield** of *L*.[^o2][^o3][^o4] For example, under the usual notions of [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) are an extension field of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438); the real numbers are a subfield of the complex numbers.

:::

Field extensions are fundamental in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), and in the study of [polynomial roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_roots) through [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), and are widely used in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997).

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Subfield (mathematics)"}

## Subfield {#Subfield}

A **subfield** $K$ of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $L$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $K\subseteq L$ that is a field with respect to the field operations inherited from $L$. Equivalently, a subfield is a subset that contains the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) $1$, and is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under the operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and taking the [inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of a nonzero element of $K$.

As $1 - 1 = 0$, the latter definition implies $K$ and $L$ have the same [zero element](wikipedia://en/3476379).

For example, the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) is a subfield of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), which is itself a subfield of the complex numbers. More generally, the field of rational numbers is (or is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to) a subfield of any field of [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_of_a_ring) $0$.

The [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of a subfield is the same as the characteristic of the larger field.

:::

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Adjoining (field theory)"}

## Extension field {#Extension_field}

If $K$ is a subfield of $L$, then $L$ is an **extension field** or simply **extension** of $K$, and this pair of fields is a **field extension**. Such a field extension is denoted $L/K$ (read as "$L$ over $K$").

If $L$ is an extension of $F$, which is in turn an extension of $K$, then $F$ is said to be an **intermediate field** (or **intermediate extension** or **subextension**) of $L/K$.

Given a field extension $L/K$, the larger field $L$ is a $K$-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). The [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of this vector space is called the [**degree** of the extension](wikipedia://en/4382697) and is denoted by $[L:K]$.

The degree of an extension is 1 if and only if the two fields are equal. In this case, the extension is a **trivial extension**. Extensions of degree 2 and 3 are called **quadratic extensions** and **cubic extensions**, respectively. A **finite extension** is an extension that has a finite degree.

Given two extensions $L/K$ and $M/L$, the extension $M/K$ is finite if and only if both $L/K$ and $M/L$ are finite. In this case, one has

$$[M : K]=[M : L]\cdot[L : K].$$

Given a field extension $L/K$ and a subset $S$ of $L$, there is a smallest subfield of $L$ that contains $K$ and $S$. It is the intersection of all subfields of $L$ that contain $K$ and $S$, and is denoted by $K(S)$ (read as "$K$ *adjoin* $S$"). One says that $K(S)$ is the field *generated* by $S$ over $K$, and that $S$ is a [generating set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set) of $K(S)$ over $K$. When $S=\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ is finite, one writes $K(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ instead of $K(\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}),$ and one says that $K(S)$ is finitely generated over $K$. If $S$ consists of a single element $s$, the extension $K(s)/K$ is called a [simple extension](wikipedia://en/4771726)[^o9][^o10] and $s$ is called a [primitive element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_element_%28field_theory%29) of the extension.[^o11]

An extension field of the form $K(S)$ is often said to result from the *adjunction* of $S$ to $K$.[^o12][^o13]

In [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_of_a_ring) 0, every finite extension is a simple extension. This is the [primitive element theorem](wikipedia://en/448933), which does not hold true for fields of non-zero characteristic.

If a simple extension $K(s)/K$ is not finite, the field $K(s)$ is isomorphic to the field of [rational fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction) in $s$ over $K$.

:::

## Caveat {#Caveat}

The notation *L* / *K* is purely formal and does not imply the formation of a [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) or [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) or any other kind of division. Instead the slash expresses the word "over". Some authors use the notations *L* : *K* or *L* | *K*, while others may simply indicate verbally that $L\supset K$ is a field extension. Towers of extensions are often depicted diagrammatically. For example, the diagram below depicts the situation where *L* is an extension of *K* and *K* is an extension of *F*:

$$\begin{array}{c} L \\ \Big| \\ K \\ \Big| \\ F \end{array}$$

It is often useful to talk about field extensions in situations where the smaller field is not actually contained in the larger one, but is "naturally embedded", where a *natural embedding* is an [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) of the smaller field into the larger one that is either unique or uniquely defined by the context. Considering such a natural embedding as a field extension is an [abuse of terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abuse_of_terminology) that makes often the wording much simpler.

For example, there is a unique field homomorphism from $\mathbb{Q}$, the field rational numbers, into any field of characteristic 0, and one says commonly that every field of characteristic zero is an extension of $\mathbb{Q}$. On the other hand, there are two injective homomorphisms of the field $K=\mathbb{Q}[X]/(X^2-X-1)$ into $\mathbb{R}$, which map $X$ either to the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) $\omega = \tfrac {1+\sqrt 5}2$ or to $-1/\omega$. Although the two homomorphisms have the same [image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_of_a_function), it would be confusing to say that $\mathbb{R}$ is an extension of $K$.

Henceforth, we will consider field homomorphisms—always injective—as inclusions, when no confusion can arise.

## Examples {#Examples}

The field of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ is an extension field of the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{R}$ in turn is an extension field of the field of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$. Clearly then, $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{Q}$ is also a field extension. We have $[\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}] =2$ because $\{1, i\}$ is a basis, so the extension $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ is finite. This is a simple extension because $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i).$ $[\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{Q}] =\mathfrak c$ (the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901)), so this extension is infinite.

The field

$$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) = \left \{ a + b\sqrt{2} \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Q} \right \},$$

is an extension field of $\mathbb{Q},$ also clearly a simple extension. The degree is 2 because $\left\{1, \sqrt{2}\right\}$ can serve as a basis.

The field

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}\right) &= \mathbb{Q} \left(\sqrt{2}\right) \left(\sqrt{3}\right) \\ &= \left\{ a+b\sqrt{3} \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{2}\right) \right\} \\ &= \left\{ a + b \sqrt{2} + c\sqrt{3} + d\sqrt{6} \mid a,b,c, d \in \mathbb{Q} \right\}, \end{aligned}$$

is an extension field of both $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ and $\mathbb{Q},$ of degree 2 and 4 respectively. It is also a simple extension, as one can show that

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}) &= \mathbb{Q} (\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}) \\ &= \left \{ a + b (\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}) + c (\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})^2 + d(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})^3 \mid a,b,c, d \in \mathbb{Q}\right\}. \end{aligned}$$

Finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$ are also called [algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822) and are important in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). Another extension field of the rationals, which is also important in number theory, although not a finite extension, is the field of [p-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) $\mathbb{Q}_p$ for a prime number *p*.

It is common to construct an extension field of a given field *K* as a [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) of the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) *K*\[*X*\] in order to "create" a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) for a given polynomial *f*(*X*). Suppose for instance that *K* does not contain any element *x* with *x*<sup>2</sup> = −1. Then the polynomial $X^2+1$ is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) in *K*\[*X*\], consequently the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) generated by this polynomial is [maximal](wikipedia://en/48164), and $L = K[X]/(X^2+1)$ is an extension field of *K* which *does* contain an element whose square is −1 (namely the [residue class](wikipedia://en/20087) of *X*).

By iterating the above construction, one can construct a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of any polynomial from *K*\[*X*\]. This is an extension field *L* of *K* in which the given polynomial splits into a product of linear factors.

If *p* is any [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) and *n* is a positive integer, there is a unique (up to isomorphism) [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $GF(p^n) = \mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ with *p<sup>n</sup>* elements; this is an extension field of the [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field) $\operatorname{GF}(p) = \mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ with *p* elements.

Given a field *K*, we can consider the field *K*(*X*) of all [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in the variable *X* with coefficients in *K*; the elements of *K*(*X*) are fractions of two [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) over *K*, and indeed *K*(*X*) is the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the polynomial ring *K*\[*X*\]. This field of rational functions is an extension field of *K*. This extension is infinite.

Given a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) *M*, the set of all [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) defined on *M* is a field, denoted by $\mathbb{C}(M).$ It is a transcendental extension field of $\mathbb{C}$ if we identify every complex number with the corresponding [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861) defined on *M*. More generally, given an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) *V* over some field *K*, the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) *K*(*V*), consisting of the rational functions defined on *V*, is an extension field of *K*.

:::

## Algebraic extension {#Algebraic_extension}

An element *x* of a field extension $L/K$ is algebraic over *K* if it is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of a nonzero [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) with coefficients in *K*. For example, $\sqrt 2$ is algebraic over the rational numbers, because it is a root of $x^2-2.$ If an element *x* of *L* is algebraic over *K*, the [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) of lowest degree that has *x* as a root is called the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) of *x*. This minimal polynomial is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) over *K*.

An element *s* of *L* is algebraic over *K* if and only if the simple extension *K*(*s*) /*K* is a finite extension. In this case the degree of the extension equals the degree of the minimal polynomial, and a basis of the *K*-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *K*(*s*) consists of $1, s, s^2, \ldots, s^{d-1},$ where *d* is the degree of the minimal polynomial.

The set of the elements of *L* that are algebraic over *K* form a subextension, which is called the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of *K* in *L*. This results from the preceding characterization: if *s* and *t* are algebraic, the extensions *K*(*s*) /*K* and *K*(*s*)(*t*) /*K*(*s*) are finite. Thus *K*(*s*, *t*) /*K* is also finite, as well as the sub extensions *K*(*s* ± *t*) /*K*, *K*(*st*) /*K* and *K*(1/*s*) /*K* (if *s* ≠ 0). It follows that *s* ± *t*, *st* and 1/*s* are all algebraic.

An *algebraic extension* $L/K$ is an extension such that every element of *L* is algebraic over *K*. Equivalently, an algebraic extension is an extension that is generated by algebraic elements. For example, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$ is an algebraic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, because $\sqrt 2$ and $\sqrt 3$ are algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}.$

A simple extension is algebraic [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is finite. This implies that an extension is algebraic if and only if it is the union of its finite subextensions, and that every finite extension is algebraic.

Every field *K* has an algebraic closure, which is [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an isomorphism the largest extension field of *K* which is algebraic over *K*, and also the smallest extension field such that every polynomial with coefficients in *K* has a root in it. For example, $\mathbb{C}$ is an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$, but not an algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$, as it is not algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ (for example $\pi$ is not algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$).

## Transcendental extension {#Transcendental_extension}

Given a field extension $L/K$, a subset *S* of *L* is called [algebraically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_independent) over *K* if no non-trivial polynomial relation with coefficients in *K* exists among the elements of *S*. The largest cardinality of an algebraically independent set is called the [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) of *L*/*K*. It is always possible to find a set *S*, algebraically independent over *K*, such that *L*/*K*(*S*) is algebraic. Such a set *S* is called a [transcendence basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_basis) of *L*/*K*. All transcendence bases have the same cardinality, equal to the transcendence degree of the extension. An extension $L/K$ is said to be **purely transcendental** if and only if there exists a transcendence basis *S* of $L/K$ such that *L* = *K*(*S*). Such an extension has the property that all elements of *L* except those of *K* are transcendental over *K*, but, however, there are extensions with this property which are not purely transcendental—a class of such extensions take the form *L*/*K* where both *L* and *K* are algebraically closed.

If *L*/*K* is purely transcendental and *S* is a transcendence basis of the extension, it doesn't necessarily follow that *L* = *K*(*S*). On the opposite, even when one knows a transcendence basis, it may be difficult to decide whether the extension is purely separable, and if it is so, it may be difficult to find a transcendence basis *S* such that *L* = *K*(*S*).

For example, consider the extension $\mathbb{Q}(x, y)/\mathbb{Q},$ where $x$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q},$ and $y$ is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_root) of the equation $y^2-x^3=0.$ Such an extension can be defined as $\mathbb{Q}(X)[Y]/\langle Y^2-X^3\rangle,$ in which $x$ and $y$ are the [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of $X$ and $Y.$ Obviously, the singleton set $\{x\}$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{Q}$ and the extension $\mathbb{Q}(x, y)/\mathbb{Q}(x)$ is algebraic; hence $\{x\}$ is a transcendence basis that does not generate the extension $\mathbb{Q}(x, y)/\mathbb{Q}(x)$. Similarly, $\{y\}$ is a transcendence basis that does not generate the whole extension. However the extension is purely transcendental since, if one set $t=y/x,$ one has $x=t^2$ and $y=t^3,$ and thus $t$ generates the whole extension.

Purely transcendental extensions of an algebraically closed field occur as [function fields](wikipedia://en/2783843) of [rational varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_varieties). The problem of finding a [rational parametrization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_parametrization) of a rational variety is equivalent with the problem of finding a transcendence basis that generates the whole extension.

:::

## Normal, separable and Galois extensions {#Normal,_separable_and_Galois_extensions}

An algebraic extension $L/K$ is called [normal](wikipedia://en/929925) if every [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) in *K*\[*X*\] that has a root in *L* completely factors into linear factors over *L*. Every algebraic extension *F*/*K* admits a normal closure *L*, which is an extension field of *F* such that $L/K$ is normal and which is minimal with this property.

An algebraic extension $L/K$ is called [separable](wikipedia://en/363340) if the minimal polynomial of every element of *L* over *K* is [separable](wikipedia://en/362400), i.e., has no repeated roots in an algebraic closure over *K*. A [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) is a field extension that is both normal and separable.

A consequence of the [primitive element theorem](wikipedia://en/448933) states that every finite separable extension has a primitive element (i.e. is simple).

Given any field extension $L/K$, we can consider its **automorphism group** $\text{Aut}(L/K)$, consisting of all field [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) *α*: *L* → *L* with *α*(*x*) = *x* for all *x* in *K*. When the extension is Galois this automorphism group is called the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of the extension. Extensions whose Galois group is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) are called [abelian extensions](wikipedia://en/174448).

For a given field extension $L/K$, one is often interested in the intermediate fields *F* (subfields of *L* that contain *K*). The significance of Galois extensions and Galois groups is that they allow a complete description of the intermediate fields: there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the intermediate fields and the [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of the Galois group, described by the [fundamental theorem of Galois theory](wikipedia://en/1947635).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Field extensions can be generalized to [ring extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_extensions) which consist of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) and one of its [subrings](wikipedia://en/24873145). A closer non-commutative analog are [central simple algebras](wikipedia://en/404082) (CSAs) – ring extensions over a field, which are [simple algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_algebra) (no non-trivial 2-sided ideals, just as for a field) and where the [center of the ring](wikipedia://en/53796787) is exactly the field. For example, the only finite field extension of the real numbers is the complex numbers, while the quaternions are a central simple algebra over the reals, and all CSAs over the reals are [Brauer equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer_equivalent) to the reals or the quaternions. CSAs can be further generalized to [Azumaya algebras](wikipedia://en/3125089), where the base field is replaced by a commutative [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391).

:::

## Extension of scalars {#Extension_of_scalars}

Given a field extension, one can "[extend scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_of_scalars)" on associated algebraic objects. For example, given a real vector space, one can produce a complex vector space via [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604). In addition to vector spaces, one can perform extension of scalars for [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112) defined over the field, such as polynomials or [group algebras](wikipedia://en/349223) and the associated [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695). Extension of scalars of polynomials is often used implicitly, by just considering the coefficients as being elements of a larger field, but may also be considered more formally. Extension of scalars has numerous applications, as discussed in [extension of scalars: applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_of_scalars#Applications).
