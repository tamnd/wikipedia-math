# Loaded question

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Loaded question"}

A **loaded question** is a form of [complex question](wikipedia://en/700051) that contains a controversial [assumption](wikipedia://en/3240770) (e.g., a [presumption of guilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presumption_of_guilt)).[^o2]

:::

Such questions may be used as a [rhetorical](wikipedia://en/25447) tool: the question attempts to limit direct replies to be those that serve the questioner's agenda.[^o3] The traditional example is the question "Have you stopped beating your wife?" Without further clarification, an answer of either yes or no suggests the respondent has beaten their wife at some time in the past. Thus, these facts are *[presupposed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presupposed)* by the question, and in this case an entrapment, because it narrows the respondent to a single answer, and the [fallacy of many questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy_of_many_questions) has been committed.[^o3]

The fallacy relies upon context for its effect: the fact that a question presupposes something does not in itself make the question [fallacious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacious). Only when some of these presuppositions are not necessarily agreed to by the person who is asked the question does the argument containing them become fallacious.[^o3] Hence, the same question may be loaded in one context, but not in the other. For example, the previous question would not be loaded if it were asked during a trial in which the defendant had already admitted to beating his wife.[^o3]

This [informal fallacy](wikipedia://en/1655450) should be distinguished from that of [begging the question](wikipedia://en/43582),[^o4] which offers a [premise](wikipedia://en/7993337) whose plausibility depends on the truth of the [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) asked about, and which is often an implicit restatement of the proposition.[^o5]

## Defense {#Defense}

A common way out of this argument is not to answer the question (e.g. with a simple 'yes' or 'no'), but to challenge the assumption behind the question. To use an earlier example, a good response to the question "Have you stopped beating your wife?" would be "I have *never* beaten my wife".[^o7] This removes the ambiguity of the expected response, therefore nullifying the tactic. However, the asker may respond to a challenge by accusing the one who answers of [dodging the question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Question_dodging).

## Historical examples {#Historical_examples}

[Diogenes Laërtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_Laërtius) wrote a brief biography of the philosopher [Menedemus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menedemus) in which he relates that:[^o9] \[O\]nce when Alexinus asked him whether he had left off beating his father, he said, "I have not beaten him, and I have not left off;" and when he said further that he ought to put an end to the doubt by answering explicitly yes or no, "It would be absurd," he rejoined, "to comply with your conditions, when I can stop you at the entrance."[^o10]

For another example, the [2009 New Zealand child discipline referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_New_Zealand_child_discipline_referendum) asked: "Should a smack as part of good parental correction be a criminal offence in New Zealand?" Murray Edridge, of Barnardos New Zealand, criticized the question as "loaded and ambiguous" and claimed "the question presupposes that smacking is a part of good parental correction".[^o11]
