The **demography of Germany** is monitored by the *Statistisches Bundesamt* ([Federal Statistical Office of Germany](wikipedia://en/7516079)). According to the most recent data, [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany)'s population is 83,467,117 (31 December 2025)[^o2] making it the most populous country in the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union) and the nineteenth-most populous country in the world. The [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) was rated at 1.32 in 2025,[^o11] significantly below the [replacement rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replacement_rate) of 2.1. Germany has had one of the world's lowest fertility rates of around 1.3 to 1.4 since the 1980s.[^o12] Due to a low birth rate, Germany has recorded more deaths than births every year since 1972,[^o13] which means 2025 was the 54th consecutive year the German population would have decreased without immigration. However, due to immigration the population actually increased by around five million people over the last half-century. In 2025, the number of people with a foreign background was about 31%;[^o14] this category includes foreigners, naturalized citizens, ethnic [German repatriates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spätaussiedler) from [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) and the children of all of the above.

:::

Until the early 20th century Germany was also a large emigrant nation; in the 19th century more than 5 million citizens of the [German Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire) emigrated [to the US alone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Americans), and in the early 20th century Germany lost another two million to the US as well as significant numbers to Latin America, Canada and Eastern Europe. However, after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) immigration began to outweigh emigration, as around 14 million ethnic [Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans) [were expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_%281944–1950%29) from the former eastern provinces of the Reich and other areas in Eastern Europe. Of these, roughly 12 million made their way to present-day Germany and several hundred thousand settled in Austria and other countries, while several hundred thousand died. Some additional 4.5 million ethnic Germans from Eastern Europe repatriated after 1950, especially around the end of the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) and mostly from the former Soviet Union, Poland and Romania.[^o15][^o16]

Large-scale immigration to [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany) began during the time of the [Wirtschaftswunder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wirtschaftswunder) from the 1950s to early 1970s when Germany had a shortage of workers and let in Southern Europeans from countries like Turkey, Italy and Spain on a temporary basis as [guest workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guest_workers). The liberalisation of guest worker legislation allowed many to stay and build a life in [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany). Germany saw another large wave of immigration towards the end of the 20th century, driven by [German reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification), refugee inflows from the [Yugoslav Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslav_Wars) and large numbers of Turkish nationals [seeking asylum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asylum_in_Germany). The next large immigration wave began after eastern expansion of the European Union in 2011 as Eastern Europeans were now allowed to live and work in Germany without a visa. During the [European migrant crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_European_migrant_crisis) of 2015 Germany took in a large number of refugees, both in absolute terms and relative to other EU member states; the country recorded 476,649 asylum seekers in 2015, 745,545 in 2016 and declining numbers thereafter.[^o17]

Germany has one of the world's highest levels of education, technological development, and economic productivity. Since the end of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), the number of students entering university has more than tripled, and the trade and technical schools are among the world's best. With a per capita income of about €40,883 in 2018,[^o18] Germany is a broadly middle-class society. However, there has been a strong increase in the number of children living in [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_in_Germany). In 1965, one in 75 children was on the welfare rolls; but by 2007 this had increased to one child in six. These children live in relative poverty, but not necessarily in absolute poverty.[^o19] Millions of Germans travel overseas each year. The social welfare system provides for [universal health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_health_care), [unemployment compensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment_compensation), [child benefits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_benefits) and other social programmes. Germany's aging population and struggling economy strained the welfare system in the 1990s, resulting in the government adopting a wide-ranging programme of controversial belt-tightening reforms, [Agenda 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agenda_2010), including the labour-market reforms known as [Hartz concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartz_concept).

## History {#History}

### 1945–1990 {#1945–1990}

*Image: Population evolution Germany.svg.* Population evolution of Germany, since 1950

After the [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) [border shifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Territorial_changes_of_Germany) and [expulsions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsions_of_Germans_after_World_War_II), the [Germans from Central and Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_%281944–1950%29) and the [former eastern territories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Former_eastern_territories_of_Germany) moved westward to [post-war Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Germany_since_1945). During the partition of Germany, many Germans from [East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germany) fled to [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany) for political and economic reasons. Since Germany's reunification, there are ongoing migrations from the eastern *[New Länder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_states_of_Germany)* to the western *Old Länder* for economic reasons.

The Federal Republic of Germany and the German Democratic Republic followed different paths when it came to demographics. The politics of the German Democratic Republic was pronatalistic[^o23] while that of the Federal Republic was compensatory.

Fertility in the GDR was higher than that in the FRG. Demographic politics was only one of the reasons. Women in the GDR had fewer "biographic options". Young motherhood was expected of them. State funded cost-free childcare was available to all mothers.[^o24]

### 1990–today {#1990–today}

About 1.7 million people have left the new federal states (the East) since the fall of the Berlin Wall, or 12% of the population;[^o28] a disproportionately high number of them were women under 35.[^o29]

After 1990, the total fertility rate (TFR) in the East dropped to 0.772 in 1994. This has been attributed to a "demographic shock": people not only had fewer children, they were also less likely to marry or divorce after the end of the GDR; the biographic options of the citizens of the former GDR had increased. Young motherhood seemed to be less attractive and the age of the first birth rose sharply.[^o24]

In the following years, the TFR in the East started to rise again, surpassing 1.0 in 1997 and 1.3 in 2004, and reaching the West's TFR (1.37) in 2007. In 2010, the East's fertility rate (1.459) clearly exceeded that of the West (1.385), while Germany's overall TFR had risen to 1.393, the highest value since 1990,[^o30][^o31] which was still far below the natural replacement rate of 2.1 and the birth rates seen under communism. In 2016, the TFR was 1.64 in the East and 1.60 in the West.[^o32]

Between 1989 and 2009, about 2,000 schools closed because there were fewer children.[^o28]

In some regions the number of women between the ages of 20 and 30 has dropped by more than 30%.[^o28] In 2004, in the age group 18–29 (statistically important for starting families) there were only 90 women for every 100 men in the new federal states (the East, including Berlin).

Until 2007 family politics in the federal republic was compensatory, which means that poor families received more family benefits (such as the *Erziehungsgeld*) than rich ones. In 2007 the so-called *Elterngeld* was introduced. According to [Christoph Butterwegge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Butterwegge) the Elterngeld was meant to "motivate highly educated women to have more children"; the poor on the other hand were disadvantaged by the *Elterngeld*, and now received lower child benefits than the middle classes.[^o33] The very well-off (who earn more than 250.000 Euro per annum) and those on welfare receive no Elterngeld payments.[^o34]

In 2013 the following most recent developments were noticed:[^o35]

- The income of families with young children has risen. Persons holding a college degree, persons older than 30 years and parents with only one child benefited the most. Single parents and young parents did not benefit.
- Fathers are becoming more involved in parenting, and 28% of them now take some time off work (3.3 months on average) when their children are born.
- Mothers are more likely to work and as a result less likely to be economically deprived than they used to be.
- The birth rate of college-educated women has risen.

In the new federal states the fertility rate of college-educated women is now higher than that of those without college degrees. Differences in value priorities and the better availability of childcare in the eastern states are discussed as possible reasons.[^o36]

In 2019, the non-profit [Austrian Institute of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Institute_of_Economic_Research) and the [Bertelsmann Stiftung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertelsmann_Stiftung) published a study about the economic impact of demographics. The researchers assume a reduction in the [per capita income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_capita_income) of [€](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro)3,700 until 2040.[^o37]

## Population {#Population}

*Image: Historical\_population\_of\_Germany.svg.* Historical population of Germany

The contemporary demographics of Germany used to also be measured by a series of full [censuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_in_Germany) mandated by the state, with the most recent held in 1987. Since reunification, German authorities rely on a *micro census*. Demographic statistics according to the World Population Review.[^o39] Due to the privacy concerns of the German population after reunification, Germany did not hold a regular census until the EU-mandated [2011 German Census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_German_Census). The requirement was met with large disapproval. As required by the EU, Germany now continues to hold censuses every 10 years.[^o40]

- One death every 34 seconds
- Net gain of one person every 4 minutes
- One net migrant every 2 minutes

## Vital statistics {#Vital_statistics}

### Statistics since 1817 {#Statistics_since_1817}

Population statistics since 1817.[^o56][^o57] Territorial changes of Germany occurred in 1866 (establishment of [North German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_German_Confederation)), 1871 (German unification and annexation of Alsace-Lorraine), 1918/1919, 1921/1922, 1945/1946 and in 1990. Death data may have slight inaccuracies for both world wars.

Notable events in German demographics:

- 1831, 1848–1855, 1866, 1892–1893 – [Cholera outbreaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cholera)
- 1848 – [Revolutions of 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848)
- 1871 – [Smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox)
- 1870–1871 – [Franco-Prussian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War)
- 1899–1900 – [Russian flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1889–1890_pandemic)
- 1914–1918 – [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War)
- 1918–1919 – [Spanish Flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu)
- 1939–1945 – [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War)
- 1944–1949 – [Flight and expulsion of Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_%281944–1950%29)

## Social issues {#Social_issues}

Most childbirths in Germany happen within marriage. Out of 778,080 births in 2019, 258,835 were to unmarried parents,[^o120] which means that around 33% or one third of the children are born out of wedlock, while two thirds are within. This percentage of unmarried birth has long been growing and reached 33% in 2010, more than twice of what it was in 1990.[^o121] However, in recent years it has started to stagnate or even decrease.[^o122]

The Mikrozensus done in 2008 revealed that the number of children a German woman aged 40 to 75 had, was closely linked to her educational achievement.[^o123] In Western Germany, women with tertiary education had the highest rates of childlessness — 26% stated that they were childless. This rate dropped to 16% for those with intermediate education, and 11% for those with only compulsory education. In Eastern Germany however, 9% of the most educated women of that age group and 7% of those who had an intermediary education were childless, while 12% of those having only compulsory education were childless.

The reason for that east-western difference is that the GDR had an "educated mother scheme" and actively tried to encourage first births among the more educated. It did so by propagandizing the opinion that every educated woman should "present at least one child to socialism" and also by financially rewarding its more educated citizen to become parents. The government especially tried to persuade students to become parents while still in college and it was quite successful in doing so. In 1986, 38% of all women, who were about to graduate from college, were mothers of at least one child and an additional 14% were pregnant and 43% of all men, who were about to graduate from college, were fathers of at least one child. There was a sharp decline in the birth rate and especially in the birth rate of the educated after the fall of the Berlin wall. Nowadays, 5% of those about to graduate from college are parents.

The more educated a Western German mother aged 40 to 75 was in 2008, the less likely she was to have a big family.

In 2011, this trend was reversed in Eastern Germany, where more highly educated women now had a somewhat higher fertility rate than the rest of the population.[^o127]

Persons who said they had no religion tend to have fewer children than those who identify as Christians, and studies also found that conservative-leaning Christians had more children compared to liberal-leaning Christians.[^o128][^o129]

A study done in 2005 in the western German state of [Nordrhein-Westfalen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordrhein-Westfalen) by the HDZ revealed that childlessness was especially widespread among scientists. It showed that 78% of the women scientists and 71% of the male scientists working in that state were childless.[^o130]

## Ethnic minorities and migrant background (*Migrationshintergrund*) {#Ethnic_minorities_and_migrant_background_(Migrationshintergrund)}

Germany does not collect data on the ethnic and racial identifications of its citizens, but does collect data on the background group by birth of an individual.[^o133] The [Federal Statistical Office](wikipedia://en/7516079) defines persons with a migrant background as all persons who migrated to the present area of the Federal Republic of Germany after 1949, plus all foreign nationals born in Germany and all persons born in Germany as German nationals with at least one parent who migrated to Germany or was born in Germany as a foreign national. The figures presented here are based on this definition only.

In 2010, 2.3 million families with children under 18 years were living in Germany, in which at least one parent had foreign roots. They represented 29% of the total of 8.1 million families with minor children. Compared with 2005 – the year when the microcensus started to collect detailed information on the population with a migrant background – the proportion of migrant families has risen by 2 percentage points.[^o134] In 2019, 40% children under 5 years old had migrant background.[^o135]

Most of the families with a migrant background live in the western part of Germany. In 2010, the proportion of migrant families in all families was 32% in the former territory of the Federal Republic. This figure was more than double that in the new Länder (incl. Berlin) where it stood at 15%.[^o134] Eastern Germany has a much lower proportion of immigrants than the West, as the GDR did not let in that many guest workers and Eastern Germany's economy is not doing as well as West Germany's and had a higher percentage of jobless persons until recently. However, in recent years the number of people with an immigrant background in East Germany has been growing as refugees (as well as German Repatriates) are distributed with the Königssteiner Schlüssel, so every German state has to take the same number of them compared to its population and economy. In 2019 19.036 million people or 89,6% of people with an immigrant background live in Western Germany (excluding Berlin), being 28,7% of its population, while 1.016 million people with immigrant background 4,8% live in Eastern States, being 8,2% of population, and 1.194 million people with an immigrant background 5,6% live in Berlin, being 33,1% of its population.[^o135]

In 2019, 26% of Germans of any age group (up from 18,4% in 2008) and 39% of German children (up from 30% in 2008) had at least one parent born abroad. Average age for Germans with at least one parent born abroad was 35.6 years (up from 33.8 years in 2008), while that for Germans, who had two parents born in Germany was 47.3 years (up from 44.6 in 2008).[^o135][^o136]

In 2022, 41 percent of the under-15 age group had a migrant background, 36 percent of the 15 to 49-year-old age group had a migrant background and 19 percent of the age group above (50+).[^o137]

The largest groups of people with an immigrant background in Germany are people that have Turkey, Poland and Russia as their ancestral homelands.

Four other sizable groups of people are referred to as "national minorities" (*nationale Minderheiten*) because they have lived in their respective regions for centuries: [Danes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danes), [Frisians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisians), [Roma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people) and [Sinti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinti), and [Sorbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorbs). There is a [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark) minority (about 50,000, according to government sources) in the northernmost state of [Schleswig-Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schleswig-Holstein). Eastern and Northern [Frisians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisians) live at Schleswig-Holstein's western coast, and in the north-western part of [Lower Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Saxony). They are part of a wider community ([Frisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisia)) stretching from Germany to the northern [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands). The [Sorbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorbs), a [Slavic people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_people) with about 60,000 members (according to government sources), are in the [Lusatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lusatia) region of [Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_State_of_Saxony) and [Brandenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandenburg). They are the last remnants of the Slavs that lived in central and eastern Germany since the 7th century to have kept their traditions and not been completely integrated into the wider German nation.

Until [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) the [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people) were recognized as one of the national minorities. In 1924 the [Union of Poles in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Poles_in_Germany) had initiated cooperation between all national minorities in Germany under the umbrella organization [Association of National Minorities in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_National_Minorities_in_Germany). Some of the union members wanted the Polish communities in easternmost Germany (now [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland)) to join the newly established Polish nation after [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I).[^o171] Even before the German invasion of Poland, leading anti-Nazi members of the Polish minority were deported to concentration camps; some were executed at the Piaśnica murder site. Minority rights for Poles in Germany were revoked by Hermann Göring's World War II decree of 27 February 1940, and their property was confiscated.

After the war ended, the German government did not re-implement national minority rights for ethnic Poles. The reason for this is that the areas of Germany which formerly had a native Polish minority were annexed to Poland and the Soviet Union, while almost all of the native German populations (formerly the ethnic majority) in these areas [subsequently fled or were expelled by force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_from_Poland_during_and_after_World_War_II). With the mixed German-Polish territories now lost, the German government subsequently regarded ethnic Poles residing in what remained of Germany as immigrants, just like any other ethnic population with a recent history of arrival. In contrast, [Germans living in Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans_in_Poland) are recognized as national minority and have granted seats in Polish Parliament. It must be said, however, that an overwhelming number of Germans in Poland have centuries-old historical ties to the lands they now inhabit, whether from living in territory that once belonged to the German state, or from centuries-old communities. In contrast, most Poles in present-day Germany are recent immigrants, though there are some communities which have been present since the 19th and perhaps even the 18th centuries. Despite protests by some in the older Polish-German communities, and despite Germany being now a signatory to the Framework Convention for the Protection of National Minorities, Germany has so far refused to re-implement minority rights for ethnic Poles, based on the fact that almost all areas of historically mixed German-Polish heritage (where the minority rights formerly existed) are no longer part of Germany and because the vast majority of ethnic Poles now residing in Germany are recent immigrants.

[Roma people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people) have been in Germany since the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages). They were persecuted by the [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazis), and thousands of Roma living in Germany were killed by the Nazi regime. Nowadays, they are spread all over Germany, mostly living in major cities. It is difficult to estimate their exact number, as the German government counts them as "persons without migrant background" in their statistics. There are also many assimilated Sinti and Roma. A vague figure given by the German Department of the Interior is about 70,000. In contrast to the old-established Roma population, the majority of them do not have German citizenship, and are classified as [immigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigrants) or [refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugee).

*Image: Bundesarchiv B 145 Bild-F079037-0017, Lager Friedland, Familie aus Sibirien.jpg.* A family of so-called "Spätaussiedler" (repatriates of ethnic German origin), because the parents were born abroad they will be counted as "persons with immigrant background"

After World War II, 14 million ethnic Germans [were expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsion_of_Germans_after_World_War_II) from the [eastern territories of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Former_eastern_territories_of_Germany) and homelands outside the former German Empire. The accommodation and integration of these *[Heimatvertriebene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heimatvertriebene)* in the remaining part of Germany, in which many cities and millions of apartments had been destroyed, was a major effort in the post-war occupation zones and later states of Germany.

Since the 1960s, ethnic Germans from the [People's Republic of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_Poland) and [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) (especially from [Kazakhstan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kazakhstan), [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia), and [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine)), have come to Germany. During the time of [Perestroika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perestroika), and after the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the number of immigrants increased heavily. Some of these immigrants are of mixed ancestry. During the 10-year period between 1987 and 2001, a total of 1,981,732 ethnic Germans from the FSU immigrated to Germany, along with more than a million of their non-German relatives. After 1997, however ethnic Slavs or those belonging to Slavic-Germanic mixed origins outnumbered those with only Germanic descent amongst the immigrants. The total number of people currently living in Germany having FSU connection is around 4 to 4.5 million (Including Germans, Slavs, Jews, and those of mixed origins), out of that more than 50% are of German descent.[^o172][^o173]

Germany now has Europe's third-largest [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews) population. In 2004, twice as many Jews from former [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) republics settled in Germany as in [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel), bringing the total inflow to more than 100,000 since 1991.[^o174] Jews have a voice in German public life through the [Central Council of Jews in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Council_of_Jews_in_Germany) (Zentralrat der Juden in Deutschland). Some Jews from the former Soviet Union are of mixed heritage. Today, less than 0.1% of the total population of Germany is Jewish.

In 2019 there were also a growing number of at least 529,000 black [Afro-Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Germans) defined as people with an African migrant background.[^o135] Out of them more than 400 thousand have a citizenship of a Subsahara-African country,[^o175] with others being German citizens. Most of them live in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin) and [Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburg). Numerous persons from northern African [Tunisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunisia) and [Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco) live in Germany. While they are considered members of a minority group, for the most part, they do not consider themselves "Afro-Germans", nor are most of them perceived as such by the German people. However, Germany does not keep any statistics regarding ethnicity or race. Hence, the exact number of Germans of African descent is unknown.

Germany's biggest East Asian minorities are the [Chinese people in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_people_in_Germany), numbering 189,000[^o135] and [Vietnamese people in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_people_in_Germany), numbering 188,000,[^o135] many of whom living in Berlin and eastern Germany. Also there are about 35,000 [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_people) citizens residing in Germany.[^o176] There are also groups of [South Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian) and [Southeast Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia) immigrants. Around 163,000 [Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Germany) and 124,000 [Pakistanis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistanis_in_Germany) live in Germany.[^o135] Additionally some 30,000 [Filipino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filipinos_in_Germany) citizens and more than 20,000 [Indonesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Indonesian) citizens reside in Germany.[^o176]

Numerous descendants of the so-called *[Gastarbeiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastarbeiter)* live in Germany. The *Gastarbeiter* mostly came from [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece), [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain), [Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco), [Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal), the former [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslavia), [Tunisia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunisia) and [Chile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chile). Also included were [Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam), [Mongolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolia), [North Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korea), [Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angola), [Mozambique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozambique) and [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba) when the former East Germany existed until reunification in 1990.[^o177] The (socialist) German Democratic Republic (East Germany) however had their guest-workers stay in single-sex dormitories.[^o178] Female guest workers had to sign contracts saying that they were not allowed to fall pregnant during their stay. If they fell pregnant nevertheless they faced [forced abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_abortion) or deportation.[^o179] This is one of the reasons why the vast majority of ethnic minorities today lives in western Germany and also one of the reasons why minorities such as the Vietnamese have the most unusual [population pyramid](wikipedia://en/989011), with nearly all second-generation Vietnamese Germans born after 1989.

## Population density and distribution {#Population_density_and_distribution}

*Image: Population density of Germany by municipality.svg.* Population density by [municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Municipalities_of_Germany)

With an estimated 83.2 million inhabitants in December 2020,[^o186] Germany is the second-most populous country in Europe after Russia, and ranks as the [19th largest country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_population) in the world in terms of population. Its [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population_density) stands at 233 inhabitants per square kilometer.

### States {#States}

Germany comprises [sixteen states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany) that are collectively referred to as *Länder*.[^o188] Due to differences in size and population the [subdivision of these states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany#Subdivisions) varies, especially between [city-states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-state) (*Stadtstaaten*) and states with larger territories (*Flächenländer*). For regional administrative purposes four states, namely Baden-Württemberg, Bavaria, Hesse and North Rhine-Westphalia, consist of a total of 19 [Government Districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regierungsbezirke) (*Regierungsbezirke*). As of 2019 Germany is divided into 400 [districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Districts_of_Germany) (*Kreise*) at municipal level; these consist of 294 [rural districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_rural_districts_of_Germany) and 106 [urban districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_districts_of_Germany).

## Immigration {#Immigration}

The [United Nations Population Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Population_Fund) lists Germany as host to the [third-highest number of international migrants worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_immigrant_population), behind the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) and [Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabia).[^o203] The largest ethnic group of non-German origin are the [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_people). Since the 1960s, [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany) and later [reunified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reunification_of_Germany) Germany has attracted immigrants primarily from [Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Europe) and [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) as well as [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey), many of whom (or their children) have acquired German citizenship over time. While most of these immigrants initially arrived as [guest workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastarbeiter), changes to guest worker legislation allowed many to stay and to build lives in Germany.

Germany had signed special visa agreements with several countries in times of severe labour shortages or when particular skills were deficient within the country. During the 1960s and 1970s, agreements were signed with the governments of [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey), [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslavia), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy) and [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain) to help Germany overcome its severe labour shortage.

As of 2012, after Germany fully legalized visa-free immigrants from the eastern states of the EU, the largest sources of net immigration to Germany were other European countries, most importantly [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland), [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania), [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria), [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy), [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain), and [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece); notably, in the case of [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey), [German Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turks_in_Germany) moving to Turkey slightly outnumbered new immigrants in 2012,[^o204] however, in recent years there are more Turkish immigrants in Germany than emigrants again, including illegal Turkish migrants.

In 2015, there was a large increase in asylum applications following the [2015 refugee crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2015_European_migrant_crisis), mainly due to the violent conflicts in Syria, Iraq and Afghanistan: 476,649 asylum applications were counted that year.[^o205] This number went up to even 745,545 in 2016 and began to decline after it.[^o17]

In 2022, some 23.8 million people in Germany, or 28.7% of the population have migrant background.[^o206]

Following the [2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine), over 1.06 million refugees from Ukraine were recorded in Germany as of April 2023.[^o207]

## Education {#Education}

*Image: MSM-sporthalle.jpg.* Cadets of the German Navy exercising in front of one of the gyms of Germany's naval officers school, the Marineschule Mürwik

Responsibility for educational oversight in Germany lies primarily with the individual [federated states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany). Since the 1960s, a reform movement has attempted to unify secondary education into a *Gesamtschule* ([comprehensive school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_school)); several West German states later simplified their school systems to two or three tiers. A system of apprenticeship called *Duale Ausbildung* ("dual education") allows pupils in [vocational training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocational_training) to learn in a company as well as in a state-run vocational school.[^o211]

Optional [kindergarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kindergarten) education is provided for all children between three and six years old, after which [school attendance is compulsory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schulpflicht) for at least nine years, depending on the [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany). Primary education usually lasts for four years and public schools are not stratified at this stage.[^o211] In contrast, secondary education includes three traditional types of schools focused on different levels of academic ability: the *[Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28school%29)* enrols the most academically promising children and prepares students for university studies; the *[Realschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realschule)* for intermediate students lasts six years; the *[Hauptschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauptschule)* prepares pupils for vocational education.[^o212]

In addition [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) has a comprehensive school known as the *[Gesamtschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesamtschule)*. While some German schools such as the [Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28Germany%29) and the [Realschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realschule) have rather strict entrance requirements, the Gesamtschule does not have such requirements. They offer college preparatory classes for the students who are doing well, general education classes for average students, and remedial courses for those who aren't doing that well. In most cases students attending a Gesamtschule may graduate with the [Hauptschulabschluss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauptschulabschluss), the [Realschulabschluss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realschulabschluss) or the [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur) depending on how well they did in school. The percentage of students attending a Gesamtschule varies by [Bundesland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany). In 2007 the State of Brandenburg more than 50% of all students attended a Gesamtschule,[^o213] while in the State of Bavaria less than 1% did.

The general entrance requirement for university is [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur), a qualification normally based on continuous assessment during the last few years at school and final examinations; however there are a number of exceptions, and precise requirements vary, depending on the state, the university and the subject. Germany's universities are recognised internationally; in the [Academic Ranking of World Universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_Ranking_of_World_Universities) (ARWU) for 2008, six of the top 100 universities in the world are in Germany, and 18 of the top 200.[^o214] Nearly all German universities are public institutions, tuition fees in the range of €500 were introduced in some states after 2006, but quickly abolished again until 2014.

### Literacy {#Literacy}

Over 99% of those of age 15 and above are estimated to be able to read and write. However, a growing number of inhabitants are functionally illiterate. The young are much more likely to be functionally illiterate than the old. According to a study done by the [University of Bremen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bremen) in cooperation with the "Bundesverband Alphabetisierung e.V.", 10% of youngsters living in Germany are functionally illiterate and one quarter are able to understand only basic level texts.[^o218] Illiteracy rates of youngsters vary by ethnic group and parents' socioeconomic class.

## Health {#Health}

The [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669) in Germany is 81.1 years (78.7 years males, 83.6 years females, 2020 est.).[^o220] , the principal cause of death was cardiovascular disease, at 33.7%, followed by malignant tumours, at 23.7%.[^o221] , about 82,000 Germans had been infected with HIV/AIDS and 26,000 had died from the disease (cumulatively, since 1982).[^o222] According to a 2005 survey, 27% of German adults are smokers.[^o222] A 2009 study shows Germany is near the median in terms of overweight and obese people in Europe.[^o223]

## Religion {#Religion}

*Image: Konfessionen-in-Deutschland-2020.svg.* 2020 map of [Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination) in the [states of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States_of_Germany) \#214478 \#3771c8 \#c83737 \#e9afaf

The national constitutions of 1919 and 1949 guarantee freedom of [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith) and [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion); earlier, these freedoms were mentioned only in state constitutions. The modern constitution of 1949 also states that no one may be [discriminated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrimination) against due to their faith or religious opinions. A state church does not exist in Germany (see [Freedom of religion in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_religion_in_Germany)).[^o225]

According to a 1990s poll by *Der Spiegel*, 45% of Germans believe in God, and a quarter in Jesus Christ.[^o226] According to the [Eurobarometer Poll](wikipedia://en/357317) 2010, 44% of German citizens responded that "they believe there is a God", 25% responded that "they believe there is some sort of spirit or life force" and 27% responded that "they don't believe there is any sort of spirit, God or life force". 4% gave no response.[^o227]

[Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) is the largest religion in Germany, comprising roughly half of the country's population.[^o228][^o229][^o230]

Smaller religious groups (less than 1%) include [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism), [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) and [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism).[^o231][^o228]

The two largest churches, the [Roman Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholicism_in_Germany) and the [Protestant Church in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Church_in_Germany) (EKD), have lost significant number of adherents. In 2023 the Catholic Church accounted for 24%[^o228][^o229][^o232] and the Evangelical Church for 21.9%[^o228][^o229][^o233] of the population. [Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church) has 1.5% and other Christian churches and groups summed up to 1% of the population.[^o228][^o231] Since the [reunification of Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reunification_of_Germany), the number of non-religious people has grown and an estimated 46.2% of the country's population [are not affiliated with any church or religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion_in_Germany).[^o228][^o229][^o231][^o230]

The other religions make up to less than 1% of the population.[^o231] [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) has around 200,000 adherents (0.2%), [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism) has around 200,000 adherents (0.2%), [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) 90,000 (0.1%), [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism) 75,000 (0.1%) and [Yazidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazidis) religion (45,000–60,000).[^o234] All other religious communities in Germany have fewer than 50,000 (\<0.1%) adherents.

[Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism) is concentrated in the north and east and [Roman Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholicism_in_Germany) is concentrated in the south and west. According to the last nationwide census, Protestantism is more widespread among the population with [German citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_citizenship); there are slightly more Catholics total because of the Catholic immigrant population (including such groups as [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poles_in_Germany) and [Italians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italians_in_Germany)).[^o236] The former [Pope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope), [Benedict XVI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedict_XVI), was born in [Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavaria). [Non-religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion) people, including [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism) and [agnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnosticism) are especially numerous in the former [East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Democratic_Republic) and major metropolitan areas.[^o237]

Of the roughly 4 million [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim), most are [Sunnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam) and [Alevites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alevites) from [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey), but there are a small number of [Shi'ites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shia_Islam) and other denominations.[^o238][^o239] 1.5% of the country's overall population declare themselves [Orthodox Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church),[^o228] with [Serbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbs_in_Germany), [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks_in_Germany), [Romanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanians_in_Germany), [Ukrainians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainians_in_Germany) and [Russians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russians_in_Germany) being the most numerous.[^o240] Germany has Europe's third-largest [Jewish population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Germany) (after France and the United Kingdom).[^o241] In 2004, twice as many Jews from former [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) republics settled in Germany as in [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel), bringing the total Jewish population to more than 200,000, compared to 30,000 prior to [German reunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification). Large cities with significant Jewish populations include Berlin, [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt) and [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich).[^o242] Around 250,000 active [Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) live in Germany; 50% of them are Asian immigrants.[^o243]

## Languages {#Languages}

[German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) is the only official and most widely spoken language. [Standard German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_German) is understood throughout the country.

### Microcensus data {#Microcensus_data}

Since 2017, the [Federal Statistical Office of Germany](wikipedia://en/7516079) included a new question regarding the language spoken at home in the micro-census, a large sample survey that yearly covers approximately 1% of the German population living in private households.[^o248] In recent years, there has been a noticeable decline in the number and proportion of people in Germany who speak only German at home, accompanied by a corresponding increase in the use of other languages or multilingual households. This can be attributed to the increasing size of the Germans with a migration background and the decreasing size of the population without a migration background.[^o249]

### Minority languages {#Minority_languages}

*Image: Róžant – znački.jpg.* Bilingual German–Sorbian city limit signs

[Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language), [Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German), [Low Rhenish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Rhenish), the [Sorbian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorbian_languages) ([Lower Sorbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Sorbian_language) and [Upper Sorbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Sorbian_language)), and the two [Frisian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisian_languages), [Saterfrisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saterland_Frisian_language) and [North Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Frisian_language), are officially recognized and protected as minority languages by the [European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Charter_for_Regional_or_Minority_Languages) in their respective regions. With speakers of [Romany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_language) living in all parts of Germany, the federal government has promised to take action to protect the language. Until now, only [Hesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesse) has followed Berlin's announcement, and agreed on implementing concrete measures to support Romany speakers.

Germany is a strong supporter of the Charter, as evidenced by things like the 2022 report from the Secretary General of the Council of Europe specifically naming Germany as an example of how to incorporate minority languages in State media.[^o283][^o284] Still, Germany—like many other signatories of the Charter—has room for improvement in its implementation of the Charter.[^o285]

### High German dialects {#High_German_dialects}

*Image: Ortsschild Emlichheim - Emmelkamp.jpg.* City limits sign; this city is called *Emlichheim* in [High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_German) and *Emmelkamp* in [Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German).

[German dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_dialects) – some quite distinct from the standard language – are used in everyday speech, especially in rural regions. Many dialects, for example the [Upper German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_German) varieties, are to some degree cultivated as symbols of regional identity and have their own literature, theaters and some TV programming. While speaking a dialect outside its native region might be frowned upon, in their native regions some dialects can be spoken by all social classes. Nevertheless, partly due to the prevalence of Standard German in media, the use of dialects has declined over the past century, especially in the younger population.

The social status of different German dialects can vary greatly. The Alemannic and Bavarian dialects of the south are positively valued by their speakers and can be used in almost all social circumstances. The Saxonian and Thuringian dialects have less prestige and are subject to derision. While Bavarian and Alemannic have kept much of their distinctiveness, the Middle German dialects, which are closer to Standard German, have lost some of their distinctive lexical and grammatical features and tend to be only pronunciation variants of Standard German.

### Low Saxon dialects {#Low_Saxon_dialects}

Although [Low Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German) is officially recognized as a language on its own, little official action has been taken to foster the language. Historically one third of Germany's territory and population was Low Saxon speaking. No data has ever been collected on the actual number of speakers, but today the number of speakers is around 5 million. Despite this relatively high number of speakers there is very little coverage in the media (mostly on [NDR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norddeutscher_Rundfunk) TV, no regular programming) and very little education in or on the language. The language is not fixed as part of the school curriculum, and Low Saxon is used as a medium of instruction in only one school in the whole of Germany (as a "model project" in primary school alongside education in Standard German). As a consequence the younger generation refused to adopt the native language of their parents. Language prevalence dropped from more than 90% (depending on the exact region) in the 1930s to less than 5% today. This accounts for a massive intergenerational gap in language use. Older people regularly use the language and take private initiatives to maintain the language, but the lack of innovative potential of the younger generation hinders language maintenance. The language also has its own literature (around 150 published books every year) and there are many theatres (mostly amateur dramatics, but some professional ones, such as [Ohnsorg-Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohnsorg-Theater)).

Use of Low Saxon is mainly restricted to use among acquaintances, such as family members, neighbours and friends. A meeting of a village council can be held almost completely in Low Saxon if all participants know each other (as long as written protocols are written in Standard German), but a single foreigner can make the whole meeting switch to Standard German.

The Low Saxon dialects differ in their status too. There is a north–south gradient in language maintenance. The southern dialects of Westfalian, Eastfalian and Brandenburgish have had much stronger speaker losses than the northern coastal dialects of Northern Low Saxon. While Eastfalian has lost speakers to Standard German, Westfalian has lost speakers both to Standard German and to the Standard German-based [regiolect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regiolect) of the Rhine-Ruhr area. Brandenburgish speakers mostly switched to the Standard German-based regiolect of Berlin. Brandenburgish has been almost completely replaced by the Berlin regiolect. Northern Low Saxon speakers switched mostly to pure Standard German.

### Foreign languages {#Foreign_languages}

English is the most common foreign language and is almost universally taught at the secondary level; it is also taught at elementary level in some states. Other commonly-taught languages are French, [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language), Spanish and [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language). [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) is taught in states bordering the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands), and [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language) in the eastern states bordering [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland).[^o298] [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) and [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) are part of the [classical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics) syllabus offered in many secondary schools.

According to a 2004 survey, two-thirds of Germany's citizens have at least basic knowledge of English. About 20% consider themselves to be competent speakers of French, followed by speakers of Russian (7%), Italian (6.1%), and Spanish (5.6%). The relatively high number of Russian speakers is a result of immigration from the former Soviet Union to Germany for almost 10 consecutive years, plus its having been learned in school by many older former East Germans as compulsory first foreign language.
