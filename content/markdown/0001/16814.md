# Kepler–Poinsot polyhedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kepler–Poinsot polyhedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **Kepler–Poinsot polyhedron** is any of four [regular](wikipedia://en/333299) [star polyhedra](wikipedia://en/7087334).[^o2]

:::

They may be obtained by [stellating](wikipedia://en/180253) and [faceting](wikipedia://en/8613537) the regular [convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) and [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084), and differ from these in having regular [pentagrammic](wikipedia://en/197544) [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) or [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719). They can all be seen as three-dimensional analogues of the pentagram in one way or another.

## Characteristics {#Characteristics}

The Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra are the regular [star polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_polyhedra), obtained by extending both [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968) and [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361), an operation named [stellation](wikipedia://en/180253). This operation results in four different polyhedra:

- [Great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441): constructed from attaching twelve [pentagonal pyramids](wikipedia://en/1128461) (with [regular polygonal](wikipedia://en/333306) faces) onto the face of a regular dodecahedron, and attached again with thirty [wedges](wikipedia://en/2951640). However, this can be constructed alternatively by [removing its polygonal faces without changing or creating new vertices](wikipedia://en/8613537) of a regular icosahedron.
- [Small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843): attaching twelve pentagonal pyramids onto a regular dodecahedron's faces. [Topologically](wikipedia://en/29954), this shares the same surface as the [pentakis dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/723196).
- [Great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898); and
- [Great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878): constructed from a great dodecahedron with twenty asymmetric triangular bipyramids, attaching to the hollow between the wedges.[^n1]

*Image: Relationship among regular star polyhedra (direction colors).png.* Conway's system of relations between the six polyhedra (ordered vertically by [density](wikipedia://en/34257549)). solid lime 2px solid #0065CC 2px solid orange 2px

[John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) introduces operators for the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra known as *greatenings*—($g$), maintaining the type of faces, shifting and resizing them into parallel planes—and *stellations*—($s$), changing pentagonal faces into pentagrams—of the convex solids. In his [naming convention](wikipedia://en/180253#Naming_stellations), the small stellated dodecahedron is just the *stellated dodecahedron*.

By the construction above, these figures have [pentagrams](wikipedia://en/197544) (star pentagons) as faces or vertex figures. The [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) of a great dodecahedron is the small stellated dodecahedron, and the dual of a great icosahedron is the great stellated dodecahedron. The four share the symmetry as both regular icosahedron and regular dodecahedron, the [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844).

### Euler characteristic {#Euler_characteristic}

A Kepler–Poinsot polyhedron covers its circumscribed sphere more than once, with the centers of faces acting as winding points in the figures which have pentagrammic faces, and the vertices in the others. Because of this, they are not necessarily topologically equivalent to the sphere as Platonic solids are, and in particular, the [Euler relation](wikipedia://en/148420)

$$\chi=V-E+F=2\$$

does not always hold. Schläfli held that all polyhedra must have $\chi = 2$, and he rejected the small stellated dodecahedron and great dodecahedron as proper polyhedra. This view was never widely held.[^o6]

A modified form of Euler's formula, using [density](wikipedia://en/34257549) ($D$) of the [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719) ($d_{v}$) and faces ($d_{f}$) was given by [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034), and holds both for convex polyhedra (where the correction factors are all 1), and the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra:

$$d_v V - E + d_f F = 2D,$$

and by this calculation, the density of the great icosahedron and the great stellated dodecahedron are 7, whereas the great dodecahedron and the small stellated dodecahedron are 3.[^n2]

### Duality and Petrie polygons {#Duality_and_Petrie_polygons}

The Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra exist in [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) pairs. Duals have the same [Petrie polygon](wikipedia://en/18571869), or more precisely, Petrie polygons with the same two-dimensional projection.

## History {#History}

Most, if not all, of the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra were known of in some form or other before Kepler. A small stellated dodecahedron appears in a marble tarsia (inlay panel) on the floor of [St. Mark's Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Mark's_Basilica), [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice), Italy. It dates from the 15th century and is sometimes attributed to [Paolo Uccello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Uccello).

In his *[Perspectiva corporum regularium](wikipedia://en/65182964)*, a book of woodcuts published in 1568, [Wenzel Jamnitzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenzel_Jamnitzer) depicts the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878) and a [great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441). It is clear from the general arrangement of the book that he regarded only the five Platonic solids as regular.

The small and great stellated dodecahedra, sometimes called the **Kepler polyhedra**, were first recognized as regular by [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) around 1619. He obtained them by [stellating](wikipedia://en/180253) the regular convex dodecahedron, for the first time treating it as a surface rather than a solid. He noticed that by extending the edges or faces of the convex dodecahedron until they met again, he could obtain star pentagons. Further, he recognized that these star pentagons are also regular. In this way, he constructed the two stellated dodecahedra. Each has the central convex region of each face "hidden" within the interior, with only the triangular arms visible. Kepler's final step was to recognize that these polyhedra fit the definition of regularity, even though they were not [convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron), as the traditional [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) were.

In 1809, [Louis Poinsot](wikipedia://en/764639) rediscovered Kepler's figures by assembling star pentagons around each vertex. He also assembled convex polygons around star vertices to discover two more regular stars, the great icosahedron and great dodecahedron. Some people call these two the **Poinsot polyhedra**. Poinsot did not know if he had discovered all the regular star polyhedra. Three years later, [Augustin Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Cauchy) proved the list complete by [stellating](wikipedia://en/180253) the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905), and almost half a century after that, in 1858, [Bertrand](wikipedia://en/488196) provided a more elegant proof by [faceting](wikipedia://en/8613537) them. The following year, [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) gave the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra the names by which they are generally known today. A hundred years later, [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) developed a [systematic terminology](wikipedia://en/180253#Naming_stellations) for stellations in up to four dimensions. Within this scheme, the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843) is just the *stellated dodecahedron*.

Artist [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127)'s interest in geometric forms often led to works based on or including regular solids; *[Gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation_%28M._C._Escher%29)* is based on a small stellated dodecahedron. A [dissection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissection_%28geometry%29) of the great dodecahedron was used for the 1980s puzzle [Alexander's Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander's_Star). Norwegian artist [Vebjørn Sand's sculpture *The Kepler Star*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vebjørn_Sand#Kepler_Star) is displayed near [Oslo Airport, Gardermoen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oslo_Airport,_Gardermoen). The star spans 14 meters and consists of both a regular icosahedron and a regular dodecahedron inside a great stellated dodecahedron.
