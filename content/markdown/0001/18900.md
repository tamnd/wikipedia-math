# Modus ponens

Forward reasoning

la

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Modus ponens"}

**Modus ponens.**

$P$ implies $Q$. $P$ is true. Therefore, $Q$ must also be true.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Modus ponens"}

In [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus), **modus ponens** (; **MP**), also known as **modus ponendo ponens** (la),[^o3] **conditional elimination**, **implication elimination**, or **affirming the antecedent**,[^o4] is a [deductive](wikipedia://en/61093) [argument form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_form) and [rule of inference](wikipedia://en/252311).[^o5] It can be summarized as "$P$ [implies](wikipedia://en/658808) $Q$. $P$ is true. Therefore, $Q$ must also be true."

:::

*Modus ponens* is a mixed [hypothetical syllogism](wikipedia://en/177633) and is closely related to another [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) form of argument, *[modus tollens](wikipedia://en/18901)*. Both have apparently similar but invalid forms: [affirming the consequent](wikipedia://en/675) and [denying the antecedent](wikipedia://en/144793). [Constructive dilemma](wikipedia://en/1316648) is the [disjunctive](wikipedia://en/7962) version of *modus ponens*.

The history of *modus ponens* goes back to [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity).[^o8] The first to explicitly describe the argument form *modus ponens* was [Theophrastus](wikipedia://en/98409).[^o6] It, along with *[modus tollens](wikipedia://en/18901)*, is one of the standard patterns of inference that can be applied to derive chains of conclusions that lead to the desired goal.

## Explanation {#Explanation}

The form of a *modus ponens* argument is a mixed [hypothetical syllogism](wikipedia://en/177633), with two premises and a conclusion:[^o8]

1. If $P$, then $Q$.
1. $$P.$$
1. Therefore, $Q$.

The first (major) premise is a [conditional](wikipedia://en/658808) ("if–then") claim, namely that $P$ implies $Q$. The second (minor) premise is an assertion that $P$, the [antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755) of the conditional claim, is the case. From these two premises it can be logically concluded that $Q$, the [consequent](wikipedia://en/804778) of the conditional claim, must be the case as well.

An example of an argument that fits the form *modus ponens*:

1. If today is Tuesday, then John will go to work.
1. Today is Tuesday.
1. Therefore, John will go to work.

This argument is [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742), but this has no bearing on whether any of the statements in the argument are actually [true](wikipedia://en/29920); for *modus ponens* to be a [sound](wikipedia://en/26819) argument, the premises must be true for any true instances of the conclusion. An [argument](wikipedia://en/13018310) can be valid but nonetheless unsound if one or more premises are false; if an argument is valid *and* all the premises are true, then the argument is sound. For example, John might be going to work on Wednesday. In this case, the reasoning for John's going to work (because it is Tuesday) is unsound. The argument is only sound on Tuesdays (when John goes to work), but valid on every day of the week. A [propositional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) argument using *modus ponens* is said to be [deductive](wikipedia://en/61093).[^o9]

Also, for example, for understanding:

1. $$(x)(Px \supset Qx)$$
1. $$Pa$$
1. $$Qa$$

The first statement represents a general rule that holds for every object $x$. The second specifies that a particular object $a$ possesses property $P$. From these two premises, we can logically infer the third statement, namely that object $a$ also possesses property $Q$.[^o10]

In single-conclusion [sequent calculi](wikipedia://en/252329), *modus ponens* is the Cut rule. The [cut-elimination theorem](wikipedia://en/910234) for a calculus says that every proof involving Cut can be transformed (generally, by a constructive method) into a proof without Cut, and hence that Cut is [admissible](wikipedia://en/1151794).[^o11]

The [Curry–Howard correspondence](wikipedia://en/254299) between proofs and programs relates *modus ponens* to [function application](wikipedia://en/20576545): if *f* is a function of type $P$ → $Q$ and $x$ is of type $P$, then *f x* is of type $Q$.[^o12]

In [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence), *modus ponens* is often called [forward chaining](wikipedia://en/568962).[^o13]

The *modus ponens* rule may be written in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation as

$$P \to Q,\; P\;\; \vdash\;\; Q$$

where $P$, $Q$ and $P$ → $Q$ are statements (or propositions) in a formal language and [⊢](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/⊢) is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) symbol meaning that $Q$ is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) of $P$ and $P$ → $Q$ in some [logical system](wikipedia://en/396102).

In classical two-valued logic, *modus ponens* can be interpreted from the [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808) (implication) operator. The notion of implication as a rigorous mathematical construct can be traced back to around the turn of the 19th century with early works of mathematical logic, such as [Begriffsschrift](wikipedia://en/900125) and [Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133). Given two variables $p$ and $q$ that can either be true or false, implication ($p$ → $q$) is defined as all cases being true except where $p$ is true and $q$ is false.[^o16] By the assumptions of *modus ponens*, $p$ → $q$ and $p$ are true, and by the definition of implication, it can be deduced that $q$ must also be true.

## Status {#Status}

While *modus ponens* is one of the most commonly used [argument forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_form) in logic, it must not be mistaken for a logical law; rather, it is one of the accepted mechanisms for the construction of deductive proofs that includes the "rule of definition" and the "rule of substitution".[^o18] *Modus ponens* allows one to eliminate a [conditional statement](wikipedia://en/658808) from a [logical proof or argument](wikipedia://en/1197184) (the antecedents) and thereby not carry these antecedents forward in an ever-lengthening string of symbols; for this reason modus ponens is sometimes called the **rule of detachment**[^o19] or the **law of detachment**.[^o20] Enderton, for example, observes that "modus ponens can produce shorter formulas from longer ones",[^o21] and Russell observes that "the process of the inference cannot be reduced to symbols. Its sole record is the occurrence of ⊦q \[the consequent\] ... an inference is the dropping of a true premise; it is the dissolution of an implication".[^o22]

A justification for the "trust in inference is the belief that if the two former assertions \[the antecedents\] are not in error, the final assertion \[the consequent\] is not in error".[^o22] In other words: if one [statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) or [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) [implies](wikipedia://en/658808) a second one, and the first statement or proposition is true, then the second one is also true. If $P$ implies $Q$ and $P$ is true, then $Q$ is true.[^o23]

## Correspondence to other mathematical frameworks {#Correspondence_to_other_mathematical_frameworks}

### Algebraic semantics {#Algebraic_semantics}

In mathematical logic, [algebraic semantics](wikipedia://en/2848519) treats every sentence as a name for an element in an ordered set. Typically, the set can be visualized as a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304)-like structure with a single element (the "always-true") at the top and another single element (the "always-false") at the bottom. Logical equivalence becomes identity, so that when $\neg{(P \wedge Q)}$ and $\neg{P} \vee \neg{Q}$, for instance, are equivalent (as is standard), then $\neg{(P \wedge Q)} = \neg{P} \vee \neg{Q}$. Logical implication becomes a matter of relative position: $P$ logically implies $Q$ just in case $P \leq Q$, i.e., when either $P = Q$ or else $P$ lies below $Q$ and is connected to it by an upward path.

In this context, to say that $P$ and $P \rightarrow Q$ together imply $Q$—that is, to affirm *modus ponens* as valid—is to say that the highest point which lies below both $P$ and $P \rightarrow Q$ lies below $Q$, i.e., that $P \wedge (P \rightarrow Q) \leq Q$. In the semantics for basic propositional logic, the algebra is [Boolean](wikipedia://en/3959), with $\rightarrow$ construed as the [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808): $P \rightarrow Q = \neg{P} \vee Q$. Confirming that $P \wedge (P \rightarrow Q) \leq Q$ is then straightforward, because $P \wedge (P \rightarrow Q) = P \wedge Q$ and $P \wedge Q \leq Q$. With other treatments of $\rightarrow$, the semantics becomes more complex, the algebra may be non-Boolean, and the validity of modus ponens cannot be taken for granted.

### Probability calculus {#Probability_calculus}

If $\Pr(P \rightarrow Q) = x$ and $\Pr(P) = y$, then $\Pr(Q)$ must lie in the interval $[x + y - 1, x]$.[^o27] For the special case $x = y = 1$, $\Pr(Q)$ must equal $1$.

### Subjective logic {#Subjective_logic}

*Modus ponens* represents an instance of the binomial deduction operator in [subjective logic](wikipedia://en/12413580) expressed as:

$$\omega^{A}_{Q\|P} = (\omega^{A}_{Q|P},\omega^{A}_{Q|\lnot P})\circledcirc \omega^{A}_{P}\,,$$

where $\omega^{A}_{P}$ denotes the subjective opinion about $P$ as expressed by source $A$, and the conditional opinion $\omega^{A}_{Q|P}$ generalizes the logical implication $P \to Q$. The deduced marginal opinion about $Q$ is denoted by $\omega^{A}_{Q\|P}$. The case where $\omega^{A}_{P}$ is an absolute TRUE opinion about $P$ is equivalent to source $A$ saying that $P$ is TRUE, and the case where $\omega^{A}_{P}$ is an absolute FALSE opinion about $P$ is equivalent to source $A$ saying that $P$ is FALSE. The deduction operator $\circledcirc$ of [subjective logic](wikipedia://en/12413580) produces an absolute TRUE deduced opinion $\omega^{A}_{Q\|P}$ when the conditional opinion $\omega^{A}_{Q|P}$ is absolute TRUE and the antecedent opinion $\omega^{A}_{P}$ is absolute TRUE. Hence, subjective logic deduction represents a generalization of both *modus ponens* and the [Law of total probability](wikipedia://en/312383).[^o29]

## Alleged cases of failure {#Alleged_cases_of_failure}

Philosophers and linguists have identified a variety of cases where *modus ponens* appears to fail. [Vann McGee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vann_McGee), for instance, argued that *modus ponens* can fail for conditionals whose consequents are themselves conditionals.[^o31] The following is an example:

1. Either [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare) or [Hobbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hobbes) wrote *[Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamlet)*.
1. If either Shakespeare or Hobbes wrote *Hamlet*, then if Shakespeare did not do it, Hobbes did.
1. Therefore, if Shakespeare did not write *Hamlet*, Hobbes did it.

Since Shakespeare did write *Hamlet*, the first premise is true. The second premise is also true, since starting with a set of possible authors limited to just Shakespeare and Hobbes and eliminating one of them leaves only the other. However, the conclusion is doubtful, since ruling out Shakespeare as the author of *Hamlet* would leave numerous possible candidates, many of them more plausible alternatives than Hobbes (if the if-thens in the inference are read as material conditionals, the conclusion comes out true simply by virtue of the false antecedent. This is one of the [paradoxes of material implication](wikipedia://en/12857474)).

The general form of McGee-type counterexamples to *modus ponens* is simply $P, P \rightarrow (Q \rightarrow R)$, therefore, $Q \rightarrow R$; however, it is not essential that $P$ be a disjunction, as in the example given. That these kinds of cases constitute failures of *modus ponens* remains a controversial view among logicians, but opinions vary on how the cases should be disposed of, and some authors have rejected McGee's argument.[^o32][^o33][^o34]

In [deontic logic](wikipedia://en/1343980), some examples of conditional obligation also raise the possibility of *modus ponens* failure. These are cases where the conditional premise describes an obligation predicated on an immoral or imprudent action, e.g., "If Doe murders his mother, he ought to do so gently," for which the dubious unconditional conclusion would be "Doe ought to gently murder his mother."[^o35] It would appear to follow that if Doe is in fact gently murdering his mother, then by *modus ponens* it appears that he is doing exactly what he should, unconditionally, be doing. Here again, *modus ponens**failure is not a popular diagnosis and is criticized by some authors, but is sometimes argued for.[^o36]***

## Possible fallacies {#Possible_fallacies}

The fallacy of [affirming the consequent](wikipedia://en/675) (inferring $P$ from if $P$, then $Q$ and $Q$) is a common misinterpretation of the *modus ponens*.[^o38]
