# Multivariate statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multivariate statistics"}

**Multivariate statistics** is a subdivision of [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) encompassing the simultaneous observation and analysis of more than one [outcome variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcome_variable), i.e., *[multivariate random variables](wikipedia://en/49821)*. Multivariate statistics concerns understanding the different aims and background of each of the different forms of multivariate analysis, and how they relate to each other. The practical application of multivariate statistics to a particular problem may involve several types of univariate and multivariate analyses in order to understand the relationships between variables and their relevance to the problem being studied.

:::

In addition, multivariate statistics is concerned with [multivariate probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_probability_distribution), in terms of both

- how these can be used to represent the distributions of observed data;
- how they can be used as part of [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577), particularly where several different quantities are of interest to the same analysis.

Certain types of problems involving multivariate data, for example [simple linear regression](wikipedia://en/2593771) and [multiple regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_regression), are *not* usually considered to be special cases of multivariate statistics because the analysis is dealt with by considering the (univariate) conditional distribution of a single outcome variable given the other variables.

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Multivariate Analysis"}

## Multivariate analysis {#Multivariate_analysis}

**Multivariate analysis** (**MVA**) is based on the principles of multivariate statistics. Typically, MVA is used to address situations where multiple measurements are made on each experimental unit and the relations among these measurements and their structures are important.[^o4] A modern, overlapping categorization of MVA includes:[^o4]

- Normal and general multivariate models and [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29)
- The study and measurement of relationships
- [Probability](wikipedia://en/22934) computations of [multidimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) regions
- The exploration of [data structures](wikipedia://en/8519) and [patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern)

Multivariate analysis can be complicated by the desire to include physics-based analysis to calculate the effects of variables for a hierarchical "system-of-systems". Often, studies that wish to use multivariate analysis are stalled by the dimensionality of the problem. These concerns are often eased through the use of [surrogate models](wikipedia://en/6500531), highly accurate approximations of the physics-based code. Since surrogate models take the form of an equation, they can be evaluated very quickly. This becomes an enabler for large-scale MVA studies: while a [Monte Carlo simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_simulation) across the design space is difficult with physics-based codes, it becomes trivial when evaluating surrogate models, which often take the form of [response-surface](wikipedia://en/4481194) equations.

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Redundancy analysis"}

### Types of analysis {#Types_of_analysis}

Many different models are used in MVA, each with its own type of analysis:

1. [Multivariate analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/682847) (MANOVA) extends the [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634) to cover cases where there is more than one dependent variable to be analyzed simultaneously; see also [Multivariate analysis of covariance](wikipedia://en/16849908) (MANCOVA).
1. Multivariate regression attempts to determine a formula that can describe how elements in a [vector](wikipedia://en/32370) of variables respond simultaneously to changes in others. For linear relations, regression analyses here are based on forms of the [general linear model](wikipedia://en/877698). Some suggest that multivariate regression is distinct from multivariable regression, however, that is debated and not consistently true across scientific fields.[^o7]
1. [Principal components analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_components_analysis) (PCA) creates a new set of [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221) variables that contain the same information as the original set. It rotates the axes of variation to give a new set of orthogonal axes, ordered so that they summarize decreasing proportions of the variation.
1. [Factor analysis](wikipedia://en/253492) is similar to PCA but allows the user to extract a specified number of synthetic variables, fewer than the original set, leaving the remaining unexplained variation as error. The extracted variables are known as latent variables or factors; each one may be supposed to account for covariation in a group of observed variables.
1. [Canonical correlation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_correlation_analysis) finds linear relationships among two sets of variables; it is the generalised (i.e. canonical) version of bivariate[^o8] correlation.
1. Redundancy analysis[^o9] (RDA) is similar to canonical correlation analysis but allows the user to derive a specified number of synthetic variables from one set of (independent) variables that explain as much variance as possible in another (independent) set. It is a multivariate analogue of [regression](wikipedia://en/826997).[^o10]
1. [Correspondence analysis](wikipedia://en/11594341) (CA), or reciprocal averaging, finds (like PCA) a set of synthetic variables that summarise the original set. The underlying model assumes chi-squared dissimilarities among records (cases).
1. [Canonical (or "constrained") correspondence analysis](wikipedia://en/40892755) (CCA) for summarising the joint variation in two sets of variables (like redundancy analysis); combination of correspondence analysis and multivariate regression analysis. The underlying model assumes chi-squared dissimilarities among records (cases).
1. [Multidimensional scaling](wikipedia://en/398786) comprises various algorithms to determine a set of synthetic variables that best represent the pairwise distances between records. The original method is [principal coordinates analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_coordinates_analysis) (PCoA; based on PCA).
1. [Discriminant analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminant_function), or canonical variate analysis, attempts to establish whether a set of variables can be used to distinguish between two or more groups of cases.
1. [Linear discriminant analysis](wikipedia://en/1470657) (LDA) computes a linear predictor from two sets of normally distributed data to allow for classification of new observations.
1. [Clustering systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster_Analysis) assign objects into groups (called clusters) so that objects (cases) from the same cluster are more similar to each other than objects from different clusters.
1. [Recursive partitioning](wikipedia://en/11845980) creates a decision tree that attempts to correctly classify members of the population based on a dichotomous dependent variable.
1. [Artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_networks) extend regression and clustering methods to non-linear multivariate models.
1. [Statistical graphics](wikipedia://en/15934463) such as tours, [parallel coordinate plots](wikipedia://en/1254148), scatterplot matrices can be used to explore multivariate data.
1. [Simultaneous equations models](wikipedia://en/250436) involve more than one regression equation, with different dependent variables, estimated together.
1. [Vector autoregression](wikipedia://en/2399016) involves simultaneous regressions of various [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) variables on their own and each other's lagged values.
1. [Principal response curves](wikipedia://en/53614260) analysis (PRC) is a method based on RDA that allows the user to focus on treatment effects over time by correcting for changes in control treatments over time.[^o11]
1. [Iconography of correlations](wikipedia://en/67646381) consists in replacing a correlation matrix by a diagram where the "remarkable" correlations are represented by a solid line (positive correlation), or a dotted line (negative correlation).

:::

### Dealing with incomplete data {#Dealing_with_incomplete_data}

It is very common that in an experimentally acquired set of data the values of some components of a given data point are [missing](wikipedia://en/7859676). Rather than discarding the whole data point, it is common to "fill in" values for the missing components, a process called "[imputation](wikipedia://en/1309220)".[^o13]

:::

## Important probability distributions {#Important_probability_distributions}

There is a set of [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) used in multivariate analyses that play a similar role to the corresponding set of distributions that are used in [univariate analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univariate_analysis) when the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) is appropriate to a dataset. These multivariate distributions are:

- [Multivariate normal distribution](wikipedia://en/50347)
- [Wishart distribution](wikipedia://en/364996)
- [Multivariate Student-t distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_Student_distribution).

The [Inverse-Wishart distribution](wikipedia://en/8143463) is important in [Bayesian inference](wikipedia://en/49571), for example in [Bayesian multivariate linear regression](wikipedia://en/7522539). Additionally, [Hotelling's T-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/934711) is a multivariate distribution, generalising [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375), that is used in multivariate [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing).

## History {#History}

[C.R. Rao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.R._Rao) made significant contributions to multivariate statistical theory throughout his career, particularly in the mid-20th century. One of his key works is the book titled "Advanced Statistical Methods in Biometric Research," published in 1952. This work laid the foundation for many concepts in multivariate statistics.[^o16] Anderson's 1958 textbook,*An Introduction to Multivariate Statistical Analysis*,[^o17] educated a generation of theorists and applied statisticians; Anderson's book emphasizes [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing) via [likelihood ratio tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood_ratio_test) and the properties of [power functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power): [admissibility](wikipedia://en/1776726), [unbiasedness](wikipedia://en/8450479) and [monotonicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity).[^o18][^o19]

MVA was formerly discussed solely in the context of statistical theories, due to the size and complexity of underlying datasets and its high computational consumption. With the dramatic growth of computational power, MVA now plays an increasingly important role in data analysis and has wide application in [Omics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omics) fields.
