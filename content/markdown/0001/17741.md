# Ludwig Wittgenstein

::: {#o7 .subject cue=lead name="Ludwig Wittgenstein"}

**Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein** ( ;[^o8] ; 26 April 1889 – 29 April 1951) was an Austro-British [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) who worked in [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439), [philosophy of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind), and [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language).

:::

Wittgenstein was born in Vienna into one of Europe's richest families. From 1911 to 1913, he studied at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) under [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) and interacted closely with [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes) and [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore), among others. During this time, he inherited a fortune, part of which he donated to various poets and artists. He also began what would become his first important philosophical work. However, at the start of World War I, he left academia to enlist in the [Austro-Hungarian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Army). After a series of personal adversities—including the death of his older brother by suicide (the third of his older brothers to die by suicide during his lifetime), the death of his close friend and lover [David Pinsent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pinsent), and his imprisonment in an Italian [prisoner of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner_of_war) camp—he became depressed, and he gave away his remaining fortune to his siblings. He worked as a schoolteacher around Vienna. Despite these setbacks, his philosophical work was published in the *[Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus](wikipedia://en/30062)* in 1921, followed by an English translation in 1922 with help from [Frank P. Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285), to considerable discussion and acclaim.

At the encouragement of Russell, Keynes, and Ramsey, Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge, where he taught from 1930 to 1947, giving lectures on philosophy, often permeated by his original research. During World War II, he left academia again to work at the [Guy's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy's_Hospital) in London and at the [Royal Victoria Infirmary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Victoria_Infirmary) in [Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne). Wittgenstein would not publish another philosophical work during his lifetime, but following his death in 1951, his voluminous manuscripts were edited and published posthumously. The first and best-known of this posthumous series is the 1953 book *[Philosophical Investigations](wikipedia://en/23674)*.

Wittgenstein's philosophy is often divided into an early period, exemplified by the *Tractatus*, and a later period, articulated primarily in the *Philosophical Investigations*. The [early Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Wittgenstein) was concerned with the logical relationship between [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094) and the world, and he believed that by providing an account of the logic underlying this relationship, he had solved all philosophical problems. The [later Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later_Wittgenstein) rejected many of the assumptions of the *Tractatus*, arguing that the [meaning](wikipedia://en/4102640) of words is best understood as their use within a given [language game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_game_%28philosophy%29).

Wittgenstein was well regarded among academics during his lifetime, and was [decorated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_awards_and_decorations) for his courage during World War I. However, he encountered controversy during his time teaching for his use of violent corporal punishment on girls and boys (see the [Haidbauer incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haidbauer_incident)). His work (both early and later) has been described as controversial and sparked a number of different reactions. Later philosophers and commentators have generally agreed that he is one of the most important figures in [20th-century philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th-century_philosophy).

## Background {#Background}

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Moses Maier"}

### The Wittgensteins {#The_Wittgensteins}

*Image: Karl Wittgenstein.jpg.* [Karl Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Wittgenstein) was one of the richest men in Europe.[^o17]

According to a family tree prepared in Jerusalem after World War II, Wittgenstein's paternal great-great-grandfather was Moses Meier,[^o12] an [Ashkenazi Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jewish) land agent who lived with his wife, Brendel Simon, in [Bad Laasphe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Laasphe) in the [Principality of Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegen-Wittgenstein), [Westphalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westphalia).[^o13] In July 1808, [Napoleon](wikipedia://en/69880) issued a decree that everyone, including Jews, must adopt an inheritable family surname, so Meier's son, also Moses, took the name of his employers, the [Sayn-Wittgensteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayn-Wittgenstein), and became Moses Meier Wittgenstein.[^n1] His son, Hermann Christian Wittgenstein — who took the middle name "Christian" to distance himself from his Jewish background — married Fanny Figdor, also Jewish, who converted to Protestantism just before they married, and the couple founded a successful business trading in wool in [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig).[^n2] Ludwig's grandmother Fanny was a first cousin of the violinist [Joseph Joachim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Joachim).[^n3]

They had 11 children – among them Wittgenstein's father. [Karl Otto Clemens Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Otto_Clemens_Wittgenstein) (1847–1913) became an industrial tycoon, and by the late 1880s was one of the richest men in Europe, with an effective monopoly on Austria's steel cartel.[^o17][^n4] Thanks to Karl, the Wittgensteins became the second wealthiest family in the [Austro-Hungarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Empire), only the [Rothschilds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rothschild_family) being wealthier.[^n4] Karl Wittgenstein was viewed as the Austrian equivalent of [Andrew Carnegie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Carnegie), with whom he was friends, and was one of the wealthiest men in the world by the 1890s.[^o17] As a result of his decision in 1898 to invest substantially in the Netherlands and in Switzerland as well as overseas, particularly in the US, the family was to an extent shielded from the [hyperinflation that hit Austria in 1922](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation#Austria).[^n5] However, their wealth diminished due to post-1918 hyperinflation and subsequently during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression), although even as late as 1938 they owned 13 mansions in Vienna alone.[^n6]

### Early life {#Early_life}

*Image: Palais Wittgenstein 03.jpg.* Palais Wittgenstein, the family home, around 1910

Wittgenstein was ethnically [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish).[^o23][^o24][^o25][^o26][^n7] His mother was Leopoldine Maria Josefa Kalmus, known among friends as "Poldi". Her father was a [Bohemian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechs) Jew, and her mother was an Austrian-[Slovene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenes) Catholic—she was Wittgenstein's only non-Jewish grandparent. Poldi was an aunt of the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize) laureate [Friedrich Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hayek) on his maternal side. Wittgenstein was born at 8:30 pm on 26 April 1889 in the "Villa Wittgenstein" at what became Neuwaldegger Straße 38 in the suburban parish Neuwaldegg outside Vienna.[^o28][^o29][^o30]

*Image: 1. The infant Ludwig Wittgenstein.jpg.* Ludwig, c. 1890s

Karl and Poldi had nine children in all—four girls: Hermine, [Margaret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Stonborough-Wittgenstein) (Gretl), Helene, and a fourth daughter Dora who died as a baby; and five boys: Johannes (Hans), Kurt, Rudolf (Rudi), [Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Wittgenstein)—who became a concert pianist despite losing an arm in World War I—and Ludwig, who was the youngest of the family.[^n8]

*Image: Ludwig as a child.jpg.* Ludwig sitting in a field as a child

Wittgenstein and his siblings were brought up in the Roman Catholic faith of their mother.[^o80] The family was at the centre of Vienna's cultural life; [Bruno Walter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruno_Walter) described the life at the Wittgensteins' palace as an "all-pervading atmosphere of humanity and culture".[^n9] Karl was a leading patron of the arts, commissioning works by [Auguste Rodin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Rodin) and financing the city's exhibition hall and art gallery, the [Secession Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secession_hall_%28Austria%29). [Gustav Klimt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Klimt) painted a portrait of Wittgenstein's sister Margaret for her wedding,[^o33] and [Johannes Brahms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Brahms) and [Gustav Mahler](wikipedia://en/65174) gave regular concerts in the family's numerous music rooms.[^n9][^n10] According to John King, Wittgenstein "was always immaculately neat and tidy, small of stature and with piercing blue eyes under a shock of curly hair."[^o36][^o28]

Wittgenstein, who valued precision and discipline, never considered contemporary classical music acceptable. He said to his friend Drury in 1930: Music came to a full stop with [Brahms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahms); and even in Brahms I can begin to hear the noise of machinery.[^o37] Ludwig Wittgenstein himself had [absolute pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_pitch),[^n11] and his devotion to music remained vitally important to him throughout his life; he made frequent use of musical examples and metaphors in his philosophical writings, and he was unusually adept at [whistling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistling) lengthy and detailed musical passages.[^n12] He was known as a amazing whistler,[^o40][^o41] a talent he developed as a boy while his brothers played instruments as children.[^o42] Wittgenstein would give tips to other whistlers if they were off tune.[^o43] He learnt to play the [clarinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarinet) in his 30s.[^n13] A fragment of music (three bars), composed by Wittgenstein, was discovered in one of his 1931 notebooks, by [Michael Nedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Nedo), director of the Wittgenstein Institute in Cambridge.[^o45]

### Family temperament and brothers' suicides {#Family_temperament_and_brothers'_suicides}

*Image: The Wittgensteins 1890.jpg.* From left, Helene, Rudi, Hermine, Ludwig (the baby), [Gretl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Stonborough-Wittgenstein), [Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Wittgenstein), Hans, and Kurt, around 1890

[Ray Monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Monk) writes that Karl's aim was to turn his sons into captains of industry; they were not sent to school lest they acquire bad habits but were educated at home to prepare them for work in Karl's industrial empire.[^n14] Three of the five brothers later committed suicide.[^o48][^o49] Psychiatrist [Michael Fitzgerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Fitzgerald_%28psychiatrist%29) argues that Karl was a harsh perfectionist who lacked empathy, and that Wittgenstein's mother was anxious and insecure, unable to stand up to her husband.[^o50] Johannes Brahms said of the family, whom he visited regularly: They seemed to act towards one another as if they were at court.[^n4]The family appeared to have a strong streak of depression running through it. [Anthony Gottlieb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Gottlieb) tells a story about Paul practising on one of the pianos in the Wittgensteins' main family mansion, when he suddenly shouted at Ludwig in the next room:I cannot play when you are in the house, as I feel your skepticism seeping towards me from under the door![^o24]

*Image: Helene, Ludwig, Hermine, Paul und Margarethe Wittgenstein OeNB 5086848 (A).jpg.* Ludwig (bottom-right), Paul, and their sisters, late 1890s

The family palace housed seven grand pianos[^n15] and each of the siblings pursued music "with an enthusiasm that, at times, bordered on the pathological".[^n16] The eldest brother, Hans, was hailed as a musical prodigy. At the age of four, writes [Alexander Waugh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Waugh), Hans could identify the [Doppler effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect) in a passing siren as a quarter-tone drop in pitch, and at five started crying "Wrong! Wrong!" when two brass bands in a carnival played the same tune in different [keys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_signature). But he died in mysterious circumstances in May 1902, when he ran away to the US and disappeared from a boat in [Chesapeake Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chesapeake_Bay), most likely having committed suicide.[^n17][^n18]

Two years later, aged 22 and studying chemistry at the [Berlin Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences), the third eldest brother, Rudi, committed suicide in a Berlin bar. He had asked the pianist to play [Thomas Koschat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Koschat)'s "*Verlassen, verlassen, verlassen bin ich*" ("Forsaken, forsaken, forsaken am I"), before mixing himself a drink of milk and [potassium cyanide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_cyanide). He had left several suicide notes, one to his parents that said he was grieving over the death of a friend, and another that referred to his "perverted disposition". It was reported at the time that he had sought advice from the [Scientific-Humanitarian Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific-Humanitarian_Committee), an organization that was campaigning against [Paragraph 175](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragraph_175) of the German Criminal Code, which prohibited homosexual sex. His father forbade the family from ever mentioning his name again.[^n19][^o57][^o58][^o24] (Ludwig also may have been a closeted homosexual, who separated sexual intercourse from love, despising all forms of the former.[^o59]) The second eldest brother, Kurt, an officer and company director, shot himself on 27 October 1918, just before the end of World War I, when the Austrian troops he was commanding refused to obey his orders and deserted *en masse*.[^n14] Hermine had said Kurt seemed to carry "the germ of disgust for life within himself".[^n20] Later, Ludwig wrote:I ought to have ... become a star in the sky. Instead of which I have remained stuck on earth.[^n21]

## 1903–1906: Secondary school {#1903–1906:_Secondary_school}

### Realschule in Linz {#Realschule_in_Linz}

*Image: Realschule in Linz.jpg.* The *[Realschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realschule)* in [Linz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linz)

Insistent that they be entirely home-schooled by tutors like their siblings, Karl Wittgenstein had prevented his youngest sons Paul and Ludwig from attending secondary school. After the 1902 disappearance (and presumed suicide) of Hans and the ensuing tensions at home, he finally relented.[^o65][^n22] Unlike Paul, Wittgenstein failed the entrance examination to the more academic [Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28school%29) in [Wiener Neustadt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiener_Neustadt). He only barely managed, after extra tutoring, to pass the exam for the more technically oriented [Imperial and Royal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_and_Royal) *[Realschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realschule)* in [Linz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linz), a small state secondary school of some 300 pupils.[^n23][^n24] In 1903, when he was 14, he began his three years of formal schooling there, lodging nearby during the term with the family of Josef Strigl, a teacher at the local gymnasium, the family giving him the nickname Luki.[^n25][^o70]

[Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) was a fellow pupil and of the same age but was never in the same class, because he had been made to repeat his 1900/1901 first year.[^o71] Placed two grades above Hitler, it is thought Wittgenstein was also moved forward a year.[^n25] Hitler left Linz in 1904 to spend the 1904/1905 academic year at the *Realschule* in [Steyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steyr).[^o73] As [Ray Monk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Monk) and [Hans Sluga](wikipedia://en/1504912) note, there is no evidence the two ever had anything to do with each other during the year that they overlapped, 1903–1904. Wittgenstein's brother Rudi died by suicide on 2 May 1904, and this resulted in his missing many classes during the remainder of the academic year and the one that followed.[^o74]

Historian [Brigitte Hamann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigitte_Hamann) writes that he stood out from the other boys, because he spoke an unusually pure form of [High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_German) with a stutter, dressed elegantly, and was sensitive and unsociable.[^n26] Monk writes that the other boys made fun of him, singing after him, "Wittgenstein wandelt wehmütig widriger Winde wegen Wienwärts"[^n13] (Wittgenstein wanders wistfully Vienna-wards \[in\] worsening winds). In his leaving certificate, he received a top mark—a 5—in [religious studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_studies); a 2 for conduct and English; 3s for French, geography, history, mathematics, and physics; and 4s for German, chemistry, geometry, and [freehand drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freehand_drawing).[^n25] He had particular difficulty with spelling and failed his written German exam because of it. He wrote in 1931:My bad spelling in youth, up to the age of about 18 or 19, is connected with the whole of the rest of my character (my weakness in study).[^n25]

### Faith {#Faith}

Wittgenstein was baptized as an infant by a [Catholic priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_priest) and received formal instruction in [Catholic doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_theology) as a child.[^o80] In an interview, his sister Gretl Stonborough-Wittgenstein says that their grandfather's "strong, severe, partly ascetic [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity)" was a strong influence on all the Wittgenstein children.[^n27] While he was at the *Realschule*, he decided he lacked religious faith.[^n28] He nevertheless believed in the importance of the practice of [confession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penance_in_the_Catholic_Church). He wrote in his diaries that he had made some confessions to his oldest sister, Hermine, while he was at the *Realschule*; Monk speculates that it may have been about his loss of faith. He also discussed it with Gretl, his other sister, and began reading [Arthur Schopenhauer](wikipedia://en/700)'s *[The World as Will and Representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Representation)* per Gretl's recommendation.[^n28] As a teenager, Wittgenstein adopted Schopenhauer's [epistemological idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemological_idealism). However, after he studied the philosophy of mathematics, he abandoned epistemological idealism for [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416)'s [conceptual realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_realism).[^n29] In later years, Wittgenstein was highly dismissive of Schopenhauer, describing him as an ultimately "shallow" thinker: One could call Schopenhauer a quite *crude* mind.... Where real depth starts, his finishes.[^o85]

Wittgenstein's engagement with Christianity and religion in general, to which he consistently expressed sincere and devoted sympathy, evolved, much as his philosophical outlook did.[^o86] In 1912, Wittgenstein wrote to [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) saying that [Mozart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozart) and [Beethoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beethoven) were the 'actual' [sons of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sons_of_God).[^n30] However, Wittgenstein resisted [organized religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organized_religion), saying it was hard for him to "[bend the knee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bend_the_knee)",[^o88] though his grandfather's beliefs continued to influence Wittgenstein—as he said, "I cannot help seeing every problem from a religious point of view."[^o89] Wittgenstein referred to [Augustine of Hippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo) in his *Philosophical Investigations*. Philosophically, Wittgenstein's thought shows alignment with religious discourse.[^o90] For example, he would become one of the century's fiercest critics of [scientism](wikipedia://en/167742).[^o91]

Wittgenstein's religious belief emerged during his service for the Austrian army in World War I,[^n31] and he was a devoted reader of [Fyodor Dostoevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fyodor_Dostoevsky)'s and [Leo Tolstoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy)'s religious writings.[^n32] He viewed his wartime experiences as a trial in which he strove to conform to the [will of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_of_God), and in a journal entry from 29 April 1915, he writes: Perhaps the nearness of death will bring me the light of life. May God enlighten me. I am a worm, but through God I become a man. God be with me. Amen.[^n33] Around this time, Wittgenstein wrote that "Christianity is indeed the only sure way to happiness", but he rejected the idea that [religious belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_belief) was merely thinking that a certain doctrine was true.[^n34] From this time on, Wittgenstein viewed religious faith as a way of living and opposed rational argumentation or proofs for the [existence of God](wikipedia://en/621169).

With age, a deepening personal spirituality led to several elucidations and clarifications, as he untangled language problems in religion—attacking, for example, the temptation to think of God's existence as a matter of scientific evidence.[^o96] In 1947, finding it rather difficult to work, he wrote:I have had a letter from an old friend in Austria, a priest. In it he says that he hopes my work will go well, if it should be God's will. Now that is all I want: if it should be God's will.[^o97] In *[Culture and Value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_and_Value)*, Wittgenstein writes: Is what I am doing (my work in philosophy) really worth the effort? Yes, but only if a light shines on it from above. His close friend [Norman Malcolm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Malcolm) wrote:

> Wittgenstein's mature life was strongly marked by religious thought and feeling. I am inclined to think that he was more deeply religious than are many people who correctly regard themselves as religious believers.[^o98]

Toward the end, Wittgenstein wrote:Bach wrote on the title page of his *[Orgelbüchlein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orgelbüchlein)*, "To the glory of the most high God, and that my neighbour may be benefited thereby". That is what I would have liked to say about my work.[^o97]

### Influence of Otto Weininger {#Influence_of_Otto_Weininger}

*Image: OttoWeiningerspring1903.jpg.* Austrian philosopher [Otto Weininger](wikipedia://en/201103) (1880–1903)

While a student at the *Realschule*, Wittgenstein was influenced by Austrian philosopher [Otto Weininger](wikipedia://en/201103)'s 1903 book *Geschlecht und Charakter* ([Sex and Character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_and_Character)). Weininger, who was Jewish, argued that the concepts of male and female exist only as [Platonic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_forms) and that Jews tend to embody the form of Platonic femininity. Furthermore, he argued, whereas men are basically rational, women operate only at the level of their emotions and sexual organs. Jews, Weininger contended, are similar, saturated with femininity, with no sense of right and wrong, and no soul. Weininger argues that man must choose between his masculine and feminine sides, consciousness and unconsciousness, Platonic love and sexuality. Love and sexual desire stand in contradiction, and love between a woman and a man is therefore doomed to misery or immorality. The only life worth living is the spiritual one—to live as a woman or a Jew means one has no right to live at all; the choice is genius or death. Weininger committed suicide, shooting himself in 1903, shortly after publishing the book.[^n35] Wittgenstein, then 14, attended Weininger's funeral.[^n36] Many years later, as a professor at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), Wittgenstein distributed copies of Weininger's book to his bemused academic colleagues. He said that Weininger's arguments were wrong, but that it was the way they were wrong that was interesting.[^o102] In a letter dated 23 August 1931, Wittgenstein wrote the following to [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore): Dear Moore, Thanks for your letter. I can quite imagine that you don't admire Weininger very much, what with that beastly translation and the fact that W. must feel very foreign to you. It is true that he is fantastic but he is great and fantastic. It isn't necessary or rather not possible to agree with him but the greatness lies in that with which we disagree. It is his enormous mistake which is great. I.e. roughly speaking if you just add a "~" to the whole book it says an important truth.[^n37] In an unusual move, Wittgenstein took out a copy of Weininger's work on 1 June 1931 from the special order books section in the university library. He met Moore on 2 June, when he probably gave Moore this copy.[^n37]

### Jewishness {#Jewishness}

There is much discussion regarding the extent to which Wittgenstein and his siblings—who were of three-quarters Jewish descent—saw themselves, were, and were seen, as Jews. In his notebooks of around 1929–1931, Wittgenstein frequently referred to himself as Jewish, often in a self-deprecating manner. For instance, while criticizing himself for being a "reproductive" rather than a "productive" thinker, he wrote: "The saint is the only Jewish 'genius'. Even the greatest Jewish thinker is no more than talented. (Myself for instance.)"[^o106][^o107]

Wittgenstein would later, in a 1949 conversation with his friend Drury, claim that "\[m\]y thoughts are one hundred percent Hebraic".[^n38][^n39] If so, [Hans Sluga](wikipedia://en/1504912) has argued,his was a profoundly self-doubting [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism), which had always the possibility of collapsing into a destructive self-hatred (as it did in [Weininger](wikipedia://en/201103)'s case) but which also held an immense promise of innovation and genius.[^n39] Although by "Hebraic", Sluga later contended, Wittgenstein meant to include the Christian tradition that descended from Judaism, in contradistinction to the Greek tradition, holding that good and evil could not be reconciled.[^n40]

## 1906–1913: University {#1906–1913:_University}

### Engineering at Berlin and Manchester {#Engineering_at_Berlin_and_Manchester}

*Image: 10. Ludwig Wittgenstein, aged about eighteen.jpg.* Ludwig Wittgenstein, aged about eighteen

*Image: 1895 technische hochschule charlottenburg.jpg.* The old [Technische Hochschule Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Universität_Berlin) in [Charlottenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottenburg), Berlin

Wittgenstein began his studies in mechanical engineering at the [Technische Hochschule Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Universität_Berlin) in [Charlottenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottenburg), Berlin, on 23 October 1906, lodging with the family of Professor Jolles. He attended for three semesters, and was awarded a [diploma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diploma) (*Abgangzeugnis*) on 5 May 1908.[^o114]

During his time at the Institute, Wittgenstein developed an interest in [aeronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeronautics).[^n41] He arrived at the [Victoria University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Manchester) in the spring of 1908 to study for a doctorate, full of plans for aeronautical projects, including designing and flying his own plane. He conducted research into the behaviour of kites in the upper atmosphere, experimenting at a meteorological observation site near [Glossop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossop) in [Derbyshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire).[^n42] Specifically, the [Royal Meteorological Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Meteorological_Society) researched and investigated the ionization of the upper atmosphere, by suspending instruments on balloons or kites. At Glossop, Wittgenstein worked under Professor of Physics Sir [Arthur Schuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schuster).[^o117]

Wittgenstein worked on the design of a propeller with small jet ([tip jet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tip_jet)) engines on the end of its blades, something he patented in 1911, and that earned him a research studentship from the university in the autumn of 1908.[^n43] At the time, contemporary propeller designs were not advanced enough to actually put Wittgenstein's ideas into practice, and it would be years before a blade design that could support Wittgenstein's innovative design was created. Wittgenstein's design required air and gas to be forced along the propeller arms to combustion chambers on the end of each blade, where they were then compressed by the centrifugal force exerted by the revolving arms and ignited. Propellers of the time were typically wood, whereas modern blades are made from pressed steel laminates as separate halves, which are then welded together. This gives the blade a hollow interior and thereby creates an ideal pathway for the air and gas.[^o117]

*Image: 11. With Eccles at the Kite-Flying Station in Glossop.jpg.* Wittgenstein with his friend William Eccles at the Kite-Flying Station in [Glossop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glossop), Derbyshire, Summer 1908

Work on the jet-powered propeller proved frustrating for Wittgenstein, who had very little experience working with machinery.[^n44] Jim Bamber, a British engineer who was his friend and classmate at the time, reported that when things went wrong, which often occurred, he would throw his arms around, stomp about, and swear volubly in German.[^n45]According to William Eccles, another friend from that period, Wittgenstein then turned to more theoretical work, focusing on the design of the propeller – a problem that required relatively sophisticated mathematics.[^n44]

It was at this time that he became interested in the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358), particularly after reading [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163)'s *[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics)* (1903), and [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416)'s *[The Basic Laws of Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Basic_Laws_of_Arithmetic)*, vol. 1 (1893) and vol. 2 (1903).[^n46] Wittgenstein's sister Hermine said he became obsessed with mathematics as a result, and was anyway losing interest in aeronautics.[^n47] He decided instead that he needed to study logic and the foundations of mathematics, describing himself as in a "constant, indescribable, almost pathological state of agitation".[^n47] In the summer of 1911 he visited Frege at the [University of Jena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Jena) to show him some philosophy of mathematics and logic he had written, and to ask whether it was worth pursuing.[^n48] He wrote: I was shown into Frege's study. Frege was a small, neat man with a pointed beard who bounced around the room as he talked. He absolutely wiped the floor with me, and I felt very depressed; but at the end he said 'You must come again', so I cheered up. I had several discussions with him after that. Frege would never talk about anything but logic and mathematics, if I started on some other subject, he would say something polite and then plunge back into logic and mathematics.[^n49]

### Arrival at Cambridge {#Arrival_at_Cambridge}

*Image: Ludwig Wittgenstein 1910.jpg.* Wittgenstein, 1910s

Wittgenstein wanted to study with Frege, but Frege suggested he attend the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) to study under Russell, so on 18 October 1911 Wittgenstein arrived unannounced at Russell's rooms in [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge).[^o128] Russell was having tea with [C. K. Ogden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._K._Ogden), when, according to Russell, an unknown German appeared, speaking very little English but refusing to speak German. He turned out to be a man who had learned engineering at Charlottenburg, but during this course had acquired, by himself, a passion for the philosophy of mathematics & has now come to Cambridge on purpose to hear me.[^n48]He was soon not only attending Russell's lectures but dominating them. The lectures were poorly attended and Russell often found himself lecturing only to [C. D. Broad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._D._Broad), [E. H. Neville](wikipedia://en/27393555), and H. T. J. Norton.[^n48] Wittgenstein started following him after lectures back to his rooms to discuss more philosophy, until it was time for the evening meal in [Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_%28university%29#Terminology). Russell grew irritated; he wrote to his lover [Lady Ottoline Morrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Ottoline_Morrell): "My German friend threatens to be an infliction."[^n50] Russell soon came to believe that Wittgenstein was a genius, especially after he had examined Wittgenstein's written work. He wrote in November 1911 that he had at first thought Wittgenstein might be a crank, but soon decided he was a genius: Some of his early views made the decision difficult. He maintained, for example, at one time that all existential propositions are meaningless. This was in a lecture room, and I invited him to consider the proposition: 'There is no hippopotamus in this room at present.' When he refused to believe this, I looked under all the desks without finding one; but he remained unconvinced.[^n51] Three months after Wittgenstein's arrival Russell told Morrell:I love him & feel he will solve the problems I am too old to solve ... He is *the* young man one hopes for.[^n52] Wittgenstein later told David Pinsent that Russell's encouragement had proven his salvation, and had ended nine years of loneliness and suffering, during which he had continually thought of suicide. In encouraging him to pursue philosophy and in justifying his inclination to abandon engineering, Russell had, quite literally, saved Wittgenstein's life.[^n52] The role-reversal between Bertrand Russell and Wittgenstein was soon such that Russell wrote in 1916 after Wittgenstein had criticized Russell's own work:His \[Wittgenstein's\] criticism, tho' I don't think you realized it at the time, was an event of first-rate importance in my life, and affected everything I have done since. I saw that he was right, and I saw that I could not hope ever again to do fundamental work in philosophy.[^n53]

### Cambridge Moral Sciences Club {#Cambridge_Moral_Sciences_Club}

*Image: Russell1907-2.jpg.* [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163), 1907

In 1912 Wittgenstein joined the [Cambridge University Moral Sciences Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Moral_Sciences_Club), an influential discussion group for philosophy dons and students, delivering his first paper there on 29 November that year, a four-minute talk defining philosophy as "all those primitive propositions which are assumed as true without proof by the various sciences".[^o137][^o138][^n54] Wittgenstein left Cambridge in 1913, but would return to Cambridge in 1929 and resume attending meetings.[^o140]

### Cambridge Apostles {#Cambridge_Apostles}

The economist [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes) also invited him to join the [Cambridge Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Apostles), an elite secret society formed in 1820, which both Bertrand Russell and [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore) had joined as students, but Wittgenstein did not greatly enjoy it and attended only infrequently. Russell had been worried that Wittgenstein would not appreciate the group's raucous style of intellectual debate, its precious sense of humour, and the fact that the members were often in love with one another.[^n55] He was admitted in 1912 but resigned almost immediately because he could not tolerate the style of discussion. Nevertheless, the Cambridge Apostles allowed Wittgenstein to participate in meetings again in the 1920s when he returned to Cambridge. Reportedly, Wittgenstein also had trouble tolerating the discussions in the Cambridge Moral Sciences Club.

### Frustrations at Cambridge {#Frustrations_at_Cambridge}

Wittgenstein was quite vocal about his depression in his years at Cambridge and before he went to war; on many occasions, he told Russell of his woes. His mental anguish seemed to stem from two sources: his work and his personal life. Wittgenstein made numerous remarks to Russell about logic driving him mad.[^n56] Wittgenstein also said to Russell that he "felt the curse of those who have half a talent".[^n57] He later told of being in mediocre spirits due to his lack of progress in his logical work.[^n58] Monk writes that Wittgenstein lived and breathed logic, and a temporary lack of inspiration plunged him into despair.[^n59] Wittgenstein told of his work in logic affecting his mental state in an extreme way. However, he also told Russell another story. Around Christmas, in 1913, he wrote: how can I be a logician before I'm a human being? For the most important thing is coming to terms with myself![^n60] He also told Russell that he was worried about logic and his sins, and upon arriving in Russell's rooms one night, he announced to Russell that he would kill himself once he left.[^n61] Ludwig's temperament was also recorded in the diary of [David Pinsent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pinsent). Pinsent wrote I have to be frightfully careful and tolerant when he gets these sulky fits and I am afraid he is in an even more sensitive neurotic state just now than usual when talking about Wittgenstein's emotional fluctuations.[^n62]

### Sexual orientation and relationship with David Pinsent {#Sexual_orientation_and_relationship_with_David_Pinsent}

*Image: Wittgenstein with friends drinking coffee.jpg.* Wittgenstein sitting with his friends and family in Vienna. Marguerite Respinger sits at the end on the left and the sculpture he made of her sits behind him on the mantel-place.

Wittgenstein had romantic relations with both men and women. He is generally believed to have fallen in love with at least three men, and had a relationship with the latter two: [David Hume Pinsent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pinsent) in 1912, [Francis Skinner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Skinner) in 1930, and Ben Richards in the late 1940s.[^n63] He later claimed that, as a teenager in Vienna, he had had an affair with a woman.[^n64] Additionally, in the 1920s Wittgenstein fell in love with a young Swiss woman, Marguerite Respinger, sculpting a bust modelled on her and seriously considering marriage, albeit on condition that they would not have children; she decided that he was not right for her.[^n65]

*Image: 15. David Pinsent.jpg.* [David Pinsent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pinsent)

Wittgenstein's relationship with David Pinsent occurred during an intellectually formative period and is well documented. Bertrand Russell introduced Wittgenstein to Pinsent in the summer of 1912. Pinsent was a mathematics undergraduate and a relation of [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume). Wittgenstein and he soon became very close.[^n66] The men worked together on experiments in the psychology laboratory about the role of rhythm in the appreciation of music, and Wittgenstein delivered a paper on the subject to the [British Psychological Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Psychological_Society) in Cambridge in 1912. They also travelled together, including to [Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceland) in September 1912—the expenses paid by Wittgenstein, including [first class travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_class_travel), the hiring of a private train, and new clothes and spending money for Pinsent. In addition to Iceland, Wittgenstein and Pinsent travelled to [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway) in 1913. In determining their destination, Wittgenstein and Pinsent visited a tourist office in search of a location that would fulfil the following criteria: a small village located on a fjord, a location away from tourists, and a peaceful destination to allow them to study logic and law.[^n67]

Choosing [Øystese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Øystese), Wittgenstein and Pinsent arrived in the small village on 4 September 1913. During a vacation lasting almost three weeks, Wittgenstein was able to work vigorously on his studies. The immense progress on logic during their stay led Wittgenstein to express to Pinsent his notion of leaving Cambridge and returning to Norway to continue his work on logic.[^n68] Pinsent's diaries provide valuable insights into Wittgenstein's personality: sensitive, nervous, and attuned to the tiniest slight or change in mood from Pinsent.[^n69][^n70] Pinsent also writes of Wittgenstein being "absolutely sulky and snappish" at times, as well.[^n62] In his diaries Pinsent wrote about shopping for furniture with Wittgenstein in Cambridge when the latter was given rooms in Trinity. Most of what they found in the stores was not minimalist enough for Wittgenstein's aesthetics: I went and helped him interview a lot of furniture at various shops ... It was rather amusing: He is terribly fastidious and we led the shopman a frightful dance, Vittgenstein \[*sic*\] ejaculating *"No – Beastly!"* to 90 percent of what he shewed us![^n71]He wrote in May 1912 that Wittgenstein had just begun to study the history of philosophy: He expresses the most naive surprise that all the philosophers he once worshipped in ignorance are after all stupid and dishonest and make disgusting mistakes![^n71]The last time they saw each other was on 8 October 1913 at Lordswood House in Birmingham, then residence of the Pinsent family: I got up at 6:15 to see Ludwig off. He had to go very early—back to Cambridge—as he has lots to do there. I saw him off from the house in a taxi at 7:00—to catch a 7:30 am train from New Street Station. It was sad parting from him.[^n70]Wittgenstein left to live in Norway.

## 1913–1920: World War I and writing of the *Tractatus* {#1913–1920:_World_War_I_and_writing_of_the_Tractatus}

### Work on *Logik* {#Work_on_Logik}

*Image: Wittgenstein notes 1914.jpg.* Entries from October 1914 in Wittgenstein's diary, on display at the [Wren Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wren_Library), [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge)

*Image: Ludwig\_Wittgenstein\_Skjolden\_Norge\_2024\_Vadim\_Chuprina.jpg.* The restored house of Ludwig Wittgenstein in Skjolden, Norway. Photo by Vadim Chuprina (2024)

Karl Wittgenstein died on 20 January 1913, and after receiving his inheritance Ludwig became one of the wealthiest men in Europe.[^n72] He "made a very generous financial bequest to a group of poets and artists chosen by Ludwig von Ficker, the editor of *Der Brenner*, from artists in need. These included \[Georg\] [Trakl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Trakl) as well as [Rainer Maria Rilke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Maria_Rilke) and the architect [Adolf Loos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Loos)",[^o167] and also the painter [Oskar Kokoschka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Kokoschka).[^o168] Trakl requested to meet his benefactor but in 1914 when Wittgenstein went to visit, Trakl had killed himself.[^n73] "In autumn 1916, as his sister reported, 'Ludwig made a donation of a million [crowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_krone) \[equivalent to about \$US million in US\] for the construction of a 30 cm mortar.[^o170]

Wittgenstein came to feel that he could not get to the heart of his most fundamental questions while surrounded by other academics, and so in 1913, he retreated to the village of [Skjolden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skjolden) in Norway, where he rented the second floor of a house for the winter.[^o171] He later saw this as one of the most productive periods of his life, writing *Logik* (*Notes on Logic*), the predecessor of much of the *Tractatus*.[^o128]

While in Norway, Wittgenstein learned [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language) to converse with the local villagers, and [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language) to read the works of the Danish philosopher [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Søren_Kierkegaard).[^o172] He adored the "quiet seriousness" of the landscape but even Skjolden became too busy for him. He soon designed a small wooden house which was erected on a remote rock overlooking the Eidsvatnet Lake just outside the village. The place was called "Østerrike" (Austria) by locals. He lived there during various periods until the 1930s, and substantial parts of his works were written there. It was during this time that Wittgenstein began addressing what he considered to be a central issue in *Notes on Logic*, a general decision procedure for determining the truth value of logical propositions that would stem from a single primitive proposition. He became convinced during this time that All the propositions of logic are generalizations of [tautologies](wikipedia://en/4495335) and all generalizations of tautologies are propositions of logic. There are no other logical propositions.[^n74][^o174] Based on this, Wittgenstein argued that propositions of logic express their truth or falsehood in the sign itself, and one need not know anything about the constituent parts of the proposition to determine it true or false. Rather, one simply needs to identify the statement as a tautology (true), a contradiction (false), or neither.

The problem lay in forming a primitive proposition that encompassed this and would act as the basis for all of logic. As he stated in correspondence with Russell in late 1913, The big question now is, how must a system of signs be constituted in order to make every tautology recognizable as such IN ONE AND THE SAME WAY? This is the fundamental problem of logic![^n58]The importance Wittgenstein placed upon this fundamental problem was so great that he believed if he did not solve it, he had no right or desire to live.[^n75] Despite this apparent life-or-death importance, Wittgenstein had given up on this primitive proposition by the time he wrote the *Tractatus*. The *Tractatus* does not offer any general process for identifying propositions as tautologies but simply asserts, Every tautology itself shows that it is a tautology.[^n76] This shift to understanding tautologies through mere identification or recognition occurred in 1914 when Wittgenstein asked Moore to assist him in dictating his notes. At Wittgenstein's insistence, Moore, who was now a Cambridge don, visited him in Norway in April 1914, reluctantly because Wittgenstein exhausted him. [David Edmonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Edmonds_%28philosopher%29) and John Eidinow write that Wittgenstein regarded Moore, an internationally known philosopher, as an example of how far someone could get in life with "absolutely no intelligence whatever".[^n77] In Norway it was clear that Moore was expected to act as Wittgenstein's secretary, taking down his notes, with Wittgenstein falling into a rage when Moore got something wrong.[^n78]

[Brian McGuinness](wikipedia://en/36615084) notes that a letter from Wittgenstein to Moore of 7 May 1914 indicates that he had intended to submit an essay he referred to as "*Logik*" as the dissertation required for his completion of a bachelor's degree.[^o180][^o181] McGuinness asserts that the essay is unlikely to be identical with "Notes on Logic" but suggests it is at least summarised in "Notes dictated to G. E. Moore in Norway" (published in Appendix II of *Notebooks 1914–1916*)[^o182] and that "much speaks" for the supposition that it was indeed these notes that Wittgenstein had intended to submit.[^o180] According to the relevant regulations, however, such a dissertation had to contain a preface and notes in which the student stated the sources on which he had relied and the extent to which he had done so, qualities lacking in Wittgenstein's essay. Moore, though himself secretary of the relevant Moral Sciences degrees committee, showed the essay to [Walter Morley Fletcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Morley_Fletcher) – perhaps, McGuinness suggests, "for an impartial opinion from an outsider" – and had "been told that it could not possibly pass for a dissertation" and wrote to Wittgenstein accordingly,[^o180]

Wittgenstein was furious, writing to Moore: If I am not worth your making an exception for me *even in some* STUPID *details* then I may as well go to HELL directly; and if I *am* worth it and you don't do it then – by God – *you* might go there.[^n79]Moore was apparently distraught; writing in his diary that he felt sick and could not get the letter out of his head.[^n80] Wittgenstein wrote to Moore in July of that year conceding that he had "probably no sufficient reason to write to you as I did"[^o180][^o181] but the two did not speak again until 1929.[^n78]

### Military service {#Military_service}

*Image: Bundesarchiv Bild 146-1970-073-25, Isonzo-Schlacht, Trainkolonne am Moistroka-Pass.jpg.* Austro-Hungarian supply line over the [Vršič Pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vršič_Pass), on the [Italian front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Campaign_%28World_War_I%29), October 1917

On the outbreak of World War I, Wittgenstein immediately volunteered for the [Austro-Hungarian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Army), despite being eligible for a medical exemption.[^o187][^o188] He served first on a ship and then in an artillery workshop "several miles from the action".[^o187] He was wounded in an accidental explosion, and hospitalised to [Kraków](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraków).[^o187] In March 1916, he was posted to a fighting unit on the front line of the Russian front, as part of the [Austrian 7th Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7th_Army_%28Austria-Hungary%29), where his unit was involved in some of the heaviest fighting, defending against the [Brusilov Offensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brusilov_Offensive).[^n81] Wittgenstein directed the fire of his own artillery from an observation post in no-man's land against Allied troops—one of the most dangerous jobs since he was targeted by enemy fire.[^o188] He was decorated with the [Military Merit Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Merit_Medal_%28Austria–Hungary%29) with Swords on the Ribbon, and was commended by the army for "exceptionally courageous behaviour, calmness, sang-froid, and heroism" that "won the total admiration of the troops".[^n82]

In January 1917, Wittgenstein was sent as a member of a [howitzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howitzer) regiment to the Russian front, where he won several more medals for bravery including the [Silver Medal for Valour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medal_for_Bravery_%28Austria-Hungary%29), First Class.[^n83] In 1918, he was promoted to lieutenant and sent to the [Italian front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Campaign_%28World_War_I%29) as part of an artillery regiment. For his part in the final Austrian offensive of June 1918, he was recommended for the Gold Medal for Valour, one of the highest honours in the Austrian army, but was instead awarded the Band of the Military Service Medal with Swords—it being decided that this particular action, although extraordinarily brave, had been insufficiently consequential to merit the highest honour.[^n84]

*Image: 27. Wittgenstein’s military identity card during the First World War.jpg.* Wittgenstein's military identity card during the First World War

Throughout the war, he kept notebooks in which he frequently wrote philosophical reflections alongside personal remarks, including his contempt for the character of the other soldiers.[^o193] His notebooks also attest to his philosophical and spiritual reflections, and it was during this time that he experienced a kind of religious awakening.[^n31] In his entry from 11 June 1915, Wittgenstein states that The meaning of life, i.e. the meaning of the world, we can call God. And connect with this the comparison of God to a father. To pray is to think about the meaning of life.[^n85] and on 8 July that To believe in God means to understand the meaning of life. To believe in God means to see that the facts of the world are not the end of the matter. To believe in God means to see that life has a meaning ... When my conscience upsets my equilibrium, then I am not in agreement with Something. But what is this? Is it *the world*? Certainly it is correct to say: Conscience is the voice of God.[^n86] He discovered [Leo Tolstoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy)'s 1896 *[The Gospel in Brief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gospel_in_Brief)* at a bookshop in [Tarnów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarnów), and carried it everywhere, recommending it to anyone in distress, to the point where he became known to his fellow soldiers as "the man with the gospels".[^n87][^o198]

The extent to which *The Gospel in Brief* influenced Wittgenstein can be seen in the *Tractatus*, in the unique way both books number their sentences.[^o199] In 1916 Wittgenstein read [Dostoevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dostoevsky)'s *[The Brothers Karamazov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brothers_Karamazov)* so often that he knew whole passages of it by heart, particularly the speeches of the elder Zosima, who represented for him a powerful Christian ideal, a holy man "who could see directly into the souls of other people".[^n32][^o201]

[Iain King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iain_King) has suggested that Wittgenstein's writing changed substantially in 1916 when he started confronting much greater dangers during frontline fighting.[^o202] Russell said he returned from the war a changed man, one with a deeply [mystical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystical) and [ascetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascetic) attitude.[^n88]

### Completion of the *Tractatus* {#Completion_of_the_Tractatus}

*Image: Wittgenstein family Vienna 1917.jpg.* The Wittgenstein family in [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866), Summer 1917, with Kurt (furthest left) and Ludwig (furthest right) in officers' uniforms

In the summer of 1918, Wittgenstein took military leave and went to stay in one of his family's Vienna summer houses, Neuwaldegg. It was there in August 1918 that he completed the *Tractatus*, which he submitted with the title *Der Satz* (German: proposition, sentence, phrase, set, but also "leap") to the publishers Jahoda and Siegel.[^n89]

A series of events around this time left him deeply upset. On 13 August, his uncle Paul died. On 25 October, he learned that Jahoda and Siegel had decided not to publish the *Tractatus*, and on 27 October, his brother Kurt killed himself, the third of his brothers to commit suicide. It was around this time he received a letter from David Pinsent's mother to say that Pinsent had been killed in a plane crash on 8 May.[^n90][^o207] Wittgenstein was distraught to the point of being suicidal. He was sent back to the Italian front after his leave and, as a result of the defeat of the Austrian army, he was captured by [Allied forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I) on 3 November in [Trentino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trentino). He subsequently spent nine months in an Italian [prisoner of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner_of_war) [camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner-of-war_camp).

He returned to his family in Vienna on 25 August 1919, by all accounts physically and mentally spent. He apparently talked incessantly about suicide, terrifying his sisters and brother Paul. He decided to do two things: to enroll in a teacher training college as an elementary school teacher, and to get rid of his fortune.[^o208][^o209] In 1914, it had been providing him with an income of 300,000 [Kronen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_krone) a year, but by 1919 was worth a great deal more, with a sizable portfolio of investments in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) and the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands). He divided it among his siblings, except for Margarete, insisting that it not be held in trust for him. His family saw him as ill and acquiesced.[^n89]

## 1920–1928: Schoolteaching and publication of the *Tractatus* {#1920–1928:_Schoolteaching_and_publication_of_the_Tractatus}

### Teacher training in Vienna {#Teacher_training_in_Vienna}

In September 1919 he enrolled in the *Lehrerbildungsanstalt* (teacher training college) in the *Kundmanngasse* in Vienna. His sister Hermine said that Wittgenstein working as an elementary teacher was like using a precision instrument to open crates, but the family decided not to interfere.[^n91] [Thomas Bernhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Bernhard), more critically, wrote of this period in Wittgenstein's life: "the multi-millionaire as a village schoolmaster is surely a piece of perversity".[^n92]

### Teaching posts in Austria {#Teaching_posts_in_Austria}

In the summer of 1920, Wittgenstein worked as a gardener for a monastery. At first, he applied under a false name, for a teaching post at Reichenau, and was awarded the job, but he declined it when his identity was discovered. As a teacher, he wished to no longer be recognized as a member of the Wittgenstein family. In response, his brother Paul wrote: It is out of the question, really completely out of the question, that anybody bearing our name and whose elegant and gentle upbringing can be seen a thousand paces off, would not be identified as a member of our family.... That one can neither simulate nor dissimulate anything including a refined education I need hardly tell you.[^n93]

In 1920, Wittgenstein was given his first job as a primary school teacher in [Trattenbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trattenbach), under his real name, in a remote village of a few hundred people. His first letters describe it as beautiful, but, in October 1921, he wrote to Russell: "I am still at Trattenbach, surrounded, as ever, by odiousness and baseness. I know that human beings on the average are not worth much anywhere, but here they are much more good-for-nothing and irresponsible than elsewhere."[^n94] He was soon the object of gossip among the villagers, who found him eccentric at best. He did not get on well with the other teachers; when he found his lodgings too noisy, he made a bed for himself in the school kitchen. He was an enthusiastic teacher, offering late-night extra tuition to several of the students, something that did not endear him to the parents, though some of them came to adore him; his sister Hermine occasionally watched him teach and said the students "literally crawled over each other in their desire to be chosen for answers or demonstrations".[^o218]

To the less able, it seems that he became something of a tyrant. The first two hours of each day were devoted to mathematics, hours that Monk writes some of the pupils recalled years later with horror.[^n95] They reported that he caned the boys and boxed their ears, and also that he pulled the girls' hair;[^n96] this was not unusual at the time for boys, but for the villagers he went too far in doing it to the girls too; girls were not expected to understand algebra, much less have their ears boxed over it. The [corporal punishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_corporal_punishment) apart, Monk writes that he quickly became a village legend, shouting "Krautsalat!" ("coleslaw" – i.e. shredded cabbage) when the headmaster played the piano, and "Nonsense!" when a priest was answering children's questions.[^n97]

### Publication of the *Tractatus* {#Publication_of_the_Tractatus}

*Image: Wittgenstein2.jpg.* Ludwig Wittgenstein, [schoolteacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoolteacher), c. 1922

While Wittgenstein was living in isolation in rural Austria, the *[Tractatus](wikipedia://en/30062)* was published to considerable interest, first in German in 1921 as *Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung*, part of [Wilhelm Ostwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Ostwald)'s journal *Annalen der Naturphilosophie*, though Wittgenstein was not happy with the result and called it a pirate edition. Russell had agreed to write an introduction to explain why it was important, because it was otherwise unlikely to be published, as it was difficult if not impossible to understand, and Wittgenstein was unknown in philosophy.[^n98] In a letter to Russell, Wittgenstein wrote, "The main point is the theory of what can be expressed (gesagt) by prop\[osition\]s – i.e. by language – (and, which comes to the same thing, what can be *thought*) and what can not be expressed by pro\[position\]s, but only shown (gezeigt); which, I believe, is the cardinal problem of philosophy."[^o224] But Wittgenstein was not happy with Russell's help and lost faith in him, finding him glib and his philosophy mechanistic. Wittgenstein thought that Russell had fundamentally misunderstood the *Tractatus*.[^n99] left An English translation was prepared in Cambridge by [C. K. Ogden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._K._Ogden), a mathematics undergraduate at King's commissioned by [Frank Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285). It was Moore who suggested *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* for the title, an allusion to [Baruch Spinoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baruch_Spinoza)'s *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus.* Initially, there were difficulties in finding a publisher for the English edition too, because Wittgenstein was insisting it appear without Russell's introduction; Cambridge University Press turned it down for that reason. Finally in 1922 an agreement was reached with Wittgenstein that Kegan Paul would print a bilingual edition with Russell's introduction and the Ramsey–Ogden translation.[^o226] This is the translation that was approved by Wittgenstein, but it is problematic in a number of ways. Wittgenstein's English was poor at the time, and Ramsey was a teenager who had only recently learned German, so philosophers often prefer to use a 1961 translation by [David Pears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Pears) and [Brian McGuinness](wikipedia://en/36615084).

An aim of the *Tractatus* is to reveal the relationship between language and the world: what can be said about it, and what can only be shown. Wittgenstein argues that the logical structure of language provides the limits of meaning. The limits of language, for Wittgenstein, are the limits of philosophy. Much of philosophy involves attempts to say the unsayable: "What we can say at all can be said clearly," he argues. Anything beyond that – religion, ethics, aesthetics, the mystical – cannot be discussed. They are not in themselves nonsensical, but any statement about them must be.[^o227] He wrote in the preface: "The book will, therefore, draw a limit to thinking, or rather – not to thinking, but to the expression of thoughts; for, in order to draw a limit to thinking we should have to be able to think both sides of this limit (we should therefore have to be able to think what cannot be thought)."[^o228]

The book is 75 pages long – "As to the shortness of the book, I am *awfully sorry for it* ... If you were to squeeze me like a lemon you would get nothing more out of me," he told Ogden – and presents seven numbered propositions (1–7), with various sub-levels (1, 1.1, 1.11):[^n100]

1. *Die Welt ist alles, was der Fall ist*.

The world is everything that is the case.[^o230]

1. *Was der Fall ist, die Tatsache, ist das Bestehen von Sachverhalten*.

What is the case, the fact, is the existence of [atomic facts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_fact).

1. *Das logische Bild der Tatsachen ist der Gedanke*.

The logical picture of the facts is the thought.

1. *Der Gedanke ist der sinnvolle Satz*.

The thought is the significant proposition.

1. *Der Satz ist eine Wahrheitsfunktion der Elementarsätze*.

Propositions are truth-functions of [elementary propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_proposition).

1. *Die allgemeine Form der Wahrheitsfunktion ist: $[\bar p,\bar\xi, N(\bar\xi)]$. Dies ist die allgemeine Form des Satzes*.

The general form of a truth-function is: $[\bar p,\bar\xi, N(\bar\xi)]$. This is the general form of proposition.

1. *Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muß man schweigen*.

Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.

### Visit from Frank Ramsey, Puchberg {#Visit_from_Frank_Ramsey,_Puchberg}

*Image: Puchberg am Schneeberg-view.jpg.* [Frank P. Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) visited Wittgenstein in [Puchberg am Schneeberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puchberg_am_Schneeberg) in September 1923.

In September 1922 he moved to a secondary school in a nearby village, [Hassbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hassbach_%28Lower_Austria%29), but considered the people there just as bad – "These people are not human *at all* but loathsome worms," he wrote to a friend – and he left after a month. In November he began work at another primary school, this time in [Puchberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puchberg_am_Schneeberg) in the [Schneeberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schneeberg_%28Alps%29) mountains. There, he told Russell, the villagers were "one-quarter animal and three-quarters human".

[Frank P. Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) visited him on 17 September 1923 to discuss the *Tractatus*, which he had already reviewed for *Mind*.[^n101][^o233][^o234] During their talks, a number of corrections were made to both the German and English texts in Ramsey's copy of the book, most of which would be incorporated into the second edition of 1933.[^n102][^o236]

Ramsey reported in a letter home that Wittgenstein was living frugally in one tiny whitewashed room that had space only for a bed, a washstand, a small table, and one small hard chair. Ramsey shared an evening meal with him of coarse bread, butter, and cocoa. Wittgenstein's school hours were eight to twelve or one, and he had afternoons free.[^o237] After Ramsey returned to Cambridge, a long campaign began among Wittgenstein's friends to persuade him to return to Cambridge and away from what they saw as a hostile environment for him. He was accepting no help even from his family.[^n103] Ramsey wrote to John Maynard Keynes:\[Wittgenstein's family\] are very rich and extremely anxious to give him money or do anything for him in any way, and he rejects all their advances; even Christmas presents or presents of invalid's food, when he is ill, he sends back. And this is not because they aren't on good terms but because he won't have any money he hasn't earned ... It is an awful pity.[^n103]

### Teaching continues, Otterthal; Standard Austrian German; Haidbauer incident {#Teaching_continues,_Otterthal;_Standard_Austrian_German;_Haidbauer_incident}

*Image: 31. Wittgenstein, 1925.jpg.* Wittgenstein, 1925

He moved schools again in September 1924, this time to [Otterthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otterthal), near Trattenbach; the socialist headmaster, Josef Putre, was someone Wittgenstein had become friends with while at Trattenbach. While he was there, he wrote a 42-page pronunciation and spelling dictionary for the children, *Wörterbuch für Volksschulen*, published in Vienna in 1926 by Hölder-Pichler-Tempsky, the only book of his apart from the *Tractatus* that was published in his lifetime.[^o226] A first edition sold in 2005 for £75,000.[^o241] In 2020, an English version entitled *Word Book* translated by art historian Bettina Funcke and illustrated by artist / publisher [Paul Chan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Chan_%28artist%29) was released.[^o242]

The *Wörterbuch für Volksschulen* is remarkable for its [pluricentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluricentric_language) conceptualization, decades before such a linguistic approach existed. In Wittgenstein's preface to the *Wörterbuch*, which was withheld at the publisher's request but which survives in a 1925 typescript, Wittgenstein takes a clear stance for a [Standard Austrian German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Austrian_German), which he aimed to document for elementary pupils in the text. Wittgenstein states (translated from German) that

> The dictionary should include only words, but all such words, that are known to Austrian elementary students. Therefore it excludes many a good German word unusual in Austria.[^o243]

Wittgenstein is through his school dictionary one of the earliest proponents of a German with more than one standard variety.[^o243] This is especially noteworthy in the German language context, in which modern expert debates over the status and relevance of standard varieties are so common that some speak of a [One Standard German Axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Standard_German_Axiom). Wittgenstein was taking a stance for multiple standards, against such an axiom, long before these debates ensued. An incident occurred in April 1926 and became known as *Der Vorfall Haidbauer* (the [Haidbauer incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haidbauer_incident)). Josef Haidbauer was an 11-year-old pupil whose father had died and whose mother worked as a local maid. He was a slow learner, and one day Wittgenstein hit him two or three times on the head, causing him to collapse. Wittgenstein carried him to the headmaster's office, then quickly left the school, bumping into a parent, Herr Piribauer, on the way out. Piribauer had been sent for by the children when they saw Haidbauer collapse; Wittgenstein had previously pulled Piribauer's daughter, Hermine, so hard by the ears that her ears had bled.[^n104] Piribauer said that when he met Wittgenstein in the hall that day: I called him all the names under the sun. I told him he wasn't a teacher, he was an animal-trainer! And that I was going to fetch the police right away![^n105]Piribauer tried to have Wittgenstein arrested, but the village's police station was empty, and when he tried again the next day he was told Wittgenstein had disappeared. On 28 April 1926, Wittgenstein handed in his resignation to Wilhelm Kundt, a local school inspector, who tried to persuade him to stay; however, Wittgenstein was adamant that his days as a schoolteacher were over.[^n105] Proceedings were initiated in May, and the judge ordered a psychiatric report; in August 1926 a letter to Wittgenstein from a friend, Ludwig Hänsel, indicates that hearings were ongoing, but nothing is known about the case after that. Alexander Waugh writes that Wittgenstein's family and their money may have had a hand in covering things up. Waugh writes that Haidbauer died shortly afterwards of [haemophilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemophilia); Monk says he died when he was 14 of [leukaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leukaemia).[^n106][^n107]

Ten years later, in 1936, as part of a series of "confessions" he engaged in that year, Wittgenstein appeared without warning at the village saying he wanted to confess personally and ask for pardon from the children he had hit. He visited at least four of the children, including Hermine Piribauer, who apparently replied only with a "Ja, ja," though other former students were more hospitable. Monk writes that the purpose of these confessions was notto hurt his pride, as a form of punishment; it was to dismantle it – to remove a barrier, as it were, that stood in the way of honest and decent thought. Of the apologies, Wittgenstein wrote, This brought me into more settled waters... and to greater seriousness.[^n108]

### The Vienna Circle {#The_Vienna_Circle}

The *Tractatus* was now the subject of much debate among philosophers, and Wittgenstein was a figure of increasing international fame. In particular, a discussion group of philosophers, scientists, and mathematicians, known as the [Vienna Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Circle), had developed purportedly as a result of the inspiration they had received from reading the *Tractatus*.[^n103] Though many assume that Wittgenstein was a part of the Vienna Circle, in reality, he was not. German philosopher [Oswald Hanfling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Hanfling) writes bluntly: "Wittgenstein was never a member of the Circle, though he was in Vienna during much of the time."[^o252] Indeed it is doubtful, as Brian McGuinness notes, that Wittgenstein ever attended any meetings of the Vienna Circle proper.[^o253] Yet, Hanfling asserts, "his influence on the Circle's thought was at least as important as that of any of its members."[^o252]

Philosopher [A. C. Grayling](wikipedia://en/2027044), however, contends that while certain superficial similarities between Wittgenstein's early philosophy and [logical positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivism) led its members to study the *Tractatus* in detail and to arrange discussions with him, Wittgenstein's influence on the Circle was rather limited. The fundamental philosophical views of Circle had been established before they met Wittgenstein and had their origins in the [British empiricists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism), [Ernst Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Mach), and the logic of [Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) and Russell. Whatever influence Wittgenstein did have on the Circle was largely limited to [Moritz Schlick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moritz_Schlick) and [Friedrich Waismann](wikipedia://en/1724365) and, even in these cases, had little lasting effect on their positivism. Grayling states that "it is no longer possible to think of the *Tractatus* as having inspired a philosophical movement, as most earlier commentators claimed."[^o254]

Schlick first met Wittgenstein in 1927 and did so several times before the latter would agree to be introduced to some of his colleagues. From 1927 to 1928 Wittgenstein met with small groups that included Schlick, almost always Waismann, sometimes [Rudolf Carnap](wikipedia://en/57987), and sometimes [Herbert Feigl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Feigl) and his future wife Maria Kesper. From 1929, Wittgenstein's contact with the Circle would be restricted to meetings with Schlick and Waismann only.[^o253] Conversations from these later meetings (December 1929 up to March 1932) were recorded by Waismann and eventually published in English translation in *Ludwig Wittgenstein and the Vienna Circle* (1979).[^o255] By the time they began Schlick had tasked Waismann with writing an exposition of Wittgenstein's philosophy. This project would undergo radical transformation but the final text, inspired by Wittgenstein but very much Waismann's own work, was eventually published in English as *The Principles of Linguistic Philosophy* (1965).[^o256][^o253][^o255] Some further draft materials for the project and dictations were published in English under the editorship of [Gordon Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Baker) in 2003.[^o257]

In his autobiography, Rudolf Carnap describes Wittgenstein as the thinker who most inspired him. However, he also wrote that "there was a striking difference between Wittgenstein's attitude toward philosophical problems and that of Schlick and myself. Our attitude toward philosophical problems was not very different from that which scientists have toward their problems." As for Wittgenstein: His point of view and his attitude toward people and problems, even theoretical problems, were much more similar to those of a creative artist than to those of a scientist; one might almost say, similar to those of a religious prophet or a seer.... When finally, sometimes after a prolonged arduous effort, his answers came forth, his statement stood before us like a newly created piece of art or a divine revelation ... the impression he made on us was as if insight came to him as through divine inspiration, so that we could not help feeling that any sober rational comment or analysis of it would be a profanation.[^o258]

### Haus Wittgenstein {#Haus_Wittgenstein}

*Image: Haus Wittgenstein, Stonborough House, Vienna.jpg.* Wittgenstein worked on [Haus Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haus_Wittgenstein) between 1926 and 1929.

left

In 1926 Wittgenstein was again working as a gardener for a number of months, this time at the monastery of Hütteldorf, where he had also inquired about becoming a monk. His sister, Margaret, invited him to help with the design of her new townhouse in Vienna's *Kundmanngasse*. Wittgenstein, his friend [Paul Engelmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Engelmann), and a team of architects developed a spare modernist house. In particular, Wittgenstein focused on the windows, doors, and radiators, demanding that every detail be exactly as he specified. When the house was nearly finished Wittgenstein had an entire ceiling raised 30 mm so that the room had the exact proportions he wanted. Monk writes that "This is not so marginal as it may at first appear, for it is precisely these details that lend what is otherwise a rather plain, even ugly house its distinctive beauty."[^o261]

It took him a year to design the door handles and another to design the radiators. Each window was covered by a metal screen that weighed 150 kg, moved by a pulley Wittgenstein designed. Bernhard Leitner, author of *The Architecture of Ludwig Wittgenstein*, said there is barely anything comparable in the history of interior design: "It is as ingenious as it is expensive. A metal curtain that could be lowered into the floor."[^o261]

The house was finished by December 1928 and the family gathered there at Christmas to celebrate its completion. Wittgenstein's sister Hermine wrote: "Even though I admired the house very much. ... It seemed indeed to be much more a dwelling for the gods."[^o260] Wittgenstein said "the house I built for Gretl is the product of a decidedly sensitive ear and *good* manners, and expression of great *understanding*... But *primordial* life, wild life striving to erupt into the open – that is lacking."[^n109] Monk comments that the same might be said of the technically excellent, but austere, terracotta sculpture Wittgenstein had modelled of Marguerite Respinger in 1926, and that, as Russell first noticed, this "wild life striving to be in the open" was precisely the substance of Wittgenstein's philosophical work.[^n109]

## 1929–1941: Return to Cambridge {#1929–1941:_Return_to_Cambridge}

### PhD and fellowship {#PhD_and_fellowship}

*Image: Ludwig Wittgenstein.jpg.* Ludwig Wittgenstein, 1930

According to Feigl (as reported by Monk), upon attending a conference in Vienna by mathematician [L. E. J. Brouwer](wikipedia://en/172896), Wittgenstein remained quite impressed and considered the possibility of a "return to Philosophy". At the urging of Ramsey and others, Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge in 1929. Keynes wrote in a letter to his wife: "Well, God has arrived. I met him on the 5.15 train."[^n110] Despite this fame, he could not initially work at Cambridge, as he had failed to obtain a degree, so he applied as an advanced undergraduate. Russell noted that his previous residency was sufficient to fulfil eligibility requirements for a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD), and urged him to offer the *Tractatus* as his thesis.[^o267] Frank Ramsey was formally appointed as his supervisor, and they met regularly during what would become the last year of Ramsey's life.[^o28][^o364]

As the *Tractatus* was already completed and published, this was a nominal role. Ramsey did, however—at Wittgenstein's request—fulfil the administrative requirement that every PhD thesis be accompanied by an abstract, by writing the abstract himself.[^o268] Wittgenstein was examined in 1929 by Russell and Moore. At the end of the thesis defence, Wittgenstein clapped the two examiners on the shoulder and said, "Don't worry, I know you'll never understand it."[^n111] Braithwaite, quoting from memory, recalls that Moore wrote in the examiners' report: "I myself consider that this is a work of genius; but, even if I am completely mistaken and it is nothing of the sort, it is well above the standard required for the Ph.D. degree."[^n112] Wittgenstein was appointed as a lecturer and was made a fellow of Trinity College. On 17 November 1929 he delivered a lecture on ethics to the [Heretics Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heretics_Society) in Cambridge.[^o271]

Wittgenstein was given a research position at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge) and began teaching classes at Cambridge in 1930.[^o272] During the following years he began a productive period of philosophical writing which he also developed in his lectures.[^o272]

### Return to Cambridge Moral Sciences Club {#Return_to_Cambridge_Moral_Sciences_Club}

Wittgenstein reportedly resumed attending the Cambridge Moral Sciences Club in 1929 and dominated meetings; for a time, he stopped attending in the early 1930s after complaints that he gave no one else a chance to speak.[^n113][^o140]

The club would become infamous within popular philosophy because of a meeting on 25 October 1946 at [Richard Braithwaite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._B._Braithwaite)'s rooms in [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge), where [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper), another Viennese philosopher, had been invited as the guest speaker. Popper's paper was "Are there philosophical problems?", in which he struck up a position against Wittgenstein's, contending that problems in philosophy are real, not just linguistic puzzles as Wittgenstein argued. Accounts vary as to what happened next, but Wittgenstein apparently started waving a hot poker, demanding that Popper give him an example of a moral rule. Popper offered one–"Not to threaten visiting speakers with pokers"—at which point Russell told Wittgenstein he had misunderstood and Wittgenstein left. Popper maintained that Wittgenstein "stormed out", but it had become accepted practice for him to leave early (because of his aforementioned ability to dominate discussion). It was the only time the philosophers, three of the most eminent in the 20th century, were ever in the same room together.[^o275][^o276] The minutes record that the meeting was "charged to an unusual degree with a spirit of controversy".[^o277]

### *Anschluss* {#Anschluss}

*Image: 21. Photographs showing Wittgenstein’s house in Norway.jpg.* Photograph showing Wittgenstein's house in Norway, sent by Wittgenstein to [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore), October 1936

From 1936 to 1937, Wittgenstein lived again in Norway,[^o279] where he worked on the *Philosophical Investigations*. In the winter of 1936/7, he delivered a series of "confessions" to close friends, most of them about minor infractions like white lies, in an effort to cleanse himself. In 1938, he travelled to Ireland to visit [Maurice O'Connor Drury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_O'Connor_Drury), a friend who became a psychiatrist, and considered such training himself, with the intention of abandoning philosophy for it.

While he was in Ireland in March 1938, Germany annexed Austria in the *[Anschluss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anschluss)*; the Viennese Wittgenstein was now a Jew under the [1935 Nuremberg racial laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws), because three of his grandparents had been born as Jews. He would also, in July, become by law a 'national' of the enlarged [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany) being, as a Jew, ineligible to become a Reich citizen.[^n114] The Nuremberg Laws classified people as Jews (*Volljuden*) if they had three or four Jewish grandparents, and as mixed blood (*[Mischling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mischling)*) if they had one or two. It meant, among other things, that the Wittgensteins were restricted in whom they could marry or have sex with, and where they could work.[^n115]

After the *Anschluss*, his brother Paul left almost immediately for England, and later the US. The Nazis discovered his relationship with Hilde Schania, a brewer's daughter with whom he had had two children but whom he had never married, though he did later. Because she was not Jewish, he was served with a summons for [Rassenschande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rassenschande) (racial defilement). He told no one he was leaving the country, except for Hilde who agreed to follow him. He left so suddenly and quietly that for a time people believed he was the fourth Wittgenstein brother to have committed suicide.[^n116]

Wittgenstein began to investigate acquiring British or Irish citizenship with the help of Keynes, and apparently had to confess to his friends in England that he had earlier misrepresented himself to them as having just one Jewish grandparent, when in fact he had three.[^n117] A few days before the [invasion of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland), Hitler personally granted *Mischling* status to the Wittgenstein siblings.[^o284] In 1939, there were 2,100 applications for *Mischling* status (or for "promotions" within such status), of which Hitler granted only 12.[^o285][^n118] Anthony Gottlieb writes that the pretext was that their paternal grandfather had been the bastard son of a German prince, which allowed the [Reichsbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsbank) to claim foreign currency, stocks and 1700 kg of gold held in Switzerland by a Wittgenstein family trust. Gretl, an American citizen by marriage, started the negotiations over the racial status of their grandfather, and the family's large foreign currency reserves were used as a bargaining tool. Paul had escaped to Switzerland and then the US in July 1938, and disagreed with the negotiations, leading to a permanent split between the siblings. After the war, when Paul was performing in Vienna, he did not visit Hermine who was dying there, and he had no further contact with Ludwig or Gretl.[^o24]

### Professor of philosophy {#Professor_of_philosophy}

After [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore) resigned the chair in philosophy in 1939, Wittgenstein was elected. He was naturalised as a [British subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_subject) shortly after on 12 April 1939. In July 1939 he travelled to Vienna to assist Gretl and his other sisters, visiting Berlin for one day to meet an official of the [Reichsbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsbank). After this, he travelled to New York to persuade Paul, whose agreement was required, to back the scheme. The required *Befreiung* was granted in August 1939. The unknown amount signed over to the Nazis by the Wittgenstein family, a week or so before the outbreak of war, included amongst many other assets 1,700 kg of gold.[^n119] [Norman Malcolm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Malcolm), at the time a post-graduate research fellow at Cambridge, describes his first impressions of Wittgenstein in 1938: At a meeting of the Moral Science Club, after the paper for the evening was read and the discussion started, someone began to stammer a remark. He had extreme difficulty in expressing himself and his words were unintelligible to me. I whispered to my neighbour, 'Who is that?': he replied, 'Wittgenstein'. I was astonished because I had expected the famous author of the *Tractatus* to be an elderly man, whereas this man looked *young* – perhaps about thirty-five. (His actual age was forty-nine.) His face was lean and brown, his profile was aquiline and strikingly beautiful, his head was covered with a curly mass of brown hair. I observed the respectful attention that everyone in the room paid to him. After this unsuccessful beginning, he did not speak for a time but was obviously struggling with his thoughts. His look was concentrated, he made striking gestures with his hands as if he was discoursing ... Whether lecturing or conversing privately, Wittgenstein always spoke emphatically and with a distinctive intonation. He spoke excellent English, with the accent of an educated Englishman, although occasional Germanisms would appear in his constructions. His voice was resonant ... His words came out, not fluently, but with great force. Anyone who heard him say anything knew that here was a singular person. His face was remarkably mobile and expressive when he talked. His eyes were deep and often fierce in their expression. His whole personality was commanding, even imperial.[^n120]

Describing Wittgenstein's lecture programme, Malcolm continues: It is hardly correct to speak of these meetings as 'lectures', although this is what Wittgenstein called them. For one thing, he was carrying on original research in these meetings.... Often the meetings consisted mainly of dialogue. Sometimes, however, when he was trying to draw a thought out of himself, he would prohibit, with a peremptory motion of the hand, any questions or remarks. There were frequent and prolonged periods of silence, with only an occasional mutter from Wittgenstein, and the stillest attention from the others. During these silences, Wittgenstein was extremely tense and active. His gaze was concentrated; his face was alive; his hands made arresting movements; his expression was stern. One knew that one was in the presence of extreme seriousness, absorption, and force of intellect.... Wittgenstein was a frightening person at these classes.[^n121]

After work, the philosopher would often relax by watching [Westerns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Movie), where he preferred to sit at the very front of the cinema, or reading detective stories especially the ones written by [Norbert Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Davis).[^n122][^o293] Norman Malcolm wrote that Wittgenstein would rush to the cinema when class ended.[^n123]

By this time, Wittgenstein's view on the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358) had changed considerably. In his early 20s, Wittgenstein had thought logic could provide a solid foundation, and he had even considered updating Russell and [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395)'s *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*. Now he denied there were any mathematical facts to be discovered. He gave a series of lectures on mathematics, discussing this and other topics, documented in a book, with some of his lectures and discussions between him and several students, including the young [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208), who described Wittgenstein as "a *very* peculiar man". The two had many discussions about the relationship between computational logic and everyday notions of truth.[^o295][^n124][^o297]

Wittgenstein's lectures from this period have also been discussed by another of his students, the Greek philosopher and educator [Helle Lambridis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elli_Lambridi). Wittgenstein's teachings in the years 1940–1941 were used in the mid-1950s by Lambridis to write a long text in the form of an imagined dialogue with him, where she begins to develop her own ideas about resemblance in relation to language, elementary concepts and basic-level mental images. Initially, only a part of it was published in 1963 in the German education theory review *Club Voltaire*, but the entire imagined dialogue with Wittgenstein was published after Lambridis's death by her archive holder, the [Academy of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Athens_%28modern%29), in 2004.[^o298][^o299]

## 1941–1947: Guy's Hospital and Royal Victoria Infirmary {#1941–1947:_Guy's_Hospital_and_Royal_Victoria_Infirmary}

Monk writes that Wittgenstein found it intolerable that a war ([World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II)) was going on and he was teaching philosophy. He grew angry when any of his students wanted to become professional philosophers. In September 1941, he asked [John Ryle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ryle_%28professor%29), the brother of the philosopher [Gilbert Ryle](wikipedia://en/1004355), if he could get a manual job at [Guy's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy's_Hospital) in London. John Ryle was professor of medicine at Cambridge and had been involved in helping Guy's prepare for [the Blitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blitz). Wittgenstein told Ryle he would die slowly if left at Cambridge, and he would rather die quickly. He started working at Guy's shortly afterwards as a dispensary porter, delivering drugs from the pharmacy to the wards where he apparently advised the patients not to take them.[^n125] In the new year of 1942, Ryle took Wittgenstein to his home in Sussex to meet his wife, who had been determined to meet him. His son recorded the weekend in his diary:

Wink is awful strange \[*sic*\] – not a very good english speaker, keeps on saying 'I mean' and 'its "tolerable meaning intolerable.[^n126] The hospital staff were not told that he was one of the world's most famous philosophers, though some of the medical staff did recognize him – at least one had attended Moral Sciences Club meetings – but they were discreet. "Good God, don't tell anybody who I am!" Wittgenstein begged one of them.[^n127] Some of them nevertheless called him Professor Wittgenstein, and he was allowed to dine with the doctors. He wrote on 1 April 1942: "I no longer feel any hope for the future of my life. It is as though I had before me nothing more than a long stretch of living death. I cannot imagine any future for me other than a ghastly one. Friendless and joyless."[^n125] It was at this time that Wittgenstein had an operation at Guy's to remove a [gallstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallstone) that had troubled him for some years.[^n128]

He had developed a friendship with Keith Kirk, a working-class teenage friend of [Francis Skinner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Skinner), the mathematics undergraduate with whom he had had a relationship until Skinner's death in 1941 from [polio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polio). Skinner had given up academia, thanks at least in part to Wittgenstein's influence, and in 1939 had been working as a mechanic, with Kirk as his apprentice. Kirk and Wittgenstein struck up a friendship, with Wittgenstein giving him lessons in physics to help him pass a [City and Guilds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_and_Guilds) exam. During his period of loneliness at Guy's, he wrote in his diary: "For ten days I've heard nothing more from K, even though I pressed him a week ago for news. I think that he has perhaps broken with me. A *tragic* thought!"[^n129] Kirk had in fact got married, and they never saw each other again.[^n130]

While Wittgenstein was at Guy's he met Basil Reeve, a young doctor with an interest in philosophy, who, with R. T. Grant,[^o310] was studying the effect of wound shock[^o308] (a state associative to [hypovolaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypovolaemia)[^o309]) on air-raid casualties. When the Blitz ended there were fewer casualties to study. In November 1942, Grant and Reeve moved to the [Royal Victoria Infirmary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Victoria_Infirmary), [Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne), to study road traffic and industrial casualties. Grant offered Wittgenstein a position as a laboratory assistant at a wage of £4 per week, and he lived in Newcastle (at 28 Brandling Park, [Jesmond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesmond)[^o310]) from 29 April 1943 until February 1944.[^n131] While there he worked [^o313] and associated socially with [Erasmus Barlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus_Darwin_Barlow),[^o313] a great-grandson of [Charles Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin).[^o314]

Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge in February 1944 briefly, before being granted a leave of absence to move to [Swansea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea) to work on his book, *Philosophical Investigations*.[^n132] Wittgenstein had made plans to publish the *Investigations* at least twice, in 1938 and 1943, but the book remained unfinished.[^n133]

### Final return to Cambridge {#Final_return_to_Cambridge}

Wittgenstein returned to Cambridge for a final time in October 1944.[^n134] This was around the same time as did his former mentor Russell, who had been living in the United States for several years (Russell had returned to Cambridge after a backlash in the US to his writings on morals and religion).[^n134] However, when the two met that fall, having not seen each other for 14 years, there was little warmth between them.[^n135] Russell saw no value in Wittgenstein's later work, and for his part, Wittgenstein believed that Russell's work in philosophy was finished.[^n136] Wittgenstein said of Russell's works to Drury:Russell's books should be bound in two colours ... those dealing with mathematical logic in red – and all students of philosophy should read them; those dealing with ethics and politics in blue – and no one should be allowed to read them.[^n134]

In the summer of 1946, Wittgenstein thought often of leaving Cambridge and resigning his position as Chair. Wittgenstein grew further dismayed at the state of philosophy, particularly because of articles published in the journal [*Mind*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_%28journal%29). It was around this time that Wittgenstein fell in love with Ben Richards, a medical student, writing in his diary, "The only thing that my love for B. has done for me is this: it has driven the other small worries associated with my position and my work into the background." On 30 September, Wittgenstein wrote about Cambridge after his return from Swansea, "Everything about the place repels me. The stiffness, the artificiality, the self-satisfaction of the people. The university atmosphere nauseates me."[^n137]

Wittgenstein had maintained contact only with Fouracre, from Guy's hospital, who had joined the army in 1943 after his marriage, returning in 1947. Wittgenstein maintained frequent correspondence with Fouracre during his time away, expressing a desire for Fouracre to return home urgently from the war.[^n137] In May 1947, Wittgenstein addressed a group of Oxford philosophers for the first time at the Jowett Society. The discussion was on the validity of [Descartes'](wikipedia://en/25525) *[Cogito ergo sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cogito_ergo_sum)*, where Wittgenstein ignored the question and applied his own philosophical method. [Harold Arthur Prichard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Arthur_Prichard) who attended the event was not pleased with Wittgenstein's methods;Wittgenstein: If a man says to me, looking at the sky, 'I think it will rain, therefore I exist', I do not understand him. Prichard: That's all very fine; what we want to know is: is the *cogito* valid or not?[^n138]

## 1947–1951: Final years {#1947–1951:_Final_years}

left Wittgenstein resigned from the professorship at Cambridge in 1947 to concentrate on his writing, and in 1947 and 1948 travelled to [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland), staying at Ross's Hotel in [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin) and at a farmhouse in [Redcross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redcross), [County Wicklow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Wicklow), where he began the manuscript MS 137, volume R.[^o329] Seeking solitude he moved to a holiday cottage in Rosroe overlooking [Killary Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killary_Harbour), [Connemara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connemara) owned by Drury's brother.[^o327]

*Image: Wittgenstein plaque, National Botanic Gardens, Ireland.jpg.* Plaque in the [National Botanic Gardens, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Botanic_Gardens_%28Ireland%29), commemorating Wittgenstein's visits in the winter of 1948–1949

He also accepted an invitation from Norman Malcolm, then a professor at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University), to stay with him and his wife for several months in [Ithaca, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ithaca,_New_York). He made the trip in April 1949, although he told Malcolm he was too unwell to do philosophical work: "I haven't done any work since the beginning of March & I haven't had the strength of even trying to do any." A doctor in Dublin had diagnosed anaemia and prescribed iron and liver pills. The details of Wittgenstein's stay in the US are recounted in Norman Malcolm's *Ludwig Wittgenstein: A Memoir*.[^n139] During his summer in the US, Wittgenstein began his epistemological discussions, in particular his engagement with [philosophical scepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_scepticism), that would posthumously be published as the book *[On Certainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Certainty)*.

*Image: Ludwig Wittgenstein Blue Plaque, 76 Storey's Way, Cambridge, UK.jpg.* The plaque at "Storey's End", 76 [Storey's Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storey's_Way), Cambridge, where Wittgenstein died

He returned to London, where he was diagnosed with an inoperable [prostate cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostate_cancer), which had spread to his bone marrow. He spent the next two months in Vienna, where his sister Hermine died on 11 February 1950; he went to see her every day, but she was hardly able to speak or recognize him. "Great loss for me and all of us," he wrote. "Greater than I would have thought." He moved frequently after Hermine's death, staying with various friends: to Cambridge in April 1950, where he stayed with [G. H. von Wright](wikipedia://en/12250); to London to stay with [Rush Rhees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rush_Rhees); then to Oxford to see [Elizabeth Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Anscombe), writing to Norman Malcolm that he was doing hardly any philosophy. He went to Norway in August with Ben Richards, then returned to Cambridge, where on 27 November he moved into *Storey's End* at 76 [Storey's Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storey's_Way), the home of his doctor, [Edward Bevan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Vaughan_Bevan), and his wife Joan; he had told them he did not want to die in a hospital, so they said he could spend his last days in their home instead. Joan at first was afraid of Wittgenstein, but they soon became good friends.[^o329][^n140]

By the beginning of 1951, it was clear that he had little time left. He wrote a new will in Oxford on 29 January, naming Rhees as his executor, and Anscombe and von Wright as his literary administrators, and wrote to Norman Malcolm that month to say, "My mind's completely dead. This isn't a complaint, for I don't really suffer from it. I know that life must have an end once and that mental life can cease before the rest does."[^n140] In February, he returned to the Bevans' home to work on MS 175 and MS 176. These and other manuscripts were later published as *[Remarks on Colour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remarks_on_Colour)* and *[On Certainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Certainty)*.[^o329] He wrote to Malcolm on 16 April, 13 days before his death: An extraordinary thing happened to me. About a month ago I suddenly found myself in the right frame of mind for doing philosophy. I had been *absolutely* certain that I'd never again be able to do it. It's the first time after more than 2 years that the curtain in my brain has gone up. – Of course, so far I've only worked for about 5 weeks & it may be all over by tomorrow; but it bucks me up a lot now.[^n141]

### Death {#Death}

*Image: Death bed.jpg.* Wittgenstein on his deathbed, 1951

*Image: Death notice Ludwig Wittgenstein.jpg.* Death notice issued by Ludwig's family

Wittgenstein began work on his final manuscript, MS 177, on 25 April 1951. It was his 62nd birthday on 26 April. He went for a walk the next afternoon, and wrote his last entry that day, 27 April. That evening, he became very ill; when his doctor told him he might live only a few days, he reportedly replied, "Good!". Joan stayed with him throughout that night, and just before losing consciousness for the last time on 28 April, he told her: "Tell them I've had a wonderful life." Norman Malcolm described this as "a mysterious and strangely moving utterance".[^n142]

Four of Wittgenstein's former students arrived at his bedside – Ben Richards, [Elizabeth Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Anscombe), [Yorick Smythies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorick_Smythies), and [Maurice O'Connor Drury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_O'Connor_Drury). Anscombe and Smythies were Catholics, and, at the latter's request, a Dominican friar, Father [Conrad Pepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_Pepler), also attended. Wittgenstein had asked for a "priest who was not a philosopher" and had met with Pepler several times.[^o335] They were at first unsure what Wittgenstein would have wanted, but then remembered he had said he hoped his Catholic friends would pray for him, so they did, and he was pronounced dead shortly afterwards.

*Image: Wittgenstein Gravestone 2021.jpg.* Wittgenstein's grave at the [Ascension Parish Burial Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_Parish_Burial_Ground) in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge), 2021

Wittgenstein was given a Catholic burial at [Ascension Parish Burial Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_Parish_Burial_Ground) in Cambridge.[^o336] Drury later said he had been troubled ever since about whether that was the right thing to do.[^n143] In 2015 the [ledger gravestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ledger_stone) was refurbished by the British Wittgenstein Society.[^o338]

As for his religious views, Wittgenstein was said to be greatly interested in [Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholicism), and was sympathetic to it, but did not consider himself to be a Catholic. According to Norman Malcolm, Wittgenstein saw Catholicism more as a way of life than as a set of beliefs he held, considering that he did not accept any religious faith.

Wittgenstein has no goal to either support or reject religion; his only interest is to keep discussions, whether religious or not, clear. — T. Labron (2006)[^o339]

Wittgenstein was said by some commentators to be [agnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnostic), in a qualified sense.[^n144] left

## Posthumous work {#Posthumous_work}

### 1953: Publication of the *Philosophical Investigations* {#1953:_Publication_of_the_Philosophical_Investigations}

*Image: Kaninchen und Ente.png.* Illustration of a "[duckrabbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit–duck_illusion)", discussed in the *Philosophical Investigations*, Part II, section XI. He used this image to explain ["seeing that" versus "seeing as"](wikipedia://en/23674#Seeing_that_vs._seeing_as).

The *[Blue Book](wikipedia://en/28341040)*, a set of notes dictated to his class at Cambridge in 1933–1934, contains the seeds of Wittgenstein's later thoughts on language and is widely read as a turning point in his philosophy of language. *Philosophical Investigations* was published in two parts in 1953. Most of Part I was ready for printing in 1946, but Wittgenstein withdrew the manuscript from his publisher. The shorter Part II was added by his editors, [Elizabeth Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Anscombe) and [Rush Rhees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rush_Rhees). Wittgenstein asks the reader to think of language as a multiplicity of language games within which parts of language develop and function.[^o344] He argues that the bewitchments of philosophical problems arise from philosophers' misguided attempts to consider the meaning of words independently of their context, usage, and grammar — what he called "language gone on holiday".[^o345] Wittgenstein wrote, "For a *large* class of cases of the employment of the word 'meaning'—though not for *all*—this word can be explained in this way: the meaning of a word is its use in the language."[^o346]

According to Wittgenstein, philosophical problems arise when language is forced from its proper home into a metaphysical environment, where all the familiar and necessary landmarks and contextual clues are removed. He describes this metaphysical environment as like being on frictionless ice: where the conditions are apparently perfect for a philosophically and logically perfect language, all philosophical problems can be solved without the muddying effects of everyday contexts; but where, precisely because of the lack of friction, language can in fact do no work at all.[^o347] Wittgenstein argues that philosophers must leave the frictionless ice and return to the "rough ground" of ordinary language in use. Much of the *Investigations* consists of examples of how the first false steps can be avoided, so that philosophical problems are dissolved, rather than solved: "The clarity we are aiming at is indeed *complete* clarity. But this simply means that the philosophical problems should *completely* disappear."[^o348]

### Other posthumous publications {#Other_posthumous_publications}

Wittgenstein's archive of unpublished papers included 83 manuscripts, 46 typescripts and 11 dictations, amounting to an estimated 20,000 pages. Choosing among repeated drafts, revisions, corrections, and loose notes, editorial work has found nearly one-third of the total suitable for print.[^n145] An Internet facility hosted by the [University of Bergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bergen) allows access to images of almost all the material and to search the available transcriptions.[^o351] In 2011, two new boxes of Wittgenstein papers, thought to have been lost during the Second World War, were found.[^o352][^o353]

What became the *[Philosophical Investigations](wikipedia://en/23674)* was already close to completion in 1951. Wittgenstein's three literary executors prioritized it, both because of its intrinsic importance and because he had explicitly intended publication. The book was published in 1953. At least three other works were more or less finished. Two were already "bulky typescripts", the *Philosophical Remarks* and *Philosophical Grammar*. Literary co-executor G. H. von Wright stated, "They are virtually completed works. But Wittgenstein did not publish them."[^o354] The third was *Remarks on Colour*. "He wrote *[i.a.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_phrases_%28I%29#inter_alia)* a fair amount on colour-concepts, and this material he did excerpt and polish, reducing it to a small compass."[^o355]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

### Assessment {#Assessment}

[Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) described Wittgenstein as "perhaps the most perfect example I have ever known of genius as traditionally conceived; passionate, profound, intense, and dominating."[^n146] The *Tractatus* is considered among the important works of 20th century philosophy and strongly influenced the [Vienna Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Circle) and [logical positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivism).[^o364] In 1999, a survey among American university and college teachers ranked the *Investigations* as the most important book of 20th-century philosophy, standing out as "the one crossover masterpiece in twentieth-century philosophy, appealing across diverse specializations and philosophical orientations".[^o359][^o360] The *Investigations* also ranked 54th on a list of most influential twentieth-century works in [cognitive science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science) prepared by the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota)'s Center for Cognitive Sciences.[^o361]

Duncan J. Richter of the [Virginia Military Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Military_Institute), writing for the *[Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Encyclopedia_of_Philosophy)*, has described Wittgenstein as "one of the most influential philosophers of the twentieth century, and regarded by some as the most important since [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)."[^o362] [Peter Hacker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Hacker) argues that Wittgenstein's influence on 20th-century analytical philosophy can be attributed to his early influence on the Vienna Circle and later influence on the Oxford ["ordinary language" school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_language_philosophy) and Cambridge philosophers.[^o363] He is considered by some to be one of the greatest philosophers of the modern era.[^o364] But despite its deep influence on analytical philosophy, Wittgenstein's work did not always gain a positive reception. Argentine-Canadian philosopher [Mario Bunge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Bunge) asserts that "Wittgenstein is popular because he is trivial."[^o365]

### Scholarly interpretation {#Scholarly_interpretation}

There are many diverging interpretations of Wittgenstein's thought. In the words of his friend and colleague [Georg Henrik von Wright](wikipedia://en/12250):

He was of the opinion ... that his ideas were generally misunderstood and distorted even by those who professed to be his disciples. He doubted that he would be better understood in the future. He once said that he felt as though he were writing for people who would think in a quite different way, breathe a different air of life, from that of present-day men.[^n147]

Since Wittgenstein's death, scholarly interpretations of his philosophy have differed. Scholars have differed on the continuity between the so-called **early Wittgenstein** and the so-called **later** **Wittgenstein** (that is, the difference between his views expressed in the *Tractatus* and those in *Philosophical Investigations*), with some seeing the two as starkly disparate and others stressing the gradual transition between the two works through analysis of Wittgenstein's unpublished papers (the *[Nachlass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nachlass)*).[^o369]

#### The New Wittgenstein {#The_New_Wittgenstein}

One significant debate in Wittgenstein scholarship concerns the work of interpreters who are referred to under the banner of [The New Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Wittgenstein) school. They include [Cora Diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cora_Diamond), [Alice Crary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_Crary), and [James F. Conant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_F._Conant). While the *Tractatus*, particularly in its conclusion, seems paradoxical and self-undermining, New Wittgenstein scholars advance a "[therapeutic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutic_approach)" understanding of Wittgenstein's work – "an understanding of Wittgenstein as aspiring, not to advance metaphysical theories, but rather to help us work ourselves out of confusions we become entangled in when philosophizing."[^n148] To support this goal, the New Wittgenstein scholars propose a reading of the *Tractatus* as "plain nonsense" – arguing it does not attempt to convey a substantive philosophical project but instead simply tries to push the reader to abandon philosophical speculation. The therapeutic approach traces its roots to the philosophical work of [John Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wisdom)[^o372] and of [Oets Kolk Bouwsma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oets_Kolk_Bouwsma).[^o373][^o369]p. 54

The therapeutic approach is not without critics: [Hans-Johann Glock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Johann_Glock) argues that the "plain nonsense" reading of the *Tractatus* "is at odds with the external evidence, writings and conversations in which Wittgenstein states that the *Tractatus* is committed to the idea of ineffable insight."[^o369]p. 56 [Hans Sluga](wikipedia://en/1504912) and [Rupert Read](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Read) have advocated a "post-[therapeutic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapeutic_approach)" or "liberatory" interpretation of Wittgenstein.[^o374][^o375][^o376]

#### Bertrand Russell {#Bertrand_Russell}

Russell praised Wittgenstein's early work, but saw no value in his later work.[^n134] In an obituary notice, he wrote that "there was a lack of intellectual sympathy between us" during Wittgenstein's later years.[^n149] About the *Philosophical Investigations,* Russell stated:I have not found in \[it\] anything that seemed to me interesting and I do not understand why a whole school finds important wisdom in its pages. Psychologically this is surprising. The earlier Wittgenstein, whom I knew intimately, was a man addicted to passionately intense thinking, profoundly aware of difficult problems of which I, like him, felt the importance, and possessed (or at least so I thought) of true philosophical genius. The later Wittgenstein, on the contrary, seems to have grown tired of serious thinking and to have invented a doctrine which would make such an activity unnecessary. I do not for one moment believe that the doctrine which has these lazy consequences is true. I realize, however, that I have an overpoweringly strong bias against it, for, if it is true, philosophy is, at best, a slight help to lexicographers, and at worst, an idle tea-table amusement.[^o380]

#### Saul Kripke {#Saul_Kripke}

[Saul Kripke](wikipedia://en/48039)'s 1982 book *[Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittgenstein_on_Rules_and_Private_Language)* contends that the central argument of Wittgenstein's *[Philosophical Investigations](wikipedia://en/23674)* is a devastating [rule-following paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule-following_paradox) that undermines the possibility of ever following rules in our use of language. Kripke writes that this paradox is "the most radical and original sceptical problem that philosophy has seen to date".[^n150] Kripke's book generated a large secondary literature, divided between those who find his sceptical problem interesting and perceptive, and others such as [John McDowell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McDowell), [Stanley Cavell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Cavell), [Gordon Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Baker), [Peter Hacker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Hacker), [Colin McGinn](wikipedia://en/632230),[^o383] and [Peter Winch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Winch) who argue that his scepticism of meaning is a pseudo-problem that stems from a confused, selective reading of Wittgenstein. In a 2006 book, Cambridge philosopher [Martin Kusch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Kusch) defended Kripke's position against these and other attacks.[^o384]

#### Daniel Dennett {#Daniel_Dennett}

[Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett) wrote that, rather than solving all the major philosophical problems, as he had intended the *Tractatus* to do, Wittgenstein wrote "a book of such tantalizing perplexity and controversy that it stays on the required-reading list for centuries to come".[^o386]
