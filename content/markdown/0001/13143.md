# Generalized mean

*Image: Generalized means of 1, x.svg.* Plot of several generalized means $M_p(1, x)$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generalized mean"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **generalized means** (or **power mean** or **Hölder mean** from [Otto Hölder](wikipedia://en/191652))[^o20] are a family of functions for aggregating sets of numbers. These include as special cases the [Pythagorean means](wikipedia://en/3314539) ([arithmetic](wikipedia://en/612), [geometric](wikipedia://en/13046), and [harmonic](wikipedia://en/14463) [means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean)).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If $p$ is a non-zero [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), and $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571), then the **generalized mean** or **power mean** with exponent $p$ of these positive real numbers is[^o17][^o4]

$$M_p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \left( \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^p \right)^{{1}/{p}} .$$

(See [$p$-norm](wikipedia://en/990534#p-norm)). For $p = 0$ we set it equal to the geometric mean (which is the limit of means with exponents approaching zero, as proved below):

$$M_0(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i\right)^{1/n} .$$

Furthermore, for a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of positive weights $w_{i}$ we define the **weighted power mean** as[^o17]

$$M_p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^p}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i} \right)^{{1}/{p}}$$

and when $p = 0$, it is equal to the [weighted geometric mean](wikipedia://en/1232419):

$$M_0(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i}\right)^{1 / \sum_{i=1}^n w_i} .$$

The unweighted means correspond to setting all $w_{i} = 1$.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

For some values of $p$, the mean $M_p(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ corresponds to a well known mean.

::: {#o15 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof of \\lim_{p \\to 0} M_p = M_0 (geometric mean)"}

**Proof of $\lim_{p \to 0} M_p = M_0$ (geometric mean).**

For the purpose of the proof, we will assume without loss of generality that

$$w_i \in [0,1]$$

and

$$\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1.$$

We can rewrite the definition of $M_p$ using the exponential function as

$$M_p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \exp{\left( \ln{\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_{i}^p \right)^{1/p}\right]} \right) } = \exp{\left( \frac{\ln{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_{i}^p \right)}}{p} \right) }$$

In the limit $p \to 0$, we can apply [L'Hôpital's rule](wikipedia://en/18531) to the argument of the exponential function. We assume that $p \in \mathbb{R}$ but $p \ne 0$, and that the sum of $w_{i}$ is equal to 1 (without loss in generality);[^o16] differentiating the numerator and denominator with respect to $p$, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \lim_{p \to 0} \frac{\ln{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_{i}^p \right)}}{p} &= \lim_{p \to 0} \frac{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^p \ln{x_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_j^p}}{1} \\ &= \lim_{p \to 0} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^p \ln{x_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_j^p} \\ &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i \ln{x_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^n w_j} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n w_i \ln{x_i} \\ &= \ln{\left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} \right)} \end{aligned}$$

By the continuity of the exponential function, we can substitute back into the above relation to obtain

$$\lim_{p \to 0} M_p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \exp{\left( \ln{\left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} \right)} \right)} = \prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} = M_0(x_1,\dots,x_n)$$

as desired.[^o17]

## Properties {#Properties}

Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be a sequence of positive real numbers, then the following properties hold:[^o20]

1. $$\min(x_1, \dots, x_n) \le M_p(x_1, \dots, x_n) \le \max(x_1, \dots, x_n).$$
1. $M_p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = M_p(P(x_1, \dots, x_n))$, where $P$ is a permutation operator.
1. $$M_p(b x_1, \dots, b x_n) = b \cdot M_p(x_1, \dots, x_n).$$
1. $$M_p(x_1, \dots, x_{n \cdot k}) = M_p\left[M_p(x_1, \dots, x_{k}), M_p(x_{k + 1}, \dots, x_{2 \cdot k}), \dots, M_p(x_{(n - 1) \cdot k + 1}, \dots, x_{n \cdot k})\right].$$

::: {#o22 .statement cue=named-heading name="Generalized mean inequality"}

### Generalized mean inequality {#Generalized_mean_inequality}

In general, if $p < q$, then

$$M_p(x_1, \dots, x_n) \le M_q(x_1, \dots, x_n)$$

and the two means are equal [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $x_{1} = x_{2} = ... = x_{n}$.

The inequality is true for real values of $p$ and $q$, as well as positive and negative infinity values.

It follows from the fact that, for all real $p$,

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial p}M_p(x_1, \dots, x_n) \geq 0$$

which can be proved using [Jensen's inequality](wikipedia://en/297811).

In particular, for $p$ in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the generalized mean inequality implies the [Pythagorean means](wikipedia://en/3314539) inequality as well as the [inequality of arithmetic and geometric means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means).

:::

## Proof of the weighted inequality {#Proof_of_the_weighted_inequality}

We will prove the weighted power mean inequality. For the purpose of the proof we will assume the following [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000):

$$\begin{aligned} w_i \in [0, 1] \\ \sum_{i=1}^nw_i = 1 \end{aligned}$$

The proof for unweighted power means can be easily obtained by substituting $w_{i} = 1/n$.

### Equivalence of inequalities between means of opposite signs {#Equivalence_of_inequalities_between_means_of_opposite_signs}

Suppose an average between power means with exponents $p$ and $q$ holds:

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^p\right)^{1/p} \geq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^q\right)^{1/q}$$

applying this, then:

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{w_i}{x_i^p}\right)^{1/p} \geq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{w_i}{x_i^q}\right)^{1/q}$$

We raise both sides to the power of −1 (strictly decreasing function in positive reals):

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^nw_ix_i^{-p}\right)^{-1/p} = \left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^nw_i\frac{1}{x_i^p}}\right)^{1/p} \leq \left(\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^nw_i\frac{1}{x_i^q}}\right)^{1/q} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^nw_ix_i^{-q}\right)^{-1/q}$$

We get the inequality for means with exponents $-p$ and $-q$, and we can use the same reasoning backwards, thus proving the inequalities to be equivalent, which will be used in some of the later proofs.

### Geometric mean {#Geometric_mean}

For any $q > 0$ and non-negative weights summing to 1, the following inequality holds:

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^{-q}\right)^{-1/q} \leq \prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} \leq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^q\right)^{1/q}.$$

The proof follows from [Jensen's inequality](wikipedia://en/297811), making use of the fact the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) is concave:

$$\log \prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i\log x_i \leq \log \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i.$$

By applying the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) to both sides and observing that as a strictly increasing function it preserves the sign of the inequality, we get

$$\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} \leq \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i.$$

Taking $q$-th powers of the $x_{i}$ yields

$$\begin{aligned} &\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{q{\cdot}w_i} \leq \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^q \\ &\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} \leq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^q\right)^{1/q}.\end{aligned}$$

Thus, we are done for the inequality with positive $q$; the case for negatives is identical but for the swapped signs in the last step:

$$\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{-q{\cdot}w_i} \leq \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^{-q}.$$

Of course, taking each side to the power of a [negative number](wikipedia://en/154616) $-1/q$ swaps the direction of the inequality.

$$\prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} \geq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^{-q}\right)^{-1/q}.$$

::: {#o28 .statement cue=named-heading name="Inequality between any two power means"}

### Inequality between any two power means {#Inequality_between_any_two_power_means}

We are to prove that for any $p < q$ the following inequality holds:

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^p\right)^{1/p} \leq \left(\sum_{i=1}^nw_ix_i^q\right)^{1/q}$$

if $p$ is negative, and $q$ is positive, the inequality is equivalent to the one proved above:

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^nw_i x_i^p\right)^{1/p} \leq \prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{w_i} \leq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^q\right)^{1/q}$$

The proof for positive $p$ and $q$ is as follows: Define the following function: $f : \mathbf{R}_{+} \to \mathbf{R}_{+}$ $f(x)=x^{\frac{q}{p}}$. $f$ is a power function, so it does have a [second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219):

$$f''(x) = \left(\frac{q}{p} \right) \left( \frac{q}{p}-1 \right)x^{\frac{q}{p}-2}$$

which is strictly positive within the domain of $f$, since $q > p$, so we know $f$ is convex.

Using this, and the Jensen's inequality we get:

$$\begin{aligned} f \left( \sum_{i=1}^nw_ix_i^p \right) &\leq \sum_{i=1}^nw_if(x_i^p) \\[3pt] \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^p\right)^{q/p} &\leq \sum_{i=1}^nw_ix_i^q \end{aligned}$$

after raising both side to the power of $1/q$ (an increasing function, since $1/q$ is positive) we get the inequality which was to be proven:

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^p\right)^{1/p} \leq \left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i^q\right)^{1/q}$$

Using the previously shown equivalence we can prove the inequality for negative $p$ and $q$ by replacing them with $-q$ and $-p$, respectively.

:::

## Generalized *f*-mean {#Generalized_f-mean}

The power mean could be generalized further to the [generalized $f$-mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_f-mean):

$$M_f(x_1,\dots,x_n) = f^{-1} \left({\frac{1}{n}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^n{f(x_i)}}\right)$$

This covers the geometric mean without using a limit with $f(x) = \log(x)$. The power mean is obtained for $f(x) = x^{p}$. Properties of these means are studied in de Carvalho (2016).[^o4]

::: {#o31 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

### Signal processing {#Signal_processing}

A power mean serves a non-linear [moving average](wikipedia://en/990809) which is shifted towards small signal values for small $p$ and emphasizes big signal values for big $p$. Given an efficient implementation of a [moving arithmetic mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lowpass) called `smooth` one can implement a moving power mean according to the following [Haskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskell_%28programming_language%29) code.

```haskell
powerSmooth :: Floating a => ([a] -> [a]) -> a -> [a] -> [a]
powerSmooth smooth p = map (** recip p) . smooth . map (**p)
```

- For big $p$ it can serve as an [envelope detector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envelope_detector) on a [rectified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifier) signal.
- For small $p$ it can serve as a [baseline detector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseline_%28spectrometry%29) on a [mass spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrum).
