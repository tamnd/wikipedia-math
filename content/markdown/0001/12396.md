# Group homomorphism

*Image: Group homomorphism ver.2.svg.* Depiction of a group homomorphism (*h*) from *G* (left) to *H* (right). The oval inside *H* is the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of *h*. *N* is the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240#Group_homomorphisms) of *h* and *aN* is a [coset](wikipedia://en/98759) of *N*.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Group homomorphism"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), given two [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), (*G*,∗) and (*H*, ·), a **group homomorphism** from (*G*,∗) to (*H*, ·) is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *h* : *G* → *H* such that for all *u* and *v* in *G* it holds that

:::

$$h(u*v) = h(u) \cdot h(v)$$

where the group operation on the left side of the equation is that of *G* and on the right side that of *H*.

From this property, one can deduce that *h* maps the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) *e<sub>G</sub>* of *G* to the identity element *e<sub>H</sub>* of *H*,

$$h(e_G) = e_H$$

and it also maps inverses to inverses in the sense that

$$h\left(u^{-1}\right) = h(u)^{-1}. \,$$

Hence one can say that *h* "is compatible with the group structure".

In areas of mathematics where one considers groups endowed with additional structure, a *homomorphism* sometimes means a map that respects not only the group structure (as above) but also the extra structure. For example, a homomorphism of [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315) is often required to be [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122).

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Let $e_{H}$ be the identity element of the group (*H*, ·) and $u \in G$, then

$$h(u) \cdot e_{H} = h(u) = h(u*e_{G}) = h(u) \cdot h(e_{G})$$

Now by multiplying by the inverse of $h(u)$ (or applying the cancellation rule) we obtain

$$e_{H} = h(e_{G})$$

Similarly,

$$e_H = h(e_G) = h(u*u^{-1}) = h(u)\cdot h(u^{-1})$$

Therefore, by the uniqueness of the inverse: $h(u^{-1}) = h(u)^{-1}$.

:::

## Types {#Types}

**[Monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538)**

A group homomorphism that is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) (or, one-to-one); i.e., preserves distinctness.

**[Epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539)**

A group homomorphism that is [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873) (or, onto); i.e., reaches every point in the codomain.

**[Isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397)**

A group homomorphism that is [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942); i.e., injective and surjective. Its inverse is also a group homomorphism. In this case, the groups *G* and *H* are called *isomorphic*; they differ only in the notation of their elements (except of identity element) and are identical for all practical purposes. I.e. we re-label all elements except identity.

**[Endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569)**

A group homomorphism, *h*: *G* → *G*; the domain and codomain are the same. Also called an endomorphism of *G*.

**[Automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160)**

A group endomorphism that is bijective, and hence an isomorphism. The set of all [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of a group *G*, with [functional composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_composition) as operation, itself forms a group, the *automorphism group* of *G*. It is denoted by Aut(*G*). As an example, the automorphism group of (**Z**, +) contains only two elements, the identity transformation and multiplication with −1; it is isomorphic to (**Z**/2**Z**, +).

## Image and kernel {#Image_and_kernel}

We define the *[kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of h* to be the set of elements in *G* that are mapped to the identity in *H*

$$\operatorname{ker}(h) := \left\{u \in G\colon h(u) = e_{H}\right\}.$$

and the *[image](wikipedia://en/579311) of h* to be

$$\operatorname{im}(h) := h(G) \equiv \left\{h(u)\colon u \in G\right\}.$$

The kernel and image of a homomorphism can be interpreted as measuring how close it is to being an isomorphism. The [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorem) states that the image of a group homomorphism, *h*(*G*) is isomorphic to the quotient group *G*/ker *h*.

The kernel of *h* is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of *G*. Assume $u \in \operatorname{ker}(h)$ and show $g^{-1} \circ u \circ g \in \operatorname{ker}(h)$ for arbitrary $u, g$:

$$\begin{aligned} h\left(g^{-1} \circ u \circ g\right) &= h(g)^{-1} \cdot h(u) \cdot h(g) \\ &= h(g)^{-1} \cdot e_H \cdot h(g) \\ &= h(g)^{-1} \cdot h(g) = e_H, \end{aligned}$$

The image of *h* is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of *H*.

The homomorphism, *h*, is a *group monomorphism*; i.e., *h* is injective (one-to-one) if and only if ker(*h*) = {*e*<sub>*G*</sub>}. Injectivity directly gives that there is a unique element in the kernel, and, conversely, a unique element in the kernel gives injectivity:

$$\begin{aligned} && h(g_1) &= h(g_2) \\ \Leftrightarrow && h(g_1) \cdot h(g_2)^{-1} &= e_H \\ \Leftrightarrow && h\left(g_1 \circ g_2^{-1}\right) &= e_H,\ \operatorname{ker}(h) = \{e_G\} \\ \Rightarrow && g_1 \circ g_2^{-1} &= e_G \\ \Leftrightarrow && g_1 &= g_2 \end{aligned}$$

## Examples {#Examples}

- Consider the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) Z${}_{3}$ = (**Z**/3**Z**, +) = ({0, 1, 2}, +) and the group of integers (**Z**, +). The map *h* : **Z** → **Z**/3**Z** with *h*(*u*) = *u* [mod](wikipedia://en/20087) 3 is a group homomorphism. It is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) and its kernel consists of all integers that are divisible by 3.

The set $G \equiv \left\{\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \bigg| a > 0, b \in \mathbf{R}\right\}$ forms a group under [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). For any [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) *u*, the function *f<sub>u</sub>* : *G* → **C<sup>\*</sup>** defined by $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \mapsto a^u$ is a group homomorphism.; Consider a multiplicative group of [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) (**R**<sup>\+</sup>, ⋅). For any complex number *u*, the function *f<sub>u</sub>* : **R**<sup>\+</sup> → **C<sup>\*</sup>** defined by $f_u(a) = a^u$ is a group homomorphism.

- The [exponential map](wikipedia://en/9678) yields a group homomorphism from the group of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) **R** with addition to the group of non-zero real numbers **R**\* with multiplication. The kernel is {0} and the image consists of the positive real numbers.
- The exponential map also yields a group homomorphism from the group of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) **C** with addition to the group of non-zero complex numbers **C**\* with multiplication. This map is surjective and has the kernel {2π*ki* : *k* ∈ **Z**}, as can be seen from [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613). Fields like **R** and **C** that have homomorphisms from their additive group to their multiplicative group are thus called [exponential fields](wikipedia://en/21313650).
- The function $\Phi: (\mathbb{Z}, +) \rightarrow (\mathbb{R}, +)$, defined by $\Phi(x) = \sqrt[]{2}x$ is a homomorphism.
- Consider the two groups $(\mathbb{R}^+, *)$ and $(\mathbb{R}, +)$, represented respectively by $G$ and $H$, where $\mathbb{R}^+$ is the positive real numbers. Then, the function $f: G \rightarrow H$ defined by the [logarithm function](wikipedia://en/17860) is a homomorphism.

:::

## Category of groups {#Category_of_groups}

If *h* : *G* → *H* and *k* : *H* → *K* are group homomorphisms, then so is *k* ∘ *h* : *G* → *K*. This shows that the [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of all groups, together with group homomorphisms as morphisms, forms a [category](wikipedia://en/5869) (specifically the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092)).

## Homomorphisms of abelian groups {#Homomorphisms_of_abelian_groups}

If *G* and *H* are [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) (i.e., commutative) groups, then the set Hom(*G*, *H*) of all group homomorphisms from *G* to *H* is itself an abelian group: the sum *h* + *k* of two homomorphisms is defined by

(*h* + *k*)(*u*) = *h*(*u*) + *k*(*u*) for all *u* in *G*.

The commutativity of *H* is needed to prove that *h* + *k* is again a group homomorphism.[^n1]

The addition of homomorphisms is compatible with the composition of homomorphisms in the following sense: if *f* is in Hom(*K*, *G*), *h*, *k* are elements of Hom(*G*, *H*), and *g* is in Hom(*H*, *L*), then

(*h* + *k*) ∘ *f* = (*h* ∘ *f*) + (*k* ∘ *f*) and *g* ∘ (*h* + *k*) = (*g* ∘ *h*) + (*g* ∘ *k*).

Since the composition is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), this shows that the set End(*G*) of all endomorphisms of an abelian group forms a [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29), the *[endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623)* of *G*. For example, the endomorphism ring of the abelian group consisting of the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/106341) of *m* copies of **Z**/*n***Z** is isomorphic to the ring of *m*-by-*m* [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with entries in **Z**/*n***Z**. The above compatibility also shows that the category of all abelian groups with group homomorphisms forms a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521); the existence of direct sums and well-behaved kernels makes this category the prototypical example of an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063).
