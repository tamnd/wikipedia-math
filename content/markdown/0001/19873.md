# Measure (mathematics)

*Image: Measure illustration (Vector).svg.* Informally, a measure has the property of being [monotone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function) in the sense that if $A$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $B,$ the measure of $A$ is less than or equal to the measure of $B.$ Furthermore, the measure of the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is required to be 0. A simple example is a volume (how much space an object occupies) as a measure.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Measure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the concept of a **measure** is a generalization and formalization of [geometrical measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrical_measures) ([length](wikipedia://en/18542), [area](wikipedia://en/1209), [volume](wikipedia://en/32498)) and other common notions, such as [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301), [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass), and [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) of events. These seemingly distinct concepts have many similarities and can often be treated together in a single mathematical context. Measures are foundational in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), [integration theory](wikipedia://en/15532), and can be generalized to assume [negative values](wikipedia://en/2985811), as with [electrical charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_charge). Far-reaching generalizations (such as [spectral measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_measure) and [positive operator-valued measures](wikipedia://en/3793084)) of measure are widely used in [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics) and physics in general.

:::

The intuition behind this concept dates back to [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), when [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) tried to calculate the [area of a circle](wikipedia://en/1896782).[^o2][^o3] But it was not until the late 19th and early 20th centuries that measure theory became a branch of mathematics. The foundations of modern measure theory were laid in the works of [Émile Borel](wikipedia://en/356161), [Henri Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/49514), [Nikolai Luzin](wikipedia://en/576228), [Johann Radon](wikipedia://en/339331), [Constantin Carathéodory](wikipedia://en/470625), and [Maurice Fréchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Fréchet), among others. According to [Thomas W. Hawkins Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_W._Hawkins_Jr.), "It was primarily through the theory of [multiple integrals](wikipedia://en/2757224) and, in particular the work of [Camille Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931) that the importance of the notion of measurability was first recognized."[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Countable additivity of a measure.svg.* Countable additivity of a measure $\mu$: The measure of a countable disjoint union is the same as the sum of all measures of each subset.

Let $X$ be a set and $\Sigma$ a [σ-algebra](wikipedia://en/29586) over $X$, defining subsets of $X$ that are "measurable". A [set function](wikipedia://en/25911217) $\mu$ from $\Sigma$ to the interval $[0,+\infty]$, that is, the non-negative real number line together with new (so-called infinite) value $+\infty$, used to denote elements greater than all other (so-called finite) elements, is called a **measure** if the following conditions hold:

- $$\mu(\varnothing) = 0$$
- **Countable additivity** (or [σ-additivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_additivity)): For all [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) collections $\{ E_k \}_{k=1}^\infty$ of pairwise [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) in $\Sigma$, $$\mu{\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^\infty E_k\right)} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mu(E_k)$$

If at least one set $E$ has finite measure, then the requirement $\mu(\varnothing) = 0$ is met automatically due to countable additivity:

$$\mu(E)=\mu(E \cup \varnothing) = \mu(E) + \mu(\varnothing),$$

and therefore $\mu(\varnothing)=0.$

Note that any sum involving $+\infty$ will equal $+\infty$, that is, $a + \infty = +\infty$ for all $a$ in the extended reals.

If the condition of non-negativity is dropped, and $\mu(E)$ only ever equals one of $+\infty$, $-\infty$, i.e. no two distinct sets have measures $+\infty$, $-\infty$, respectively, then $\mu$ is called a *[signed measure](wikipedia://en/2985811)*.

The pair $(X, \Sigma)$ is called a *[measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333)*, and the members of $\Sigma$ are called **measurable sets**.

A [triple](wikipedia://en/132729) $(X, \Sigma, \mu)$ is called a *[measure space](wikipedia://en/45270)*. A [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) is a measure with total measure one – that is, $\mu(X) = 1.$ A [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) is a measure space with a probability measure.

For measure spaces that are also [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) various compatibility conditions can be placed for the measure and the topology. Most measures met in practice in [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_%28mathematics%29) (and in many cases also in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542)) are [Radon measures](wikipedia://en/521933) (usually defined on [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637)). When working with [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) Hausdorff spaces, Radon measures have an alternative, equivalent definition in terms of linear functionals on the [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844) of [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) with [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support). This approach is taken by [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) (2004) and a number of other sources. For more details, see the article on [Radon measures](wikipedia://en/521933).

:::

## Instances {#Instances}

Measures (measure theory) Some important measures are listed here.

- The [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786) is defined by $\mu(S)$ = number of elements in $S.$
- The [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on $\mathbb{R}$ is a [complete](wikipedia://en/44775) [translation-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_invariance) measure on a *σ*-algebra containing the [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172) in $\mathbb{R}$ such that $\mu([0, 1]) = 1$; and every other measure with these properties extends the Lebesgue measure.
- The [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) of an interval on the unit circle in the Euclidean plane extends to a measure on the $\sigma$-algebra they generate. It can be called angle measure since the arc length of an interval equals the angle it supports. This measure is invariant under [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) preserving the circle. Similarly, [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) measure is invariant under [squeeze mapping](wikipedia://en/1140043).
- The [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) for a [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315). For example, $\mathbb R$ is such a group and its Haar measure is the Lebesgue measure; for the unit circle (seen as a subgroup of the multiplicative group of $\mathbb C$) its Haar measure is the angle measure. For a [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) the counting measure is a Haar measure.
- Every (pseudo) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) $(M,g)$ has a canonical measure $\mu_g$ that in local coordinates $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ looks like $\sqrt{\left|\det g \right|}d^nx$ where $d^nx$ is the usual Lebesgue measure.
- The [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109) is a generalization of the Lebesgue measure to sets with non-integer dimension, in particular, fractal sets.
- Every [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) gives rise to a measure which takes the value 1 on the whole space (and therefore takes all its values in the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) \[0, 1\]). Such a measure is called a *probability measure* or *distribution*. See the [list of probability distributions](wikipedia://en/2879918) for instances.
- The [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) *δ*<sub>*a*</sub> (cf. [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021)) is given by *δ*<sub>*a*</sub>(*S*) = *χ*<sub>*S*</sub>(a), where *χ*<sub>*S*</sub> is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $S.$ The measure of a set is 1 if it contains the point $a$ and 0 otherwise.

Other 'named' measures used in various theories include: [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322), [Jordan measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_measure), [ergodic measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_measure), [Gaussian measure](wikipedia://en/6572909), [Baire measure](wikipedia://en/13520509), [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933), [Young measure](wikipedia://en/9659484), and [Loeb measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loeb_measure).

In physics an example of a measure is spatial distribution of [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) (see for example, [gravity potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_potential)), or another non-negative [extensive property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extensive_property), [conserved](wikipedia://en/2458485) (see [conservation law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29) for a list of these) or not. Negative values lead to signed measures, see "generalizations" below.

- [Liouville measure](wikipedia://en/312301#Symplectic_geometry), known also as the natural volume form on a symplectic manifold, is useful in classical statistical and Hamiltonian mechanics.
- [Gibbs measure](wikipedia://en/3085914) is widely used in statistical mechanics, often under the name [canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1137672).

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

Let $\mu$ be a measure.

### Monotonicity {#Monotonicity}

If $E_1$ and $E_2$ are measurable sets with $E_1 \subseteq E_2$ then

$$\mu(E_1) \leq \mu(E_2).$$

### Measure of countable unions and intersections {#Measure_of_countable_unions_and_intersections}

#### Countable subadditivity {#Countable_subadditivity}

For any [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29) $E_1, E_2, E_3, \ldots$ of (not necessarily disjoint) measurable sets $E_n$ in $\Sigma:$

$$\mu\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_i\right) \leq \sum_{i=1}^\infty \mu(E_i).$$

#### Continuity from below {#Continuity_from_below}

If $E_1, E_2, E_3, \ldots$ are measurable sets that are increasing (meaning that $E_1 \subseteq E_2 \subseteq E_3 \subseteq \ldots$) then the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the sets $E_n$ is measurable and

$$\mu\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_i\right) ~=~ \lim_{i\to\infty} \mu(E_i) = \sup_{i \geq 1} \mu(E_i).$$

#### Continuity from above {#Continuity_from_above}

If $E_1, E_2, E_3, \ldots$ are measurable sets that are decreasing (meaning that $E_1 \supseteq E_2 \supseteq E_3 \supseteq \ldots$) then the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of the sets $E_n$ is measurable; furthermore, if at least one of the $E_n$ has finite measure then

$$\mu\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^\infty E_i\right) = \lim_{i\to\infty} \mu(E_i) = \inf_{i \geq 1} \mu(E_i).$$

This property is false without the assumption that at least one of the $E_n$ has finite measure. For instance, for each $n \in \mathbb{N},$ let $E_n = [n, \infty) \subseteq \mathbb{R},$ which all have infinite Lebesgue measure, but the intersection is empty.

:::

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

### Completeness {#Completeness}

A measurable set $X$ is called a *[null set](wikipedia://en/21520)* if $\mu(X) = 0.$ A subset of a null set is called a *negligible set*. A negligible set need not be measurable, but every measurable negligible set is automatically a null set. A measure is called *complete* if every negligible set is measurable.

A measure can be extended to a complete one by considering the σ-algebra of subsets $Y$ which differ by a negligible set from a measurable set $X,$ that is, such that the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of $X$ and $Y$ is contained in a null set. One defines $\mu(Y)$ to equal $\mu(X).$

### "Dropping the edge" {#"Dropping_the_edge"}

If $f:X\to[0,+\infty]$ is $(\Sigma,{\cal B}([0,+\infty]))$-measurable, then

$$\mu\{x\in X: f(x) \geq t\} = \mu\{x\in X: f(x) > t\}$$

for [almost all](wikipedia://en/44688) $t \in [-\infty,\infty].$[^o18] This property is used in connection with [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288).

::: {#o19 .proof cue=math-proof}

Both $F(t) := \mu\{x\in X : f(x) > t\}$ and $G(t) := \mu\{x\in X : f(x) \geq t\}$ are monotonically non-increasing functions of $t,$ so both of them have [at most countably many discontinuities](wikipedia://en/22278053) and thus they are continuous almost everywhere, relative to the Lebesgue measure. If $t < 0$ then $\{x\in X : f(x) \geq t\} = X = \{x\in X : f(x) > t\},$ so that $F(t) = G(t),$ as desired.

If $t$ is such that $\mu\{x\in X : f(x) > t\} = +\infty$ then monotonicity implies

$$\mu\{x\in X : f(x) \geq t\} = +\infty,$$

so that $F(t) = G(t),$ as required. If $\mu\{x\in X : f(x) > t\} = +\infty$ for all $t$ then we are done, so assume otherwise. Then there is a unique $t_0 \in \{-\infty\} \cup [0,+\infty)$ such that $F$ is infinite to the left of $t$ (which can only happen when $t_0 \geq 0$) and finite to the right. Arguing as above, $\mu\{x\in X : f(x) \geq t\} = +\infty$ when $t < t_0.$ Similarly, if $t_0 \geq 0$ and $F\left(t_0\right) = +\infty$ then $F\left(t_0\right) = G\left(t_0\right).$

For $t > t_0,$ let $t_n$ be a monotonically non-decreasing sequence converging to $t.$ The monotonically non-increasing sequences $\{x\in X : f(x) > t_n\}$ of members of $\Sigma$ has at least one finitely $\mu$-measurable component, and

$$\{x\in X : f(x) \geq t\} = \bigcap_n \{x\in X : f(x) > t_n\}.$$

Continuity from above guarantees that

$$\mu\{x\in X : f(x) \geq t\} = \lim_{t_n \uparrow t} \mu\{x\in X : f(x) > t_n\}.$$

The right-hand side $\lim_{t_n \uparrow t} F\left(t_n\right)$ then equals $F(t) = \mu\{x\in X : f(x) > t\}$ if $t$ is a point of continuity of $F.$ Since $F$ is continuous almost everywhere, this completes the proof.

:::

### Additivity {#Additivity}

Measures are required to be countably additive. However, the condition can be strengthened as follows. For any set $I$ and any set of nonnegative $r_i$ where $i\in I$ define:

$$\sum_{i\in I} r_i=\sup\left\lbrace\sum_{i\in J} r_i : |J|<\infty, J\subseteq I\right\rbrace.$$

That is, we define the sum of the $r_i$ to be the supremum of all the sums of finitely many of them.

A measure $\mu$ on $\Sigma$ is $\kappa$-additive if for any $\lambda<\kappa$ and any family of disjoint sets $X_\alpha,\alpha<\lambda$ the following hold:

$$\bigcup_{\alpha\in\lambda} X_\alpha \in \Sigma$$

$$\mu\left(\bigcup_{\alpha\in\lambda} X_\alpha\right) = \sum_{\alpha\in\lambda}\mu\left(X_\alpha\right).$$

The second condition is equivalent to the statement that the [ideal](wikipedia://en/12633368) of null sets is $\kappa$-complete.

### Sigma-finite measures {#Sigma-finite_measures}

A measure space $(X, \Sigma, \mu)$ is called finite if $\mu(X)$ is a finite real number (rather than $\infty$).[^o22] A measure $\mu$ is called *σ-finite* if $X$ can be decomposed into a countable union of measurable sets of finite measure.[^o23]

For example, the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) with the standard [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) are σ-finite but not finite.[^o23] The natural numbers are also σ-finite with respect to [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786).[^o23] However, the real numbers are not σ-finite with respect to counting measure.[^o23]

### Semifinite measures {#Semifinite_measures}

Let $X$ be a set, let ${\cal A}$ be a sigma-algebra on $X,$ and let $\mu$ be a measure on ${\cal A}.$ We say $\mu$ is **semifinite** to mean that for all $A\in\mu^\text{pre}\{+\infty\},$ ${\cal P}(A)\cap\mu^\text{pre}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\ne\emptyset.$[^n1]

Semifinite measures generalize sigma-finite measures, in such a way that some big theorems of measure theory that hold for sigma-finite but not arbitrary measures can be extended with little modification to hold for semifinite measures. (To-do: add examples of such theorems; cf. the talk page.)

#### Basic examples {#Basic_examples}

- Every sigma-finite measure is semifinite.
- Assume ${\cal A}={\cal P}(X),$ let $f:X\to[0,+\infty],$ and assume $\mu(A)=\sum_{a\in A}f(a)$ for all $A\subseteq X.$
  - We have that $\mu$ is sigma-finite if and only if $f(x)<+\infty$ for all $x\in X$ and $f^\text{pre}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})$ is countable. We have that $\mu$ is semifinite if and only if $f(x)<+\infty$ for all $x\in X.$[^n2]
  - Taking $f=X\times\{1\}$ above (so that $\mu$ is counting measure on ${\cal P}(X)$), we see that counting measure on ${\cal P}(X)$ is
    - sigma-finite if and only if $X$ is countable; and
    - semifinite (without regard to whether $X$ is countable). (Thus, counting measure, on the power set ${\cal P}(X)$ of an arbitrary uncountable set $X,$ gives an example of a semifinite measure that is not sigma-finite.)
- Let $d$ be a complete, separable metric on $X,$ let ${\cal B}$ be the [Borel sigma-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma-algebra) induced by $d,$ and let $s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.$ Then the [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109) ${\cal H}^s|{\cal B}$ is semifinite.[^n3]
- Let $d$ be a complete, separable metric on $X,$ let ${\cal B}$ be the [Borel sigma-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma-algebra) induced by $d,$ and let $s\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.$ Then the [packing measure](wikipedia://en/13844097#Definitions) ${\cal H}^s|{\cal B}$ is semifinite.[^n4]

#### Involved example {#Involved_example}

The zero measure is sigma-finite and thus semifinite. In addition, the zero measure is clearly less than or equal to $\mu.$ It can be shown there is a greatest measure with these two properties:

::: {#o33 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (semifinite part)"}

**Theorem (semifinite part)[^n5].**

For any measure $\mu$ on ${\cal A},$ there exists, among semifinite measures on ${\cal A}$ that are less than or equal to $\mu,$ a [greatest](wikipedia://en/663041) element $\mu_\text{sf}.$

:::

We say the **semifinite part** of $\mu$ to mean the semifinite measure $\mu_\text{sf}$ defined in the above theorem. We give some nice, explicit formulas, which some authors may take as definition, for the semifinite part:

- $\mu_\text{sf}=(\sup\{\mu(B):B\in{\cal P}(A)\cap\mu^\text{pre}(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0})\})_{A\in{\cal A}}.$[^n5]
- $\mu_\text{sf}=(\sup\{\mu(A\cap B):B\in\mu^\text{pre}(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0})\})_{A\in{\cal A}}\}.$[^n6]
- $\mu_\text{sf}=\mu|_{\mu^\text{pre}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}\cup\{A\in{\cal A}:\sup\{\mu(B):B\in{\cal P}(A)\}=+\infty\}\times\{+\infty\}\cup\{A\in{\cal A}:\sup\{\mu(B):B\in{\cal P}(A)\}<+\infty\}\times\{0\}.$[^n7]

Since $\mu_\text{sf}$ is semifinite, it follows that if $\mu=\mu_\text{sf}$ then $\mu$ is semifinite. It is also evident that if $\mu$ is semifinite then $\mu=\mu_\text{sf}.$

::: {#o38 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

#### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

Every *$0-\infty$ measure* that is not the zero measure is not semifinite. (Here, we say *$0-\infty$ measure* to mean a measure whose range lies in $\{0,+\infty\}$: $(\forall A\in{\cal A})(\mu(A)\in\{0,+\infty\}).$) Below we give examples of $0-\infty$ measures that are not zero measures.

- Let $X$ be nonempty, let ${\cal A}$ be a $\sigma$-algebra on $X,$ let $f:X\to\{0,+\infty\}$ be not the zero function, and let $\mu=(\sum_{x\in A}f(x))_{A\in{\cal A}}.$ It can be shown that $\mu$ is a measure.
  - $\mu=\{(\emptyset,0)\}\cup({\cal A}\setminus\{\emptyset\})\times\{+\infty\}.$[^n8]
    - $X=\{0\},$ ${\cal A}=\{\emptyset,X\},$ $\mu=\{(\emptyset,0),(X,+\infty)\}.$[^n9]
- Let $X$ be uncountable, let ${\cal A}$ be a $\sigma$-algebra on $X,$ let ${\cal C}=\{A\in{\cal A}:A\text{ is countable}\}$ be the countable elements of ${\cal A},$ and let $\mu={\cal C}\times\{0\}\cup({\cal A}\setminus{\cal C})\times\{+\infty\}.$ It can be shown that $\mu$ is a measure.[^n1]

:::

#### Involved non-example {#Involved_non-example}

::: {#o46 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Luther decomposition)"}

**Theorem (Luther decomposition)[^n10][^n11].**

For any measure $\mu$ on ${\cal A},$ there exists a $0-\infty$ measure $\xi$ on ${\cal A}$ such that $\mu=\nu+\xi$ for some semifinite measure $\nu$ on ${\cal A}.$ In fact, among such measures $\xi,$ there exists a [least](wikipedia://en/663041) measure $\mu_{0-\infty}.$ Also, we have $\mu=\mu_\text{sf}+\mu_{0-\infty}.$

:::

We say the **$\mathbf{0-\infty}$ part** of $\mu$ to mean the measure $\mu_{0-\infty}$ defined in the above theorem. Here is an explicit formula for $\mu_{0-\infty}$: $\mu_{0-\infty}=(\sup\{\mu(B)-\mu_\text{sf}(B):B\in{\cal P}(A)\cap\mu_\text{sf}^\text{pre}(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0})\})_{A\in{\cal A}}.$

#### Results regarding semifinite measures {#Results_regarding_semifinite_measures}

- Let $\mathbb F$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C},$ and let $T:L_\mathbb{F}^\infty(\mu)\to\left(L_\mathbb{F}^1(\mu)\right)^*:g\mapsto T_g=\left(\int fgd\mu\right)_{f\in L_\mathbb{F}^1(\mu)}.$ Then $\mu$ is semifinite if and only if $T$ is injective.[^n12][^n13] (This result has import in the study of the [dual space of $L^1=L_\mathbb{F}^1(\mu)$](wikipedia://en/45194#Dual_spaces).)
- Let $\mathbb F$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C},$ and let ${\cal T}$ be the topology of convergence in measure on $L_\mathbb{F}^0(\mu).$ Then $\mu$ is semifinite if and only if ${\cal T}$ is Hausdorff.[^n14][^n15]
- (Johnson) Let $X$ be a set, let ${\cal A}$ be a sigma-algebra on $X,$ let $\mu$ be a measure on ${\cal A},$ let $Y$ be a set, let ${\cal B}$ be a sigma-algebra on $Y,$ and let $\nu$ be a measure on ${\cal B}.$ If $\mu,\nu$ are both not a $0-\infty$ measure, then both $\mu$ and $\nu$ are semifinite if and only if [$(\mu\times_\text{cld}\nu)$](wikipedia://en/2986564)$(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)$ for all $A\in{\cal A}$ and $B\in{\cal B}.$ (Here, $\mu\times_\text{cld}\nu$ is the measure defined in Theorem 39.1 in Berberian '65.[^n16])

### Localizable measures {#Localizable_measures}

Localizable measures are a special case of semifinite measures and a generalization of sigma-finite measures.

Let $X$ be a set, let ${\cal A}$ be a sigma-algebra on $X,$ and let $\mu$ be a measure on ${\cal A}.$

- Let $\mathbb F$ be $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C},$ and let $T : L_\mathbb{F}^\infty(\mu) \to \left(L_\mathbb{F}^1(\mu)\right)^* : g \mapsto T_g = \left(\int fgd\mu\right)_{f\in L_\mathbb{F}^1(\mu)}.$ Then $\mu$ is localizable if and only if $T$ is bijective (if and only if $L_\mathbb{F}^\infty(\mu)$ "is" $L_\mathbb{F}^1(\mu)^*$).[^n17][^n13]

### s-finite measures {#s-finite_measures}

A measure is said to be s-finite if it is a countable sum of finite measures. S-finite measures are more general than sigma-finite ones and have applications in the theory of [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes).

## Non-measurable sets {#Non-measurable_sets}

If the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) is assumed to be true, it can be proved that not all subsets of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) are [Lebesgue measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_measurable); examples of such sets include the [Vitali set](wikipedia://en/424189), and the non-measurable sets postulated by the [Hausdorff paradox](wikipedia://en/634759) and the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

For certain purposes, it is useful to have a "measure" whose values are not restricted to the non-negative reals or infinity. For instance, a countably additive [set function](wikipedia://en/25911217) with values in the (signed) real numbers is called a *[signed measure](wikipedia://en/2985811)*, while such a function with values in the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) is called a *[complex measure](wikipedia://en/3057532)*. Observe, however, that complex measure is necessarily of finite [variation](wikipedia://en/683561), hence complex measures include [finite signed measures](wikipedia://en/4316185) but not, for example, the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198).

Measures that take values in [Banach spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_spaces) have been studied extensively.[^o60] A measure that takes values in the set of self-adjoint projections on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) is called a *[projection-valued measure](wikipedia://en/1046155)*; these are used in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) for the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495). When it is necessary to distinguish the usual measures which take non-negative values from generalizations, the term **positive measure** is used. Positive measures are closed under [conical combination](wikipedia://en/19634578) but not general [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632), while signed measures are the linear closure of positive measures. More generally see [measure theory in topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/79849108).

Another generalization is the *finitely additive measure*, also known as a [content](wikipedia://en/9999200). This is the same as a measure except that instead of requiring *countable* additivity we require only *finite* additivity. Historically, this definition was used first. It turns out that in general, finitely additive measures are connected with notions such as [Banach limits](wikipedia://en/2591539), the dual of [$L^\infty$](wikipedia://en/45194) and the [Stone–Čech compactification](wikipedia://en/52897). All these are linked in one way or another to the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840). Contents remain useful in certain technical problems in [geometric measure theory](wikipedia://en/12169694); this is the theory of [Banach measures](wikipedia://en/1339598).

A *charge* is a generalization in both directions: it is a finitely additive, signed measure.[^o61] (Cf. [ba space](wikipedia://en/629005) for information about *bounded* charges, where we say a charge is *bounded* to mean its range is a bounded subset of *R*.)
