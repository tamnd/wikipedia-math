# Functional analysis

*Image: Drum vibration mode12.gif.* One of the possible modes of [vibration of a circular membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibration_of_a_circular_membrane). These modes are [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of a linear operator on a function space, a common construction in functional analysis.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Functional analysis"}

**Functional analysis** is a branch of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the core of which is formed by the study of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) endowed with some kind of limit-related structure (for example, [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856#Definition), [norm](wikipedia://en/990534#Definition), or [topology](wikipedia://en/30450#Definitions)) and the [linear functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) defined on these spaces and suitably respecting these structures. The historical roots of functional analysis lie in the study of [spaces of functions](wikipedia://en/340630) and the formulation of properties of transformations of functions such as the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) as transformations defining, for example, [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) or [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999) operators between function spaces. This point of view turned out to be particularly useful for the study of [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations) and [integral equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_equations).

:::

The usage of the word *[functional](wikipedia://en/1374948)* as a noun goes back to the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882), implying a [function whose argument is a function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_function). The term was first used in [Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675)'s 1910 book on that subject. However, the general concept of a functional had previously been introduced in 1887 by the Italian mathematician and physicist [Vito Volterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vito_Volterra).[^o2][^o3] The theory of nonlinear functionals was continued by students of Hadamard, in particular [Fréchet](wikipedia://en/290931) and [Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462). Hadamard also founded the modern school of linear functional analysis further developed by [Riesz](wikipedia://en/1073126) and the [group](wikipedia://en/1024328) of [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) mathematicians around [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646).

In modern introductory texts on functional analysis, the subject is seen as the study of vector spaces endowed with a topology, in particular [infinite-dimensional spaces](wikipedia://en/38267).[^o4][^o5] In contrast, [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) deals mostly with finite-dimensional spaces, and does not use topology. An important part of functional analysis is the extension of the theories of [measure](wikipedia://en/19873), [integration](wikipedia://en/15532), and [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) to infinite-dimensional spaces, also known as **infinite dimensional analysis**.

## Normed vector spaces {#Normed_vector_spaces}

The basic and historically first class of spaces studied in functional analysis are [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) [normed vector spaces](wikipedia://en/21538) over the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). Such spaces are called [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). An important example is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), where the norm arises from an inner product. These spaces are of fundamental importance in many areas, including the [mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/20728), [machine learning](wikipedia://en/651196), [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations), and [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659).

More generally, functional analysis includes the study of [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801) and other [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) not endowed with a norm.

An important object of study in functional analysis are the [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) defined on Banach and Hilbert spaces. These lead naturally to the definition of [C\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184) and other [operator algebras](wikipedia://en/455987).

### Hilbert spaces {#Hilbert_spaces}

[Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) can be completely classified: there is a unique Hilbert space [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) for every [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6173) of the [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063).[^o8] Finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces are fully understood in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), and infinite-dimensional [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) Hilbert spaces are isomorphic to [$\ell^{\,2}(\aleph_0)\,$](wikipedia://en/1701075#ℓp_spaces). Separability being important for applications, functional analysis of Hilbert spaces consequently mostly deals with this space. One of the open problems in functional analysis is to prove that every bounded linear operator on a Hilbert space has a proper [invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826). Many special cases of this [invariant subspace problem](wikipedia://en/691927) have already been proven.

### Banach spaces {#Banach_spaces}

General [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) are more complicated than Hilbert spaces, and cannot be classified in such a simple manner as those. In particular, many Banach spaces lack a notion analogous to an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063).

Examples of Banach spaces are [$L^p$-spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) for any real number $p\geq1$. Given also a measure $\mu$ on set $X$, then $L^p(X)$, sometimes also denoted $L^p(X,\mu)$ or $L^p(\mu)$, has as its vectors equivalence classes $[\,f\,]$ of [measurable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue-measurable_function) whose [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991)'s $p$-th power has finite integral; that is, functions $f$ for which one has

$$\int_{X}\left|f(x)\right|^p\,d\mu(x) < \infty.$$

If $\mu$ is the [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786), then the integral may be replaced by a sum. That is, we require

$$\sum_{x\in X}\left|f(x)\right|^p < \infty .$$

Then it is not necessary to deal with equivalence classes, and the space is denoted $\ell^p(X)$, written more simply $\ell^p$ in the case when $X$ is the set of non-negative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563).

In Banach spaces, a large part of the study involves the [dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual): the space of all [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) linear maps from the space into its underlying field, so-called functionals. A Banach space can be canonically identified with a subspace of its bidual, which is the dual of its dual space. The corresponding map is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) but in general not onto. A general Banach space and its bidual need not even be isometrically isomorphic in any way, contrary to the finite-dimensional situation. This is explained in the dual space article.

Also, the notion of [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) can be extended to arbitrary functions between Banach spaces. See, for instance, the [Fréchet derivative](wikipedia://en/2735727) article.

## Major and foundational results {#Major_and_foundational_results}

There are four major theorems which are sometimes called the four pillars of functional analysis:

- the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860)
- the [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276)
- the [closed graph theorem](wikipedia://en/64620077)
- the [uniform boundedness principle](wikipedia://en/247392), also known as the [Banach–Steinhaus theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach–Steinhaus_theorem).

Important results of functional analysis include:

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="Uniform boundedness principle"}

### Uniform boundedness principle {#Uniform_boundedness_principle}

The [uniform boundedness principle](wikipedia://en/247392) or [Banach–Steinhaus theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach–Steinhaus_theorem) is one of the fundamental results in functional analysis. Together with the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860) and the [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276), it is considered one of the cornerstones of the field. In its basic form, it asserts that for a family of [continuous linear operators](wikipedia://en/1802169) (and thus bounded operators) whose domain is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), pointwise boundedness is equivalent to uniform boundedness in operator norm.

The theorem was first published in 1927 by [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) and [Hugo Steinhaus](wikipedia://en/71735) but it was also proven independently by [Hans Hahn](wikipedia://en/1075212).

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Uniform Boundedness Principle)"}

**Theorem (Uniform Boundedness Principle).**

Let $X$ be a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) and $Y$ be a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538). Suppose that $F$ is a collection of continuous linear operators from $X$ to $Y$. If for all $x$ in $X$ one has

$$\sup\nolimits_{T \in F} \|T(x)\|_Y < \infty,$$

then

$$\sup\nolimits_{T \in F} \|T\|_{B(X,Y)} < \infty.$$

:::

:::

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Spectral theorem"}

### Spectral theorem {#Spectral_theorem}

There are many theorems known as the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495), but one in particular has many applications in functional analysis.

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Spectral theorem"}

**Spectral theorem[^o18].**

Let $A$ be a bounded self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space $H$. Then there is a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ and a real-valued [essentially bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ess_sup) measurable function $f$ on $X$ and a unitary operator $U:H\to L^2_\mu(X)$ such that

$$U^* T U = A$$

where *T* is the [multiplication operator](wikipedia://en/976642):

$$[T \varphi](x) = f(x) \varphi(x).$$

and $\|T\| = \|f\|_\infty$.

:::

This is the beginning of the vast research area of functional analysis called [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675); see also the [spectral measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_measure#Spectral_measure).

There is also an analogous spectral theorem for bounded [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167) on Hilbert spaces. The only difference in the conclusion is that now $f$ may be complex-valued.

:::

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Hahn–Banach theorem"}

### Hahn–Banach theorem {#Hahn–Banach_theorem}

The [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860) is a central tool in functional analysis. It allows the extension of [bounded linear functionals](wikipedia://en/455961) defined on a subspace of some [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) to the whole space, and it also shows that there are "enough" [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) linear functionals defined on every [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) to make the study of the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) "interesting".

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Hahn–Banach theorem:"}

**Hahn–Banach theorem:[^o22].**

If $p:V\to\mathbb{R}$ is a [sublinear function](wikipedia://en/1750541), and $\varphi:U\to\mathbb{R}$ is a [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) $U\subseteq V$ which is dominated by $p$ on $U$; that is,

$$\varphi(x) \leq p(x)\qquad\forall x \in U$$

then there exists a linear extension $\psi:V\to\mathbb{R}$ of $\varphi$ to the whole space $V$ which is dominated by $p$ on $V$; that is, there exists a linear functional $\psi$ such that

$$\begin{aligned} \psi(x) &= \varphi(x) &\forall x\in U, \\ \psi(x) &\le p(x) &\forall x\in V. \end{aligned}$$

:::

:::

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Open mapping theorem"}

### Open mapping theorem {#Open_mapping_theorem}

The [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276), also known as the Banach–Schauder theorem (named after [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) and [Juliusz Schauder](wikipedia://en/685314)), is a fundamental result which states that if a [continuous linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) between [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) then it is an [open map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map). More precisely,[^o22]

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Open mapping theorem"}

**Open mapping theorem.**

If $X$ and $Y$ are Banach spaces and $A:X\to Y$ is a surjective continuous linear operator, then $A$ is an open map (that is, if $U$ is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in $X$, then $A(U)$ is open in $Y$).

:::

The proof uses the [Baire category theorem](wikipedia://en/55227), and completeness of both $X$ and $Y$ is essential to the theorem. The statement of the theorem is no longer true if either space is just assumed to be a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space), but is true if $X$ and $Y$ are taken to be [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801).

:::

::: {#o28 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Closed graph theorem"}

### Closed graph theorem {#Closed_graph_theorem}

::: {#o29 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Closed graph theorem"}

**Closed graph theorem.**

If $X$ is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) and $Y$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) topological vector space, then the graph of a linear map $T$ from $X$ to $Y$ is closed if and only if $T$ is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29).[^o30]

:::

:::

### Other topics {#Other_topics}

## Foundations of mathematics considerations {#Foundations_of_mathematics_considerations}

Most spaces considered in functional analysis have infinite dimension. To show the existence of a [vector space basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space_basis) for such spaces may require [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442). However, a somewhat different concept, the [Schauder basis](wikipedia://en/3460662), is usually more relevant in functional analysis. Many theorems require the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860), usually proved using the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), although the strictly weaker [Boolean prime ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/314919) suffices. The [Baire category theorem](wikipedia://en/55227), needed to prove many important theorems, also requires a form of axiom of choice.

## Points of view {#Points_of_view}

Functional analysis includes the following tendencies:

- *Abstract analysis*. An approach to analysis based on [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315), [topological rings](wikipedia://en/60097), and [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752).
- *Geometry of [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989)* contains many topics. One is [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial) approach connected with [Jean Bourgain](wikipedia://en/1024131); another is a characterization of Banach spaces in which various forms of the [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055) hold.
- *[Noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917)*. Developed by [Alain Connes](wikipedia://en/340), partly building on earlier notions, such as [George Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey)'s approach to [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986).
- *Connection with [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202)*. Either narrowly defined as in [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), or broadly interpreted by, for example, [Israel Gelfand](wikipedia://en/364643), to include most types of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200).
