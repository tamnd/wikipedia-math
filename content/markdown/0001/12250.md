# Georg Henrik von Wright

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Georg Henrik von Wright"}

*Image: Georg Henrik von Wright home 1.jpg.* Von Wright's home on Laivurinkatu Street, Helsinki: a commemorative plaque marking his long-term residence was installed in 2006

*Image: G-H-von-Wright-1961.jpg.* G. H. von Wright in 1961

**Georg Henrik von Wright** (; 14 June 1916 – 16 June 2003) was a Finnish philosopher. He is particularly known for his work in philosophical logic, especially [deontic logic](wikipedia://en/1343980), his work on [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741)'s later philosophy, and his work on moral pessimism, especially regarding the [Myth of Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_Progress).

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Georg Henrik von Wright was born in [Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki), Finland on 14 June 1916 to Tor von Wright, a [managing director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Managing_director),[^o12] and his wife Ragni Elisabeth Alfthan.[^o4] Von Wright was of both Finnish and 17th-century [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_people) ancestry;[^o5] the Scottish [von Wright family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Wright) had been raised to the [Finnish nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_nobility) in 1772.[^o6][^o7] He was an undergraduate at the [University of Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Helsinki) from 1934 to 1937, majoring in philosophy, history and political science while minoring in mathematics. His philosophy advisor was [Eino Kaila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eino_Kaila), an affiliate of the [Vienna Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_Circle) who introduced von Wright to [logical empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_empiricism).[^o4] He was also particularly inspired by the lectures of [Rolf Nevanlinna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Nevanlinna) on probability and relativity.[^o8]

As von Wright recounts, he visited Vienna in 1937 and met with [Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) and [Viktor Kraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Kraft), but the Vienna Circle was already 'rapidly dispersing' by then and the *[Anschluss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anschluss)* of 1938 put an end to his 'dreams' of studying there.[^o9] Instead, it being customary for a Finnish postgraduate to spend some time at a foreign university, he went to [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge) to work on his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) thesis in March of 1939.[^o9] There, he worked with [C. D. Broad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._D._Broad) and [R. B. Braithwaite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._B._Braithwaite) and met [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore) and [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741).[^o4][^o8]

He returned to Helsinki in the summer of 1939 and began working as a propagandist for Finland's [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) home front and then a military [ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics) scientist. In 1941, he married Maria Elisabeth von Troil and completed his doctoral dissertation, titled *The Logical Problem of Induction*.[^o4][^o8]

## Career {#Career}

Von Wright became a lecturer at the [University of Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Helsinki) in 1943 and a chaired professor in 1946.[^o4] He became known to the international philosophical community in the early 1940s via commentaries on his writings on the logic of induction by [C. D. Broad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._D._Broad) in the journal *[Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_%28journal%29)*.[^o8]

He returned to Cambridge in 1947 to lecture and attended Wittgenstein's lectures on psychology. Wittgenstein reportedly said von Wright was, as Risto Vilkko puts it, "the only one of his students who he had not spoiled with his education, the only one who did not attempt to imitate his way of thinking or his mode of expression."[^o8] After Wittgenstein retired as professor at Cambridge in 1947, von Wright succeeded him in 1948, remaining until after Wittgenstein's death in 1951 and then returning to Helsinki.[^o4] He was one of three scholars left Wittgenstein's [literary estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_estate), together with [G. E. M. Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._M._Anscombe) and [Rush Rhees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rush_Rhees).[^o8] In 1951 he published *A Treatise on Induction and Probability*, which he regarded as the end of the phase of his philosophical career begun under Kaila's supervision in the 1930s.[^o8]

Upon returning he began focusing on the development of [deontic logic](wikipedia://en/1343980), writing the seminal essay "Deontic Logic" for *Mind* in 1951, and then moved on to the general philosophy of [norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_%28philosophy%29).[^o8] He gave the [Gifford Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Lectures) in 1959 and used that material to write his books *The Varieties of Goodness* (1963) and *Norm and Action* (1963).[^o4]

In the 1960s, he turned his attention to the [logic of time](wikipedia://en/321481) and to organizational leadership roles.[^o8] By the end of the 1960s, von Wright had begun to take an interest in political questions, having opposed the heavy bombing ordered by [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson) during the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War) in his "The War Against Vietnam" (1967).[^o11] He became a member of the [Academy of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Finland) in 1961[^o4] and its chairman from 1968 to 1970, remaining until 1986.[^o8] He served as president of the Philosophical Society of Finland 1962–1973, president of the [International Union of History and Philosophy of Science](wikipedia://en/33072433) 1963–1965, and the [Institut Internationale de Philosophie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_International_de_Philosophie) 1975–1977.[^o8]

Traveling from Helsinki, he was a visiting professor at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University) in 1954 and a visiting professor at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) 1964[^o8] and then a repeated visitor (the [Andrew D. White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_D._White) professor-at-large) at Cornell 1965–1977.[^o4] In 1973, interested in developing a [Marxist humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxist_humanism), he attended the [Praxis School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Praxis_School)-organised [Korčula Summer School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korčula_Summer_School) in [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslavia).[^o11] From 1968 to 1977, he was the chancellor of [Åbo Akademi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Åbo_Akademi_University).[^o4] He was the first Leibniz visiting professor to [Leipzig University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_University), for the academic year 1994–1995.[^o8]

He published in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), [Finnish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_language), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), and [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language), belonging to the [Swedish-speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finland_Swedish) [minority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish-speaking_population_of_Finland) of Finland. His career also included editorial work; he was editor-in-chief of *[Studentbladet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studentbladet)* in 1939, an editor for *Finsk Tidskrift* from 1941 to 1946, and an editor for *[Nya Argus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nya_Argus)* from 1947 to 1960.

## Death {#Death}

Von Wright died at his home in Helsinki on 16 June 2003. His library and papers were donated to the [University of Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Helsinki) and have become available to researchers via the [National Library of Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Finland).[^o8]

## Work {#Work}

Von Wright's writings come under two broad [categories](wikipedia://en/232426). The first is [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211) and [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939) in the [Anglo-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-America) vein. His 1951 texts *An Essay in Modal Logic* and "Deontic Logic" were landmarks in the postwar rise of formal [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) and [deontic logic](wikipedia://en/1343980).[^o8] He was an authority on [Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittgenstein), editing his later works and assembling the collection *[Culture and Value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_and_Value)*.[^o11] He was the leading figure in the Finnish philosophy of his time, specialising in [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939), [philosophical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_analysis), [philosophy of action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_action), [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), and [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology).[^o8]

The other vein in von Wright's writings is moralist and pessimist. During the last twenty years of his life, under the influence of [Oswald Spengler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Spengler), [Jürgen Habermas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Habermas) and the [Frankfurt School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_School)'s reflections about modern rationality, he wrote prolifically.[^o11] His article "The Myth of Progress" (1993) questions whether our apparent material and technological progress can really be considered "progress" (see [Myth of Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_Progress)).

## Awards {#Awards}

Von Wright was elected to an honorary fellowship at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), in 1983.[^o4] In the last year of his life, he was awarded several [honorary degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_degree), including one by the [University of Bergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bergen).[^o16] He also received honorary doctorates from the [University of Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Helsinki), the [University of Innsbruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Innsbruck), the [University of Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Buenos_Aires), [Stockholm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_University), the [University of Turku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Turku), the [University of Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Liverpool), [St. Olaf College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Olaf_College), and [Lund University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lund_University).[^o8]

He was awarded the [Swedish Academy Finland Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy_Finland_Prize) in 1968. Other awards included the [Wihuri International Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wihuri_International_Prize) in 1976 and the Swedish Cultural Foundation's Culture Prize in 1982. He received the [Humboldt Research Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_Research_Award) and the Academy of Sweden's gold medal in 1986.[^o4][^o8] He won the [Selma Lagerlöf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma_Lagerlöf_Prize) in 1993, the [Tage Danielsson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tage_Danielsson) award in 1998, and the Critical European Prize in 2002.[^o8]
