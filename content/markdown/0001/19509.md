# Finitary relation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Finitary relation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **finitary relation** over a sequence of sets *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) *X*<sub>1</sub> × ... × *X*<sub>*n*</sub>; that is, it is a set of *n*-[tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) (*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>), each being a sequence of elements *x*<sub>*i*</sub> in the corresponding *X*<sub>*i*</sub>.[^n1][^o3][^o4] Typically, the relation describes a possible connection between the elements of an *n*-tuple. For example, the relation "*x* is divisible by *y* and *z*" consists of the set of triples such that when substituted to *x*, *y* and *z*, respectively, make the sentence true.

:::

The non-negative integer *n* that gives the number of "places" in the relation is called the *[arity](wikipedia://en/42301)*, *adicity* or *degree* of the relation. A relation with *n* "places" is variously called an ***n*-ary relation**, an ***n*-adic relation** or a **relation of degree *n***. Relations with a finite number of places are called *finitary relations* (or simply *relations* if the context is clear). It is also possible to generalize the concept to *infinitary relations* with [infinite sequences](wikipedia://en/27838).[^n2]

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

When two objects, qualities, classes, or attributes, viewed together by the mind, are seen under some connexion, that connexion is called a relation.

::: {#o9 .definition cue=math-theorem name="Definition"}

**Definition.**

An *n*-ary **relation** *R* on sets $X_{1}, ..., X_{n}$ is a subset of the Cartesian product $X_{1} \times ... \times X_{n}$.[^n1]

:::

Since the definition is predicated on the underlying sets $X_{1}, ..., X_{n}$, *R* may be more formally defined as the ($n + 1$)-tuple $(X_{1}, ..., X_{n}, G)$, where *G*, called the *graph* of *R*, is a subset of the Cartesian product $X_{1} \times ... \times X_{n}$.

As is often done in mathematics, the same symbol is used to refer to the mathematical object and an underlying set, so the statement $(x_{1}, ..., x_{n}) \in R$ is often used to mean $(x_{1}, ..., x_{n}) \in G$, is read "*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub> are *R*-related", and is denoted using [prefix notation](wikipedia://en/25056) by $Rx_{1}\cdots x_{n}$ and using [postfix notation](wikipedia://en/26513) by $x_{1}\cdots x_{n}R$. In the case where *R* is a binary relation, those statements are also denoted using [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014) by $x_{1}Rx_{2}$.

The following considerations apply:

- The set *X*<sub>*i*</sub> is called the th *domain* of *R*.[^n1] In the case where *R* is a binary relation, *X*<sub>1</sub> is also called simply the [*domain*](wikipedia://en/3931#Definition) or *set of departure* of *R*, and *X*<sub>2</sub> is also called the [*codomain*](wikipedia://en/3931#Definition) or *set of destination* of *R*.
- When the elements of *X*<sub>*i*</sub> are relations, *X*<sub>*i*</sub> is called a *nonsimple domain* of *R*.[^n1]
- The set of $\forall x_{i} \in X_{i}$ such that $Rx_{1}\cdots x_{i-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}\cdots x_{n}$ for at least one $(x_{1}, ..., x_{n})$ is called the *i*th *domain of definition* or *active domain* of *R*.[^n1] In the case where *R* is a binary relation, its first domain of definition is also called simply the [*domain of definition*](wikipedia://en/3931#Definition) or *active domain* of *R*, and its second domain of definition is also called the [*codomain of definition*](wikipedia://en/3931#Definition) or *active codomain* of *R*.
- When the th domain of definition of *R* is equal to *X*<sub>*i*</sub>, *R* is said to be *total* on its th domain (or on *X*<sub>*i*</sub>, when this is not ambiguous). In the case where *R* is a binary relation, when *R* is total on *X*<sub>1</sub>, it is also said to be [*left-total*](wikipedia://en/3931#Special_types_of_binary_relations) (or *serial*, in the special case where *X*<sub>1</sub> = *X*<sub>2</sub>), and when *R* is total on *X*<sub>2</sub>, it is also said to be [*right-total*](wikipedia://en/3931#Special_types_of_binary_relations) or *surjective*.
- When $\forall x \forall y \in X_{i}.$ $\forall z \in X_{j}.$ $xR_{ij}z \land yR_{ij}z \Rightarrow x = y$, where $i \in I$, $j \in J$, $R_{ij} = \pi_{ij} R$, and $\{I, J\}$ is a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of $\{1, ..., n\}$, *R* is said to be *unique* on $\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}$, and $\{X_{i}\}_{i\in J}$ is called *a [primary key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_key)*[^n1] of *R*. In the case where *R* is a binary relation, when *R* is unique on $\{X_{1}\}$, it is also said to be [*left-unique*](wikipedia://en/3931#Special_types_of_binary_relations) or *injective*, and when *R* is unique on $\{X_{2}\}$, it is also said to be [*univalent*](wikipedia://en/3931#Special_types_of_binary_relations) or *right-unique*.
- When all *X*<sub>*i*</sub> are the same set *X*, it is simpler to refer to *R* as an *n*-ary relation over *X*, called a *[homogeneous relation](wikipedia://en/12987179)*. Without this restriction, *R* is called a *[heterogeneous relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterogeneous_relation)*.
- When any of *X*<sub>*i*</sub> is empty, the defining Cartesian product is empty, and the only relation over such a sequence of domains is the empty relation $R = \emptyset$.

Let a [Boolean domain](wikipedia://en/3954077) *B* be a two-element set, say, $B = \{0, 1\}$, whose elements can be interpreted as logical values, typically $0 = \operatorname{false}$ and $1 = \operatorname{true}$. The [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) of *R*, denoted by *χ*<sub>*R*</sub>, is the [Boolean-valued function](wikipedia://en/2757237) $\chi_{R}: X_{1} \times ... \times X_{n} \to B$, defined by $\chi_{R}((x_{1}, ..., x_{n})) = 1$ if $Rx_{1}\cdots x_{n}$ and $\chi_{R}((x_{1}, ..., x_{n})) = 0$ otherwise.

In [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), it is common to refer to a Boolean-valued function as an *n*-ary [*predicate*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematics%29). From the more abstract viewpoint of [formal logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_logic) and [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), the relation *R* constitutes a *logical model* or a *relational structure*, that serves as one of many possible [interpretations](wikipedia://en/14511671) of some *n*-ary predicate symbol.

Because relations arise in many scientific disciplines, as well as in many branches of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), there is considerable variation in terminology. Aside from the [set-theoretic](wikipedia://en/27553) [extension](wikipedia://en/39095) of a relational concept or [term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Term_%28linguistics%29), the term "relation" can also be used to refer to the corresponding logical entity, either the [logical comprehension](wikipedia://en/3885297), which is the totality of [intensions](wikipedia://en/14570) or abstract properties shared by all elements in the relation, or else the symbols denoting these elements and intensions. Further, some writers of the latter persuasion introduce terms with more concrete connotations (such as "relational structure" for the set-theoretic extension of a given relational concept).

:::

## Specific values of *n* {#Specific_values_of_n}

### Nullary {#Nullary}

Nullary (0-ary) relations count only two members: the empty nullary relation, which never holds, and the universal nullary relation, which always holds. This is because there is only one 0-tuple, the empty tuple (), and there are exactly two subsets of the (singleton) set of all 0-tuples. They are sometimes useful for constructing the base case of an [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) argument.

::: {#o18 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Unary relation"}

### Unary {#Unary}

Unary (1-ary) relations can be viewed as a collection of members (such as the collection of [Nobel laureates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_laureates)) having some property (such as that of having been awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize)).

Every nullary function is a unary relation.

:::

### Binary {#Binary}

[Binary](wikipedia://en/3931) (2-ary) relations are the most commonly studied form of finitary relations. Homogeneous binary relations (where $X_{1} = X_{2}$) include

- [Equality](wikipedia://en/90446) and [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489), denoted by signs such as = and \< in statements such as "$5 < 12$", or
- [Divisibility](wikipedia://en/49492), denoted by the sign | in statements such as "$13 | 143$".

Heterogeneous binary relations include

- [Set membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29), denoted by the sign ∈ in statements such as "$1 \in N$".

### Ternary {#Ternary}

[Ternary](wikipedia://en/3784565) (3-ary) relations include, for example, the [binary functions](wikipedia://en/3943), which relate two inputs and the output. All three of the domains of a homogeneous ternary relation are the same set.

::: {#o22 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider the ternary relation *R* "*x* thinks that *y* likes *z*" over the set of people *P* = $\{\operatorname{Alice}, \operatorname{Bob}, \operatorname{Charles}, \operatorname{Denise}\}$, defined by:

*R* = $\{(\operatorname{Alice}, \operatorname{Bob}, \operatorname{Denise}), (\operatorname{Charles}, \operatorname{Alice}, \operatorname{Bob}), (\operatorname{Charles}, \operatorname{Charles}, \operatorname{Alice}), (\operatorname{Denise}, \operatorname{Denise}, \operatorname{Denise})\}$.

*R* can be represented equivalently by the following table:

*Relation *R* "*x* thinks that *y* likes *z*"*

::: {#o23 .table cue=table name="Relation R \"x thinks that y likes z\""}

| *x* | *y* | *z* |
|---|---|---|
| Alice | Bob | Denise |
| Charles | Alice | Bob |
| Charles | Charles | Alice |
| Denise | Denise | Denise |

:::

Here, each row represents a triple of *R*, that is it makes a statement of the form "*x* thinks that *y* likes *z*". For instance, the first row states that "Alice thinks that Bob likes Denise". All rows are distinct. The ordering of rows is insignificant but the ordering of columns is significant.[^n1]

The above table is also a simple example of a [relational database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_database), a field with theory rooted in [relational algebra](wikipedia://en/175285) and applications in [data management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_management).[^o25] Computer scientists, logicians, and mathematicians, however, tend to have different conceptions of what a general relation is, and what of what they are consistituted. For example, databases are designed to deal with empirical data, which is by definition finite, whereas in mathematics, relations with infinite arity (i.e., infinitary relation) are also considered.

:::

## History {#History}

The logician [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289), in work published around 1860, was the first to articulate the notion of relation in anything like its present sense. He also stated the first formal results in the theory of relations (on De Morgan and relations, see Merrill 1990).

[Charles Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117), [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216), [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) and others advanced the theory of relations. Many of their ideas, especially on relations called [orders](wikipedia://en/361924), were summarized in *[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics)* (1903) where [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) made free use of these results.

In 1970, [Edgar Codd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_F._Codd) proposed a [relational model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_model) for [databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database), thus anticipating the development of [database management systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database_management_system).[^n1]
