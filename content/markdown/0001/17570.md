# Linear equation

*Image: Linear Function Graph.svg.* Two graphs of linear equations in two variables

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **linear equation** is an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) that may be put in the form $a_1x_1+\ldots+a_nx_n+b=0,$ where $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ are the [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) (or [unknowns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unknown_%28mathematics%29)), and $b,a_1,\ldots,a_n$ are the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), which are often [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). The coefficients may be considered as [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) of the equation and may be arbitrary [expressions](wikipedia://en/609125), provided they do not contain any of the variables. To yield a meaningful equation, the coefficients $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ are required to not all be zero.

:::

Alternatively, a linear equation can be obtained by equating to zero a [linear polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polynomial) over some [field](wikipedia://en/10603), from which the coefficients are taken.

The [solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_%28equation%29) of such an equation are the values that, when substituted for the unknowns, make the equality true.

In the case of just one variable, there is exactly one solution (provided that $a_1\ne 0$). Often, the term *linear equation* refers implicitly to this particular case, in which the variable is sensibly called the *unknown*.

In the case of two variables, each solution may be interpreted as the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of a point of the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). The solutions of a linear equation form a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) in the Euclidean plane, and, conversely, every line can be viewed as the set of all solutions of a linear equation in two variables. This is the origin of the term *linear* for describing this type of equation. More generally, the solutions of a linear equation in $n$ variables form a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) (a subspace of dimension $n - 1$) in the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of dimension $n$.

Linear equations occur frequently in all mathematics and their applications in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), partly because [non-linear systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_system) are often well approximated by linear equations.

This article considers the case of a single equation with coefficients from the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), for which one studies the real solutions. All of its content applies to [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) solutions and, more generally, to linear equations with coefficients and solutions in any [field](wikipedia://en/10603). For the case of several simultaneous linear equations, see [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087).

## One variable {#One_variable}

A linear equation in one variable $x$ can be written as $ax+b=0,$ with $a\neq 0$.

The solution is $x=-\frac ba$.

## Two variables {#Two_variables}

A linear equation in two variables $x$ and $y$ can be written as $ax+by+c=0,$ where $a$ and $b$ are not both $0$.[^o4]

If $a$ and $b$ are real numbers, it has infinitely many solutions.

### Linear function {#Linear_function}

If $b \ne 0$, the equation

$$ax+by+c=0$$

is a linear equation in the single variable $y$ for every value of $x$. It therefore has a unique solution for $y$, which is given by

$$y=-\frac ab x-\frac cb.$$

This defines a [function](wikipedia://en/185427). The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of this function is a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) with [slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_%28mathematics%29) $-\frac ab$ and [$y$-intercept](wikipedia://en/464331) $-\frac cb.$ The functions whose graph is a line are generally called *linear functions* in the context of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176). However, in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a [linear function](wikipedia://en/152111) is a function that maps a sum to the sum of the images of the summands. So, for this definition, the above function is linear only when $c = 0$, that is when the line passes through the origin. To avoid confusion, the functions whose graph is an arbitrary line are often called *[affine functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_function)*, and the linear functions such that $c = 0$ are often called *[linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102)*.

### Geometric interpretation {#Geometric_interpretation}

*Image: x is a.svg.* Vertical line of equation $x = a$

*Image: y is b.svg.* Horizontal line of equation $y = b$

Each solution $(x, y)$ of a linear equation

$$ax+by+c=0$$

may be viewed as the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of a point in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). With this interpretation, all solutions of the equation form a [line](wikipedia://en/946975), provided that $a$ and $b$ are not both zero. Conversely, every line is the set of all solutions of a linear equation.

The phrase "linear equation" takes its origin in this correspondence between lines and equations: a *linear equation* in two variables is an equation whose solutions form a line.

If $b \ne 0$, the line is the [graph of the function](wikipedia://en/87352) of $x$ that has been defined in the preceding section. If $b = 0$, the line is a *vertical line* (that is a line parallel to the $y$-axis) of equation $x=-\frac ca,$ which is not the graph of a function of $x$.

Similarly, if $a \ne 0$, the line is the graph of a function of $y$, and, if $a = 0$, one has a horizontal line of equation $y=-\frac cb.$

### Equation of a line {#Equation_of_a_line}

There are various ways of defining a line. In the following subsections, a linear equation of the line is given in each case.

#### Slope–intercept form or Gradient-intercept form {#Slope–intercept_form_or_Gradient-intercept_form}

A non-vertical line can be defined by its slope $m$, and its $y$-intercept $y_{0}$ (the $y$ coordinate of its intersection with the $y$-axis). In this case, its *linear equation* can be written

$$y=mx+y_0.$$

If, moreover, the line is not horizontal, it can be defined by its slope and its $x$-intercept $x_{0}$. In this case, its equation can be written

$$y=m(x-x_0),$$

or, equivalently,

$$y=mx-mx_0.$$

These forms rely on the habit of considering a nonvertical line as the [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352).[^o9] For a line given by an equation

$$ax+by+c = 0,$$

these forms can be easily deduced from the relations

$$\begin{aligned} m&=-\frac ab,\\ x_0&=-\frac ca,\\ y_0&=-\frac cb. \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Point-slope form"}

#### Point–slope form or Point-gradient form {#Point–slope_form_or_Point-gradient_form}

A non-vertical line can be defined by its slope $m$, and the coordinates $x_1, y_1$ of any point of the line. In this case, a linear equation of the line is

$$y=y_1 + m(x-x_1),$$

or

$$y=mx +y_1-mx_1.$$

This equation can also be written

$$y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$$

to emphasize that the slope of a line can be computed from the coordinates of any two points.

:::

#### Intercept form {#Intercept_form}

A line that is not parallel to an axis and does not pass through the origin cuts the axes into two different points. The intercept values $x_{0}$ and $y_{0}$ of these two points are nonzero, and an equation of the line is[^o13]

$$\frac{x}{x_0} + \frac{y}{y_0} = 1.$$

(It is easy to verify that the line defined by this equation has $x_{0}$ and $y_{0}$ as intercept values).

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Equation of a line through 2 points"}

#### Two-point form {#Two-point_form}

Given two different points $(x_{1}, y_{1})$ and $(x_{2}, y_{2})$, there is exactly one line that passes through them. There are several ways to write a linear equation of this line.

If $x_{1} \ne x_{2}$, the slope of the line is $\frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}.$ Thus, a point-slope form is[^o13]

$$y - y_1 = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} (x - x_1).$$

By [clearing denominators](wikipedia://en/56260115), one gets the equation

$$(x_2 - x_1)(y - y_1) - (y_2 - y_1)(x - x_1)=0,$$

which is valid also when $x_{1} = x_{2}$ (to verify this, it suffices to verify that the two given points satisfy the equation).

This form is not symmetric in the two given points, but a symmetric form can be obtained by regrouping the constant terms:

$$(y_1-y_2)x + (x_2-x_1)y + (x_1y_2 - x_2y_1) =0$$

(exchanging the two points changes the sign of the left-hand side of the equation).

:::

#### Determinant form {#Determinant_form}

The two-point form of the equation of a line can be expressed simply in terms of a [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468). There are two common ways for that.

The equation $(x_2 - x_1)(y - y_1) - (y_2 - y_1)(x - x_1)=0$ is the result of expanding the determinant in the equation

$$\begin{vmatrix}x-x_1&y-y_1\\x_2-x_1&y_2-y_1\end{vmatrix}=0.$$

The equation $(y_1-y_2)x + (x_2-x_1)y + (x_1y_2 - x_2y_1)=0$ can be obtained by expanding with respect to its first row the determinant in the equation

$$\begin{vmatrix} x&y&1\\ x_1&y_1&1\\ x_2&y_2&1 \end{vmatrix}=0.$$

Besides being very simple and mnemonic, this form has the advantage of being a special case of the more general equation of a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) passing through $n$ points in a space of dimension $n - 1$. These equations rely on the condition of [linear dependence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_dependence) of points in a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135).

## More than two variables {#More_than_two_variables}

A linear equation with more than two variables may always be assumed to have the form

$$a_1 x_1 + a_2 x_2 + \cdots + a_n x_n + b=0.$$

The coefficient $b$, often denoted $a_{0}$ is called the *constant term* (sometimes the *absolute term* in old books[^o18][^o19]). Depending on the context, the term *coefficient* can be reserved for the $a_{i}$ with $i > 0$.

When dealing with $n=3$ variables, it is common to use $x,\; y$ and $z$ instead of indexed variables.

A solution of such an equation is a $n$-tuple such that substituting each element of the tuple for the corresponding variable transforms the equation into a true equality.

For an equation to be meaningful, the coefficient of at least one variable must be non-zero. If every variable has a zero coefficient, then, as mentioned for one variable, the equation is either *inconsistent* (for $b \ne 0$) as having no solution, or all $n$-tuples are solutions.

The $n$-tuples that are solutions of a linear equation in $n$ variables are the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of the points of an $(n - 1)$-dimensional [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) in an $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) (or [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) if the coefficients are complex numbers or belong to any field). In the case of three variables, this hyperplane is a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29).

If a linear equation is given with $a_{j} \ne 0$, then the equation can be solved for $x_{j}$, yielding

$$x_j = -\frac b{a_j} -\sum_{i\in \{1,\ldots,n\}, i\ne j} \frac {a_i}{a_j}x_i .$$

If the coefficients are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), this defines a [real-valued](wikipedia://en/22284647) [function of $n$ real variables](wikipedia://en/39783039).
