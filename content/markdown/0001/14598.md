# Demographics of India

::: {#o15 .subject cue=lead name="Demographics of India"}

[India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India) is the [most populous country in the world](wikipedia://en/69058), comprising approximately one-sixth of the [world population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population). According to the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)' World Population Dashboard, India's population reached slightly over 1.428 billion on 1 July 2023, surpassing [that of China](wikipedia://en/23238).[^o16][^o17]

:::

India remains a relatively young country, though it is undergoing a gradual demographic transition. As of 2024, the [median age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_age) was approximately 29.8 years,[^o18] compared to 40.2 in China. The [United Nations Population Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Population_Fund) (UNFPA) estimates that 68% of the population is between 15 and 64 years old, creating a potential "[demographic dividend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographic_dividend)" where a large working-age population can drive economic growth.[^o19] However, the number of children is declining; the under-5 population peaked in 2007 and the under-15 cohort peaked in 2011.[^o20]

Between 1975 and 2010, the population doubled to 1.2 billion. While the population continues to grow, the rate has slowed significantly from 2.3% annually (1972–1983) to roughly 0.89% in 2023. Current projections suggest the population will reach 1.7 billion by 2050.

India is highly diverse, hosting thousands of ethnic groups and four major [language families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_family): [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages), [Dravidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dravidian_languages), [Austroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austroasiatic_languages), and [Tibeto-Burman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Tibetan_languages).[^o21] The country also includes linguistic isolates such as [Nihali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihali_language) and [Burushaski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burushaski), and distinct minority communities including [Anglo-Indians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Indians) and [Siddi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddi).

The [human sex ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sex_ratio) has shown an upward trend after decades of decline, reaching 943 females per 1,000 males in 2011 and estimated at 1,060 males per 1,000 females (approx. 944 females/1000 males) in 2023.[^o22]

The population grew from the [South Asian Stone Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_Stone_Age) in 10,000 BC to the [Maurya Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurya_Empire) in 200 BC at a steadily increasing growth rate,[^o39] before population growth slowed down in the [classical era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_India) up to 500 AD, and then became largely stagnant during the [early medieval era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_kingdoms_of_India) up to 1000 AD.[^o28][^o30] The population growth rate then increased in the [late medieval era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_India) (during the [Delhi Sultanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi_Sultanate)) from 1000 to 1500.[^o28][^o30]

Under the [Mughal Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_Empire), India experienced a high economic and demographic upsurge,[^o39] due to Mughal [agrarian reforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrarian_reform) that intensified agricultural production.[^o40] 15% of the population lived in urban centres, higher than the percentage of the population in 19th-century [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India)[^o41] and contemporary Europe[^o41] up until the 19th century.[^o42] These estimates by [Abraham Eraly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Eraly)[^o41] and [Paolo Malanima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Malanima)[^o42] have been criticised by [Tim Dyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Dyson), who considers them exaggerations and estimates urbanisation of the Mughal Empire to be less than 9% of the population.[^o43]

Under the reign of [Akbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akbar) (reigned 1556–1605) in 1600, the Mughal Empire's urban population was up to 17 million people, larger than the urban population in Europe.[^o44] By 1700, Mughal India had an urban population of 23 million people, larger than British India's urban population of 22.3 million in 1871.[^o45] [Nizamuddin Ahmad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizamuddin_Ahmad) (1551–1621) reported that, under Akbar's reign, Mughal India had 120 large cities and 3,200 townships.[^o41] A number of cities in India had a population between a quarter-million and half-million people,[^o41] with larger cities including [Agra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agra) (in [Agra Subah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agra_Subah)) with up to 800,000 people[^o46] and [Dhaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhaka) (in [Bengal Subah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_Subah)) with over 1 million people.[^o47] Mughal India also had a large number of villages, with 455,698 villages by the time of [Aurangzeb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurangzeb) (reigned 1658–1707).[^o44]

Studies of India's population since 1881 have focused on such topics as total population, birth and death rates, geographic distribution, literacy, the rural and urban divide, cities of a million, and the three cities with populations over eight million: Delhi, Greater Mumbai (Bombay), and Kolkata (Calcutta).[^o58]

Mortality rates fell in the period 1920–45, primarily due to biological immunisation. Suggestions that it was the benefits of colonialism are refuted by academic thinking: "There can be no serious, informed belief... that... late colonial era mortality diminished and population grew rapidly because of improvements in income, living standards, nutrition, environmental standards, sanitation or health policies, nor was there a cultural transformation...".[^o59]

## Ethnic groups {#Ethnic_groups}

The national Census of India does not recognise [racial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_groups_of_India) or ethnic groups within India,[^o210] but recognises many of the tribal groups as [Scheduled Castes and Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduled_Castes_and_Tribes) (see [list of Scheduled Tribes in India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Scheduled_Tribes_in_India)). For a list of ethnic groups in the Republic of India (as well as neighbouring countries), see [South Asian ethnic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_ethnic_groups). According to a 2009 study published by Reich *et al.*, the modern Indian population is composed of two genetically divergent and heterogeneous populations which mixed in ancient times (about 1,200–3,500 BP), known as Ancestral North Indians (ANI) and Ancestral South Indians (ASI). ANI corresponds to the Indo-Aryan-speaking population of northern India, whereas ASI corresponds to the Dravidian-speaking population of southern India.[^o211][^o212] 700,000 people from the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) of any race live in India.[^o213] Around 150,000 [Anglo-Indian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Indian_people) still live in India.[^o214][^o215] India is a country with a vast diversity of ethnic groups, each with its own distinct culture, language, and traditions. The major ethnic groups in India include the Indo-Aryans, Dravidians, Sino-Tibetans, Austroasiatic peoples, and various indigenous tribes. These groups are spread across different regions of India, each contributing to the country's rich cultural mosaic.

## Population size and structure {#Population_size_and_structure}

*Image: 2011 Census India population density map, states and union territories.svg.* Map showing the population density in India, per 2011 Census.[^o217]

India occupies 2.41% of the world's land area but supports over 18% of the world's population. At the 2001 census 72.2% of the population[^o218] lived in about 638,000 villages[^o219] and the remaining 27.8%[^o218] lived in more than 5,100 towns and over 380 [urban agglomerations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_agglomerations).[^o220] By 2011, the share of the population living in rural areas decreased to 68.84%, while that of those living in urban areas increased to 31.16%.[^o234] India's population exceeded that of the entire continent of Africa by 200 million people in 2010.[^o221] However, because Africa's population growth is extremely high compared to the rest of the world,[^o222][^o223] it is expected to surpass both China and India by the early 2030s.[^o224] **Comparative demographics**
