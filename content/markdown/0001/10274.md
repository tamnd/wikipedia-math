# Enthalpy

potentials

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Enthalpy"}

**Enthalpy** () is the sum of a [thermodynamic system](wikipedia://en/425850)'s [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757) and the product of its [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) and [volume](wikipedia://en/32498). It is a [state function](wikipedia://en/341436) in [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) used in many measurements in chemical, biological, and physical systems at a constant external pressure, which is conveniently provided by Earth's ambient atmosphere. The pressure–volume term expresses the [work](wikipedia://en/149861) $W$ that was done against constant external pressure $P_\text{ext}$ to establish the system's physical dimensions from $V_\text{system, initial}=0$ to some final volume $V_\text{system, final}$ (as $W=P_\text{ext}\Delta V$), i.e. to make room for itself by displacing its surroundings.[^o4][^o5] The pressure-volume term is very small for solids and liquids at common conditions, and fairly small for gases. Therefore, enthalpy is a stand-in for [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) in chemical systems; [bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_energy), [lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_energy), [solvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvation), and other chemical "energies" are actually enthalpy differences. As a state function, enthalpy depends only on the final configuration of internal energy, pressure, and volume, not on the path taken to achieve it.

:::

In the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI), the unit of measurement for enthalpy is the [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule). Other historical conventional units still in use include the [calorie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie) and the [British thermal unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_thermal_unit) (BTU).

The total enthalpy of a system cannot be measured directly because the internal energy contains components that are unknown, not easily accessible, or are not of interest for the thermodynamic problem at hand. In practice, a change in enthalpy is the preferred expression for measurements at constant pressure, because it simplifies the description of [energy transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transfer). When transfer of matter into or out of the system is also prevented and no electrical or mechanical (stirring shaft or lift pumping) work is done, at constant pressure the enthalpy change equals the energy exchanged with the environment by [heat](wikipedia://en/19593167).

In chemistry, the standard [enthalpy of reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_reaction) is the enthalpy change when reactants in their [standard states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_state) ($p = 1$ [bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_%28unit%29); usually $T = 298$ [K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin_%28unit%29)) change to products in their standard states.[^o6] This quantity is the [standard heat of reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_enthalpy_of_reaction) at constant pressure and temperature, but it can be measured by [calorimetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorimeter) methods even if the temperature does vary during the measurement, provided that the initial and final pressure and temperature correspond to the standard state. The value does not depend on the path from initial to final state because enthalpy is a [state function](wikipedia://en/341436).

Enthalpies of chemical substances are usually listed for 1 bar pressure as a standard state. Enthalpies and enthalpy changes for reactions vary as a function of temperature,[^o7] but tables generally list the standard heats of formation of substances at 25. For [endothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermic) (heat-absorbing) processes, the change $\Delta H$ is a positive value; for [exothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic) (heat-releasing) processes it is negative.

The enthalpy of an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905) is independent of its pressure or volume, and depends only on its temperature, which correlates to its thermal energy. Real gases at common temperatures and pressures often closely approximate this behavior, which simplifies practical thermodynamic design and analysis.

The word "enthalpy" is derived from the Greek word *enthalpein*, which means "to heat".[^o8][^o9]

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The enthalpy $H$ of a thermodynamic system is defined as the sum of its internal energy and the product of its pressure and volume:[^o3]

$$H = U + p V,$$

where $U$ is the [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757), $p$ is [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure), and $V$ is the [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_%28thermodynamics%29) of the system; $pV$ is sometimes referred to as the pressure energy $\text{Ɛ}$${}_{p}$.[^o12]

Enthalpy is an [extensive property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extensive_property); it is proportional to the size of the system (for homogeneous systems). As [intensive properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_properties), the *[specific enthalpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_enthalpy)* $h = H/m$ is referenced to a unit of [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) $m$ of the system, and the *molar enthalpy* $H_{m} = H/n$, where $n$ is the number of [moles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29). For inhomogeneous systems the enthalpy is the sum of the enthalpies of the component subsystems:

$$H = \sum_k H_k,$$

where

$H$ is the total enthalpy of all the subsystems,

$k$ refers to the various subsystems,

$H_{k}$ refers to the enthalpy of each subsystem.

A closed system may lie in thermodynamic equilibrium in a static [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field), so that its pressure $p$ varies continuously with [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude), while, because of the equilibrium requirement, its temperature $T$ is invariant with altitude. (Correspondingly, the system's [gravitational potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential_energy) density also varies with altitude.) Then the enthalpy summation becomes an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532):

$$H = \int \rho h \,\mathrm{d}V,$$

where

$\rho$ ("[rho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rho)") is [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) (mass per unit volume),

$h$ is the specific enthalpy (enthalpy per unit mass),

$\rho h$ represents the [enthalpy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density) (enthalpy per unit volume),

$dV$ denotes an [infinitesimally](wikipedia://en/160990) small element of volume within the system, for example, the volume of an infinitesimally thin horizontal layer.

The integral therefore represents the sum of the enthalpies of all the elements of the volume.

The enthalpy of a closed homogeneous system is its energy function $H(S, p)$, with its entropy $S[p]$ and its pressure $p$ as [natural state variables](wikipedia://en/255446#Natural_variables) which provide a differential relation for $dH$ of the simplest form, derived as follows. We start from the [first law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/166404) for closed systems for an infinitesimal process:

$$\mathrm{d}U = \delta Q - \delta W,$$

where

$\delta Q$ is a small amount of heat added to the system,

$\delta W$ is a small amount of work performed by the system.

In a homogeneous system in which only [reversible](wikipedia://en/416681) processes or pure heat transfer are considered, the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/133017) gives $\delta Q = TdS$, with $T$ the [absolute temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_temperature) and $dS$ the infinitesimal change in [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) $S$ of the system. Furthermore, if only $pV$ work is done, $\delta W = pdV$. As a result,

$$\mathrm{d}U = T\,\mathrm{d}S - p\,\mathrm{d}V.$$

Adding $d(pV)$ to both sides of this expression gives

$$\mathrm{d}U + \mathrm{d}(pV) = T\,\mathrm{d}S - p\,\mathrm{d}V + \mathrm{d}(p\,V),$$

or

$$\mathrm{d}(U + pV) = T\,\mathrm{d}S + V\,\mathrm{d}p.$$

So

$$\mathrm{d}H(S, p) = T\,\mathrm{d}S + V\,\mathrm{d}p,$$

and the coefficients of the natural variable differentials $dS$ and $dp$ are just the single variables $T$ and $V$.

:::

## Other expressions {#Other_expressions}

The above expression of $dH$ in terms of entropy and pressure may be unfamiliar to some readers. There are also expressions in terms of more directly measurable variables such as temperature and pressure:[^o14]p. 88[^o15]

$$\mathrm{d}H = C_\mathsf{p}\,\mathrm{d}T + V(1 - \alpha T)\,\mathrm{d}p,$$

where $C_{p}$ is the [heat capacity](wikipedia://en/194227) at *constant [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure)*, and $\alpha$ is the [coefficient of (cubic) thermal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_thermal_expansion):

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{V} \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p.$$

With this expression one can, in principle, determine the enthalpy if $C_{p}$ and $V$ are known as functions of $p$ and $T$. However the expression is more complicated than $\mathrm{d}H = T\,\mathrm{d}S + V\,\mathrm{d}p$ because $T$ is not a natural variable for the enthalpy $H$.

At constant pressure, $\mathrm{d}p = 0$ so that $\mathrm{d}H = C_\mathsf{p}\,\mathrm{d}T.$ For an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905), $\mathrm{d}H$ reduces to this form even if the process involves a pressure change, because $\alpha T = 1$.[^o16]

In a more general form, the first law describes the internal energy with additional terms involving the [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628) and the number of particles of various types. The differential statement for $dH$ then becomes

$$\mathrm{d}H = T\,\mathrm{d}S + V\,\mathrm{d}p + \sum_i \mu_i\,\mathrm{d}N_i,$$

where $\mu_{i}$ is the chemical potential per particle for a type $i$ particle, and $N_{i}$ is the number of such particles. The last term can also be written as $\mu_{i}\,dn_{i}$ (with $dn_{i\,0}$ the number of moles of component $i$ added to the system and, in this case, $\mu_{i}$ the molar chemical potential) or as $\mu_{i}dm_{i}$ (with $dm_{i}$ the mass of component $i$ added to the system and, in this case, $\mu_{i}$ the specific chemical potential).

### Characteristic functions and natural state variables {#Characteristic_functions_and_natural_state_variables}

The enthalpy $H(S[p], p, \{N_{i}\})$ expresses the thermodynamics of a system in the *energy representation*. As a [function of state](wikipedia://en/341436), its arguments include one intensive and several extensive [state variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_variable). The state variables $S[p]$, $p$, and $\{N_{i}\}$ are said to be the [*natural state variables*](wikipedia://en/255446#Natural_variables) in this representation. They are good for describing processes in which they are determined by factors in the surroundings. For example, when a virtual parcel of atmospheric air moves to a different altitude, the pressure surrounding it changes, and the process is often so rapid that there is too little time for heat transfer. This is the basis of the so-called [adiabatic approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_approximation) that is used in [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904).[^o18]

Conjugate with the enthalpy, with these arguments, the other characteristic function of state of a thermodynamic system is its entropy, as a function $S[p](H, p, \{N_{i}\})$ of the same list of variables of state, except that the entropy $S[p]$ is replaced in the list by the enthalpy $H$. It expresses the *entropy representation*. The state variables $H$, $p$, and $\{N_{i}\}$ are said to be the *natural state variables* in this representation. They are suitable for describing processes in which they are experimentally controlled. For example, $H$ and $p$ can be controlled by allowing heat transfer, and by varying only the external pressure on the piston that sets the volume of the system.[^o19][^o20][^o21]

## Physical interpretation {#Physical_interpretation}

The $U$ term is the energy of the system, and the $pV$ term can be interpreted as the [work](wikipedia://en/3616613) that would be required to "make room" for the system if the pressure of the environment remained constant. When a system, for example, $n$ [moles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29) of a gas of [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_%28thermodynamics%29) $V$ at [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) $p$ and [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) $T$, is created or brought to its present state from [absolute zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_zero), energy must be supplied equal to its internal energy $U$ plus $pV$, where $pV$ is the [work](wikipedia://en/149861) done in pushing against the ambient (atmospheric) pressure.

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) it may be more interesting to study the internal properties of a constant-volume system and therefore the internal energy is used.[^o23][^o24] In [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), experiments are often conducted at constant [atmospheric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure), and the pressure–volume work represents a small, well-defined energy exchange with the atmosphere, so that $\Delta H$ is the appropriate expression for the [heat of reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_reaction). For a [heat engine](wikipedia://en/13654), the change in its enthalpy after a full cycle is equal to zero, since the final and initial state are equal.

## Relationship to heat {#Relationship_to_heat}

In order to discuss the relation between the enthalpy increase and heat supply, we return to the first law for closed systems, with the physics sign convention: $dU = \delta Q - \delta W$, where the heat $\delta Q$ is supplied by conduction, radiation, [Joule heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule_heating). We apply it to the special case with a constant pressure at the surface. In this case the work is given by $pdV$ (where $p$ is the pressure at the surface, $dV$ is the increase of the volume of the system). Cases of long-range electromagnetic interaction require further state variables in their formulation and are not considered here. In this case the first law reads:

$$\mathrm{d}U = \delta Q - p \,\mathrm{d}V.$$

Now,

$$\mathrm{d}H = \mathrm{d}U + \mathrm{d}(pV),$$

so

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}H &= \delta Q + V \,\mathrm{d}p + p \,\mathrm{d}V - p \,\mathrm{d}V \\ &= \delta Q + V \,\mathrm{d}p. \end{aligned}$$

If the system is under [constant pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobaric_system), $dp = 0$ and consequently, the increase in enthalpy of the system is equal to the [heat](wikipedia://en/19593167) added:

$$\mathrm{d}H = \delta Q.$$

This is why the now-obsolete term *heat content* was used for enthalpy in the 19th century.

## Applications {#Applications}

In thermodynamics, one can calculate enthalpy by determining the requirements for creating a system from "nothingness"; the mechanical work required, $pV$, differs based upon the conditions that obtain during the creation of the [thermodynamic system](wikipedia://en/425850).

[Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) must be supplied to remove particles from the surroundings to make space for the creation of the system, assuming that the pressure $p$ remains constant; this is the $pV$ term. The supplied energy must also provide the change in internal energy $U$, which includes [activation energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activation_energy), ionization energies, mixing energies, vaporization energies, chemical bond energies, and so forth. Together, these constitute the change in the enthalpy $U + pV$. For systems at constant pressure, with no external work done other than the $pV$ work, the change in enthalpy is the heat received by the system.

For a simple system with a constant number of particles at constant pressure, the difference in enthalpy is the maximum amount of thermal energy derivable from an isobaric thermodynamic process.[^o28]

### Heat of reaction {#Heat_of_reaction}

The total enthalpy of a system cannot be measured directly; the *enthalpy change* of a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_%28thermodynamics%29) is measured instead. Enthalpy change is defined by the following equation:

$$\Delta H = H_\text{f} - H_\text{i},$$

where

For an [exothermic reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic_reaction) at constant [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure), the system's change in enthalpy, $\Delta H$, is negative due to the products of the reaction having a smaller enthalpy than the reactants, and equals the heat released in the reaction if no electrical or mechanical work is done. In other words, the overall decrease in enthalpy is achieved by the generation of heat.[^o30] Conversely, for a constant-pressure [endothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endothermic) reaction, $\Delta H$ is positive and equal to the heat *absorbed* in the reaction.

From the definition of enthalpy as $H = U + pV$, the enthalpy change at constant pressure is $\Delta H = \Delta U + p\Delta V$. However, for most chemical reactions, the work term $p\Delta V$ is much smaller than the internal energy change $\Delta U$, which is approximately equal to $\Delta H$. As an example, for the combustion of carbon monoxide 2 CO(g) + O${}_{2}$(g) → 2 CO${}_{2}$(g), $\Delta H = -566.0$ kJ and $\Delta U = -563.5$ kJ.[^o31] Since the differences are so small, reaction enthalpies are often described as reaction energies and analyzed in terms of [bond energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_energy).

::: {#o33 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Joules per kilogram"}

### Specific enthalpy {#Specific_enthalpy}

The *specific enthalpy* of a uniform system is defined as $h = H/m$, where $m$ is the mass of the system. Its [SI unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_unit) is joule per kilogram. It can be expressed in other specific quantities by $h = u + pv$, where $u$ is the specific [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757), $p$ is the pressure, and $v$ is [specific volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_volume), which is equal to $1/\rho$, where $\rho$ is the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density).

:::

### Enthalpy changes {#Enthalpy_changes}

An enthalpy change describes the change in enthalpy observed in the constituents of a thermodynamic system when undergoing a transformation or chemical reaction. It is the difference between the enthalpy after the process has completed, i.e. the enthalpy of the [products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_%28chemistry%29) assuming that the reaction goes to completion, and the initial enthalpy of the system, namely the reactants. These processes are specified solely by their initial and final states, so that the enthalpy change for the reverse is the negative of that for the forward process.

A common standard enthalpy change is the [enthalpy of formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_formation), which has been determined for a large number of substances. Enthalpy changes are routinely measured and compiled in chemical and physical reference works, such as the [CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRC_Handbook_of_Chemistry_and_Physics). The following is a selection of enthalpy changes commonly recognized in thermodynamics.

When used in these recognized terms the qualifier *change* is usually dropped and the property is simply termed *enthalpy of "process"*. Since these properties are often used as reference values, it is very common to quote them for a standardized set of environmental parameters, or [standard conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_conditions), including:

- A [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) of one atmosphere (1 atm = 1013.25 hPa) or 1 bar
- A [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) of 25 °C = 298.15 K
- A [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration) of 1.0 [M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration) when the element or compound is present in solution
- Elements or compounds in their normal physical states, i.e. [standard state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_state)

For such standardized values the name of the enthalpy is commonly prefixed with the term *standard*, e.g. *standard enthalpy of formation*.

#### Chemical properties {#Chemical_properties}

[Enthalpy of reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_reaction) is defined as the enthalpy change observed in a constituent of a thermodynamic system when one mole of substance reacts completely.

[Enthalpy of formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_formation) is defined as the enthalpy change observed in a constituent of a thermodynamic system when one mole of a compound is formed from its elementary antecedents.

[Enthalpy of combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_combustion) is defined as the enthalpy change observed in a constituent of a thermodynamic system when one mole of a substance burns completely with oxygen.

[Enthalpy of hydrogenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_hydrogenation) is defined as the enthalpy change observed in a constituent of a thermodynamic system when one mole of an unsaturated compound reacts completely with an excess of hydrogen to form a [saturated compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturated_and_unsaturated_compounds).

[Enthalpy of atomization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_atomization) is defined as the enthalpy change required to separate one mole of a substance into its constituent [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) completely.

[Enthalpy of neutralization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_neutralization) is defined as the enthalpy change observed in a constituent of a thermodynamic system when one mole of water is formed when an acid and a base react.

Standard [enthalpy of solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_solution) is defined as the enthalpy change observed in a constituent of a thermodynamic system when one mole of a solute is dissolved completely in an excess of solvent, so that the solution is at infinite dilution.

Standard enthalpy of [denaturation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denaturation_%28biochemistry%29) is defined as the enthalpy change required to denature one mole of compound.

[Enthalpy of hydration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydration_energy) is defined as the enthalpy change observed when one mole of gaseous ions is completely dissolved in water forming one mole of aqueous ions.

#### Physical properties {#Physical_properties}

[Enthalpy of fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_fusion) is defined as the enthalpy change required to completely change the state of one mole of substance from solid to liquid.

[Enthalpy of vaporization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_vaporization) is defined as the enthalpy change required to completely change the state of one mole of substance from liquid to gas.

[Enthalpy of sublimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_sublimation) is defined as the enthalpy change required to completely change the state of one mole of substance from solid to gas.

[Lattice enthalpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_enthalpy) is defined as the energy required to separate one mole of an ionic compound into separated gaseous ions to an infinite distance apart (meaning no force of attraction).

[Enthalpy of mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_mixing) is defined as the enthalpy change upon mixing of two (non-reacting) chemical substances.

### Open systems {#Open_systems}

In [thermodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic) [open systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_system_%28systems_theory%29), mass (of substances) may flow in and out of the system boundaries. The first law of thermodynamics for open systems states: The increase in the internal energy of a system is equal to the amount of energy added to the system by mass flowing in and by heating, minus the amount lost by mass flowing out and in the form of work done by the system:

$$\mathrm{d}U = \delta Q + \mathrm{d}U_\text{in} - \mathrm{d}U_\text{out} - \delta W,$$

where $U$${}_{\text{in}}$ is the average internal energy entering the system, and $U$${}_{\operatorname{out}}$ is the average internal energy leaving the system.

*Image: First law open system.svg.* During [steady, continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state_%28chemical_engineering%29) operation, an energy balance applied to an open system equates shaft work performed by the system to heat added plus net enthalpy added

The region of space enclosed by the boundaries of the open system is usually called a [control volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_volume), and it may or may not correspond to physical walls. If we choose the shape of the control volume such that all flow in or out occurs perpendicular to its surface, then the flow of mass into the system performs work as if it were a piston of fluid pushing mass into the system, and the system performs work on the flow of mass out as if it were driving a piston of fluid. There are then two types of work performed: *flow work* described above, which is performed on the fluid (this is also often called *$pV$ work*), and *mechanical work* (*shaft work*), which may be performed on some mechanical device such as a turbine or pump.

These two types of work are expressed in the equation

$$\delta W = \mathrm{d}(p_\text{out} V_\text{out}) - \mathrm{d}(p_\text{in} V_\text{in}) + \delta W_\text{shaft}.$$

Substitution into the equation above for the control volume (cv) yields

$$\mathrm{d}U_\text{cv} = \delta Q + \mathrm{d}U_\text{in} + \mathrm{d}(p_\text{in} V_\text{in}) - \mathrm{d}U_\text{out} - \mathrm{d}(p_\text{out} V_\text{out}) - \delta W_\text{shaft}.$$

The definition of enthalpy $H$ permits us to use this [thermodynamic potential](wikipedia://en/255446) to account for both internal energy and $pV$ work in fluids for open systems:

$$\mathrm{d}U_\text{cv} = \delta Q + \mathrm{d}H_\text{in} - \mathrm{d}H_\text{out} - \delta W_\text{shaft}.$$

If we allow also the system boundary to move (e.g. due to moving pistons), we get a rather general form of the first law for open systems.[^o38] In terms of time derivatives, using [Newton's dot notation](wikipedia://en/10265555#Newton's_notation) for time derivatives, it reads:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t} = \sum_k \dot Q_k + \sum_k \dot H_k - \sum_k p_k\frac{\mathrm{d}V_k}{\mathrm{d}t} - P,$$

with sums over the various places $k$ where heat is supplied, mass flows into the system, and boundaries are moving. The $\boldsymbol{.}_{k}$ terms represent enthalpy flows, which can be written as

$$\dot H_k = h_k \dot m_k = H_\text{m} \dot n_k,$$

with $\dot m_k$ the mass flow and $\dot n_k$ the molar flow at position $k$ respectively. The term $dV_{k}/dt$ represents the rate of change of the system volume at position $k$ that results in $pV$ power done by the system. The parameter $P$ represents all other forms of power done by the system such as shaft power, but it can also be, say, electric power produced by an electrical power plant.

Note that the previous expression holds true only if the kinetic energy flow rate is conserved between system inlet and outlet. Otherwise, it has to be included in the enthalpy balance. During [steady-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state_%28chemical_engineering%29) operation of a device (such as a [turbine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine), [pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pump), or [engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine)), the average $dU/dt$ may be set equal to zero. This yields a useful expression for the average [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28physics%29) generation for these devices in the absence of chemical reactions:

$$P = \sum_k \big\langle \dot Q_k \big\rangle + \sum_k \big\langle \dot H_k \big\rangle - \sum_k \left\langle p_k \frac{\mathrm{d}V_k}{\mathrm{d}t} \right\rangle,$$

where the [angle brackets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bracket) denote time averages. The technical importance of the enthalpy is directly related to its presence in the first law for open systems, as formulated above.

The pressures are indicated in bar. The blue curves are isenthalps (curves of constant enthalpy). The values are indicated in blue in $\tfrac{\,\operatorname{kJ}\,}{\operatorname{kg}}$. The specific points **a**, **b**, etc., are treated in the main text.

The enthalpy values of important substances can be obtained using commercial software. Practically all relevant material properties can be obtained either in tabular or in graphical form. There are many types of diagrams, such as $h - T$ diagrams, which give the specific enthalpy as function of temperature for various pressures, and $h - p$ diagrams, which give $h$ as function of $p$ for various $T$. One of the most common diagrams is the temperature–specific entropy diagram ($T - s$ diagram). It gives the melting curve and saturated liquid and vapor values together with isobars and isenthalps. These diagrams are powerful tools in the hands of the thermal engineer.

### Throttling {#Throttling}

*Image: Schematic of throttling.png.* Schematic diagram of a throttling in the steady state. Fluid enters the system (dotted rectangle) at point 1 and leaves it at point 2. The mass flow is $\text{ṁ}$.

One of the simple applications of the concept of enthalpy is the so-called throttling process, also known as [Joule–Thomson expansion](wikipedia://en/113469). It concerns a steady adiabatic flow of a fluid through a flow resistance (valve, porous plug, or any other type of flow resistance) as shown in the figure. This process is very important, since it is at the heart of domestic [refrigerators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigerator), where it is responsible for the temperature drop between ambient temperature and the interior of the refrigerator. It is also the final stage in many types of [liquefiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquefier).

For a steady state flow regime, the enthalpy of the system (dotted rectangle) has to be constant. Hence

$$0 = \dot m h_1 - \dot m h_2 ~.$$

Since the mass flow is constant, the specific enthalpies at the two sides of the flow resistance are the same:

$$h_1 = h_2 \; ,$$

that is, the enthalpy per unit mass does not change during the throttling. The consequences of this relation can be demonstrated using the $T - s$ diagram above.

#### Example 1 {#Example_1}

Point **c** is at 200 bar and room temperature (300 K). A Joule–Thomson expansion from 200 bar to 1 bar follows a curve of constant enthalpy of roughly 425 $\tfrac{\,\operatorname{kJ}\,}{\operatorname{kg}}$ (not shown in the diagram) lying between the 400 and 450 $\tfrac{\,\operatorname{kJ}\,}{\operatorname{kg}}$ isenthalps and ends in point **d**, which is at a temperature of about 270 K. Hence the expansion from 200 bar to 1 bar cools nitrogen from 300 K to 270 K. In the valve, there is a lot of friction, and a lot of entropy is produced, but still the final temperature is below the starting value.

#### Example 2 {#Example_2}

Point **e** is chosen so that it is on the saturated liquid line with $h = 100$$\tfrac{\,\operatorname{kJ}\,}{\operatorname{kg}}$. It corresponds roughly with $p = 13$ bar and $T = 108$ K. Throttling from this point to a pressure of 1 bar ends in the two-phase region (point **f**). This means that a mixture of gas and liquid leaves the throttling valve. Since the enthalpy is an extensive parameter, the enthalpy in **f** $( h_{\mathbf{f}} )$ is equal to the enthalpy in **g** $( h_{\mathbf{g}} )$ multiplied by the liquid fraction in **f** $( x_{\mathbf{f}} )$ plus the enthalpy in **h** $( h_{\mathbf{h}} )$ multiplied by the gas fraction in **f** $(1 - x_{\mathbf{f}} )$. So

$$h_\mathbf\mathsf{f} = x_\mathbf\mathsf{f} h_\mathbf\mathsf{g} + (1 - x_\mathbf\mathsf{f})h_\mathsf\mathbf{h} ~.$$

With numbers:

$100 = x_{\mathbf{f}} \times 28 + (1 - x_{\mathbf{f}}) \times 230$ , so $x_{\mathbf{f}} = 0.64$.

This means that the mass fraction of the liquid in the liquid–gas mixture that leaves the throttling valve is 64%.

### Compressors {#Compressors}

*Image: Schematic of compressor.png.* Schematic diagram of a compressor in the steady state. Fluid enters the system (dotted rectangle) at point 1 and leaves it at point 2. The mass flow is $\text{ṁ}$. A power $P$ is applied and a heat flow $Q\text{̇}$ is released to the surroundings at ambient temperature $T_{a}$.

A power $P$ is applied e.g. as electrical power. If the compression is [adiabatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic), the gas temperature goes up. In the reversible case it would be at constant entropy, which corresponds with a vertical line in the $T - s$ diagram. For example, compressing nitrogen from 1 bar (point **a**) to 2 bar (point **b**) would result in a temperature increase from 300 K to 380 K. In order to let the compressed gas exit at ambient temperature $T_{a}$, heat exchange, e.g. by cooling water, is necessary. In the ideal case the compression is isothermal. The average heat flow to the surroundings is $Q\text{̇}$. Since the system is in the steady state the first law gives

$$0 = -\dot Q + \dot m\, h_1 - \dot m\, h_2 + P ~.$$

The minimal power needed for the compression is realized if the compression is reversible. In that case the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/133017) for open systems gives

$$0 = -\frac{\, \dot Q \,}{T_\mathsf{a}} + \dot m \, s_1 - \dot m \, s_2 ~.$$

Eliminating $Q\text{̇}$ gives for the minimal power

$$\frac{\, P_\mathsf{min} \,}{ \dot m } = h_2 - h_1 - T_\mathsf{a}\left( s_2 - s_1 \right) ~.$$

For example, compressing 1 kg of nitrogen from 1 bar to 200 bar costs at least : $( h_{\mathbf{c}} - h_{\mathbf{a}} ) - T_{a}( s_{\mathbf{c}} - s_{\mathbf{a}} )$. With the data, obtained with the $T - s$ diagram, we find a value of $(430 - 461) - 300 \times (5.16 - 6.85) = 476$$\tfrac{\,\operatorname{kJ}\,}{\operatorname{kg}}$.

The relation for the power can be further simplified by writing it as

$$\frac{\, P_\mathsf{min} \,}{ \dot m } = \int_1^2 \left( \mathrm{d}h - T_\mathsf{a}\,\mathrm{d}s \right) ~.$$

With

$dh = T\,ds + v\,dp$ ,

this results in the final relation

$$\frac{\, P_\mathsf{min} }{ \dot m } = \int_1^2 v\,\mathrm{d}p ~.$$

## History and etymology {#History_and_etymology}

The term *enthalpy* was coined relatively late in the history of thermodynamics, in the early 20th century. [Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) was introduced in a modern sense by [Thomas Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_%28scientist%29) in 1802, while [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) by [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius) in 1865. *Energy* uses the root of the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language) word $\text{ἔ}\rho\gamma o\nu$ (*ergon*), meaning "work",[^o48] to express the idea of capacity to perform work. *Entropy* uses the Greek word τροπή (*tropē*) meaning *transformation* or *turning*.[^o49] *Enthalpy* uses the root of the Greek word $\theta\text{ά}\lambda\pi o\varsigma$ (*thalpos*) "warmth, heat".[^o50]

The term expresses the obsolete concept of *heat content*, as $dH$ refers to the amount of heat gained in a process at constant pressure only,[^o51] but not in the general case when pressure is variable.[^o30] [J. W. Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) used the term "a heat function for constant pressure" for clarity.

Introduction of the concept of "heat content" $H$ is associated with [Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoît_Paul_Émile_Clapeyron) and [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius) ([Clausius–Clapeyron relation](wikipedia://en/1780425), 1850).

The term *enthalpy* first appeared in print in 1909.[^o52] It is attributed to [Heike Kamerlingh Onnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heike_Kamerlingh_Onnes), who most likely introduced it orally the year before, at the first meeting of the Institute of Refrigeration in Paris.[^o53] It gained currency only in the 1920s, notably with the *[Mollier Steam Tables and Diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy–entropy_chart)*, published in 1927.

Until the 1920s, the symbol $H$ was used, somewhat inconsistently, for "heat" in general. The definition of $H$ as strictly limited to enthalpy or "heat content at constant pressure" was formally proposed by A. W. Porter in 1922.[^o54][^o55]
