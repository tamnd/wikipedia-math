# Euclidean domain

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euclidean domain"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a **Euclidean domain** (also called a **Euclidean ring**) is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) that can be endowed with a Euclidean function which allows a suitable generalization of [Euclidean division](wikipedia://en/679987) of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). This generalized Euclidean algorithm can be put to many of the same uses as Euclid's original algorithm in the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of integers: in any Euclidean domain, one can apply the Euclidean algorithm to compute the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354#In_commutative_rings) of any two elements. In particular, the greatest common divisor of any two elements exists and can be written as a linear combination of them ([Bézout's identity](wikipedia://en/4947)). In particular, the existence of efficient algorithms for Euclidean division of integers and of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in one variable over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is of basic importance in [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805).

:::

It is important to compare the [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of Euclidean domains with the larger class of [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039) (PIDs). An arbitrary PID has much the same "structural properties" of a Euclidean domain (or, indeed, even of the ring of integers), but lacks an analogue of the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) and [extended Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/99438) to compute greatest common divisors. So, given an integral domain $R$, it is often very useful to know that $R$ has a Euclidean function: in particular, this implies that $R$ is a PID. However, if there is no "obvious" Euclidean function, then determining whether $R$ is a PID is generally a much easier problem than determining whether it is a Euclidean domain.

Every [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) in a Euclidean domain is [principal](wikipedia://en/145126), which implies a suitable generalization of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556): every Euclidean domain is also a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546). ## Definition {#Definition}

Let $R$ be an integral domain. A **Euclidean function** on $R$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ from $R\,\backslash\,\{0\}$to the non-negative integers satisfying the following fundamental division-with-remainder property:

- (EF1) If $a$ and $b$ are in $R$ and $b$ is nonzero, then there exist $q$ and $r$ in $R$ such that $a = bq + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $f\,(r) < f\,(b)$.

A **Euclidean domain** is an integral domain which can be endowed with at least one Euclidean function. A particular Euclidean function $f$ is *not* part of the definition of a Euclidean domain, as, in general, a Euclidean domain may admit many different Euclidean functions.

In this context, $q$ and $r$ are called respectively a *quotient* and a *remainder* of the *division* (or *Euclidean division*) of $a$ by $b$. In contrast with the case of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), the quotient is generally not uniquely defined, but when a quotient has been chosen, the remainder is uniquely defined.

Most algebra texts require a Euclidean function to have the following additional property:

- (EF2) For all nonzero $a$ and $b$ in $R$, $f\,(a) \le f\,(ab)$.

However, one can show that (EF1) alone suffices to define a Euclidean domain; if an integral domain $R$ is endowed with a function $g$ satisfying (EF1), then $R$ can also be endowed with a function satisfying both (EF1) and (EF2) simultaneously. Indeed, for $a$ in $R\,\backslash\,\{0\}$, one can define $f\,(a)$ as follows:[^o4]

$$f(a) = \min_{x \in R \setminus \{0\}} g(xa)$$

In words, one may define $f\,(a)$ to be the minimum value attained by $g$ on the set of all non-zero elements of the principal ideal generated by $a$.

A Euclidean function $f$ is **multiplicative** if $f\,(ab) = f\,(a)\,f\,(b)$ and $f\,(a)$ is never zero. It follows that $f\,(1) = 1$. More generally, $f\,(a) = 1$ if and only if $a$ is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666).

### Notes on the definition {#Notes_on_the_definition}

Many authors use other terms in place of "Euclidean function", such as "degree function", "valuation function", "gauge function" or "norm function".[^o6] Some authors also require the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of the Euclidean function to be the entire ring $R$;[^o6] however, this does not essentially affect the definition, since (EF1) does not involve the value of $f\,(0)$. The definition is sometimes generalized by allowing the Euclidean function to take its values in any [well-ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered_set); this weakening does not affect the most important implications of the Euclidean property.

The property (EF1) can be restated as follows: for any principal ideal $I$ of $R$ with nonzero generator $b$, all nonzero classes of the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $R/I$ have a representative $r$ with $f\,(r) < f\,(b)$. Since the possible values of $f$ are well-ordered, this property can be established by showing that $f\,(r) < f\,(b)$ for any $r \notin I$ with minimal value of $f\,(r)$ in its class. Note that, for a Euclidean function that is so established, there need not exist an effective method to determine $q$ and $r$ in (EF1).

## Examples {#Examples}

Examples of Euclidean domains include:

- Any field. Define $f\,(x) = 1$ for all nonzero $x$.
- $\mathbf{Z}$, the ring of integers. Define $f\,(n) = |n|$, the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of $n$.[^o9]
- $\mathbf{Z}[i]$, the ring of [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628). Define $f\,(a + bi) = a^{2} + b^{2}$, the [norm](wikipedia://en/450555) of the Gaussian integer $a + bi$.
- $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ (where $\omega=\tfrac{-1\pm \sqrt{-3} }{2}$ is a [primitive cube root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity)), the ring of [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035). Define $f\,(a + b\omega) = a^{2} - ab + b^{2}$, the norm of the Eisenstein integer $a + b\omega$.
- $\mathbf{Z}[\varphi]$, the ring of [golden integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_integer), where $\varphi=\tfrac{1+\sqrt 5}{2}$ is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). Define $f\,(a + b\varphi) = |a^{2} + ab - b^{2}|$, the absolute value of the [field norm](wikipedia://en/450555) of $a + b\varphi$.
- $K[X]$, the [ring of polynomials](wikipedia://en/373065) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$. For each nonzero polynomial $P$, define $f\,(P)$ to be the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of $P$.[^o10]
- $KX$, the ring of [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) over the field $K$. For each nonzero [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) $P$, define $f\,(P)$ as the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_%28power_series%29) of $P$, that is the degree of the smallest power of $X$ occurring in $P$. In particular, for two nonzero power series $P$ and $Q$, $f\,(P) \le f\,(Q)$ if and only if $P$ [divides](wikipedia://en/60012#Division) $Q$.
- Any [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633). Define $f\,(x)$ to be the highest power of the [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) $M$ containing $x$. Equivalently, let $g$ be a generator of $M$, and $v$ be the unique integer such that $g^{v}$ is an [associate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_elements) of $x$, then define $f\,(x) = v$. The previous example $KX$ is a special case of this.
- A [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) with finitely many [nonzero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) $P_{1}, ..., P_{n}$. Define $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n v_i(x)$, where $v_{i}$ is the [discrete valuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_valuation) corresponding to the ideal $P_{i}$.[^o11]

Examples of domains that are *not* Euclidean domains include:

- Every domain that is not a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039), such as the ring of polynomials in at least two indeterminates over a field, or the ring of univariate polynomials with integer [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), or the number ring $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.
- The [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-19})$, consisting of the numbers $\tfrac{a + b\sqrt{-19}}{2}$ where $a$ and $b$ are integers and both even or both odd, is a principal ideal domain that is not Euclidean. This was proved by [Theodore Motzkin](wikipedia://en/13554055) and was the first case known[^o12] and subsequently proved for $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{D})$ for $D$ = −19, −43, −67, −163.
- The ring $A = \mathbf{R}[X, Y]/(X^{\,2} + Y^{\,2} + 1)$ is also a principal ideal domain[^o13] that is not Euclidean. To see that it is not a Euclidean domain, it suffices to show that for every non-zero prime $p\in A$, the map $A^\times\to(A/p)^\times$ induced by the quotient map $A\to A/p$ is not [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective).[^o14]

## Properties {#Properties}

Let *R* be a domain and *f* a Euclidean function on *R*. Then:

- *R* is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) (PID). In fact, if *I* is a nonzero [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of *R* then any element *a* of *I* \\ {0} with minimal value (on that set) of *f*(*a*) is a generator of *I*.[^o17] As a consequence *R* is also a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) and a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129). With respect to general principal ideal domains, the existence of factorizations (i.e., that *R* is an [atomic domain](wikipedia://en/9599592)) is particularly easy to [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) in Euclidean domains: choosing a Euclidean function *f* satisfying (EF2), *x* cannot have any decomposition into more than *f*(*x*) nonunit factors, so starting with *x* and repeatedly decomposing reducible factors is bound to produce a factorization into [irreducible elements](wikipedia://en/60693).
- Any element of *R* at which *f* takes its globally minimal value is invertible in *R*. If an *f* satisfying (EF2) is chosen, then the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) also holds, and *f* takes its minimal value exactly at the invertible elements of *R*.
- If Euclidean division is algorithmic, that is, if there is an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for computing the quotient and the remainder, then an [extended Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/99438) can be defined exactly as in the case of integers.[^o18]
- If a Euclidean domain is not a field then it has a non-unit element *a* called a *universal side divisor*[^o19][^o20][^o21][^o22] with the following property: any element *x* not divisible by *a* can be written as *x* = *ay* + *u* for some unit *u* and some element *y*. This follows by taking *a* to be a non-unit with *f*(*a*) as small as possible. This strange property can be used to show that some principal ideal domains are not Euclidean domains, as not all PIDs have this property. For example, for *d* = −19, −43, −67, −163, the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d}\,)$ is a PID which is not Euclidean (because it doesn't have this property), but the cases *d* = −1, −2, −3, −7, −11 are Euclidean.[^o19]

However, in many [finite extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_extension) of **Q** with [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) [class group](wikipedia://en/150907), the ring of integers is Euclidean (not necessarily with respect to the absolute value of the field norm; see below). Assuming the [extended Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_Riemann_hypothesis), if *K* is a finite [extension](wikipedia://en/11634) of **Q** and the ring of integers of *K* is a PID with an infinite number of units, then the ring of integers is Euclidean.[^o23] In particular this applies to the case of [totally real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_real_field) [quadratic number fields](wikipedia://en/454781) with trivial class group. In addition (and without assuming ERH), if the field *K* is a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) of **Q**, has trivial class group and [unit rank](wikipedia://en/460227) strictly greater than three, then the ring of integers is Euclidean.[^o24] An immediate [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) of this is that if the [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) is Galois over **Q**, its class group is trivial and the extension has [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) greater than 8 then the ring of integers is necessarily Euclidean.

## Norm-Euclidean fields {#Norm-Euclidean_fields}

[Algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822) *K* come with a canonical norm function on them: the absolute value of the [field norm](wikipedia://en/450555) *N* that takes an [algebraic element](wikipedia://en/186291) *α* to the product of all the [conjugates](wikipedia://en/458467) of *α*. This norm maps the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of a number field *K*, say *O*<sub>*K*</sub>, to the nonnegative [rational integers](wikipedia://en/14563), so it is a candidate to be a Euclidean norm on this ring. If this norm satisfies the axioms of a Euclidean function then the number field *K* is called *norm-Euclidean* or simply *Euclidean*.[^o27][^o28] Strictly speaking it is the ring of integers that is Euclidean since fields are trivially Euclidean domains, but the terminology is standard.

If a field is not norm-Euclidean then that does not mean the ring of integers is not Euclidean, just that the field norm does not satisfy the axioms of a Euclidean function. In fact, the rings of integers of number fields may be divided in several classes:

- Those that are not [principal](wikipedia://en/25039) and therefore not Euclidean, such as the integers of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-5}\,)$
- Those that are principal and not Euclidean, such as the integers of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-19}\,)$
- Those that are Euclidean and not norm-Euclidean, such as the integers of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{69}\,)$[^o29]
- Those that are norm-Euclidean, such as [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) (integers of $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-1}\,)$)

The norm-Euclidean [quadratic fields](wikipedia://en/454781) have been fully classified; they are $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d}\,)$ where $d$ takes the values

−11, −7, −3, −2, −1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 21, 29, 33, 37, 41, 57, 73 .[^o30]

Every Euclidean imaginary quadratic field is norm-Euclidean and is one of the five first fields in the preceding list.
