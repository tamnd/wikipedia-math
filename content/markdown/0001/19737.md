# Maxwell's equations

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Maxwell's equations"}

*Image: James Clerk Maxwell Statue Equations.jpg.* Maxwell's equations on a plaque attached to his statue in Edinburgh

**Maxwell's equations** are a set of coupled [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) that describe how [electric](wikipedia://en/41092) and [magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/36563) are generated by [electric charges](wikipedia://en/9804) and [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current). Together with the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) law, they form the foundation of [classical electromagnetism](wikipedia://en/426219), classical [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), [electric](wikipedia://en/9559) and [magnetic circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_circuit). The equations provide a mathematical model for electric, optical, and radio technologies, such as power generation, electric motors, [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless) communication, lenses, and radar.

:::

Maxwell's equations have two major variants:

- The *microscopic* equations have universal applicability but are unwieldy for common calculations. They relate the electric and magnetic fields to total charge and total current, including the complicated charges and currents in materials at the [atomic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_scale).
- The *macroscopic* equations define two new auxiliary fields that describe the large-scale behaviour of matter without having to consider atomic-scale charges and quantum phenomena like spins. However, their use requires experimentally determined parameters for a phenomenological description of the electromagnetic response of materials.

The term "Maxwell's equations" is often also used for equivalent alternative formulations. Versions of Maxwell's equations based on the [electric](wikipedia://en/59615) and [magnetic scalar potentials](wikipedia://en/2395496) are preferred for explicitly solving the equations as a [boundary value problem](wikipedia://en/436779), [analytical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenz_force#Lorentz_force_and_analytical_mechanics), or for use in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). The [covariant formulation](wikipedia://en/4603176) (on [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) rather than space and time separately) makes the compatibility of Maxwell's equations with [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) [manifest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifest_covariance). [Maxwell's equations in curved spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_equations_in_curved_spacetime), commonly used in [high-energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics) and [gravitational physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_physics), are compatible with [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).[^o2]

Maxwell's equations are named after the physicist and mathematician [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696), who, in 1861 and 1862, published an early form of the equations that included the Lorentz force law. The publication of the equations marked the [unification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unification_%28physics%29) of a theory for previously separately described phenomena: magnetism, electricity, light, and associated radiation. The modern form of the equations in their most common formulation is credited to [Oliver Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831).[^o3]

Since the mid-20th century, it has been understood that Maxwell's equations do not give an exact description of electromagnetic phenomena, but are instead a [classical](wikipedia://en/1293340) limit of the more precise theory of [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268).

## Summary {#Summary}

The two most commonly used variants of Maxwell's equations are the *microscopic* and *macroscopic* differential form in [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) units.

### Microscopic version in SI units {#Microscopic_version_in_SI_units}

In [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) form and in [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) units, Maxwell's microscopic equations are written as (top to bottom: [Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law), [Gauss's law for magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law_for_magnetism), [Faraday's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction), [Ampère-Maxwell law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law))

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} &=& \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &=& 0 \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{E} &=& -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{B} &=& \mu_0 \left(\mathbf{J} + \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} \right) \end{array}$$

In these equations, $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field, $\mathbf{B}$ the magnetic field, $\rho$ the total [electric charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge_density), $\mathbf{J}$ the total [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density), $\varepsilon_0$ the [vacuum permittivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permittivity), $\mu_0$ the [vacuum permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permeability), $\nabla \cdot$ the [divergence operator](wikipedia://en/8328), and $\nabla \times$ the [curl operator](wikipedia://en/6123).

### Macroscopic version in SI units {#Macroscopic_version_in_SI_units}

In [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) form and in [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) units, Maxwell's macroscopic equations are written as:

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} &=& \rho_f \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &=& 0 \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{E} &=& -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{H} &=& \mathbf{J}_f + \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} \end{array}$$

In these equations, $\mathbf{D}$ is the [electric displacement field](wikipedia://en/1191101), $\mathbf{E}$ the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092), $\mathbf{B}$ the [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563), $\mathbf{H}$ the [magnetic field intensity](wikipedia://en/36563#The_H-field), $\rho_f$ the free [electric charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge_density), $\mathbf{J}_f$ the free [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density), $\nabla \cdot$ the [divergence operator](wikipedia://en/8328), and $\nabla \times$ the [curl operator](wikipedia://en/6123).

## Conceptual descriptions {#Conceptual_descriptions}

::: {#o10 .statement cue=named-heading name="Gauss's law"}

### Gauss's law {#Gauss's_law}

*Image: VFPt charges plus minus thumb.svg.* Electric field from positive to negative charges

[Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law) describes the relationship between an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) and [electric charges](wikipedia://en/9804): an electric field points away from positive charges and towards negative charges, and the net [outflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_flux) of the electric field through a [closed surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_surface) is proportional to the enclosed charge, including bound charge due to polarization of material. The coefficient of the proportion is the [permittivity of free space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permittivity).

:::

::: {#o12 .statement cue=named-heading name="Gauss's law for magnetism"}

### Gauss's law for magnetism {#Gauss's_law_for_magnetism}

*Image: VFPt dipole magnetic1.svg.* [Gauss's law for magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law_for_magnetism): magnetic field lines never begin nor end but form loops or extend to infinity as shown here with the magnetic field due to a ring of current.

[Gauss's law for magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law_for_magnetism) states that electric charges have no magnetic analogues, called [magnetic monopoles](wikipedia://en/175470); no north or south magnetic poles exist in isolation.[^o13] Instead, the magnetic field of a material is attributed to a [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole), and the net outflow of the magnetic field through a closed surface is zero. Magnetic dipoles may be represented as loops of current or inseparable pairs of equal and opposite "magnetic charges". Precisely, the total [magnetic flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux) through a [Gaussian surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_surface) is zero, and the magnetic field is a [solenoidal vector field](wikipedia://en/419444).[^o14]

:::

::: {#o16 .statement cue=named-heading name="Faraday's law"}

### Faraday's law {#Faraday's_law}

*Image: Magnetosphere rendition.jpg.* In a [geomagnetic storm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagnetic_storm), solar wind plasma impacts [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field) causing a time-dependent change in the field, thus inducing electric fields in Earth's atmosphere and conductive [lithosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithosphere) which can destabilize [power grids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_grid). (Not to scale.)

The [Maxwell–Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction#Maxwell–Faraday_equation) version of [Faraday's law of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction) describes how a time-varying [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) corresponds to the negative [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) of an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092).[^o13] In integral form, it states that the work per unit charge required to move a charge around a closed loop equals the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the enclosed surface.

The [electromagnetic induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_induction) is the operating principle behind many [electric generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator): for example, a rotating [bar magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_magnet) creates a changing magnetic field and generates an electric field in a nearby wire.

:::

::: {#o18 .statement cue=named-heading name="Ampère–Maxwell law"}

### Ampère–Maxwell law {#Ampère–Maxwell_law}

*Image: Magnetic core.jpg.* [Magnetic-core memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic-core_memory) (1954) is an application of [Ampère's circuital law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law). Each [core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_core) stores one [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) of data.

The original law of Ampère states that magnetic fields relate to [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current). [Maxwell's addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère–Maxwell_law) states that magnetic fields also relate to changing electric fields, which Maxwell called [displacement current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_current). The integral form states that electric and displacement currents are associated with a proportional magnetic field along any enclosing curve.

Maxwell's modification of Ampère's circuital law is important because the laws of Ampère and Gauss must otherwise be adjusted for static fields.[^o19] As a consequence, it predicts that a rotating magnetic field occurs with a changing electric field.[^o13][^o20] A further consequence is the existence of self-sustaining [electromagnetic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_waves) which [travel through empty space](wikipedia://en/2924436).

The speed calculated for electromagnetic waves, which could be predicted from experiments on charges and currents,[^o21] matches the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light); indeed, [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) *is* one form of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426) (as are [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray), [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave), and others). Maxwell understood the connection between electromagnetic waves and light in 1861, thereby unifying the theories of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) and [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics).

:::

## Microscopic formulation in terms of electric and magnetic fields (in vacuum version) {#Microscopic_formulation_in_terms_of_electric_and_magnetic_fields_(in_vacuum_version)}

In the electric and magnetic field formulation there are four equations that determine the fields for given charge and current distribution. A separate [law of nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law), the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) law, describes how the electric and magnetic fields act on charged particles and currents. By convention, a version of this law in the original equations by Maxwell is no longer included. The [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640) formalism below, the work of [Oliver Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831),[^o23][^o24] has become standard. It is rotationally invariant, and therefore mathematically more transparent than Maxwell's original 20 equations in *x*, *y* and *z* components. The relativistic formulations are more symmetric and Lorentz invariant. For the same equations expressed using tensor calculus or differential forms (see ).

The differential and integral formulations are mathematically equivalent; both are useful. The integral formulation relates fields within a region of space to fields on the boundary and can often be used to simplify and directly calculate fields from symmetric distributions of charges and currents. On the other hand, the differential equations are purely *local* and are a more natural starting point for calculating the fields in more complicated (less symmetric) situations, for example using [finite element analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element_analysis).[^o25]

### Key to the notation {#Key_to_the_notation}

Symbols in **bold** represent [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) quantities, and symbols in *italics* represent [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_%28physics%29) quantities, unless otherwise indicated. The equations introduce the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092), $\mathbf{E}$, a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641), and the [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563), $\mathbf{B}$, a [pseudovector](wikipedia://en/166356) field, each generally having a time and location dependence. The sources are

- the total electric [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) (total charge per unit volume), $\rho$, and
- the total electric [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density) (total current per unit area), $\mathbf{J}$.

The [universal constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_constant) appearing in the equations (the first two ones explicitly only in the SI formulation) are:

- the [permittivity of free space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permittivity_of_free_space), $\varepsilon_{0}$, and
- the [permeability of free space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_of_free_space), $\mu_{0}$, and
- the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), $c = ({\varepsilon_0\mu_0})^{-1/2}$

#### Differential equations {#Differential_equations}

In the differential equations,

- the [nabla symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabla_symbol), $\nabla$, denotes the three-dimensional [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) operator, [del](wikipedia://en/151925),
- the $\nabla\cdot$ symbol (pronounced "del dot") denotes the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) operator,
- the $\nabla\times$ symbol (pronounced "del cross") denotes the [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) operator.

#### Integral equations {#Integral_equations}

In the integral equations,

- $\Omega$ is any volume with closed [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) surface $\partial\Omega$, and
- $\Sigma$ is any surface with closed boundary curve $\partial\Sigma$,

The equations are a little easier to interpret with time-independent surfaces and volumes. Time-independent surfaces and volumes are "fixed" and do not change over a given time interval. For example, since the surface is time-independent, we can bring the [differentiation under the integral sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_under_the_integral_sign) in Faraday's law:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \iint_{\Sigma} \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = \iint_{\Sigma} \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}\,,$$

Maxwell's equations can be formulated with possibly time-dependent surfaces and volumes by using the differential version and using Gauss' and Stokes' theorems as appropriate.

- ${\vphantom{\int}}_{\scriptstyle\partial \Omega}$ is a [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) over the boundary surface $\partial\Omega$, with the loop indicating the surface is closed
- $\iiint_\Omega$ is a [volume integral](wikipedia://en/1247265) over the volume $\Omega$,
- $\oint_{\partial \Sigma}$ is a [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) around the boundary curve $\partial\Sigma$, with the loop indicating the curve is closed.
- $\iint_\Sigma$ is a [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) over the surface $\Sigma$,
- The *total* [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) $Q$ enclosed in $\Omega$ is the [volume integral](wikipedia://en/1247265) over $\Omega$ of the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) $\rho$ (see the "macroscopic formulation" section below): $$Q = \iiint_\Omega \rho \ \mathrm{d}V,$$ where $dV$ is the [volume element](wikipedia://en/1920694).
- The *net* [magnetic flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux) $\Phi_{B}$ is the [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) of the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ passing through a fixed surface, $\Sigma$: $$\Phi_B = \iint_{\Sigma} \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S},$$
- The *net* [electric flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_flux) $\Phi_{E}$ is the surface integral of the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ passing through $\Sigma$: $$\Phi_E = \iint_{\Sigma} \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S},$$
- The *net* [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) $I$ is the surface integral of the [electric current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current_density) $\mathbf{J}$ passing through $\Sigma$: $$I = \iint_{\Sigma} \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S},$$ where $d\mathbf{S}$ denotes the differential [vector element](wikipedia://en/3103326) of surface area $S$, [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) to surface $\Sigma$. (Vector area is sometimes denoted by $\mathbf{A}$ rather than $\mathbf{S}$, but this conflicts with the notation for [magnetic vector potential](wikipedia://en/1191067)).

### Formulation in the Gaussian system {#Formulation_in_the_Gaussian_system}

The definitions of charge, electric field, and magnetic field can be altered to simplify theoretical calculation, by absorbing [dimensioned](wikipedia://en/8267) factors of $\varepsilon_{0}$ and $\mu_{0}$ into the units (and thus redefining these). With a corresponding change in the values of the quantities for the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) law this yields the same physics, i.e. trajectories of charged particles, or [work](wikipedia://en/149861) done by an electric motor. These definitions are often preferred in theoretical and high energy physics where it is natural to take the electric and magnetic field with the same units, to simplify the appearance of the [electromagnetic tensor](wikipedia://en/1912367): the Lorentz covariant object unifying electric and magnetic field would then contain components with uniform unit and dimension.[^o36] Such modified definitions are conventionally used with the Gaussian ([CGS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre_gram_second_system_of_units#Alternate_derivations_of_CGS_units_in_electromagnetism)) units. Using these definitions, colloquially "in Gaussian units",[^o37] the Maxwell equations become:[^o38]

::: {#o39 .table cue=table}

| Name | Integral equations | Differential equations |
|---|---|---|
| [Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law) | $\vphantom{\oint}_{\scriptstyle\partial \Omega }\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = 4\pi \iiint_\Omega \rho \,\mathrm{d}V$ | $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 4\pi\rho$ |
| [Gauss's law for magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law_for_magnetism) | $\vphantom{\oint}_{\scriptstyle \partial \Omega }\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = 0$ | $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ |
| Maxwell–Faraday equation ([Faraday's law of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction)) | $\oint_{\partial \Sigma} \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell} = -\frac{1}{c}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\iint_\Sigma \mathbf{B}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}$ | $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}} {\partial t}$ |
| [Ampère–Maxwell law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère–Maxwell_law) | $\begin{aligned} \oint_{\partial \Sigma} & \mathbf{B}\cdot\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell} = \frac{1}{c} \left( 4\pi \iint_\Sigma \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} + \frac{\mathrel{\mathrm{d}}}{\mathrm{d}t} \iint_\Sigma \mathbf{E}\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}\right) \end{aligned}$ | $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \frac{1}{c}\left( 4\pi\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}\right)$ |

:::

The equations simplify slightly when a system of quantities is chosen in the speed of light, *c*, is used for [nondimensionalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondimensionalization), so that, for example, seconds and lightseconds are interchangeable, and *c* = 1.

Further changes are possible by absorbing factors of $4\pi$. This process, called rationalization, affects whether [Coulomb's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law) or [Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law) includes such a factor (see *[Heaviside–Lorentz units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaviside–Lorentz_units)*, used mainly in [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics)).

## Relationship between differential and integral formulations {#Relationship_between_differential_and_integral_formulations}

The equivalence of the differential and integral formulations are a consequence of the [Gauss divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864) and the [Kelvin–Stokes theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin–Stokes_theorem).

### Flux and divergence {#Flux_and_divergence}

*Image: Divergence theorem in EM.svg.* Volume $\Omega$ and its closed boundary $\partial\Omega$, containing (respectively enclosing) a source $(+)$ and sink $(-)$ of a vector field $\mathbf{F}$. Here, $\mathbf{F}$ could be the $\mathbf{E}$ field with source electric charges, but *not* the $\mathbf{B}$ field, which has no magnetic charges as shown. The outward [unit normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_normal) is **n**.

According to the (purely mathematical) [Gauss divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864), the [electric flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_flux) through the [boundary surface](wikipedia://en/142432) $\partial\Omega$ can be rewritten as

$\vphantom{\oint}_{\scriptstyle\partial \Omega} \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}=\iiint_{\Omega} \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}\, \mathrm{d}V$ The integral version of Gauss's equation can thus be rewritten as

$$\iiint_{\Omega} \left(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} - \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\right) \, \mathrm{d}V = 0$$

Since $\Omega$ is arbitrary (e.g. an arbitrary small ball with arbitrary center), this is satisfied [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the integrand is zero everywhere. This is the differential equations formulation of Gauss equation up to a trivial rearrangement.

Similarly rewriting the [magnetic flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux) in Gauss's law for magnetism in integral form gives $\vphantom{\oint}_{\scriptstyle\partial \Omega} \mathbf{B}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = \iiint_{\Omega} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}\, \mathrm{d}V = 0.$ which is satisfied for all $\Omega$ if and only if $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ everywhere.

### Circulation and curl {#Circulation_and_curl}

*Image: Curl theorem in EM.svg.* Surface $\Sigma$ with closed boundary $\partial\Sigma$. $\mathbf{F}$ could be the $\mathbf{E}$ or $\mathbf{B}$ fields. Again, $\mathbf{n}$ is the [unit normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_normal). (The curl of a vector field does not literally look like the "circulations", this is a heuristic depiction.)

By the [Kelvin–Stokes theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497) we can rewrite the [line integrals](wikipedia://en/21245414) of the fields around the closed boundary curve $\partial\Sigma$ to an integral of the "circulation of the fields" (i.e. their [curls](wikipedia://en/6123)) over a surface it bounds, i.e.

$$\oint_{\partial \Sigma} \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell} = \iint_\Sigma (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S},$$

Hence the [Ampère–Maxwell law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère–Maxwell_law), the modified version of Ampère's circuital law, in integral form can be rewritten as

$$\iint_\Sigma \left(\nabla \times \mathbf{B} - \mu_0 \left(\mathbf{J} + \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}\right)\right)\cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = 0.$$

Since $\Sigma$ can be chosen arbitrarily, e.g. as an arbitrary small, arbitrary oriented, and arbitrary centered disk, we conclude that the integrand is zero [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the Ampère–Maxwell law in differential equations form is satisfied. The equivalence of Faraday's law in differential and integral form follows likewise.

The line integrals and curls are analogous to quantities in classical [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034): the [circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulation_%28fluid_dynamics%29) of a fluid is the line integral of the fluid's [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) field around a closed loop, and the [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010) of the fluid is the curl of the velocity field.

## Charge conservation {#Charge_conservation}

The invariance of charge can be derived as a corollary of Maxwell's equations. The left-hand side of the Ampère–Maxwell law has zero divergence by the [div–curl identity](wikipedia://en/3114930#Divergence_of_curl_is_zero). Expanding the divergence of the right-hand side, interchanging derivatives, and applying Gauss's law gives:

$$0 = \nabla\cdot (\nabla\times \mathbf{B}) = \nabla \cdot \left(\mu_0 \left(\mathbf{J} + \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}} {\partial t} \right) \right) = \mu_0\left(\nabla\cdot \mathbf{J} + \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\cdot \mathbf{E}\right) = \mu_0\left(\nabla\cdot \mathbf{J} +\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}\right)$$

i.e.,

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0.$$

In particular, in an isolated system the total charge is conserved.

## Vacuum equations, electromagnetic waves and speed of light {#Vacuum_equations,_electromagnetic_waves_and_speed_of_light}

*Image: Electromagneticwave3D.gif.* This 3D diagram shows a plane linearly polarized wave propagating from left to right, defined by $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_{0} \sin(-\omega t + \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})$ and $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}_{0} \sin(-\omega t + \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{r})$ The oscillating fields are detected at the flashing point. The horizontal wavelength is *λ*. $\mathbf{E}_{0} \cdot \mathbf{B}_{0} = 0 = \mathbf{E}_{0} \cdot \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{B}_{0} \cdot \mathbf{k}$

In a region with no charges ($\rho = 0$) and no currents ($\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{0}$), such as in vacuum, Maxwell's equations reduce to:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} &= 0, & \nabla \times \mathbf{E} + \frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} = 0, \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0, & \nabla \times \mathbf{B} - \mu_0\varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} = 0. \end{aligned}$$

Taking the curl $(\nabla\times)$ of the curl equations, and using the [curl of the curl identity](wikipedia://en/3114930#Curl_of_curl) we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_0\varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \mathbf{E} = 0, \\ \mu_0\varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{B}}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \mathbf{B} = 0. \end{aligned}$$

The quantity $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$ has the dimension (T/L)<sup>2</sup>. Defining $c = (\mu_0 \varepsilon_0)^{-1/2}$, the equations above have the form of the standard [wave equations](wikipedia://en/33691)

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \mathbf{E} = 0, \\ \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{B}}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \mathbf{B} = 0. \end{aligned}$$

Already during Maxwell's lifetime, it was found that the known values for $\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ give $c \approx 2.998 \times 10^8~\text{m/s}$, then already known to be the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) in free space. This led him to propose that light and radio waves were propagating electromagnetic waves, since amply confirmed. In the [old SI system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_system) of units, the values of $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times 10^{-7}$ and $c = 299\,792\,458~\text{m/s}$ are defined constants, (which means that by definition $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\,187\,8... \times 10^{-12}~\text{F/m}$) that define the ampere and the metre. In the [new SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_SI) system, only *c* keeps its defined value, and the electron charge gets a defined value.

In materials with [relative permittivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_permittivity), $\varepsilon_{r}$, and [relative permeability](wikipedia://en/754487#Relative_permeability_and_magnetic_susceptibility), $\mu_{r}$, the [phase velocity](wikipedia://en/25098) of light becomes

$$v_\text{p} = \frac{1}\sqrt{\mu_0\mu_\text{r} \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_\text{r}},$$

which is usually[^o49] less than $c$.

In addition, $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of wave propagation, and are in [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) with each other. A [sinusoidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinusoidal) plane wave is one special solution of these equations. Maxwell's equations explain how these waves can physically propagate through space. The changing magnetic field creates a changing electric field through [Faraday's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction). In turn, that electric field creates a changing magnetic field through [Maxwell's modification of Ampère's circuital law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère–Maxwell_law). This perpetual cycle allows these waves, now known as [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426), to move through space at velocity $c$.

## Macroscopic formulation in terms of displacement and magnetizing fields (in matter version) {#Macroscopic_formulation_in_terms_of_displacement_and_magnetizing_fields_(in_matter_version)}

The above equations are the microscopic version of Maxwell's equations, expressing the electric and the magnetic fields in terms of the (possibly atomic-level) charges and currents present. This is sometimes called the "general" form, but the macroscopic version below is equally general, the difference being one of bookkeeping.

The microscopic version is sometimes called "Maxwell's equations in vacuum": this refers to the fact that the material medium is not built into the structure of the equations, but appears only in the charge and current terms. The microscopic version was introduced by Lorentz, who tried to use it to derive the macroscopic properties of bulk matter from its microscopic constituents.[^o51]

"Maxwell's macroscopic equations", also known as **Maxwell's equations in matter**, are more similar to those that Maxwell introduced himself.

| Ampère–Maxwell law | $\begin{aligned} \oint_{\partial \Sigma} & \mathbf{H} \cdot \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell} = \\ & \iint_{\Sigma} \mathbf{J}_\text{f} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} + \frac{d}{dt} \iint_{\Sigma} \mathbf{D} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} \\ \end{aligned}$ | $\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}_\text{f} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}} {\partial t}$ | $\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \frac{1}{c} \left(4\pi\mathbf{J}_\text{f} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}} {\partial t} \right)$ |

In the macroscopic equations, the influence of bound charge $Q_{b}$ and bound current $I_{b}$ is incorporated into the [displacement field](wikipedia://en/1191101) $\mathbf{D}$ and the [magnetizing field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetizing_field) $\mathbf{H}$, while the equations depend only on the free charges $Q_{f}$ and free currents $I_{f}$. This reflects a splitting of the total electric charge *Q* and current *I* (and their densities $\rho$ and **J**) into free and bound parts:

$$\begin{aligned} Q &= Q_\text{f} + Q_\text{b} = \iiint_\Omega \left(\rho_\text{f} + \rho_\text{b} \right) \, \mathrm{d}V = \iiint_\Omega \rho \,\mathrm{d}V, \\ I &= I_\text{f} + I_\text{b} = \iint_\Sigma \left(\mathbf{J}_\text{f} + \mathbf{J}_\text{b} \right) \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S} = \iint_\Sigma \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{S}. \end{aligned}$$

The cost of this splitting is that the additional fields $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ need to be determined through phenomenological constituent equations relating these fields to the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ and the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$, together with the bound charge and current.

See below for a detailed description of the differences between the microscopic equations, dealing with *total* charge and current including material contributions, useful in air/vacuum;[^o53] and the macroscopic equations, dealing with *free* charge and current, practical to use within materials.

### Bound charge and current {#Bound_charge_and_current}

*Image: Polarization and magnetization.svg.* *Left:* A schematic view of how an assembly of microscopic dipoles produces opposite surface charges as shown at top and bottom. *Right:* How an assembly of microscopic current loops add together to produce a macroscopically circulating current loop. Inside the boundaries, the individual contributions tend to cancel, but at the boundaries no cancelation occurs.

When an electric field is applied to a [dielectric material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric) its molecules respond by forming microscopic [electric dipoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_dipole) – their [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) move a tiny distance in the direction of the field, while their [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) move a tiny distance in the opposite direction. This produces a *macroscopic* *bound charge* in the material even though all of the charges involved are bound to individual molecules. For example, if every molecule responds the same, similar to that shown in the figure, these tiny movements of charge combine to produce a layer of positive [bound charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bound_charge#Bound_charge) on one side of the material and a layer of negative charge on the other side. The bound charge is most conveniently described in terms of the [polarization](wikipedia://en/698648) $\mathbf{P}$ of the material, its dipole moment per unit volume. If $\mathbf{P}$ is uniform, a macroscopic separation of charge is produced only at the surfaces where $\mathbf{P}$ enters and leaves the material. For non-uniform $\mathbf{P}$, a charge is also produced in the bulk.[^o55]

Somewhat similarly, in all materials the constituent atoms exhibit [magnetic moments](wikipedia://en/540979) that are intrinsically linked to the [angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyromagnetic_ratio) of the components of the atoms, most notably their [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron). The [connection to angular momentum](wikipedia://en/36563#Magnetic_dipoles) suggests the picture of an assembly of microscopic current loops. Outside the material, an assembly of such microscopic current loops is not different from a macroscopic current circulating around the material's surface, despite the fact that no individual charge is traveling a large distance. These *[bound currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bound_current#Magnetization_current)* can be described using the [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348) $\mathbf{M}$.[^o56]

The very complicated and granular bound charges and bound currents, therefore, can be represented on the macroscopic scale in terms of $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{M}$, which average these charges and currents on a sufficiently large scale so as not to see the granularity of individual atoms, but also sufficiently small that they vary with location in the material. As such, *Maxwell's macroscopic equations* ignore many details on a fine scale that can be unimportant to understanding matters on a gross scale by calculating fields that are averaged over some suitable volume.

### Auxiliary fields, polarization and magnetization {#Auxiliary_fields,_polarization_and_magnetization}

The *[definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_electromagnetism_equations#Definitions)* of the auxiliary fields are:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{r}, t) &= \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) + \mathbf{P}(\mathbf{r}, t), \\ \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{r}, t) &= \frac{1}{\mu_0} \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}, t) - \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r}, t), \end{aligned}$$

where $\mathbf{P}$ is the [polarization](wikipedia://en/698648) field and $\mathbf{M}$ is the [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348) field, which are defined in terms of microscopic bound charges and bound currents respectively. The macroscopic bound charge density $\rho_{b}$ and bound current density $\mathbf{J}_{b}$ in terms of [polarization](wikipedia://en/698648) $\mathbf{P}$ and [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348) $\mathbf{M}$ are then defined as

$$\begin{aligned} \rho_\text{b} &= -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}, \\ \mathbf{J}_\text{b} &= \nabla\times\mathbf{M} + \frac{\partial\mathbf{P}}{\partial t}. \end{aligned}$$

If we define the total, bound, and free charge and current density by

$$\begin{aligned} \rho &= \rho_\text{b} + \rho_\text{f}, \\ \mathbf{J} &= \mathbf{J}_\text{b} + \mathbf{J}_\text{f}, \end{aligned}$$

and use the defining relations above to eliminate $\mathbf{D}$, and $\mathbf{H}$, the "macroscopic" Maxwell's equations reproduce the "microscopic" equations.

:::

### Constitutive relations {#Constitutive_relations}

In order to apply 'Maxwell's macroscopic equations', it is necessary to specify the relations between [displacement field](wikipedia://en/1191101) $\mathbf{D}$ and the electric field $\mathbf{E}$, as well as the [magnetizing](wikipedia://en/36563#H-field_and_magnetic_materials) field $\mathbf{H}$ and the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$. Equivalently, we have to specify the dependence of the polarization $\mathbf{P}$ (hence the bound charge) and the magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ (hence the bound current) on the applied electric and magnetic field. The equations specifying this response are called [constitutive relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutive_relation). For real-world materials, the constitutive relations are rarely simple, except approximately, and usually determined by experiment. See the main article on constitutive relations for a fuller description.[^o60]

For materials without polarization and magnetization, the constitutive relations are (by definition)[^o36]

$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon_0\mathbf{E}, \quad \mathbf{H} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{B},$$

where $\varepsilon_{0}$ is the [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) of free space and $\mu_{0}$ the [permeability](wikipedia://en/754487) of free space. Since there is no bound charge, the total and the free charge and current are equal.

An alternative viewpoint on the microscopic equations is that they are the macroscopic equations *together* with the statement that vacuum behaves like a perfect linear "material" without additional polarization and magnetization. More generally, for linear materials the constitutive relations are[^o60]

$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}, \quad \mathbf{H} = \frac{1}{\mu}\mathbf{B},$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) and $\mu$ the [permeability](wikipedia://en/754487) of the material. For the displacement field $\mathbf{D}$ the linear approximation is usually excellent because for all but the most extreme electric fields or temperatures obtainable in the laboratory (high power pulsed lasers) the interatomic electric fields of materials of the order of 10<sup>11</sup> V/m are much higher than the external field. For the magnetizing field $\mathbf{H}$, however, the linear approximation can break down in common materials like iron leading to phenomena like [hysteresis](wikipedia://en/147003). Even the linear case can have various complications, however.

- For homogeneous materials, $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ are constant throughout the material, while for inhomogeneous materials they depend on [location](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_vector) within the material (and perhaps time).[^o61]
- For isotropic materials, $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ are scalars, while for anisotropic materials (e.g. due to crystal structure) they are [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965).[^o60][^o61]
- Materials are generally [dispersive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersion_%28optics%29), so $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ depend on the [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) of any incident EM waves.[^o60][^o61]

Even more generally, in the case of non-linear materials (see for example [nonlinear optics](wikipedia://en/21723)), $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ are not necessarily proportional to $\mathbf{E}$, similarly $\mathbf{H}$ or $\mathbf{M}$ is not necessarily proportional to $\mathbf{B}$. In general $\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ depend on both $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, on location and time, and possibly other physical quantities.

In applications one also has to describe how the free currents and charge density behave in terms of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ possibly coupled to other physical quantities like pressure, and the mass, number density, and velocity of charge-carrying particles. E.g., the original equations given by Maxwell (see *[History of Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/39304805)*) included [Ohm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm's_law) in the form

$$\mathbf{J}_\text{f} = \sigma \mathbf{E}.$$

## Alternative formulations {#Alternative_formulations}

Following are some of the several other mathematical formalisms of Maxwell's equations, with the columns separating the two homogeneous Maxwell equations from the two inhomogeneous ones. Each formulation has versions directly in terms of the electric and magnetic fields, and indirectly in terms of the [electrical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_potential) $\varphi$ and the [vector potential](wikipedia://en/422936) $\mathbf{A}$. Potentials were introduced as a convenient way to solve the homogeneous equations, but it was thought that all observable physics was contained in the electric and magnetic fields (or relativistically, the Faraday tensor). The potentials play a central role in quantum mechanics, however, and act quantum mechanically with observable consequences even when the electric and magnetic fields vanish ([Aharonov–Bohm effect](wikipedia://en/277664)).

Each table describes one formalism. See the [main article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_descriptions_of_the_electromagnetic_field) for details of each formulation.

The direct spacetime formulations make manifest that the Maxwell equations are [relativistically invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistically_invariant), where space and time are treated on equal footing. Because of this symmetry, the electric and magnetic fields are treated on equal footing and are recognized as components of the [Faraday tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_tensor). This reduces the four Maxwell equations to two, which simplifies the equations, although we can no longer use the familiar vector formulation. Maxwell equations in formulation that do not treat space and time manifestly on the same footing have Lorentz invariance as a hidden symmetry. This was a major source of inspiration for the development of relativity theory. Indeed, even the formulation that treats space and time separately is not a non-relativistic approximation and describes the same physics by simply renaming variables. For this reason the relativistic invariant equations are usually called the Maxwell equations as well.

Each table below describes one formalism.

- In the tensor calculus formulation, the [electromagnetic tensor](wikipedia://en/1912367) $F_{\alpha\beta}$ is an antisymmetric covariant order 2 tensor; the [four-potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-potential), $A_{\alpha}$, is a covariant vector; the current, $J^{\alpha}$, is a vector; the square brackets, $[ ]$, denote [antisymmetrization of indices](wikipedia://en/35456546#Symmetric_and_antisymmetric_parts); $\partial_{\alpha}$ is the partial derivative with respect to the coordinate, $x^{\alpha}$. In Minkowski space coordinates are chosen with respect to an [inertial frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame); $(x^{\alpha}) = (ct, x, y, z)$, so that the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) used to raise and lower indices is $\eta_{\alpha\beta} = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$. The [d'Alembert operator](wikipedia://en/293511) on Minkowski space is $\text{◻} = \partial_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}$ as in the vector formulation. In general spacetimes, the coordinate system $x^{\alpha}$ is arbitrary, the [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) $\nabla_{\alpha}$, the [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor), $R_{\alpha\beta}$ and raising and lowering of indices are defined by the Lorentzian metric, $g_{\alpha\beta}$ and the d'Alembert operator is defined as $\text{◻} = \nabla_{\alpha}\nabla^{\alpha}$. The topological restriction is that the second real [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) group of the space vanishes (see the differential form formulation for an explanation). This is violated for Minkowski space with a line removed, which can model a (flat) spacetime with a point-like monopole on the complement of the line.
- In the [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519) formulation on arbitrary space times, $F = \tfrac{1}{2}F_{\alpha\beta}dx^{\alpha} \land dx^{\beta}$ is the electromagnetic tensor considered as a 2-form, $A = A_{\alpha}dx^{\alpha}$ is the potential 1-form, $J = - J_\alpha {\star}\mathrm{d}x^\alpha$ is the current 3-form, $d$ is the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782), and ${\star}$ is the [Hodge star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_star) on forms defined (up to its orientation, i.e. its sign) by the Lorentzian metric of spacetime. In the special case of 2-forms such as *F*, the Hodge star ${\star}$ depends on the metric tensor only for its local scale. This means that, as formulated, the differential form field equations are [conformally invariant](wikipedia://en/295907), but the [Lorenz gauge condition](wikipedia://en/1718317) breaks conformal invariance. The operator $\Box = (-{\star} \mathrm{d} {\star} \mathrm{d} - \mathrm{d} {\star} \mathrm{d} {\star})$ is the [d'Alembert–Laplace–Beltrami operator](wikipedia://en/1763356) on 1-forms on an arbitrary [Lorentzian spacetime](wikipedia://en/287555#Lorentzian_manifold). The topological condition is again that the second real cohomology group is 'trivial' (meaning that its form follows from a definition). By the isomorphism with the second [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) this condition means that every closed 2-form is exact.

Other formalisms include the [geometric algebra formulation](wikipedia://en/12939#Spacetime_model) and a [matrix representation of Maxwell's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_representation_of_Maxwell's_equations). Historically, a [quaternionic](wikipedia://en/51440) formulation[^o70][^o71] was used.

## Solutions {#Solutions}

Maxwell's equations are [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) that relate the electric and magnetic fields to each other and to the electric charges and currents. Often, the charges and currents are themselves dependent on the electric and magnetic fields via the [Lorentz force equation](wikipedia://en/18631) and the constitutive relations. These all form a set of coupled partial differential equations which are often very difficult to solve: the solutions encompass all the diverse phenomena of [classical electromagnetism](wikipedia://en/426219). Some general remarks follow.

As for any differential equation, [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_condition)[^o73][^o74][^o75] and [initial conditions](wikipedia://en/1186804)[^o76] are necessary for a [unique solution](wikipedia://en/55016824). For example, even with no charges and no currents anywhere in spacetime, there are the obvious solutions for which **E** and **B** are zero or constant, but there are also non-trivial solutions corresponding to electromagnetic waves. In some cases, Maxwell's equations are solved over the whole of space, and boundary conditions are given as asymptotic limits at infinity.[^o77] In other cases, Maxwell's equations are solved in a finite region of space, with appropriate conditions on the boundary of that region, for example an [artificial absorbing boundary](wikipedia://en/4262792) representing the rest of the universe,[^o78][^o79] or [periodic boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/9667552), or walls that isolate a small region from the outside world (as with a [waveguide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveguide) or cavity [resonator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonator)).[^o80]

[Jefimenko's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jefimenko's_equations) (or the closely related [Liénard–Wiechert potentials](wikipedia://en/9999827)) are the explicit solution to Maxwell's equations for the electric and magnetic fields created by any given distribution of charges and currents. It assumes specific initial conditions to obtain the so-called "retarded solution", where the only fields present are the ones created by the charges. However, Jefimenko's equations are unhelpful in situations when the charges and currents are themselves affected by the fields they create.

[Numerical methods for differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_partial_differential_equations) can be used to compute approximate solutions of Maxwell's equations when exact solutions are impossible. These include the [finite element method](wikipedia://en/18233581) and [finite-difference time-domain method](wikipedia://en/1908142).[^o73][^o75][^o81][^o82][^o83] For more details, see [Computational electromagnetics](wikipedia://en/3849994).

## Overdetermination of Maxwell's equations {#Overdetermination_of_Maxwell's_equations}

Maxwell's equations *seem* [overdetermined](wikipedia://en/10160606), in that they involve six unknowns (the three components of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$) but eight equations (one for each of the two Gauss's laws, three vector components each for Faraday's and Ampère's circuital laws). (The currents and charges are not unknowns, being freely specifiable subject to [charge conservation](wikipedia://en/3094621).) This is related to a certain limited kind of redundancy in Maxwell's equations: It can be proven that any system satisfying Faraday's law and Ampère's circuital law *automatically* also satisfies the two Gauss's laws, as long as the system's initial condition does, and assuming conservation of charge and the nonexistence of magnetic monopoles.[^o85][^o86] This explanation was first introduced by [Julius Adams Stratton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Adams_Stratton) in 1941.[^o87]

Although it is possible to simply ignore the two Gauss's laws in a numerical algorithm (apart from the initial conditions), the imperfect precision of the calculations can lead to ever-increasing violations of those laws. By introducing dummy variables characterizing these violations, the four equations become not overdetermined after all. The resulting formulation can lead to more accurate algorithms that take all four laws into account.[^o88]

Both identities $\nabla\cdot \nabla\times \mathbf{B} \equiv 0, \nabla\cdot \nabla\times \mathbf{E} \equiv 0$, which reduce eight equations to six independent ones, are the true reason of overdetermination.[^o89][^o90]

Equivalently, the overdetermination can be viewed as implying conservation of electric and magnetic charge, as they are required in the derivation described above but implied by the two Gauss's laws.

For linear algebraic equations, one can make 'nice' rules to rewrite the equations and unknowns. The equations can be linearly dependent. But in differential equations, and especially partial differential equations (PDEs), one needs appropriate boundary conditions, which depend in not so obvious ways on the equations. Even more, if one rewrites them in terms of vector and scalar potential, then the equations are underdetermined because of [gauge fixing](wikipedia://en/2000736).

## Maxwell's equations as the classical limit of QED {#Maxwell's_equations_as_the_classical_limit_of_QED}

Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force law (along with the rest of classical electromagnetism) are extraordinarily successful at explaining and predicting a variety of phenomena. However, they do not account for quantum effects, and so their domain of applicability is limited. Maxwell's equations are thought of as the classical limit of [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) (QED).

Some observed electromagnetic phenomena cannot be explained with Maxwell's equations if the source of the electromagnetic fields are the classical distributions of charge and current. These include [photon–photon scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon–photon_scattering) and many other phenomena related to [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) or [virtual photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_particle), "[nonclassical light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonclassical_light)" and [quantum entanglement](wikipedia://en/25336) of electromagnetic fields (see *[Quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_optics)*). E.g. [quantum cryptography](wikipedia://en/28676005) cannot be described by Maxwell theory, not even approximately. The approximate nature of Maxwell's equations becomes more and more apparent when going into the extremely strong field regime (see *[Euler–Heisenberg Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Heisenberg_Lagrangian)*) or to extremely small distances.

Finally, Maxwell's equations cannot explain any phenomenon involving individual [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) interacting with quantum matter, such as the [photoelectric effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectric_effect), [Planck's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck's_law), the [Duane–Hunt law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duane–Hunt_law), and [single-photon light detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-photon_avalanche_diode). However, many such phenomena may be explained using a halfway theory of quantum matter coupled to a classical electromagnetic field, either as external field or with the expected value of the charge current and density on the right hand side of Maxwell's equations. This is known as semiclassical theory or self-field QED and was initially discovered by de Broglie and Schrödinger and later fully developed by E. T. Jaynes and A. O. Barut.

## Variations {#Variations}

Popular variations on the Maxwell equations as a classical theory of electromagnetic fields are relatively scarce because the standard equations have stood the test of time remarkably well.

### Magnetic monopoles {#Magnetic_monopoles}

Maxwell's equations posit that there is [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804), but no [magnetic charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_charge) (also called [magnetic monopoles](wikipedia://en/175470)), in the universe. Indeed, magnetic charge has never been observed, despite extensive searches,[^o95] and may not exist. If they did exist, both Gauss's law for magnetism and Faraday's law would need to be modified, and the resulting four equations would be fully symmetric under the interchange of electric and magnetic fields.[^o36]

### Axions {#Axions}

The [axion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axion) is a hypothetical particle that couples to the photon. If it exists, it would modify Maxwell's equations via its coupling to the photon.[^o97][^o98] In [natural units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_units) with $\hbar = c = \varepsilon_0 = 1$:

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} &=& \rho - g_{a\gamma\gamma} \mathbf{B} \cdot \nabla a \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &=& 0 \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{E} &=& -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} \\ \nabla \times \mathbf{B} &=& \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} - g_{a\gamma\gamma} \left( \mathbf{E} \times \nabla a - \frac{\partial a}{\partial t} \mathbf{B} \right) \\ \frac{\partial^2 a}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 a + m_a^2 a &=& -g_{a\gamma\gamma} \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B} \end{array}$$

where $a$ is the axion field (a [pseudoscalar](wikipedia://en/475035), since the axion has spin 0<sup>−</sup>), $g_{a\gamma\gamma}$ is the axion–photon coupling strength, and $m_a$ is the axion's mass. The latter two are proportional to each other if the axion is the solution to the [strong CP problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_CP_problem). The axion field causes magnetic fields to oscillate, converting to a photon via the [Primakoff effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primakoff_effect). The [haloscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haloscope) is a detector that attempts to detect axion radiation with this principle.[^o97]

### Other dimensions {#Other_dimensions}

In $d$-dimensional space, the vector form of Maxwell's equations is meaningful only for $d = 3$, since the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) is inherently three-dimensional. However, the tensor formulation is always valid. The electric field is always a vector, but the magnetic field becomes a [$(d-2)$-form](wikipedia://en/221519), which behaves as an antisymmetric [pseudotensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudotensor) of rank $d-2$ and has $d(d-1)/2$ components. This change in character of the magnetic field is related to the [generalization of curl in higher dimensions](wikipedia://en/6123#Differential_forms).[^o100]
