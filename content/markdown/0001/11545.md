# Feedback

*Image: General Feedback Loop.svg.* A feedback loop where all outputs of a process are available as causal inputs to that process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Feedback"}

**Feedback** occurs when outputs of a system are routed back as inputs as part of a [chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_chain_%28signal_processing_chain%29) of [cause and effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cause_and_effect) that forms a circuit or loop.[^o2] The system can then be said to *feed back* into itself. The notion of cause-and-effect has to be handled carefully when applied to feedback systems:

:::

## History {#History}

Self-regulating mechanisms have existed since antiquity, and the idea of feedback started to enter [economic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) in Britain by the 18th century, but it was not at that time recognized as a universal abstraction and so did not have a name.[^o6]

The first ever known artificial feedback device was a [float valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_valve), for maintaining water at a constant level, invented in 270 BC in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria), Egypt.[^o7] This device illustrated the principle of feedback: a low water level opens the valve, the rising water then provides feedback into the system, closing the valve when the required level is reached. This then reoccurs in a circular fashion as the water level fluctuates.[^o7]

[Centrifugal governors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_governor) were used to regulate the distance and pressure between [millstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millstone) in [windmills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill) since the 17th century. In 1788, [James Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt) designed his first centrifugal governor following a suggestion from his business partner [Matthew Boulton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Boulton), for use in the [steam engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine) of their production. Early steam engines employed a purely [reciprocating motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocating_motion), and were used for pumping water – an application that could tolerate variations in the working speed, but the use of steam engines for other applications called for more precise control of the speed.

In [1868](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1868), [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) wrote a famous paper, "On governors", that is widely considered a classic in feedback control theory.[^o8] This was a landmark paper on [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) and the mathematics of feedback.

The verb phrase *to feed back*, in the sense of returning to an earlier position in a mechanical process, was in use in the US by the 1860s,[^o9][^o10] and in 1909, Nobel laureate [Karl Ferdinand Braun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Ferdinand_Braun) used the term "feed-back" as a noun to refer to (undesired) [coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_%28electronics%29) between components of an [electronic circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_circuit).[^o11]

By the end of 1912, researchers using early electronic amplifiers ([audions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audion_tube)) had discovered that deliberately coupling part of the output signal back to the input circuit would boost the amplification (through [regeneration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenerative_circuit)), but would also cause the audion to howl or sing.[^o12] This action of feeding back of the signal from output to input gave rise to the use of the term "feedback" as a distinct word by 1920.[^o12] Increasing the feedback produced [superregeneration.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superregenerative_receiver)

The development of [cybernetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics) from the 1940s onwards was centred around the study of circular causal feedback mechanisms.

Over the years there has been some dispute as to the best definition of feedback. According to cybernetician [Ashby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ross_Ashby) (1956), mathematicians and theorists interested in the principles of feedback mechanisms prefer the definition of "circularity of action", which keeps the theory simple and consistent. For those with more practical aims, feedback should be a deliberate effect via some more tangible connection. \[Practical experimenters\] object to the mathematician's definition, pointing out that this would force them to say that feedback was present in the ordinary pendulum ... between its position and its momentum—a "feedback" that, from the practical point of view, is somewhat mystical. To this the mathematician retorts that if feedback is to be considered present only when there is an actual wire or nerve to represent it, then the theory becomes chaotic and riddled with irrelevancies.[^o13]p. 54

Focusing on uses in management theory, Ramaprasad (1983) defines feedback generally as "...information about the gap between the actual level and the reference level of a system parameter" that is used to "alter the gap in some way". He emphasizes that the information by itself is not feedback unless translated into action.[^o14]

## Types {#Types}

### Positive and negative feedback {#Positive_and_negative_feedback}

*Image: Set-point control.png.* Maintaining a desired system performance despite disturbance using negative feedback to reduce system error

*Image: Outcome Feedback Negative Feedback Loop.png.* An example of a negative feedback loop with goals

*Image: Process Feedback Loop.png.* A positive feedback loop example

Positive feedback: If the signal feedback from output is in phase with the input signal, the feedback is called positive feedback.

Negative feedback: If the signal feedback is out of phase by 180° with respect to the input signal, the feedback is called negative feedback.

As an example of negative feedback, the diagram might represent a [cruise control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruise_control) system in a car that matches a target speed, such as the speed limit. The controlled system is the car; its input includes the combined torque from the engine and from the changing slope of the road (the disturbance). The car's speed (status) is measured by a [speedometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speedometer). The error signal is the difference between the speed as measured by the speedometer and the target speed (set point). The controller interprets the speed to adjust the accelerator, commanding the fuel flow to the engine (the effector). The resulting change in engine torque, the feedback, combines with the torque exerted by the change of road grade to reduce the error in speed, minimising the changing slope.

The terms "positive" and "negative" were first applied to feedback prior to WWII. The idea of positive feedback already existed in the 1920s when the [regenerative circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenerative_circuit) was made.[^o17] Friis and Jensen (1924) described this circuit in a set of electronic amplifiers as a case where *the "feed-back" action is positive* in contrast to negative feed-back action, which they mentioned only in passing.[^o18] [Harold Stephen Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Stephen_Black)'s classic 1934 paper first details the use of negative feedback in electronic amplifiers. According to Black:

Positive feed-back increases the gain of the amplifier, negative feed-back reduces it.[^o19] According to Mindell (2002) confusion in the terms arose shortly after this: ... Friis and Jensen had made the same distinction Black used between "positive feed-back" and "negative feed-back", based not on the sign of the feedback itself but rather on its effect on the amplifier's gain. In contrast, Nyquist and Bode, when they built on Black's work, referred to negative feedback as that with the sign reversed. Black had trouble convincing others of the utility of his invention in part because confusion existed over basic matters of definition.[^o17]p. 121

Even before these terms were being used, [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) had described their concept through several kinds of "component motions" associated with the [centrifugal governors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_governor) used in steam engines. He distinguished those that lead to a continued *increase* in a disturbance or the amplitude of a wave or oscillation, from those that lead to a *decrease* of the same quality.[^o20]

#### Terminology {#Terminology}

The terms positive and negative feedback are defined in different ways within different disciplines.

1. the change of the *gap* between reference and actual values of a parameter or trait, based on whether the gap is *widening* (positive) or *narrowing* (negative).[^o14]
1. the [valence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_%28psychology%29) of the *action* or *effect* that alters the gap, based on whether it makes the recipient or observer *happy* (positive) or *unhappy* (negative).[^o23]

The two definitions may be confusing, like when an incentive (reward) is used to boost poor performance (narrow a gap). Referring to definition 1, some authors use alternative terms, replacing *positive* and *negative* with *self-reinforcing* and *self-correcting*,[^o24] *reinforcing* and *balancing*,[^o25] *discrepancy-enhancing* and *discrepancy-reducing*[^o26] or *regenerative* and *degenerative*[^o27] respectively. And for definition 2, some authors promote describing the action or effect as *positive* and *negative* *[reinforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement#Reinforcement)* or *[punishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement#Punishment)* rather than feedback.[^o14][^o28] Yet even within a single discipline an example of feedback can be called either positive or negative, depending on how values are measured or referenced.[^o29]

This confusion may arise because feedback can be used to provide *information* or *motivate*, and often has both a *[qualitative](wikipedia://en/1901895)* and a *[quantitative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_property)* component. As Connellan and Zemke (1993) put it: *Quantitative* feedback tells us how much and how many. *Qualitative* feedback tells us how good, bad or indifferent.[^o30]p. 102

:::

#### Limitations of negative and positive feedback {#Limitations_of_negative_and_positive_feedback}

While simple systems can sometimes be described as one or the other type, many systems with feedback loops cannot be shoehorned into either type, and this is especially true when multiple loops are present.

When there are only two parts joined so that each affects the other, the properties of the feedback give important and useful information about the properties of the whole. But when the parts rise to even as few as four, if every one affects the other three, then twenty circuits can be traced through them; and knowing the properties of all the twenty circuits does not give complete information about the system.[^o13]p. 54

### Other types of feedback {#Other_types_of_feedback}

In general, feedback systems can have many signals fed back, and the feedback loops frequently contain mixtures of positive and negative feedback where positive and negative feedback can dominate at different frequencies or different points in the state space of a system.

The term bipolar feedback has been coined to refer to biological systems where positive and negative feedback systems can interact, the output of one affecting the input of another, and vice versa.[^o33]

Some systems with feedback can have very complex behaviors, such as [chaotic behaviors](wikipedia://en/6295) in non-linear systems, while others have much more predictable behaviors, such as those that are used to make and design digital systems.

Feedback is used extensively in digital systems. For example, binary counters and similar devices employ feedback where the current state and inputs are used to calculate a new state, which is then fed back and clocked back into the device to update it.

## Applications {#Applications}

### Mathematics and dynamical systems {#Mathematics_and_dynamical_systems}

*Image: Mandel zoom 00 mandelbrot set.jpg.* Feedback can give rise to incredibly complex behaviors. The [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562) (black) within a continuously colored environment is plotted by repeatedly feeding back values through a simple equation and recording the points on the complex plane that fail to diverge.

By using feedback properties, the behavior of a system can be altered to meet the needs of an application; systems can be made stable, responsive or held constant. It is shown that dynamical systems with a feedback experience an adaptation to the [edge of chaos](wikipedia://en/428932).[^o37]

### Physics {#Physics}

Physical systems present feedback through the mutual interactions of their parts. Feedback is also relevant for the regulation of experimental conditions, noise reduction, and signal control.[^o39] The thermodynamics of feedback-controlled systems has intrigued physicist since the [Maxwell's demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_demon), with recent advances on the consequences for entropy reduction and performance increase.[^o40][^o41]

### Biology {#Biology}

In [biological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological) systems such as [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism), [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem), or the [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere), most parameters must stay under control within a narrow range around a certain optimal level under certain environmental conditions. The deviation of the optimal value of the controlled parameter can result from changes in internal and external environments. A change of some of the environmental conditions may also require a change of that range for the system to function. The value of the parameter to maintain is recorded by a reception system and conveyed to a regulation module via an information channel. An example of this is [insulin oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulin_oscillation).

Biological systems contain many types of regulatory circuits, both positive and negative. As in other contexts, *positive* and *negative* do not imply that the feedback causes *good* or *bad* effects. A negative feedback loop is one that tends to slow down a process, whereas a positive feedback loop tends to accelerate it. The [mirror neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_neuron) are part of a social feedback system, when an observed action is *mirrored* by the brain, like a self-performed action.

Normal tissue integrity is preserved by feedback interactions between diverse cell types mediated by adhesion molecules and secreted molecules that act as mediators; failure of key feedback mechanisms in cancer disrupts tissue function.[^o43] In an injured or infected tissue, inflammatory mediators elicit feedback responses in cells, which alter gene expression and change the groups of molecules expressed and secreted, including molecules that induce diverse cells to cooperate and restore tissue structure and function. This type of feedback is important because it enables coordination of immune responses and recovery from infections and injuries. During cancer, key elements of this feedback fail. This disrupts tissue function and immunity.[^o44][^o45]

Mechanisms of feedback were first elucidated in bacteria, where a nutrient elicits changes in some of their metabolic functions.[^o46] Feedback is also central to the operations of [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene) and [gene regulatory networks](wikipedia://en/356382). [Repressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repressor) (see [Lac repressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac_repressor)) and [activator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activator_protein) [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) are used to create genetic [operons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operon), which were identified by [François Jacob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Jacob) and [Jacques Monod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Monod) in 1961 as *feedback loops*.[^o47] These feedback loops may be positive (as in the case of the coupling between a sugar molecule and the proteins that import sugar into a bacterial cell), or negative (as is often the case in [metabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic) consumption).

On a larger scale, feedback can have a stabilizing effect on animal populations even when profoundly affected by external changes, although time lags in feedback response can give rise to [predator-prey cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka–Volterra_equation).[^o48]

In [zymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zymology), feedback serves as regulation of activity of an enzyme by its direct product(s) or downstream metabolite(s) in the metabolic pathway (see [Allosteric regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allosteric_regulation)).

The [hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal_axis) is largely controlled by positive and negative feedback, much of which is still unknown.

In [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), the body receives a stimulus from the environment or internally that causes the release of [hormones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hormone). Release of hormones then may cause more of those hormones to be released, causing a positive feedback loop. This cycle is also found in certain behaviour. For example, "shame loops" occur in people who blush easily. When they realize that they are blushing, they become even more embarrassed, which leads to further blushing, and so on.[^o49]

### Climate science {#Climate_science}

*Image: 20220726 Feedbacks affecting global warming and climate change - block diagram.svg.* Some effects of global warming can either enhance ([positive feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback)) or inhibit ([negative feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_feedback)) warming.[^o51][^o52]

The climate system is characterized by strong positive and negative feedback loops between processes that affect the state of the atmosphere, ocean, and land. A simple example is the [ice–albedo positive feedback](wikipedia://en/21435575) loop whereby melting snow exposes more dark ground (of lower [albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo)), which in turn absorbs heat and causes more snow to melt.

### Control theory {#Control_theory}

Feedback is extensively used in control theory, using a variety of methods including [state space (controls)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space_%28controls%29), [full state feedback](wikipedia://en/5425217), and so forth. In the context of control theory, "feedback" is traditionally assumed to specify "negative feedback".[^o54]

The most common general-purpose [controller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controller_%28control_theory%29) using a control-loop feedback mechanism is a [proportional-integral-derivative](wikipedia://en/66256) (PID) controller. Heuristically, the terms of a PID controller can be interpreted as corresponding to time: the proportional term depends on the *present* error, the integral term on the accumulation of *past* errors, and the derivative term is a prediction of *future* error, based on the current rate of change.[^o55]

### Mechanical engineering {#Mechanical_engineering}

In ancient times, the [float valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_valve) was used to regulate the flow of water in Greek and Roman [water clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_clock); similar float valves are used to regulate fuel in a [carburettor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carburettor) and also used to regulate tank water level in the [flush toilet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flush_toilet).

The Dutch inventor [Cornelius Drebbel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Drebbel) (1572–1633) built thermostats (c1620) to control the temperature of chicken incubators and chemical furnaces. In 1745, the windmill was improved by blacksmith Edmund Lee, who added a [fantail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill_fantail) to keep the face of the windmill pointing into the wind. In 1787, [Tom Mead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Mead) regulated the rotation speed of a windmill by using a [centrifugal pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conical_pendulum) to adjust the distance between the bedstone and the runner stone (i.e., to adjust the load).

The use of the [centrifugal governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_governor) by [James Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watt) in 1788 to regulate the speed of his [steam engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine) was one factor leading to the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution). Steam engines also use float valves and [pressure release valves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relief_valve) as mechanical regulation devices. A [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) of Watt's governor was done by [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) in 1868.[^o20]

The *[Great Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Great_Eastern)* was one of the largest steamships of its time and employed a steam-powered rudder with a feedback mechanism designed in 1866 by [John McFarlane Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McFarlane_Gray). [Joseph Farcot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Farcot) coined the word *[servo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servomechanism)* in 1873 to describe steam-powered steering systems. Hydraulic servos were later used to position guns. [Elmer Ambrose Sperry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Ambrose_Sperry) of the [Sperry Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperry_Corporation) designed the first [autopilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autopilot) in 1912. [Nicolas Minorsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Minorsky) published a theoretical analysis of automatic ship steering in 1922 and described the [PID controller](wikipedia://en/66256).[^o58]

Internal combustion engines of the late 20th century employed mechanical feedback mechanisms such as the [vacuum timing advance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignition_timing#Vacuum_timing_advance) but mechanical feedback was replaced by electronic [engine management systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_control_unit) once small, robust and powerful single-chip [microcontrollers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcontroller) became affordable.

### Electronic engineering {#Electronic_engineering}

*Image: Ideal feedback model.svg.* The simplest form of a feedback amplifier can be represented by the *ideal block diagram* made up of [unilateral elements](https://www.google.com/search?tbo=p&tbm=bks&q=%22A+unilateral+block+or+network+is+one+in+which+power+may+be+transmitted+in+one+direction+only.%22&num=10&gws_rd=ssl).[^o60]

The use of feedback is widespread in the design of [electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) components such as [amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplifier), [oscillators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillator), and stateful [logic circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_circuit) elements such as [flip-flops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flip-flop_%28electronics%29) and [counters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter_%28digital%29). Electronic feedback systems are also very commonly used to control mechanical, thermal and other physical processes.

If the signal is inverted on its way round the control loop, the system is said to have *[negative feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_feedback_amplifier)*;[^o61] otherwise, the feedback is said to be *positive*. Negative feedback is often deliberately introduced to increase the [stability](wikipedia://en/1145733) and accuracy of a system by correcting or reducing the influence of unwanted changes. This scheme can fail if the input changes faster than the system can respond to it. When this happens, the lag in arrival of the correcting signal can result in over-correction, causing the output to [oscillate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation) or "hunt".[^o62] While often an unwanted consequence of system behaviour, this effect is used deliberately in electronic oscillators.

[Harry Nyquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Nyquist) at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) derived the [Nyquist stability criterion](wikipedia://en/1697331) for determining the stability of feedback systems. An easier method, but less general, is to use [Bode plots](wikipedia://en/155562) developed by [Hendrik Bode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Bode) to determine the [gain margin and phase margin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gain_margin). Design to ensure stability often involves [frequency compensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_compensation) to control the location of the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of the amplifier.

Electronic feedback loops are used to control the output of [electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) devices, such as [amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplifiers). A feedback loop is created when all or some portion of the output is fed back to the input. A device is said to be operating *open loop* if no output feedback is being employed and *closed loop* if feedback is being used.[^o63]

When two or more amplifiers are cross-coupled using positive feedback, complex behaviors can be created. These *[multivibrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivibrator)* are widely used and include:

- astable circuits, which act as oscillators
- monostable circuits, which can be pushed into a state, and will return to the stable state after some time
- bistable circuits, which have two stable states that the circuit can be switched between

#### Negative feedback {#Negative_feedback}

Negative feedback occurs when the fed-back output signal has a relative phase of 180° with respect to the input signal (upside down). This situation is sometimes referred to as being *out of phase*, but that term is also used to indicate other phase separations, as in "90° out of phase". Negative feedback can be used to correct output errors or to desensitize a system to unwanted fluctuations.[^o65] In feedback amplifiers, this correction is generally for waveform [distortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distortion) reduction[^o66] or to establish a specified [gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gain_%28electronics%29) level. A general expression for the gain of a negative feedback amplifier is the [asymptotic gain model](wikipedia://en/997021).

#### Positive feedback {#Positive_feedback}

Positive feedback occurs when the fed-back signal is in phase with the input signal. Under certain gain conditions, positive feedback reinforces the input signal to the point where the output of the device [oscillates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillates) between its maximum and minimum possible states. Positive feedback may also introduce [hysteresis](wikipedia://en/147003) into a circuit. This can cause the circuit to ignore small signals and respond only to large ones. It is sometimes used to eliminate noise from a digital signal. Under some circumstances, positive feedback may cause a device to latch, i.e., to reach a condition in which the output is locked to its maximum or minimum state. This fact is very widely used in digital electronics to make [bistable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flip-flop_%28electronics%29) circuits for volatile storage of information.

The loud squeals that sometimes occur in [sound reinforcement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_reinforcement), [public address systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_address_system), and [rock music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_music) are known as [audio feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_feedback). If a microphone is in front of a loudspeaker that it is connected to, the sound that the microphone picks up comes out of the speaker, and is picked up by the microphone and re-amplified. If the [loop gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loop_gain) is sufficient, howling or squealing at the maximum power of the amplifier is possible.

#### Loop gain {#Loop_gain}

**Loop gain** is the sum of the [gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gain_%28electronics%29), expressed as a ratio or in [decibels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel), around a feedback loop. In a feedback loop, the output of a device, process or plant is sampled and applied to alter the input, to better control the output. The loop gain, along with the related concept of loop [phase shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_shift), determines the behavior of the device, and particularly whether the output is [stable](wikipedia://en/1145733), or unstable, which can result in [oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation). The importance of loop gain as a parameter for characterizing electronic feedback amplifiers was first recognized by [Heinrich Barkhausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Barkhausen) in 1921, and was developed further by [Hendrik Wade Bode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Wade_Bode) and [Harry Nyquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Nyquist) at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) in the 1930s.

The input signal is applied to the amplifier with [open-loop gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-loop_gain) *A* and amplified. The output of the amplifier is applied to a feedback network with gain *B*, and subtracted from the input to the amplifier. The loop gain is the product of all gains in the loop. In the diagram shown, the loop gain is the product of the gains of the amplifier and the feedback network, *−AB*. The minus sign is because the feedback signal is subtracted from the input.

The gains *A* and *B*, and therefore the loop gain, generally vary with the [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) of the input signal, and so are usually expressed as functions of the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) *ω* in [radians per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radians_per_second). It is often displayed as a graph with the horizontal axis frequency *ω* and the vertical axis gain. In amplifiers, the loop gain is the difference between the open-loop gain curve and the closed-loop gain curve (actually, the 1/B curve) on a dB scale.[^o70][^o71][^o72]

#### Oscillator {#Oscillator}

*Image: OpAmpHystereticOscillator.svg.* A popular [op-amp relaxation oscillator](wikipedia://en/195967#Comparator–based_electronic_relaxation_oscillator)

An [electronic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_oscillator) is an [electronic circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_circuit) that produces a periodic, [oscillating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation) electronic signal, often a [sine wave](wikipedia://en/324749) or a [square wave](wikipedia://en/314652).[^o74][^o75] Oscillators convert [direct current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_current) (DC) from a power supply to an [alternating current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current) signal. They are widely used in many electronic devices. Common examples of signals generated by oscillators include signals broadcast by [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_transmitter) and [television transmitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_transmitter), clock signals that regulate computers and [quartz clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz_clock), and the sounds produced by electronic beepers and [video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game).[^o74]

Oscillators are often characterized by the [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) of their output signal:

- A [low-frequency oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-frequency_oscillator) (LFO) is an electronic oscillator that generates a frequency below ≈20 Hz. This term is typically used in the field of audio [synthesizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthesizers), to distinguish it from an audio frequency oscillator.
- An audio oscillator produces frequencies in the [audio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_frequency) range, about 16 Hz to 20 kHz.[^o75]
- An RF oscillator produces signals in the [radio frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_frequency) (RF) range of about 100 kHz to 100 GHz.[^o75]

Oscillators designed to produce a high-power AC output from a DC supply are usually called [inverters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverter).

There are two main types of electronic oscillator: the linear or harmonic oscillator and the nonlinear or [relaxation oscillator](wikipedia://en/195967).[^o75][^o76]

#### Latches and flip-flops {#Latches_and_flip-flops}

*Image: JohnsonCounter2.png.* A 4-bit [ring counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_counter) using [D-type flip flops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flip-flop_%28electronics%29#D_flip-flop)

A latch or a [flip-flop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flip-flop_%28electronics%29) is a [circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_circuit) that has two stable states and can be used to store state information. They typically constructed using feedback that crosses over between two arms of the circuit, to provide the circuit with a state. The circuit can be made to change state by signals applied to one or more control inputs and will have one or two outputs. It is the basic storage element in [sequential logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_logic). Latches and flip-flops are fundamental building blocks of [digital electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_electronics) systems used in computers, communications, and many other types of systems.

Latches and flip-flops are used as data storage elements. Such data storage can be used for storage of *[state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_%28computer_science%29)*, and such a circuit is described as [sequential logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_logic). When used in a [finite-state machine](wikipedia://en/10931), the output and next state depend not only on its current input, but also on its current state (and hence, previous inputs). It can also be used for counting of pulses, and for synchronizing variably-timed input signals to some reference timing signal.

Flip-flops can be either simple (transparent or opaque) or [clocked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock_signal) (synchronous or edge-triggered). Although the term flip-flop has historically referred generically to both simple and clocked circuits, in modern usage it is common to reserve the term *flip-flop* exclusively for discussing clocked circuits; the simple ones are commonly called *latches*.[^o78][^o79]

Using this terminology, a latch is level-sensitive, whereas a flip-flop is edge-sensitive. That is, when a latch is enabled, it becomes transparent, while a flip flop's output only changes on a single type (positive going or negative going) of clock edge.

### Software {#Software}

Feedback loops provide generic mechanisms for controlling the running, maintenance, and evolution of software and computing systems.[^o81] Feedback loops are important models in the engineering of adaptive software, as they define the behaviour of the interactions among the control elements over the adaptation process, to guarantee system properties at run-time. Feedback loops and foundations of control theory have been successfully applied to computing systems.[^o82] In particular, they have been applied to the development of products such as [IBM Db2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Db2) and [IBM Tivoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Tivoli). From a software perspective, the [autonomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomic_computing) (MAPE, monitor analyze plan execute) loop proposed by researchers of IBM is another valuable contribution to the application of feedback loops to the control of dynamic properties and the design and evolution of autonomic software systems.[^o83][^o84]

### Video feedback {#Video_feedback}

[Video feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_feedback) is the [video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video) equivalent of [acoustic feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_feedback). It involves a loop between a [video camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_camera) input and a video output, e.g., a [television screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television) or [monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_monitor). Aiming the camera at the display produces a complex video image based on the feedback.[^o88]
