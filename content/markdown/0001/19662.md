# Mean value theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mean value theorem"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), the **mean value theorem** (or **[Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793)'s mean value theorem**) is a theorem about [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206), roughly stating that the average rate of change of such a function over an interval is equal to the instantaneous rate of change at some point in the interval. For example, if a car smoothly travels a certain distance over a given finite time interval, then at some moment during the trip, its instantaneous speed equals its average speed for the whole trip.

:::

The theorem states precisely that if a real-valued function is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) on a closed interval $[a,b]$, with $a<b$, and [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) on the interior $(a,b)$, then there is at least one point in $(a,b)$ where the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) equals the function's average rate of change over the whole interval. Geometrically, this means that at some point the [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the graph is parallel to the [secant line](wikipedia://en/241886) through the interval's endpoints. It is used in proving other general properties of differentiable functions.

## History {#History}

A special case of this [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) for inverse interpolation of the sine was first described by [Parameshvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameshvara) (1380–1460), from the [Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_School_of_Astronomy_and_Mathematics) in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India), in his commentaries on [Govindasvāmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Govindasvāmi) and [Bhāskara II](wikipedia://en/480509).[^o3] A restricted form of the theorem was proved by [Michel Rolle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Rolle) in 1691; the result was what is now known as [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305), and was proved only for polynomials, without the techniques of calculus. The mean value theorem in its modern form was stated and proved by [Augustin Louis Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Louis_Cauchy) in 1823.[^o4] Many variations of this theorem have been proved since then.[^o5][^o6]

::: {#o8 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

*Image: Mittelwertsatz3 c version.svg.* The function $f$ attains the slope of the secant between $a$ and $b$ as the derivative at the point $c\in(a,b)$.

*Image: Mittelwertsatz6.svg.* It is also possible that there are multiple tangents parallel to the secant.

Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ be a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) on the [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) $[a,b]$, and [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) on the [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) $(a,b)$, where $a < b$. Then there exists some $c$ in $(a,b)$ such that:[^n1]

$$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$

The mean value theorem is a generalization of [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305), which assumes $f(a)=f(b)$, so that the right-hand side above is zero.

The mean value theorem is still valid in a slightly more general setting. One only needs to assume that $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) on $[a,b]$, and that for every $x$ in $(a,b)$ the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759)

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

exists as a finite number or equals $\infty$ or $-\infty$. If finite, that limit equals $f'(x)$. An example where this version of the theorem applies is given by the real-valued [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506) function mapping $x \mapsto x^{1/3}$, whose [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) tends to infinity at the origin.

::: {#o11 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

The expression $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ gives the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of the line joining the points $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$, which is a [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) of the graph of $f$, while $f'(x)$ gives the slope of the tangent to the curve at the point $(x,f(x))$. Thus the mean value theorem says that given any chord of a smooth curve, we can find a point on the curve lying between the end-points of the chord such that the tangent of the curve at that point is parallel to the chord. The following proof illustrates this idea.

Define $g(x)=f(x)-rx$, where $r$ is a constant. Since $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, the same is true for $g$. We now want to choose $r$ so that $g$ satisfies the conditions of [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305). Namely

$$\begin{aligned} g(a)=g(b)&\iff f(a)-ra=f(b)-rb\\ &\iff r(b-a)=f(b)-f(a) \\ &\iff r=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} . \end{aligned}$$

By [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305), since $g$ is differentiable and $g(a)=g(b)$, there is some $c$ in $(a,b)$ for which $g'(c)=0$ , and it follows from the equality $g(x)=f(x)-rx$ that,

$$\begin{aligned} &g'(x) = f'(x) -r \\ & g'(c) = 0\\ &g'(c) = f'(c) - r = 0 \\ &\Rightarrow f'(c) = r = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Implications {#Implications}

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem 1"}

**Theorem 1:** Assume that $f$ is a continuous real-valued function defined on an arbitrary interval $I$ of the real line. If the derivative of $f$ at every [interior point](wikipedia://en/55610) of the interval $I$ exists and is zero, then $f$ is [constant](wikipedia://en/320861) on $I$.

:::

::: {#o14 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** Assume the derivative of $f$ at every [interior point](wikipedia://en/55610) of the interval $I$ exists and is zero. Let $(a, b)$ be an arbitrary open interval in $I$. By the mean value theorem, there exists a point $c$ in $(a, b)$ such that

$$0=f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$

This implies that $f(a) = f(b)$. Thus, $f$ is constant on the interior of $I$ and thus is constant on $I$ by continuity. (See below for a multivariable version of this result.)

**Remarks:**

- Only continuity of $f$, not differentiability, is needed at the endpoints of the interval $I$. No hypothesis of continuity needs to be stated if $I$ is an [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval), since the existence of a derivative at a point implies the continuity at this point. (See the section [continuity and differentiability](wikipedia://en/7921#Continuity_and_differentiability) of the article [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921).)
- The differentiability of $f$ can be relaxed to [one-sided differentiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-sided_derivatives), a proof is given in the article on [semi-differentiability](wikipedia://en/3084709).

:::

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem 2"}

**Theorem 2:** If $f'(x) = g'(x)$ for all $x$ in an interval $I$ of the domain of these functions, then $f - g$ is constant, i.e. $f = g + c$ where $c$ is a constant.

:::

::: {#o16 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** Let $F(x) = f(x) - g(x)$, then $F'(x)=f'(x)-g'(x)=0$ on the interval $I$. By theorem 1, $F$ is constant over $I$, so $f = g + c$ over $I$, where $c$ is a constant.

:::

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem 3"}

**Theorem 3:** If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ on an interval $I$, then the most general antiderivative of $f$ on $I$ is $F(x) + c$ where $c$ is a constant.

:::

::: {#o18 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** It directly follows from the theorem 2 above.

:::

## Cauchy's mean value theorem {#Cauchy's_mean_value_theorem}

**Cauchy's mean value theorem**, also known as the **extended mean value theorem**, is a generalization of the mean value theorem.[^n2] It states: if the functions $f$ and $g$ are both continuous on the closed interval $[a,b]$ and differentiable on the open interval $(a,b)$, then there exists some $c \in (a,b)$, such that

*Image: Cauchy.svg.* Geometrical meaning of Cauchy's theorem

$$(f(b)-f(a))g'(c)=(g(b)-g(a))f'(c).$$

Of course, if $g(a) \neq g(b)$ and $g'(c) \neq 0$, this is equivalent to:

$$\frac{f'(c)}{g'(c)}=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{g(b)-g(a)}.$$

Geometrically, this means that there is some [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the graph of the [curve](wikipedia://en/89246)[^o23]

$$\begin{cases}[a,b] \to \mathbb{R}^2\\t\mapsto (f(t),g(t))\end{cases}$$

which is [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to the line defined by the points $(f(a), g(a))$ and $(f(b), g(b))$. However, Cauchy's theorem does not claim the existence of such a tangent in all cases where $(f(a), g(a))$ and $(f(b), g(b))$ are distinct points, since it might be satisfied only for some value $c$ with $f'(c) = g'(c) = 0$, in other words a value for which the mentioned curve is [stationary](wikipedia://en/885651); in such points no tangent to the curve is likely to be defined at all. An example of this situation is the curve given by

$$t \mapsto \left(t^3,1-t^2\right),$$

which on the interval $[-1,1]$ goes from the point $(-1, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$, yet never has a horizontal tangent; however it has a stationary point (in fact a [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758)) at $t = 0$.

Cauchy's mean value theorem can be used to prove [L'Hôpital's rule](wikipedia://en/18531). The mean value theorem is the special case of Cauchy's mean value theorem when $g(t) = t$.

::: {#o25 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

The proof of Cauchy's mean value theorem is based on the same idea as the proof of the mean value theorem.

Define $h(x) = (g(b)-g(a))f(x) - (f(b)-f(a))g(x)$, then we easily see $h(a)=h(b)=f(a)g(b)-f(b)g(a)$.

Since $f$ and $g$ are continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, the same is true for $h$. All in all, $h$ satisfies the conditions of [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305). Consequently, there is some $c$ in $(a,b)$ for which $h'(c) = 0$. Now using the definition of $h$ we have:

$$0=h'(c)=(g(b)-g(a))f'(c)-(f(b)-f(a))g'(c)$$

The result easily follows.

## Mean value theorem in several variables {#Mean_value_theorem_in_several_variables}

The mean value theorem generalizes to real functions of multiple variables. The trick is to use parametrization to create a real function of one variable, and then apply the one-variable theorem.

Let $G$ be an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $f:G\to\mathbb{R}$ be a differentiable function. Fix points $x,y\in G$ such that the line segment between $x, y$ lies in $G$, and define $g(t)=f\big((1-t)x+ty\big)$. Since $g$ is a differentiable function in one variable, the mean value theorem gives:

$$g(1)-g(0)=g'(c)$$

for some $c$ between 0 and 1. But since $g(1)=f(y)$ and $g(0)=f(x)$, computing $g'(c)$ explicitly we have:

$$f(y)-f(x)=\nabla f\big((1-c)x+cy\big)\cdot (y-x)$$

where $\nabla$ denotes a [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) and $\cdot$ a [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093). This is an exact analog of the theorem in one variable (in the case $n=1$ this *is* the theorem in one variable). By the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128), the equation gives the estimate:

$$\Bigl|f(y)-f(x)\Bigr| \le \Bigl|\nabla f\big((1-c)x+cy\big)\Bigr|\ \Bigl|y - x\Bigr|.$$

In particular, when $G$ is convex and the partial derivatives of $f$ are bounded, $f$ is [Lipschitz continuous](wikipedia://en/51731) (and therefore [uniformly continuous](wikipedia://en/32337)).

As an application of the above, we prove that $f$ is constant if the open subset $G$ is connected and every partial derivative of $f$ is 0. Pick some point $x_0\in G$, and let $g(x)=f(x)-f(x_0)$. We want to show $g(x)=0$ for every $x\in G$. For that, let $E=\{x\in G:g(x)=0\}$. Then $E$ is closed in $G$ and nonempty. It is open too: for every $x\in E$ ,

$$\Big|g(y)\Big|=\Big|g(y)-g(x)\Big|\le (0)\Big|y-x\Big|=0$$

for every $y$ in open ball centered at $x$ and contained in $G$. Since $G$ is connected, we conclude $E=G$.

The above arguments are made in a coordinate-free manner; hence, they generalize to the case when $G$ is a subset of a Banach space.

:::

## Mean value theorem for vector-valued functions {#Mean_value_theorem_for_vector-valued_functions}

There is no exact analog of the mean value theorem for vector-valued functions (see below). However, there is an inequality which can be applied to many of the same situations to which the mean value theorem is applicable in the one dimensional case:[^n3]

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

*For a continuous vector-valued function $\mathbf{f}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^k$ differentiable on $(a,b)$, there exists a number $c\in(a,b)$ such that*

$$|\mathbf{f}(b)-\mathbf{f}(a)| \le (b-a)\left|\mathbf{f}'(c)\right|.$$

:::

::: {#o32 .proof cue=math-proof}

Take $\varphi(t) = (\textbf{f}(b) - \textbf{f}(a)) \cdot \textbf{f}(t)$. Then $\varphi$ is real-valued and thus, by the mean value theorem,

$\varphi(b) - \varphi(a) = \varphi'(c)(b-a)$ for some $c \in (a, b)$. Now,

$$\varphi(b) - \varphi(a) = |\textbf{f}(b) - \textbf{f}(a)|^2$$

and

$$\varphi'(c) = (\textbf{f}(b) - \textbf{f}(a)) \cdot \textbf{f}'(c).$$

Hence, using the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128), from the above equation, we get:

$|\textbf{f}(b) - \textbf{f}(a)|^2 \le |\textbf{f}(b) - \textbf{f}(a)| |\textbf{f}'(c) |(b-a).$ If $\textbf{f}(b) = \textbf{f}(a)$, the theorem holds trivially. Otherwise, dividing both sides by $|\textbf{f}(b) - \textbf{f}(a)|$ yields the theorem.

:::

::: {#o34 .statement cue=named-heading name="Mean value inequality"}

### Mean value inequality {#Mean_value_inequality}

[Jean Dieudonné](wikipedia://en/1070084) in his classic treatise *Foundations of Modern Analysis* discards the mean value theorem and replaces it by mean inequality as the proof is not constructive and one cannot find the mean value and in applications one only needs mean inequality. [Serge Lang](wikipedia://en/872991) in *Analysis I*uses the mean value theorem, in integral form, as an instant reflex but this use requires the continuity of the derivative. If one uses the [Henstock–Kurzweil integral](wikipedia://en/412708) one can have the mean value theorem in integral form without the additional assumption that derivative should be continuous as every derivative is Henstock–Kurzweil integrable.

The reason why there is no analog of mean value equality is the following: If $f : U \to \mathbf{R}^{m}$ is a differentiable function (where $U \subset \mathbf{R}^{n}$ is open) and if $x + th$, $x, h \in \mathbf{R}^{n}, t \in [0, 1]$ is the line segment in question (lying inside $U$), then one can apply the above parametrization procedure to each of the component functions $f_{i} (i = 1, \ldots, m)$ of *f* (in the above notation set $y = x + h$). In doing so one finds points $x + t_{i}h$ on the line segment satisfying

$$f_i(x+h) - f_i(x) = \nabla f_i (x + t_ih) \cdot h.$$

But generally there will not be a *single* point $x + t*h$ on the line segment satisfying

$$f_i(x+h) - f_i(x) = \nabla f_i (x + t^* h) \cdot h.$$

for all $i$ *simultaneously*. For example, define:

$$\begin{cases} f : [0, 2 \pi] \to \mathbb{R}^2 \\ f(x) = (\cos(x), \sin(x)) \end{cases}$$

Then $f(2\pi) - f(0) = \mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{R}^2$, but $f_1'(x) = -\sin (x)$ and $f_2'(x) = \cos (x)$ are never simultaneously zero as $x$ ranges over $\left[0, 2 \pi\right]$.

The above theorem implies the following:

::: {#o36 .statement cue=math-theorem name="Mean value inequality"}

**Mean value inequality[^o35].**

For a continuous function $\textbf{f} : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}^k$, if $\textbf{f}$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$, then

$$|\textbf{f}(b) - \textbf{f}(a)| \le (b-a)\sup_{(a, b)} |\textbf{f}'|.$$

:::

In fact, the above statement suffices for many applications and can be proved directly as follows. (We shall write $f$ for $\textbf{f}$ for readability.)

::: {#o37 .proof cue=math-proof}

First assume $f$ is differentiable at $a$ too. If $f'$ is unbounded on $(a, b)$, there is nothing to prove. Thus, assume $\sup_{(a, b)} |f'| < \infty$. Let $M > \sup_{(a, b)} |f'|$ be some real number. Let

$$E = \{ 0 \le t \le 1 \mid |f(a + t(b-a)) - f(a)| \le Mt(b-a) \}.$$

We want to show $1 \in E$. By continuity of $f$, the set $E$ is closed. It is also nonempty as $0$ is in it. Hence, the set $E$ has the largest element $s$. If $s = 1$, then $1 \in E$ and we are done. Thus suppose otherwise. For $1 > t > s$,

$$\begin{aligned} &|f(a + t(b-a)) - f(a)| \\ &\le |f(a + t(b-a)) - f(a+s(b - a)) - f'(a + s(b-a))(t-s)(b-a)| + |f'(a+s(b-a))|(t-s)(b-a) \\ &+|f(a + s(b-a)) - f(a)|. \end{aligned}$$

Let $\epsilon > 0$ be such that $M - \epsilon > \sup_{(a, b)} |f'|$. By the differentiability of $f$ at $a + s(b-a)$ (note $s$ may be 0), if $t$ is sufficiently close to $s$, the first term is $\le \epsilon (t-s)(b-a)$. The second term is $\le (M - \epsilon) (t-s)(b-a)$. The third term is $\le Ms(b-a)$. Hence, summing the estimates up, we get: $|f(a + t(b-a)) - f(a)| \le tM|b-a|$, a contradiction to the maximality of $s$. Hence, $1 = s \in M$ and that means:

$|f(b) - f(a)| \le M(b-a).$ Since $M$ is arbitrary, this then implies the assertion. Finally, if $f$ is not differentiable at $a$, let $a' \in (a, b)$ and apply the first case to $f$ restricted on $[a', b]$, giving us:

$|f(b) - f(a')| \le (b-a')\sup_{(a, b)} |f'|$ since $(a', b) \subset (a, b)$. Letting $a' \to a$ finishes the proof.

:::

:::

## Cases where the theorem cannot be applied {#Cases_where_the_theorem_cannot_be_applied}

All conditions for the mean value theorem are necessary:

1. **$\boldsymbol{f}$ is differentiable on $\boldsymbol{(a,b)}$**
1. **$\boldsymbol{f}$ is continuous on $\boldsymbol{[a,b]}$**
1. **$\boldsymbol{f}$ is real-valued**

When one of the above conditions is not satisfied, the mean value theorem is not valid in general, and so it cannot be applied.

*Image: Absolute Value.svg.*

The necessity of the first condition can be seen by the counterexample where the function $f$ given by $f(x)=|x|$ on $[-1,1]$ is non-differentiable at at least one point in $(-1,1)$.

The necessity of the second condition can be seen by the counterexample where the function $f$ given by

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{at }x=0 \\ 0, & \text{if }x\in( 0,1] \end{cases}$$

satisfies criteria 1 since $f'(x)=0$ on $(0,1)$ but not criteria 2 since $f$ is not left-continuous at $0$. The average rate of change of $f$ over $[0,1]$ is given by

$$\frac{f(1)-f(0)}{1-0}=-1$$

but there doesn't exist $x\in (0,1)$ such that $f'(x)=-1$, so the theorem doesn't hold.

The theorem is false if a differentiable function is complex-valued instead of real-valued. For example, if $f(x) = e^{xi}$ for all real $x$, then

$$f(2\pi)-f(0)=0=0(2\pi-0)$$

while $f'(x)\ne 0$ for any real $x$.

:::

## Mean value theorems for definite integrals {#Mean_value_theorems_for_definite_integrals}

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="First mean value theorem for definite integrals"}

### First mean value theorem for definite integrals {#First_mean_value_theorem_for_definite_integrals}

*Image: 积分中值定理.jpg.* Geometrically: interpreting *f*(*c*) as the height of a rectangle and *b* – *a* as the width, this rectangle has the same area as the region below the curve from *a* to *b*[^o44]

Let *f* : \[*a*, *b*\] → **R** be a continuous function. Then there exists *c* in (*a*, *b*) such that

$$\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = f(c)(b - a).$$

This is proved by letting $m$ and $M$ be the minimum and maximum values of $f$ on $[a,b]$, respectively. Then $m \le \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\le M$. By the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884), since $f$ maps $[a,b]$ onto $[m,M]$, there exists a $c$ with the required property.

Since the mean value of *f* on \[*a*, *b*\] is defined as

$$\frac{1}{b-a} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx,$$

we can interpret the conclusion as *f* achieves its mean value at some *c* in (*a*, *b*).[^o45]

In general, if *f* : \[*a*, *b*\] → **R** is continuous and *g* is an integrable function that does not change sign on \[*a*, *b*\], then there exists *c* in (*a*, *b*) such that

$$\int_a^b f(x) g(x) \, dx = f(c) \int_a^b g(x) \, dx.$$

:::

::: {#o47 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Second mean value theorem for definite integrals"}

### Second mean value theorem for definite integrals {#Second_mean_value_theorem_for_definite_integrals}

There are various slightly different theorems called the **second mean value theorem for definite integrals**. A commonly found version is as follows:

If $G : [a,b]\to \mathbb{R}$ is a positive [monotonically decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function) function and $\varphi : [a,b]\to \mathbb{R}$ is an integrable function, then there exists a number *x* in (*a*, *b*\] such that

$$\int_a^b G(t)\varphi(t)\,dt = G(a^+) \int_a^x \varphi(t)\,dt.$$

Here $G(a^+)$ stands for ${\lim_{x\to a^+}G(x)}$, the existence of which follows from the conditions. Note that it is essential that the interval (*a*, *b*\] contains *b*. A variant not having this requirement is:[^o48]

If $G : [a,b]\to \mathbb{R}$ is a [monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function) (not necessarily decreasing and positive) function and $\varphi : [a,b]\to \mathbb{R}$ is an integrable function, then there exists a number *x* in (*a*, *b*) such that

$$\int_a^b G(t)\varphi(t)\,dt = G(a^+) \int_a^x \varphi(t)\,dt + G(b^-) \int_x^b \varphi(t)\,dt.$$

If the function $G$ returns a multi-dimensional vector, then the MVT for integration is not true, even if the domain of $G$ is also multi-dimensional.

For example, consider the following 2-dimensional function defined on an $n$-dimensional cube:

$$\begin{cases} G: [0,2\pi]^n \to \mathbb{R}^2 \\ G(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \left(\sin(x_1 + \cdots + x_n), \cos(x_1 + \cdots + x_n) \right) \end{cases}$$

Then, by symmetry it is easy to see that the mean value of $G$ over its domain is (0,0):

$$\int_{[0,2\pi]^n} G(x_1,\dots,x_n) dx_1 \cdots dx_n = (0,0)$$

However, there is no point in which $G=(0,0)$, because $|G|=1$ everywhere.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Linear algebra {#Linear_algebra}

Assume that $f, g,$ and $h$ are differentiable functions on $(a,b)$ that are continuous on $[a,b]$. Define

$$D(x) = \begin{vmatrix} f(x) & g(x) & h(x)\\ f(a) & g(a) & h(a)\\ f(b) & g(b) & h(b) \end{vmatrix}$$

There exists $c\in(a,b)$ such that $D'(c)=0$.

Notice that

$$D'(x) = \begin{vmatrix} f'(x) & g'(x)& h'(x)\\ f(a) & g(a) & h(a)\\ f(b) & g(b)& h(b) \end{vmatrix}$$

and if we place $h(x)=1$, we get **Cauchy's mean value theorem**. If we place $h(x)=1$ and $g(x)=x$ we get **Lagrange's mean value theorem**.

The proof of the generalization is quite simple: each of $D(a)$ and $D(b)$ are [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468) with two identical rows, hence $D(a)=D(b)=0$. Then [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305) implies that there exists $c\in (a,b)$ such that $D'(c)=0$.

### Probability theory {#Probability_theory}

Let *X* and *Y* be non-negative [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) such that E\[*X*\] \< E\[*Y*\] \< ∞ and $X \leq_{st} Y$ (i.e. *X* is smaller than *Y* in the [usual stochastic order](wikipedia://en/15088121)). Then there exists an absolutely continuous non-negative random variable *Z* having [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487)

$$f_Z(x)={\Pr(Y>x)-\Pr(X>x)\over {\rm E}[Y]-{\rm E}[X]}\,, \qquad x\geqslant 0.$$

Let *g* be a [measurable](wikipedia://en/44055) and [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) such that E\[*g*(*X*)\], E\[*g*(*Y*)\] \< ∞, and let its derivative *g′* be measurable and [Riemann-integrable](wikipedia://en/26390) on the interval \[*x*, *y*\] for all *y* ≥ *x* ≥ 0. Then, E\[*g′*(*Z*)\] is finite and[^o53]

$${\rm E}[g(Y)]-{\rm E}[g(X)]={\rm E}[g'(Z)]\,[{\rm E}(Y)-{\rm E}(X)].$$

### Complex analysis {#Complex_analysis}

As noted above, the theorem does not hold for differentiable complex-valued functions. Instead, a generalization of the theorem is stated such:[^o55]

Let *f* : Ω → **C** be a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) on the open convex set Ω, and let *a* and *b* be distinct points in Ω. Then there exist points *u*, *v* on the interior of the line segment from *a* to *b* such that

$$\operatorname{Re}(f'(u)) = \operatorname{Re}\left ( \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \right),$$

$$\operatorname{Im}(f'(v)) = \operatorname{Im}\left ( \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \right).$$

Where Re() is the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_Number) and Im() is the [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_Number) of a complex-valued function.
