::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Life expectancy"}

**Human life expectancy** is a statistical measure of the estimate of the average remaining years of life at a given age. The most commonly used measure is **life expectancy at birth** (**LEB**, in demographic notation *e*<sub>0</sub>, where *e*<sub>x</sub> denotes the average life remaining at age *x*). This can be defined in two ways. *Cohort* LEB is the mean length of life of a birth [cohort](wikipedia://en/1509191) (in this case, all individuals born in a given year) and can be computed only for cohorts born so long ago that all their members have died. *Period* LEB is the mean length of life of a [hypothetical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothetical) cohort[^o4][^o5] assumed to be exposed, from birth through death, to the [mortality rates](wikipedia://en/191305) observed at a given year.[^o6] National LEB figures reported by national agencies and international organizations for human populations are estimates of *period* LEB.

:::

Human remains from the early [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) indicate an LEB of 24.[^o7] In 2019, world LEB was 73.3.[^o8] A combination of high [infant mortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality) and deaths in young adulthood from accidents, [epidemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemic), plagues, wars, and childbirth, before modern medicine was widely available, significantly lowers LEB. For example, a society with a LEB of 40 would have relatively few people dying at exactly 40: most will die before 30 or after 55. In populations with high infant mortality rates, LEB is highly sensitive to the rate of death in the first few years of life. Because of this sensitivity, LEB can be grossly misinterpreted, leading to the belief that a population with a low LEB would have a small proportion of older people.[^o9] A different measure, such as life expectancy at age 5 (e<sub>5</sub>), can be used to exclude the effect of infant mortality to provide a simple measure of overall mortality rates other than in early childhood. For instance, in a society with a life expectancy of 30, it may nevertheless be common to have a 40-year remaining timespan at age 5.

Aggregate population measures—such as the proportion of the population in various age groups—are also used alongside individual-based measures—such as formal life expectancy—when analyzing population structure and dynamics. Pre-modern societies had universally higher mortality rates and lower life expectancies at every age for both males and females.

Life expectancy, [longevity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longevity), and [maximum lifespan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_lifespan) are not synonymous. Longevity refers to the relatively long lifespan of some members of a population. Maximum lifespan is the age at death for the longest-lived individual of a species. Mathematically, life expectancy is denoted $e_x$ and is the mean number of years of life remaining at a given age $x$, with a particular mortality.[^o10] Because life expectancy is an average, a particular person may die many years before or after the expected survival.

Life expectancy is also used in plant or animal [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology),[^o11] and in [life tables](wikipedia://en/1614128) (also known as [actuarial](wikipedia://en/43405) tables). The concept of life expectancy may also be used in the context of manufactured objects,[^o12] though the related term [shelf life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelf_life) is commonly used for consumer products, and the terms "mean time to breakdown" and "[mean time between failures](wikipedia://en/63397)" are used in engineering.

## History {#History}

The earliest documented work on life expectancy was done in the 1660s by [John Graunt](wikipedia://en/30875381),[^o14] [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127), and [Lodewijck Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lodewijck_Huygens).[^o15]

## Human patterns {#Human_patterns}

### Maximum {#Maximum}

[The longest verified lifespan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oldest_people) for any human is that of French woman [Jeanne Calment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeanne_Calment), who is verified as having lived to age 122 years, 164 days, between 21 February 1875 and 4 August 1997. This is referred to as the "[maximum life span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_life_span)", which is the upper boundary of life, the maximum number of years any human is known to have lived. Although maximum life expectancy is around 125 years, genetic enhancements could allow humans to live for a maximum of 245 years, according to InsideTracker.[^o18] According to a study by biologists Bryan G. Hughes and Siegfried Hekimi, there is no evidence for a limit on human lifespan.[^o19][^o20] However, this view has been questioned on the basis of error patterns.[^o21] A theoretical study shows that the maximum life expectancy at birth is limited by the human life characteristic value δ, which is around 104 years.[^o22]

English life expectancy at birth averaged about 36 years in the 17th and 18th centuries, one of the highest levels in the world although infant and child mortality remained higher than in later periods. Life expectancy was under 25 years in the early [Colony of Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_of_Virginia),[^o73] and in seventeenth-century New England, about 40% died before reaching adulthood.[^o74] During the [Industrial Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution), the life expectancy of children increased dramatically.[^o75] Recorded deaths among children under the age of 5 years fell in London from 74.5% of the recorded births in 1730–49 to 31.8% in 1810–29,[^o76][^o77] though this overstates mortality and its fall because of net immigration (hence more dying in the metropolis than were born there) and incomplete registration (particularly of births, and especially in the earlier period). English life expectancy at birth reached 41 years in the 1840s, 43 in the 1870s and 46 in the 1890s, though infant mortality remained at around 150 per thousand throughout this period.

*Image: Life expectancy in 1800, 1950, and 2015.png.* Life expectancy in 1800, 1950, and 2015 – visualization by [Our World in Data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_World_in_Data)

[Public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health) measures are credited with much of the recent increase in life expectancy. During the 20th century, despite a brief drop due to the [1918 flu pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1918_flu_pandemic),[^o78] the average lifespan in the United States increased by more than 30 years, of which 25 years can be attributed to advances in public health.[^o79]

### Regional variations {#Regional_variations}

There are great variations in life expectancy between different parts of the world, mostly caused by differences in [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health), medical care, and diet.[^o81]

Human beings are expected to live on average 60 years in [Eswatini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eswatini)[^o82] and 82.6 years in Japan. An analysis published in 2011 in *[The Lancet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lancet)* attributes Japanese life expectancy to [equal opportunities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_opportunities), excellent [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health), and a healthy diet.[^o83][^o84]

The [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization) announced that the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) reversed the trend of steady gain in life expectancy at birth. The pandemic wiped out nearly a decade of progress in improving life expectancy.[^o85]

#### Africa {#Africa}

*Image: Comparison subsaharan life expectancy.svg.* Graphs of life expectancy at birth for some sub-Saharan countries showing the fall in the 1990s primarily due to the [HIV pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV_pandemic)[^o87]

During the last 200 years, African countries have generally not had the same improvements in mortality rates that have been enjoyed by countries in Asia, Latin America, and Europe.[^o88][^o89] This is most apparent by the impact of [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDS) on many African countries. According to projections made by the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations) in 2002, the life expectancy at birth for 2010–2015 (if [HIV/AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS) did not exist) would have been:[^o90]

- \70.7 years instead of 31.6 years, Botswana
- \69.9 years instead of 41.5 years, South Africa
- \70.5 years instead of 31.8 years, Zimbabwe

#### Eastern Europe {#Eastern_Europe}

On average, [eastern Europeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) tend to live shorter lives than their western counterparts. For example, [Spaniards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaniards) from [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid) can expect to live to 85, but [Bulgarians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarians) from the region of [Severozapaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severozapaden) are predicted to live just past their 73rd birthday. This is in large part due to poor health habits, such as heavy smoking and high alcoholism in the region, and environmental factors, such as high air pollution.[^o92]

#### United States {#United_States}

*Image: Life expectancy post-COVID.png.* Life expectancy from 1990 to 2021 in the US, UK, Netherlands, and Austria, showing a fall in US life expectancy around 2019

In 2024, average life expectancy at birth in the United States reached 79.0 years (76.5 years for men and 81.4 years for women), a record high for the country.[^o95] According to the [US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Centers_for_Disease_Control_and_Prevention), the chief federal agency for vital statistics, life expectancy increased 0.6 years in 2024, mostly due to a significant decline in fatal drug overdoses; this followed similar increases in US life expectancy in 2022 and 2023.[^o96] Average life expectancy was 76.4 years in 2021,[^o97]p. 1 having declined for the second year in a row—the first two-year drop recorded in the United States since 1961–1963.[^o98] American life expectancy has otherwise generally improved, from 73.7 years in 1980 to 79 years in 2024. However, compared to peer industrialized countries, the United States has fallen significantly behind in life expectancy, with the gap between the US and those countries having increased from 0.9 years in 1980 to 4.1 years in 2023.[^o99]

From 2019 until 2022, average life expectancy fell in the United States, with the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) being responsible for approximately 61% of the total decrease. Increases in mortality from [unintentional injuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unintentional_injuries), [heart disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease), [homicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homicide), and [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes) contributed to 11.7%, 5.8%, 2.9%, and 2.8%, respectively, of the decrease during that period. Life expectancy has also varied by racial and ethnic group, with non-Hispanic Asians having the highest life expectancy and non-Hispanic American Indians having the lowest.

In what has been described as a "life expectancy crisis", there were a total of 14.7 million "missing Americans" from 1980 to 2023, deaths that would have been averted if it had the standard mortality rate of "[wealthy nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_country)".[^o100] The annual number of "missing Americans" has been increasing, with 622,534 in 2019 alone.[^o101]

[Black Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Americans) have generally shorter life expectancies than their [White American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_American) counterparts. For example, white Americans in 2010 are expected to live until age 78.9, but black Americans only until age 75.1. This 3.8-year gap, however, is the lowest it has been since 1975 at the latest, the greatest difference being 7.1 years in 1993.[^o102] In contrast, [Asian American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_American) women live the longest of all ethnic and gender groups in the United States, with a life expectancy of 85.8 years.[^o103] The life expectancy of [Hispanic Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanic_Americans) is 81.2 years.[^o102]

:::

#### Japan {#Japan}

In 2023, the life expectancy was 84.5 in Japan, 4.2 years above the [OECD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OECD) average, and one of the highest in the world. This represents a significant change from the 1960s, when Japan's life expectancy used to be among the lower [G7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G7) countries, due to high rates of [cerebrovascular disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebrovascular_disease) and [stomach cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomach_cancer). Since then, these rates have significantly decreased, as have the rates of [coronary artery disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronary_artery_disease) and other types of cancer, which were already low. High intake of fish and plant food with a modest intake of meat, milk, and dairy products is thought to contribute to these changes.[^o105]

#### In cities {#In_cities}

Cities also experience a wide range of life expectancy based on neighborhood breakdowns. This is largely due to economic clustering and poverty conditions that tend to associate based on geographic location. Multi-generational poverty found in struggling neighborhoods also contributes. In American cities such as [Cincinnati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati), the life expectancy gap between low income and high-income neighborhoods touches 20 years.[^o107]

### Economic circumstances {#Economic_circumstances}

*Image: Life expectancy vs healthcare spending.jpg.* Life expectancy vs healthcare spending of rich OECD countries. [US average of \$10,447 in 2018](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_total_health_expenditure_per_capita).[^o109]

Economic circumstances also affect life expectancy. For example, in the United Kingdom, life expectancy in the wealthiest and richest areas is several years higher than in the poorest areas. This may reflect factors such as diet and lifestyle, as well as access to medical care. It may also reflect a selective effect: people with chronic life-threatening illnesses are less likely to become wealthy or to reside in affluent areas.[^o110] In [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow), the disparity is [amongst the highest in the world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow_effect): life expectancy for males in the heavily deprived [Calton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calton,_Glasgow) area stands at 54, which is 28 years less than in the affluent area of [Lenzie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenzie), which is only 8 km away.[^o111][^o112]

A study published in the [American Geriatrics Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Geriatrics_Society) found that the average life expectancy of the Chinese emperors (which have much wealth) from the first Qin Dynasty (221–207 BC) to the last Qing Dynasty, was 41.3 years. This is much lower than that of the Buddhist monks (66.9 years) traditional Chinese doctors (75.1 years) and the emperors' servant, who survived to 71.3 years (range 55–94), during the same time.[^o113]

A 2013 study found a pronounced relationship between [economic inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_inequality) and life expectancy.[^o114] However, in contrast, a study by José A. Tapia Granados and [Ana Diez Roux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ana_Diez-Roux) at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) found that life expectancy actually *increased* during the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression), and during recessions and depressions in general.[^o115] The authors suggest that when people are working harder during prosperous economic times, they undergo more [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28biology%29), exposure to [pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollution), and the likelihood of injury among other longevity-limiting factors.

Life expectancy is also likely to be affected by exposure to high levels of [highway air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roadway_air_dispersion_model) or industrial [air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution). This is one way that occupation can have a major effect on life expectancy. Coal miners (and in prior generations, asbestos cutters) have lower life expectancies than average. Other factors affecting an individual's life expectancy are genetic disorders, drug use, [tobacco smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco_smoking), excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, access to health care, diet, and exercise. In the United States, people without [health insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_insurance) have reduced life expectancy.[^o116]

### Sex differences {#Sex_differences}

*Image: Healthy life expectancy bar chart -world -sex.png.* Life expectancy and healthy life expectancy by sex in 2019[^o1]

*Image: LifeExpectancyBetweenFemaleAndMales.jpg.* Pink: Countries where female life expectancy at birth is higher than males. Blue (excluding oceans): A few countries in southern Africa where females have shorter lives due to [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDS). (2015)[^o119]

*Image: Comparison of male and female life expectancy -world.svg.* *"Gender Die Gap"*: global female life expectancy gap at birth for countries and territories as defined by [WHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization) for 2019. Open the original [svg-file](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b3/Comparison_of_male_and_female_life_expectancy_-world.svg) and hover over a bubble to show its data. The area of the bubbles is proportional to country population based on estimation of the [UN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN).

Modern female human life expectancy is greater than that of males, despite females having higher morbidity rates (see [health survival paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_survival_paradox)). There are several potential reasons for this. Traditional arguments tend to favor sociology-environmental factors: historically, men have consumed more [tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco), [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_drink), and [drugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug) than women in most societies, and are more likely to die from many associated diseases such as [lung cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung_cancer), [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis), and [cirrhosis of the liver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirrhosis_of_the_liver).[^o120] Men are also more likely to die from injuries, whether unintentional (such as [occupational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_safety_and_health), [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War), or [car wrecks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car_wrecks)) or intentional ([suicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide)).[^o120] Men are also more likely to die from the leading causes of death (some already stated) than women. Some of these in the United States include cancer of the respiratory system, motor vehicle accidents, suicide, cirrhosis of the liver, emphysema, prostate cancer, and coronary heart disease.[^o18] These far outweigh the female mortality rate from breast cancer and cervical cancer. In the past, [mortality rates for females in child-bearing age groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maternal_death) were higher than for males at the same age.

A paper from 2015 found that female foetuses have a higher mortality rate than male foetuses.[^o121] This finding contradicts papers dating from 2002 and earlier that describe male foetuses as having higher in-utero mortality rates.[^o122][^o123][^o124] Among the smallest premature babies (those under 2 lb), females have a higher survival rate. At the other extreme, about 90% of individuals aged 110 are female. The difference in life expectancy between men and women in the United States dropped from 7.8 years in 1979 to 5.3 years in 2005, with women expected to live to age 80.1 in 2005.[^o125] Data from the United Kingdom shows the gap in life expectancy between men and women decreasing in later life. This may be attributable to the effects of infant mortality and young adult death rates.[^o126]

Some argue that shorter male life expectancy is another manifestation of the general rule, seen in all mammal species, that larger-sized individuals within a species tend, on average, to have shorter lives.[^o127][^o128] This biological difference occurs because women have more resistance to infections and degenerative diseases.[^o18]

In her extensive review of the existing literature, Kalben concluded that the fact that women live longer than men was observed at least as far back as 1750 and that, with relatively equal treatment, modern males in all parts of the world experience greater mortality than females. However, Kalben's study was restricted to data in Western Europe alone, where the demographic transition occurred relatively early. United Nations statistics from mid-twentieth century onward, show that in all parts of the world, females have a higher life expectancy at age 60 than males.[^o129] Of 72 selected causes of death, only 6 yielded greater female than male age-adjusted death rates in 1998 in the United States. Except for birds, males of almost all animal species studied have higher mortality than females. Evidence suggests that the sex mortality differential in humans is due to both biological/genetic and environmental/behavioral risk and protective factors.[^o122]

One recent suggestion is that [mitochondrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondria) mutations which shorten lifespan continue to be expressed in males (but less so in females) because mitochondria are inherited only through the mother. By contrast, [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection) weeds out mitochondria that reduce female survival; therefore, such mitochondria are less likely to be passed on to the next generation. This thus suggests that females tend to live longer than males. The authors claim that this is a partial explanation.[^o130][^o131]

Another explanation is the [unguarded X hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unguarded_X_hypothesis).[^o132] According to this hypothesis, one reason for why the average lifespan of males is shorter than females––by 18% on average, according to the study––is that they have a [Y chromosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y_chromosome) which cannot protect an individual from harmful genes expressed on the X chromosome, while a duplicate X chromosome, as present in female organisms, can ensure harmful genes are not [expressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_expression).[^o133][^o134] However, the contribution of the unguarded X hypothesis to observed sex differences in lifespan has been questioned.[^o135][^o136]

In developed countries, starting around 1880, death rates decreased faster among women, leading to differences in mortality rates between males and females. Before 1880, death rates were the same. In people born after 1900, the death rate of 50- to 70-year-old men was double that of women of the same age. Men may be more vulnerable to cardiovascular disease, but this susceptibility was evident only after deaths from other causes, such as infections, started to decline.[^o137] Most of the difference in life expectancy between the sexes is accounted for by differences in the rate of death by cardiovascular diseases among persons aged 50–70.[^o138]

:::

### Genetics {#Genetics}

The [heritability](wikipedia://en/155624) of lifespan is estimated to be less than 10%, meaning the majority of [variation](wikipedia://en/1012687) in lifespan is attributable due to differences in environment rather than [genetic variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_variation).[^o140] However, researchers have identified regions of the [genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome) which can influence the length of life and the number of years lived in good health. For example, a [genome-wide association study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome-wide_association_study) of 1 million lifespans found 12 [genetic loci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_loci) which influenced lifespan by modifying susceptibility to [cardiovascular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease) and [smoking-related disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking#Health_effects).[^o141] The locus with the largest effect is [APOE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apolipoprotein_E). Carriers of the APOE ε4 [allele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele) live approximately one year less than average (per copy of the ε4 allele), mainly due to increased risk of [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease).[^o141]

*Image: Healthspan, parental lifespan, and longevity are highly genetically correlated.webp.* "Healthspan, parental lifespan, and longevity are highly genetically correlated."[^o142]

In July 2020, scientists identified 10 genomic loci with consistent effects across multiple lifespan-related traits, including [healthspan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthspan), lifespan, and [longevity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longevity).[^o142] The genes affected by variation in these loci highlighted [haem metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_iron_metabolism) as a promising candidate for further research within the field. This study suggests that high levels of iron in the blood likely reduce, and genes involved in metabolising iron likely increase healthy years of life in humans.[^o143]

A follow-up study which investigated the genetics of [frailty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frailty_syndrome) and self-rated health in addition to healthspan, lifespan, and longevity also highlighted haem metabolism as an important pathway, and found genetic variants which lower blood protein levels of [LPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipoprotein%28a%29) and [VCAM1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VCAM1) were associated with increased healthy lifespan.[^o144]

### Centenarians {#Centenarians}

In developed countries, the number of centenarians is increasing at approximately 5.5% per year, which doubles the centenarian population every 13 years, pushing it from some 455,000 in 2009 to 4.1 million in 2050.[^o146] Japan has the highest ratio of centenarians (347 for every 1 million inhabitants in September 2010). [Shimane Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimane_Prefecture) had an estimated 743 centenarians per million inhabitants.[^o147]

In the United States, the number of centenarians grew from 32,194 in 1980 to 71,944 in November 2010 (232 centenarians per million inhabitants).[^o148]

### Mental illness {#Mental_illness}

Mental illness is reported to occur in approximately 18% of the American population.[^o151][^o152]

*Image: Smi graph by Mark.png.* Life expectancy in the seriously mentally ill is much shorter than the general population.[^o153]

The mentally ill have been shown to have a 10- to 25-year reduction in life expectancy.[^o154] The reduction of lifespan in the mentally ill population compared to the mentally stable population has been studied and documented.[^o155][^o156][^o157][^o158][^o159]

The greater mortality of people with mental disorders may be due to death from injury, from [co-morbid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-morbid) conditions, or medication side effects.[^o160] For instance, psychiatric medications can increase the risk of developing [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olanzapine#Metabolic_effects).[^o161][^o162][^o163][^o164] The psychiatric medication [olanzapine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olanzapine) can increase risk of developing [agranulocytosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agranulocytosis), among other comorbidities.[^o165][^o166] Psychiatric medicines also affect the [gastrointestinal tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_tract); the mentally ill have a four times risk of gastrointestinal disease.[^o167][^o168][^o169]

As of 2020 and the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic), researchers have found an increased risk of death in the mentally ill.[^o170][^o171][^o172]

:::

### Other illnesses {#Other_illnesses}

The life expectancy of people with diabetes, which is 9.3% of the U.S. population, is reduced by roughly 10–20 years.[^o174][^o175] People over 60 years old with [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease) have about a 50% life expectancy of 3–10 years.[^o176] Other people that tend to have a lower life expectancy than average include transplant recipients[^o177] and the obese.[^o178]

### Education {#Education}

Education on all levels has been strongly associated with increased life expectancy.[^o180] This association may be due partly to higher income,[^o181] which can lead to increased life expectancy. Despite the association, among identical twin pairs with different education levels, there is only weak evidence of a relationship between educational attainment and adult mortality.[^o180]

According to a paper from 2015, the mortality rate for the Caucasian population in the United States from 1993 to 2001 is four times higher for those who did not complete high school compared to those who have at least 16 years of education.[^o180] In fact, within the U.S. adult population, people with less than a high school education have the shortest life expectancies.

Preschool education also plays a large role in life expectancy. It was found that high-quality early-stage childhood education had positive effects on health. Researchers discovered this by analyzing the results of the [Carolina Abecedarian Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abecedarian_Early_Intervention_Project), finding that the disadvantaged children who were randomly assigned to treatment had lower instances of risk factors for cardiovascular and metabolic diseases in their mid-30s.[^o182]

## Evolution and aging rate {#Evolution_and_aging_rate}

Various species of plants and animals, including humans, have different lifespans. Evolutionary theory states that organisms which—by virtue of their defenses or lifestyle—live for long periods and avoid accidents, disease, predation, etc. are likely to have genes that code for slow aging, which often translates to good cellular repair. One theory is that if predation or accidental deaths prevent most individuals from living to an old age, there will be less natural selection to increase the intrinsic life span.[^o184] That finding was supported in a classic study of opossums by Austad;[^o185] however, the opposite relationship was found in an equally prominent study of guppies by Reznick.[^o186][^o187]

One prominent and very popular theory states that lifespan can be lengthened by a tight budget for food energy called [caloric restriction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_restriction).[^o188] Caloric restriction observed in many animals (most notably mice and rats) shows a near doubling of life span from a very limited calorific intake. Support for the theory has been bolstered by several new studies linking lower [basal metabolic rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_metabolic_rate) to increased life expectancy.[^o189][^o190][^o191] That is the key to why animals like giant [tortoises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tortoise) can live so long.[^o192] Studies of humans with life spans of at least 100 have shown a link to decreased thyroid activity, resulting in their lowered metabolic rate.

The ability of [skin fibroblasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dermal_fibroblast) to perform [DNA repair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_repair) after [UV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UV) irradiation was measured in [shrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrew), [mouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouse), [rat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rat), [hamster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamster), [cow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cow), [elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elephant) and [human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human).[^o193] It was found that DNA repair capability increased systematically with species [life span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_life_span). Since this original study in 1974, at least 14 additional studies were performed on [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal) to test this correlation.[^o194] In all, but two of these studies, lifespan correlated with DNA repair levels, suggesting that DNA repair capability contributes to life expectancy.[^o194] See [DNA damage theory of aging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_damage_theory_of_aging).

In a broad survey of zoo animals, no relationship was found between investment of the animal in reproduction and its life span.[^o195]

## Calculation {#Calculation}

*Image: Survivaltree.png.* A survival tree to explain the calculations of life-expectancy. Red numbers indicate a chance of survival at a specific age, and blue ones indicate age-specific death rates.

In [actuarial notation](wikipedia://en/852721), the probability of surviving from age $x$ to age $x+n$ is denoted $\,_np_x\!$ and the probability of dying during age $x$ (i.e. between ages $x$ and $x+1$) is denoted $q_x\!$ . For example, if 10% of a group of people alive at their 90th birthday die before their 91st birthday, the age-specific death probability at 90 would be 10%. This probability describes the *likelihood* of dying at that age, and is not the *rate* at which people of that age die. It can be shown that

::: {#o197 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$${}_kp_x \, q_{x+k} = {}_k p_x - {}_{k+1}p_x \tag{1}$$

:::

The *curtate future lifetime*, denoted $K(x)$, is a discrete random variable representing the remaining lifetime at age $x$, rounded down to whole years. Life expectancy, more technically called the *curtate expected lifetime* and denoted *$\,e_x\!$ ,* is the [mean](wikipedia://en/9653) of $K(x)$—that is to say, the expected number of whole years of life remaining, assuming survival to age $x$.[^o198] So,

::: {#o199 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$e_x = \operatorname{E}[K(x)] = \sum_{k=0}^\infty k\, \cdot \Pr(K(x)=k) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k\, \,_kp_x \,\, q_{x+k} \tag{2}$$

:::

Substituting ((1)) into the sum and simplifying gives the final result[^o200]

::: {#o201 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$e_x = \sum_{k=1}^\infty {} \, \,\, _k p_x \tag{3}$$

:::

If the assumption is made that, on average, people live a half year on the year of their death, the complete life expectancy at age $x$ would be $e_x + 1/2$, which is denoted by e̊<sub>x</sub>, and is the intuitive definition of life expectancy.

By definition, life expectancy is an [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612). It can also be calculated by integrating the survival curve from 0 to positive infinity (or equivalently to the maximum lifespan, sometimes called 'omega'). For an extinct or completed [cohort](wikipedia://en/1509191) (all people born in the year 1850, for example), it can of course simply be calculated by averaging the ages at death. For cohorts with some survivors, it is estimated by using mortality experience in recent years. The estimates are called period cohort life expectancies.

The starting point for calculating life expectancy is the [age-specific death rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age-specific_death_rate) of the population members. If a large amount of data is available, a [statistical population](wikipedia://en/27585) can be created that allow the age-specific death rates to be simply taken as the mortality rates actually experienced at each age (the number of deaths divided by the number of years "exposed to risk" in each data cell). However, it is customary to apply smoothing to remove (as much as possible) the random statistical fluctuations from one year of age to the next. In the past, a very simple model used for this purpose was the [Gompertz function](wikipedia://en/3350262), but more sophisticated methods are now used.[^o202] The most common modern methods include:

- fitting a mathematical formula (such as the Gompertz function, or an extension of it) to the data.
- looking at an established [mortality table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortality_table) derived from a larger population and making a simple adjustment to it (such as multiplying by a constant factor) to fit the data. (In cases of relatively small amounts of data.)
- looking at the mortality rates actually experienced at each age and applying a piecewise model (such as by [cubic splines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_splines)) to fit the data. (In cases of relatively large amounts of data.)

The age-specific death rates are calculated separately for separate groups of data that are believed to have different mortality rates (such as males and females, or smokers and non-smokers) and are then used to calculate a [life table](wikipedia://en/1614128) from which one can calculate the probability of surviving to each age. While the data required are easily identified in the case of humans, the computation of life expectancy of industrial products and wild animals involves more indirect techniques. The life expectancy and demography of wild animals are often estimated by capturing, marking, and recapturing them.[^o203] The life of a product, more often termed [shelf life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelf_life), is also computed using similar methods. In the case of long-lived components, such as those used in critical applications (e.g. aircraft), methods like [accelerated aging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerated_aging) are used to model the life expectancy of a component.[^o12]

The life expectancy statistic is usually based on past mortality experience and assumes that the same age-specific mortality rates will continue. Thus, such life expectancy figures need to be adjusted for temporal trends before calculating how long a currently living individual of a particular age is expected to live. Period life expectancy remains a commonly used statistic to summarize the current health status of a population. However, for some purposes, such as pensions calculations, it is usual to adjust the life table used by assuming that age-specific death rates will continue to decrease over the years, as they have usually done in the past. That is often done by simply extrapolating past trends, but some models exist to account for the evolution of mortality, like the [Lee–Carter model](wikipedia://en/28975424).[^o204]

As discussed above, on an individual basis, some factors correlate with longer life. Factors that are associated with variations in life expectancy include family history, marital status, economic status, physique, exercise, diet, drug use (including smoking and alcohol consumption), disposition, education, environment, sleep, climate, and health care.[^o18] As of 2025, some AI apps claim to be able to predict individual life expectancy and death dates through a combination of population statistics and individual factors.[^o205][^o206]

## Healthy life expectancy {#Healthy_life_expectancy}

*Healthspan* is the number of years a person spends in good health.[^o209] The World Health Organization publishes statistics called *healthy life expectancy* (HALE') which is the average number of years a person can expect to live in "full health", excluding the years lived in less than full health due to disease or injury.[^o210] Since 2004, [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat) publishes annual statistics called [Healthy Life Years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthy_Life_Years) based on reported activity limitations. The United States uses similar indicators in the framework of the national health promotion and disease prevention plan – the "[Healthy People program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthy_People_program)".

Besides the benefits of keeping people healthier longer, a goal is to reduce health-care expenses on diseases of [aging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aging), using a balanced diet in moderation, regular exercise, stress management, and adequate sleep.[^o209]

:::

## Forecasting {#Forecasting}

Forecasting life expectancy and mortality form an important subdivision of [demography](wikipedia://en/45642). Future trends in life expectancy have huge implications for old-age support programs (like [U.S. Social Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Social_Security) and [pension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pension)) since the cash flow in these systems depends on the number of recipients who are still living (along with the rate of return on the investments or the tax rate in [pay-as-you-go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pay-as-you-go_tax) systems). With longer life expectancies, the systems see increased cash outflow; if the systems underestimate increases in life-expectancies, they will be unprepared for the large payments that will occur, as humans live longer and longer.

Life expectancy forecasting is usually based on one of two different approaches:

1. Forecasting the life expectancy directly, generally using [ARIMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARIMA) or other time-series extrapolation procedures. This has the advantage of simplicity, but it cannot account for changes in mortality at specific ages, and the forecast number cannot be used to derive other [life table](wikipedia://en/1614128) results. Analyses and forecasts using this approach can be done with any common statistical/mathematical software package, like [EViews](wikipedia://en/3933682), [R](wikipedia://en/376707), [SAS](wikipedia://en/990677), [Stata](wikipedia://en/1809002), [Matlab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matlab), or [SPSS](wikipedia://en/179088).
1. Forecasting age-specific [death rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_rates) and computing the life expectancy from the results with life table methods. This is usually more complex than simply forecasting life expectancy because the analyst must deal with correlated age-specific mortality rates, but it seems to be more robust than simple one-dimensional [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) approaches. It also yields a set of age-specific rates that may be used to derive other measures, such as survival curves or life expectancies at different ages. The most important approach in this group is the [Lee-Carter model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee-Carter_model),[^o212] which uses the [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207) on a set of transformed age-specific mortality rates to reduce their dimensionality to a single time series, forecasts that time series, and then recovers a full set of age-specific mortality rates from that forecasted value. The software includes Professor [Rob J. Hyndman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_J._Hyndman)'s [R package called 'demography'](https://web.archive.org/web/20100822170736/http://robjhyndman.com/software/demography/) and [UC Berkeley's LCFIT system](http://lcfit.demog.berkeley.edu/) (18 April 2022). <https://web.archive.org/web/20220418014625/http://lcfit.demog.berkeley.edu/>.

## Policy uses {#Policy_uses}

Life expectancy is one of the factors in measuring the [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index) (HDI) of each nation along with adult literacy, education, and standard of living.[^o214]

Life expectancy is used in describing the [physical quality of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quality-of-life_index) of an area. It is also used for an individual when the value of a life settlement is determined a life insurance policy is sold for a cash asset.

Disparities in life expectancy are often cited as demonstrating the need for better medical care or increased social support. A strongly associated indirect measure is [income inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_inequality). For the top 21 industrialized countries, if each person is counted equally, life expectancy is lower in more unequal countries (r = −0.907).[^o215] There is a similar relationship among states in the U.S. (r = −0.620).[^o216]

## Life expectancy vs. other measures of longevity {#Life_expectancy_vs._other_measures_of_longevity}

*Image: 20200101 Remaining life expectancy - US.svg.* "Remaining" life expectancy—expected number of remaining years of life as a function of *current age*—is used in [retirement income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pension) planning.[^o218]

Life expectancy may be confused with the average age an adult could expect to live, creating the misunderstanding that an adult's lifespan would be unlikely to exceed their life expectancy at birth. This is not the case, as life expectancy is an average of the lifespans of all individuals, including those who die before adulthood. One may compare the life expectancy of the period after childhood to estimate also the life expectancy of an adult.[^o222]

As a measure of the years of life remaining, life expectancy decreases with age after initially rising in early childhood, but the average age to which a person is likely to live increases as they survive to successive higher ages.[^o219] In the table above, the estimated modern hunter-gatherer average expectation of life at birth of 33 years (often considered an upper-bound for Paleolithic populations) equates to a life expectancy at 15 of 39 years, so that those surviving to age 15 will on average die at 54.

In England in the 13th–19th centuries with life expectancy at birth rising from perhaps 25 years to over 40, expectation of life at age 30 has been estimated at 20–30 years,[^o220] giving an average age at death of about 50–60 for those (a minority at the start of the period but two-thirds at its end) surviving beyond their twenties.

*Image: 20200101 Life expectancy increases with age already achieved - chart.svg.* Life expectancy[^o218] increases with age already achieved.

The table above gives the life expectancy at birth among 13th-century English nobles as 30–33, but having surviving to the age of 21, a male member of the English aristocracy could expect to live:

- 1200–1300: to age 64
- 1300–1400: to age 45 (because of the [bubonic plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubonic_plague))
- 1400–1500: to age 69
- 1500–1550: to age 71[^o53]

A further concept is that of modal age at death, the single age when deaths among a population are more numerous than at any other age. In all pre-modern societies the most common age at death is the first year of life: it is only as infant mortality falls below around 33–34 per thousand (roughly a tenth of estimated ancient and medieval levels) that deaths in a later year of life (usually around age 80) become more numerous. While the most common age of death in adulthood among modern hunter-gatherers (often taken as a guide to the likely most favourable Paleolithic demographic experience) is estimated to average 72 years,[^o221] the number dying at that age is dwarfed by those (over a fifth of all infants) dying in the first year of life, and only around a quarter usually survive to the higher age.

[Maximum life span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_life_span) is an individual-specific concept, and therefore is an upper bound rather than an average.[^o222] Science author [Christopher Wanjek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Wanjek) writes, "\[H\]as the human race increased its life span? Not at all. This is one of the biggest misconceptions about old age: we are not living any longer." The maximum life span, or oldest age a human can live, may be constant.[^o222] Further, there are many examples of people living significantly longer than the average life expectancy of their time period, such as [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) (71), [Saint Anthony the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Anthony_the_Great) (105), [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo) (88), and [John Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Adams) (90).[^o222]

However, anthropologist [John D. Hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Hawks) criticizes the popular conflation of life span (life expectancy) and [maximum life span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_life_span) when popular science writers falsely imply that the average adult human does not live longer than their ancestors. He writes, "\[a\]ge-specific mortality rates have declined across the adult lifespan. A smaller fraction of adults die at 20, at 30, at 40, at 50, and so on across the lifespan. As a result, we live longer on average... In every way we can measure, human lifespans are longer today than in the immediate past, and longer today than they were 2000 years ago... age-specific mortality rates in adults really have reduced substantially."[^o223]
