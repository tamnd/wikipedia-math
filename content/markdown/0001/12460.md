# Green

Perso-Arabic

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Green"}

**Green** is the [color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color) between [cyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyan) and [yellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow) on the [visible spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum). It has 295–350 different shades. It is evoked by light which has a [dominant wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominant_wavelength) of roughly 495–570 [nm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometre). In [subtractive color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtractive_color) systems, used in [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting) and color printing, it is created by a combination of yellow and cyan; in the [RGB color model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RGB_color_model), used on television and computer screens, it is one of the [additive primary colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_primary_colors), along with [red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red) and [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue), which are mixed in different combinations to create all other colors. By far the largest contributor to green in nature is [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll), the chemical by which plants [photosynthesize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis) and convert sunlight into chemical energy. Many creatures have adapted to their green environments by taking on a green hue themselves as [camouflage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camouflage). Several [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) have a green color, including the [emerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald), which is colored green by its [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium) content.

:::

During [post-classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-classical_history) and [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period) Europe, green was the color commonly associated with wealth, merchants, bankers, and the gentry, while red was reserved for the nobility. For this reason, the costume of the *[Mona Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)* by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) and the benches in the British [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom) are green while those in the [House of Lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lords) are red.[^n1] It also has a long historical tradition as the color of [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland) and of [Gaelic culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_culture). It is the historic color of [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam), representing the lush vegetation of [Paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise). It was the color of the banner of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad), and is found in the flags of nearly all Islamic countries.[^n2]

In surveys made in [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas), European, and [Islamic countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world), green is the color most commonly associated with [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature), [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life), [health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health), [youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Youth), [spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_%28season%29), [hope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hope), and [envy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy).[^n3] In the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union) and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), green is also sometimes associated with [toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicity) and poor health,[^n4] but in China and most of Asia, its associations are very positive, as the symbol of fertility and happiness.[^n3] Because of its association with nature, it is the color of the [environmental movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_movement). Political groups advocating environmental protection and social justice describe themselves as part of the [Green movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_politics), some naming themselves [Green parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_party). This has led to similar campaigns in advertising, as companies have sold green, or [environmentally friendly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentally_friendly), products. Green is also the traditional color of safety and permission; a green light means go ahead, a [green card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_card) permits permanent residence in the United States.

## Etymology and linguistic definitions {#Etymology_and_linguistic_definitions}

*Image: Leavessnipedale.jpg.* The word *green* has the same Germanic root as the words for *grass* and *grow*

The word *green* comes from the [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) and [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) word *grene*, which, like the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) word *grün*, has the same root as the words *grass* and *grow*.[^o9] It is from a [Common Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Germanic) *\*gronja-*, which is also reflected in [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) *grænn*, [Old High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_High_German) *gruoni* (but unattested in [East Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germanic_languages)), ultimately from a [PIE root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PIE_root) ghre- "to grow", and root-cognate with *grass* and *to grow*.[^o10] The first recorded use of the word as a color term in Old English dates to ca. AD 700.[^o11]

[Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) with *viridis* also has a genuine and widely used term for "green". Related to *virere* "to grow" and *ver* "spring", it gave rise to words in several [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages), French *vert*, Italian *verde* (and English *vert*, *verdure* etc.).[^o12] Likewise the [Slavic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages) with *zelenъ*. [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) had three terms for various greens: yellowish, pale green – χλωρός (*khloros*, cf. the color of [chlorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine); cognate with χλοερός "*khloeros*, verdant" and χλόη "*khloe*, the green of new growth"), leek green or light green – πράσινος (*prasinos*), and dark green – χλωρομέλᾱς (*khloromelas*).

Thus, the languages mentioned above (Germanic, Romance, Slavic, Greek) have old terms for "green" which are derived from words for fresh, sprouting vegetation. However, [comparative linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_linguistics) makes clear that these terms were coined independently, over the past few millennia, and there is no identifiable single [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) or word for "green". For example, the Slavic *zelenъ* is cognate with [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) *harithah* "yellow, ochre, golden".[^o13] The [Turkic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages) also have *jašɨl* "green" or "yellowish green", compared to a Mongolian word for "meadow".[^o14]

### Languages where green and blue are one color {#Languages_where_green_and_blue_are_one_color}

*Image: Linear visible spectrum.svg.* The notion of "green" in modern European languages corresponds to about 520–570 nm, but many historical and non-European languages make other choices, e.g. using a term for the range of ca. 450–530 nm ("blue/green") and another for ca. 530–590 nm ("green/yellow").

In some languages, including old [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language), [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_language), old [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language), and [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language), the same word can mean either blue or green.[^o16] The [Chinese character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character) 青 (pronounced *qīng* in [Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Mandarin), *[ao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ao_%28color%29)* in Japanese, and *thanh* in [Sino-Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Vietnamese_vocabulary)) has a meaning that covers both blue and green; blue and green are traditionally considered shades of "青". In more contemporary terms, they are 藍 (*lán*, in Mandarin) and 綠 (*lǜ*, in Mandarin) respectively. Japanese also has two terms that refer specifically to the color green, 緑 (*midori*, which is derived from the classical Japanese descriptive verb *midoru* "to be in leaf, to flourish" in reference to trees) and グリーン (*guriin*, which is derived from the English word "green"). However, in Japan, although the traffic lights have the same colors as other countries have, the green light is described using the same word as for blue, *aoi*, because green is considered a shade of aoi; similarly, green variants of certain fruits and vegetables such as green apples, green *[shiso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perilla)* (as opposed to red apples and red shiso) will be described with the word *aoi*. Vietnamese uses a single word for both blue and green, *xanh*, with variants such as *xanh da trời* (azure, lit. "sky blue"), *lam* (blue), and *lục* (green; also *xanh lá cây*, lit. "leaf green").

"Green" in modern European languages corresponds to about 520–570 nm, but many historical and non-European languages make other choices, e.g. using a term for the range of ca. 450–530 nm ("blue/green") and another for ca. 530–590 nm ("green/yellow"). In the comparative study of [color terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_term) in the world's languages, green is only found as a separate category in languages with the fully developed range of six colors (white, black, red, green, yellow, and blue), or more rarely in systems with five colors (white, red, yellow, green, and black/blue).[^o17][^o18] These languages have introduced supplementary vocabulary to denote "green", but these terms are recognizable as recent adoptions that are not in origin color terms (much like the English adjective orange being in origin not a color term but the name of a fruit). Thus, the [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_language) word เขียว *kheīyw*, besides meaning "green", also means "rank" and "smelly" and holds other unpleasant associations.[^o19]

The [Celtic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_languages) had a term for "blue/green/grey", [Proto-Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Celtic) *\*glasto-*, which gave rise to [Old Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Irish) *glas* "green, grey" and to [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language) *glas* "blue". This word is cognate with the Ancient Greek γλαυκός "bluish green", contrasting with χλωρός "yellowish green" discussed above.

*Image: Green dark square.jpg.* A dark green rectangle

In modern Japanese, the term for green is 緑, while the old term for "blue/green", blue now means "blue". But in certain contexts, green is still conventionally referred to as 青, as in *blue [traffic light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_light)* and *blue [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaves)*, reflecting the absence of blue-green distinction in old Japanese (more accurately, the [traditional Japanese color terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_colors_of_Japan) grouped some [shades of green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shades_of_green) with blue, and others with yellow tones).

### Color vision and colorimetry {#Color_vision_and_colorimetry}

In optics, the [perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception) of green is evoked by light having a spectrum dominated by energy with a [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) of roughly 495–570 [nm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometre). The sensitivity of the dark-adapted human eye is greatest at about 507 nm, a blue-green color, while the light-adapted eye is most sensitive about 555 nm, a yellow-green; these are the peak locations of the rod and cone (scotopic and photopic, respectively) [luminosity functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity_function).[^o23]

The perception of greenness (in opposition to redness forming one of the [opponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opponent_process) mechanisms in human [color vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_vision)) is evoked by light which triggers the medium-wavelength *M* [cone cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_cell) in the eye more than the long-wavelength *L* cones. Light which triggers this greenness response more than the yellowness or blueness of the other color opponent mechanism is called green. A green light source typically has a spectral power distribution dominated by energy with a wavelength of roughly 487–570 nm.

*Image: RGB illumination.jpg.* Red. green, blue ed are [additive colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_colors). All the colors seen are made by mixing them in different intensities.

Human eyes have color receptors known as cone cells, of which there are three types. In some cases, one is missing or faulty, which can cause [color blindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_blind), including the common inability to distinguish red and yellow from green, known as [deuteranopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuteranopia) or red-green color blindness.[^o24] Green is restful to the eye. Studies show that a green environment can reduce fatigue.[^o25]

In the [subtractive color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtractive_color) system, used in painting and color printing, green is created by a combination of yellow and blue, or yellow and [cyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyan); in the [RGB color model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RGB_color_model), used on television and computer screens, it is one of the [additive primary colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_primary_colors), along with red and blue, which are mixed in different combinations to create all other colors. On the [HSV color wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSV_color_space), also known as the RGB color wheel, the [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_color) of green is [magenta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magenta); that is, a color corresponding to an equal mixture of [red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red) and [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue) light (one of the [purples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple)). On a traditional color wheel, based on subtractive color, the complementary color to green is considered to be red.[^o26]

In additive color devices such as computer displays and televisions, one of the [primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_colors) light sources is typically a narrow-spectrum yellowish-green of dominant wavelength ≈550 [nm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometre); this "green" primary is combined with an orangish-red "red" primary and a purplish-blue "blue" primary to produce any color in between – the RGB color model. A [unique green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_hues) (green appearing neither yellowish nor bluish) is produced on such a device by mixing light from the green primary with some light from the blue primary.

### Lasers {#Lasers}

*Image: Starfire Optical Range - three lasers into space.jpg.* Three green lasers being fired at a single spot in the sky from the [Starfire Optical Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starfire_Optical_Range)

[Lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser) emitting in the green part of the spectrum are widely available to the general public in a wide range of output powers. Green laser pointers outputting at 532 [nm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometre) (563.5 [THz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terahertz_radiation)) are relatively inexpensive compared to other wavelengths of the same power, and are very popular due to their good beam quality and very high apparent brightness. The most common green lasers use diode pumped solid state ([DPSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DPSS)) technology to create the green light.[^o29] An infrared [laser diode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_diode) at 808 nm is used to pump a crystal of neodymium-[doped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_%28semiconductor%29) yttrium vanadium oxide (Nd:YVO4) or neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet (Nd:YAG) and induces it to emit 281.76 THz (1064 nm). This deeper infrared light is then passed through another crystal containing potassium, titanium and phosphorus (KTP), whose non-linear properties generate light at a frequency that is twice that of the incident beam (563.5 THz); in this case corresponding to the wavelength of 532 nm ("green").[^o30] Other green wavelengths are also available using DPSS technology ranging from 501 nm to 543 nm.[^o31] Green wavelengths are also available from [gas lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_laser), including the [helium–neon laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium–neon_laser) (543 nm), the Argon-[ion laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_laser) (514 nm) and the Krypton-ion laser (521 nm and 531 nm), as well as liquid [dye lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dye_laser). Green lasers have a wide variety of applications, including pointing, illumination, surgery, [laser light shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_light_shows), [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy), [interferometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferometry), [fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescence), [holography](wikipedia://en/66338), [machine vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_vision), [non-lethal weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-lethal_weapons), and [bird control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_control).[^o32]

As of mid-2011, direct green laser diodes at 510 nm and 500 nm have become generally available,[^o33] although the price remains relatively prohibitive for widespread public use. The efficiency of these lasers (peak 3%) compared to that of DPSS green lasers (peak 35%)[^o34] may also be limiting adoption of the diodes to niche uses.

:::

### Pigments, food coloring and fireworks {#Pigments,_food_coloring_and_fireworks}

*Image: Chicago River dyed green, focus on river.jpg.* The [Chicago River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_River) is dyed green every year to mark [St. Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Patrick's_Day)

Many minerals provide [pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigment) which have been used in green paints and dyes over the centuries. Pigments, in this case, are minerals which reflect the color green, rather that emitting it through [luminescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminescent) or [phosphorescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorescent) qualities. The large number of green pigments makes it impossible to mention them all. Among the more notable green minerals, however is the [emerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald), which is colored green by trace amounts of [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium) and sometimes [vanadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanadium).[^o36] Chromium(III) oxide (Cr<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>), is called [chrome green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium%28III%29_oxide), also called [viridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viridian) or institutional green when used as a pigment.[^o43] For many years, the source of [amazonite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazonite)'s color was a mystery. Widely thought to have been due to [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) because copper compounds often have blue and green colors, the blue-green color is likely to be derived from small quantities of [lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead) and water in the [feldspar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feldspar).[^o37] Copper is the source of the green color in [malachite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malachite) pigments, chemically known as basic [copper(II) carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper%28II%29_carbonate).[^o38]

[Verdigris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verdigris) is made by placing a plate or blade of copper, brass or bronze, slightly warmed, into a vat of fermenting wine, leaving it there for several weeks, and then scraping off and drying the green powder that forms on the metal. The process of making verdigris was described in ancient times by [Pliny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder). It was used by the Romans in the murals of Pompeii, and in Celtic medieval manuscripts as early as the 5th century AD. It produced a blue-green which no other pigment could imitate, but it had drawbacks: it was unstable, it could not resist dampness, it did not mix well with other colors, it could ruin other colors with which it came into contact, and it was [toxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxic). [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), in his treatise on painting, warned artists not to use it. It was widely used in miniature paintings in Europe and Persia in the 16th and 17th centuries. Its use largely ended in the late 19th century, when it was replaced by the safer and more stable [chrome green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrome_green).[^n5] Viridian, as described above, was [patented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent) in 1859. It became popular with painters, since, unlike other synthetic greens, it was stable and not toxic. [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh) used it, along with [Prussian blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_blue), to create a dark blue sky with a greenish tint in his painting *[Café Terrace at Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Café_Terrace_at_Night)*.[^o43]

[Green earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_earth) is a natural pigment used since the time of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire). It is composed of clay colored by [iron oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_oxide), [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium), [aluminum silicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminum_silicate), or [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium). Large deposits were found in the South of France near [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice), and in Italy around [Verona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verona), on [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus), and in [Bohemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemia). The clay was crushed, washed to remove impurities, then powdered. It was sometimes called Green of Verona.[^n6]

Mixtures of oxidized [cobalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt) and [zinc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc) were also used to create green paints as early as the 18th century.[^o41]

[Cobalt green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt_green), sometimes known as [Rinman's green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt_green#Rinman's_green) or zinc green, is a translucent green pigment made by heating a mixture of cobalt (II) oxide and zinc oxide. [Sven Rinman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sven_Rinman), a Swedish chemist, discovered this compound in 1780.[^o42] Green chrome oxide was a new synthetic green created by a chemist named Pannetier in Paris in about 1835. Emerald green was a synthetic deep green made in the 19th century by hydrating chrome oxide. It was also known as Guignet green.[^o43]

*Image: Fireworks 2.JPG.* [Fireworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fireworks) typically use [barium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium) salts to create green sparks

There is no natural source for green [food colorings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_coloring) which has been approved by the US [Food and Drug Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_and_Drug_Administration). Chlorophyll, the [E numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_number) E140 and E141, is the most common green chemical found in nature, and only allowed in certain medicines and cosmetic materials.[^o44] [Quinoline Yellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinoline_Yellow_WS) (E104) is a commonly used coloring in the United Kingdom but is banned in Australia, Japan, Norway and the United States.[^o45] [Green S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_S) (E142) is prohibited in many countries, for it is known to cause [hyperactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperactivity), [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma), [urticaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urticaria), and [insomnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insomnia).[^o46]

To create green sparks, [fireworks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firework) use [barium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium) [salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_%28chemistry%29), such as [barium chlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_chlorate), [barium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_nitrate) crystals, or [barium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_chloride), also used for green fireplace logs.[^o47] Copper salts typically burn blue, but [cupric chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cupric_chloride) (also known as "campfire blue") can also produce green flames.[^o47] Green pyrotechnic flares can use a mix ratio 75:25 of [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron) and [potassium nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_nitrate).[^o47] Smoke can be turned green by a mixture: solvent yellow 33, solvent green 3, [lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose), [magnesium carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium_carbonate) plus [sodium carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_carbonate) added to [potassium chlorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_chlorate).[^o47]

### Biology {#Biology}

Green is common in nature, as many plants are green because of a complex chemical known as chlorophyll, which is involved in [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis). Chlorophyll absorbs the long wavelengths of light (red) and short wavelengths of light (blue) much more efficiently than the wavelengths that appear green to the human eye, so light reflected by plants is enriched in green.[^o49] Chlorophyll absorbs green light poorly because it first arose in organisms living in oceans where purple [halobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halobacteria) were already exploiting photosynthesis. Their purple color arose because they extracted energy in the green portion of the spectrum using [bacteriorhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriorhodopsin). The new organisms that then later came to dominate the extraction of light were selected to exploit those portions of the spectrum not used by the halobacteria.[^o50]

*Image: Mamba Dendroaspis angusticeps.jpg.* A green [mamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mamba) at the [Hellabrunn Zoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellabrunn_Zoo)

Animals typically use the color green as [camouflage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camouflage), blending in with the chlorophyll green of the surrounding environment.[^o24] Most fish, reptiles, amphibians, and birds appear green because of a [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28physics%29) of blue light coming through an over-layer of yellow pigment. Perception of color can also be affected by the surrounding environment. For example, broadleaf forests typically have a yellow-green light about them as the trees filter the light. [Turacoverdin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turacoverdin) is one chemical which can cause a green hue in birds, especially.[^o24] Invertebrates such as insects or mollusks often display green colors because of [porphyrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyrin) pigments, sometimes caused by diet. This can causes their feces to look green as well. Other chemicals which generally contribute to greenness among organisms are [flavins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavins) (lychochromes) and hemanovadin.[^o24] Humans have imitated this by wearing green clothing as a camouflage in military and other fields. Substances that may impart a greenish hue to one's skin include [biliverdin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biliverdin), the green pigment in [bile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bile), and [ceruloplasmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceruloplasmin), a [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) that carries copper [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) in [chelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelation).

The [green huntsman spider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrommata_virescens) is green due to the presence of bilin pigments in the spider's hemolymph (circulatory system fluids) and [tissue fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_fluid).[^o51] It hunts insects in green vegetation, where it is well camouflaged.

### Green eyes {#Green_eyes}

There is no green pigment in green eyes; like the color of blue eyes, it is an optical illusion; its appearance is caused by the combination of an amber or light brown pigmentation of the [stroma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroma_of_cornea), given by a low or moderate concentration of [melanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanin), with the blue tone imparted by the [Rayleigh scattering](wikipedia://en/26176) of the reflected light.[^o53] Nobody is brought into the world with green eyes. An infant has one of two eye hues: dark or blue. Following birth, cells called melanocytes start to discharge melanin, the earthy colored shade, in the child's irises. This begins happening since melanocytes respond to light in time.[^o54] Green eyes are most common in [Northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe) and [Central Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe).[^o55][^o56] They can also be found in [Southern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Europe), [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia), [Central Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Asia), and [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia). In [Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceland), 89% of women and 87% of men have either blue or green eye color.[^o57] A study of Icelandic and Dutch adults found green eyes to be much more prevalent in women than in men.[^o58]

## In history and art {#In_history_and_art}

### Prehistoric history {#Prehistoric_history}

[Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic) cave paintings do not have traces of green pigments, but neolithic peoples in northern Europe did make a green dye for clothing, made from the leaves of the [birch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birch) tree. It was of very poor quality, more brown than green. Ceramics from ancient [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) show people wearing vivid green costumes, but it is not known how the colors were produced.[^o61]

### Ancient history {#Ancient_history}

In [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt), green was the symbol of regeneration and rebirth, and of the crops made possible by the annual flooding of the Nile. For painting on the walls of tombs or on papyrus, Egyptian artists used finely ground [malachite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malachite), mined in the west Sinai and the eastern desert; a paintbox with malachite pigment was found inside the tomb of King [Tutankhamun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutankhamun). They also used less expensive green earth pigment, or mixed yellow ochre and blue [azurite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azurite). To dye fabrics green, they first colored them yellow with dye made from [saffron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saffron) and then soaked them in blue dye from the roots of the [woad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woad) plant.[^o61]

For the ancient Egyptians, green had very positive associations. The [hieroglyph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieroglyph) for green represented a growing [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus) sprout, showing the close connection between green, vegetation, vigor and growth. In wall paintings, the ruler of the underworld, [Osiris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osiris), was typically portrayed with a green face, because green was the symbol of good health and rebirth. Palettes of green facial makeup, made with malachite, were found in tombs. It was worn by both the living and the dead, particularly around the eyes, to protect them from evil. Tombs also often contained small green amulets in the shape of [scarab beetles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarab_beetles) made of malachite, which would protect and give vigor to the deceased. It also symbolized the sea, which was called the "Very Green".[^o63]

In Ancient Greece, green and blue were sometimes considered the same color, and the same word sometimes described the color of the sea and the color of trees. The philosopher [Democritus](wikipedia://en/8211) described two different greens: grc, or pale green, and grc, or [leek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leek) green. [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) considered that green was located midway between black, symbolizing the earth, and white, symbolizing water. However, green was not counted among the four classic colors of Greek painting – red, yellow, black and white – and is rarely found in Greek art.[^n7]

The Romans had a greater appreciation for the color green; it was the color of [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus_%28mythology%29), the goddess of gardens, vegetables and vineyards. The Romans made a fine green earth pigment that was widely used in the wall paintings of [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii), [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum), [Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyon), [Vaison-la-Romaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaison-la-Romaine), and other Roman cities. They also used the pigment verdigris, made by soaking copper plates in fermenting wine.[^o65] By the second century AD, the Romans were using green in paintings, mosaics and glass, and there were ten different words in Latin for varieties of green.[^n8]

### Postclassical history {#Postclassical_history}

In the Middle Ages and Renaissance, the color of clothing showed a person's social rank and profession. Red could only be worn by the nobility, brown and gray by peasants, and green by merchants, bankers and the gentry and their families. The [Mona Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa) wears green in her portrait, as does the bride in the Arnolfini portrait by [Jan van Eyck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_van_Eyck).

There were no good vegetal green dyes which resisted washing and sunlight for those who wanted or were required to wear green. Green dyes were made out of the [fern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fern), [plantain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantain_%28cooking%29), [buckthorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckthorn) berries, the juice of [nettles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urtica) and of [leeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeks), the [digitalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digitalis) plant, the [broom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broom_%28shrub%29) plant, the leaves of the [fraxinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraxinus), or ash tree, and the bark of the [alder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alder) tree, but they rapidly faded or changed color. Only in the 16th century was a good green dye produced, by first dyeing the cloth blue with woad, and then yellow with *[Reseda luteola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reseda_luteola)*, also known as yellow-weed.[^n9]

The pigments available to painters were more varied; monks in monasteries used verdigris, made by soaking copper in fermenting wine, to color medieval manuscripts. They also used finely-ground malachite, which made a luminous green. They used green earth colors for backgrounds.

During the early Renaissance, painters such as [Duccio di Buoninsegna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duccio_di_Buoninsegna) learned to paint faces first with a green undercoat, then with pink, which gave the faces a more realistic hue. Over the centuries the pink has faded, making some of the faces look green.[^o70]

### Modern history {#Modern_history}

#### In the 18th and 19th century {#In_the_18th_and_19th_century}

The 18th and 19th centuries brought the discovery and production of synthetic green pigments and dyes, which rapidly replaced the earlier mineral and vegetable pigments and dyes. These new dyes were more stable and brilliant than the vegetable dyes, but some contained high levels of [arsenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsenic), and were eventually banned.

In the 18th and 19th centuries, green was associated with the [romantic movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romantic_movement) in literature and art.[^o73] The German poet and philosopher [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe) declared that green was the most restful color, suitable for decorating bedrooms. Painters such as [John Constable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Constable) and [Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste-Camille_Corot) depicted the lush green of rural landscapes and forests. Green was contrasted to the smoky grays and blacks of the Industrial Revolution.

The second half of the 19th century saw the use of green in art to create specific emotions, not just to imitate nature. One of the first to make color the central element of his picture was the American artist [James McNeill Whistler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_McNeill_Whistler), who created a series of paintings called "symphonies" or "noctures" of color, including *Symphony in gray and green; The Ocean* between 1866 and 1872.

The late 19th century also brought the systematic study of color theory, and particularly the study of how complementary colors such as red and green reinforced each other when they were placed next to each other. These studies were avidly followed by artists such as [Vincent van Gogh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_van_Gogh). Describing his painting, [The *Night Cafe*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Night_Café), to his brother Theo in 1888, Van Gogh wrote: "I sought to express with red and green the terrible human passions. The hall is blood red and pale yellow, with a green billiard table in the center, and four lamps of lemon yellow, with rays of orange and green. Everywhere it is a battle and antithesis of the most different reds and greens."[^o74] Van Gogh also stated that he attempted to depict the café as a place where “one can ruin oneself, go mad or commit a crime.” To express this, he used “Soft Louis XV green and malachite, contrasting with yellow-greens and harsh blue-greens, and all this in an atmosphere like a devil’s furnace, of pale sulfur.”[^o75]

#### In the 20th and 21st century {#In_the_20th_and_21st_century}

In the 1980s, green became a political symbol, the color of the [Green Party in Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliance_'90/The_Greens) and in many other European countries. It symbolized the [environmental movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_movement), and also a new politics of the left which rejected traditional socialism and communism. (See Green section below.)

## Symbolism and associations {#Symbolism_and_associations}

### Safety and permission {#Safety_and_permission}

*Image: LED traffic light.jpg.* A green light is the universal symbol of permission to go

Green can communicate safety to proceed, as in [traffic lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_light).[^o80] Green and red were standardized as the colors of international railroad signals in the 19th century.[^o81] The first traffic light, using green and red gas lamps, was erected in 1868 in front of the [Houses of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houses_of_Parliament) in London. It exploded the following year, injuring the policeman who operated it. In 1912, the first modern electric traffic lights were put up in [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City), [Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah). Red was chosen largely because of its high visibility, and its association with danger, while green was chosen largely because it could not be mistaken for red. Today green lights universally signal that a system is turned on and working as it should. In many video games, green signifies both [health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_%28gaming%29) and completed objectives, opposite red.

### Nature, vivacity, and life {#Nature,_vivacity,_and_life}

Green is the color most commonly associated in Europe and the United States with nature, vivacity and life.[^o83] It is the color of many environmental organizations, such as [Greenpeace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenpeace), and of the [Green Parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Parties) in Europe. Many cities have designated a garden or park as a green space, and use green trash bins and containers. A green cross is commonly used to designate pharmacies in Europe.

In China, green symbolizes the east, sunrise, and life and growth.[^o84] In Thailand, the color green is considered auspicious for those born on a Wednesday (light green for those born at night).[^o85]

### Springtime, freshness, and hope {#Springtime,_freshness,_and_hope}

Green is the color most commonly associated in the United States and Europe with springtime, freshness, and hope.[^n10] Green is often used to symbolize rebirth and renewal and immortality. In [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt); the god [Osiris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osiris), king of the underworld, was depicted as green-skinned.[^o88] Green as the color of hope is connected with the color of springtime; hope represents the faith that things will improve after a period of difficulty, like the renewal of flowers and plants after the winter season.[^n11]

### Youth and inexperience {#Youth_and_inexperience}

Green the color most commonly associated in Europe and the United States with youth. It also often is used to describe anyone young, inexperienced, probably by the analogy to immature and unripe fruit.[^o91][^n12] Examples include [green cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_cheese), a term for a fresh, unaged cheese, and [greenhorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowboy#Symbolism), an inexperienced person.

### Food and diet {#Food_and_diet}

*Image: FSSAI new labels for veg and non-veg.svg.* Indian FSSAI labels. The green dot symbol (top-left) identifies lacto-vegetarian food.

The color green has been increasingly used by food companies, governments, and practitioners themselves to identify veganism and vegetarianism. The government of [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India) requires food that is vegetarian to be marked with a green circle as part of the *Food Safety and Standards Act of 2006* with changes to symbolism since but still maintaining the color green. In 2021, India introduced a green V to exclusively label vegan options.[^o94] In the west, the [V-Label](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-Label), a green V designed by the [European Vegetarian Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Vegetarian_Union), has been used by food distributors to label vegan and vegetarian options.[^o95]

### Calm, tolerance, and the agreeable {#Calm,_tolerance,_and_the_agreeable}

Surveys also show that green is the color most associated with the calm, the agreeable, and tolerance. Red is associated with heat, blue with cold, and green with an agreeable temperature. Red is associated with dry, blue with wet, and green, in the middle, with dampness. Red is the most active color, blue the most passive; green, in the middle, is the color of neutrality and calm, sometimes used in architecture and design for these reasons. Blue and green together symbolize harmony and balance.[^n13] Experimental studies also show this calming effect in a statistical significant decrease of negative emotions[^o98] and increase of creative performance.[^o99]

### Jealousy and envy {#Jealousy_and_envy}

Green is often associated with [jealousy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jealousy) and [envy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy). The expression "green-eyed monster" was first used by [William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare) in [Othello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othello): "it is the green-eyed monster which doth mock the meat it feeds on." Shakespeare also used it in the [Merchant of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_of_Venice), speaking of "green-eyed jealousy".[^o101]

### Love and sexuality {#Love_and_sexuality}

Green today is not commonly associated in Europe and the United States with love and sexuality,[^n14] but in stories of the [medieval period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) it sometimes represented love[^o104] and the base, natural desires of man.[^o105] It was the color of the [serpent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serpents_in_the_Bible) in the [Garden of Eden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_Eden) who caused the downfall of [Adam and Eve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_and_Eve). However, for the [troubadours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troubadours), green was the color of growing love, and light green clothing was reserved for young women who were not yet married.[^n15]

In Persian and Sudanese poetry, dark-skinned women, called "green" women, were considered erotic.[^o107] The Chinese term for [cuckold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckold) is "to wear a green hat."[^o108] This was because in ancient China, prostitutes were called "the family of the green lantern" and a [prostitute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostitute)'s family would wear a green headscarf.[^n16]

In [Victorian England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_England), the color green was associated with [homosexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality).[^o110] [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde) popularized the green [carnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dianthus_caryophyllus) as representing homosexuality. [Homosexual men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gay_men) would wear a green carnation to represent their sexuality after Oscar Wilde popularized it.[^o111]

### Dragons, fairies, monsters, and devils {#Dragons,_fairies,_monsters,_and_devils}

In legends, folk tales and films, [fairies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairies), [dragons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragons), [monsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monsters), and the devil are often shown as green.

In the Middle Ages, the devil was usually shown as either red, black or green. Dragons were usually green, because they had the heads, claws and tails of reptiles.

Modern [Chinese dragons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_dragons) are also often green, but unlike European dragons, they are benevolent; Chinese dragons traditionally symbolize potent and auspicious powers, particularly control over water, rainfall, hurricane, and floods. The dragon is also a symbol of power, strength, and good luck. The Emperor of China usually used the dragon as a symbol of his imperial power and strength. The [dragon dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_dance) is a popular feature of Chinese festivals.

In [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_folklore) and [English folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_folklore), the color was sometimes associated with witchcraft, and with [faeries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faeries) and spirits.[^o113][^o114][^o115] The type of Irish fairy known as a [leprechaun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprechaun) is commonly portrayed wearing a green suit, though before the 20th century he was usually described as wearing a red suit.

In theater and film, green was often connected with monsters and the inhuman. The earliest films of Frankenstein were in black and white, but in the poster for the 1935 version *[The Bride of Frankenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bride_of_Frankenstein)*, the monster had a green face. Actor [Bela Lugosi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bela_Lugosi) wore green-hued makeup for the role of [Dracula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_Dracula) in the 1927–1928 Broadway stage production.[^o116][^o117]

### Poison and sickness {#Poison_and_sickness}

Like other common colors, green has several completely opposite associations. While it is the color most associated by Europeans and Americans with good health, it is also the color most often associated with [toxicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicity) and poison. There was a solid foundation for this association; in the nineteenth century several popular paints and pigments, notably verdigris, vert de Schweinfurt and vert de Paris, were highly toxic, containing copper or arsenic.[^n17] The intoxicating drink [absinthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absinthe) was known as "the green fairy".

A green tinge in the skin is sometimes associated with nausea and sickness.[^o120] The expression 'green at the gills' means appearing sick. The color, when combined with gold, is sometimes seen as representing the fading of youth.[^o121] In some Far East cultures the color green is used as a symbol of sickness or nausea.[^o122]

### Social status, prosperity and the dollar {#Social_status,_prosperity_and_the_dollar}

Green in Europe and the United States is sometimes associated with status and prosperity. From the Middle Ages to the 19th century it was often worn by bankers, merchants country gentlemen and others who were wealthy but not members of the nobility. The benches in the [House of Commons of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom), where the landed gentry sat, are colored green.

In the United States green was connected with the dollar bill. Since 1861, the reverse side of the dollar bill has been green. Green was originally chosen because it deterred counterfeiters, who tried to use early camera equipment to duplicate banknotes. Also, since the banknotes were thin, the green on the back did not show through and muddle the pictures on the front of the banknote. Green continues to be used because the public now associates it with a strong and stable currency.[^o124]

One of the more notable uses of this meaning is found in *[The Wonderful Wizard of Oz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wonderful_Wizard_of_Oz)*. The [Emerald City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald_City) in this story is a place where everyone wears tinted glasses that make everything appear green. According to the populist interpretation of the story, the city's color is used by the author, [L. Frank Baum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._Frank_Baum), to illustrate the financial system of America in his day, as he lived in a time when America was debating the use of paper money versus gold.[^o125]

## On flags {#On_flags}

- The [flag of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Italy) (1797) was modeled after the French tricolor. It was originally the flag of the Cisalpine Republic, whose capital was [Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan); red and white were the colors of Milan, and green was the color of the military uniforms of the army of the Cisalpine Republic. Other versions say it is the color of the Italian landscape, or symbolizes hope.[^o129]
- The [flag of Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Brazil) has a green field adapted from the flag of the Empire of Brazil. The green represented the royal family.
- The [flag of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_India) was inspired by an earlier flag of the independence movement of [Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gandhi), which had a red band for Hinduism and a green band representing Islam, the second largest religion in India.[^o130]
- The flag of Pakistan symbolizes Pakistan's commitment to Islam and equal rights of religious minorities where the larger portion (3:2 ratio) of flag is dark green representing Muslim majority (98% of total population) while a white vertical bar (3:1 ratio) at the mast representing equal rights for religious minorities and minority religions in country. The crescent and star symbolizes progress and bright future respectively.
- The flag of Bangladesh has a green field based on a similar flag used during the [Bangladesh Liberation War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh_Liberation_War) of 1971. It consists of a red disc on top of a green field. The red disc represents the sun rising over [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal), and also the blood of those who died for the independence of Bangladesh. The green field stands for the lushness of the land of Bangladesh.
- The flag of the international [constructed language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructed_language) [Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto) has a green field and a green star in a white area. The green represents hope (*"esperanto"* means "one who hopes"), the white represents peace and neutrality and the star represents the five inhabited continents.

Green is one of the three colors (along with red and black, or red and gold) of [Pan-Africanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-Africanism). Several African countries thus use the color on their flags, including [Nigeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigeria), [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa), [Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana), [Senegal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senegal), [Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali), [Ethiopia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopia), [Togo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Togo), [Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinea), [Benin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benin), and [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe). The [Pan-African colors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-African_colours) are borrowed from the [Ethiopian flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Ethiopia), one of the oldest independent African countries. Green on some African flags represents the natural richness of Africa.[^o131]

Many flags of the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_world) are green, as the color is considered sacred in Islam (see below). The flag of [Hamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamas),[^o132] as well as the [flag of Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Iran), is green, symbolizing their [Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamism) ideology.[^o133] The 1977 flag of Libya consisted of a simple green field with no other characteristics. It was the only national flag in the world with just one color and no design, insignia, or other details.[^o134] Some countries used green in their flags to represent their country's lush vegetation, as in the [flag of Jamaica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Jamaica),[^o135] and hope in the future, as in the flags of [Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Portugal) and Nigeria.[^o136] The green [cedar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cedrus_libani) of Lebanon tree on the Flag of Lebanon officially represents steadiness and tolerance.[^o137] The Wearing of the Green

Green is a symbol of Ireland, which is often referred to as the "Emerald Isle". The color is particularly identified with the [republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fianna_Fáil) and [nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalist) traditions in modern times. It is used this way on the flag of the [Republic of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Ireland), in balance with white and the Protestant orange.[^o138] Green is a strong trend in the Irish holiday [St. Patrick's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Patrick's_Day).[^o139]

## In politics {#In_politics}

The first recorded green party was [a political faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greens_%28Byzantine_Empire%29) in [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) during the 6th century [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire). which took its name from a popular [chariot racing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot_racing) team. They were bitter opponents of the [blue faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues_%28Byzantine_Empire%29), which supported Emperor [Justinian I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian_I) and which had its own chariot racing team. In 532 AD rioting between the factions began after one race, which led to the massacre of green supporters and the destruction of much of the center of Constantinople.[^o142] (See [Nika Riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nika_Riots)).

Green was the traditional color of [Irish nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalism), beginning in the 17th century. The green harp flag, with a traditional [gaelic harp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaelic_harp), became the symbol of the movement. It was the banner of the [Society of United Irishmen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_United_Irishmen), which organized the ultimately unsuccessful [Irish Rebellion of 1798](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Rebellion_of_1798). When Ireland achieved independence in 1922, green was incorporated into the [national flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_Ireland).[^o143]

In the 1970s, green became the color of the third biggest Swiss Federal Council political party, the [Swiss People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_People's_Party) SVP. The ideology is Swiss nationalism, national conservatism, right-wing populism, economic liberalism, agrarianism, isolationism, euroscepticism. The SVP was founded on September 22, 1971, and has 90,000 members.[^o144]

In the 1980s, green became the color of a number of new European political parties organized around an agenda of [environmentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentalism). Green was chosen for its association with nature, health, and growth. The largest green party in Europe is Alliance '90/The Greens (German: Bündnis 90/Die Grünen) in Germany, which was formed in 1993 from the merger of the German Green Party, founded in West Germany in 1980, and Alliance 90, founded during the Revolution of 1989–1990 in East Germany. In the 2009 federal elections, the party won 11% of the votes and 68 out of 622 seats in the [Bundestag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundestag).

Green parties in Europe have programs based on [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology_movement), [grassroots democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassroots_democracy), [nonviolence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolence), and [social justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_justice). Green parties are found in over one hundred countries, and most are members of the [Global Green Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Greens).[^o145]

[Greenpeace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenpeace) is a non-governmental environmental organization which emerged from the anti-nuclear and peace movements in the 1970s. Its ship, the [Rainbow Warrior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow_Warrior_%282011%29), frequently tried to interfere with nuclear tests and whaling operations. The movement now has branches in forty countries.

The [Australian Greens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Greens) was founded in 1992. In the 2010 federal election, the party received 13% of the vote (more than 1.6 million votes) in the Senate, a first for any Australian minor party.

Green is the color associated with [Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico)'s [Independence Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rican_Independence_Party), the smallest of that country's three major political parties, which advocates Puerto Rican independence from the United States.

In [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia), green is used by several [Islamist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamism) political party, including [National Awakening Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Awakening_Party), [Crescent Star Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crescent_Star_Party_%28Indonesia%29), [United Development Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Development_Party), and the local [Aceh Just and Prosperous Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_Just_and_Prosperous_Party).

In [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan), green is used by [Democratic Progressive Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Progressive_Party). Green in Taiwan associates with [Taiwan independence movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan_independence_movement).

## In religion {#In_religion}

Green is the traditional color of Islam. According to tradition, the robe and banner of [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) were green, and according to the [Koran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koran) (XVIII, 31 and LXXVI, 21) those fortunate enough to live in paradise wear green silk robes.[^o147][^o148][^o149] Muhammad is quoted in a [hadith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadith) as saying that "water, greenery, and a beautiful face" were three universally good things.[^o150] Green was accordingly adopted as a [Shi'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'a) color.

[Al-Khidr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khidr) ("The Green One"), was an important [Qur'anic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'an) figure who was said to have met and traveled with [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses).[^o152] He was given that name because of his role as a diplomat and negotiator. Green was also considered to be the median color between light and obscurity.[^o148]

In [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity), especially [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic) and more traditional [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) clergy wear green [vestments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vestments) at liturgical celebrations during [Ordinary Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_Time).[^o153] In the [Eastern Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Catholic_Church), green is the color of [Pentecost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentecost).[^o154] Green is one of the [Christmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christmas) colors as well, possibly dating back to pre-Christian times, when evergreens were worshiped for their ability to maintain their color through the winter season. Romans used green [holly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holly) and [evergreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evergreen) as decorations for their [winter solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_solstice) celebration called [Saturnalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturnalia), which eventually evolved into a Christmas celebration.[^o155] In Ireland and [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland) especially, green is used to represent Catholics, while [orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_%28color%29) is used to represent [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism). This is shown on the national flag of Ireland.

In [Paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism), green represents abundance, growth, wealth, renewal, and balance. In [magickal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_%28supernatural%29) practices, green is often used to bring money and luck.[^o156] One figure who shares parallels with various deities is the [Green Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Man).[^o157]

## In gambling and sports {#In_gambling_and_sports}

- [Gambling](wikipedia://en/11921) tables in a [casino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casino) are traditionally green. The tradition is said to have started in gambling rooms in [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) in the 16th century.[^o159]
- [Billiards tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billiards_tables) are traditionally covered with green woolen cloth. The first indoor tables, dating to the 15th century, were colored green after the grass courts used for the similar lawn games of the period.[^o160]
- Green was the traditional color worn by hunters in the 19th century, particularly the shade called [hunter green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter_green). In the 20th century most hunters began wearing the color [olive drab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_drab), a shade of green, instead of hunter green.[^o161]
- Green is a common color for sports teams. Well-known teams include [A.S. Saint-Étienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.S._Saint-Étienne) of France, known as *Les Verts* (The Greens). The [Green Bay Packers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Bay_Packers), an [American football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_football) team, has the color in its official name and wears green uniforms. The [NBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBA) basketball team [Boston Celtics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Celtics) is known for the green and white colors. In [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel), the green and white colors are identified with [Maccabi Haifa F.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maccabi_Haifa_F.C.), a successful football club known as "The Greens". A number of national soccer teams feature the color, with the color usually reflective of the teams' national flag.
- [British racing green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_racing_green) was the international motor racing color of Britain from the early 1900s until the 1960s, when it was replaced by the colors of the sponsoring automobile companies.
- A green belt in [karate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karate), [taekwondo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taekwondo), and [judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo) symbolizes a level of proficiency in the sport.[^o162][^o163][^o164]

## Idioms and expressions {#Idioms_and_expressions}

- **Having a green thumb** (American English) or **green fingers** (British English). To be passionate about or talented at gardening.[^o167] The expression was popularized beginning in 1925 by a BBC gardening program.[^o101]
- **Greenhorn**. Someone who is inexperienced.
- **Green-eyed monster**. Refers to jealousy. (See section above on jealousy and envy).
- **Greenmail**. A term used in finance and corporate takeovers. It refers to the practice of a company paying a high price to buy back shares of its own stock to prevent an unfriendly takeover by another company or businessman. It originated in the 1980s on [Wall Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street), and originates from the green of dollars.[^o101]
- **Green room**. A room at a theater where actors rest when not onstage, or a room at a television studio where guests wait before going on-camera. It originated in the late 17th century from a room of that color at the [Theatre Royal, Drury Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_Royal,_Drury_Lane) in London.[^o101]
- **[Greenwashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwashing)**. Environmental activists sometimes use this term to describe the advertising of a company that promotes its positive environmental practices to cover up its environmental destruction.[^o168]
- **Green around the gills**. A description of a person who looks physically ill.[^o169]
- **Going green**. An expression commonly used to refer to preserving the natural environment, and participating in activities such as recycling materials.
- **Looking green**. A description of a person who looks revolted or repulsed.
