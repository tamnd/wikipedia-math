# Group action

*Image: Group action on equilateral triangle.svg.* The [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $C_{3}$ consisting of the [rotations](wikipedia://en/410009) by 0°, 120° and 240° acts on the set of the three vertices.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Group action"}

In mathematics, an **action** of a group $G$ on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ is, loosely speaking, an operation that takes an element of $G$ and an element of $S$ and produces another element of $S.$ More formally, it is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from $G$ to the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of $S$ (the set of all [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942) on $S$ along with group operation being [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947)). One says that $G$ **acts** on $S.$

:::

Many sets of [transformations](wikipedia://en/1904373) form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under function composition; for example, the [rotations](wikipedia://en/410009) around a point in the plane. It is often useful to consider the group as an [abstract group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_group), and to say that one has a group action of the abstract group that consists of performing the transformations of the group of transformations. The reason for distinguishing the group from the transformations is that, generally, a group of transformations of a [structure](wikipedia://en/1809181) acts also on various related structures; for example, the above rotation group also acts on triangles by transforming triangles into triangles.

If a group acts on a structure, it will usually also act on objects built from that structure. For example, the group of [Euclidean isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_isometry) acts on [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) and also on the figures drawn in it; in particular, it acts on the set of all [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654). Similarly, the group of [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetries) of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) acts on the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417), the [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034), and the [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) of the polyhedron.

A group action on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) is called a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of the group. In the case of a finite-dimensional vector space, it allows one to identify many groups with [subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgroups) of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\operatorname{GL}(n,K)$, the group of the [invertible matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_matrices) of [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) $n$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$.

The [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_n$ acts on any [set](wikipedia://en/26691) with $n$ elements by permuting the elements of the set. Although the group of all [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of a set depends formally on the set, the concept of group action allows one to consider a single group for studying the permutations of all sets with the same [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Left group action {#Left_group_action}

If $G$ is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) with [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) $e$, and $X$ is a set, then a (*left*) *group action* $\alpha$ of $G$ on $X$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427)

$$\alpha : G \times X \to X$$

that satisfies the following two [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axioms):[^o5]

{|

\|Identity: |$\alpha(e,x)=x$ |- |Compatibility: |$\alpha(g,\alpha(h,x))=\alpha(gh,x)$ |} for all $g$ and $h$ in $G$ and all $x$ in $X$.

The group $G$ is then said to act on $X$ (from the left). A set $X$ together with an action of $G$ is called a (*left*) $G$-*set*.

It can be notationally convenient to [curry](wikipedia://en/6600) the action $\alpha$, so that, instead, one has a collection of [transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_%28geometry%29) $\alpha_g:X\rightarrow X$, with one transformation $\alpha_g$ for each group element $g\in G$. The identity and compatibility relations then read

$$\alpha_e(x) = x$$

and

$$\alpha_g(\alpha_h(x)) = (\alpha_g \circ \alpha_h)(x) = \alpha_{gh}(x)$$

The second axiom states that the [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947) is compatible with the group multiplication; they form a [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484). This axiom can be shortened even further, and written as $\alpha_g\circ\alpha_h=\alpha_{gh}$.

With the above understanding, it is very common to avoid writing $\alpha$ entirely, and to replace it with either a dot, or with nothing at all. Thus, $\alpha(g,x)$ can be shortened to $g\cdot x$ or $gx$, especially when the action is clear from context. The axioms are then

$$\left\{\begin{aligned}& e\cdot x=x \\ & g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x\end{aligned}\right.$$

From these two axioms, it follows that for any fixed $g$ in $G$, the function from $X$ to itself which maps $x$ to $g\cdot x$ is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942), with inverse bijection the corresponding map for $g^{-1}$. Therefore, one may equivalently define a group action of $G$ on $X$ as a group homomorphism from $G$ into the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ of all bijections from $X$ to itself.[^o6]

### Right group action {#Right_group_action}

Likewise, a *right group action* of $G$ on $X$ is a function

$$\alpha : X \times G \to X,$$

that satisfies the analogous axioms:[^o8]

{|

\|Identity: |$\alpha(x,e)=x$ |- |Compatibility: |$\alpha(\alpha(x,g),h)=\alpha(x,gh)$ |} (with $\alpha(x,g)$ often shortened to $xg$ or $x\cdot g$ when the action being considered is clear from context)

{|

\|Identity: |$x \cdot e = x$ |- |Compatibility: |$(x\cdot g)\cdot h = x\cdot(gh)$ |}

for all $g$ and $h$ in $G$ and all $x$ in $X$.

The difference between left and right actions is in the order in which a product $gh$ acts on $x$. For a left action, $h$ acts first, followed by $g$ second. For a right action, $g$ acts first, followed by $h$ second. Because of the formula $(gh)^{-1}=h^{-1}g^{-1}$, a left action can be constructed from a right action by composing with the inverse operation of the group. Also, a right action of a group $G$ on $X$ can be considered as a left action of its [opposite group](wikipedia://en/31366779) $G^{\text{op}}$ on $X$. Thus, for establishing general properties of a single group action, it suffices to consider only left actions.

:::

## Notable properties of actions {#Notable_properties_of_actions}

Let $G$ be a group acting on a set $X$. The action is called *faithful* or *effective* if $g\cdot x=x$ for all $x\in X$ implies that $g=e_G$. Equivalently, the [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from $G$ to the group of bijections of $X$ corresponding to the action is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective).

The action is called *free* (or *semiregular* or *fixed-point free*) if the statement that $g\cdot x=x$ for some $x\in X$ already implies that $g=e_G$. In other words, no non-trivial element of $G$ fixes a point of $X$. This is a much stronger property than faithfulness.

For example, the action of any group on itself by left multiplication is free. This observation implies [Cayley's theorem](wikipedia://en/105967) that any group can be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in a symmetric group (which is infinite when the group is). A finite group may act faithfully on a set of size much smaller than its cardinality (however such an action cannot be free). For instance the abelian 2-group $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^n$ (of cardinality $2^n$) acts faithfully on a set of size $2n$. This is not always the case, for example the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $\mathbb{Z}/2^n\mathbb{Z}$ cannot act faithfully on a set of size less than $2^n$.

In general, the smallest set on which a faithful action can be defined can vary greatly for groups of the same size. For example, three groups of size 120 are the symmetric group $S_5$, the [icosahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_group) $A_5\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/120\mathbb{Z}$. The smallest sets on which faithful actions can be defined for these groups are of size 5, 7, and 16 respectively.

### Transitivity properties {#Transitivity_properties}

The action of $G$ on $X$ is called *transitive* if for any two points $x,y\in X$ there exists a $g\in G$ so that $g\cdot x=y$.

The action is *simply transitive* (or *sharply transitive*, or *regular*) if it is both transitive and free. This means that given $x,y\in X$ there is exactly one $g\in G$ such that $g\cdot x=y$. If $X$ is acted upon simply transitively by a group $G$ then it is called a [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441) for $G$ or a $G$-torsor.

For an integer $n\geq 1$, the action is n-transitive if $X$ has at least $n$ elements, and for any pair of $n$-tuples $(x_1,\ldots,x_n),(y_1,\ldots,y_n)\in X^n$ with pairwise distinct entries (that is $x_i\neq x_j$, $y_i\neq y_j$ when $i\neq j$) there exists a $g\in G$ such that $g\cdot x_i=y_i$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$. In other words, the action on the subset of $X^n$ of tuples without repeated entries is transitive. For $n=2,3$ this is often called double, respectively triple, transitivity. The class of [2-transitive groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-transitive_group) (that is, subgroups of a finite symmetric group whose action is 2-transitive) and more generally [multiply transitive groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiply_transitive_group) is well-studied in finite group theory.

An action is sharply n-transitive when the action on tuples without repeated entries in $X^n$ is sharply transitive.

::: {#o12 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

The action of the symmetric group of $X$ is transitive, in fact $n$-transitive for any $n$ up to the cardinality of $X$. If $X$ has cardinality $n$, the action of the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) is $(n-2)$-transitive but not $(n-1)$-transitive.

The action of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) of a vector space $V$ on the set $V\setminus\{0\}$ of non-zero vectors is transitive, but not 2-transitive (similarly for the action of the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) if the dimension of $V$ is at least 2). The action of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) of a Euclidean space on nonzero vectors is not transitive, but its action on the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) is.

:::

### Homogeneity properties {#Homogeneity_properties}

For an integer $n\geq 1$, the action of $G$ on $X$ is called n-homogeneous if $X$ has at least $n$ elements, and for any pair of $n$-subsets $S,T\subseteq X$ there exists a $g\in G$ such that $gS=T$. In other words, the action on the set of $n$-subsets of $X$ is transitive.[^n1]

An action is sharply n-homogeneous when the action on $n$-subsets of $X$ is sharply transitive.

An $n$-transitive action is also $n$-homogeneous. As 1-subsets, 1-tuples and points are equivalent, the concepts of 1-homogeneity, 1-transitivity and transitivity coincide.

A group that acts $n$-homogeneously on a set $X$ of $m$ elements also acts $(m-n)$-homogeneously, as $gS=T$ implies $g\left(X\setminus S\right)=X\setminus T$.

::: {#o16 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

The action of the symmetric group of $X$ is $n$-homogeneous for any $n$ up to the cardinality of $X$. So is the action of the alternating group, which is $(n-1)$-homogeneous (since it is transitive, and thus 1-homogeneous) without being $(n-1)$-transitive. For $n=3$, this has a geometric interpretation: the full group of Euclidean symmetries of an equilateral triangle acts as the full symmetric group on its vertices and hence 2-transitively thereon, while the subgroup of in-plane rotations (which correspond to alternating permutations) merely acts 2-homogeneously.

:::

### Primitive actions {#Primitive_actions}

The action of $G$ on $X$ is called *primitive* if there is no [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of $X$ preserved by all elements of $G$ apart from the trivial partitions (the partition in a single piece and its [dual](wikipedia://en/7988), the partition into [singletons](wikipedia://en/381798)).

:::

### Topological properties {#Topological_properties}

Assume that $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) and the action of $G$ is by [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660).

The action is *wandering* if every $x\in X$ has a [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $U$ such that there are only finitely many $g\in G$ with $(g\cdot U)\cap U\neq\emptyset$.[^n2]

More generally, a point $x\in X$ is called a point of discontinuity for the action of $G$ if there is an open subset $U\ni x$ such that there are only finitely many $g\in G$ with $(g\cdot U)\cap U\neq\emptyset$. The *domain of discontinuity* of the action is the set of all points of discontinuity. Equivalently it is the largest $G$-stable open subset $\Omega\subset X$ such that the action of $G$ on $\Omega$ is wandering.[^n3] In a dynamical context this is also called a *[wandering set](wikipedia://en/2870010)*.

The action is *properly discontinuous* if for every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subset $K\subset X$ there are only finitely many $g\in G$ such that $(g\cdot K)\cap K\neq\emptyset$. This is strictly stronger than wandering; for instance the action of $\mathbb{Z}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2\backslash\{(0,0)\}$ given by $n\cdot(x,y)=(2^nx,2^{-n}y)$ is wandering and free but not properly discontinuous.[^n4]

The action by [deck transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deck_transformation) of the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of a locally [simply connected space](wikipedia://en/523879) on a [universal cover](wikipedia://en/250323#Universal_covering) is wandering and free. Such actions can be characterized by the following property: every $x\in X$ has a neighbourhood $U$ such that $(g\cdot U)\cap U=\emptyset$ for every $g\in G\backslash\{e_G\}$.[^n5] Actions with this property are sometimes called *freely discontinuous*, and the largest subset on which the action is freely discontinuous is then called the *free regular set*.[^n6]

An action of a group $G$ on a [locally compact space](wikipedia://en/48632) $X$ is called *[cocompact](wikipedia://en/12746615)* if there exists a compact subset $A\subset X$ such that $X=G\cdot A$. For a properly discontinuous action, cocompactness is equivalent to compactness of the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) $X/G$.

:::

### Actions of topological groups {#Actions_of_topological_groups}

Now assume $G$ is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and $X$ a topological space on which it acts by homeomorphisms. The action is said to be *continuous* if the map $G\times X\rightarrow X$ is continuous for the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910).

The action is said to be *proper* if the map $G\times X\rightarrow X\times X$ defined by $(g,x)\mapsto(x,g\cdot x)$ is [proper](wikipedia://en/1190262).[^n7] This means that given compact sets $K,K'$ the set of $g\in G$ such that $(g\cdot K)\cap K'\neq\varnothing$ is compact. In particular, this is equivalent to proper discontinuity if $G$ is a [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536).

It is said to be *locally free* if there exists a neighbourhood $U$ of $e_G$ such that $g\cdot x\neq x$ for all $x\in X$ and $g\in U\setminus\{e_G\}$.

The action is said to be *strongly continuous* if the orbital map $g\mapsto g\cdot x$ is continuous for every $x\in X$. Contrary to what the name suggests, this is a weaker property than continuity of the action.

If $G$ is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) and $X$ a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219), then the subspace of *smooth points* for the action is the set of points $x\in X$ such that the map $g\mapsto g\cdot x$ is [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map). There is a well-developed theory of [Lie group actions](wikipedia://en/39904936), i.e. action which are smooth on the whole space.

### Linear actions {#Linear_actions}

If $g$ acts by [linear transformations](wikipedia://en/18102) on a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the action is said to be [irreducible](wikipedia://en/385982) if there are no proper nonzero $g$-invariant submodules. It is said to be *[semisimple](wikipedia://en/45256442)* if it decomposes as a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of irreducible actions.

## Orbits and stabilizers {#Orbits_and_stabilizers}

*Image: Compound of five tetrahedra.png.* In the [compound of five tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/3645795), the symmetry group is the (rotational) icosahedral group $I$ of order 60, while the stabilizer of a single chosen tetrahedron is the (rotational) [tetrahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_group) $T$ of order 12, and the orbit space $I / T$ (of order 60/12 = 5) is naturally identified with the 5 tetrahedra – the coset $gT$ corresponds to the tetrahedron to which $g$ sends the chosen tetrahedron.

Consider a group $G$ acting on a set $X$. The *orbit* of an element $x$ in $X$ is the set of elements in $X$ to which $x$ can be moved by the elements of $G$. The orbit of $x$ is denoted by $G \cdot x$:

$$G \cdot x = \{ g \cdot x : g \in G \}.$$

The defining properties of a group guarantee that the set of orbits of (points $x$ in) $X$ under the action of $G$ form a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of $X$. The associated [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) is defined by saying $x \sim y$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there exists a $g$ in $G$ with $g \cdot x = y$. The orbits are then the [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) under this relation; two elements $x$ and $y$ are equivalent if and only if their orbits are the same, that is, $G \cdot x = G \cdot y$.

The group action is transitive if and only if it has exactly one orbit, that is, if there exists $x$ in $X$ with $G \cdot x = X$. This is the case if and only if $G \cdot x = X$ for all $x$ in $X$ (given that $X$ is non-empty).

The set of all orbits of $X$ under the action of $G$ is written as $X / G$ (or, less frequently, as $G \backslash X$), and is called the *quotient* of the action. In geometric situations it may be called the *orbit space*, while in algebraic situations it may be called the space of *coinvariants*, and written $X_{G}$, by contrast with the invariants (fixed points), denoted $X^{G}$: the coinvariants are a quotient while the invariants are a subset. The coinvariant terminology and notation are used particularly in [group cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) and [group homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homology), which use the same superscript/subscript convention.

### Invariant subsets {#Invariant_subsets}

If $Y$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $X$, then $G\cdot Y$ denotes the set $\{g\cdot y\mid g\in G,y\in Y\}$. The subset $Y$ is said to be *invariant under*$G$ if $G\cdot Y=Y$ (which is equivalent $G\cdot Y\subseteq Y$). In that case, $G$ also operates on $Y$ by [restricting](wikipedia://en/1589032) the action to $Y$. The subset $Y$ is called *fixed under*$G$ if $g\cdot y=y$ for all $g$ in $G$ and all $y$ in $Y$. Every subset that is fixed under $G$ is also invariant under $G$, but not conversely.

Every orbit is an invariant subset of $X$ on which $G$ acts transitively. Conversely, any invariant subset of $X$ is a union of orbits. The action of $G$ on $X$ is *transitive* if and only if all elements are equivalent, meaning that there is only one orbit.

A $G$*\-invariant* element of $X$ is $x\in X$ such that $g\cdot x=x$ for all $g\in G$. The set of all such $x$ is denoted $X^G$ and called the $G$*\-invariants* of $X$. When $X$ is a [$G$-module](wikipedia://en/26068776), $X^G$ is the zeroth [cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) group of $G$ with coefficients in $X$, and the higher cohomology groups are the [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) of the [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) of $G$-invariants.

::: {#o32 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Isotropy group"}

### Fixed points and stabilizer subgroups {#Fixed_points_and_stabilizer_subgroups}

Given $g$ in $G$ and $x$ in $X$ with $g\cdot x=x$, it is said that "$x$ is a fixed point of $g$" or that "$g$ fixes $x$". For every $x$ in $X$, the **stabilizer subgroup** of $G$ with respect to $x$ (also called the **isotropy group** or **little group**[^o33]) is the set of all elements in $G$ that fix $x$:

$$G_x = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}.$$

This is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $G$, though typically not a normal one. The action of $G$ on $X$ is free if and only if all stabilizers are trivial. The kernel $N$ of the homomorphism with the symmetric group, $G\rightarrow\operatorname{Sym}(X)$, is given by the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of the stabilizers $G_x$ for all $x$ in $X$. If $N$ is trivial, the action is said to be faithful (or effective).

Let $x$ and $y$ be two elements in $X$, and let $g$ be a group element such that $y=g\cdot x$. Then the two stabilizer groups $G_x$ and $G_y$ are related by $G_y=gG_xg^{-1}$.

Proof: by definition, $h\in G_y$ if and only if $h\cdot(g\cdot x)=g\cdot x$. Applying $g^{-1}$ to both sides of this equality yields $(g^{-1}hg)\cdot x=x$; that is, $g^{-1}hg\in G_x$.

An opposite inclusion follows similarly by taking $h\in G_x$ and $x=g^{-1}\cdot y$.

The above says that the stabilizers of elements in the same orbit are [conjugate](wikipedia://en/49176) to each other. Thus, to each orbit, we can associate a [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176) of a subgroup of $G$ (that is, the set of all conjugates of the subgroup). Let $(H)$ denote the conjugacy class of $H$. Then the orbit $O$ has type $(H)$ if the stabilizer $G_x$ of some/any $x$ in $O$ belongs to $(H)$. A maximal orbit type is often called a [principal orbit type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_orbit_type).

:::

### Orbit–stabilizer theorem

Orbits and stabilizers are closely related. For a fixed $x$ in $X$, consider the map $f : G \to X$ given by $g \mapsto g \cdot x$. By definition the image $f(G)$ of this map is the orbit $G \cdot x$. The condition for two elements to have the same image is

$$f(g)=f(h) \iff g \cdot x = h \cdot x \iff g^{-1}h \cdot x = x \iff g^{-1}h \in G_x \iff h \in gG_x.$$

In other words, $f(g) = f(h)$ *if and only if* $g$ and $h$ lie in the same [coset](wikipedia://en/98759) for the stabilizer subgroup $G_{x}$. Thus, the [fiber](wikipedia://en/6603892) $f^{-1}(\{y\})$ of $f$ over any $y$ in $G \cdot x$ is contained in such a coset, and every such coset also occurs as a fiber. Therefore $f$ induces a bijection

$$G / G_x \cong G \cdot x$$

between the set $G / G_{x}$ of cosets for the stabilizer subgroup and the orbit $G \cdot x$, which sends $gG_{x} \mapsto g \cdot x$.[^n8] This result is known as the *orbit–stabilizer theorem*.

If $G$ is finite then the orbit–stabilizer theorem, together with [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150), gives

$$|G \cdot x| = [G\,:\,G_x] = |G| / |G_x|.$$

In other words, the length of the orbit of $x$ times the order of its stabilizer is the [order of the group](wikipedia://en/493527). In particular that implies that the orbit length is a divisor of the group order.

::: {#o35 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**

Let $G$ be a group of prime order $p$ acting on a set $X$ with $k$ elements. Since each orbit has either $1$ or $p$ elements, there are at least $k \bmod p$ orbits of length $1$ which are $G$-invariant elements. More specifically, $k$ and the number of $G$-invariant elements are congruent modulo $p$.[^n9]

This result is especially useful since it can be employed for counting arguments (typically in situations where $X$ is finite as well).

*Image: Labeled cube graph.png.* Cubical graph with vertices labeled

:::

::: {#o37 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**

We can use the orbit–stabilizer theorem to count the automorphisms of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806). Consider the [cubical graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubical_graph) as pictured, and let $G$ denote its [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186) group. Then $G$ acts on the set of vertices $\{1, 2, ..., 8\}$, and this action is transitive as can be seen by composing rotations about the center of the cube. Thus, by the orbit–stabilizer theorem, $\lvert G\rvert = \lvert G \cdot 1\rvert \lvert G_{1}\rvert = 8 \lvert G_{1}\rvert$. Applying the theorem now to the stabilizer $G_{1}$, we can obtain $\lvert G_{1}\rvert = \lvert (G_{1}) \cdot 2\rvert \lvert (G_{1})_{2}\rvert$. Any element of $G$ that fixes 1 must send 2 to either 2, 4, or 5. As an example of such automorphisms consider the rotation around the diagonal axis through 1 and 7 by $2\pi/3$, which permutes 2, 4, 5 and 3, 6, 8, and fixes 1 and 7. Thus, $\lvert (G_{1}) \cdot 2\rvert = 3$. Applying the theorem a third time gives $\lvert (G_{1})_{2}\rvert = \lvert ((G_{1})_{2}) \cdot 3\rvert \lvert ((G_{1})_{2})_{3}\rvert$. Any element of $G$ that fixes 1 and 2 must send 3 to either 3 or 6. Reflecting the cube at the plane through 1, 2, 7 and 8 is such an automorphism sending 3 to 6, thus $\lvert ((G_{1})_{2}) \cdot 3\rvert = 2$. One also sees that $((G_{1})_{2})_{3}$ consists only of the identity automorphism, as any element of $G$ fixing 1, 2 and 3 must also fix all other vertices, since they are determined by their adjacency to 1, 2 and 3. Combining the preceding calculations, we can now obtain $\lvert G\rvert = 8 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 48$.

:::

### Burnside's lemma {#Burnside's_lemma}

A result closely related to the orbit–stabilizer theorem is [Burnside's lemma](wikipedia://en/251900):

$$|X/G|=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G} |X^g|,$$

where $X^{g}$ is the set of points fixed by $g$. This result is mainly of use when $G$ and $X$ are finite, when it can be interpreted as follows: the number of orbits is equal to the average number of points fixed per group element.

:::

### The Burnside ring {#The_Burnside_ring}

Fixing a group $G$, the set of formal differences of finite $G$-sets forms a ring called the [Burnside ring](wikipedia://en/4933121) of $G$, where addition corresponds to [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946), and multiplication to [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095).

::: {#o42 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The *trivial* action of any group $G$ on any set $X$ is defined by $g \cdot x = x$ for all $g$ in $G$ and all $x$ in $X$; that is, every group element induces the [identity permutation](wikipedia://en/15069) on $X$.[^o43]
- In every group $G$, left multiplication is an action of $G$ on $G$: $g \cdot x = gx$ for all $g$, $x$ in $G$. This action is free and transitive (regular), and forms the basis of a rapid proof of [Cayley's theorem](wikipedia://en/105967) – that every group is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group of permutations of the set $G$.
- In every group $G$ with subgroup $H$, left multiplication is an action of $G$ on the set of cosets $G / H$: $g \cdot aH = gaH$ for all $g$, $a$ in $G$. In particular if $H$ contains no nontrivial [normal subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_subgroups) of $G$ this induces an isomorphism from $G$ to a subgroup of the permutation group of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_a_permutation_group) $[G : H]$.
- In every group $G$, [conjugation](wikipedia://en/61839) is an action of $G$ on $G$: $g \cdot x = gxg^{-1}$. An exponential notation is commonly used for the right-action variant: $x^{g} = g^{-1}xg$; it satisfies ($x^{g})^{h} = x^{gh}$.
- In every group $G$ with subgroup $H$, conjugation is an action of $G$ on conjugates of $H$: $g \cdot K = gKg^{-1}$ for all $g$ in $G$ and $K$ conjugates of $H$.
- An action of $\mathbf{Z}$ on a set $X$ uniquely determines and is determined by an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of $X$, given by the action of 1. Similarly, an action of $\mathbf{Z} / 2\mathbf{Z}$ on $X$ is equivalent to the data of an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) of $X$.
- The symmetric group $S_{n}$ and its subgroups act on the set $\{1, ..., n\}$ by permuting its elements
- The [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of a polyhedron acts on the set of vertices of that polyhedron. It also acts on the set of faces or the set of edges of the polyhedron.
- The symmetry group of any geometrical object acts on the set of points of that object.
- For a [coordinate space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_space) $V$ over a field $F$ with group of units $F*$, the mapping $F* \times V \to V$ given by $a \times (x_{1}, x_{2}, ..., x_{n}) \mapsto (ax_{1}, ax_{2}, ..., ax_{n})$ is a group action called [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373).
- The automorphism group of a vector space (or [graph](wikipedia://en/12401), or group, or ring ...) acts on the vector space (or set of vertices of the graph, or group, or ring ...).
- The general linear group $\operatorname{GL}(n, K)$ and its subgroups, particularly its [Lie subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_subgroup) (including the special linear group $\operatorname{SL}(n, K)$, [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $O(n, K)$, special orthogonal group $\operatorname{SO}(n, K)$, and [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977) $\operatorname{Sp}(n, K)$) are [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) that act on the vector space $K^{n}$. The group operations are given by multiplying the matrices from the groups with the vectors from $K^{n}$.
- The general linear group $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbf{Z})$ acts on $\mathbf{Z}^{n}$ by natural matrix action. The orbits of its action are classified by the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) of coordinates of the vector in $\mathbf{Z}^{n}$.
- The [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828) acts transitively on the points of an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), and the subgroup V of the affine group (that is, a vector space) has transitive and free (that is, *regular*) action on these points;[^n10] indeed this can be used to give a definition of an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834#Definition).
- The [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) $\operatorname{PGL}(n + 1, K)$ and its subgroups, particularly its Lie subgroups, which are Lie groups that act on the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbf{P}^{n}(K)$. This is a quotient of the action of the general linear group on projective space. Particularly notable is $\operatorname{PGL}(2, K)$, the symmetries of the projective line, which is sharply 3-transitive, preserving the [cross ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_ratio); the [Möbius group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_group) $\operatorname{PGL}(2, \mathbf{C})$ is of particular interest.
- The [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of the plane act on the set of 2D images and patterns, such as [wallpaper patterns](wikipedia://en/386214). The definition can be made more precise by specifying what is meant by image or pattern, for example, a function of position with values in a set of colors. Isometries are in fact one example of affine group (action).
- The sets acted on by a group $G$ comprise the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of $G$-sets in which the objects are $G$-sets and the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) are $G$-set homomorphisms: functions $f : X \to Y$ such that $g \cdot (f(x)) = f(g \cdot x)$ for every $g$ in $G$.
- The [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $L / K$ acts on the field $L$ but has only a trivial action on elements of the subfield $K$. Subgroups of $\operatorname{Gal}(L / K)$ correspond to subfields of $L$ that contain $K$, that is, intermediate field extensions between $L$ and $K$.
- The additive group of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $(\mathbf{R}, +)$ acts on the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) of "[well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved)" systems in [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) (and in more general [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems)) by [time translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_translation): if $t$ is in $\mathbf{R}$ and $x$ is in the phase space, then $x$ describes a state of the system, and $t + x$ is defined to be the state of the system $t$ seconds later if $t$ is positive or $-t$ seconds ago if $t$ is negative.
- The additive group of the real numbers $(\mathbf{R}, +)$ acts on the set of real [functions of a real variable](wikipedia://en/1268958) in various ways, with $(t \cdot f)(x)$ equal to, for example, $f(x + t)$, $f(x) + t$, $f(xe^{t})$, $f(x)e^{t}$, $f(x + t)e^{t}$, or $f(xe^{t}) + t$, but not $f(xe^{t} + t)$.
- Given a group action of $G$ on $X$, we can define an induced action of $G$ on the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $X$, by setting $g \cdot U = \{g \cdot u : u \in U\}$ for every subset $U$ of $X$ and every $g$ in $G$. This is useful, for instance, in studying the action of the large [Mathieu group](wikipedia://en/380207) on a 24-set and in studying symmetry in certain models of [finite geometries](wikipedia://en/267061).
- The [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) with [norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_of_a_quaternion) 1 (the [versors](wikipedia://en/2644621)), as a multiplicative group, act on $\mathbf{R}^{3}$: for any such quaternion $z = \cos \alpha/2 + \mathbf{v} \sin \alpha/2$, the mapping $f(\mathbf{x}) = z\mathbf{x}z^{*}$ is a counterclockwise rotation through an angle $\alpha$ about an axis given by a unit vector $\mathbf{v}$; $z$ is the same rotation; see [quaternions and spatial rotation](wikipedia://en/186057). This is not a faithful action because the quaternion $-1$ leaves all points where they were, as does the quaternion $1$.
- Given left $G$-sets $X$, $Y$, there is a left $G$-set $Y^{X}$ whose elements are $G$-equivariant maps $\alpha : X \times G \to Y$, and with left $G$-action given by $g \cdot \alpha = \alpha \circ (\operatorname{id}_{X} \times -g)$ (where "$-g$" indicates right multiplication by $g$). This $G$-set has the property that its fixed points correspond to equivariant maps $X \to Y$; more generally, it is an [exponential object](wikipedia://en/2237858) in the category of $G$-sets.

:::

## Group actions and groupoids {#Group_actions_and_groupoids}

The notion of group action can be encoded by the *action [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543)* $G' = G \ltimes X$ associated to the group action. The stabilizers of the action are the vertex groups of the groupoid and the orbits of the action are its components.

::: {#o47 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Isomorphism of G-sets"}

## Morphisms and isomorphisms between *G*-sets {#Morphisms_and_isomorphisms_between_G-sets}

If $X$ and $Y$ are two $G$-sets, a *morphism* from $X$ to $Y$ is a function $f : X \to Y$ such that $f(g \cdot x) = g \cdot f(x)$ for all $g$ in $G$ and all $x$ in $X$. Morphisms of $G$-sets are also called *[equivariant maps](wikipedia://en/422711)* or $G$-*maps*.

The composition of two morphisms is again a morphism. If a morphism $f$ is bijective, then its inverse is also a morphism. In this case $f$ is called an *[isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)*, and the two $G$-sets $X$ and $Y$ are called *isomorphic*; for all practical purposes, isomorphic $G$-sets are indistinguishable.

Some example isomorphisms:

- Every regular $G$ action is isomorphic to the action of $G$ on $G$ given by left multiplication.
- Every free $G$ action is isomorphic to $G \times S$, where $S$ is some set and $G$ acts on $G \times S$ by left multiplication on the first coordinate. ($S$ can be taken to be the set of orbits $X / G$.)
- Every transitive $G$ action is isomorphic to left multiplication by $G$ on the set of left cosets of some subgroup $H$ of $G$. ($H$ can be taken to be the stabilizer group of any element of the original $G$-set.)

With this notion of morphism, the collection of all $G$-sets forms a [category](wikipedia://en/5869); this category is a [Grothendieck topos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_topos) (in fact, assuming a classical [metalogic](wikipedia://en/387934), this [topos](wikipedia://en/21391464) will even be Boolean).

:::

## Variants and generalizations {#Variants_and_generalizations}

We can also consider actions of [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652) on sets, by using the same two axioms as above. This does not define bijective maps and equivalence relations however. See [semigroup action](wikipedia://en/1058218).

Instead of actions on sets, we can define actions of groups and monoids on objects of an arbitrary category: start with an object $X$ of some category, and then define an action on $X$ as a monoid homomorphism into the monoid of [endomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endomorphisms) of $X$. If $X$ has an underlying set, then all definitions and facts stated above can be carried over. For example, if we take the category of vector spaces, we obtain [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695) in this fashion.

We can view a group $G$ as a category with a single object in which every morphism is [invertible](wikipedia://en/45199).[^o49] A (left) group action is then nothing but a (covariant) [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from $G$ to the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), and a group representation is a functor from $G$ to the [category of vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_spaces).[^o50] A morphism between $G$-sets is then a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) between the group action functors.[^o51] In analogy, an action of a [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) is a functor from the groupoid to the category of sets or to some other category.

In addition to [continuous actions](wikipedia://en/7522685) of topological groups on topological spaces, one also often considers [smooth actions](wikipedia://en/39904936) of Lie groups on [smooth manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), regular actions of [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924) on [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808), and [actions](wikipedia://en/56600253) of [group schemes](wikipedia://en/510523) on [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754). All of these are examples of [group objects](wikipedia://en/53507) acting on objects of their respective category.
