# If and only if

3

*Image: Venn1001.svg.* “If and only if” depicted with a Venn diagram

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="If and only if"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and related fields such as [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), "**if and only if**" (often shortened as "**iff**") states that the truth values of two statements are equal. It is paraphrased by the [biconditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biconditional), a [logical connective](wikipedia://en/18153)[^o2] between statements. The biconditional is true in two cases, where either both statements are true or both are false. The connective is [biconditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biconditional) (a statement of **material equivalence**),[^o3] and can be likened to the standard [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808) ("only if", equal to "if ... then") combined with its converse ("if"); hence the name. The result is that the truth of either one of the connected statements requires the truth of the other (i.e. either both statements are true, or both are false), though it is controversial whether the connective thus defined is properly rendered by the English "if and only if"—with its pre-existing meaning. For example, *P if and only if Q* means that *P* is true whenever *Q* is true, and the only case in which *P* is true is if *Q* is also true, whereas in the case of *P if Q*, there could be other scenarios where *P* is true and *Q* is false.

:::

In writing, phrases commonly used as alternatives to P "if and only if" Q include: *Q is [necessary and sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_conditions#Simultaneous_necessity_and_sufficiency) for P*, *for P it is necessary and sufficient that Q*, *P is equivalent (or materially equivalent) to Q* (compare with [material implication](wikipedia://en/658808)), *P precisely if Q*, *P precisely (or exactly) when Q*, *P exactly in case Q*, and *P just in case Q*.[^o4] Some authors regard "iff" as unsuitable in formal writing;[^o5] others consider it a "borderline case" and tolerate its use.[^o6] In [logical formulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula_%28mathematical_logic%29), logical symbols, such as $\leftrightarrow$ and $\Leftrightarrow$,[^o7] are used instead of these phrases; see below.

## Usage {#Usage}

::: {#o15 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

### Notation {#Notation}

400% Logical symbols representing *iff*

The corresponding logical symbols are "$\leftrightarrow$", "$\Leftrightarrow$",[^o7] and $\equiv$,[^o16] and sometimes "iff". These are usually treated as equivalent. However, some texts of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) (particularly those on [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), rather than [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154)) make a distinction between these, in which the first, $\leftrightarrow$, is used as a symbol in logic formulas, while $\Leftrightarrow$ or $\equiv$ is used in reasoning about those logic formulas (e.g., in [metalogic](wikipedia://en/387934)). In [Łukasiewicz](wikipedia://en/305064)'s [Polish notation](wikipedia://en/25056), it is the prefix symbol $E$.[^o17]

Another term for the [logical connective](wikipedia://en/18153), i.e., the symbol in logic formulas, is [exclusive nor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_nor).

In [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX), "if and only if" is shown as a long double arrow: $\iff$ via command `\iff` or `\Longleftrightarrow`.[^o18]

:::

::: {#o20 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

### Proofs {#Proofs}

In most [logical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system), one [proves](wikipedia://en/183478) a statement of the form "P iff Q" by proving either "if P, then Q" and "if Q, then P", or "if P, then Q" and "if not-P, then not-Q". Proving these pairs of statements sometimes leads to a more natural proof, since there are no obvious conditions under which one would infer a biconditional directly. An alternative is to prove the [disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction) "(P and Q) or (not-P and not-Q)", which itself can be inferred directly from either of its disjuncts—that is, because "iff" is [truth-functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth-function), "P iff Q" follows if P and Q have been shown to be both true, or both false.

:::

### Origin of iff and pronunciation {#Origin_of_iff_and_pronunciation}

Usage of the abbreviation "iff" first appeared in print in [John L. Kelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Kelley)'s 1955 book *General Topology*.[^o22] Its invention is often credited to [Paul Halmos](wikipedia://en/318598), who wrote "I invented 'iff,' for 'if and only if'—but I could never believe I was really its first inventor."[^o23]

It is somewhat unclear how "iff" was meant to be pronounced. In current practice, the single 'word' "iff" is almost always read as the four words "if and only if". However, in the preface of *General Topology*, Kelley suggests that it should be read differently: "In some cases where mathematical content requires 'if and only if' and [euphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphony) demands something less I use Halmos' 'iff. The authors of one discrete mathematics textbook suggest:[^o24] "Should you need to pronounce iff, really [hang on to the 'ff'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant_gemination) so that people hear the difference from 'if, implying that "iff" could be pronounced as .

### Usage in definitions {#Usage_in_definitions}

Conventionally, [definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definitions) are "if and only if" statements; some texts — such as Kelley's *General Topology* — follow this convention, and use "if and only if" or *iff* in definitions of new terms.[^o26] However, this usage of "if and only if" is relatively uncommon and overlooks the linguistic fact that the "if" of a definition is interpreted as meaning "if and only if". The majority of textbooks, research papers and articles (including English Wikipedia articles) follow the linguistic convention of interpreting "if" as "if and only if" whenever a mathematical definition is involved (as in "a topological space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover").[^o27] Moreover, in the case of a [recursive definition](wikipedia://en/7964#recursive_definitions), the *only if* half of the definition is interpreted as a sentence in the metalanguage stating that the sentences in the definition of a predicate are the *only sentences* determining the extension of the predicate.

## In terms of Euler diagrams {#In_terms_of_Euler_diagrams}

[Euler diagrams](wikipedia://en/2427526) show logical relationships among events, properties, and so forth. "P only if Q", "if P then Q", and "P→Q" all mean that P is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631), either proper or improper, of Q. "P if Q", "if Q then P", and Q→P all mean that Q is a proper or improper subset of P. "P if and only if Q" and "Q if and only if P" both mean that the sets P and Q are identical to each other.

::: {#o30 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="All and only"}

## More general usage {#More_general_usage}

*Iff* is used outside the field of logic as well. Wherever logic is applied, especially in [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) discussions, it has the same meaning as above: it is an abbreviation for *if and only if*, indicating that one statement is both [necessary and sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_conditions) for the other. This is an example of [mathematical jargon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_jargon) (although, as noted above, *if* is more often used than *iff* in statements of definition).

The elements of *X* are *all and only* the elements of *Y* means: "For any *z* in the [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180), *z* is in *X* if and only if *z* is in *Y*."

:::

## When "if" means "if and only if" {#When_"if"_means_"if_and_only_if"}

In their *[Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_Intelligence:_A_Modern_Approach)*, [Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_J._Russell) and [Norvig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Norvig) note that it is often more natural to express *if and only if* as *if* together with a "database (or logic programming) semantics". They give the example of the English sentence "Richard has two brothers, Geoffrey and John".[^o32]

In a [database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database) or [logic program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_programming), this could be represented simply by two sentences:

Brother(Richard, Geoffrey).

Brother(Richard, John).

The database semantics interprets the database (or program) as containing *all* and *only* the knowledge relevant for problem solving in a given domain. It interprets *only if* as expressing in the metalanguage that the sentences in the database represent the *only* knowledge that should be considered when drawing conclusions from the database.

In [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) with the standard semantics, the same English sentence would need to be represented, using *if and only if*, with *only if* interpreted in the object language, in some such form as:

$\forall$ X(Brother(Richard, X) iff X = Geoffrey or X = John).

Geoffrey ≠ John.

Compared with the standard semantics for first-order logic, the database semantics has a more efficient implementation. Instead of reasoning with sentences of the form:

*conclusion iff conditions*

it uses sentences of the form:

*conclusion if conditions*

to [reason forwards](wikipedia://en/568962) from *conditions* to *conclusions* or [backwards](wikipedia://en/568967) from *conclusions* to *conditions*.

The database semantics is analogous to the legal principle [expressio unius est exclusio alterius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressio_unius_est_exclusio_alterius) (the express mention of one thing excludes all others). Moreover, it underpins the application of logic programming to the representation of legal texts and legal reasoning.[^o33]
