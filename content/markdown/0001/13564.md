# Homomorphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Homomorphism"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), a **homomorphism** is a [structure-preserving](wikipedia://en/27716891) [map](wikipedia://en/516931) between two [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) of the same type (such as two [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), two [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), or two [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370)). The word *homomorphism* comes from the [Ancient Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language): grc (grc) meaning "same" and grc (grc) meaning "form" or "shape". However, the word was apparently introduced to mathematics due to a (mis)translation of German de meaning "similar" to ὁμός meaning "same".[^o2] The term "homomorphism" appeared as early as 1892, when it was attributed to the German mathematician [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) (1849–1925).[^o3]

:::

Homomorphisms of vector spaces are also called [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102), and their study is the subject of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).

The concept of homomorphism has been generalized, under the name of [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891), to many other structures that either do not have an underlying set, or are not algebraic. This generalization is the starting point of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869).

A homomorphism may also be an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569), an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160), etc. (see below). Each of those can be defined in a way that may be generalized to any class of morphisms.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A homomorphism is a map between two [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) of the same type (e.g. two groups, two fields, two vector spaces), that preserves the [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245) of the structures. This means a [map](wikipedia://en/516931) $f: A \to B$ between two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) $A$, $B$ equipped with the same structure such that, if $\cdot$ is an operation of the structure (supposed here, for simplification, to be a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948)), then

$$f(x\cdot y)=f(x)\cdot f(y)$$

for every pair $x$, $y$ of elements of $A$.[^o6] One says often that $f$ preserves the operation or is compatible with the operation.

Formally, a map $f: A\to B$ preserves an operation $\mu$ of [arity](wikipedia://en/42301) $k$, defined on both $A$ and $B$ if

$$f(\mu_A(a_1, \ldots, a_k)) = \mu_B(f(a_1), \ldots, f(a_k)),$$

for all elements $a_1, ..., a_k$ in $A$.

The operations that must be preserved by a homomorphism include [0-ary operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0-ary_function), that is the constants. In particular, when an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) is required by the type of structure, the identity element of the first structure must be mapped to the corresponding identity element of the second structure.

For example:

- A [semigroup homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semigroup_homomorphism) is a map between [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) that preserves the semigroup operation.
- A [monoid homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_homomorphism) is a map between [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652) that preserves the monoid operation and maps the identity element of the first monoid to that of the second monoid (the identity element is a [0-ary operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0-ary_function)).
- A [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) is a map between [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) that preserves the group operation. This implies that the group homomorphism maps the identity element of the first group to the identity element of the second group, and maps the [inverse](wikipedia://en/45199) of an element of the first group to the inverse of the image of this element. Thus a semigroup homomorphism between groups is necessarily a group homomorphism.
- A [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) is a map between [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) that preserves the ring addition, the ring multiplication, and the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity). Whether the multiplicative identity is to be preserved depends upon the definition of *ring* in use. If the multiplicative identity is not preserved, one has a [rng](wikipedia://en/3405004) homomorphism.
- A [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) is a homomorphism of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370); that is, a group homomorphism between vector spaces that preserves the abelian group structure and [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373).
- A [module homomorphism](wikipedia://en/474118), also called a linear map between [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), is defined similarly.
- An [algebra homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) is a map that preserves the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) operations.

An algebraic structure may have more than one operation, and a homomorphism is required to preserve each operation. Thus a map that preserves only some of the operations is not a homomorphism of the structure, but only a homomorphism of the substructure obtained by considering only the preserved operations. For example, a map between monoids that preserves the monoid operation and not the identity element, is not a monoid homomorphism, but only a semigroup homomorphism.

The notation for the operations does not need to be the same in the source and the target of a homomorphism. For example, the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) form a group for addition, and the positive real numbers form a group for multiplication. The [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678)

$$x\mapsto e^x$$

satisfies

$$e^{x+y} = e^xe^y,$$

and is thus a homomorphism between these two groups. It is even an isomorphism (see below), as its [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907), the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), satisfies

$$\ln(xy)=\ln(x)+\ln(y),$$

and is also a group homomorphism.

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Exponentiation as monoid homomorphism svg.svg.* [Monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) homomorphism $f$ from the monoid $\#008000$ to the monoid $\#800000$, defined by $f(x) = 2^x$. It is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196), but not [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873).

The [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) are a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), having both addition and multiplication. The set of all 2×2 [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) is also a ring, under [matrix addition](wikipedia://en/125276) and [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). If we define a function between these rings as follows:

$$f(r) = \begin{pmatrix} r & 0 \\ 0 & r \end{pmatrix}$$

where $r$ is a real number, then $f$ is a homomorphism of rings, since $f$ preserves both addition:

$$f(r+s) = \begin{pmatrix} r+s & 0 \\ 0 & r+s \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} r & 0 \\ 0 & r \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix} = f(r) + f(s)$$

and multiplication:

$$f(rs) = \begin{pmatrix} rs & 0 \\ 0 & rs \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} r & 0 \\ 0 & r \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix} = f(r)\,f(s).$$

For another example, the nonzero [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under the operation of multiplication, as do the nonzero real numbers. (Zero must be excluded from both groups since it does not have a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940), which is required for elements of a group.) Define a function $f$ from the nonzero complex numbers to the nonzero real numbers by

$$f(z) = |z| .$$

That is, $f$ is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) (or modulus) of the complex number $z$. Then $f$ is a homomorphism of groups, since it preserves multiplication:

$$f(z_1 z_2) = |z_1 z_2| = |z_1| |z_2| = f(z_1) f(z_2).$$

Note that $f$ cannot be extended to a homomorphism of rings (from the complex numbers to the real numbers), since it does not preserve addition:

$$|z_1 + z_2| \ne |z_1| + |z_2|.$$

As another example, the diagram shows a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) homomorphism $f$ from the monoid $(\mathbb{N}, +, 0)$ to the monoid $(\mathbb{N}, \times, 1)$. Due to the different names of corresponding operations, the structure preservation properties satisfied by $f$ amount to $f(x+y) = f(x) \times f(y)$ and $f(0) = 1$.

A [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635) $A$ over a field $F$ has a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478), called a *norm*, $N: A \to F$, which is a group homomorphism from the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of $A$ to the multiplicative group of $F$.

## Special homomorphisms {#Special_homomorphisms}

Several kinds of homomorphisms have a specific name, which is also defined for general [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891).

*Image: Venn Diagram of Homomorphisms.jpg.* General relationship of homomorphisms (including [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839), labelled as "Inner")

### Isomorphism {#Isomorphism}

An [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) between [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) of the same type is commonly defined as a [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) homomorphism.[^o11][^o12]

In the more general context of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), an isomorphism is defined as a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) that has an [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) that is also a morphism. In the specific case of algebraic structures, the two definitions are equivalent, although they may differ for non-algebraic structures, which have an underlying set.

More precisely, if

$$f: A\to B$$

is a (homo)morphism, it has an inverse if there exists a homomorphism

$$g: B\to A$$

such that

$$f\circ g = \operatorname{Id}_B \qquad \text{and} \qquad g\circ f = \operatorname{Id}_A.$$

If $A$ and $B$ have underlying sets, and $f: A \to B$ has an inverse $g$, then $f$ is bijective. In fact, $f$ is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), as $f(x) = f(y)$ implies $x = g(f(x)) = g(f(y)) = y$, and $f$ is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), as, for any $x$ in $B$, one has $x = f(g(x))$, and $x$ is the image of an element of $A$.

Conversely, if $f: A \to B$ is a bijective homomorphism between algebraic structures, let $g: B \to A$ be the map such that $g(y)$ is the unique element $x$ of $A$ such that $f(x) = y$. One has $f \circ g = \operatorname{Id}_B \text{ and } g \circ f = \operatorname{Id}_A,$ and it remains only to show that $g$ is a homomorphism. If $*$ is a binary operation of the structure, for every pair $x$, $y$ of elements of $B$, one has

$$g(x*_B y) = g(f(g(x))*_Bf(g(y))) = g(f(g(x)*_A g(y))) = g(x)*_A g(y),$$

and $g$ is thus compatible with $*.$ As the proof is similar for any [arity](wikipedia://en/42301), this shows that $g$ is a homomorphism.

This proof does not work for non-algebraic structures. For example, for [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), a morphism is a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map), and the inverse of a bijective continuous map is not necessarily continuous. An isomorphism of topological spaces, called [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) or bicontinuous map, is thus a bijective continuous map, whose inverse is also continuous.

### Endomorphism {#Endomorphism}

An [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) is a homomorphism whose [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) equals the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264), or, more generally, a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) whose source is equal to its target.[^o11]

The endomorphisms of an algebraic structure, or of an object of a [category](wikipedia://en/53759), form a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) under composition.

The endomorphisms of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) or of a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). In the case of a vector space or a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) of finite [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267), the choice of a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28vector_space%29) induces a [ring isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) between the ring of endomorphisms and the ring of [square matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_matrices) of the same dimension.

### Automorphism {#Automorphism}

An [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) is an endomorphism that is also an isomorphism.[^o11]

The automorphisms of an algebraic structure or of an object of a category form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under composition, which is called the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of the structure.

Many groups that have received a name are automorphism groups of some algebraic structure. For example, the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\operatorname{GL}_n(k)$ is the automorphism group of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of dimension $n$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $k$.

The automorphism groups of [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) were introduced by [Évariste Galois](wikipedia://en/9815) for studying the [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), and are the basis of [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316).

### Monomorphism {#Monomorphism}

For algebraic structures, [monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) are commonly defined as [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) homomorphisms.[^o11] [^o12]

In the more general context of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a monomorphism is defined as a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) that is **[left cancelable](wikipedia://en/490646)**.[^o16] This means that a (homo)morphism $f:A \to B$ is a monomorphism if, for any pair $g$, $h$ of morphisms from any other object $C$ to $A$, then $f \circ g = f \circ h$ implies $g = h$.

These two definitions of *monomorphism* are equivalent for all common algebraic structures. More precisely, they are equivalent for [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), for which every homomorphism is a monomorphism, and for [varieties](wikipedia://en/1021753) of [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), that is algebraic structures for which operations and axioms (identities) are defined without any restriction (the fields do not form a variety, as the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) is defined either as a [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006) or as a property of the multiplication, which are, in both cases, defined only for nonzero elements).

In particular, the two definitions of a monomorphism are equivalent for [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), [magmas](wikipedia://en/141916), [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799), [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652), [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) and [modules](wikipedia://en/276410).

A **[split monomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_monomorphism)** is a homomorphism that has a [left inverse](wikipedia://en/14907#Left_and_right_inverses) and thus it is itself a right inverse of that other homomorphism. That is, a homomorphism $f\colon A \to B$ is a split monomorphism if there exists a homomorphism $g\colon B \to A$ such that $g \circ f = \operatorname{Id}_A.$ A split monomorphism is always a monomorphism, for both meanings of *monomorphism*. For sets and vector spaces, every monomorphism is a split monomorphism, but this property does not hold for most common algebraic structures.

*An injective homomorphism is left cancelable*: If $f\circ g = f\circ h,$ one has $f(g(x))=f(h(x))$ for every $x$ in $C$, the common source of $g$ and $h$. If $f$ is injective, then $g(x) = h(x)$, and thus $g = h$. This proof works not only for algebraic structures, but also for any [category](wikipedia://en/53759) whose objects are sets and arrows are maps between these sets. For example, an injective continuous map is a monomorphism in the category of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450).

For proving that, conversely, a left cancelable homomorphism is injective, it is useful to consider a *[free object](wikipedia://en/333835) on $x$*. Given a [variety](wikipedia://en/1021753) of algebraic structures a free object on $x$ is a pair consisting of an algebraic structure $L$ of this variety and an element $x$ of $L$ satisfying the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245): for every structure $S$ of the variety, and every element $s$ of $S$, there is a unique homomorphism $f: L\to S$ such that $f(x) = s$. For example, for sets, the free object on $x$ is simply $\{x\}$; for [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799), the free object on $x$ is $\{x, x^2, \ldots, x^n, \ldots\},$ which, as, a semigroup, is isomorphic to the additive semigroup of the positive integers; for [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652), the free object on $x$ is $\{1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^n, \ldots\},$ which, as, a monoid, is isomorphic to the additive monoid of the nonnegative integers; for [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), the free object on $x$ is the [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) $\{\ldots, x^{-n}, \ldots, x^{-1}, 1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^n, \ldots\},$ which, as, a group, is isomorphic to the additive group of the integers; for [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), the free object on $x$ is the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $\mathbb{Z}[x];$ for [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) or [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), the free object on $x$ is the vector space or free module that has $x$ as a basis.

*If a free object over $x$ exists, then every left cancelable homomorphism is injective*: let $f\colon A \to B$ be a left cancelable homomorphism, and $a$ and $b$ be two elements of $A$ such $f(a) = f(b)$. By definition of the free object $F$, there exist homomorphisms $g$ and $h$ from $F$ to $A$ such that $g(x) = a$ and $h(x) = b$. As $f(g(x)) = f(h(x))$, one has $f \circ g = f \circ h,$ by the uniqueness in the definition of a universal property. As $f$ is left cancelable, one has $g = h$, and thus $a = b$. Therefore, $f$ is injective.

*Existence of a free object on $x$ for a [variety](wikipedia://en/1021753)* (see also Free object): For building a free object over $x$, consider the set $W$ of the [well-formed formulas](wikipedia://en/404582) built up from $x$ and the operations of the structure. Two such formulas are said equivalent if one may pass from one to the other by applying the axioms ([identities](wikipedia://en/298428) of the structure). This defines an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259), if the identities are not subject to conditions, that is if one works with a variety. Then the operations of the variety are well defined on the set of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of $W$ for this relation. It is straightforward to show that the resulting object is a free object on $x$.

### Epimorphism {#Epimorphism}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), **epimorphisms** are often defined as [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) homomorphisms.[^o11][^o12] On the other hand, in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), [epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/59539) are defined as **right cancelable** [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891).[^o16] This means that a (homo)morphism $f: A \to B$ is an epimorphism if, for any pair $g$, $h$ of morphisms from $B$ to any other object $C$, the equality $g \circ f = h \circ f$ implies $g = h$.

A surjective homomorphism is always right cancelable, but the converse is not always true for algebraic structures. However, the two definitions of *epimorphism* are equivalent for [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) (see below for a proof), and [groups](wikipedia://en/19447).[^o18] The importance of these structures in all mathematics, especially in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), may explain the coexistence of two non-equivalent definitions.

Algebraic structures for which there exist non-surjective epimorphisms include [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) and [rings](wikipedia://en/48404). The most basic example is the inclusion of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) into [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), which is a homomorphism of rings and of multiplicative semigroups. For both structures it is a monomorphism and a non-surjective epimorphism, but not an isomorphism.[^o16][^o19]

A wide generalization of this example is the [localization of a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) by a multiplicative set. Every localization is a ring epimorphism, which is not, in general, surjective. As localizations are fundamental in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), this may explain why in these areas, the definition of epimorphisms as right cancelable homomorphisms is generally preferred.

A **[split epimorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_epimorphism)** is a homomorphism that has a [right inverse](wikipedia://en/14907#Left_and_right_inverses) and thus it is itself a left inverse of that other homomorphism. That is, a homomorphism $f\colon A \to B$ is a split epimorphism if there exists a homomorphism $g\colon B \to A$ such that $f\circ g = \operatorname{Id}_B.$ A split epimorphism is always an epimorphism, for both meanings of *epimorphism*. For sets and vector spaces, every epimorphism is a split epimorphism, but this property does not hold for most common algebraic structures.

In summary, one has

$$\text {split epimorphism} \implies \text{epimorphism (surjective)}\implies \text {epimorphism (right cancelable)};$$

the last implication is an equivalence for sets, vector spaces, modules, abelian groups, and groups; the first implication is an equivalence for sets and vector spaces.

Let $f\colon A \to B$ be a homomorphism. We want to prove that if it is not surjective, it is not right cancelable.

In the case of sets, let $b$ be an element of $B$ that not belongs to $f(A)$, and define $g, h\colon B \to B$ such that $g$ is the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069), and that $h(x) = x$ for every $x \in B,$ except that $h(b)$ is any other element of $B$. Clearly $f$ is not right cancelable, as $g \neq h$ and $g \circ f = h \circ f.$

In the case of vector spaces, abelian groups and modules, the proof relies on the existence of [cokernels](wikipedia://en/279715) and on the fact that the [zero maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_map) are homomorphisms: let $C$ be the cokernel of $f$, and $g\colon B \to C$ be the canonical map, such that $g(f(A)) = 0$. Let $h\colon B\to C$ be the zero map. If $f$ is not surjective, $C \neq 0$, and thus $g \neq h$ (one is a zero map, while the other is not). Thus $f$ is not cancelable, as $g \circ f = h \circ f$ (both are the zero map from $A$ to $C$).

## Kernel {#Kernel}

Any homomorphism $f: X \to Y$ defines an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\sim$ on $X$ by $a \sim b$ if and only if $f(a) = f(b)$. The relation $\sim$ is called the **kernel** of $f$. It is a [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167) on $X$. The [quotient set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set) $X/{\sim}$ can then be given a structure of the same type as $X$, in a natural way, by defining the operations of the quotient set by $[x] \ast [y] = [x \ast y]$, for each operation $\ast$ of $X$. In that case the image of $X$ in $Y$ under the homomorphism $f$ is necessarily [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $X/\!\sim$; this fact is one of the [isomorphism theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorem).

When the algebraic structure is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) for some operation, the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) $K$ of the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of this operation suffices to characterize the equivalence relation. In this case, the quotient by the equivalence relation is denoted by $X/K$ (usually read as "$X$ [mod](wikipedia://en/25977) $K$"). Also in this case, it is $K$, rather than $\sim$, that is called the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of $f$. The kernels of homomorphisms of a given type of algebraic structure are naturally equipped with some structure. This structure type of the kernels is the same as the considered structure, in the case of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) and [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), but is different and has received a specific name in other cases, such as [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) for kernels of [group homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homomorphisms) and [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) for kernels of [ring homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411) (in the case of non-commutative rings, the kernels are the [two-sided ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_ideal)).

## Relational structures {#Relational_structures}

In [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), the notion of an algebraic structure is generalized to structures involving both operations and relations. Let *L* be a signature consisting of function and relation symbols, and *A*, *B* be two *L*-structures. Then a **homomorphism** from *A* to *B* is a mapping *h* from the domain of *A* to the domain of *B* such that

- *h*(*F*<sup>*A*</sup>(*a*<sub>1</sub>,...,*a*<sub>*n*</sub>)) = *F*<sup>*B*</sup>(*h*(*a*<sub>1</sub>),...,*h*(*a*<sub>*n*</sub>)) for each *n*-ary function symbol *F* in *L*,
- *R*<sup>*A*</sup>(*a*<sub>1</sub>,...,*a*<sub>*n*</sub>) implies *R*<sup>*B*</sup>(*h*(*a*<sub>1</sub>),...,*h*(*a*<sub>*n*</sub>)) for each *n*-ary relation symbol *R* in *L*.

In the special case with just one binary relation, we obtain the notion of a [graph homomorphism](wikipedia://en/676328).[^o22]

## Formal language theory {#Formal_language_theory}

Homomorphisms are also used in the study of [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939)[^o25] and are often briefly referred to as *morphisms*.[^o26] Given alphabets $\Sigma_1$ and $\Sigma_2$, a function $h \colon \Sigma_1^* \to \Sigma_2^*$ such that $h(uv) = h(u) h(v)$ for all $u,v \in \Sigma_1$ is called a *homomorphism* on $\Sigma_1^*$.[^o27] If $h$ is a homomorphism on $\Sigma_1^*$ and $\varepsilon$ denotes the empty string, then $h$ is called an $\varepsilon$*\-free homomorphism* when $h(x) \neq \varepsilon$ for all $x \neq \varepsilon$ in $\Sigma_1^*$.

A homomorphism $h \colon \Sigma_1^* \to \Sigma_2^*$ on $\Sigma_1^*$ that satisfies $|h(a)| = k$ for all $a \in \Sigma_1$ is called a $k$*\-uniform* homomorphism.[^n1] If $|h(a)| = 1$ for all $a \in \Sigma_1$ (that is, $h$ is 1-uniform), then $h$ is also called a *coding* or a *projection*.

The set $\Sigma^*$ of words formed from the alphabet $\Sigma$ may be thought of as the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) generated by $\Sigma$. Here the monoid operation is [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474) and the identity element is the empty word. From this perspective, a language homomorphism is precisely a monoid homomorphism.[^o29]
