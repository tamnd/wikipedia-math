# Inverse limit

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inverse limit"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **inverse limit** (also called a **projective limit**) is a construction that allows one to "glue together" several related [objects](wikipedia://en/19453961), the precise gluing process being specified by [morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphisms) between the objects. Inverse limits can be defined in any [category](wikipedia://en/53759), although their existence depends on the category that is considered. They are a special case of the concept of a [limit](wikipedia://en/39248) in category theory.

:::

By working in the [dual category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_category)—that is, by reversing the arrows—an inverse limit becomes a [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) or *inductive limit*, and a *limit* becomes a [colimit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

### Algebraic objects {#Algebraic_objects}

We start with the definition of an **inverse system** (or projective system) of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) and [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396). Let $(I, \leq)$ be a [directed](wikipedia://en/9165) [poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) (not all authors require *I* to be directed). Let $(A_i)_{i\in I}$ be a [family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of groups and suppose we have a family of homomorphisms $f_{ij}: A_j \to A_i$ for all $i \leq j$ (note the order) with the following properties:

1. $f_{ii}$ is the identity on $A_i$,
1. $f_{ik} = f_{ij} \circ f_{jk}$ for all $i \leq j \leq k.$

Then the pair $((A_i)_{i\in I}, (f_{ij})_{i\leq j\in I})$ is called an *inverse system* of groups and morphisms over $I$, and the morphisms $f_{ij}$ are called the transition morphisms of the system.

The **inverse limit** of the inverse system $((A_i)_{i\in I}, (f_{ij})_{i\leq j\in I})$ is the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of the $A_i$'s defined as

$$A = \varprojlim_{i\in I}{A_i} = \left\{\;\left.\vec a \in \prod_{i\in I}A_i \;\right|\; a_i = f_{ij}(a_j) \text{ for all } i \leq j \text{ in } I\;\right\}.$$

The definition above of an inverse system implies, that $A$ is closed under pointwise multiplication, and therefore a group, since

$$f_{ij}(a_j\cdot b_j)=f_{ij}(a_j)\cdot f_{ij}(b_j)=a_i\cdot b_i$$

for all $i<j$ and every $\vec a, \vec b\in A$

The inverse limit $A$ comes equipped with *natural projections* $\pi_{i}: A \to A_{i}$ which pick out the $i$th component of the direct product for each $i$ in $I$. The inverse limit and the natural projections satisfy a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) described in the next section.

This same construction may be carried out if the $A_i$'s are [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799), [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) (over a fixed ring), [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788) (over a fixed ring), etc., and the [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) are morphisms in the corresponding [category](wikipedia://en/5869). The inverse limit will also belong to that category.[^o5] More generally, this construction applies when the $A_i$ belong to a [variety](wikipedia://en/1021753) in the sense of [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), that is, a type of algebraic structures, whose axioms are unconditional ([fields](wikipedia://en/10603) do not form an algebra, since zero does not have a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940)).

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="General definition"}

### General definition {#General_definition}

The inverse limit can be defined abstractly in an arbitrary [category](wikipedia://en/53759) by means of a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245). Let $(X_i, f_{ij})$ be an inverse system of objects and [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) in a category *C* (same definition as above). The **inverse limit** of this system is an object *X* in *C* together with morphisms $\pi$<sub>*i*</sub>: *X* → *X*<sub>*i*</sub> (called *projections*) satisfying $\pi$<sub>*i*</sub> = $f_{ij}$ ∘ $\pi$<sub>*j*</sub> for all *i* ≤ *j*. The pair (*X*, $\pi$<sub>*i*</sub>) must be universal in the sense that for any other such pair (*Y*, ψ<sub>*i*</sub>) there exists a unique morphism *u*: *Y* → *X* such that the diagram

*Image: InverseLimit-01.svg.*

[commutes](wikipedia://en/138484) for all *i* ≤ *j*. The inverse limit is often denoted

$$X = \varprojlim X_i$$

with the inverse system $(X_i, f_{ij})$ and the canonical projections $\pi_i$ being understood.

In some categories, the inverse limit of certain inverse systems does not exist. If it does, however, it is unique in a strong sense: given any two inverse limits *X* and *X**of an inverse system, there exists a*****unique*[isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)*X*′ →*X*commuting with the projection maps.***

Inverse systems and inverse limits in a category *C* admit an alternative description in terms of [functors](wikipedia://en/10987). Any partially ordered set *I* can be considered as a [small category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category) where the morphisms consist of arrows *i* → *j* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *i* ≤ *j*. An inverse system is then just a [contravariant functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_functor) *I* → *C*. Let $C^{I^\mathrm{op}}$ be the category of these functors (with [natural transformations](wikipedia://en/45022) as morphisms). An object *X* of *C* can be considered a trivial inverse system, where all objects are equal to *X* and all arrow are the identity of *X*. This defines a "trivial functor" from *C* to $C^{I^\mathrm{op}}.$ The inverse limit, if it exists, is defined as a [right adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_adjoint) of this trivial functor.

## Examples {#Examples}

- The ring of [*p*-adic integers](wikipedia://en/51423) is the inverse limit of the rings $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ (see [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087)) with the index set being the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) with the usual order, and the morphisms being "take remainder". That is, one considers sequences of integers $(n_1, n_2, \dots)$ such that each element of the sequence "projects" down to the previous ones, namely, that $n_i\equiv n_j \mbox{ mod } p^{i}$ whenever $i<j.$ The natural topology on the *p*-adic integers is the one implied here, namely the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) with [cylinder sets](wikipedia://en/2613658) as the open sets.
- The [*p*-adic solenoid](wikipedia://en/2217712) is the inverse limit of the topological groups $\mathbb{R}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ with the index set being the natural numbers with the usual order, and the morphisms being "take remainder". That is, one considers sequences of real numbers $(x_1, x_2, \dots)$ such that each element of the sequence "projects" down to the previous ones, namely, that $x_i\equiv x_j \mbox{ mod } p^{i}$ whenever $i<j.$ Its elements are exactly of form $n + r$, where $n$ is a *p*-adic integer, and $r\in [0, 1)$ is the "remainder".
- The ring $\textstyle R[[t]]$ of [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) over a commutative ring *R* can be thought of as the inverse limit of the rings $\textstyle R[t]/t^nR[t]$, indexed by the natural numbers as usually ordered, with the morphisms from $\textstyle R[t]/t^{n+j}R[t]$ to $\textstyle R[t]/t^nR[t]$ given by the natural projection.
- [Pro-finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro-finite_group) are defined as inverse limits of (discrete) finite groups.
- Let the index set *I* of an inverse system (*X*<sub>*i*</sub>, $f_{ij}$) have a [greatest element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_element) *m*. Then the natural projection $\pi$<sub>*m*</sub>: *X* → *X*<sub>*m*</sub> is an isomorphism.
- In the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), every inverse system has an inverse limit, which can be constructed in an elementary manner as a subset of the product of the sets forming the inverse system. The inverse limit of any inverse system of non-empty finite sets is non-empty. This is a generalization of [Kőnig's lemma](wikipedia://en/153788) in graph theory and may be proved with [Tychonoff's theorem](wikipedia://en/82739), viewing the finite sets as compact discrete spaces, and then applying the [finite intersection property](wikipedia://en/365026) characterization of compactness.
- In the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051), every inverse system has an inverse limit. It is constructed by placing the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123) (with respect to the projection maps into the constituent spaces of the inverse system) on the underlying set-theoretic inverse limit. This is known as the **limit topology**.
  - The set of infinite [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) is the inverse limit of the set of finite strings, and is thus endowed with the limit topology. As the original spaces are [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology), the limit space is [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected). This is one way of realizing the [*p*-adic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic) and the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) (as infinite strings).

## Derived functors of the inverse limit {#Derived_functors_of_the_inverse_limit}

For an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) *C*, the inverse limit functor

$$\varprojlim:C^I\rightarrow C$$

is [left exact](wikipedia://en/453945). If *I* is ordered (not simply partially ordered) and [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable), and *C* is the category **Ab** of abelian groups, the Mittag-Leffler condition is a condition on the transition morphisms *f*<sub>*ij*</sub> that ensures the exactness of $\varprojlim$. Specifically, [Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501) constructed a functor

$$\varprojlim{}^1:\operatorname{Ab}^I\rightarrow\operatorname{Ab}$$

(pronounced "lim one") such that if (*A*<sub>*i*</sub>, *f*<sub>*ij*</sub>), (*B*<sub>*i*</sub>, *g*<sub>*ij*</sub>), and (*C*<sub>*i*</sub>, *h*<sub>*ij*</sub>) are three inverse systems of abelian groups, and

$$0\rightarrow A_i\rightarrow B_i\rightarrow C_i\rightarrow0$$

is a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of inverse systems, then

$$0\rightarrow\varprojlim A_i\rightarrow\varprojlim B_i\rightarrow\varprojlim C_i\rightarrow\varprojlim{}^1A_i$$

is an exact sequence in **Ab**.

### Mittag-Leffler condition {#Mittag-Leffler_condition}

If the ranges of the morphisms of an inverse system of abelian groups (*A*<sub>*i*</sub>, *f*<sub>*ij*</sub>) are *stationary*, that is, for every *k* there exists *j* ≥ *k* such that for all *i* ≥ *j* :$f_{kj}(A_j)=f_{ki}(A_i)$ one says that the system satisfies the **Mittag-Leffler condition**.

The name "Mittag-Leffler" for this condition was given by Bourbaki in their chapter on uniform structures for a similar result about inverse limits of complete Hausdorff uniform spaces. Mittag-Leffler used a similar argument in the proof of [Mittag-Leffler's theorem](wikipedia://en/2466640).

The following situations are examples where the Mittag-Leffler condition is satisfied:

- a system in which the morphisms *f*<sub>*ij*</sub> are surjective
- a system of finite-dimensional [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) or finite abelian groups or modules of finite [length](wikipedia://en/816070) or [Artinian modules](wikipedia://en/542336).

An example where $\varprojlim{}^1$ is non-zero is obtained by taking *I* to be the non-negative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), letting *A*<sub>*i*</sub> = *p*<sup>*i*</sup>**Z**, *B*<sub>*i*</sub> = **Z**, and *C*<sub>*i*</sub> = *B*<sub>*i*</sub> / *A*<sub>*i*</sub> = **Z**/*p*<sup>*i*</sup>**Z**. Then

$$\varprojlim{}^1A_i=\mathbf{Z}_p/\mathbf{Z}$$

where **Z**<sub>*p*</sub> denotes the [p-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integers).

### Further results {#Further_results}

More generally, if *C* is an arbitrary abelian category that has [enough injectives](wikipedia://en/473779#Enough_injectives), then so does *C*<sup>*I*</sup>, and the right [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) of the inverse limit functor can thus be defined. The *n*th right derived functor is denoted

$$R^n\varprojlim:C^I\rightarrow C.$$

In the case where *C* satisfies [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck)'s axiom [(AB4\*)](wikipedia://en/45063#Grothendieck's_axioms), [Jan-Erik Roos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan-Erik_Roos) generalized the functor lim<sup>1</sup> on **Ab**<sup>*I*</sup> to series of functors lim<sup>n</sup> such that

$$\varprojlim{}^n\cong R^n\varprojlim.$$

It was thought for almost 40 years that Roos had proved (in Sur les foncteurs dérivés de lim. Applications.) that lim<sup>1</sup> *A*<sub>*i*</sub> = 0 for (*A*<sub>*i*</sub>, *f*<sub>*ij*</sub>) an inverse system with surjective transition morphisms and *I* the set of non-negative integers (such inverse systems are often called "[Mittag-Leffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mittag-Leffler) sequences"). However, in 2002, [Amnon Neeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amnon_Neeman) and [Pierre Deligne](wikipedia://en/394544) constructed an example of such a system in a category satisfying (AB4) (in addition to (AB4\*)) with lim<sup>1</sup> *A*<sub>*i*</sub> ≠ 0. Roos has since shown (in "Derived functors of inverse limits revisited") that his result is correct if *C* has a set of generators (in addition to satisfying (AB3) and (AB4\*)).

[Barry Mitchell](wikipedia://en/80837588) has shown (in "The cohomological dimension of a directed set") that if *I* has [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) $\aleph_d$ (the *d*th [infinite cardinal](wikipedia://en/293961)), then *R*<sup>*n*</sup>lim is zero for all *n* ≥ *d* + 2. This applies to the *I*-indexed diagrams in the category of *R*-modules, with *R* a commutative ring; it is not necessarily true in an arbitrary abelian category (see Roos' "Derived functors of inverse limits revisited" for examples of abelian categories in which lim<sup>*n*</sup>, on diagrams indexed by a countable set, is nonzero for *n* > 1).

:::

## Related concepts and generalizations {#Related_concepts_and_generalizations}

The [categorical dual](wikipedia://en/310885) of an inverse limit is a [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) (or inductive limit). More general concepts are the [limits and colimits](wikipedia://en/39248) of category theory. The terminology is somewhat confusing: inverse limits are a class of limits, while direct limits are a class of colimits.
