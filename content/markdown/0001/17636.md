# Law of noncontradiction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Law of noncontradiction"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), the **law of noncontradiction** (**LNC**; also known as the **law of contradiction**, **principle of non-contradiction** (**PNC**), or the **principle of contradiction**) states that for any given proposition, the proposition and its negation cannot both be simultaneously true, e.g., the proposition "the house is white" and its negation "the house is not white" are [mutually exclusive](wikipedia://en/312648).

:::

To express the fact that the law is tenseless and to avoid [equivocation](wikipedia://en/17690), sometimes the law is amended to say "contradictory propositions cannot both be true at the same time and in the same sense".

[Formally](wikipedia://en/3331706), the law is expressed as the [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) $\neg(p \land \neg p).$ One reason to have this law is the [principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394), which states that anything follows from a contradiction, resulting in [trivialism](wikipedia://en/10886610). The law is employed in a *[reductio ad absurdum](wikipedia://en/22456146)* proof. [Paraconsistent logics](wikipedia://en/421085) are those logics which deny explosion.

## History {#History}

Early in philosophy, it is hard to distinguish between different conceptions of the law of non-contradiction. One can interpret a logical law ontologically, e. g. to say nothing in reality is contradictory; one can interpret it psychologically, to say one cannot *believe* in a contradiction, and one can interpret it more strictly logically, that contradictory propositions cannot be true.[^o3]

### East {#East}

#### Indian philosophy {#Indian_philosophy}

The [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) [Tripitaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripitaka) attributes to [Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahavira), who lived in the 6th century BCE, the implicit formulation of the law of noncontradiction, See how upright, honest and sincere Citta, the householder, is'; and, a little later, he also says: 'See how Citta, the householder, is not upright, honest or sincere.' To this, Citta replies: 'if your former statement is true, your latter statement is false and if your latter statement is true, your former statement is false. Early explicit formulations of the law of noncontradiction were [ontic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology), with later 2nd century Buddhist philosopher [Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna) stating "when something is a single thing, it cannot be both existent and non-existent", similar to Aristotle's own ontic formulation that "a thing cannot at the same time be and not be".[^o6]

It is found in ancient [Indian logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_logic) as a meta-rule in the *[Shrauta Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrauta_Sutras)*, the grammar of [Pāṇini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pāṇini),[^o7] and the *[Brahma Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahma_Sutras)* attributed to [Vyasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyasa). It was later elaborated on by medieval commentators such as [Madhvacharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhvacharya).[^o8]

### West {#West}

#### Pre-Socratics {#Pre-Socratics}

According to both [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) and [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308),[^o11] [Heraclitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus) was *said* to have denied the law of non-contradiction, stating "[one cannot step into the same river twice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_cannot_step_into_the_same_river_twice)". So was the sophist [Protagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protagoras) whose most famous saying is: "[Man is the measure of all things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_is_the_measure_of_all_things): of things which are, that they are, and of things which are not, that they are not".[^o12]

Parmenides seemed to deploy an ontological version of the law of non-contradiction in saying what is not cannot be, and thereby to deny the void, change, and motion.[^o13]

#### Socrates {#Socrates}

The earlier dialogues of [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) (424–348 BCE), relating the discourses of [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates), raised the use of reductio arguments to a formal dialectical method ([elenchus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_elenchus)), also called the [Socratic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_method).[^o15]

#### Plato {#Plato}

[Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s version of the law of non-contradiction states that "The same thing clearly cannot act or be acted upon in the same part or in relation to the same thing at the same time, in contrary ways" (*[The Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_%28dialogue%29)* (436b)). In this, Plato carefully phrases three [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/928) restrictions on *action* or reaction: in the same part, in the same relation, at the same time. The effect is to momentarily create a frozen, timeless [state](wikipedia://en/25718122), somewhat like figures frozen in action on the frieze of the Parthenon.[^o17]

This way, he accomplishes two essential goals for his philosophy. First, he logically separates the Platonic world of constant change[^o18] from the formally knowable world of momentarily fixed physical objects.[^o19][^o20] Second, he provides the conditions for the [dialectic](wikipedia://en/52813) method to be used in finding definitions, as for example in the *[Sophist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophist_%28dialogue%29)*. So Plato's law of non-contradiction is the empirically derived necessary starting point for all else he has to say.[^o21]

In contrast, Aristotle reverses Plato's order of derivation. Rather than starting with *experience*, Aristotle begins *a priori* with the law of non-contradiction as the fundamental axiom of an analytic philosophical system.[^o22] This axiom then necessitates the fixed, realist model. Aristotle starts with much stronger logical foundations than Plato's single decisive action in response to conflicting demands from the [three parts of the soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato's_theory_of_soul).

#### Aristotle {#Aristotle}

Aristotle calls the law of non-contradiction "[the most certain of all principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_principle)" in *[Metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics_%28Aristotle%29)* Book IV.[^o24] Ever since, the law has been high orthodoxy.[^o25]

Aristotle gives three different versions.[^o26]

- [Ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological): "It is impossible that the same thing belong and not belong to the same thing at the same time and in the same respect." (1005b19-20)
- [Psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological): "No one can believe that the same thing can (at the same time) be and not be." (1005b23–24)[^o27]
- [Logical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical) (aka the medieval *Lex Contradictoriarum*):[^o28] "The most certain of all basic principles is that contradictory [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094) are not true simultaneously." (1011b13-14)

Aristotle attempts several proofs of this law. He first argues that every expression has a single meaning (otherwise we could not communicate with one another). This rules out the possibility that by "to be a man", "not to be a man" is meant. But "man" means "two-footed animal" (for example), and so if anything is a man, it is necessary (by virtue of the meaning of "man") that it must be a two-footed animal, and so it is impossible at the same time for it *not* to be a two-footed animal. Thus "it is not possible to say truly at the same time that the same thing is and is not a man" (*Metaphysics* 1006b 35). Another argument is that anyone who believes something cannot believe its contradiction (1008b).

However, note for Aristotle this seems a principle of *metaphysics* rather than one of logic. Aristotle notes his logic would still work even if the law of non contradiction were false.[^o29][^o30] This seems to mean Aristotle's logic denies explosion and so is paraconsistent.

#### Thomas Aquinas {#Thomas_Aquinas}

[Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas) argued that the principle of non-contradiction is essential to the reasoning of human beings ("One cannot reasonably hold two mutually exclusive beliefs at the same time") (*Met.* IV, lect. 6). He argued that human reasoning without the principle of non-contradiction is utterly impossible because reason itself can't function with two contradictory ideas. Aquinas argued that this is the same both for moral arguments as well as theological arguments and even machinery ("the parts must work together, the machine can't work if two parts are incompatible").[^o32][^o33]

[Duns Scotus](wikipedia://en/34293405) (*Quaest. sup. Met.* IV, Q. 3) and writer [Francisco Suárez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Suárez) (*Disp. Met.* III, § 3) also follow the Aristotelian view about the law of non-contradiction.

#### Leibniz and Kant {#Leibniz_and_Kant}

[Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) and [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) both used the law of non-contradiction to define the difference between [analytic and synthetic](wikipedia://en/6395956) propositions.[^o35] For Leibniz, analytic statements follow from the law of non-contradiction, and synthetic ones from the [principle of sufficient reason](wikipedia://en/1137736).

#### Russell {#Russell}

The principle was stated as a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) by [Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) and [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) in *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* as:[^o37]

$$\mathbf{*3\cdot24}. \ \ \vdash. \thicksim(p.\thicksim p)$$

#### Dialetheism {#Dialetheism}

[Graham Priest](wikipedia://en/3659382) advocates the view that *under some conditions*, some statements can be both true and false simultaneously, or may be true and false at different times. [Dialetheism](wikipedia://en/591359) arises from formal logical [paradoxes](wikipedia://en/24390), such as the [liar's paradox](wikipedia://en/17967) and [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095), even though it is not the only solution to them.[^o39][^o40][^o41] Prior to Priest, [Nicholas of Cusa](wikipedia://en/282522) and [Hegel](wikipedia://en/12598) were both dialetheists.

## Alleged impossibility of its proof or denial {#Alleged_impossibility_of_its_proof_or_denial}

The law of non-contradiction is alleged to be neither verifiable nor falsifiable, on the ground that any proof or disproof must use the law itself prior to reaching the conclusion. In other words, in order to verify or falsify such a principle of logic, one must resort to using it in the verification, an act that is argued to be [self-defeating](wikipedia://en/9287936) (rather like trying to bite one's own teeth).[^o43] Aristotle said trying to prove the law of non-contradiction shows "want of education".[^o24]

Since the early 20th century, certain logicians have proposed logics that deny the law. Logics known as "[paraconsistent](wikipedia://en/421085)" are inconsistency-tolerant logics in that there, from P together with ¬P, it does not imply that any proposition follows. Paraconsistent logics deny [explosion](wikipedia://en/591394). Nevertheless, not all paraconsistent logics deny the law of non-contradiction and some such logics even prove it.[^o44][^o45]

Some, such as [David Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lewis_%28philosopher%29), have objected to paraconsistent logic on the ground that it is simply impossible for a statement and its negation to be jointly true.[^o46] A related objection is that "negation" in paraconsistent logic is not really *[negation](wikipedia://en/161019)*; it is merely a [subcontrary](wikipedia://en/246976)-forming operator.[^o47][^o48] Those who (like the dialetheists) claim that the Law of Non-Contradiction can be violated are in fact using a different definition of negation, and therefore talking about something else other than the Law of Non-Contradiction which is based on a particular definition of negation and therefore cannot be violated.[^o49]

## In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

The *[Fargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fargo_%28TV_series%29)* episode "[The Law of Non-Contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Law_of_Non-Contradiction)", which takes its name from the law, was noted for its several elements relating to the law of non-contradiction, as the episode's main character faces several paradoxes. For example, she is still the acting [chief of police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_of_police) while having been demoted from the position, and tries to investigate a man that both was and was not named Ennis Stussy, and who both was and was not her stepfather.[^o51]
