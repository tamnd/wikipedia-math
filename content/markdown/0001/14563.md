# Integer

*Image: NumberLineIntegers.svg.* The integers arranged on a [number line](wikipedia://en/287188)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integer"}

An **integer** is the [number](wikipedia://en/21690) zero ([0](wikipedia://en/34513)), a positive [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) (1, 2, 3, ...), or the negation of a positive natural number ([−1](wikipedia://en/449568), −2, −3, ...).[^o2] The negations or [additive inverses](wikipedia://en/228312) of the positive natural numbers are referred to as **negative integers**.[^o3] The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all integers is often denoted by the [boldface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boldface) $\mathbf{Z}$ or [blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276) $\mathbb{Z}$.[^o14][^o4]

:::

The set of natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\mathbb{Z}$, which in turn is a subset of the set of all [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\mathbb{Q}$, itself a subset of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$. Like the set of natural numbers, the set of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ is [countably infinite](wikipedia://en/6026). An integer may be regarded as a real number that can be written without a [fractional component](wikipedia://en/1704824). For example, 21, 4, 0, and −2048 are integers, while 9.75, $5\tfrac{1}{2}$, 5/4, and the [square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682) are not.[^o5]

The integers form the smallest [group](wikipedia://en/19447) and the smallest [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) containing the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). In [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), integers are sometimes called **rational integers** to distinguish them from the more general [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981). In fact, (rational) integers are algebraic integers that are also [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024).

## History {#History}

The word integer comes from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *integer* meaning "whole" or (literally) "untouched", from *in* ("not") plus *tangere* ("to touch"). "Entire" derives from the same origin via the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) word *entier*, which means both *entire* and *integer*.[^o7] Historically the term was used for a [number](wikipedia://en/21690) that was a multiple of 1,[^o8][^o9] or to the whole part of a [mixed number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_number).[^o10][^o11] Only positive integers were considered, making the term synonymous with the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). The definition of integer expanded over time to include [negative numbers](wikipedia://en/154616) as their usefulness was recognized.[^o12] For example [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in his 1765 *[Elements of Algebra](wikipedia://en/8181025)* defined integers to include both positive and negative numbers.[^o13]

The phrase *the set of the integers* was not used before the end of the 19th century, when [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) introduced the concept of [infinite sets](wikipedia://en/142679) and [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553). The use of the letter Z to denote the set of integers comes from the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) word *Zahlen* ("numbers")[^o14][^o15] and has been attributed to [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302).[^o16] The earliest known use of the notation in a textbook occurs in [Algèbre](wikipedia://en/38333812) written by the collective [Nicolas Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394), dating to 1947.[^o14][^o17] The notation was not adopted immediately. For example, another textbook used the letter J,[^o18] and a 1960 paper used Z to denote the non-negative integers.[^o19] But by 1961, Z was generally used by modern algebra texts to denote the positive and negative integers.[^o20]

The symbol $\mathbb{Z}$ is often annotated to denote various sets, with varying usage amongst different authors: $\mathbb{Z}^+$, $\mathbb{Z}_+$, or $\mathbb{Z}^>$ for the positive integers, $\mathbb{Z}^{0+}$ or $\mathbb{Z}^\geq$ for non-negative integers, and $\mathbb{Z}^\neq$ for non-zero integers. Some authors use $\mathbb{Z}^*$ for non-zero integers, while others use it for non-negative integers, or for {−1,1} (the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of $\mathbb{Z}$). Additionally, $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is used to denote either the set of [integers modulo $p$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers_modulo_n) (i.e., the set of [congruence classes](wikipedia://en/48167) of integers), or the set of [$p$-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer).[^o21][^o22]

The *whole numbers* were synonymous with the integers up until the early 1950s.[^o23][^o24][^o25] In the late 1950s, as part of the [New Math](wikipedia://en/404988) movement,[^o26] American elementary school teachers began teaching that *whole numbers* referred to the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), excluding negative numbers, while *integer* included the negative numbers.[^o27][^o28] The *whole numbers* remain ambiguous to the present day.[^o29]

## Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

*Image: Number-line.svg.* Integers can be thought of as discrete, equally spaced points on an infinitely long [number line](wikipedia://en/287188). In the above, non-[negative](wikipedia://en/7951270#Terminology_for_signs) integers are shown in blue and negative integers in red.

Like the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers), $\mathbb{Z}$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under the [operations](wikipedia://en/3948) of addition and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), that is, the sum and product of any two integers is an integer. However, with the inclusion of 0 and the negation of the natural numbers, $\mathbb{Z}$, unlike the natural numbers, is also closed under [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141).[^o32]

The integers form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) which is the most basic one, in the following sense: for any ring, there is a unique [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from the integers into this ring. This [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245), namely to be an [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object) in the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181), characterizes the ring $\mathbb{Z}$. This unique homomorphism is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) if and only if the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of the ring is zero. It follows that every ring of characteristic zero contains a subring isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$, which is its smallest subring.

$\mathbb{Z}$ is not closed under [division](wikipedia://en/53696), since the quotient of two integers (e.g., 1 divided by 2) need not be an integer. Although the natural numbers are closed under [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491), the integers are not (since the result can be a fraction when the exponent is negative).

The following table lists some of the basic properties of addition and multiplication for any integers $a$, $b$, and $c$:

*Properties of addition and multiplication on integers*

::: {#o33 .table cue=table name="Properties of addition and multiplication on integers"}

|  | Addition | Multiplication |
|---|---|---|
| [Closure](wikipedia://en/293802): | $a + b$ is an integer | $a \times b$ is an integer |
| [Associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity): | $a + (b + c) = (a + b) + c$ | $a \times (b \times c) = (a \times b) \times c$ |
| [Commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity): | $a + b = b + a$ | $a \times b = b \times a$ |
| Existence of an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962): | $a + 0 = a$ | $a \times 1 = a$ |
| Existence of [inverse elements](wikipedia://en/45199): | $a + (-a) = 0$ | The only invertible integers (called [units](wikipedia://en/289666)) are −1 and 1. |
| [Distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity): | $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$ and $(a + b) \times c = (a \times c) + (b \times c)$ |  |
| No [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441): |  | If $a \times b = 0$, then $a = 0$ or $b = 0$ (or both) |

:::

The first five properties listed above for addition say that $\mathbb{Z}$, under addition, is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974). It is also a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327), since every non-zero integer can be written as a finite sum 1 + 1 + ... + 1 or (−1) + (−1) + ... + (−1). In fact, $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition is the *only* infinite cyclic group—in the sense that any infinite cyclic group is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to $\mathbb{Z}$.

The first four properties listed above for multiplication say that $\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication is a [commutative monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_monoid). However, not every integer has a multiplicative inverse (as is the case of the number 2), which means that $\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication is not a group.

All the rules from the above property table (except for the last), when taken together, say that $\mathbb{Z}$ together with addition and multiplication is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) with [unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity). It is the prototype of all objects of such [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364). Only those [equalities](wikipedia://en/90446) of [expressions](wikipedia://en/4283745) are true in $\mathbb{Z}$ [for all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_all) values of variables, which are true in any unital commutative ring. Certain non-zero integers map to [zero](wikipedia://en/4178225) in certain rings.

The lack of [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) in the integers (last property in the table) means that the commutative ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462).

The lack of multiplicative inverses, which is equivalent to the fact that $\mathbb{Z}$ is not closed under division, means that $\mathbb{Z}$ is *not* a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). The smallest field containing the integers as a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) is the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). The process of constructing the rationals from the integers can be mimicked to form the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of any integral domain. And back, starting from an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) (an extension of rational numbers), its [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) can be extracted, which includes $\mathbb{Z}$ as its [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145).

Although ordinary division is not defined on $\mathbb{Z}$, the division "with remainder" is defined on them. It is called [Euclidean division](wikipedia://en/679987), and possesses the following important property: given two integers $a$ and $b$ with $b \ne 0$, there exist unique integers $q$ and $r$ such that $a = q \times b + r$ and $0 \le r < |b|$, where $|b|$ denotes the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of $b$. The integer $q$ is called the *quotient* and $r$ is called the *[remainder](wikipedia://en/502897)* of the division of $a$ by $b$. The [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) for computing [greatest common divisors](wikipedia://en/12354) works by a sequence of Euclidean divisions.

The above says that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [Euclidean domain](wikipedia://en/10376). This implies that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039), and any positive integer can be written as the products of [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) in an [essentially unique](wikipedia://en/7918341) way.[^o41] This is the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556).

:::

## Order-theoretic properties {#Order-theoretic_properties}

$\mathbb{Z}$ is a [totally ordered set](wikipedia://en/30330) without [upper or lower bound](wikipedia://en/42693). The ordering of $\mathbb{Z}$ is given by:

$$\cdots < -3 < -2 < -1 < 0 < 1 < 2 < 3 < \cdots.$$

An integer is *positive* if it is greater than [zero](wikipedia://en/34513), and *negative* if it is less than zero. Zero is defined as neither negative nor positive.

The ordering of integers is compatible with the algebraic operations in the following way:

1. If $a < b$ and $c < d$, then $a + c < b + d$
1. If $a < b$ and $0 < c$, then $ac < bc$

Thus it follows that $\mathbb{Z}$ together with the above ordering is an [ordered ring](wikipedia://en/525697).

The integers are the only nontrivial [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered) [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) whose positive elements are [well-ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered).[^o43] This is equivalent to the statement that any [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) [valuation ring](wikipedia://en/966856) is either a [field](wikipedia://en/10603)—or a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633).

## Construction {#Construction}

### Traditional development {#Traditional_development}

In elementary school teaching, integers are often intuitively defined as the union of the (positive) natural numbers, [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero), and the negations of the natural numbers. This can be formalized as follows.[^o47] First construct the set of natural numbers according to the [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005), call this $P$. Then construct a set $P^-$ which is [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) from $P$ and in one-to-one correspondence with $P$ via a function $\psi$. For example, take $P^-$ to be the [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) $(1,n)$ with the mapping $\psi = n \mapsto (1,n)$. Finally let 0 be some object not in $P$ or $P^-$, for example the ordered pair (0,0). Then the integers are defined to be the union $P \cup P^- \cup \{0\}$.

The traditional arithmetic operations can then be defined on the integers in a [piecewise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise) fashion, for each of positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero. For example [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) is defined as follows:

$$-x = \begin{cases} \psi(x), & \text{if } x \in P \\ \psi^{-1}(x), & \text{if } x \in P^- \\ 0, & \text{if } x = 0 \end{cases}$$

The traditional style of definition leads to many different cases (each arithmetic operation needs to be defined on each combination of types of integer) and makes it tedious to prove that integers obey the various laws of arithmetic.[^o48]

### Equivalence classes of ordered pairs {#Equivalence_classes_of_ordered_pairs}

*Image: Relative numbers representation.svg.* Red points represent ordered pairs of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). Linked red points are equivalence classes representing the blue integers at the end of the line.

In modern set-theoretic mathematics, a more abstract construction[^o50][^o51] allowing one to define arithmetical operations without any case distinction is often used instead.[^o52] The integers can thus be formally constructed as the [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) $(a,b)$.[^o53]

The intuition is that $(a,b)$ stands for the result of subtracting $b$ from $a$.[^o53] To confirm our expectation that 1 − 2 and 4 − 5 denote the same number, we define an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\sim$ on these pairs with the following rule:

$$(a,b)\sim(c,d)$$

precisely when

$$a+d=b+c.$$

Addition and multiplication of integers can be defined in terms of the equivalent operations on the natural numbers;[^o53] by using $[(a,b)]$ to denote the equivalence class having $(a,b)$ as a member, one has:

$$[(a,b)]+[(c,d)]:=[(a+c,b+d)].$$

$$[(a,b)]\cdot[(c,d)]:=[(ac+bd,ad+bc)].$$

The negation (or additive inverse) of an integer is obtained by reversing the order of the pair:

$$-[(a,b)]:=[(b,a)].$$

Hence subtraction can be defined as the addition of the additive inverse:

$$[(a,b)]-[(c,d)]:=[(a+d,b+c)].$$

The standard ordering on the integers is given by:

$[(a,b)]<[(c,d)]$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $a+d<b+c$.

It is easily verified that these definitions are independent of the choice of representatives of the equivalence classes.

Every equivalence class has a unique member that is of the form $(n,0)$ or $(0,n)$ (or both at once). The natural number $n$ is identified with the class $[(n,0)]$ (i.e., the natural numbers are [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) into the integers by map sending $n$ to $[(n,0)]$), and the class $[(0,n)]$ is denoted $-n$ (this covers all remaining classes, and gives the class $[(0,0)]$ a second time since −0 = 0.

Thus, $[(a,b)]$ is denoted by

$$\begin{cases} a-b,&\mbox{if }a\ge b\\ -(b-a),&\mbox{if }a<b \end{cases}$$

If the natural numbers are identified with the corresponding integers (using the embedding mentioned above), this convention creates no ambiguity.

This notation recovers the familiar [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of the integers as $\{..., -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, ...\}$.

Some examples are:

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} 0 &= [(0,0)] &&= [(1,1)] &&= \ \cdots \ &&= [(k,k)],\\ 1 &= [(1,0)] &&= [(2,1)] &&= \ \cdots \ &&= [(k+1,k)],\\ -1 &= [(0,1)] &&= [(1,2)] &&= \ \cdots \ &&= [(k,k+1)],\\ 2 &= [(2,0)] &&= [(3,1)] &&= \ \cdots \ &&= [(k+2,k)],\\ -2 &= [(0,2)] &&= [(1,3)] &&= \ \cdots \ &&= [(k,k+2)]. \end{alignedat}$$

### Other approaches {#Other_approaches}

In theoretical computer science, other approaches for the construction of integers are used by [automated theorem provers](wikipedia://en/2546) and [term rewrite engines](wikipedia://en/415847). Integers are represented as [algebraic terms](wikipedia://en/2216678) built using a few basic operations (e.g., **zero**, **succ**, **pred**) and using [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), which are assumed to be already constructed (using the [Peano approach](wikipedia://en/25005)).

There exist at least ten such constructions of signed integers.[^o55] These constructions differ in several ways: the number of basic operations used for the construction, the number (usually, between 0 and 2), and the types of arguments accepted by these operations; the presence or absence of natural numbers as arguments of some of these operations, and the fact that these operations are free constructors or not, i.e., that the same integer can be represented using only one or many algebraic terms.

The technique for the construction of integers presented in the previous section corresponds to the particular case where there is a single basic operation **pair**$(x,y)$ that takes as arguments two natural numbers $x$ and $y$, and returns an integer (equal to $x-y$). This operation is not free since the integer 0 can be written **pair**(0,0), or **pair**(1,1), or **pair**(2,2), etc.. This technique of construction is used by the [proof assistant](wikipedia://en/1258607) [Isabelle](wikipedia://en/161886); however, many other tools use alternative construction techniques, notable those based upon free constructors, which are simpler and can be implemented more efficiently in computers.

:::

## Computer science {#Computer_science}

An integer is often a primitive [data type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_type) in [computer languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_language). However, integer data types can only represent a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of all integers, since practical computers are of finite capacity. Also, in the common [two's complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two's_complement) representation, the inherent definition of [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270) distinguishes between "negative" and "non-negative" rather than "negative, positive, and 0". (It is, however, certainly possible for a computer to determine whether an integer value is truly positive.) Fixed length integer approximation data types (or subsets) are denoted *int* or Integer in several programming languages (such as [Algol68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algol68), [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28computer_language%29), [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29), [Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_Pascal), etc.).

Variable-length representations of integers, such as [bignums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bignum), can store any integer that fits in the computer's memory. Other integer data types are implemented with a fixed size, usually a number of bits which is a power of 2 (4, 8, 16, etc.) or a memorable number of decimal digits (e.g., 9 or 10).

## Cardinality {#Cardinality}

The set of integers is [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite), meaning it is possible to pair each integer with a unique natural number. An example of such a pairing is

$(0,\,1), (1,\,2), (-1,\,3), (2,\,4), (-2,\,5),$ $(3,\,6), .\,.\,.\,,(1\,-\,k,\,2k\,-\,1), (k,\,2k\,), .\,.\,.$

More technically, the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of $\mathbb{Z}$ is said to equal $\aleph_{0}$ ([aleph-null](wikipedia://en/293961)). The pairing between elements of $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{N}$ is called a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942).
