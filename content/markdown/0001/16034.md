# Johnson solid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Johnson solid"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **Johnson solid**, sometimes also known as a **Johnson–Zalgaller solid**,[^o2] is a [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) whose faces[^o3] are [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) and that is not a [uniform polyhedron](wikipedia://en/2537223). There are 92 such [solids](wikipedia://en/507960):

:::

- 48 composed of the primitive [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045), [cupolas](wikipedia://en/689743), and [rotundas](wikipedia://en/35582167) assembled in various ways together with [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007) and [antiprisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiprisms);
- 35 formed by modifying [uniform polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polyhedra), by augmenting, diminishing, or gyrating with primitives; and
- 9 which are not derived from "cut-and-paste" manipulations of uniform solids.

## Definition and background {#Definition_and_background}

A [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) is the convex hull of a finite set of points in 3-dimensional space, not all in a plane. Its boundary is a finite union of polygons, no two in the same plane; those polygons are called the *faces*. A *Johnson solid* is a convex polyhedron[^o3] whose faces are all regular polygons, but not a [uniform polyhedron](wikipedia://en/2537223); the last condition excludes the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905), [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847), [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007), and [antiprisms](wikipedia://en/1851).

The solids are named after [Norman Johnson](wikipedia://en/715527) and [Victor Zalgaller](wikipedia://en/4235763). (Johnson 1966) published a list of 92 such solids and assigned them their names and numbers. (Zalgaller 1969) proved Johnson's conjecture that there were none beyond these 92.

A convex polyhedron in which all faces are nearly regular, but some are not precisely regular, is known as a [near-miss Johnson solid](wikipedia://en/3881930).

## Naming and construction of solids {#Naming_and_construction_of_solids}

\- invalid, - [Platonic](wikipedia://en/23905), - [Archimedean](wikipedia://en/1847), - Gyrated sections.

The naming of Johnson solids follows a flexible and precise descriptive formula that allows many solids to be named in multiple different ways without compromising the accuracy of each name as a description. The names of the Johnson solids are described in the following sections.

### Pyramids, cupolas, rotundas {#Pyramids,_cupolas,_rotundas}

The first 48 Johnson solids are constructed from [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045), [cupolas](wikipedia://en/689743), or [rotundas](wikipedia://en/35582167), combined with [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007) or [antiprisms](wikipedia://en/1851). The following prefixes are attached to the word to indicate specific combinations of shapes:

- *Bi-* indicates that two copies of the solid are joined base-to-base.
  - For cupolas and rotundas, *ortho-* indicates that like faces meet.
  - For cupolas and rotundas, *gyro-* indicates that unlike faces meet.
- *Elongated* indicates a [prism](wikipedia://en/283007) is joined to the base of the solid, or between the bases.
- *Gyroelongated* indicates an [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851) is joined to the base of the solid, or between the bases.

Using this nomenclature, a [pentagonal bipyramid](wikipedia://en/728038) is a solid constructed by attaching two bases of pentagonal pyramids. [Triangular orthobicupola](wikipedia://en/1196250) is constructed by two triangular cupolas along their bases.

Excluded solids: - coplanar, - [Platonic](wikipedia://en/23905), - [Archimedean](wikipedia://en/1847).

### Modified uniform polyhedra {#Modified_uniform_polyhedra}

The next 35 Johnson solids are constructed by modifying uniform polyhedra such as [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007), [Platonic](wikipedia://en/23905), or [Archimedean](wikipedia://en/1847) solids by adding, subtracting, or rotating [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045) or [cupolas](wikipedia://en/689743). The following prefixes are attached to the word to indicate additions, subtractions, or rotations:

- *Augmented* indicates a [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045) or [cupola](wikipedia://en/689743) is added to one or more faces of the solid in question.
- *Diminished* indicates a pyramid or cupola is removed from one or more faces of the solid in question.
- *[Gyrate](wikipedia://en/1830939)* indicates a cupola mounted on or featured in the solid in question is rotated such that different edges match up.

The three operations—*augmentation*, *diminution*, and *gyration*—can be performed multiple times for certain large solids. *Bi-* & *Tri-* indicate a double and triple operation respectively. For example, a *bigyrate* solid has two rotated cupolas, and a *tridiminished* solid has three removed pyramids or cupolas. In certain large solids, a distinction is made between solids where altered faces are parallel and solids where altered faces are oblique. *Para-* indicates the former, that the solid in question has altered parallel faces, and *meta-* the latter, altered oblique faces. For example, a *parabiaugmented* solid has had two parallel faces augmented, and a *metabigyrate* solid has had two oblique cupolas gyrated.

### Non cut-and-paste {#Non_cut-and-paste}

*Image: Johnson lune and rotunda nets.svg.* Nets of a lune (left) and a partial rotunda (right)

The last 9 Johnson solids have names based on certain polygon complexes from which they are assembled. These names are defined by Johnson with the following nomenclature:

- A *lune* is a figure of two triangles attached to opposite sides of a square. Prefixes indicating a complex of lunes are:
  - *Spheno*- is a wedgelike complex of two adjacent lunes. *Dispheno-* indicates two such complexes.
  - *Hebespheno*- is a blunt complex of three adjacent lunes.
- Suffixes indicating a complex of triangles are:
  - \-*corona* is a crownlike complex of eight triangles.
  - \-*megacorona* is a larger crownlike complex of twelve triangles.
  - \-*cingulum* is a belt of twelve triangles.
- Suffix -*rotunda* indicates a complex of two or three pentagons with triangles between them, bearing a structural resemblance to the [pentagonal rotunda](wikipedia://en/1128491).

## Characteristics of solids {#Characteristics_of_solids}

Every polyhedron has its own [characteristics](wikipedia://en/23470#Characteristics), including [symmetry](wikipedia://en/44362809) and measurement. An object is said to have symmetry if there is a [transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_%28geometry%29) that maps it to itself. All of those transformations may be composed in a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), alongside the group's number of [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29), known as the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group). In two-dimensional space, these transformations include [rotating](wikipedia://en/701096) around the center of a polygon and [reflecting an object](wikipedia://en/1390873) around the [perpendicular bisector](wikipedia://en/152547#Perpendicular_line_segment_bisector) of a polygon. The mensuration of polyhedra includes the [surface area](wikipedia://en/27725) and [volume](wikipedia://en/32498). An [area](wikipedia://en/1209) is a two-dimensional measurement calculated by the product of length and width; for a polyhedron, the surface area is the sum of the areas of all of its faces.[^n1] A volume is a measurement of a region in three-dimensional space.[^n2] The volume of a polyhedron may be ascertained in different ways: either through its base and height (like for [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045) and [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007)), by slicing it off into pieces and summing their individual volumes, or by finding the [root](wikipedia://en/264210) of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) representing the polyhedron.[^o43]

A polygon that is rotated symmetrically by $\frac{360^\circ}{n}$ is denoted by $C_n$, a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order $n$; combining this with the reflection symmetry results in the symmetry of [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) $D_n$ of order $2n$.[^o44] In [three-dimensional symmetry point groups](wikipedia://en/2694525), the transformations preserving a polyhedron's symmetry include the rotation around the line passing through the base center, known as the [axis of symmetry](wikipedia://en/6025673), and the reflection relative to perpendicular planes passing through the bisector of a base, which is known as the [pyramidal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramidal_symmetry) $C_{n \mathrm{v}}$ of order $2n$. The transformation that preserves a polyhedron's symmetry by reflecting it across a horizontal plane is known as the [prismatic symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prismatic_symmetry) $D_{n \mathrm{h}}$ of order $4n$. The [antiprismatic symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiprismatic_symmetry) $D_{n \mathrm{d}}$ of order $4n$ preserves the symmetry by rotating its half bottom and reflection across the horizontal plane.[^n3] The symmetry group $C_{n \mathrm{h}}$ of order $2n$ preserves the symmetry by rotation around the axis of symmetry and reflection on the horizontal plane; the specific case preserving the symmetry by one full rotation is $C_{1 \mathrm{h}}$ of order 2, often denoted as $C_s$.[^o46]
