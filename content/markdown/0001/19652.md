# Monoid

*Image: Magma to group2.svg.* Algebraic structures between [magmas](wikipedia://en/141916) and [groups](wikipedia://en/19447). For example, monoids are [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) with identity.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Monoid"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **monoid** is a set equipped with an [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) and an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962). For example, the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) with addition form a monoid, the identity element being $0$.

:::

Monoids are [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) with identity. Such [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) occur in several branches of mathematics.

The functions from a set into itself form a monoid with respect to function composition. More generally, in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the morphisms of an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) to itself form a monoid, and, conversely, a monoid may be viewed as a category with a single object.

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) and [computer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming), the set of [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) built from a given set of [characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_%28computing%29) is a [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166). [Transition monoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_monoid) and [syntactic monoids](wikipedia://en/670026) are used in describing [finite-state machines](wikipedia://en/10931). [Trace monoids](wikipedia://en/10739141) and [history monoids](wikipedia://en/10833335) provide a foundation for [process calculi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_calculi) and [concurrent computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_computing).

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392), the study of monoids is fundamental for [automata theory](wikipedia://en/103356) ([Krohn–Rhodes theory](wikipedia://en/513506)), and [formal language theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_language_theory) ([star height problem](wikipedia://en/27647)).

See [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) for the history of the subject, and some other general properties of monoids.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ equipped with a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) $S \times S \to S$, which we will denote $\bullet$, is a **monoid** if it satisfies the following two axioms:

**Associativity**

For all $a$, $b$ and $c$ in $S$, the equation $(a \bullet b) \bullet c = a \bullet (b \bullet c)$ holds.

**Identity element**

There exists an element $e$ in $S$ such that for every element $a$ in $S$, the equalities $e \bullet a = a$ and $a \bullet e = a$ hold.

In other words, a monoid is a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) with an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962). It can also be thought of as a [magma](wikipedia://en/141916) with associativity and identity. The identity element of a monoid is unique. For this reason the identity is regarded as a [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132), i. e. $0$-ary (or nullary) operation. The monoid therefore is characterized by specification of the [triple](wikipedia://en/132729) $(S, \bullet , e)$.

Depending on the context, the symbol for the binary operation may be omitted, so that the operation is denoted by [juxtaposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juxtaposition); for example, the monoid axioms may be written $(ab)c = a(bc)$ and $ea = ae = a$. This notation does not imply that it is numbers being multiplied.

A [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a special case of a monoid where every element has an inverse.

:::

## Monoid structures {#Monoid_structures}

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Submonoid"}

### Submonoids {#Submonoids}

A **submonoid** of a monoid $(M, \bullet)$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $N$ of $M$ that is closed under the monoid operation and contains the identity element $e$ of $M$.[^n1] Symbolically, $N$ is a submonoid of $M$ if $e \in N \subseteq M$, and $x \bullet y \in N$ whenever $x, y \in N$. In this case, $N$ is a monoid under the binary operation inherited from $M$.

On the other hand, if $N$ is a subset of a monoid that is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under the monoid operation, and is a monoid for this inherited operation, then $N$ is not always a submonoid, since the identity elements may differ. For example, the [singleton set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) $\{0\}$ is closed under multiplication, and is not a submonoid of the (multiplicative) monoid of the [nonnegative integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative_integer).

:::

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Finitely generated monoid"}

### Generators {#Generators}

A subset $S$ of $M$ is said to *generate* $M$ if the smallest submonoid of $M$ containing $S$ is $M$. If there is a finite set that generates $M$, then $M$ is said to be a **finitely generated monoid**.

:::

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Abelian monoid"}

### Commutative monoid {#Commutative_monoid}

A monoid whose operation is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) is called a **commutative monoid** (or, less commonly, an **abelian monoid**). Commutative monoids are often written additively. Any commutative monoid is endowed with its *algebraic* [preordering](wikipedia://en/23582) $\le$, defined by $x \le y$ if there exists $z$ such that $x + z = y$.[^n2] An *order-unit* of a commutative monoid $M$ is an element $u$ of $M$ such that for any element $x$ of $M$, there exists $v$ in the set generated by $u$ such that $x \le v$. This is often used in case $M$ is the [positive cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_group) of a [partially ordered](wikipedia://en/23572) [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) $G$, in which case we say that $u$ is an order-unit of $G$.

:::

### Partially commutative monoid {#Partially_commutative_monoid}

A monoid for which the operation is commutative for some, but not all elements is a [trace monoid](wikipedia://en/10739141); trace monoids commonly occur in the theory of [concurrent computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_computation).

::: {#o15 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Out of the 16 possible [binary Boolean operators](wikipedia://en/25512250#Truth_table_for_all_binary_logical_operators), four have a two-sided identity that is also commutative and associative. These four each make the set $\{\operatorname{False}, \operatorname{True}\}$ a commutative monoid. Under the standard definitions, [AND](wikipedia://en/18152) and [XNOR](wikipedia://en/228783) have the identity $\operatorname{True}$ while [XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exclusive_disjunction) and [OR](wikipedia://en/7962) have the identity $\operatorname{False}$. The monoids from AND and OR are also [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) while those from XOR and XNOR are not.
- The set of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) $\mathbf{N} = \{0, 1, 2, ...\}$ is a commutative monoid under addition (identity element [$0$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29)) or multiplication (identity element [$1$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_%28number%29)). A submonoid of $\mathbf{N}$ under addition is called a [numerical monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_monoid).
- The set of [positive integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) $\mathbf{N} \setminus \{0\}$ is a commutative monoid under multiplication (identity element $1$).
- Given a set $A$, the set of subsets of $A$ is a commutative monoid under intersection (identity element is $A$ itself).
- Given a set $A$, the set of subsets of $A$ is a commutative monoid under union (identity element is the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566)).
- Generalizing the previous example, every bounded [semilattice](wikipedia://en/755647) is an [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) commutative monoid.
  - In particular, any bounded [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) can be endowed with both a [meet](wikipedia://en/3956618)- and a [join](wikipedia://en/3956618)- monoid structure. The identity elements are the lattice's top and its bottom, respectively. Being lattices, [Heyting algebras](wikipedia://en/309343) and [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) are endowed with these monoid structures.
- Every [singleton set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) $\{x\}$ closed under a binary operation $\bullet$ forms the trivial (one-element) monoid, which is also the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059).
- Every [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a monoid and every [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) a commutative monoid.
- Any [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) $S$ may be turned into a monoid simply by adjoining an element $e$ not in $S$ and defining $e \bullet s = s = s \bullet e$ for all $s \in S$. This conversion of any semigroup to the monoid is done by the [free functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_functor) between the category of semigroups and the category of monoids.[^n3]
  - Thus, an idempotent monoid (sometimes known as *find-first*) may be formed by adjoining an identity element $e$ to the [left zero semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_zero_semigroup) over a set $S$. The opposite monoid (sometimes called *find-last*) is formed from the [right zero semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_zero_semigroup) over $S$.
    - Adjoin an identity $e$ to the left-zero semigroup with two elements $\{\operatorname{lt}, \operatorname{gt}\}$. Then the resulting idempotent monoid $\{\operatorname{lt}, e, \operatorname{gt}\}$ models the [lexicographical order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicographical_order) of a sequence given the orders of its elements, with *e* representing equality.
- The underlying set of any [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29), with addition or multiplication as the operation. (By definition, a ring has a multiplicative identity $1$.)
  - The [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), with addition or multiplication as operation.[^n4]
  - The set of all $n$ by $n$ [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) over a given ring, with [matrix addition](wikipedia://en/125276) or [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) as the operation.
- The set of all finite [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) over some fixed alphabet $\Sigma$ forms a monoid with [string concatenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_concatenation) as the operation. The [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827) serves as the identity element. This monoid is denoted $\Sigma^{\ast}$ and is called the *[free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166)* over $\Sigma$. It is not commutative if $\Sigma$ has at least two elements.
- Given any monoid $M$, the *opposite monoid* $M^{\operatorname{op}}$ has the same carrier set and identity element as $M$, and its operation is defined by $x \bullet^{\operatorname{op}} y = y \bullet x$. Any commutative monoid is the opposite monoid of itself.
- Given two sets $M$ and $N$ endowed with monoid structure (or, in general, any finite number of monoids, $M_{1}, ..., M_{k}$), their [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $M \times N$, with the binary operation and identity element defined on corresponding coordinates, called the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887), is also a monoid (respectively, $M_{1} \times \cdot\cdot\cdot \times M_{k}$).[^n5]
- Fix a monoid $M$. The set of all functions from a given set to $M$ is also a monoid. The identity element is a [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861) mapping any value to the identity of $M$; the associative operation is defined [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797).
- Fix a monoid $M$ with the operation $\bullet$ and identity element $e$, and consider its [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) $P(M)$ consisting of all [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of $M$. A binary operation for such subsets can be defined by $S \bullet T = \{s \bullet t : s \in S, t \in T\}$. This turns $P(M)$ into a monoid with identity element $\{e\}$. In the same way the power set of a group $G$ is a monoid under the [product of group subsets](wikipedia://en/24734).
- Let $S$ be a set. The set of all functions $S \to S$ forms a monoid under [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947). The identity is just the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069). It is also called the *[full transformation monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_transformation_monoid)* of $S$. If $S$ is finite with $n$ elements, the monoid of functions on $S$ is finite with $n^{n}$ elements.
- Generalizing the previous example, let $C$ be a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) and $X$ an object of $C$. The set of all [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) of $X$, denoted $\operatorname{End}_{C}(X)$, forms a monoid under composition of [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891). For more on the relationship between category theory and monoids see below.
- The set of [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) [classes](wikipedia://en/20696) of [compact surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_surface) with the [connected sum](wikipedia://en/490134). Its unit element is the class of the ordinary 2-sphere. Furthermore, if $a$ denotes the class of the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), and $b$ denotes the class of the projective plane, then every element $c$ of the monoid has a unique expression in the form $c = na + mb$ where $n$ is a positive integer and $m = 0, 1$, or $2$. We have $3b = a + b$.
- Let $\langle f\rangle$ be a cyclic monoid of order $n$, that is, $\langle f\rangle = \{f^{0}, f^{1}, ..., f^{n-1}\}$. Then $f^{n} = f^{k}$ for some $0 \le k < n$. Each such $k$ gives a distinct monoid of order $n$, and every cyclic monoid is isomorphic to one of these. Moreover, $f$ can be considered as a function on the points $\{0, 1, 2, ..., n-1\}$ given by

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 & \cdots & n-2 & n-1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & \cdots & n-1 & k\end{bmatrix}$$

or, equivalently

$$f(i) := \begin{cases} i+1, & \text{if } 0 \le i < n-1 \\ k, & \text{if } i = n-1. \end{cases}$$

Multiplication of elements in $\langle f\rangle$ is then given by function composition. When $k = 0$ then the function $f$ is a permutation of $\{0, 1, 2, ..., n-1\}$, and gives the unique [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order $n$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

By the monoid axioms the identity element $e$ is unique, since if $e$ and $f$ were identity elements of a monoid, then $e = ef = f$.

### Products and powers {#Products_and_powers}

For each nonnegative integer $n$, one can define the product $p_n = \textstyle \prod_{i=1}^n a_i$ of any sequence $(a_{1}, ..., a_{n})$ of $n$ elements of a monoid recursively: let $p_{0} = e$ and let $p_{m} = p_{m-1} \bullet a_{m}$ for $1 \le m \le n$.

As a special case, one can define nonnegative integer powers of an element $x$ of a monoid: $x^{0} = 1$ and $x^{n} = x^{n-1} \bullet x$ for $n \ge 1$. Then $x^{m+n} = x^{m} \bullet x^{n}$ for all $m, n \ge 0$.

### Invertible elements {#Invertible_elements}

An element $x$ is called [invertible](wikipedia://en/45199) if there exists an element $y$ such that $x \bullet y = e$ and $y \bullet x = e$. The element $y$ is called the inverse of $x$. Inverses, if they exist, are unique: if $y$ and $z$ are inverses of $x$, then by associativity $y = ey = (zx)y = z(xy) = ze = z$.[^n6]

If $x$ is invertible, say with inverse $y$, then one can define negative powers of $x$ by setting $x^{-n} = y^{n}$ for each $n \ge 1$; this makes the equation $x^{m+n} = x^{m} \bullet x^{n}$ hold for all $m, n \in \mathbf{Z}$.

The set of all invertible elements in a monoid, together with the operation •, forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447).

### Grothendieck group {#Grothendieck_group}

Not every monoid sits inside a group. For instance, it is perfectly possible to have a monoid in which two elements $a$ and $b$ exist such that $a \bullet b = a$ holds even though $b$ is not the identity element (for example, take *a* = 0 and *b* = 5 in the multiplicative monoid of nonnegative integers.) Such a monoid cannot be embedded in a group, because in the group multiplying both sides with the inverse of $a$ would get that $b = e$, which is not true.

A monoid $(M, \bullet)$ has the [cancellation property](wikipedia://en/490646) (or is cancellative) if for all $a$, $b$ and $c$ in $M$, the equality $a \bullet b = a \bullet c$ implies $b = c$, and the equality $b \bullet a = c \bullet a$ implies $b = c$.

A commutative monoid with the cancellation property can always be embedded in a group via the *Grothendieck group construction*. That is how the additive group of the integers (a group with operation $+$) is constructed from the additive monoid of natural numbers (a commutative monoid with operation $+$ and cancellation property). However, a non-commutative cancellative monoid need not be embeddable in a group.

If a monoid has the cancellation property and is *finite*, then it is in fact a group.

The right- and left-cancellative elements of a monoid each in turn form a submonoid (i.e. are closed under the operation and obviously include the identity). This means that the cancellative elements of any commutative monoid can be extended to a group.

The cancellative property in a monoid is not necessary to perform the Grothendieck construction – commutativity is sufficient. However, if a commutative monoid does not have the cancellation property, the homomorphism of the monoid into its Grothendieck group is not injective. More precisely, if $a \bullet b = a \bullet c$, then $b$ and $c$ have the same image in the Grothendieck group, even if $b \ne c$. In particular, if the monoid has an [absorbing element](wikipedia://en/14206224), then its Grothendieck group is the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059).

### Types of monoids {#Types_of_monoids}

An **inverse monoid** is a monoid where for every $a$ in $M$, there exists a unique $a^{-1}$ in $M$ such that $a = a \bullet a^{-1} \bullet a$ and $a^{-1} = a^{-1} \bullet a \bullet a^{-1}$. If an inverse monoid is cancellative, then it is a group.

In the opposite direction, a *[zerosumfree monoid](wikipedia://en/5848497)* is an additively written monoid in which $a + b = 0$ implies that $a = 0$ and $b = 0$:[^n7] equivalently, that no element other than zero has an additive inverse.

:::

## Acts and operator monoids {#Acts_and_operator_monoids}

Let $M$ be a monoid, with the binary operation denoted by $\bullet$ and the identity element denoted by $e$. Then a (left) **$M$-act** (or left act over $M$) is a set $X$ together with an operation $\cdot : M \times X \to X$ which is compatible with the monoid structure as follows:

- for all $x$ in $X$: $e \cdot x = x$;
- for all $a$, $b$ in $M$ and $x$ in $X$: $a \cdot (b \cdot x) = (a \bullet b) \cdot x$.

This is the analogue in monoid theory of a (left) [group action](wikipedia://en/12781). Right $M$-acts are defined in a similar way. A monoid with an act is also known as an *[operator monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_monoid)*. Important examples include [transition systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_system) of [semiautomata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiautomata). A [transformation semigroup](wikipedia://en/19313303) can be made into an operator monoid by adjoining the identity transformation.

## Monoid homomorphisms {#Monoid_homomorphisms}

*Image: Exponentiation as monoid homomorphism svg.svg.* Example monoid homomorphism $x \mapsto 2^{x}$ from $\#004000$ to $\#400000$. It is injective, but not surjective.

A [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) between two monoids $(M, \ast)$ and $(N, \bullet)$ is a function $f : M \to N$ such that

- $f(x \ast y) = f(x) \bullet f(y)$ for all $x$, $y$ in $M$
- $$f(e_{M}) = e_{N},$$

where $e_{M}$ and $e_{N}$ are the identities on $M$ and $N$ respectively. Monoid homomorphisms are sometimes simply called **monoid morphisms**.

Not every [semigroup homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semigroup_homomorphism) between monoids is a monoid homomorphism, since it may not map the identity to the identity of the target monoid, even though the identity is the identity of the image of the homomorphism. For example, consider $[\mathbf{Z}]_{n}$, the set of [residue classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residue_class) modulo $n$ equipped with multiplication. In particular, $[1]_{n}$ is the identity element. Function $f : [\mathbf{Z}]_{3} \to [\mathbf{Z}]_{6}$ given by $[k]_{3} \mapsto [3k]_{6}$ is a semigroup homomorphism, since $[3k \cdot 3l]_{6} = [9kl]_{6} = [3kl]_{6}$. However, $f([1]_{3}) = [3]_{6} \ne [1]_{6}$, so a monoid homomorphism is a semigroup homomorphism between monoids that maps the identity of the first monoid to the identity of the second monoid and the latter condition cannot be omitted.

In contrast, a semigroup homomorphism between groups is always a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396), as it necessarily preserves the identity (because, in the target group of the homomorphism, the identity element is the only element $x$ such that $x \cdot x = x$).

A [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) monoid homomorphism is called a monoid [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). Two monoids are said to be isomorphic if there is a monoid isomorphism between them.

:::

## Equational presentation {#Equational_presentation}

Monoids may be given a *presentation*, much in the same way that groups can be specified by means of a [group presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation). One does this by specifying a set of generators $\Sigma$, and a set of relations on the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) $\Sigma^{\ast}$. One does this by extending (finite) [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931) on $\Sigma^{\ast}$ to monoid congruences, and then constructing the quotient monoid, as above.

Given a binary relation $R \subset \Sigma^{\ast} \times \Sigma^{\ast}$, one defines its symmetric closure as $R \cup R^{-1}$. This can be extended to a symmetric relation $E \subset \Sigma^{\ast} \times \Sigma^{\ast}$ by defining $x \sim_{E} y$ if and only if $x = sut$ and $y = svt$ for some strings $u, v, s, t \in \Sigma^{\ast}$ with $(u,v) \in R \cup R^{-1}$. Finally, one takes the reflexive and transitive closure of $E$, which is then a monoid congruence.

In the typical situation, the relation $R$ is simply given as a set of equations, so that $R = \{u_{1} = v_{1}, ..., u_{n} = v_{n}\}$. Thus, for example,

$$\langle p,q\,\vert\; pq=1\rangle$$

is the equational presentation for the [bicyclic monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicyclic_monoid), and

$$\langle a,b \,\vert\; aba=baa, bba=bab\rangle$$

is the [plactic monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plactic_monoid) of degree $2$ (it has infinite order). Elements of this plactic monoid may be written as $a^ib^j(ba)^k$ for integers $i$, $j$, $k$, as the relations show that $ba$ commutes with both $a$ and $b$.

## Relation to category theory {#Relation_to_category_theory}

Monoids can be viewed as a special class of [categories](wikipedia://en/5869). Indeed, the axioms required of a monoid operation are exactly those required of [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) composition when restricted to the set of all morphisms whose source and target is a given object.[^n8] That is,

*A monoid is, essentially, the same thing as a category with a single object.*

More precisely, given a monoid $(M, \bullet)$, one can construct a small category with only one object and whose morphisms are the elements of $M$. The composition of morphisms is given by the monoid operation $\bullet$.

Likewise, monoid homomorphisms are just [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) between single object categories.[^n8] So this construction gives an [equivalence](wikipedia://en/379051) between the [category of (small) monoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_monoids) **Mon** and a full subcategory of the category of (small) categories **Cat**. Similarly, the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092) is equivalent to another full subcategory of **Cat**.

In this sense, category theory can be thought of as an extension of the concept of a monoid. Many definitions and theorems about monoids can be generalised to small categories with more than one object. For example, a quotient of a category with one object is just a quotient monoid.

Monoids, just like other algebraic structures, also form their own category, **Mon**, whose objects are monoids and whose morphisms are monoid homomorphisms.[^n8]

There is also a notion of [monoid object](wikipedia://en/3955446) which is an abstract definition of what is a monoid in a category. A monoid object in [**Set**](wikipedia://en/26829) is just a monoid.

## Monoids in computer science {#Monoids_in_computer_science}

In computer science, many [abstract data types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_data_types) can be endowed with a monoid structure. In a common pattern, a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of elements of a monoid is "[folded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fold_%28higher-order_function%29)" or "accumulated" to produce a final value. For instance, many iterative algorithms need to update some kind of "running total" at each iteration; this pattern may be elegantly expressed by a monoid operation. Alternatively, the associativity of monoid operations ensures that the operation can be [parallelized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelization) by employing a [prefix sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_sum) or similar algorithm, in order to utilize multiple cores or processors efficiently.

Given a sequence of values of type $M$ with identity element $\varepsilon$ and associative operation $\bullet$, the *fold* operation is defined as follows:

$$\mathrm{fold}: M^{*} \rightarrow M = \ell \mapsto \begin{cases} \varepsilon & \text{if } \ell = \mathrm{nil} \\ m \bullet \mathrm{fold} \, \ell' & \text{if } \ell = \mathrm{cons} \, m \, \ell' \end{cases}$$

In addition, any [data structure](wikipedia://en/8519) can be 'folded' in a similar way, given a serialization of its elements. For instance, the result of "folding" a [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321) might differ depending on pre-order vs. post-order [tree traversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_traversal).

## MapReduce {#MapReduce}

An application of monoids in computer science is the so-called [MapReduce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MapReduce) programming model (see [Encoding Map-Reduce As A Monoid With Left Folding](https://erikerlandson.github.io/blog/2016/09/05/expressing-map-reduce-as-a-left-folding-monoid/)). MapReduce, in computing, consists of two or three operations. Given a dataset, "Map" consists of mapping arbitrary data to elements of a specific monoid. "Reduce" consists of folding those elements, so that in the end we produce just one element.

For example, if we have a [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303), in a program it is represented as a map from elements to their numbers. Elements are called keys in this case. The number of distinct keys may be too big, and in this case, the [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) is being sharded. To finalize reduction properly, the "Shuffling" stage regroups the data among the nodes. If we do not need this step, the whole Map/Reduce consists of mapping and reducing; both operations are parallelizable, the former due to its element-wise nature, the latter due to associativity of the monoid.

::: {#o38 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Complete monoid"}

## Complete monoids {#Complete_monoids}

A **complete monoid** is a commutative monoid equipped with an [infinitary](wikipedia://en/404456) sum operation $\Sigma_I$ for any [index set](wikipedia://en/393055) $I$ such that[^n9][^n10][^n11][^n12]

$$\sum_{i \in \emptyset}{m_i} = 0;\quad \sum_{i \in \{j\}}{m_i} = m_j;\quad \sum_{i \in \{j, k\}}{m_i} = m_j+m_k \quad \text{ for } j\neq k$$

and

$$\sum_{j \in J} {\sum_{i \in I_j}{m_i}} = \sum_{i \in I} m_i \quad \text{ if } \bigcup_{j\in J} I_j=I \text{ and } I_j \cap I_{j'} = \emptyset \quad \text{ for } j\neq j'$$

.

An **ordered commutative monoid** is a commutative monoid $M$ together with a [partial ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_ordering) $\le$ such that $a \ge 0$ for every $a \in M$, and $a \le b$ implies $a + c \le b + c$ for all $a, b, c \in M$.

A **continuous monoid** is an ordered commutative monoid $(M, \le)$ in which every [directed subset](wikipedia://en/9165) has a [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound), and these least upper bounds are compatible with the monoid operation:

$$a + \sup S = \sup(a + S)$$

for every $a \in M$ and directed subset $S$ of $M$.

If $(M, \le)$ is a continuous monoid, then for any index set $I$ and collection of elements $(a_{i})_{i\in I}$, one can define

$$\sum_I a_i = \sup_{\text{finite } E \subset I} \; \sum_E a_i,$$

and $M$ together with this infinitary sum operation is a complete monoid.[^n12]
