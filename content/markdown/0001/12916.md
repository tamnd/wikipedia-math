# Gauss–Legendre algorithm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gauss–Legendre algorithm"}

The **Gauss–Legendre algorithm** is an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) to compute the digits of [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601). It is notable for being rapidly convergent, with only 25 iterations producing 45 million correct digits of $\pi$. However, it has some drawbacks (for example, it is [computer memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random-access_memory)-intensive) and therefore all record-breaking calculations for many years have used other methods, almost always the [Chudnovsky algorithm](wikipedia://en/15893520). For details, see [Chronology of computation of $\pi$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_computation_of_π).

:::

The method is based on the individual work of [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) (1777–1855) and [Adrien-Marie Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) (1752–1833) combined with modern algorithms for multiplication and [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208). It repeatedly replaces two numbers by their [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/612) and [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046), in order to approximate their [arithmetic-geometric mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic-geometric_mean).

The version presented below is also known as the **Gauss–Euler**, **Brent–Salamin** (or **Salamin–Brent**) **algorithm**;[^o2] it was independently discovered in 1975 by [Richard Brent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brent_%28scientist%29) and [Eugene Salamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Salamin_%28mathematician%29). It was used in 1999 to compute the first 200 billion decimal digits of $\pi$, with results checked using [Borwein's algorithm](wikipedia://en/233353).

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithm"}

## Algorithm {#Algorithm}

1. Initial value setting: $$a_0 = 1\qquad b_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\qquad p_0 = 1\qquad t_0 = \frac{1}{4}.$$
1. Repeat the following instructions until the difference between $a_{n+1}$ and $b_{n+1}$ is within the desired accuracy: $$\begin{aligned} a_{n+1} & = \frac{a_n + b_n}{2}, \\ \\ b_{n+1} & = \sqrt{a_n b_n}, \\ \\ p_{n+1} & = 2p_n, \\ \\ t_{n+1} & = t_n - p_n(a_{n+1}-a_{n})^2. \\ \end{aligned}$$
1. $\pi$ is then approximated as: $$\pi \approx \frac{(a_{n+1}+b_{n+1})^2}{4t_{n+1}}.$$

The first five iterations give (approximations given up to and including the first incorrect digit):

$$3.140\dots$$

$$3.14159264\dots$$

$$3.1415926535897932382\dots$$

$$3.14159265358979323846264338327950288419711\dots$$

$$3.141592653589793238462643383279502884197169399375105820974944592307816406286208998625\dots$$

The algorithm has [quadratic convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_convergence), which essentially means that the number of correct digits doubles with each [iteration](wikipedia://en/68833) of the algorithm.

:::

## Mathematical background {#Mathematical_background}

### Limits of the arithmetic–geometric mean {#Limits_of_the_arithmetic–geometric_mean}

The [arithmetic–geometric mean](wikipedia://en/3090) of two numbers, a<sub>0</sub> and b<sub>0</sub>, is found by calculating the limit of the sequences

$$\begin{aligned} a_{n+1} & = \frac{a_n+b_n}{2}, \\[6pt] b_{n+1} & = \sqrt{a_n b_n}, \end{aligned}$$

which both converge to the same limit. If $a_0=1$ and $b_0=\cos\varphi$ then the limit is ${\pi \over 2K(\sin\varphi)}$ where $K(k)$ is the [complete elliptic integral of the first kind](wikipedia://en/9960#Complete_elliptic_integral_of_the_first_kind)

$$K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^2 \sin^2\theta}}.$$

If $c_0 = \sin\varphi$, $c_{i+1} = a_i - a_{i+1}$, then

$$\sum_{i=0}^\infty 2^{i-1} c_i^2 = 1 - {E(\sin\varphi)\over K(\sin\varphi)}$$

where $E(k)$ is the [complete elliptic integral of the second kind](wikipedia://en/9960#Complete_elliptic_integral_of_the_second_kind):

$$E(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt {1-k^2 \sin^2\theta}\; d\theta$$

Gauss knew of these two results.[^o7] [^o8] [^o9]

::: {#o11 .statement cue=named-heading name="Legendre’s identity"}

### Legendre’s identity {#Legendre’s_identity}

Legendre proved the following identity:

$$K(\cos \theta) E(\sin \theta ) + K(\sin \theta ) E(\cos \theta) - K(\cos \theta) K(\sin \theta) = {\pi \over 2},$$

for all $\theta$.[^o7]

:::
