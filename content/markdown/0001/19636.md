# Mathematical logic

Quine's theory sometimes called "Mathematical Logic"

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematical logic"}

**Mathematical logic** is the study of [formal logic](wikipedia://en/46426065#Formal_logic) within [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). Major subareas include [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478), [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), and [recursion theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion_theory) (also known as computability theory). Research in mathematical logic commonly addresses the mathematical properties of formal systems of logic such as their expressive or deductive power. However, it can also include usage of logic to characterize correct mathematical reasoning or to establish [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358).

:::

Since its inception, mathematical logic has both contributed to and been motivated by the study of the foundations of mathematics. This study began in the late 19th century with the development of [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/928) frameworks for [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), and [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). In the early 20th century it was shaped by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s [program](wikipedia://en/607286) to prove the consistency of foundational theories. Results of [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736), [Gerhard Gentzen](wikipedia://en/46027), and others provided partial resolution to the program, and clarified the issues involved in proving consistency. Work in set theory showed that almost all ordinary mathematics can be formalized in terms of sets, although there are some theorems that cannot be proven in common axiom systems for set theory. Contemporary work in the foundations of mathematics often focuses on establishing which parts of mathematics can be formalized in particular formal systems (as in [reverse mathematics](wikipedia://en/326365)) rather than trying to find theories in which all of mathematics can be developed.

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Subfields of mathematical logic"}

## Subfields and scope {#Subfields_and_scope}

The *Handbook of Mathematical Logic*[^n1] in 1977 makes a rough division of contemporary mathematical logic into four areas:

1. [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553)
1. [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858)
1. [recursion theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion_theory), and
1. [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478) and [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics) (considered as parts of a single area).

Additionally, sometimes the field of [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543) is also included together with mathematical logic.[^o5][^o6] Each area has a distinct focus, although many techniques and results are shared among multiple areas. The borderlines amongst these fields, and the lines separating mathematical logic and other fields of mathematics, are not always sharp. [Gödel's incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_incompleteness_theorem) marks not only a milestone in recursion theory and proof theory, but has also led to [Löb's theorem](wikipedia://en/693002) in modal logic. The method of [forcing](wikipedia://en/152205) is employed in set theory, model theory, and recursion theory, as well as in the study of intuitionistic mathematics.

The mathematical field of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) uses many formal axiomatic methods, and includes the study of [categorical logic](wikipedia://en/1063799), but category theory is not ordinarily considered a subfield of mathematical logic. Because of its applicability in diverse fields of mathematics, mathematicians including [Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574) have proposed category theory as a foundational system for mathematics, independent of set theory. These foundations use [elementary toposes](wikipedia://en/815802), which resemble generalized models of set theory that may employ classical or nonclassical logic.

:::

## History {#History}

Mathematical logic emerged in the mid-19th century as a subfield of mathematics, reflecting the confluence of two traditions: formal [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939) and mathematics.[^n2] Mathematical logic, also called 'logistic', 'symbolic logic', the '[algebra of logic](wikipedia://en/54476844)', and, more recently, simply 'formal logic', is the set of logical theories elaborated in the course of the nineteenth century with the aid of an artificial notation and a rigorously deductive method.[^n3] Before this emergence, logic was studied with [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447), with *calculationes*,[^n4] through the [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046), and with [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy). The first half of the 20th century saw an explosion of fundamental results, accompanied by vigorous debate over the foundations of mathematics.

### Early history {#Early_history}

Theories of logic were developed in many cultures in history, including in ancient [China](wikipedia://en/1978752), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_in_India), [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_in_Greece), [Roman empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_philosophy) and the [Islamic world](wikipedia://en/1978133). Greek methods, particularly [Aristotelian logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_logic) (or term logic) as found in the *[Organon](wikipedia://en/499019)*, found wide application and acceptance in Western science and mathematics for millennia.[^n5] The [Stoics](wikipedia://en/19224834), especially [Chrysippus](wikipedia://en/37143), began the development of [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus). In 18th-century Europe, attempts to treat the operations of formal logic in a symbolic or algebraic way had been made by philosophical mathematicians including [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) and [Lambert](wikipedia://en/93085), but their labors remained isolated and little known.

### 19th century {#19th_century}

In the middle of the nineteenth century, [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298) and then [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289) presented systematic mathematical treatments of logic. Their work, building on work by algebraists such as [George Peacock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Peacock_%28mathematician%29), extended the traditional Aristotelian doctrine of logic into a sufficient framework for the study of [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358).[^n6] In 1847, [Vatroslav Bertić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vatroslav_Bertić) made substantial work on algebraization of logic, independently from Boole.[^o15] [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) later built upon the work of Boole to develop a logical system for relations and quantifiers, which he published in several papers from 1870 to 1885.

[Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) presented an independent development of logic with quantifiers in his *[Begriffsschrift](wikipedia://en/900125)*, published in 1879, a work generally considered as marking a turning point in the history of logic. Frege's work remained obscure, however, until [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) began to promote it near the turn of the century. The two-dimensional notation Frege developed was never widely adopted and is unused in contemporary texts.

From 1890 to 1905, [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015) published *Vorlesungen über die Algebra der Logik* in three volumes. This work summarized and extended the work of Boole, De Morgan, and Peirce, and was a comprehensive reference to symbolic logic as it was understood at the end of the 19th century.

#### Foundational theories {#Foundational_theories}

Concerns that mathematics had not been built on a proper foundation led to the development of axiomatic systems for fundamental areas of mathematics such as arithmetic, analysis, and geometry.

In logic, the term *arithmetic* refers to the theory of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043)[^n7] published a set of axioms for arithmetic that came to bear his name ([Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005)), using a variation of the logical system of Boole and Schröder but adding quantifiers. Peano was unaware of Frege's work at the time. Around the same time [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) showed that the natural numbers are uniquely characterized by their [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) properties. Dedekind proposed a different characterization, which lacked the formal logical character of Peano's axioms.[^n8] Dedekind's work, however, proved theorems inaccessible in Peano's system, including the uniqueness of the set of natural numbers (up to isomorphism) and the recursive definitions of addition and multiplication from the [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637) and mathematical induction.

In the mid-19th century, flaws in Euclid's axioms for geometry became known.[^n9] In addition to the independence of the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493), established by [Nikolai Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/648527) in 1826,[^n10] mathematicians discovered that certain theorems taken for granted by Euclid were not in fact provable from his axioms. Among these is the theorem that a line contains at least two points, or that circles of the same radius whose centers are separated by that radius must intersect. Hilbert[^n11] developed a complete set of [axioms for geometry](wikipedia://en/838151), building on [previous work](wikipedia://en/5751973) by Pasch.[^n12] The success in axiomatizing geometry motivated Hilbert to seek complete axiomatizations of other areas of mathematics, such as the natural numbers and the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line). This would prove to be a major area of research in the first half of the 20th century.

The 19th century saw great advances in the theory of [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), including theories of convergence of functions and [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). Mathematicians such as [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459) began to construct functions that stretched intuition, such as [nowhere-differentiable continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous,_nowhere_differentiable_function). Previous conceptions of a function as a rule for computation, or a smooth graph, were no longer adequate. Weierstrass began to advocate the [arithmetization of analysis](wikipedia://en/294085), which sought to axiomatize analysis using properties of the natural numbers. The modern [(ε, δ)-definition of limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%28ε,_δ%29-definition_of_limit) and [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) was already developed by [Bolzano](wikipedia://en/302185) in 1817,[^n13] but remained relatively unknown. [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy) in 1821 defined continuity in terms of [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990) (see Cours d'Analyse, page 34). In 1858, Dedekind proposed a definition of the real numbers in terms of [Dedekind cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_cuts) of rational numbers, a definition still employed in contemporary texts.[^n14]

[Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) developed the fundamental concepts of infinite set theory. His early results developed the theory of [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) and [proved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor's_first_uncountability_proof) that the reals and the natural numbers have different cardinalities.[^n15] Over the next twenty years, Cantor developed a theory of [transfinite numbers](wikipedia://en/189734) in a series of publications. In 1891, he published a new proof of the uncountability of the real numbers that introduced the [diagonal argument](wikipedia://en/51426), and used this method to prove [Cantor's theorem](wikipedia://en/341442) that no set can have the same cardinality as its [powerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerset). Cantor believed that every set could be [well-ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered), but was unable to produce a proof for this result, leaving it as an open problem in 1895.[^n16]

### 20th century {#20th_century}

In the early decades of the 20th century, the main areas of study were set theory and formal logic. The discovery of paradoxes in informal set theory caused some to wonder whether mathematics itself is inconsistent, and to look for proofs of consistency.

In 1900, [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) posed a famous list of [23 problems](wikipedia://en/154584) for the next century. The first two of these were to resolve the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) and prove the consistency of elementary arithmetic, respectively; the tenth was to produce a method that could decide whether a multivariate polynomial equation over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) has a solution. Subsequent work to resolve these problems shaped the direction of mathematical logic, as did the effort to resolve Hilbert's *[Entscheidungsproblem](wikipedia://en/9672)*, posed in 1928. This problem asked for a procedure that would decide, given a formalized mathematical statement, whether the statement is true or false.

#### Set theory and paradoxes {#Set_theory_and_paradoxes}

[Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655) gave a proof that [every set could be well-ordered](wikipedia://en/33458), a result [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) had been unable to obtain.[^n17] To achieve the proof, Zermelo introduced the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), which drew heated debate and research among mathematicians and the pioneers of set theory. The immediate criticism of the method led Zermelo to publish a second exposition of his result, directly addressing criticisms of his proof.[^n18] This paper led to the general acceptance of the axiom of choice in the mathematics community.

Skepticism about the axiom of choice was reinforced by recently discovered paradoxes in [naive set theory](wikipedia://en/4944). [Cesare Burali-Forti](wikipedia://en/2452025)[^n19] was the first to state a paradox: the [Burali-Forti paradox](wikipedia://en/51653) shows that the collection of all [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932) cannot form a set. Very soon thereafter, [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) discovered [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095) in 1901, and [Jules Richard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Richard_%28mathematician%29) discovered [Richard's paradox](wikipedia://en/97812).[^n20]

Zermelo provided the first set of axioms for set theory.[^n21] These axioms, together with the additional [axiom of replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_replacement) proposed by [Abraham Fraenkel](wikipedia://en/528451), are now called [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) (ZF). Zermelo's axioms incorporated the principle of [limitation of size](wikipedia://en/4190174) to avoid Russell's paradox.

In 1910, the first volume of *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* by Russell and [Alfred North Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) was published. This seminal work developed the theory of functions and cardinality in a completely formal framework of [type theory](wikipedia://en/40282), which Russell and Whitehead developed in an effort to avoid the paradoxes. *Principia Mathematica* is considered one of the most influential works of the 20th century, although the framework of type theory did not prove popular as a foundational theory for mathematics.[^n22]

Fraenkel[^n23] proved that the axiom of choice cannot be proved from the axioms of Zermelo's set theory with [urelements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urelements). Later work by [Paul Cohen](wikipedia://en/22994)[^n24] showed that the addition of urelements is not needed, and the axiom of choice is unprovable in ZF. Cohen's proof developed the method of [forcing](wikipedia://en/152205), which is now an important tool for establishing [independence results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_result) in set theory.[^o38]

#### Symbolic logic {#Symbolic_logic}

[Leopold Löwenheim](wikipedia://en/755668)[^n25] and [Thoralf Skolem](wikipedia://en/453765)[^n26] obtained the [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482), which says that [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) cannot control the [cardinalities](wikipedia://en/6173) of infinite structures. Skolem realized that this theorem would apply to first-order formalizations of set theory, and that it implies any such formalization has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [model](wikipedia://en/4055928). This counterintuitive fact became known as [Skolem's paradox](wikipedia://en/1348798).

*Image: Young Kurt Gödel as a student in 1925.jpg.* Portrait of young [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) as a student in [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866), 1925

In his doctoral thesis, [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) proved the [completeness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completeness_theorem), which establishes a correspondence between syntax and semantics in first-order logic.[^n27] Gödel used the completeness theorem to prove the [compactness theorem](wikipedia://en/152207), demonstrating the finitary nature of first-order [logical consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624). These results helped establish first-order logic as the dominant logic used by mathematicians.

In 1931, Gödel published *[On Formally Undecidable Propositions of Principia Mathematica and Related Systems](wikipedia://en/1450110)*, which proved the incompleteness (in a different meaning of the word) of all sufficiently strong, effective first-order theories. This result, known as [Gödel's incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_incompleteness_theorem), establishes severe limitations on axiomatic foundations for mathematics, striking a strong blow to Hilbert's program. It showed the impossibility of providing a consistency proof of arithmetic within any formal theory of arithmetic. Hilbert, however, did not acknowledge the importance of the incompleteness theorem for some time.

Gödel's theorem shows that a [consistency](wikipedia://en/75802) proof of any sufficiently strong, effective axiom system cannot be obtained in the system itself, if the system is consistent, nor in any weaker system. This leaves open the possibility of consistency proofs that cannot be formalized within the system they consider. Gentzen proved the consistency of arithmetic using a finitistic system together with a principle of [transfinite induction](wikipedia://en/30461).[^n28] Gentzen's result introduced the ideas of [cut elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_elimination) and [proof-theoretic ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof-theoretic_ordinal), which became key tools in proof theory. Gödel gave a different consistency proof, which reduces the consistency of classical arithmetic to that of intuitionistic arithmetic in higher types.[^n29]

The first textbook on symbolic logic for the layman was written by [Lewis Carroll](wikipedia://en/26586083),[^o45] author of *[Alice's Adventures in Wonderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice's_Adventures_in_Wonderland)*, in 1896.[^n30]

#### Beginnings of the other branches {#Beginnings_of_the_other_branches}

[Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) developed the basics of [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858).

Beginning in 1935, a group of prominent mathematicians collaborated under the pseudonym [Nicolas Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) to publish *[Éléments de mathématique](wikipedia://en/38333812)*, a series of encyclopedic mathematics texts. These texts, written in an austere and axiomatic style, emphasized rigorous presentation and set-theoretic foundations. Terminology coined by these texts, such as the words [*bijection*, *injection*, and *surjection*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijection,_injection,_and_surjection), and the set-theoretic foundations the texts employed, were widely adopted throughout mathematics.

The study of computability came to be known as recursion theory or [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414), because early formalizations by Gödel and Kleene relied on recursive definitions of functions. When these definitions were shown equivalent to Turing's formalization involving [Turing machines](wikipedia://en/30403), it became clear that a new concept – the [computable function](wikipedia://en/1139338) – had been discovered, and that this definition was robust enough to admit numerous independent characterizations. In his work on the incompleteness theorems in 1931, Gödel lacked a rigorous concept of an effective formal system; he immediately realized that the new definitions of computability could be used for this purpose, allowing him to state the incompleteness theorems in generality that could only be implied in the original paper.

Numerous results in recursion theory were obtained in the 1940s by [Stephen Cole Kleene](wikipedia://en/28805) and [Emil Leon Post](wikipedia://en/362382). Kleene[^n31] introduced the concepts of relative computability, foreshadowed by Turing,[^n32] and the [arithmetical hierarchy](wikipedia://en/186475). Kleene later generalized recursion theory to higher-order functionals. Kleene and [Georg Kreisel](wikipedia://en/11761785) studied formal versions of intuitionistic mathematics, particularly in the context of proof theory.

## Formal logical systems {#Formal_logical_systems}

At its core, mathematical logic deals with mathematical concepts expressed using [formal logical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system). These systems, though they differ in many details, share the common property of considering only expressions in a fixed [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939). The systems of [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) and [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) are the most widely studied today, because of their applicability to [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358) and because of their desirable proof-theoretic properties. Stronger classical logics such as [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428) or [infinitary logic](wikipedia://en/630478) are also studied, along with [Non-classical logics](wikipedia://en/3192556) such as [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262).

### First-order logic {#First-order_logic}

**First-order logic** is a particular [formal system of logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system). Its [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) involves only finite expressions as [well-formed formulas](wikipedia://en/404582), while its [semantics](wikipedia://en/10983#Semantics) are characterized by the limitation of all [quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantifiers_%28logic%29) to a fixed [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180).

Early results from formal logic established limitations of first-order logic. The [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482) (1919) showed that if a set of sentences in a countable first-order language has an infinite model then it has at least one model of each infinite cardinality. This shows that it is impossible for a set of first-order axioms to characterize the natural numbers, the real numbers, or any other infinite structure up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). As the goal of early foundational studies was to produce axiomatic theories for all parts of mathematics, this limitation was particularly stark.

[Gödel's completeness theorem](wikipedia://en/12450) established the equivalence between semantic and syntactic definitions of [logical consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) in first-order logic.[^n27] It shows that if a particular sentence is true in every model that satisfies a particular set of axioms, then there must be a finite deduction of the sentence from the axioms. The [compactness theorem](wikipedia://en/152207) first appeared as a lemma in Gödel's proof of the completeness theorem, and it took many years before logicians grasped its significance and began to apply it routinely. It says that a set of sentences has a model if and only if every finite subset has a model, or in other words that an inconsistent set of formulas must have a finite inconsistent subset. The completeness and compactness theorems allow for sophisticated analysis of logical consequence in first-order logic and the development of [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), and they are a key reason for the prominence of first-order logic in mathematics.

[Gödel's incompleteness theorems](wikipedia://en/58863) establish additional limits on first-order axiomatizations.[^n33] The **first incompleteness theorem** states that for any consistent, effectively given (defined below) logical system that is capable of interpreting arithmetic, there exists a statement that is true (in the sense that it holds for the natural numbers) but not provable within that logical system (and which indeed may fail in some [non-standard models of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/6251420) which may be consistent with the logical system). For example, in every logical system capable of expressing the [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005), the Gödel sentence holds for the natural numbers but cannot be proved.

Here a logical system is said to be effectively given if it is possible to decide, given any formula in the language of the system, whether the formula is an axiom, and one which can express the Peano axioms is called "sufficiently strong." When applied to first-order logic, the first incompleteness theorem implies that any sufficiently strong, consistent, effective first-order theory has models that are not [elementarily equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_substructure), a stronger limitation than the one established by the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem. The **second incompleteness theorem** states that no sufficiently strong, consistent, effective axiom system for arithmetic can prove its own consistency, which has been interpreted to show that [Hilbert's program](wikipedia://en/607286) cannot be reached.

### Other classical logics {#Other_classical_logics}

Many logics besides first-order logic are studied. These include [infinitary logics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitary_logics), which allow for formulas to provide an infinite amount of information, and [higher-order logics](wikipedia://en/562883), which include a portion of set theory directly in their semantics.

The most well studied infinitary logic is $L_{\omega_1,\omega}$. In this logic, quantifiers may only be nested to finite depths, as in first-order logic, but formulas may have finite or countably infinite conjunctions and disjunctions within them. Thus, for example, it is possible to say that an object is a whole number using a formula of $L_{\omega_1,\omega}$ such as

$$(x = 0) \lor (x = 1) \lor (x = 2) \lor \cdots.$$

Higher-order logics allow for quantification not only of elements of the [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180), but subsets of the domain of discourse, sets of such subsets, and other objects of higher type. The semantics are defined so that, rather than having a separate domain for each higher-type quantifier to range over, the quantifiers instead range over all objects of the appropriate type. The logics studied before the development of first-order logic, for example Frege's logic, had similar set-theoretic aspects. Although higher-order logics are more expressive, allowing complete axiomatizations of structures such as the natural numbers, they do not satisfy analogues of the completeness and compactness theorems from first-order logic, and are thus less amenable to proof-theoretic analysis.

Another type of logics are **fixed-point logics** that allow [inductive definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_definition), like one writes for [primitive recursive functions](wikipedia://en/24829).

One can formally define an extension of first-order logic — a notion which encompasses all logics in this section because they behave like first-order logic in certain fundamental ways, but does not encompass all logics in general, e.g. it does not encompass intuitionistic, modal or [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180).

[Lindström's theorem](wikipedia://en/7510462) implies that the only extension of first-order logic satisfying both the [compactness theorem](wikipedia://en/152207) and the [downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482#Downward_part) is first-order logic.

### Nonclassical and modal logic {#Nonclassical_and_modal_logic}

[Modal logics](wikipedia://en/333365) include additional modal operators, such as an operator which states that a particular formula is not only true, but necessarily true. Although modal logic is not often used to axiomatize mathematics, it has been used to study the properties of first-order provability[^n34] and set-theoretic forcing.[^n35]

[Intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262) was developed by Heyting to study Brouwer's program of intuitionism, in which Brouwer himself avoided formalization. Intuitionistic logic specifically does not include the [law of the excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_excluded_middle), which states that each sentence is either true or its negation is true. Kleene's work with the proof theory of intuitionistic logic showed that constructive information can be recovered from intuitionistic proofs. For example, any provably total function in intuitionistic arithmetic is [computable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computable); this is not true in classical theories of arithmetic such as [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic).

### Algebraic logic {#Algebraic_logic}

[Algebraic logic](wikipedia://en/7870034) uses the methods of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) to study the semantics of formal logics. A fundamental example is the use of [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) to represent [truth values](wikipedia://en/161711) in classical propositional logic, and the use of [Heyting algebras](wikipedia://en/309343) to represent truth values in intuitionistic propositional logic. Stronger logics, such as first-order logic and higher-order logic, are studied using more complicated algebraic structures such as [cylindric algebras](wikipedia://en/7852887).

## Set theory {#Set_theory}

**[Set theory](wikipedia://en/27553)** is the study of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), which are abstract collections of objects. Many of the basic notions, such as ordinal and cardinal numbers, were developed informally by Cantor before formal axiomatizations of set theory were developed. The [first such axiomatization](wikipedia://en/322623), due to Zermelo,[^n21] was extended slightly to become [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) (ZF), which is now the most widely used foundational theory for mathematics.

Other formalizations of set theory have been proposed, including [von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory](wikipedia://en/528491) (NBG), [Morse–Kelley set theory](wikipedia://en/2693655) (MK), and [New Foundations](wikipedia://en/945957) (NF). Of these, ZF, NBG, and MK are similar in describing a [cumulative hierarchy](wikipedia://en/7393195) of sets. New Foundations takes a different approach; it allows objects such as the set of all sets at the cost of restrictions on its set-existence axioms. The system of [Kripke–Platek set theory](wikipedia://en/1124695) is closely related to generalized recursion theory.

Two famous statements in set theory are the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) and the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705). The axiom of choice, first stated by Zermelo,[^n17] was proved independent of ZF by Fraenkel,[^n23] but has come to be widely accepted by mathematicians. It states that given a collection of nonempty sets there is a single set *C* that contains exactly one element from each set in the collection. The set *C* is said to "choose" one element from each set in the collection. While the ability to make such a choice is considered obvious by some, since each set in the collection is nonempty, the lack of a general, concrete rule by which the choice can be made renders the axiom nonconstructive. [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) and [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) showed that the axiom of choice can be used to decompose a solid ball into a finite number of pieces which can then be rearranged, with no scaling, to make two solid balls of the original size.[^n36] This theorem, known as the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220), is one of many counterintuitive results of the axiom of choice.

The continuum hypothesis, first proposed as a conjecture by Cantor, was listed by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) as one of his 23 problems in 1900. Gödel showed that the continuum hypothesis cannot be disproven from the axioms of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory (with or without the axiom of choice), by developing the [constructible universe](wikipedia://en/343335) of set theory in which the continuum hypothesis must hold. In 1963, [Paul Cohen](wikipedia://en/22994) showed that the continuum hypothesis cannot be proven from the axioms of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory.[^n24] This independence result did not completely settle Hilbert's question, however, as it is possible that new axioms for set theory could resolve the hypothesis. Recent work along these lines has been conducted by [W. Hugh Woodin](wikipedia://en/2275071), although its importance is not yet clear.[^n37]

Contemporary research in set theory includes the study of [large cardinals](wikipedia://en/383644) and [determinacy](wikipedia://en/2596030). Large cardinals are [cardinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_numbers) with particular properties so strong that the existence of such cardinals cannot be proved in ZFC. The existence of the smallest large cardinal typically studied, an [inaccessible cardinal](wikipedia://en/248076), already implies the consistency of ZFC. Despite the fact that large cardinals have extremely high [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174), their existence has many ramifications for the structure of the real line. *Determinacy* refers to the possible existence of winning strategies for certain two-player games (the games are said to be *determined*). The existence of these strategies implies structural properties of the real line and other [Polish spaces](wikipedia://en/396622).

## Model theory {#Model_theory}

**[Model theory](wikipedia://en/19858)** studies the models of various formal theories. Here a [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103) is a set of formulas in a particular formal logic and [signature](wikipedia://en/7788156), while a [model](wikipedia://en/4055928) is a structure that gives a concrete interpretation of the theory. Model theory is closely related to [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), although the methods of model theory focus more on logical considerations than those fields.

The set of all models of a particular theory is called an [elementary class](wikipedia://en/1284226); classical model theory seeks to determine the properties of models in a particular elementary class, or determine whether certain classes of structures form elementary classes.

The method of [quantifier elimination](wikipedia://en/2150441) can be used to show that definable sets in particular theories cannot be too complicated. Tarski established quantifier elimination for [real-closed fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-closed_field), a result which also shows the theory of the field of real numbers is [decidable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decidable_set).[^n38] He also noted that his methods were equally applicable to algebraically closed fields of arbitrary characteristic. A modern subfield developing from this is concerned with [o-minimal structures](wikipedia://en/30865852).

[Morley's categoricity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morley's_categoricity_theorem), proved by [Michael D. Morley](wikipedia://en/2921675),[^n39] states that if a first-order theory in a countable language is categorical in some uncountable cardinality, i.e. all models of this cardinality are isomorphic, then it is categorical in all uncountable cardinalities.

A trivial consequence of the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) is that a complete theory with less than continuum many nonisomorphic countable models can have only countably many. [Vaught's conjecture](wikipedia://en/13797188), named after [Robert Lawson Vaught](wikipedia://en/3550212), says that this is true even independently of the continuum hypothesis. Many special cases of this conjecture have been established.

## Recursion theory {#Recursion_theory}

**[Recursion theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion_theory)**, also called **computability theory**, studies the properties of [computable functions](wikipedia://en/1139338) and the [Turing degrees](wikipedia://en/764405), which divide the uncomputable functions into sets that have the same level of uncomputability. Recursion theory also includes the study of generalized computability and definability. Recursion theory grew from the work of [Rózsa Péter](wikipedia://en/1297277), [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731) and [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) in the 1930s, which was greatly extended by [Kleene](wikipedia://en/28805) and [Post](wikipedia://en/362382) in the 1940s.[^n40]

Classical recursion theory focuses on the computability of functions from the natural numbers to the natural numbers. The fundamental results establish a robust, canonical class of computable functions with numerous independent, equivalent characterizations using [Turing machines](wikipedia://en/30403), [λ calculus](wikipedia://en/18203), and other systems. More advanced results concern the structure of the Turing degrees and the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) of [recursively enumerable sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable_set).

Generalized recursion theory extends the ideas of recursion theory to computations that are no longer necessarily finite. It includes the study of computability in higher types as well as areas such as [hyperarithmetical theory](wikipedia://en/12383591) and [α-recursion theory](wikipedia://en/12008116).

Contemporary research in recursion theory includes the study of applications such as [algorithmic randomness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_randomness), [computable model theory](wikipedia://en/11574361), and [reverse mathematics](wikipedia://en/326365), as well as new results in pure recursion theory.

### Algorithmically unsolvable problems {#Algorithmically_unsolvable_problems}

An important subfield of recursion theory studies algorithmic unsolvability; a [decision problem](wikipedia://en/8336) or [function problem](wikipedia://en/663345) is **algorithmically unsolvable** if there is no possible computable algorithm that returns the correct answer for all legal inputs to the problem. The first results about unsolvability, obtained independently by Church and Turing in 1936, showed that the [Entscheidungsproblem](wikipedia://en/9672) is algorithmically unsolvable. Turing proved this by establishing the unsolvability of the [halting problem](wikipedia://en/21391870), a result with far-ranging implications in both recursion theory and computer science.

There are many known examples of undecidable problems from ordinary mathematics. The [word problem for groups](wikipedia://en/33563) was proved algorithmically unsolvable by [Pyotr Novikov](wikipedia://en/1139033) in 1955 and independently by W. Boone in 1959. The [busy beaver](wikipedia://en/67911) problem, developed by [Tibor Radó](wikipedia://en/1490393) in 1962, is another well-known example.

[Hilbert's tenth problem](wikipedia://en/101851) asked for an algorithm to determine whether a multivariate polynomial equation with integer coefficients has a solution in the integers. Partial progress was made by [Julia Robinson](wikipedia://en/952987), [Martin Davis](wikipedia://en/953041) and [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233). The algorithmic unsolvability of the problem was proved by [Yuri Matiyasevich](wikipedia://en/2627712) in 1970.[^n41]

## Proof theory and constructive mathematics {#Proof_theory_and_constructive_mathematics}

**[Proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478)** is the study of formal proofs in various logical deduction systems. These proofs are represented as formal mathematical objects, facilitating their analysis by mathematical techniques. Several deduction systems are commonly considered, including [Hilbert-style deduction systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert-style_deduction_system), systems of [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072), and the [sequent calculus](wikipedia://en/252329) developed by Gentzen.

The study of **constructive mathematics**, in the context of mathematical logic, includes the study of systems in non-classical logic such as intuitionistic logic, as well as the study of [predicative](wikipedia://en/1930406) systems. An early proponent of predicativism was [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), who showed it is possible to develop a large part of real analysis using only predicative methods.[^n42]

Because proofs are entirely finitary, whereas truth in a structure is not, it is common for work in constructive mathematics to emphasize provability. The relationship between provability in classical (or nonconstructive) systems and provability in intuitionistic (or constructive, respectively) systems is of particular interest. Results such as the [Gödel–Gentzen negative translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel–Gentzen_negative_translation) show that it is possible to embed (or *[translate](wikipedia://en/73032003)*) classical logic into intuitionistic logic, allowing some properties about intuitionistic proofs to be transferred back to classical proofs.

Recent developments in proof theory include the study of [proof mining](wikipedia://en/14484306) by [Ulrich Kohlenbach](wikipedia://en/18204005) and the study of [proof-theoretic ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof-theoretic_ordinal) by [Michael Rathjen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Rathjen).

## Connections with computer science {#Connections_with_computer_science}

The study of [computability theory in computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computability_theory_%28computer_science%29) is closely related to the study of computability in mathematical logic. There is a difference of emphasis, however. [Computer scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) often focus on concrete programming languages and [feasible computability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feasible_computability), while researchers in mathematical logic often focus on computability as a theoretical concept and on noncomputability.

The theory of [semantics of programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_semantics) is related to [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), as is [program verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_verification) (in particular, [model checking](wikipedia://en/321157)). The [Curry–Howard correspondence](wikipedia://en/254299) between proofs and programs relates to [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478), especially [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262). Formal calculi such as the [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203) and [combinatory logic](wikipedia://en/149848) are now studied as idealized [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages).

Computer science also contributes to mathematics by developing techniques for the automatic checking or even finding of proofs, such as [automated theorem proving](wikipedia://en/2546) and [logic programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_programming).

[Descriptive complexity theory](wikipedia://en/1212009) relates logics to [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/7543). The first significant result in this area, [Fagin's theorem](wikipedia://en/3122050) (1974) established that [NP](wikipedia://en/21562) is precisely the set of languages expressible by sentences of existential [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428).

## Foundations of mathematics {#Foundations_of_mathematics}

In the 19th century, mathematicians became aware of logical gaps and inconsistencies in their field. It was shown that [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s axioms for geometry, which had been taught for centuries as an example of the axiomatic method, were incomplete. The use of [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990), and the very definition of [function](wikipedia://en/185427), came into question in analysis, as pathological examples such as Weierstrass' nowhere-[differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) continuous function were discovered.

Cantor's study of arbitrary infinite sets also drew criticism. [Leopold Kronecker](wikipedia://en/318450) famously stated "God made the integers; all else is the work of man," endorsing a return to the study of finite, concrete objects in mathematics. Although Kronecker's argument was carried forward by constructivists in the 20th century, the mathematical community as a whole rejected them. [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) argued in favor of the study of the infinite, saying "No one shall expel us from the Paradise that Cantor has created."

Mathematicians began to search for axiom systems that could be used to formalize large parts of mathematics. In addition to removing ambiguity from previously naive terms such as function, it was hoped that this axiomatization would allow for consistency proofs. In the 19th century, the main method of proving the consistency of a set of axioms was to provide a model for it. Thus, for example, [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) can be proved consistent by defining *point* to mean a point on a fixed sphere and *line* to mean a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) on the sphere. The resulting structure, a model of [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020), satisfies the axioms of plane geometry except the parallel postulate.

With the development of formal logic, Hilbert asked whether it would be possible to prove that an axiom system is consistent by analyzing the structure of possible proofs in the system, and showing through this analysis that it is impossible to prove a contradiction. This idea led to the study of [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478). Moreover, Hilbert proposed that the analysis should be entirely concrete, using the term *finitary* to refer to the methods he would allow but not precisely defining them. This project, known as [Hilbert's program](wikipedia://en/607286), was seriously affected by Gödel's incompleteness theorems, which show that the consistency of formal theories of arithmetic cannot be established using methods formalizable in those theories. Gentzen showed that it is possible to produce a proof of the consistency of arithmetic in a finitary system augmented with axioms of [transfinite induction](wikipedia://en/30461), and the techniques he developed to do so were seminal in proof theory.

A second thread in the history of foundations of mathematics involves [nonclassical logics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonclassical_logic) and [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics). The study of constructive mathematics includes many different programs with various definitions of *constructive*. At the most accommodating end, proofs in ZF set theory that do not use the axiom of choice are called constructive by many mathematicians. More limited versions of constructivism limit themselves to [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers), [number-theoretic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number-theoretic_function), and sets of natural numbers (which can be used to represent real numbers, facilitating the study of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396)). A common idea is that a concrete means of computing the values of the function must be known before the function itself can be said to exist.

In the early 20th century, [Luitzen Egbertus Jan Brouwer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luitzen_Egbertus_Jan_Brouwer) founded [intuitionism](wikipedia://en/19513) as a part of [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439). This philosophy, poorly understood at first, stated that in order for a mathematical statement to be true to a mathematician, that person must be able to *intuit* the statement, to not only believe its truth but understand the reason for its truth. A consequence of this definition of truth was the rejection of the [law of the excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_excluded_middle), for there are statements that, according to Brouwer, could not be claimed to be true while their negations also could not be claimed true. Brouwer's philosophy was influential, and the cause of bitter disputes among prominent mathematicians. Kleene and Kreisel would later study formalized versions of intuitionistic logic (Brouwer rejected formalization, and presented his work in unformalized natural language). With the advent of the [BHK interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BHK_interpretation) and [Kripke models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kripke_model), intuitionism became easier to reconcile with classical mathematics.
