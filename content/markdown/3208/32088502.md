::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Coxeter notation"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Coxeter notation** (also **Coxeter symbol**) is a system of classifying [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186), describing the angles between fundamental reflections of a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) in a bracketed notation expressing the structure of a [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram), with modifiers to indicate certain subgroups. The notation is named after [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328), and has been more comprehensively defined by [Norman Johnson](wikipedia://en/715527).

:::

## Reflectional groups {#Reflectional_groups}

For [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004), defined by pure reflections, there is a direct correspondence between the bracket notation and [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram). The numbers in the bracket notation represent the mirror reflection orders in the branches of the Coxeter diagram. It uses the same simplification, suppressing 2s between orthogonal mirrors.

The Coxeter notation is simplified with exponents to represent the number of branches in a row for linear diagram. So the *A*<sub>*n*</sub> group is represented by \[3<sup>*n*−1</sup>\], to imply *n* nodes connected by *n−1* order-3 branches. Example *A*<sub>2</sub> = \[3,3\] = \[3<sup>2</sup>\] or \[3<sup>1,1</sup>\] represents diagrams or .

Coxeter initially represented bifurcating diagrams with vertical positioning of numbers, but later abbreviated with an exponent notation, like \[...,3<sup>p,q</sup>\] or \[3<sup>p,q,r</sup>\], starting with \[3<sup>1,1,1</sup>\] or \[3,3<sup>1,1</sup>\] = or as D<sub>4</sub>. Coxeter allowed for zeros as special cases to fit the *A*<sub>*n*</sub> family, like *A*<sub>3</sub> = \[3,3,3,3\] = \[3<sup>4,0,0</sup>\] = \[3<sup>4,0</sup>\] = \[3<sup>3,1</sup>\] = \[3<sup>2,2</sup>\], like = = .

Coxeter groups formed by cyclic diagrams are represented by parentheses inside of brackets, like \[(p,q,r)\] = for the [triangle group](wikipedia://en/2069342) (p q r). If the branch orders are equal, they can be grouped as an exponent as the length the cycle in brackets, like \[(3,3,3,3)\] = \[3<sup>\[4\]</sup>\], representing Coxeter diagram or . can be represented as \[3,(3,3,3)\] or \[3,3<sup>\[3\]</sup>\].

More complicated looping diagrams can also be expressed with care. The [paracompact Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/5358554#Ranks_4.E2.80.9310) can be represented by Coxeter notation \[(3,3,(3),3,3)\], with nested/overlapping parentheses showing two adjacent \[(3,3,3)\] loops, and is also represented more compactly as \[3<sup>\[ \]×\[ \]</sup>\], representing the [rhombic symmetry](wikipedia://en/183884) of the Coxeter diagram. ## Subgroups {#Subgroups}

Coxeter's notation represents rotational/translational symmetry by adding a **<sup>\+</sup>** superscript operator outside the brackets, \[X\]<sup>\+</sup> which cuts the order of the group \[X\] in half, thus an index 2 subgroup. This operator implies an even number of operators must be applied, replacing reflections with rotations (or translations). When applied to a Coxeter group, this is called a **direct subgroup** because what remains are only direct isometries without reflective symmetry.

The **<sup>\+</sup>** operators can also be applied inside of the brackets, like \[X,Y<sup>\+</sup>\] or \[X,(Y,Z)<sup>\+</sup>\], and creates **"semidirect" subgroups** that may include both reflective and nonreflective generators. Semidirect subgroups can only apply to Coxeter group subgroups that have even order branches adjacent to it. Elements by parentheses inside of a Coxeter group can be give a **<sup>\+</sup>** superscript operator, having the effect of dividing adjacent ordered branches into half order, thus is usually only applied with even numbers. For example, \[4,3<sup>\+</sup>\] and \[4,(3,3)<sup>\+</sup>\] ().

If applied with adjacent odd branch, it doesn't create a subgroup of index 2, but instead creates overlapping fundamental domains, like \[5,1<sup>\+</sup>\] = \[5/2\], which can define doubly wrapped polygons like a [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544), {5/2}, and \[5,3<sup>\+</sup>\] relates to [Schwarz triangle](wikipedia://en/2069325) \[5/2,3\], [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytope_density) 2.

### Radical subgroups {#Radical_subgroups}

*Image: 43-radial subgroups.svg.* A radical subgroup is similar to an alternation, but removes the rotational generators.

Johnson also added an [asterisk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asterisk) or star **\*** operator for "radical" subgroups,[^o14] that acts similar to the <sup>\+</sup> operator, but removes rotational symmetry. The index of the radical subgroup is the order of the removed element. For example, \[4,3\*\] ≅ \[2,2\]. The removed \[3\] subgroup is order 6 so \[2,2\] is an index 6 subgroup of \[4,3\].

The radical subgroups represent the inverse operation to an extended symmetry operation. For example, \[4,3\*\] ≅ \[2,2\], and in reverse \[2,2\] can be extended as \[3\[2,2\]\] ≅ \[4,3\]. The subgroups can be expressed as a Coxeter diagram: or ≅ . The removed node (mirror) causes adjacent mirror virtual mirrors to become real mirrors.

If \[4,3\] has generators {0,1,2}, \[4,3<sup>\+</sup>\], index 2, has generators {0,12}; \[1<sup>\+</sup>,4,3\] ≅ \[3,3\], index 2 has generators {010,1,2}; while radical subgroup \[4,3\*\] ≅ \[2,2\], index 6, has generators {01210, 2, (012)<sup>3</sup>}; and finally \[1<sup>\+</sup>,4,3\*\], index 12 has generators {0(12)<sup>2</sup>0, (012)<sup>2</sup>01}.

### Central inversion {#Central_inversion}

*Image: Point Reflection.png.* A 2D central inversion is a 180 degree rotation, \[2\]<sup>\+</sup>

A [central inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_inversion), order 2, is operationally differently by dimension. The group \[ \]<sup>*n*</sup> = \[2<sup>*n*−1</sup>\] represents *n* orthogonal mirrors in n-dimensional space, or an [n-flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-flat) subspace of a higher dimensional space. The mirrors of the group \[2<sup>*n*−1</sup>\] are numbered $0 \dots n-1$. The order of the mirrors doesn't matter in the case of an inversion. The matrix of a central inversion is $-I$, the Identity matrix with negative one on the diagonal.

From that basis, the central inversion has a generator as the product of all the orthogonal mirrors. In Coxeter notation this inversion group is expressed by adding an alternation <sup>\+</sup> to each 2 branch. The alternation symmetry is marked on Coxeter diagram nodes as open nodes.

A [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram) can be marked up with explicit 2 branches defining a linear sequence of mirrors, open-nodes, and shared double-open nodes to show the chaining of the reflection generators.

For example, \[2<sup>\+</sup>,2\] and \[2,2<sup>\+</sup>\] are subgroups index 2 of \[2,2\], , and are represented as (or ) and (or ) with generators {01,2} and {0,12} respectively. Their common subgroup index 4 is \[2<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>\], and is represented by (or ), with the double-open marking a shared node in the two alternations, and a single [rotoreflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotoreflection) generator {012}.

### Rotations and rotary reflections {#Rotations_and_rotary_reflections}

Rotations and [rotary reflections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_reflection) are constructed by a single single-generator product of all the reflections of a prismatic group, \[2*p*\]×\[2*q*\]×... where [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)(*p*,*q*,...)=1, they are isomorphic to the abstract [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) Z<sub>n</sub>, of order *n*=2*pq*.

The 4-dimensional double rotations, \[2*p*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,2*q*<sup>\+</sup>\] (with [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)(*p*,*q*)=1), which include a central group, and are expressed by Conway as ±\[C<sub>*p*</sub>×C<sub>*q*</sub>\],[^o21] order 2*pq*. From Coxeter diagram , generators {0,1,2,3}, requires two generator for \[2*p*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,2*q*<sup>\+</sup>\], as {0123,0132}. Half groups, \[2*p*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,2*q*<sup>\+</sup>\]<sup>\+</sup>, or cyclic graph, \[(2*p*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,2*q*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>)\], expressed by Conway is \[C<sub>*p*</sub>×C<sub>*q*</sub>\], order *pq*, with one generator, like {0123}.

If there is a common factor *f*, the double rotation can be written as $\tfrac{1}{f}$\[2*pf*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,2*qf*<sup>\+</sup>\] (with [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)(*p*,*q*)=1), generators {0123,0132}, order 2*pqf*. For example, *p*=*q*=1, *f*=2, $\tfrac{1}{2}$\[4<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,4<sup>\+</sup>\] is order 4. And $\tfrac{1}{f}$\[2*pf*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,2*qf*<sup>\+</sup>\]<sup>\+</sup>, generator {0123}, is order *pqf*. For example, $\tfrac{1}{2}$\[4<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>,4<sup>\+</sup>\]<sup>\+</sup> is order 2, a [central inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_inversion).

In general a *n*-rotation group, \[2*p*<sub>1</sub><sup>\+</sup>,2,2*p*<sub>2</sub><sup>\+</sup>,2,...,*p*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>\+</sup>\] may require up to *n* generators if gcd(*p*<sub>1</sub>,..,*p*<sub>*n*</sub>)>1, as a product of all mirrors, and then swapping sequential pairs. The half group, \[2*p*<sub>1</sub><sup>\+</sup>,2,2*p*<sub>2</sub><sup>\+</sup>,2,...,*p*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>\+</sup>\]<sup>\+</sup> has generators squared. *n*-rotary reflections are similar.

### Commutator subgroups {#Commutator_subgroups}

*Image: Subgroups of 442.png.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) subgroups of \[4,4\], down to its commutator subgroup, index 8

Simple groups with only odd-order branch elements have only a single rotational/translational subgroup of order 2, which is also the [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847), examples \[3,3\]<sup>\+</sup>, \[3,5\]<sup>\+</sup>, \[3,3,3\]<sup>\+</sup>, \[3,3,5\]<sup>\+</sup>. For other Coxeter groups with even-order branches, the commutator subgroup has index 2<sup>*c*</sup>, where c is the number of disconnected subgraphs when all the even-order branches are removed.[^o24]

For example, \[4,4\] has three independent nodes in the Coxeter diagram when the **4**s are removed, so its commutator subgroup is index 2<sup>3</sup>, and can have different representations, all with three **<sup>\+</sup>** operators: \[4<sup>\+</sup>,4<sup>\+</sup>\]<sup>\+</sup>, \[1<sup>\+</sup>,4,1<sup>\+</sup>,4,1<sup>\+</sup>\], \[1<sup>\+</sup>,4,4,1<sup>\+</sup>\]<sup>\+</sup>, or \[(4<sup>\+</sup>,4<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>)\]. A general notation can be used with +*c* as a group exponent, like \[4,4\]<sup>\+3</sup>.

### Parabolic subgroups {#Parabolic_subgroups}

A [parabolic subgroup](wikipedia://en/75737969) of a Coxeter group can be identified by removing one or more generator mirrors represented with a Coxeter diagram. For example, the octahedral group has parabolic subgroups , , , , , . In bracket notation \[4,3\] has parabolic subgroups \[4\],\[2\],\[3\], and a single mirror \[\]. The order of the subgroup is known, and always an integer divisor group order, or index. Parabolic subgroups can also be written with x nodes, like =\[4,3\] subgroup by removing second mirror: or = = \[4,1<sup>×</sup>,3\] = \[2\].

### Petrie subgroup {#Petrie_subgroup}

A *petrie subgroup* of an irreducible coxeter group can be created by the product of all of the generators. It can be seen in the skew regular [petrie polygon](wikipedia://en/18571869) of a [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693). The order of the new group is called the [Coxeter number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_number) of the original Coxeter group. The *Coxeter number* of a Coxeter group is 2*m*/*n*, where *n* is the rank, and *m* is the number of reflections. A petrie subgroup can be written with a $\pi$ superscript. For example, \[3,3\]<sup>$\pi$</sup> is the petrie subgroup of a tetrahedral group, cyclic group order 4, generated by a [rotoreflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotoreflection). A rank 4 Coxeter group will have a [double rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_rotation) generator, like \[4,3,3\]<sup>$\pi$</sup> is order 8.

### Affine {#Affine}

In the Euclidean plane there's 3 fundamental reflective groups generated by 3 mirrors, represented by Coxeter diagrams , , and , and are given Coxeter notation as \[4,4\], \[6,3\], and \[(3,3,3)\]. The parentheses of the last group imply the diagram cycle, and also has a shorthand notation \[3<sup>\[3\]</sup>\].

## Complex reflections {#Complex_reflections}

*Image: Rank2\_shephard\_subgroups3.png.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) with all subgroup relations on rank 2 Shephard groups.

Coxeter notation has been extended to [Complex space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space), C<sup>n</sup> where nodes are [unitary reflections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_reflection) of period 2 or greater. Nodes are labeled by an index, assumed to be 2 for ordinary real reflection if suppressed. [Complex reflection groups](wikipedia://en/5381096) are called [Shephard groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shephard_group) rather than [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004), and can be used to construct [complex polytopes](wikipedia://en/8581934).

In $\mathbb{C}^1$, a rank 1 Shephard group , order *p*, is represented as <sub>*p*</sub>\[ \], \[ \]<sub>*p*</sub> or \]<sub>*p*</sub>\[. It has a single generator, representing a 2*π*/*p* radian rotation in the [Complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628): $e^{2\pi i/p}$.

Coxeter writes the rank 2 complex group, <sub>*p*</sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*r*</sub> represents [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) . ## Computation with reflection matrices as symmetry generators {#Computation_with_reflection_matrices_as_symmetry_generators}

A Coxeter group, represented by [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) , is given Coxeter notation \[p,q\] for the branch orders. Each node in the Coxeter diagram represents a mirror, by convention called ρ<sub>i</sub> (and matrix R<sub>i</sub>). The *generators* of this group \[p,q\] are reflections: ρ<sub>0</sub>, ρ<sub>1</sub>, and ρ<sub>2</sub>. Rotational subsymmetry is given as products of reflections: By convention, σ<sub>0,1</sub> (and matrix S<sub>0,1</sub>) = ρ<sub>0</sub>ρ<sub>1</sub> represents a rotation of angle π/p, and σ<sub>1,2</sub> = ρ<sub>1</sub>ρ<sub>2</sub> is a rotation of angle π/q, and σ<sub>0,2</sub> = ρ<sub>0</sub>ρ<sub>2</sub> represents a rotation of angle π/2.

\[p,q\]<sup>\+</sup>, , is an index 2 subgroup represented by two rotation generators, each a products of two reflections: σ<sub>0,1</sub>, σ<sub>1,2</sub>, and representing rotations of π/*p*, and π/*q* angles respectively.

With one even branch, \[*p*<sup>\+</sup>,2*q*\], or , is another subgroup of index 2, represented by rotation generator σ<sub>0,1</sub>, and reflectional ρ<sub>2</sub>.

With even branches, \[2*p*<sup>\+</sup>,2*q*<sup>\+</sup>\], , is a subgroup of index 4 with two generators, constructed as a product of all three reflection matrices: By convention as: ψ<sub>0,1,2</sub> and ψ<sub>1,2,0</sub>, which are [rotary reflections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_reflection), representing a reflection and rotation or reflection.

In the case of affine Coxeter groups like , or , one mirror, usually the last, is translated off the origin. A [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) generator τ<sub>0,1</sub> (and matrix T<sub>0,1</sub>) is constructed as the product of two (or an even number of) reflections, including the affine reflection. A [transreflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transreflection) (reflection plus a translation) can be the product of an odd number of reflections φ<sub>0,1,2</sub> (and matrix V<sub>0,1,2</sub>), like the index 4 subgroup : \[4<sup>\+</sup>,4<sup>\+</sup>\] = .

Another composite generator, by convention as ζ (and matrix Z), represents the [inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_point), mapping a point to its inverse. For \[4,3\] and \[5,3\], ζ = (ρ<sub>0</sub>ρ<sub>1</sub>ρ<sub>2</sub>)<sup>h/2</sup>, where *h* is 6 and 10 respectively, the [Coxeter number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_number) for each family. For 3D Coxeter group \[p,q\] (), this subgroup is a rotary reflection \[2<sup>\+</sup>,h<sup>\+</sup>\].

Coxeter groups are categorized by their rank, being the number of nodes in its [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram). The structure of the groups are also given with their abstract group types: In this article, the abstract [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267) are represented as *Dih*<sub>n</sub>, and [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) are represented by *Z*<sub>n</sub>, with *Dih*<sub>1</sub>=*Z*<sub>2</sub>.

### Rank 3 {#Rank_3}

The finite rank 3 Coxeter groups are \[1,*p*\], \[2,*p*\], \[3,3\], \[3,4\], and \[3,5\].

To reflect a point through a plane $ax + by + cz = 0$ (which goes through the origin), one can use $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I} - 2\mathbf{NN}^T$, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the 3×3 identity matrix and $\mathbf{N}$ is the three-dimensional [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) for the vector normal of the plane. If the [L2 norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L2_norm) of $a, b,$ and $c$ is unity, the transformation matrix can be expressed as:

$$\mathbf{A} = \left [\begin{smallmatrix} 1 - 2 a^2 & - 2 a b & - 2 a c \\ - 2 a b & 1 - 2 b^2 & - 2 b c \\ - 2 a c & - 2 b c & 1 - 2c^2 \end{smallmatrix}\right ]$$
