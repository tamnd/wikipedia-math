# Compactness measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Compactness measure"}

**Compactness measure** is a numerical quantity representing the degree to which a [shape](wikipedia://en/169191) is compact. The circle and the sphere are the most compact planar and solid shapes, respectively.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Various compactness measures are used. However, these measures have the following in common:

- They are applicable to all geometric shapes.
- They are independent of scale and orientation.
- They are [dimensionless numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_number).
- They are not overly dependent on one or two extreme [points](wikipedia://en/593693) in the shape.
- They agree with intuitive notions of what makes a shape compact.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

A common compactness measure is the [isoperimetric quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isoperimetric_quotient), the ratio of the area of the shape to the area of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) (the most compact shape) having the same perimeter. In the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), this is equivalent to the [Polsby–Popper test](wikipedia://en/53292468). Alternatively, the shape's area could be compared to that of its [bounding circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounding_circle),[^o15][^o16] its [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634),[^o15][^o17] or its [minimum bounding box](wikipedia://en/12087300).[^o17]

Similarly, a comparison can be made between the perimeter of the shape and that of its convex hull,[^o17] its bounding circle,[^o15] or a circle having the same area.[^o15]

Other tests involve determining how much area overlaps with a circle of the same area[^o16] or a reflection of the shape itself.[^o15]

Compactness measures can be defined for three-dimensional shapes as well, typically as functions of [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) and surface [area](wikipedia://en/1209). One example of a compactness measure is [sphericity](wikipedia://en/2628049) $\Psi$. Another measure in use is $(\text{surface area})^{1.5}/(\text{volume})$,[^o6] which is proportional to $\Psi^{-3/2}$.

For [raster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raster_graphics) shapes, *i.e.* shapes composed of pixels or cells, some tests involve distinguishing between exterior and interior edges (or faces).[^o16][^o18]

More sophisticated measures of compactness include calculating the shape's [moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700)[^o16][^o17] or boundary [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770).[^o17]

:::

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

A common use of compactness measures is in [redistricting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redistricting). The goal is to maximize the compactness of [electoral districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituency), subject to other constraints, and thereby to avoid [gerrymandering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrymandering).[^o9] Another use is in [zoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoning), to regulate the manner in which land can be subdivided into [building lots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lot_%28real_estate%29).[^o10]

:::

## Human perception {#Human_perception}

There is evidence that *compactness* is one of the basic dimensions of shape features extracted by the human visual system.[^o12]
