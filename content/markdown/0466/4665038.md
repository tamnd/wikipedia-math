# Sobolev inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sobolev inequality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), there is in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) a class of **Sobolev inequalities**, relating norms including those of [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964). These are used to prove the **Sobolev embedding theorem**, giving inclusions between certain [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964), and the [Rellich–Kondrachov theorem](wikipedia://en/7907151) showing that under slightly stronger conditions some Sobolev spaces are [compactly embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactly_embedded) in others. They are named after [Sergei Lvovich Sobolev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Lvovich_Sobolev).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Sobolev embedding theorem"}

## Sobolev embedding theorem {#Sobolev_embedding_theorem}

*Image: Sobolev embedding theorem.svg.* Graphical representation of the embedding conditions. The space $W^{\,3,p}$, represented by a blue dot at the point $(1/p, 3)$, embeds into the spaces indicated by red dots, all lying on a line with slope $n$. The white circle at $(0,0)$ indicates the impossibility of optimal embeddings into $L^{\,\infty}$.

Let $W^{\,k,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})$ denote the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) consisting of all real-valued functions on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ whose [weak derivatives](wikipedia://en/1411087) up to order $k$ are functions in $L^{p}$. Here $k$ is a non-negative integer and $1 \le p < \infty$. The first part of the Sobolev embedding theorem states that if $k > \ell$, $p < n$ and $1 \le p < q < \infty$ are two real numbers such that

$$\frac{1}{p}-\frac{k}{n} = \frac{1}{q} -\frac{\ell}{n},$$

(given $n$, $p$, $k$ and $\ell$ this is satisfied for some $q \in [1, \infty)$ provided $(k- \ell) p < n$), then

$$W^{k,p}(\mathbf{R}^n)\subseteq W^{\ell,q}(\mathbf{R}^n)$$

and the embedding is continuous: for every $f \in W^{k,p}(\mathbf{R}^n)$, one has $f \in W^{l,q}(\mathbf{R}^n)$, and

$$\biggl(\int_{\mathbf{R}^n} \vert \nabla^\ell f\vert^q \biggr)^\frac{1}{q} \le C \biggl(\int_{\mathbf{R}^n} \vert \nabla^k f\vert^p \biggr)^\frac{1}{p}.$$

In the special case of $k = 1$ and $\ell = 0$, Sobolev embedding gives

$$W^{1,p}(\mathbf{R}^n) \subseteq L^{p^*}(\mathbf{R}^n)$$

where $p^{\ast}$ is the **[Sobolev conjugate](wikipedia://en/4700705)** of $p$, given by

$$\frac{1}{p^*} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{n}$$

and for every $f \in W^{1,p}(\mathbf{R}^n)$, one has $f \in L^{p^*}(\mathbf{R}^n)$ and

$$\biggl(\int_{\mathbf{R}^n} \vert f\vert^{p^*} \biggr)^{\frac{1}{p^*}} \le C \biggl(\int_{\mathbf{R}^n} \vert \nabla f\vert^p \biggr)^{\frac{1}{p}}.$$

This special case of the Sobolev embedding is a direct consequence of the Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequality. The result should be interpreted as saying that if a function $f$ in $L^p(\mathbf{R}^n)$ has one derivative in $L^p$, then $f$ itself has improved local behavior, meaning that it belongs to the space $L^{p^*}$ where $p^*>p$. (Note that $1/p^*<1/p$, so that $p^*>p$.) Thus, any local singularities in $f$ must be more mild than for a typical function in $L^p$.

*Image: Sobolev embedding theorem (Morrey case).svg.* If the line from the picture above intersects the y-axis at *s = r + α*, the embedding into a Hölder space $C^{\,r, \alpha}$ (red) holds. White circles indicate intersection points at which *optimal* embeddings are not valid.

The second part of the Sobolev embedding theorem applies to embeddings in [Hölder spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_space) $C^{\,r,\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{n})$. If $n < pk$ and

$$\frac{1}{p}-\frac{k}{n} = -\frac{r + \alpha}{n}, \mbox{ or, equivalently, } r + \alpha = k - \frac{n}{p}$$

with $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ then one has the embedding

$$W^{k,p}(\mathbf{R}^n)\subset C^{r,\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^n).$$

In other words, for every $f \in W^{k,p}(\mathbf{R}^n)$ and $x, y \in \mathbf{R}^n$, one has $f \in C^r (\mathbf{R}^n)$, in addition,

$$\vert \nabla^r f (x) - \nabla^r f (y) \vert \le C \biggl(\int_{\mathbf{R}^n} \vert \nabla^k f \vert^p\biggr)^\frac{1}{p} \vert x - y \vert^\alpha.$$

This part of the Sobolev embedding is a direct consequence of Morrey's inequality. Intuitively, this inclusion expresses the fact that the existence of sufficiently many weak derivatives implies some continuity of the classical derivatives. If $\alpha = 1$ then $W^{k,p}(\mathbf{R}^n)\subset C^{r,\gamma}(\mathbf{R}^n)$ for every $\gamma \in (0,1)$.

In particular, as long as $pk>n$, the embedding criterion will hold with $r=0$ and some positive value of $\alpha$. That is, for a function $f$ on $\mathbb R^n$, if $f$ has $k$ derivatives in $L^p$ and $pk>n$, then $f$ will be continuous (and actually Hölder continuous with some positive exponent $\alpha$).

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The Sobolev embedding theorem holds for Sobolev spaces $W^{\,k,p}(M)$ on other suitable domains $M$. In particular ((Aubin 1982, Chapter 2); (Aubin 1976)), both parts of the Sobolev embedding hold when

- $M$ is a [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with [Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/51731) boundary (or whose boundary satisfies the [cone condition](wikipedia://en/49932318); (Adams 1975, Theorem 5.4))
- $M$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652)
- $M$ is a compact Riemannian [manifold with boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_boundary) and the boundary is Lipschitz (meaning that the boundary can be locally represented as a graph of a Lipschitz continuous function).
- $M$ is a [complete](wikipedia://en/6875557) Riemannian manifold with [injectivity radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injectivity_radius) $\delta > 0$ and bounded [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618).

If $M$ is a bounded open set in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with continuous boundary, then $W^{\,1,2}(M)$ is compactly embedded in $L^{2}(M)$ ((Nečas 2012, Section 1.1.5, Theorem 1.4)).

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Kondrachov embedding theorem"}

### Kondrachov embedding theorem {#Kondrachov_embedding_theorem}

On a compact manifold $M$ with $C^{1}$ boundary, the **Kondrachov embedding theorem** states that if $k > \ell$ and

$$\frac{1}{p}-\frac{k}{n} < \frac{1}{q} -\frac{\ell}{n}$$

then the Sobolev embedding

$$W^{k,p}(M)\subset W^{\ell,q}(M)$$

is [completely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_continuous) (compact).[^o12] Note that the condition is just as in the first part of the Sobolev embedding theorem, with the equality replaced by an inequality, thus requiring a more regular space $W^{\,k,p}(M)$.

:::

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequality"}

## Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequality {#Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev_inequality}

Assume that $u$ is a continuously differentiable real-valued function on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support). Then for $1 \le p < n$ there is a constant $C$ depending only on $n$ and $p$ such that

$$\|u\|_{L^{p^*}(\mathbf{R}^n)}\leq C \|Du\|_{L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^n)}.$$

with $1/p^* = 1/p - 1/n$. The case $1< p < n$ is due to Sobolev[^o15] and the case $p =1$ to [Gagliardo](wikipedia://en/69121596) and [Nirenberg](wikipedia://en/1814725) independently.[^o16][^o17] The Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequality implies directly the Sobolev embedding

$$W^{1,p}(\mathbf{R}^n) \subset L^{p^*}(\mathbf{R}^n).$$

The embeddings in other orders on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ are then obtained by suitable iteration.

:::

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev lemma"}

## Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev lemma {#Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev_lemma}

Sobolev's original proof of the Sobolev embedding theorem relied on the following, sometimes known as the Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev [fractional integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_integration) theorem. An equivalent statement is known as the **Sobolev lemma** in (Aubin 1982, Chapter 2). A proof is in (Stein 1970, Chapter V, §1.3).

Let $0 < \alpha < n$ and $1 < p < q < \infty$. Let $I_{\alpha} = (-\Delta)^{-\alpha/2}$ be the [Riesz potential](wikipedia://en/6779393) on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. Then, for $q$ defined by

$$\frac 1 q = \frac 1 p - \frac \alpha n$$

there exists a constant $C$ depending only on $p$ such that

$$\left \|I_\alpha f \right \|_q \le C \|f\|_p.$$

If $p = 1$, then one has two possible replacement estimates. The first is the more classical weak-type estimate:

$$m \left \{x : \left |I_\alpha f(x) \right | > \lambda \right \} \le C \left( \frac{\|f\|_1}{\lambda} \right )^q,$$

where $1/q = 1 - \alpha/n$. Alternatively one has the estimate for dimension n≥2

$$\left \|I_\alpha f \right \|_q \le C \|Rf\|_1,$$

where $Rf$ is the vector-valued [Riesz transform](wikipedia://en/18673900), cf. (Schikorra Spector Van Schaftingen 2017). The boundedness of the [Riesz transforms](wikipedia://en/18673900) implies that the latter inequality gives a unified way to write the family of inequalities for the Riesz potential.

The Hardy–Littlewood–Sobolev lemma implies the Sobolev embedding essentially by the relationship between the [Riesz transforms](wikipedia://en/18673900) and the Riesz potentials.

:::

::: {#o24 .statement cue=named-heading name="Morrey's inequality"}

## Morrey's inequality {#Morrey's_inequality}

Assume $n < p \le \infty$. Then there exists a constant $C$, depending only on $p$ and $n$, such that

$$\|u\|_{C^{0,\gamma}(\mathbf{R}^n)}\leq C \|u\|_{W^{1,p}(\mathbf{R}^n)}$$

for all $u \in C^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{n}) \cap L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})$, where

$$\gamma=1-\frac{n}{p}.$$

Thus if $u \in W^{\,1,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})$, then $u$ is in fact [Hölder continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_continuous) of exponent $\gamma$, after possibly being redefined on a set of measure 0.

A similar result holds in a bounded domain $U$ with Lipschitz boundary. In this case,

$$\|u\|_{C^{0,\gamma}(U)}\leq C \|u\|_{W^{1,p}(U)}$$

where the constant $C$ depends now on $n, p$ and $U$. This version of the inequality follows from the previous one by applying the norm-preserving extension of $W^{\,1,p}(U)$ to $W^{\,1,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})$. The inequality is named after [Charles B. Morrey Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_B._Morrey_Jr.)

:::

## General Sobolev inequalities {#General_Sobolev_inequalities}

Let $U$ be a bounded open subset of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, with a $C^{1}$ boundary. ($U$ may also be unbounded, but in this case its boundary, if it exists, must be sufficiently well-behaved.)

Assume $u \in W^{\,k,p}(U)$. Then we consider two cases:

### $k < n/p$ or $k = n$, $p = 1$ {#or_,}

In this case we conclude that $u \in L^{q}(U)$, where

$$\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{p}-\frac{k}{n}.$$

We have in addition the estimate

$$\|u\|_{L^q(U)}\leq C \|u\|_{W^{k,p}(U)},$$

the constant $C$ depending only on $k, p, n$, and $U$.

### $k > n/p$

Here, we conclude that $u$ belongs to a [Hölder space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_space), more precisely:

$$u \in C^{k-\left[\frac{n}{p}\right]-1,\gamma}(U),$$

where

$$\gamma = \begin{cases} \left[\frac{n}{p}\right]+1-\frac{n}{p} & \frac{n}{p} \notin \mathbf{Z} \\ \text{any element in } (0, 1) & \frac{n}{p} \in \mathbf{Z} \end{cases}$$

We have in addition the estimate

$$\|u\|_{C^{k-\left[\frac{n}{p}\right]-1,\gamma}(U)}\leq C \|u\|_{W^{k,p}(U)},$$

the constant $C$ depending only on $k, p, n, \gamma$, and $U$. In particular, the condition $k>n/p$ guarantees that $u$ is continuous (and actually Hölder continuous with some positive exponent).

## Case $p=n, k=1$ {#Case}

If $u\in W^{1,n}(\mathbf{R}^n)$, then $u$ is a function of [bounded mean oscillation](wikipedia://en/4694026) and

$$|u|_{\operatorname{BMO}(\mathbf R^n)} \leq C \|Du\|_{L^n(\mathbf{R}^n)},$$

for some constant $C$ depending only on $n$.[^o28] This estimate is a corollary of the [Poincaré inequality](wikipedia://en/4722074). (It should as well be stressed that on the left hand side is the BMO *semi*norm.)

## Case $kp=n$ {#Case}

The prior result may be extended to arbitrary degree $k$ [^o30]. For $k\geq 2$, if $kp=n$, then for every $u\in W^{k,n/k}(\mathbf R^n)$ there exists a polynomial $P$ of degree $k-1$ such that

$$\Vert u-P\circ u\Vert_{\operatorname{BMO}(\mathbf R^n)}\leq C\Vert D^k u\Vert_{L^{n/k}(\mathbf R^n)}$$

additionally, for any $j=1,\dots,k-1$, we have

$$\Vert D^j(u-P\circ u)\Vert_{L^{n/j}(\mathbf R^n)}\leq C \Vert D^k u\Vert_{L^{n/k}(\mathbf R^n)}$$

In particular this implies the inclusion embedding

$$W^{k,n/k}(\mathbf R^n) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{BMO}(\mathbf R^n)\cap W^{1,q_1}(\mathbf R^n)\cap \dots\cap W^{k-1,q_{k-1}}(\mathbf R^n)$$

for all exponents $q_j$ satisfying $n/k\leq q_j\leq n/j$, $j=1,\dots,k-1$.

::: {#o32 .statement cue=named-heading name="Nash inequality"}

## Nash inequality {#Nash_inequality}

The Nash inequality, introduced by (txt), states that there exists a constant $C > 0$, such that for all $u \in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{n}) \cap W^{\,1,2}(\mathbf{R}^{n})$,

$$\|u\|_{L^2(\mathbf{R}^n)}^{1+2/n} \leq C\|u\|_{L^1(\mathbf{R}^n)}^{2/n} \| Du\|_{L^2(\mathbf{R}^n)}.$$

The inequality follows from basic properties of the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247). Indeed, integrating over the complement of the ball of radius $\rho$,

::: {#o34 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\int_{|x|\ge\rho} \left |\hat{u}(x) \right |^2\,dx \le \int_{|x|\ge\rho} \frac{|x|^2}{\rho^2} \left |\hat{u}(x) \right |^2\,dx\le \rho^{-2}\int_{\mathbf{R}^n}|D u|^2\,dx \tag{1}$$

:::

because $1\le|x|^2/\rho^2$. On the other hand, one has

$$|\hat{u}| \le \|u\|_{L^1}$$

which, when integrated over the ball of radius $\rho$ gives

::: {#o35 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\int_{|x|\le\rho} |\hat{u}(x)|^2\,dx \le \rho^n\omega_n \|u\|_{L^1}^2 \tag{2}$$

:::

where $\omega_{n}$ is the volume of the [$n$-ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N_sphere). Choosing $\rho$ to minimize the sum of ((1)) and ((2)) and applying Parseval's theorem:

$$\|\hat{u}\|_{L^2} = \|u\|_{L^2}$$

gives the inequality.

In the special case of $n = 1$, the Nash inequality can be extended to the $L^{p}$ case, in which case it is a generalization of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality ((Brezis 2011), Comments on Chapter 8). In fact, if $I$ is a bounded interval, then for all $1 \le r < \infty$ and all $1 \le q \le p < \infty$ the following inequality holds

$$\| u\|_{L^p(I)}\le C\| u\|^{1-a}_{L^q(I)} \|u\|^a_{W^{1,r}(I)},$$

where:

$$a\left(\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{r}+1\right)=\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{p}.$$

:::

::: {#o38 .statement cue=named-heading name="Logarithmic Sobolev inequality"}

## Logarithmic Sobolev inequality {#Logarithmic_Sobolev_inequality}

The simplest of the Sobolev embedding theorems, described above, states that if a function $f$ in $L^p(\mathbb R^n)$ has one derivative in $L^p$, then $f$ itself is in $L^{p^*}$, where

$$1/p^*=1/p-1/n.$$

We can see that as $n$ tends to infinity, $p^*$ approaches $p$. Thus, if the dimension $n$ of the space on which $f$ is defined is large, the improvement in the local behavior of $f$ from having a derivative in $L^p$ is small ($p^*$ is only slightly larger than $p$). In particular, for functions on an infinite-dimensional space, we cannot expect any direct analog of the classical Sobolev embedding theorems.

There is, however, a type of Sobolev inequality, established by [Leonard Gross](wikipedia://en/64592988) ((Gross 1975)) and known as a [logarithmic Sobolev inequality](wikipedia://en/64602455), that has dimension-independent constants and therefore continues to hold in the infinite-dimensional setting. The logarithmic Sobolev inequality says, roughly, that if a function is in $L^p$ with respect to a Gaussian measure and has one derivative that is also in $L^p$, then $f$ is in "$L^p$-log", meaning that the integral of $|f|^p\log|f|$ is finite. The inequality expressing this fact has constants that do not involve the dimension of the space and, thus, the inequality holds in the setting of a Gaussian measure on an infinite-dimensional space. It is now known that logarithmic Sobolev inequalities hold for many different types of measures, not just Gaussian measures.

Although it might seem as if the $L^p$-log condition is a very small improvement over being in $L^p$, this improvement is sufficient to derive an important result, namely hypercontractivity for the associated [Dirichlet form](wikipedia://en/25002118) operator. This result means that if a function is in the range of the exponential of the Dirichlet form operator—which means that the function has, in some sense, infinitely many derivatives in $L^p$—then the function does belong to $L^{p^*}$ for some $p^*>p$ ((Gross 1975) Theorem 6).
