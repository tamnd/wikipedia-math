# Isbell's zigzag theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isbell's zigzag theorem"}

**Isbell's zigzag theorem**, a theorem of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) characterizing the notion of a dominion, was introduced by American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [John R. Isbell](wikipedia://en/31277386) in 1966.[^o2] Dominion is a concept in [semigroup theory](wikipedia://en/27799), within the study of the properties of [epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/59539). For example, let $U$ be a subsemigroup of $S$ containing $U$, the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) $U \hookrightarrow S$ is an epimorphism [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\rm{Dom}_S (U) = S$, furthermore, a map $\alpha \colon S \to T$ is an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) if and only if $\rm{Dom}_T (\rm{im} \; \alpha) = T$. The categories of rings and semigroups are examples of categories with non-surjective epimorphism, and the Zig-zag theorem gives necessary and sufficient conditions for determining whether or not a given morphism is epi. Proofs of this theorem are [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) in nature, beginning with (Isbell 1966) for [semigroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semigroups), and continuing by (Philip 1974), completing Isbell's original proof. The pure algebraic proofs were given by (Howie 1976) and (Storrer 1976).

:::

## Statement {#Statement}

### Zig-zag {#Zig-zag}

*Image: Zigzag theorem 2.svg.* The dashed line is the spine of the zig-zag.

Zig-zag:[^o10][^o11][^o12] If $U$ is a submonoid of a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) (or a subsemigroup of a semigroup) $S$, then a system of equalities;

$$\begin{aligned} d &= x_1 u_1, &u_1 &= v_1 y_1 \\ x_{i - 1} v_{i - 1} &= x_i u_i, &u_i y_{i - 1} &= v_i y_i \; (i = 2, \dots, m) \\ x_{m} v_{m} &= u_{m+1}, &u_{m + 1} y_{m} &= d \end{aligned}$$

in which $u_1, \dots , u_{m + 1}, v_1, \dots , v_{m} \in U$ and $x_1, \dots , x_{m}, y_1, \dots , y_{m} \in S$, is called a zig-zag of length $m$ in $S$ over $U$ with value $d$. By the spine of the zig-zag we mean the ordered $(2m + 1)$-[tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) $(u_1,v_1,u_2,v_2,\dots,u_{m},v_{m}, u_{m+1})$.

### Dominion {#Dominion}

Dominion: Let $U$ be a submonoid of a monoid (or a subsemigroup of a semigroup) $S$. The dominion $\rm{Dom}_S (U)$ is the set of all elements $s \in S$ such that, for all homomorphisms $f, g : S \to T$ coinciding on $U$, $f(s) = g(s)$.

We call a subsemigroup $U$ of a semigroup $S$ closed if $\rm{Dom}_S (U) = U$, and dense if $\rm{Dom}_S (U) = S$.[^o14]

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Isbell's zigzag theorem"}

### Isbell's zigzag theorem {#Isbell's_zigzag_theorem}

Isbell's zigzag theorem:[^o17]

If $U$ is a submonoid of a monoid $S$ then $d \in \rm{Dom}_S (U)$ if and only if either $d \in U$ or there exists a zig-zag in $S$ over $U$ with value $d$ that is, there is a sequence of factorizations of $d$ of the form

$$d = x_1 u_1=x_1 v_1 y_1 = x_2 u_2 y_1 = x_2 v_2 y_2 = \cdots = x_{m} v_{m} y_{m} = u_{m+1} y_{m}$$

This statement also holds for [semigroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semigroups).[^o18][^o19]

For monoids, this theorem can be written more concisely:[^o20][^o21]

Let $S$ be a monoid, let $U$ be a submonoid of $S$, and let $d \in S$. Then $d \in \mathrm{Dom}_{S} (U)$ if and only if $d \otimes 1 = 1 \otimes d$ in the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) $S \otimes_{U} S$.

## Application {#Application}

- Let $U$ be a commutative subsemigroup of a semigroup $S$. Then $\rm{Dom}_S (U)$ is commutative.[^o24]
- Every epimorphism $\alpha \colon S \to T$ from a finite commutative semigroup $S$ to another semigroup $T$ is surjective.
- [Inverse semigroups](wikipedia://en/1757814) are absolutely closed.[^o25]
- Example of non-surjective epimorphism in the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181):[^o26] The inclusion $i: (\mathbb{Z},\cdot)\hookrightarrow (\mathbb{Q},\cdot)$ is an epimorphism in the category of all rings and ring homomorphisms by proving that any pair of [ring homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411) $\beta, \gamma: \mathbb{Q} \to R$ to any ring $R$ which agree on $\mathbb{Z}$ are fact equal.

We show that: Let $\beta, \gamma$ to be ring homomorphisms, and $n,m \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \neq 0$. When $\beta(m) = \gamma(m)$ for all $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $\beta\left(\frac{m}{n}\right) = \gamma\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)$ for all $\frac{m}{n} \in \mathbb{Q}$.

$$\begin{aligned} \beta\left(\frac{m}{n}\right) &=\beta\left(\frac{1}{n} \cdot m \right) = \beta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot \beta(m)\\ &= \beta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot \gamma(m) = \beta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot \gamma \left(mn \cdot \frac{1}{n} \right)\\ &= \beta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\cdot \gamma(mn) \cdot \gamma\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) = \beta\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) \cdot \beta(mn)\cdot \gamma\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\\ &= \beta\left(\frac{1}{n} \cdot mn \right)\cdot \gamma \left(\frac{1}{n} \right) = \beta(m )\cdot \gamma\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) = \gamma(m) \cdot \gamma\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\\ &= \gamma \left(m \cdot \frac{1}{n}\right) = \gamma \left(\frac{m}{n}\right), \end{aligned}$$

as required.

:::
