# Illumination problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Illumination problem"}

*Image: Penrose\_unilluminable\_room.svg.* [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193)'s solution of the illumination problem using [elliptical](wikipedia://en/9277) arcs (blue) and straight line segments (green), with 3 positions of the single light source (red spot). The purple crosses are the [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) of the larger arcs. Lit and unlit regions are shown in yellow and grey respectively.

**Illumination problems** are a class of [mathematical problems](wikipedia://en/256700) that study the [illumination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illumination_%28lighting%29) of rooms with [mirrored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror) walls by [point light sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_light_source).

:::

## Original formulation {#Original_formulation}

The original formulation was attributed to [Ernst Straus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Straus) in the 1950s and has been resolved. Straus asked whether a room with mirrored walls can always be illuminated by a single point light source, allowing for repeated reflection of light off the mirrored walls. Alternatively, the question can be stated as asking that if a [billiard table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billiard_table) can be constructed in any required shape, is there a shape possible such that there is a point where it is impossible to hit the [billiard ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cue_ball) at another point, assuming the ball is point-like and continues infinitely rather than stopping due to [friction](wikipedia://en/11062).

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Penrose unilluminable room"}

## Penrose unilluminable room {#Penrose_unilluminable_room}

The original problem was first solved in 1958 by [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193) using ellipses to form the **Penrose unilluminable room**. He showed that there exists a room with curved walls that must always have dark regions if lit only by a single point source.

:::

## Polygonal rooms {#Polygonal_rooms}

*Image: Tokarsky\_Castro\_illumination\_problem.svg.* Solutions to the illumination problem by George W. Tokarsky (26 sides) and David Castro (24 sides)

This problem was also solved for [polygonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygonal) rooms by George Tokarsky in 1995 for 2 and 3 dimensions, which showed that there exists an unilluminable polygonal 26-sided room with a "dark spot" which is not illuminated from another point in the room, even allowing for repeated reflections.[^o6] These were rare cases, when a finite number of dark *points* (rather than regions) are unilluminable only from a fixed position of the point source.

In 1995, Tokarsky found the first polygonal unilluminable room which had 4 sides and two fixed boundary points.[^o7] He also in 1996 found a 20-sided unilluminable room with two distinct interior points. In 1997, two different 24-sided rooms with the same properties were put forward by George Tokarsky and David Castro separately.[^o8][^o9]

In 2016, Samuel Lelièvre, Thierry Monteil, and Barak Weiss showed that a light source in a polygonal room whose angles (in degrees) are all rational numbers will illuminate the entire polygon, with the possible exception of a finite number of points.[^o10] In 2019 this was strengthened by Amit Wolecki who showed that for each such polygon, the number of pairs of points which do not illuminate each other is finite.[^o11]
