# Scattering generator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Scattering generator"}

In [scattering theory](wikipedia://en/164483#Theoretical_physics), the **scattering generator** or ***S*-generator** is an effective [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) that directly generates the [interaction-picture](wikipedia://en/901382) time evolution from far past to far future.

:::

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), the scattering generator is the log of the [*S*-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053): $\hat{\Omega} = i\hbar \log\hat{S}$. While the concept traces back to '80s, an active revival has taken place in the modern literature[^o2][^o3] on scattering theory and its applications. In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), the classical scattering generator describes the Hamiltonian generator of the *S*-symplectomorphism, which is the classical analog of the [*S*-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053). The quantum and classical scattering generators are related by the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit) by a precise correspondence.[^o4]

## History and Motivation {#History_and_Motivation}

### Exponential representation of *S*-matrix {#Exponential_representation_of_S-matrix}

The idea of taking the log of *S*-matrix traces back to early days of [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267)[^o7][^o8] and has been known as the [exponential representation](wikipedia://en/44612290) of [*S*-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053), which refers to the formula[^o9]

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{S} \,=\, \exp\bigg(\frac{1}{i\hbar}\, \hat{\Omega}\bigg) \,. \end{aligned}$$

### Exponential representation as unit-time flow {#Exponential_representation_as_unit-time_flow}

Modern literature interprets $\hat{\Omega}$ as the unit-time generator of scattering,[^o11][^o12] while prototypical observations trace back to the '80s. The above formula is viewed as an effective [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) through dimensionless unit time:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{S} \,=\, \exp\bigg(\frac{(\text{“}\mathit{\Delta}t = 1\text{”})}{i\hbar}\, \hat{\Omega}\bigg) \,. \end{aligned}$$

This means that the result of scattering $\hat{S}$ is directly reproduced within "one second" ($\mathit{\Delta}t = 1$) by taking $\hat{\Omega}$ as the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381).[^o15] In this sense, $\hat{\Omega}$ is viewed as an "effective Hamiltonian" that encapsulates and summarizes the entire history of scattering from $t=-\infty$ to $t=+\infty$.

### Manifest unitarity {#Manifest_unitarity}

The motivation behind the exponential representation is manifest [unitarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarity) in scattering, i.e., trivializing the conservation of probability. Provided hermiticity $\hat{\Omega}^\dagger = \hat{\Omega}$,the *S*-matrix is automatically unitary as

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{S}^\dagger \hat{S} \,=\, \exp\bigg({-\frac{1}{i\hbar}\, \hat{\Omega}}\bigg) \exp\bigg(\frac{1}{i\hbar}\, \hat{\Omega}\bigg) \,=\, \hat{1} \,. \end{aligned}$$

This also applies for the classical scattering generator as well, the conservation of classical probability in the sense of [Liouville theorem](wikipedia://en/312301).[^o15]

## Definition by Magnus Expansion {#Definition_by_Magnus_Expansion}

The [*S*-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) is concretely defined and computed by the [Dyson series](wikipedia://en/1409609), which expands a [time-ordered exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-ordered_exponential).The scattering generator is concretely defined and computed by the [Magnus series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_series).[^o17]

### In quantum mechanics {#In_quantum_mechanics}

For the quantum scattering generator $\hat{\Omega}$, the Magnus series formula reads[^o17]

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\Omega} \,=\, {}&{} \int dt_1\,\, \hat{V}_\text{I}(t_1) + \frac{1}{2(i\hbar)^1} \int_{t_1>t_2} d^2t\,\, [\hat V_\text{I}(t_1),\hat V_\text{I}(t_2)] \\ {}&{} + \frac{1}{6(i\hbar)^2} \int_{t_1>t_2>t_3} d^3t\,\, \Big(\,{ [ \hat V_\text{I}(t_1) , [ \hat V_\text{I}(t_2) , \hat V_\text{I}(t_3) ] ] + [ \hat V_\text{I}(t_3) , [ \hat V_\text{I}(t_2) , \hat V_\text{I}(t_1) ] ] }\,\Big) + \cdots \,. \end{aligned}$$

This describes a sum of integrals whose integrands are nested commutators between the [interaction-picture](wikipedia://en/901382) potential $\hat{V}_\mathrm{I}(t)$ at different times. Provided the free and interaction Hamiltonians are Hermitian, the scattering generator $\hat{\Omega}$ is also a Hermitian operator.

### In classical mechanics {#In_classical_mechanics}

For the classical scattering generator $\Omega$, the Magnus series formula reads

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega \,=\, {}&{} \int dt_1\,\, V_\text{I}(t_1) + \frac{1}{2} \int_{t_1>t_2} d^2t\,\, \{V_\text{I}(t_1),V_\text{I}(t_2)\} \\ {}&{} + \frac{1}{6} \int_{t_1>t_2>t_3} d^3t\,\, \Big(\,{ \{ V_\text{I}(t_1) , \{ V_\text{I}(t_2) , V_\text{I}(t_3) \} \} + \{ V_\text{I}(t_3) , \{ V_\text{I}(t_2) , V_\text{I}(t_1) \} \} }\,\Big) + \cdots \,, \end{aligned}$$

which can be deduced by taking the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit) to the above quantum formula n spirit of the [correspondence principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_principle) and [canonical quantization](wikipedia://en/869590#Classical_and_quantum_brackets).[^o17] This assumes a [Hamiltonian system](wikipedia://en/198319) defined on a [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) equipped with a [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340). $\Omega$ is a function on the phase space. $V_\text{I}(t)$ is a time-dependent function on the phase space, encoding the classical interaction Hamiltonian in the [interaction picture](wikipedia://en/901382).

More precisely, the frameworks of [phase space formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space_formulation) and [deformation quantization](wikipedia://en/4278245) have been employed to establish the relationship between $\hat{\Omega}$ and $\Omega$ in a rigorous fashion.[^o4] The classical scattering generator is well-defined on [Poisson manifolds](wikipedia://en/882902).[^o15]

The *S*-symplectomorphism $S$, i.e., the canonical transformation from the initial phase space to the final phase space in classical scattering, arises by exponentiating the [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270) of $\Omega$.

## Use {#Use}

### In quantum mechanics {#In_quantum_mechanics}

In the [interaction picture](wikipedia://en/901382), the [quantum Liouville equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Liouville_equation) reads

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\, \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(t) \,=\, \frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[ \hat{V}_\mathrm{I}(t), \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(t) ] \,, \end{aligned}$$

where $\hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(t)$ is the [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) in the [interaction picture](wikipedia://en/901382). Solving this equation gives rise to the [*S*-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) $\hat{S}$ as

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(+\infty) \,=\, \hat{S}\, \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty)\, \hat{S}{}^{-1} \,=\, \mathrm{Ad}_{\hat{S}}\, \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty) \,, \end{aligned}$$

in terms of the [adjoint action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_action) $\mathrm{Ad}_{\hat{S}}$. The formula $\hat{S} = e^{\hat{\Omega}/i\hbar}$ then implies

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(+\infty) \,&=\, \exp\Big(\,{ \mathrm{ad}_{\hat{\Omega}/i\hbar} }\,\Big)\, \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty) \,,\\ \,&=\, \exp\bigg(\,{ \frac{1}{i\hbar}\, [ \hat{\Omega} , \,\,\, ] }\,\bigg)\, \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty) \,,\\ \,&=\, \hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty) + \frac{1}{i\hbar}\, [\hat{\Omega},\hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty)] + \frac{1}{2!(i\hbar)^2}\, [\hat{\Omega},[\hat{\Omega},\hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty)]] + \cdots \,, \end{aligned}$$

which describes a sum of nested commutators.

This describes that the entire time evolution of the quantum ([ensemble](wikipedia://en/59052)) state from $\hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty)$ to $\hat{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(+\infty)$ is reproduced by a unit-time, [exponentiated](wikipedia://en/44612290) [adjoint action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_action) of the scattering generator $\hat{\Omega}$.

### In classical mechanics {#In_classical_mechanics}

In classical [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319), an analogous formula holds for the classical probability [distribution](wikipedia://en/365876) $\rho(t)$, representing a [statistical ensemble](wikipedia://en/59052) and evolving under the classical [Liouville equation](wikipedia://en/312301#Liouville_equation):[^o4]

$$\begin{aligned} \rho_\mathrm{I}(+\infty) \,&=\, \exp\Big(\,{ \{ \Omega , \,\,\, \} }\,\Big)\, {\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty) \,,\\ \,&=\, {\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty) + \{{\Omega},{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty)\} + \frac{1}{2!}\, \{{\Omega},\{{\Omega},{\rho}_\mathrm{I}(-\infty)\}\} + \cdots \,. \end{aligned}$$

This is the nested Poisson bracket formula in the [*S*-symplectomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-symplectomorphism) framework.

### Impulse formula {#Impulse_formula}

In quantum mechanics, the expectation value of an observable $\hat{\mathcal{O}}$ with respect to the density matrix $\hat{\rho}_\text{I}(t)$ in the interaction picture is

$$\begin{aligned} \big\langle \hat{\mathcal{O}} \big\rangle_t \,=\, \mathrm{Tr}\big(\,\hat{\rho}_\text{I}(t)\,\hat{\mathcal{O}}\,\big) \,. \end{aligned}$$

The impulse of observables in quantum scattering is

$$\begin{aligned} \big\langle \hat\mathcal{O} \big\rangle_{+\infty} - \big\langle \hat\mathcal{O} \big\rangle_{-\infty} \,&=\, e^{[\,\,\,\,,\hat{\Omega}/i\hbar]} \hat\mathcal{O} -\hat\mathcal{O} \,=\, \frac{1}{i\hbar}\, [\hat\mathcal{O},\hat{\Omega}] + \frac{1}{2!(i\hbar)^2}\, [[\hat\mathcal{O},\hat{\Omega}],\hat{\Omega}] + \cdots \,. \end{aligned}$$

In classical mechanics, the expectation value of an observable $\mathcal{O}$ with respect to the probability distribution $\rho_\text{I}(t)$ in the interaction picture is

$$\begin{aligned} \big\langle \mathcal{O} \big\rangle_t \,=\, \int \rho_\text{I}(t)\, \mathcal{O}\,\, \mu \,, \end{aligned}$$

where $\mu$ is the Liouville measure. The impulse of observables in classical scattering is then

$$\begin{aligned} \big\langle \mathcal{O} \big\rangle_{+\infty} - \big\langle \mathcal{O} \big\rangle_{-\infty} \,&=\, e^{\{ \,\,\, , \Omega \}} \mathcal{O} - \mathcal{O} \,=\, \{\mathcal{O},{\Omega}\} + \frac{1}{2!}\, \{\{\mathcal{O},{\Omega}\},{\Omega}\} + \cdots \,. \end{aligned}$$

In both cases, the scattering generator acts on the observables as a Hamiltonian (thus in terms of right adjoint action). Note that the classical impulse formula also follows by implementing the pullback by the inverse of the [*S*-symplectomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-symplectomorphism), which maps functions on the final phase space to functions on the initial phase space.[^o4][^o17]

## Conventions and Terminology {#Conventions_and_Terminology}

Another prominent convention for the scattering generator uses the definition $\hat{N} = -i\hbar \log\hat{S}$ and the name "*N*-matrix."[^o9][^o27] Technically speaking, the scattering generator should be distinguished from the [Magnusian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnusian),[^o28] which is a broader term that can refer to the logarithm of time-evolution maps (not necessarily in scattering). A clarification has been made between the classical scattering generator, the eikonal phase, and the on-shell action.[^o28]
