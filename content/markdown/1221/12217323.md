# Bounded deformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bounded deformation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a function of **bounded deformation** is a function whose [distributional derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative) are not quite [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved)-enough to qualify as functions of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673), although the symmetric part of the derivative matrix does meet that condition. Thought of as deformations of [elasto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29)-[plastic](wikipedia://en/240123) bodies, functions of bounded deformation play a major role in the mathematical study of [materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science), e.g. the Francfort-Marigo model of [brittle crack evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture).

:::

More precisely, given an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) Ω of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, a function *u* : Ω → **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is said to be of **bounded deformation** if the [symmetrized gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetrized_gradient) *ε*(*u*) of *u*,

$$\varepsilon(u) = \frac{\nabla u + \nabla u^{\top}}{2}$$

is a [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509), [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) *n* × *n* matrix-valued [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933). The collection of all functions of bounded deformation is denoted BD(Ω; **R**<sup>*n*</sup>), or simply BD, introduced essentially by P.-M. Suquet in 1978. BD is a strictly larger space than the space BV of functions of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673).

One can show that if *u* is of bounded deformation then the measure *ε*(*u*) can be decomposed into three parts: one [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous) with respect to [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), denoted *e*(*u*) d*x*; a jump part, [supported](wikipedia://en/6591796) on a rectifiable (*n* − 1)-dimensional set *J*<sub>*u*</sub> of points where *u* has two different approximate limits *u*<sub>\+</sub> and *u*<sub>−</sub>, together with a [normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector) *ν*<sub>*u*</sub>; and a "[Cantor part](wikipedia://en/6172)", which vanishes on Borel sets of finite *H*<sup>*n*−1</sup>-measure (where *H*<sup>*k*</sup> denotes *k*-dimensional [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109)).

A function *u* is said to be of **special bounded deformation** if the Cantor part of *ε*(*u*) vanishes, so that the measure can be written as

$$\varepsilon(u) = e(u) \, \mathrm{d} x + \big( u_{+}(x) - u_{-}(x) \big) \odot \nu_{u} (x) H^{n - 1} | J_{u},$$

where *H*<sup>*n*−1</sup> | *J*<sub>*u*</sub> denotes *H*<sup>*n*−1</sup> on the jump set *J*<sub>*u*</sub> and $\odot$ denotes the symmetrized [dyadic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyadic_product):

$$a \odot b = \frac{a \otimes b + b \otimes a}{2}.$$

The collection of all functions of special bounded deformation is denoted SBD(Ω; **R**<sup>*n*</sup>), or simply SBD.
