# Demographics of Europe

*Image: European countries by population (2025).svg.* European countries by population in 2025: \#501628 \#78213d \#a42d53 \#cc4771 \#d87393 \#e9afc1 \#f4d7e0

*Image: Demographics of Europe.svg.* [Population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth) in 2021

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Demographics of Europe"}

Figures for the **population of Europe** vary according to the particular [definition of Europe's boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe#Definition). In 2018, Europe had a total population of over 745 million people.ref Around 448 million of them lived in the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union) and around 110 million in [European Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Russia); Russia is the [most populous country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_European_countries_by_population) in Europe.

:::

Europe's [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth) is low, and its [median age](wikipedia://en/989011) high. Most of Europe is in a mode of [sub-replacement fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-replacement_fertility), which means that each new(-born) generation is less populous than the one before.[^o2] Nonetheless, most West European countries still have growing populations, mainly due to [immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration) within Europe and from outside Europe and some due to increases in [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669) and [population momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_momentum). Some current and past factors in European demography have included [emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emigration), [ethnic relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_relations), [economic immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration#Why_do_people_immigrate?), a declining [birth rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_rate) and an [ageing population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ageing_of_Europe).

## History {#History}

### Prehistory {#Prehistory}

Approximately 5,000–130,000 people lived in Europe during the [Last Glacial Maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Maximum) about 20,000 years ago.[^o5][^o6]

According to Volker Heyd, an archaeologist at the [University of Helsinki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Helsinki), up to 7 million people lived in [Neolithic Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Europe) in 3000 BC.[^o7]

According to archaeologist Johannes Müller, the European population was about 1 million around 6500 BC, but increased to 8 million in 2000 BC.[^o8]

## Population {#Population}

*Image: Europe population pyramid 2026.png.* Population pyramid of Europe in 2026 based on the collective [United Nations geoscheme for Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_geoscheme_for_Europe)

*Image: Europe population pyramid 1950-2026.gif.* Europe population pyramid from 1950 to 2026

330,000,000 people lived in Europe in 1916.[^o43] In 1950 there were 549,000,000.[^o44] The population of Europe in 2015 was estimated to be 741 million according to the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations),[^o44] which was slightly less than 11% of the [world population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population). The precise figure depends on the exact definition of the geographic extent of [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe). The population of the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union) (EU) was 509 million as of 2015.[^o45] Non-EU countries situated in Europe in their entirety[^o46] account for another 90 million. Five transcontinental countries[^o47] have a total of 247 million people, of which about half reside in Europe proper.

As it stands now, around 10% of the world's people live in Europe. If demographic trends keep their pace, its share may fall to around 7% in 2050, but still amounting to 716 million people in absolute numbers, according to the United Nations estimate.[^o44] (The decline in the [percentage](wikipedia://en/64493) is partly due to high [fertility rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_rate) in Africa and South America.) The sub-replacement fertility and high life expectancy in most European states mean a declining and aging population.

### Population growth {#Population_growth}

*Image: 2001- Fertility rate in the European Union.svg.* EU fertility has declined in the 2020s.[^o49]

According to Eurostat, the average birth rate in the European Union was 1.5 children per woman in 2020. The EU countries with the highest rates were France (1.83 live births per woman), Romania (1.80) and Czechia (1.71). The lowest rates were found in Malta (1.13), Spain (1.19) and Italy (1.24).[^o50]

The reasons that Italian citizens give for not having children are economic costs, fear of losing their jobs, and lack of services for families.[^o51]

Eurostat says that the proportion of children born to foreign mothers, including both from other EU member states and from non-EU countries, has been increasing in the EU since 2013 and stood at 21% in 2020.[^o50]

### Age {#Age}

*Image: Life expectancy map -Europe -2021 -with names.png.* [Life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669) in Europe in 2021

Mirroring their mostly sub-replacement fertility and high [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669), European countries tend to have older populations overall. They had nine of the top ten highest median ages in national populations in 2005. Only [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) had an older population.[^o53] Population pyramids by country (mostly 2020 and 2023 unless stated otherwise)

## Religion {#Religion}

*Image: Largest (non-)religious group by EU member state - Eurobarometer 2019.svg.* Largest (non-)religious group by EU member state according to Eurobarometer survey 2019.[^o64] \#0000FF \#2A7FFF \#AACCFF \#7F2AFF \#800000 \#FFCC00 \#FFEEAA \#C8B7B7

*Image: Second largest religion in European countries.png.* Second largest religion in European countries

Over the last several centuries, religious practice has been on the decline in a process of [secularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secularization). Several European countries have experienced a decline in church attendance as well as a decline in the number of people professing a religious belief. The 2010 [Eurobarometer](wikipedia://en/357317) survey found that, on average, 51% of the citizens of the European Union that they believe there is a God, 26% believe there is some sort of spirit or life force and 20% don't believe there is any sort of spirit, God or life force. 3% declined to answer.[^o65] The Eurobarometer survey must be taken with caution, however, as there are discrepancies between it and national census results. For example, in the United Kingdom, the 2001 census revealed that over 70% of the population regarded themselves as "Christians" with only 15% professing to have no religion, though the wording of the question has been criticized as "misleading" by the [British Humanist Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Humanist_Association).[^o66] The 2011 census showed a dramatic reduction to less than 60% of the population regarding themselves as "Christians".[^o67]

Despite its decline, [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) is still the largest religion in Europe. According to a survey published in 2010, 76.2% of Europeans identified themselves as Christians.[^o68][^o69] [Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholics) were the largest Christian group in Europe, accounting for more than 48% of European Christians.[^o70] The second-largest Christian group in Europe was the [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church), who made up 32% of European Christians.[^o70] And about 19% of European Christians were part of the [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) tradition. Europe constitutes in absolute terms the [world's largest Christian population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_by_country).[^o71] According to Scholars, in 2017, Europe's population was 77.8% Christian (up from 74.9% 1970),[^o72][^o73] these changes were largely result of the [collapse of Communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_Communism) and [switching to Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convert_to_Christianity) in the former Soviet Union and [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc) countries.[^o72]

According to a 2003 study,[^o74] 47% of French people declared themselves as agnostics in 2003. This situation is often called "[Post-Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Christian) Europe". A decrease in religiousness and church attendance in western Europe (especially in [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium), the [Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic), [Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finland), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany), the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway), the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands) and [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden)) has been noted. According to a survey published in 2012, [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheists) and [agnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnostics) make up about 18.2% of the European population.[^o75] According to the same survey the [religiously unaffiliated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreligion) make up the majority of the population only in two [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) countries: [Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic) (75%) and [Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonia) (60%).[^o75]

According to another survey about Religiosity in the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union) from 2012 by Eurobarometer, [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) was the largest religion in the Union (accounting for 72% of the total population), [Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholics) were with 48% the largest [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christians) group in the Union, [Protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant) made up 12%, [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church) made up 8% and other Christians accounted for 4% of the total population.[^o76] [Non-believers/agnostics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnostic) accounted for 16%, [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheists) accounted for 7% and [Muslims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims) accounted for 2%.[^o77]

## Ethnicities {#Ethnicities}

Pan and Pfeil (2004) count 87 distinct "**peoples of Europe**", of which 33 form the majority population in at least one sovereign state, while the remaining 54 constitute [ethnic minorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_minority). The total number of national minority populations in Europe is estimated at 105 million people, or 14% of 770 million Europeans. (including Europeans in Siberia)[^o79]

The largest ethnic groups are the [Russians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russians), with 117 million, and the [Germans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans), with 72 million. In some countries such as the United Kingdom, France and Spain, the designation of [nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationality) may controversially take on [ethnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnicity) aspects, subsuming smaller ethnic groups such as [Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_people), [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_people), [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_people), [Northern Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Irish), [Bretons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breton_people), [Catalans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_people), and [Basques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_people), making it difficult to quantify a "British" or "French" ethnicity, for example.

There are an estimated 10 million [Romani people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people) in Europe.[^o80]

## Language {#Language}

*Image: Rectified\_Languages\_of\_Europe\_map.png.* Map of Europe showing the major languages

Most of the languages of Europe belong to the [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages) [language family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_family). This family is divided into a number of branches, including [Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages), [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages), [Baltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_languages), [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_languages), [Albanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_language), [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_language), [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language) and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language). The [Uralic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uralic_languages), which include [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language), [Finnish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_language), [Estonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_language), [Udmurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Udmurt_language), [Mordvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordvin_language) and [Sami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sami_language) also have a significant presence in Europe. The [Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkic_languages) family also has several European members, while the [North Caucasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Caucasian_languages) and [Kartvelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kartvelian_languages) families which include [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_language), [Circassian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassian_language), [Chechen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chechen_language) and [Abkhaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abkhaz_language) anong others are important in the southeastern extremity of geographical Europe. The [Basque language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language) of the western [Pyrenees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrenees) is an [isolate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_isolate) unrelated to any other group, while [Maltese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltese_language) is the only [Semitic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_language) in Europe with national language status, although [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language), [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) and [Neo-Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Aramaic) dialects are spoken by migrant populations. The [Kalmyk language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmyk_language), is a [Mongolic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolic_languages), spoken in [Kalmykia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalmykia), located directly north of the [North Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Caucasus) in [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe). The most spoken language of Europe is [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language), which belongs to the group of Slavic languages.

Languages that are not official state languages are protected in many European countries by the [European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Charter_for_Regional_or_Minority_Languages). These can include languages spoken by relatively many people, such as [Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_language) and [Basque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basque_language) in Spain, [Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisian_language) in the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands), as well as languages spoken by relatively few such as [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_language), [Manx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manx_language) and [Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic) in the United Kingdom, and [Romansch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romansch_language) in [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland).

## Genetic origins {#Genetic_origins}

*Image: Yamnaya Steppe Pastoralists.jpg.* Bronze Age spread of [Yamnaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yamnaya_culture) [Steppe pastoralist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Steppe_Herders) ancestry. The Yamnaya culture is identified with the late [Proto-Indo-Europeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Europeans).

*[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens)* appeared in Europe roughly 40,000 years ago, with the settlement of the [Cro-Magnons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cro-Magnon), followed by European hunter-gatherers and [Early European Farmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_European_Farmers) (EEF). Over the prehistoric period there was continuous settlement in Europe, notably by the immediate descendants of the [Proto-Indo-Europeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Europeans) who migrated west after the advent of the [Neolithic Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution).[^o83]

### Mitochondrial DNA and Y-chromosome DNA {#Mitochondrial_DNA_and_Y-chromosome_DNA}

Studies of [mitochondrial DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA) (mtDNA) have suggested substantial genetic homogeneity of European populations,[^o85] with only a few geographic or linguistic isolates appearing to be genetic isolates as well.[^o86] On the other hand, analyses of the [Y chromosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y_chromosome)[^o87][^o88] and of autosomal diversity[^o89] have shown a general gradient of genetic similarity running from the southeast to the northwest of the continent.

### Autosomal DNA {#Autosomal_DNA}

*Image: Genetic Diversity in Asia Fig 2-B.jpg.* Population genomic [PCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_Component_Analysis) map, showing Europeans (CEU) among other sampled Eurasian populations[^o91]

According to geneticist [David Reich](wikipedia://en/6133800), based on [ancient human genomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_DNA) that his laboratory sequenced in 2016, Europeans formed from four West-Eurasian ancestral components in varying degrees: [Western Hunter-Gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Hunter-Gatherer) (WHG), [Eastern Hunter-Gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Hunter-Gatherer) (EHG), [Neolithic Levant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistory_of_the_Levant#Neolithic_period) farmers and [Neolithic Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus_hunter-gatherer) farmers respectively.[^o92]

### Population structure {#Population_structure}

A study in May 2009[^o94] that examined 19 populations from Europe using 270,000 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) highlighted the genetic diversity of European populations corresponding to the northwest to southeast gradient and distinguished "several distinct regions" within Europe:

- [Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finland)
- the [Baltic states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_states) ([Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonia), [Latvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvia) and [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuania)), western [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) and [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland)
- Central and Western Europe
- [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy), South-Eastern Europe, Southern Russia

In this study, Fst ([fixation index](wikipedia://en/4196163)) was found to correlate considerably with geographic distances ranging from ≤0.0010 for neighbouring populations to 0.0230 for [Southern Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Italy) and [Finland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finland). For comparisons, pair-wise Fst of non-European samples were as follows: Europeans – Yoruba (West Africans) 0.1530; Europeans – Chinese 0.1100; Yoruba (West Africans) – Chinese 0.1900.[^o94]p.
