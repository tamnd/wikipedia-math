# Brianchon's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brianchon's theorem"}

*Image: Brianchon-6-tangents.svg.* solid black 2px solid blue 2px solid purple 1px

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Brianchon's theorem** is a theorem stating that when a [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) is [circumscribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribed) around a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673), its principal [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) (those connecting opposite vertices) meet in a single point. It is named after [Charles Julien Brianchon](wikipedia://en/1402609) (1783–1864).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Formal statement"}

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

Let $P_{1}P_{2}P_{3}P_{4}P_{5}P_{6}$ be a [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) formed by six [tangent lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) of a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673). Then lines $\overline{P_{1}P_{4}}$, $\overline{P_{2}P_{5}}$, $\overline{P_{3}P_{6}}$ (extended diagonals each connecting opposite vertices) intersect at a single [point](wikipedia://en/593693) $B$, the **Brianchon point**.[^o4][^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Connection to Pascal's theorem"}

## Connection to Pascal's theorem {#Connection_to_Pascal's_theorem}

The [polar reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_reciprocation) and [projective dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_dual) of this theorem give [Pascal's theorem](wikipedia://en/699966).

:::

## Degenerations {#Degenerations}

*Image: Brianchon-3-tangents.svg.* 3-tangents degeneration of Brianchon's theorem

As for Pascal's theorem there exist *degenerations* for Brianchon's theorem, too: Let coincide two neighbored tangents. Their point of intersection becomes a point of the conic. In the diagram three pairs of neighbored tangents coincide. This procedure results in a statement on [inellipses](wikipedia://en/43943006) of triangles. From a projective point of view the two triangles $P_{1}P_{3}P_{5}$ and $P_{2}P_{4}P_{6}$ lie perspectively with center $B$. That means there exists a central collineation, which maps the one onto the other triangle. But only in special cases this collineation is an affine scaling. For example for a [Steiner inellipse](wikipedia://en/17185864), where the Brianchon point is the centroid.

## In the affine plane {#In_the_affine_plane}

Brianchon's theorem is true in both the [affine plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) and the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325). However, its statement in the affine plane is in a sense less informative and more complicated than that in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). Consider, for example, five tangent lines to a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231). These may be considered sides of a hexagon whose sixth side is the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142), but there is no line at infinity in the affine plane. In two instances, a line from a (non-existent) vertex to the opposite vertex would be a line *parallel to* one of the five tangent lines. Brianchon's theorem stated only for the affine plane would therefore have to be stated differently in such a situation.

The projective dual of Brianchon's theorem has exceptions in the affine plane but not in the projective plane.

::: {#o11 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Brianchon's theorem can be proved by the idea of [radical axis](wikipedia://en/2093844) or reciprocation. To prove it take an arbitrary length ($\overline{MN}$) and carry it on the tangents starting from the contact points: $\overline{PL} = \overline{RJ} = \overline{QH} = \overline{MN}$ etc. Draw circles $a$, $b$, $c$ tangent to opposite sides of the hexagon at the created points $(H, W)$, $(J, V)$ and $(L, Y)$ respectively. One sees easily that the concurring lines coincide with the radical axes $ab$, $bc$, $ca$ respectively, of the three circles taken in pairs. Thus $O$ coincides with the radical center of these three circles.

The theorem takes particular forms in the case of circumscriptible pentagons e.g. when $R$ and $Q$ tend to coincide with $F$, a case where $AFE$ is transformed to the tangent at $F$. Then, taking a further similar identification of points $T$, $C$ and $U$, we obtain a corresponding theorem for quadrangles.

:::
