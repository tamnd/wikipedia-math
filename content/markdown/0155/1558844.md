# Fast wavelet transform

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fast wavelet transform"}

The **fast wavelet transform** is a [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) designed to turn a [waveform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveform) or signal in the [time domain](wikipedia://en/173419) into a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of coefficients based on an [orthogonal basis](wikipedia://en/755400) of small finite waves, or [wavelets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelets). The transform can be easily extended to multidimensional signals, such as images, where the time domain is replaced with the space domain. This algorithm was introduced in 1989 by [Stéphane Mallat](wikipedia://en/724496).[^o2]

:::

It has as theoretical foundation the device of a finitely generated, orthogonal [multiresolution analysis](wikipedia://en/1963076) (MRA). In the terms given there, one selects a sampling scale *J* with [sampling rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_rate) of 2<sup>*J*</sup> per unit interval, and projects the given signal *f* onto the space $V_J$; in theory by computing the [scalar products](wikipedia://en/157093)

$$s^{(J)}_n:=2^J \langle f(t),\varphi(2^J t-n) \rangle,$$

where $\varphi$ is the [scaling function](wikipedia://en/50903#Scaling_function) of the chosen wavelet transform; in practice by any suitable sampling procedure under the condition that the signal is highly oversampled, so

$$P_J[f](x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} s^{(J)}_n\,\varphi(2^Jx-n)$$

is the [orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) or at least some good approximation of the original signal in $V_J$.

The MRA is characterised by its scaling sequence

$a=(a_{-N},\dots,a_0,\dots,a_N)$ or, as [Z-transform](wikipedia://en/171589), $a(z)=\sum_{n=-N}^Na_nz^{-n}$

and its wavelet sequence

$b=(b_{-N},\dots,b_0,\dots,b_N)$ or $b(z)=\sum_{n=-N}^Nb_nz^{-n}$

(some coefficients might be zero). Those allow to compute the wavelet coefficients $d^{(k)}_n$, at least some range *k=M,...,J-1*, without having to approximate the integrals in the corresponding scalar products. Instead, one can directly, with the help of convolution and decimation operators, compute those coefficients from the first approximation $s^{(J)}$.

## Forward DWT {#Forward_DWT}

For the [discrete wavelet transform](wikipedia://en/857564) (DWT), one computes [recursively](wikipedia://en/25407), starting with the coefficient sequence $s^{(J)}$ and counting down from *k = J − 1* to some *M \< J*,

*Image: Wavelets\_-\_DWT.png.* single application of a wavelet filter bank, with filters g=a<sup>\*</sup>, h=b<sup>\*</sup>

$s^{(k)}_n:=\frac12 \sum_{m=-N}^N a_m s^{(k+1)}_{2n+m}$ or $s^{(k)}(z):=(\downarrow 2)(a^*(z)\cdot s^{(k+1)}(z))$

and

$d^{(k)}_n:=\frac12 \sum_{m=-N}^N b_m s^{(k+1)}_{2n+m}$ or $d^{(k)}(z):=(\downarrow 2)(b^*(z)\cdot s^{(k+1)}(z))$,

for *k = J − 1, J − 2, ..., M* and all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$. In the Z-transform notation:

*Image: Wavelets\_-\_Filter\_Bank.png.* recursive application of the filter bank

- The [downsampling operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downsampling) $(\downarrow 2)$ reduces an infinite sequence, given by its [Z-transform](wikipedia://en/171589), which is simply a [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213), to the sequence of the coefficients with even indices, $(\downarrow 2)(c(z))=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}c_{2k}z^{-k}$.
- The starred Laurent-polynomial $a^*(z)$ denotes the [adjoint filter](wikipedia://en/7869295), it has *time-reversed* adjoint coefficients, $a^*(z)=\sum_{n=-N}^N a_{-n}^*z^{-n}$. (The adjoint of a real number being the number itself, of a complex number its conjugate, of a real matrix the transposed matrix, of a complex matrix its hermitian adjoint).
- Multiplication is polynomial multiplication, which is equivalent to the convolution of the coefficient sequences.

It follows that

$$P_k[f](x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} s^{(k)}_n\,\varphi(2^kx-n)$$

is the orthogonal projection of the original signal *f* or at least of the first approximation $P_J[f](x)$ onto the [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) $V_k$, that is, with sampling rate of 2<sup>*k*</sup> per unit interval. The difference to the first approximation is given by

$$P_J[f](x)=P_k[f](x)+D_k[f](x)+\dots+D_{J-1}[f](x),$$

where the difference or detail signals are computed from the detail coefficients as

$$D_k[f](x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} d^{(k)}_n\,\psi(2^kx-n),$$

with $\psi$ denoting the *mother wavelet* of the wavelet transform.

## Inverse DWT {#Inverse_DWT}

Given the coefficient sequence $s^{(M)}$ for some *M* \< *J* and all the difference sequences $d^{(k)}$, *k* = *M*,...,*J* − 1, one computes recursively

$s^{(k+1)}_n:=\sum_{k=-N}^N a_k s^{(k)}_{2n-k}+\sum_{k=-N}^N b_k d^{(k)}_{2n-k}$ or $s^{(k+1)}(z)=a(z)\cdot(\uparrow 2)(s^{(k)}(z))+b(z)\cdot(\uparrow 2)(d^{(k)}(z))$

for *k* = *J* − 1,*J* − 2,...,*M* and all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$. In the Z-transform notation:

- The [upsampling operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upsampling) $(\uparrow 2)$ creates zero-filled holes inside a given sequence. That is, every second element of the resulting sequence is an element of the given sequence, every other second element is zero or $(\uparrow 2)(c(z)):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}c_nz^{-2n}$. This linear operator is, in the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R})$, the adjoint to the downsampling operator $(\downarrow 2)$.
