# Countable chain condition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Countable chain condition"}

In [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) *X* is said to satisfy the **countable chain condition**, or to be **ccc**, if every [strong antichain](wikipedia://en/1550685) in *X* is [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

There are really two conditions: the *upwards* and *downwards* countable chain conditions. These are not equivalent. The countable chain condition means the downwards countable chain condition, in other words no two elements have a common lower bound.

This is called the "countable chain condition" rather than the more logical term "countable antichain condition" for historical reasons related to certain chains of [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) and [chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_%28order_theory%29) in [complete Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/1839944), where chain conditions sometimes happen to be equivalent to antichain conditions. For example, if *κ* is a [cardinal](wikipedia://en/6173), then in a [complete Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/1839944) every antichain has size less than *κ* if and only if there is no descending *κ*-sequence of elements, so chain conditions are equivalent to antichain conditions.

Partial orders and spaces satisfying the ccc are used in the statement of [Martin's axiom](wikipedia://en/1550783).

In the theory of [forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forcing_%28set_theory%29), ccc partial orders are used because forcing with any generic set over such an order preserves cardinals and [cofinalities](wikipedia://en/6693). Furthermore, the ccc property is preserved by finite support iterations (see [iterated forcing](wikipedia://en/43053125)). For more information on ccc in the context of forcing, see Forcing (set theory)#The countable chain condition.

More generally, if *κ* is a cardinal then a poset is said to satisfy the ***κ*-chain condition**, also written as *κ*-c.c., if every strong antichain has size less than *κ*. The countable chain condition is the ℵ<sub>1</sub>-chain condition.

## Examples and properties in topology {#Examples_and_properties_in_topology}

A [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is said to satisfy the countable chain condition, or **[Suslin's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Yakovlevich_Suslin) Condition**, if the partially ordered set of non-empty [open subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of *X* satisfies the countable chain condition, *i.e.* every [pairwise disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pairwise_disjoint) collection of non-empty open subsets of *X* is countable. The name originates from [Suslin's Problem](wikipedia://en/228668).

- Every [separable topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_topological_space) has the ccc. Furthermore, a [product space](wikipedia://en/24910) of an arbitrary number of separable spaces has the ccc.
- A [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) has the ccc if and only if it's separable.
- In general, a topological space with ccc need not be separable. For example, a [Cantor cube](wikipedia://en/4999981) $\{ 0, 1 \}^\kappa$ with the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) has the ccc for any cardinal $\kappa$, though *not* separable for $\kappa > \mathfrak{c}$.
- Paracompact ccc spaces are [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480).
- An example of a topological space with the ccc is the real line.
