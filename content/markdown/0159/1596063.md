# Mollifier

*Image: Mollified Illustration.svg.* A mollifier (top) in [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics%29) one. At the bottom, in red is a function with a corner (left) and sharp jump (right), and in blue is its mollified version.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mollifier"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **mollifiers** (also known as *approximations to the identity*) are particular [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function), used for example in [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) to create [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) of smooth functions approximating nonsmooth [(generalized) functions](wikipedia://en/1580246), via [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519). Intuitively, given a (generalized) function, convolving it with a mollifier "mollifies" it, that is, its sharp features are smoothed, while still remaining close to the original.[^o2]

:::

They are also known as **Friedrichs mollifiers** after [Kurt Otto Friedrichs](wikipedia://en/3626171), who introduced them.[^o3]

## Historical notes {#Historical_notes}

Mollifiers were introduced by [Kurt Otto Friedrichs](wikipedia://en/3626171) in his paper (Friedrichs 1944, 136–139), which is considered a watershed in the modern theory of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations).[^o6] The name of this mathematical object has a curious genesis, and [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365) tells the story in his commentary on that paper published in Friedrichs' "*Selecta*".[^o7] According to him, at that time, the mathematician Donald Alexander Flanders was a colleague of Friedrichs; since he liked to consult colleagues about English usage, he asked Flanders for advice on naming the smoothing operator he was using.[^o6] Flanders was a modern-day [puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan), nicknamed by his friends Moll after [Moll Flanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moll_Flanders) in recognition of his moral qualities: he suggested calling the new mathematical concept a "**mollifier**" as a pun incorporating both Flanders' nickname and the verb 'to mollify', meaning 'to smooth over' in a figurative sense.[^o8]

Previously, [Sergei Sobolev](wikipedia://en/380798) had used mollifiers in his epoch making 1938 paper,[^o9] which contains the proof of the [Sobolev embedding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobolev_embedding_theorem): Friedrichs himself acknowledged Sobolev's work on mollifiers, stating "*These mollifiers were introduced by Sobolev and the author...*".[^o10]

It must be pointed out that the term "mollifier" has undergone [linguistic drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_drift) since the time of these foundational works: Friedrichs defined as "**mollifier**" the [integral operator](wikipedia://en/2649421) whose [kernel](wikipedia://en/2649421) is one of the functions nowadays called mollifiers. However, since the properties of a linear integral operator are completely determined by its kernel, the name mollifier was inherited by the kernel itself as a result of common usage.

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Heat eqn.gif.* A function undergoing progressive mollification.

### Modern (distribution based) definition {#Modern_(distribution_based)_definition}

(Definition 1.) Let $\varphi$ be a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) on $\mathbb{R}^n$, $n \ge 1$, and put $\varphi_\epsilon(x) := \epsilon^{-n}\varphi(x / \epsilon)$ for $\epsilon > 0 \in\mathbb{R}$. Then $\varphi$ is a **mollifier** if it satisfies the following three requirements:

((1)) it is [compactly supported](wikipedia://en/381013),[^o14]

((2))$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\!\varphi(x)\mathrm{d}x=1$,

((3))$\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\varphi_\epsilon(x) = \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-n}\varphi(x / \epsilon)=\delta(x)$,

where $\delta(x)$ is the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021), and the limit must be understood as taking place in the space of Schwartz [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29). The function $\varphi$ may also satisfy further conditions of interest;[^o15] for example, if it satisfies

((4))$\varphi(x)\ge 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$,

then it is called a **positive mollifier**, and if it satisfies

((5))$\varphi(x)=\mu(|x|)$ for some [infinitely differentiable function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely_differentiable_function) $\mu:\mathbb{R}^+\to\mathbb{R}$,

then it is called a **symmetric mollifier**.

### Notes on Friedrichs' definition {#Notes_on_Friedrichs'_definition}

**Note 1**. When the theory of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) was still not widely known nor used,[^o17] property ((3)) above was formulated by saying that the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of the function *$\scriptstyle\varphi_\epsilon$* with a given function belonging to a proper [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/20598932) or [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) will [converge](wikipedia://en/285773#Metric_spaces), in the metric of that space, as *ε* → 0 to that function:[^o18] this is exactly what [Friedrichs](wikipedia://en/3626171) did.[^o19] This also clarifies why mollifiers are related to [approximate identities](wikipedia://en/949628).[^o20]

**Note 2**. As briefly pointed out in the "Historical notes" section of this entry, originally, the term "mollifier" identified the following [convolution operator](wikipedia://en/7519)[^o20][^o21]:

$$\Phi_\epsilon(f)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\varphi_\epsilon(x-y) f(y)\mathrm{d}y$$

where $\varphi_\epsilon(x)=\epsilon^{-n}\varphi(x/\epsilon)$ and *$\varphi$* is a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) satisfying the first three conditions stated above and one or more supplementary conditions as positivity and symmetry.

:::

## Concrete example {#Concrete_example}

Consider the [bump function](wikipedia://en/815799) *$\varphi$*$(x)$ of a [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) in $\mathbb{R}^n$ defined by

$$\varphi(x) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/(1-|x|^2)}/I_n& \text{ if } |x| < 1\\ 0& \text{ if } |x|\geq 1 \end{cases}$$

where the numerical constant $I_n$ ensures normalization. This function is [infinitely differentiable, non analytic](wikipedia://en/421463) with vanishing [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) for $|x| = 1$. *$\varphi$* can be therefore used as mollifier as described above: one can see that *$\varphi$*$(x)$ defines a *positive and symmetric mollifier*.[^o23]

## Properties {#Properties}

All properties of a mollifier are related to its behaviour under the operation of [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519): we list the following ones, whose proofs can be found in every text on [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).[^o26]

### Smoothing property {#Smoothing_property}

For any distribution $T$, the following family of convolutions indexed by the [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\epsilon$

$$T_\epsilon = T\ast\varphi_\epsilon$$

where $\ast$ denotes [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519), is a family of [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function).

::: {#o29 .statement cue=named-heading name="Approximation of identity"}

### Approximation of identity {#Approximation_of_identity}

For any distribution $T$, the following family of convolutions indexed by the [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\epsilon$ converges to $T$

$$\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}T_\epsilon = \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}T\ast\varphi_\epsilon=T\in D^\prime(\mathbb{R}^n)$$

:::

### Support of convolution {#Support_of_convolution}

For any distribution $T$,

$$\operatorname{supp}T_\epsilon=\operatorname{supp}(T\ast\varphi_\epsilon)\subset\operatorname{supp}T+\operatorname{supp}\varphi_\epsilon,$$

where $\operatorname{supp}$ indicates the [support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Support_of_a_distribution) in the sense of distributions, and $+$ indicates their [Minkowski addition](wikipedia://en/504105).

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

The basic application of mollifiers is to prove that properties valid for [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) are also valid in nonsmooth situations.

### Product of distributions {#Product_of_distributions}

In some theories of [generalized functions](wikipedia://en/1580246), mollifiers are used to define the [multiplication of distributions](wikipedia://en/1580246#Multiplication_of_distributions). Given two [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) $S$ and $T$, the limit of the product of the [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) obtained from one operand via mollification, with the other operand defines, when it exists, their product in various theories of [generalized functions](wikipedia://en/1580246):

$$S\cdot T := \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}S_\epsilon\cdot T=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}S\cdot T_\epsilon.$$

::: {#o35 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="\"Weak=Strong\" theorems"}

### "Weak=Strong" theorems {#"Weak=Strong"_theorems}

Mollifiers are used to prove the identity of two different kind of extension of differential operators: the strong extension and the [weak extension](wikipedia://en/3368981). The paper by Friedrichs which introduces mollifiers (Friedrichs 1944) illustrates this approach.

:::

::: {#o38 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cutoff function"}

### Smooth cutoff functions {#Smooth_cutoff_functions}

By convolution of the [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) $B_1 = \{x : |x|<1\}$ with the [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) *$\varphi_{1/2}$* (defined as in ((3)) with $\epsilon = 1/2$), one obtains the function

$$\begin{aligned} \chi_{B_1,1/2}(x) &=\chi_{B_1}\ast\varphi_{1/2}(x) \\ &=\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\!\!\!\chi_{B_1}(x-y)\varphi_{1/2}(y)\mathrm{d}y \\ &=\int_{B_{1/2}}\!\!\! \chi_{B_1}(x-y) \varphi_{1/2}(y)\mathrm{d}y \ \ \ (\because\ \mathrm{supp}(\varphi_{1/2})=B_{1/2}) \end{aligned}$$

which is a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) equal to $1$ on $B_{1/2} = \{ x: |x| < 1/2 \}$, with support contained in $B_{3/2}=\{ x: |x| < 3/2 \}$. This can be seen easily by observing that if $|x| \le 1/2$ and $|y| \le 1/2$ then $|x-y| \le 1$. Hence for $|x| \le 1/2$,

$$\int_{B_{1/2}}\!\!\!\chi_{B_1}(x-y) \varphi_{1/2}(y)\mathrm{d}y= \int_{B_{1/2}}\!\!\! \varphi_{1/2}(y)\mathrm{d}y=1.$$

One can see how this construction can be generalized to obtain a smooth function identical to one on a [neighbourhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood_%28topology%29) of a given [compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set), and equal to zero in every point whose [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from this set is greater than a given $\epsilon$.[^o39] Such a function is called a (smooth) **cutoff function**; these are used to eliminate singularities of a given ([generalized](wikipedia://en/1580246)) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) via [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845). They leave unchanged the value of the multiplicand on a given [set](wikipedia://en/26691), but modify its [support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Support_of_a_distribution). Cutoff functions are used to construct [smooth partitions of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function#Smooth_partitions_of_unity).
