# Proof procedure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proof procedure"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), and in particular [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478), a **proof procedure** for a given [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system) is a systematic method for producing [proofs](wikipedia://en/27952971) in some [proof calculus](wikipedia://en/1250665) of (provable) statements.

:::

## Types of proof calculi used {#Types_of_proof_calculi_used}

There are several types of proof calculi. The most popular are [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072), [sequent calculi](wikipedia://en/252329) (i.e., [Gentzen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentzen)-type systems), [Hilbert systems](wikipedia://en/8529655), and [semantic tableaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_tableau) or trees. A given proof procedure will target a specific proof calculus, but can often be reformulated so as to produce proofs in other proof styles.

## Completeness {#Completeness}

A proof procedure for a logic is *complete* if it produces a proof for each provable statement. The [theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem_%28logic%29) of logical systems are typically [recursively enumerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable), which implies the existence of a complete but usually extremely inefficient proof procedure; however, a proof procedure is only of interest if it is reasonably efficient.

Faced with an unprovable statement, a complete proof procedure may sometimes succeed in detecting and signalling its unprovability. In the general case, where provability is only a [semidecidable](wikipedia://en/332090) property, this is not possible, and instead the procedure will diverge (not terminate).
