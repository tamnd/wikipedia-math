# Cantor–Dedekind axiom

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cantor–Dedekind axiom"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), the **Cantor–Dedekind axiom** is the thesis that the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438#Axiomatic_approach) are order-[isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [linear continuum](wikipedia://en/17368856) of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446). In other words, the axiom states that there is a one-to-one correspondence between real numbers and points on a line.

:::

This axiom became a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) proved by [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) in his book *[Geometric Algebra](wikipedia://en/31102294)*. More precisely, [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697) defined over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) satisfy the [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) of [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), and, from the axioms of Euclidean geometry, one can construct a field that is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the real numbers.

[Analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) was developed from the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) introduced by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525). It implicitly assumed this axiom by blending the distinct concepts of real numbers and points on a line, sometimes referred to as the [real number line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number_line). Artin's proof, not only makes this blend explicitly, but also that analytic geometry is strictly equivalent with the traditional [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484), in the sense that exactly the same theorems can be proved in the two frameworks.

Another consequence is that [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920)'s proof of the [decidability of first-order theories of the real numbers](wikipedia://en/43578228) could be seen as an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) to solve any first-order problem in [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417).
