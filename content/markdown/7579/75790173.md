# Second variation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Second variation"}

In the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882), the **second variation** extends the idea of the [second derivative test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_derivative_test) to [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948).[^o2] Much like for [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), at a [stationary point](wikipedia://en/885651) where the [first derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_derivative) is zero, the second derivative determines the nature of the stationary point; it may be negative (if the point is a [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum) point), positive (if a [minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum)) or zero (if a [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249)).

:::

Via the second functional, it is possible to derive powerful [necessary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_condition) for solving variational problems, such as the [Legendre–Clebsch condition](wikipedia://en/18409176) and the [Jacobi](wikipedia://en/210340) necessary condition detailed below.[^o3]

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Much of the calculus of variations relies on the [first variation](wikipedia://en/3655074), which is a generalization of the [first derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_derivative) to a functional.[^o5] An example of a class of variational problems is to find the function $y$ which [minimizes](wikipedia://en/52033) the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532)

$$J[y] = \int_a^b f(x, y, y')dx$$

on the interval $[a, b]$; $J$ here is a functional (a [mapping](wikipedia://en/516931) which takes a function and returns a [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331)). It is known that any [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) $y$ which minimizes this functional satisfies the [Euler-Lagrange equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler-Lagrange_equation)

$$f_{y} - \frac{d}{dx} f_{y'} = 0.$$

These solutions are [stationary](wikipedia://en/885651), but there is no guarantee that they are the type of extremum desired (completely analogously to the first derivative, they may be a minimum, maximum or [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249)). A test via the second variation would ensure that the solution is a minimum.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

Take an extremum $y$. The [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of the integrand of our variational functional about a nearby point $y + \varepsilon h$ where $\varepsilon$ is small and $h$ is a smooth function which is zero at $a$ and $b$ is

$$f(x, y, y') = f(x, y, y') + \varepsilon (h f_y + h' f_{y'}) + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} (h^2 f_{yy} + 2hh' f_{yy'} + h'^2 f_{y'y'}) + O(\varepsilon^3).$$

The first term of the series is the [first variation](wikipedia://en/3655074), and the second is defined to be the second variation:

$$\delta^2J(h, y) := \int_a^b h^2 f_{yy} + 2hh' f_{yy'} + f_{y'y'} h'^2.$$

It can then be shown that $J$ has a [local](wikipedia://en/1462457) minimum at $y_0$ if it is stationary (i.e. the first variation is zero) and $\delta^2J(h, y_0) \geq 0$ for all $h$.[^o7]

## The Jacobi necessary condition {#The_Jacobi_necessary_condition}

### The accessory problem and Jacobi differential equation {#The_accessory_problem_and_Jacobi_differential_equation}

As discussed above, a minimum of the problem requires that $\delta^2J(h, y_0) \geq 0$ for all $h$; furthermore, the trivial solution $h=0$ gives $\delta^2J(h, y_0) = 0$. Thus consider $\delta^2J(h, y_0)$ can be considered as a variational problem in itself - this is called the **accessory problem** with integrand denoted $\Omega$. The Jacobi differential equation is then the Euler-Lagrange equation for this accessory problem:[^o10]

$$\Omega_h - \frac{d}{dx} \Omega_{h'} = 0.$$

### Conjugate points and the Jacobi necessary condition {#Conjugate_points_and_the_Jacobi_necessary_condition}

As well as being easier to construct than the original Euler-Lagrange equation (due $h$ and $h'$ being at most quadratic) the Jacobi equation also expresses the [conjugate points](wikipedia://en/2850652) of the original variational problem in its solutions. A point $c$ is **conjugate** to the lower boundary $a$ if there is a nontrivial solution $h$ to the Jacobi differential equation with $h(a)=h(c)=0$.

The **Jacobi necessary condition** then follows:

In particular, if $f$ satisfies the **strengthened Legendre condition** $f_{y'y'} > 0$, then $y$ is only an extremal if it has no conjugate points.[^o7]

The Jacobi necessary condition is named after [Carl Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jacobi), who first utilized the solutions for the accessory problem in his article [*Zur Theorie der Variations-Rechnung und der Differential-Gleichungen*](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1837.17.68), and the term 'accessory problem' was introduced by von Escherich.[^o12]

## An example: shortest path on a sphere {#An_example:_shortest_path_on_a_sphere}

As an example, the problem of finding a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) (shortest path) between two points on a sphere can be represented as the variational problem with functional[^o5]

$$J[y] = \int_0^b \sqrt{\cos^2y + y'^2}dx.$$

The equator of the sphere, $y=0$ minimizes this functional with $f_{y'y'} = 1 > 0$; for this problem the Jacobi differential equation is

$$h'' + h = 0$$

which has solutions $h = A\sin(x) + B\cos(x)$. If a solution satisfies $h(0)=0$, then it must have the form $h = A\sin(x)$. These functions have zeroes at $k\pi, k \in \mathbb{Z}$, and so the equator is only a solution if $b < \pi$.

This makes intuitive sense; if one draws a great circle through two points on the sphere, there are two paths between them, one longer than the other. If $b > \pi$, then we are going over halfway around the circle to get to the other point, and it would be quicker to get there in the other direction.
