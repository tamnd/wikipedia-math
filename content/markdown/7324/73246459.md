# Dividing a square into similar rectangles

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dividing a square into similar rectangles"}

*Image: Plastic square partitions.svg.* Three partitions of a square into similar rectangles

**Dividing a square into similar rectangles** (or, equivalently, **tiling a square with similar rectangles**) is a problem in mathematics.

:::

## Three rectangles {#Three_rectangles}

There is only one way ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) rotation and reflection) to divide a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) into two [similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_%28geometry%29) rectangles.

However, there are three distinct ways of partitioning a square into three similar rectangles:[^o3][^o4]

1. The trivial solution given by three congruent rectangles with [aspect ratio](wikipedia://en/1021) 3:1.
1. The solution in which two of the three rectangles are congruent and the third one has twice the side length of the other two, where the rectangles have aspect ratio 3:2.
1. The solution in which the three rectangles are all of different sizes and where they have aspect ratio *ρ*<sup>2</sup>, where *ρ* is the [plastic ratio](wikipedia://en/2148532).

The fact that a rectangle of aspect ratio *ρ*<sup>2</sup> can be used for dissections of a square into similar rectangles is equivalent to an algebraic property of the number *ρ*<sup>2</sup> related to the [Routh–Hurwitz theorem](wikipedia://en/2067581): all of its conjugates have positive real part.[^o5][^o6]

## Generalization to *n* rectangles {#Generalization_to_n_rectangles}

In 2022, the mathematician [John Baez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Baez) brought the problem of generalizing this problem to *n* rectangles to the attention of the [Mathstodon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathstodon) online mathematics community.[^o8][^o9]

The problem has two parts: what aspect ratios are possible, and how many different solutions are there for a given *n*.[^o10] Frieling and Rinne had previously published a result in 1994 that states that the aspect ratio of rectangles in these dissections must be an [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) and that each of its conjugates must have a positive real part.[^o5] However, their proof was not a constructive proof.

Numerous participants have attacked the problem of finding individual dissections using exhaustive computer search of possible solutions. One approach is to exhaustively enumerate possible coarse-grained placements of rectangles, then convert these to candidate topologies of connected rectangles. Given the topology of a potential solution, the determination of the rectangle's aspect ratio can then trivially be expressed as a set of simultaneous equations, thus either determining the solution exactly, or eliminating it from possibility.[^o11]
