# Netto's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Netto's theorem"}

*Image: Hilbert curve 3.svg.* The first three steps of construction of the [Hilbert curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_curve), a space-filling curve that by Netto's theorem has many self-intersections

*Image: Osgood curve.svg.* An [Osgood curve](wikipedia://en/26717031), with no self-intersections. By Netto's theorem it is impossible for such a curve to entirely cover any two-dimensional region.

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), **Netto's theorem** states that [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942) of [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) preserve [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398). That is, there does not exist a continuous bijection between two smooth manifolds of different dimension. It is named after [Eugen Netto](wikipedia://en/12894892).

:::

The case for maps from a higher-dimensional manifold to a one-dimensional manifold was proven by [Jacob Lüroth](wikipedia://en/33271640) in 1878, using the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884) to show that no manifold containing a topological [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) can be mapped continuously and bijectively to the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line). Both Netto in 1878, and [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) in 1879, gave faulty proofs of the general theorem. The faults were later recognized and corrected.

An important special case of this theorem concerns the non-existence of continuous bijections from one-dimensional spaces, such as the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) or [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324), to two-dimensional spaces, such as the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) or [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301). The conditions of the theorem can be relaxed in different ways to obtain interesting classes of functions from one-dimensional spaces to two-dimensional spaces:

- [Space-filling curves](wikipedia://en/352398) are [surjective continuous functions](wikipedia://en/27873) from one-dimensional spaces to two-dimensional spaces. They cover every point of the plane, or of a unit square, by the image of a line or unit interval. Examples include the [Peano curve](wikipedia://en/358330) and [Hilbert curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_curve). Neither of these examples has any self-crossings, but by Netto's theorem there are many points of the square that are covered multiple times by these curves.
- [Osgood curves](wikipedia://en/26717031) are continuous bijections from one-dimensional spaces to subsets of the plane that have nonzero [area](wikipedia://en/1209). They form [Jordan curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve) in the plane. However, by Netto's theorem, they cannot cover the entire plane, unit square, or any other [two-dimensional region](wikipedia://en/16796643).
- If one relaxes the requirement of continuity, then all smooth manifolds of bounded dimension have equal [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174), the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901). Therefore, there exist discontinuous bijections between any two of them, as [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) showed in 1878. Cantor's result came as a surprise to many mathematicians and kicked off the line of research leading to space-filling curves, Osgood curves, and Netto's theorem. A near-bijection from the unit square to the unit interval can be obtained by interleaving the digits of the decimal representations of the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate) of points in the square. The ambiguities of decimal, exemplified by the two decimal representations of 1 = [0.999...](wikipedia://en/1849799), cause this to be an [injection](wikipedia://en/45196) rather than a bijection, but this issue can be repaired by using the [Schröder–Bernstein theorem](wikipedia://en/44218028).
