# Banach–Stone theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Banach–Stone theorem"}

An important question in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) is whether a space can be completely described by the functions defined on it—that is, by its "observables." The **Banach–Stone theorem** is a classical result in this direction. It shows that certain well-behaved spaces (specifically, [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637)) can be recovered from the [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) of [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) defined on them. The theorem is named after the [mathematicians](wikipedia://en/18902) [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) and [Marshall Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Stone).

:::

In brief, the Banach–Stone theorem allows one to recover a [compact Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Hausdorff_space) *X* from the Banach space structure of the space *C*(*X*) of continuous real- or complex-valued functions on *X*. If one is allowed to invoke the algebra structure of *C*(*X*) then recovering *X* is easy – we can identify *X* with the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/918414) of *C*(*X*), the set of [algebra homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) into the field of scalars, equipped with the weak\*-topology inherited from the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) *C*(*X*)\*. What makes the Banach–Stone theorem striking is that it avoids reference to multiplicative structure by recovering *X* from the extreme points of the unit ball of *C*(*X*)\*.

Thus the Banach–Stone theorem states that if *C*(*X*) and *C*(*Y*) are isometrically isomorphic as Banach spaces, then *X* and *Y* are homeomorphic.

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

For a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) *X*, let *C*(*X*) denote the [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) of continuous real- or complex-valued [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on *X*, equipped with the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm) ‖·‖<sub>∞</sub>.

Given compact Hausdorff spaces *X* and *Y*, suppose *T* : *C*(*X*) → *C*(*Y*) is a [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873) [linear isometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isometry). Then there exists a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) *φ* : *Y* → *X* and a function *g* ∈ *C*(*Y*) with

$$| g(y) | = 1 \mbox{ for all } y \in Y$$

such that

$$(T f) (y) = g(y) f(\varphi(y)) \mbox{ for all } y \in Y, f \in C(X).$$

The case where *X* and *Y* are compact [metric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_spaces) is due to Banach,[^o4] while the extension to compact Hausdorff spaces is due to Stone.[^o5] In fact, they both prove a slight generalization—they do not assume that *T* is linear, only that it is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) in the sense of metric spaces, and use the [Mazur–Ulam theorem](wikipedia://en/3794727) to show that *T* is affine, and so $T - T(0)$ is a linear isometry.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The Banach–Stone theorem has some generalizations for vector-valued continuous functions on compact, Hausdorff topological spaces. For example, if *E* is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) with trivial [centralizer](wikipedia://en/12772705) and *X* and *Y* are compact, then every linear isometry of *C*(*X*; *E*) onto *C*(*Y*; *E*) is a [strong Banach–Stone map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_Banach–Stone_map).

A similar technique has also been used to recover a space *X* from the extreme points of the duals of some other spaces of functions on *X*.

The noncommutative analog of the Banach-Stone theorem is the folklore theorem that two unital C\*-algebras are isomorphic if and only if they are completely isometric (i.e., isometric at all matrix levels). Mere isometry is not enough, as shown by the existence of a C\*-algebra that is not isomorphic to its opposite algebra (which trivially has the same Banach space structure).
