# Nilpotent Lie algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nilpotent Lie algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{g}$ is **nilpotent** if its lower central series terminates in the zero subalgebra. The *lower central series* is the sequence of subalgebras

:::

$$\mathfrak{g} \supseteq [\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}] \supseteq [\mathfrak{g},[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]] \supseteq [\mathfrak{g},[\mathfrak{g},[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]]] \supseteq ...$$

We write $\mathfrak{g}_0 = \mathfrak{g}$, and $\mathfrak{g}_n = [\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{n-1}]$ for all $n > 0$. If the lower central series eventually arrives at the zero subalgebra, then the Lie algebra is called nilpotent. The lower central series for Lie algebras is analogous to the [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), and nilpotent Lie algebras are analogs of [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569).

The nilpotent Lie algebras are precisely those that can be obtained from abelian Lie algebras, by successive [central extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_extension_%28mathematics%29).

Note that the definition means that, viewed as a non-associative non-unital algebra, a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent if it is nilpotent as an ideal.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944). One says that $\mathfrak{g}$ is **nilpotent** if the [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) terminates, i.e. if $\mathfrak{g}_n = 0$ for some $n\in\mathbb{N}.$

Explicitly, this means that

$$[X_1, [X_2,[\cdots[X_{n}, Y]\cdots]] = \mathrm{ad}_{X_1}\mathrm{ad}_{X_2}\cdots\mathrm{ad}_{X_{n}}Y = 0$$

$$\forall X_1, X_2,\ldots, X_{n}, Y \in \mathfrak{g},\qquad(1)$$

so that $\operatorname{ad}_{X_{1}}\operatorname{ad}_{X_{2}} \cdot\cdot\cdot \operatorname{ad}_{X_{n}} = 0$.

:::

## Equivalent conditions {#Equivalent_conditions}

A very special consequence of (1) is that

$$[X, [X,[\cdots[X, Y]\cdots] = {\mathrm{ad}_X}^{n}Y \in \mathfrak{g}_n = 0 \quad \forall X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}.\qquad(2)$$

Thus $(\operatorname{ad}_{X})^{n} = 0$ for all $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. That is, $\operatorname{ad}_{X}$ is a [nilpotent endomorphism](wikipedia://en/252235) in the usual sense of linear endomorphisms (rather than of Lie algebras). We call such an element $X$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ **ad-nilpotent**.

Remarkably, if $\mathfrak{g}$ is finite dimensional, the apparently much weaker condition (2) is actually equivalent to (1), as stated by

*[Engel's theorem](wikipedia://en/1341657): A finite dimensional Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent if and only if all elements of $\mathfrak{g}$ are ad-nilpotent,*

which we will not prove here.

A somewhat easier equivalent condition for the nilpotency of $\mathfrak{g}$ : $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent if and only if $\mathrm{ad} \, \mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent (as a Lie algebra). To see this, first observe that (1) implies that $\mathrm{ad} \, \mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent, since the expansion of an $(n - 1)$-fold nested bracket will consist of terms of the form in (1). Conversely, one may write[^o5]

$$[[\cdots[X_{n},X_{n-1}],\cdots,X_2],X_1] = \mathrm{ad}[\cdots[X_{n},X_{n-1}], \cdots, X_2](X_1),$$

and since $\operatorname{ad}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism,

$$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{ad}[\cdots[X_{n},X_{n-1}], \cdots, X_2] &= [\mathrm{ad}[\cdots[X_{n},X_{n-1}],\cdots X_3], \mathrm{ad}_{X_2}]\\ &= \ldots = [\cdots[\mathrm{ad}_{X_{n}}, \mathrm{ad}_{X_{n-1}}], \cdots \mathrm{ad}_{X_2}].\end{aligned}$$

If $\mathrm{ad} \, \mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent, the last expression is zero for large enough *n*, and accordingly the first. But this implies (1), so $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent.

Also, a finite-dimensional Lie algebra is nilpotent if and only if there exists a descending chain of ideals $\mathfrak g = \mathfrak g_0 \supset \mathfrak g_1 \supset \cdots \supset \mathfrak g_n = 0$ such that $[\mathfrak g, \mathfrak g_i] \subset \mathfrak g_{i+1}$.[^o6]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Strictly upper triangular matrices {#Strictly_upper_triangular_matrices}

If $\mathfrak{gl}(k,\mathbb R)$ is the set of $k \times k$ matrices with entries in $\mathbb{R}$, then the [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) consisting of strictly [upper triangular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular_matrices) is a nilpotent Lie algebra.

### Heisenberg algebras {#Heisenberg_algebras}

A [Heisenberg algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisenberg_algebra) is nilpotent. For example, in dimension 3, the commutator of two matrices

> $$\left[ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a & b \\ 0 & 0 & c \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} , \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a' & b' \\ 0 & 0 & c' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \right] = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & a'' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

where $a'' = ac' - a'c$.

### Cartan subalgebras {#Cartan_subalgebras}

A [Cartan subalgebra](wikipedia://en/1336000) $\mathfrak{c}$ of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{l}$ is nilpotent and [self-normalizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-normalizing)[^o12] <sup>page 80</sup>. The self-normalizing condition is equivalent to being the normalizer of a Lie algebra. This means $\mathfrak{c} = N_\mathfrak{l}(\mathfrak{c}) = \{ x \in \mathfrak{l} : [x,c] \subset \mathfrak{c} \text{ for } c \in \mathfrak{c} \}$. This includes all diagonal matrices $\mathfrak{d}(n)$ in $\mathfrak{gl}(n)$.

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

If a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{g}$ has an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of prime period with no fixed points except at $0$, then $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent.[^o14]

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Nilpotent Lie algebras are solvable {#Nilpotent_Lie_algebras_are_solvable}

Every nilpotent Lie algebra is [solvable](wikipedia://en/2966308). This is useful in proving the solvability of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) since, in practice, it is usually easier to prove nilpotency (when it holds!) rather than solvability. However, in general, the converse of this property is false. For example, the subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(k,\mathbb R)$ ($k \ge 2$) consisting of upper triangular matrices, $\mathfrak{b}(k,\mathbb R)$, is solvable but not nilpotent.

### Subalgebras and images {#Subalgebras_and_images}

If a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent, then all [subalgebras](wikipedia://en/45239) and homomorphic images are nilpotent.

### Nilpotency of the quotient by the center {#Nilpotency_of_the_quotient_by_the_center}

If the [quotient algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_Lie_algebra) $\mathfrak{g}/Z(\mathfrak g)$, where $Z(\mathfrak{g})$ is the [center](wikipedia://en/342453) of $\mathfrak{g}$, is nilpotent, then so is $\mathfrak{g}$. This is to say that a central extension of a nilpotent Lie algebra by a nilpotent Lie algebra is nilpotent.

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Engel's theorem"}

### Engel's theorem {#Engel's_theorem}

[Engel's theorem](wikipedia://en/1341657): A finite dimensional Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is nilpotent if and only if all elements of $\mathfrak{g}$ are ad-nilpotent.

:::

### Zero Killing form {#Zero_Killing_form}

The [Killing form](wikipedia://en/982386) of a nilpotent Lie algebra is $0$.

### Have outer automorphisms {#Have_outer_automorphisms}

A nonzero nilpotent Lie algebra has an [outer automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_automorphism), that is, an automorphism that is not in the image of Ad.

### Derived subalgebras of solvable Lie algebras {#Derived_subalgebras_of_solvable_Lie_algebras}

The [derived subalgebra](wikipedia://en/2966308) of a finite dimensional solvable Lie algebra over a field of characteristic 0 is nilpotent.

:::
