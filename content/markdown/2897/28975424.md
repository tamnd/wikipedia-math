# Lee–Carter model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lee–Carter model"}

**The Lee–Carter model** is a numerical algorithm used in [mortality forecasting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortality_forecasting) and [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669) [forecasting](wikipedia://en/246074).[^o2] The input to the model is a matrix of age specific [mortality rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortality_rates) ordered monotonically by time, usually with ages in columns and years in rows. The output is a forecasted matrix of mortality rates in the same format as the input.

:::

The model uses [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207) (SVD) to find:

- A [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) vector $\mathbf{k}_t$ that captures 80–90% of the mortality trend (here the subscript $t$ refers to time),
- A vector $\mathbf{b}_x$ that describes the relative mortality at each age (here the subscript $x$ refers to age), and
- A scaling constant (referred to here as $s_1$ but unnamed in the literature).

$\mathbf{k}_t$ is usually linear, implying that gains to life expectancy are fairly constant year after year in most populations. Prior to computing SVD, age specific mortality rates are first transformed into $\mathbf{A}_{x,t}$, by taking their [logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithms), and then [centering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centering) them by subtracting their age-specific means over time. The age-specific mean over time is denoted by $\mathbf{a}_x$. The subscript $x,t$ refers to the fact that $\mathbf{A}_{x,t}$ spans both age and time.

Many researchers adjust the $\mathbf{k}_t$ vector by fitting it to empirical life expectancies for each year, using the $\mathbf{a}_x$ and $\mathbf{b}_x$ generated with SVD. When adjusted using this approach, changes to $\mathbf{k}_t$ are usually small.

To forecast mortality, $\mathbf{k}_t$ (either adjusted or not) is projected into $n$ future years using an [ARIMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARIMA) model. The corresponding forecasted $\mathbf{A}_{x,t+n}$ is recovered by multiplying $\mathbf{k}_{t+n}$ by $\mathbf{b}_x$ and the first diagonal element of S (when $\mathbf{U} \mathbf{S} \mathbf{V^*} = \text{svd}(\mathbf{A}_{x,t})$). The actual mortality rates are recovered by taking exponentials of this vector.

Because of the linearity of $\mathbf{k}_t$, it is generally modeled as a [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451) with trend. Life expectancy and other [life table](wikipedia://en/1614128) measures can be calculated from this forecasted matrix after adding back the means and taking exponentials to yield regular mortality rates.

In most implementations, [confidence intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_intervals) for the forecasts are generated by simulating multiple mortality forecasts using [Monte Carlo Methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_Method). A band of mortality between 5% and 95% percentiles of the simulated results is considered to be a valid forecast. These simulations are done by extending $\mathbf{k}_t$ into the future using randomization based on the [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520) of $\mathbf{k}_t$ derived from the input data.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithm"}

## Algorithm {#Algorithm}

The algorithm seeks to find the least squares solution to the equation:

$$\ln{(\mathbf{m}_{x,t})} = \mathbf{a}_x + \mathbf{b}_x \mathbf{k}_t + \epsilon_{x,t}$$

where $\mathbf{m}_{x,t}$ is a matrix of mortality rate for each age $x$ in each year $t$.

1. Compute $\mathbf{a}_x$ which is the average over time of $\ln{(\mathbf{m}_{x,t})}$ for each age:

$$\mathbf{a}_x = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\ln{(\mathbf{m}_{x,t})}}}{T}$$

1. Compute $\mathbf{A}_{x,t}$ which will be used in SVD:

$$\mathbf{A}_{x,t} = \ln{(\mathbf{m}_{x,t})} - \mathbf{a}_x$$

1. Compute the singular value decomposition of $\mathbf{A}_{x,t}$:

$$\mathbf{U} \mathbf{S} \mathbf{V^*} = \text{svd}(\mathbf{A}_{x,t})$$

1. Derive $\mathbf{b}_x$, $s_1$ (the scaling eigenvalue), and $\mathbf{k}_t$ from $\mathbf{U}$, $\mathbf{S}$, and $\mathbf{V^*}$:

$$\mathbf{b}_x = (u_{1,1}, u_{2,1}, ..., u_{x,1})$$

$$\mathbf{k}_t = (v_{1,1}, v_{1,2}, ..., v_{1,t})$$

1. Forecast $\mathbf{k}_t$ using a standard univariate [ARIMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARIMA) model to $n$ additional years:

$$\mathbf{k}_{t+n} = \text{ARIMA}(\mathbf{k}_t, n)$$

1. Use the forecasted $\mathbf{k}_{t+n}$, with the original $\mathbf{b}_x$, and $\mathbf{a}_x$ to calculate the forecasted mortality rate for each age:

$$\mathbf{m}_{x,t+n} = \exp(\mathbf{a}_x + s_1 \mathbf{k}_{t+n} \mathbf{b}_x)$$

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

Without applying SVD or some other method of [dimension reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_reduction) the table of mortality data is a highly correlated multivariate data series, and the complexity of these multidimensional time series makes them difficult to forecast. SVD has become widely used as a method of dimension reduction in many different fields, including by [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google) in their [page rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Page_rank) algorithm.

The Lee–Carter model was introduced by [Ronald D. Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_D._Lee) and Lawrence Carter in 1992 with the article "Modeling and Forecasting U.S. Mortality".[^o6] The model grew out of their work in the late 1980s and early 1990s attempting to use [inverse projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_projection) to infer rates in [historical demography](wikipedia://en/10817881).[^o7] The model has been used by the United States [Social Security Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Security_Administration), the US [Census Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Census_Bureau), and the United Nations. It has become the most widely used mortality forecasting technique in the world today.[^o8]

There have been extensions to the Lee–Carter model, most notably to account for missing years, correlated male and female populations, and large scale coherency in populations that share a mortality regime (western Europe, for example). Many related papers can be found on [Professor Ronald Lee's](http://www.ceda.berkeley.edu/People/rlee.html) website.

## Implementations {#Implementations}

There are few software packages for forecasting with the Lee–Carter model.

- [LCFIT](http://lcfit.demog.berkeley.edu) (April 18, 2022). <https://web.archive.org/web/20220418014625/http://lcfit.demog.berkeley.edu/> is a web-based package with interactive forms.
- Professor [Rob J. Hyndman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_J._Hyndman) provides an [R package for demography](https://robjhyndman.com/software/) that includes routines for creating and forecasting a Lee–Carter model.
- Alternatives in R include the [StMoMo package](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/StMoMo/vignettes/StMoMoVignette.pdf) of Villegas, Millossovich and Kaishev (2015).
- Professor German Rodriguez provides [code for the Lee–Carter Model](http://data.princeton.edu/eco572/LeeCarter.html) using [Stata](wikipedia://en/1809002).
- Using [Matlab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matlab), Professor Eric Jondeau and Professor Michael Rockinger have put together the [Longevity Toolbox](https://github.com/mrockinger/Matlab-Longevity-Toolbox/) for parameter estimation.
