# Closed graph theorem (functional analysis)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Closed graph theorem"}

In mathematics, particularly in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **closed graph theorem** is a result connecting the [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) of a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) to a topological property of their [graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_%28function%29). Precisely, the theorem states that a linear operator between two [Banach spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_spaces) is continuous [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the graph of the operator is closed (such an operator is called a [closed linear operator](wikipedia://en/64713671); see also [closed graph property](wikipedia://en/61808035)).

:::

Since an operator between two [normed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) is a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) if and only if it is a continuous linear operator, one can replace "continuous" with "bounded" in the statement above.

An important question in functional analysis is whether a given linear operator is continuous (or bounded). The closed graph theorem gives one answer to that question.

## Explanation {#Explanation}

Let $T : X \to Y$ be a linear operator between Banach spaces (or more generally Fréchet spaces). Then the continuity of $T$ means that $Tx_i \to Tx$ for each convergent sequence $x_i \to x$. On the other hand, the closedness of the graph of $T$ means that for each convergent sequence $x_i \to x$ such that $Tx_i \to y$, we have $y = Tx$. Hence, the closed graph theorem says that in order to check the continuity of $T$, one can show $T x_i \to Tx$ under the additional assumption that $Tx_i$ is convergent.

In fact, for the graph of *T* to be closed, it is enough that if $x_i \to 0, \, Tx_i \to y$, then $y = 0$. Indeed, assuming that condition holds, if $(x_i, Tx_i) \to (x, y)$, then $x_i - x \to 0$ and $T(x_i - x) \to y - Tx$. Thus, $y = Tx$; i.e., $(x, y)$ is in the graph of *T*.

Note, to check the closedness of a graph, it's not even necessary to use the norm topology: if the graph of *T* is closed in some topology coarser than the norm topology, then it is closed in the norm topology.[^o3] In practice, this works like this: *T* is some operator on some function space. One shows *T* is continuous with respect to the [distribution topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29); thus, the graph is closed in that topology, which implies closedness in the norm topology. If the closed graph theorem applies, then *T* is continuous under the original topology. See for an explicit example.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

[^o7] If $T : X \to Y$ is a linear operator between [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) (or more generally [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801)), then the following are equivalent:

$T$ is continuous.

The graph of $T$ is closed in the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) on $X \times Y.$

:::

The usual proof of the closed graph theorem employs the [open mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/17395276). It simply uses a general recipe of obtaining the closed graph theorem from the open mapping theorem; see closed\_graph\_theorem (this deduction is formal and does not use linearity; the linearity is needed to appeal to the open mapping theorem which relies on the linearity.)

In fact, the open mapping theorem can in turn be deduced from the closed graph theorem as follows. As noted in Open\_mapping\_theorem\_(functional\_analysis), it is enough to prove the open mapping theorem for a continuous linear operator that is bijective (not just surjective). Let *T* be such an operator. Then by continuity, the graph $\Gamma_T$ of *T* is closed. Then $\Gamma_T \simeq \Gamma_{T^{-1}}$ under $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$. Hence, by the closed graph theorem, $T^{-1}$ is continuous; i.e., *T* is an open mapping.

Since the closed graph theorem is equivalent to the open mapping theorem, one knows that the theorem fails without the completeness assumption. But more concretely, an operator with closed graph that is not bounded (see [unbounded operator](wikipedia://en/1422584)) exists and thus serves as a counterexample.

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The [Hausdorff–Young inequality](wikipedia://en/22386332) says that the Fourier transformation $\widehat{\cdot} : L^p(\mathbb{R}^n) \to L^{p'}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a well-defined bounded operator with operator norm one when $1/p + 1/p' = 1$. This result is usually proved using the [Riesz–Thorin interpolation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesz–Thorin_interpolation_theorem) and is highly nontrivial. The closed graph theorem can be used to prove a soft version of this result; i.e., the Fourier transformation is a bounded operator with the unknown operator norm.[^o10]

Here is how the argument would go. Let *T* denote the Fourier transformation. First we show $T : L^p \to Z$ is a continuous linear operator for *Z* = the space of tempered distributions on $\mathbb{R}^n$. Second, we note that *T* maps the space of [Schwartz functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_function) to itself (in short, because smoothness and rapid decay transform to rapid decay and smoothness, respectively). This implies that the graph of *T* is contained in $L^p \times L^{p'}$ and $T : L^p \to L^{p'}$ is defined but with unknown bounds. Since $T : L^p \to Z$ is continuous, the graph of $T : L^p \to L^{p'}$ is closed in the distribution topology; thus in the norm topology. Finally, by the closed graph theorem, $T : L^p \to L^{p'}$ is a bounded operator.

:::

## Generalization {#Generalization}

### On Hilbert spaces {#On_Hilbert_spaces}

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space, and $A: D(A) \subset H \to H$ be a possibly [partially-defined](wikipedia://en/23577) linear operator.

Define the **graph inner product** on $D(A)$ by $\langle x, y\rangle_{G(A)}=\langle x, y\rangle+\langle A x, A y\rangle$, and similarly the graph norm $\|x \|_{G(A)} = \sqrt{\|x\|^2 + \|Ax\|^2}$. We have the following:[^n1]

- $A$ is closed iff $(D(A), \|\cdot \|_{G(A)})$ is Banach.
- If $A$ is bounded, then $A$ is closed iff $D(A)$ is closed.

### Complete metrizable codomain {#Complete_metrizable_codomain}

The closed graph theorem can be generalized from Banach spaces to more abstract [topological vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_vector_spaces) in the following ways.

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

A linear operator from a [barrelled space](wikipedia://en/1733592) $X$ to a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) $Y$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/1802169) if and only if its graph is closed.

:::

#### Between F-spaces {#Between_F-spaces}

There are versions that does not require $Y$ to be locally convex.

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

A linear map between two [F-spaces](wikipedia://en/200440) is continuous if and only if its graph is closed.[^n2][^o20]

:::

This theorem is restated and extend it with some conditions that can be used to determine if a graph is closed:

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If $T : X \to Y$ is a linear map between two [F-spaces](wikipedia://en/200440), then the following are equivalent:

$T$ is continuous.

$T$ has a closed graph.

If $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty} \to x$ in $X$ and if $T\left(x_{\bullet}\right) := \left(T\left(x_i\right)\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ converges in $Y$ to some $y \in Y,$ then $y = T(x).$[^n3]

If $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty} \to 0$ in $X$ and if $T\left(x_{\bullet}\right)$ converges in $Y$ to some $y \in Y,$ then $y = 0.$

:::

### Complete pseudometrizable codomain {#Complete_pseudometrizable_codomain}

Every [metrizable topological space](wikipedia://en/19738) is [pseudometrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudometrizable). A [pseudometrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudometrizable) space is metrizable if and only if it is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637).

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Closed Graph Theorem"}

**Closed Graph Theorem[^n4].**

Also, a closed linear map from a locally convex [ultrabarrelled space](wikipedia://en/64268157) into a complete [pseudometrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) is continuous.

:::

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Closed Graph Theorem"}

**Closed Graph Theorem.**

A closed and bounded linear map from a locally convex [infrabarreled space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrabarreled_space) into a complete [pseudometrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) locally convex space is continuous.[^n4]

:::

### Codomain not complete or (pseudo) metrizable {#Codomain_not_complete_or_(pseudo)_metrizable}

::: {#o30 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n5].**

Suppose that $T : X \to Y$ is a linear map whose graph is closed. If $X$ is an inductive limit of [Baire](wikipedia://en/200550) TVSs and $Y$ is a [webbed space](wikipedia://en/41717785) then $T$ is continuous.

:::

::: {#o32 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Closed Graph Theorem"}

**Closed Graph Theorem[^n4].**

A closed surjective linear map from a complete [pseudometrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) onto a locally convex [ultrabarrelled space](wikipedia://en/64268157) is continuous.

:::

An even more general version of the closed graph theorem is

::: {#o34 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n6].**

Suppose that $X$ and $Y$ are two topological vector spaces (they need not be Hausdorff or locally convex) with the following property:

If $G$ is any closed subspace of $X \times Y$ and $u$ is any continuous map of $G$ onto $X,$ then $u$ is an open mapping.

Under this condition, if $T : X \to Y$ is a linear map whose graph is closed then $T$ is continuous.

:::

:::

## Borel graph theorem {#Borel_graph_theorem}

The Borel graph theorem, proved by L. Schwartz, shows that the closed graph theorem is valid for linear maps defined on and valued in most spaces encountered in analysis.[^n7] Recall that a topological space is called a [Polish space](wikipedia://en/396622) if it is a separable complete metrizable space and that a [Souslin space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Souslin_space) is the continuous image of a Polish space. The weak dual of a separable Fréchet space and the strong dual of a separable Fréchet-Montel space are Souslin spaces. Also, the space of distributions and all [Lp-spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lp-space) over open subsets of Euclidean space as well as many other spaces that occur in analysis are Souslin spaces. The [Borel graph theorem](wikipedia://en/63907968) states:

::: {#o38 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Borel Graph Theorem"}

**Borel Graph Theorem.**

Let $u : X \to Y$ be linear map between two [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) spaces $X$ and $Y.$ If $X$ is the inductive limit of an arbitrary family of Banach spaces, if $Y$ is a Souslin space, and if the graph of $u$ is a Borel set in $X \times Y,$ then $u$ is continuous.[^n7]

:::

An improvement upon this theorem, proved by A. Martineau, uses K-analytic spaces.

A topological space $X$ is called a $K_{\sigma\delta}$ if it is the countable intersection of countable unions of compact sets.

A Hausdorff topological space $Y$ is called **K-analytic** if it is the continuous image of a $K_{\sigma\delta}$ space (that is, if there is a $K_{\sigma\delta}$ space $X$ and a continuous map of $X$ onto $Y$).

Every compact set is K-analytic so that there are non-separable K-analytic spaces. Also, every Polish, Souslin, and [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091) [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) is K-analytic as is the weak dual of a Frechet space. The generalized Borel graph theorem states:

::: {#o41 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Generalized Borel Graph Theorem"}

**Generalized Borel Graph Theorem[^n8].**

Let $u : X \to Y$ be a linear map between two locally convex Hausdorff spaces $X$ and $Y.$ If $X$ is the inductive limit of an arbitrary family of Banach spaces, if $Y$ is a K-analytic space, and if the graph of $u$ is closed in $X \times Y,$ then $u$ is continuous.

:::
