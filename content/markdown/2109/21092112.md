# Non-Desarguesian plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-Desarguesian plane"}

In mathematics, a **non-Desarguesian plane** is a [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) (or sometimes an [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139)) that does not satisfy [Desargues' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desargues'_theorem) (named after [Girard Desargues](wikipedia://en/358484)), or in other words a plane that is not a [Desarguesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desarguesian_plane). The theorem of Desargues is true in all [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135) of dimension not 2;[^o2] in other words, the only projective spaces of dimension not equal to 2 are the classical [projective geometries](wikipedia://en/243849) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) (or [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067)). However, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) found that some projective planes do not satisfy it.[^o3][^o4] The current state of knowledge of these examples is not complete.[^o5]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

There are many examples of both [finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_Plane#Finite_projective_planes) and infinite non-Desarguesian planes. Some of the known examples of infinite non-Desarguesian planes include:

- The [Moulton plane](wikipedia://en/9787563).
- [Moufang planes](wikipedia://en/14860374) over alternative division algebras that are not associative, such as the [projective plane over the octonions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octonionic_projective_plane). Since all finite alternative division rings are fields ([Artin–Zorn theorem](wikipedia://en/14818089)), the only non-Desarguesian Moufang planes are infinite.

Regarding finite non-Desarguesian planes, every projective plane of order at most 8 is Desarguesian, but there are three non-Desarguesian examples of order 9, each with 91 points and 91 lines.[^o8] They are:

- The [Hughes plane](wikipedia://en/21116300) of order 9.
- The [Hall plane of order 9](wikipedia://en/35791745#The_Hall_plane_of_order_9). Initially discovered by [Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen) and [Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494), this plane was generalized to an infinite family of planes by [Marshall Hall](wikipedia://en/7758584). Hall planes are a subclass of the more general [André planes](wikipedia://en/21121170).
- The [dual](wikipedia://en/539925) of the Hall plane of order 9.

Numerous other constructions of both finite and infinite non-Desarguesian planes are known, see for example (Dembowski 1968). All known constructions of finite non-Desarguesian planes produce planes whose order is a proper prime power, that is, an integer of the form *p*<sup>*e*</sup>, where *p* is a prime and *e* is an integer greater than 1.

:::

## Classification {#Classification}

Hanfried Lenz gave a classification scheme for projective planes in 1954,[^o11] which was refined by Adriano Barlotti in 1957.[^o12] This classification scheme is based on the types of point–line transitivity permitted by the [collineation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collineation_group) of the plane and is known as the *Lenz–Barlotti classification of projective planes*. The list of 53 types is given in (Dembowski 1968, 124–125) and a table of the then known existence results (for both collineation groups and planes having such a collineation group) in both the finite and infinite cases appears on page 126. As of 2007, "36 of them exist as finite groups. Between 7 and 12 exist as finite projective planes, and either 14 or 15 exist as infinite projective planes."[^o5]

Other classification schemes exist. One of the simplest is based on special types of [planar ternary ring](wikipedia://en/4027813) (PTR) that can be used to coordinatize the projective plane. These types are [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), [skewfields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_field), [alternative division rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_division_ring), [semifields](wikipedia://en/10417428), [nearfields](wikipedia://en/4033255), [right nearfields](wikipedia://en/4033255), [quasifields](wikipedia://en/4029839) and [right quasifields](wikipedia://en/4029839).[^o14]

## Conics and ovals {#Conics_and_ovals}

In a Desarguesian projective plane a [conic](wikipedia://en/19008673) can be defined in several different ways that can be proved to be equivalent. In non-Desarguesian planes these proofs are no longer valid and the different definitions can give rise to non-equivalent objects.[^o16] Theodore G. Ostrom had suggested the name *conicoid* for these conic-like figures but did not provide a formal definition and the term does not seem to be widely used.[^o17]

There are several ways that conics can be defined in Desarguesian planes:

1. The set of absolute points of a polarity is known as a [von Staudt conic](wikipedia://en/46503286). If the plane is defined over a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29) of [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28field%29) two, only [degenerate conics](wikipedia://en/392586) are obtained.
1. The set of points of intersection of corresponding lines of two pencils which are projectively, but not perspectively, related is known as a [Steiner conic](wikipedia://en/46462753). If the pencils are perspectively related, the conic is degenerate.
1. The set of points whose coordinates satisfy an irreducible homogeneous equation of degree two.

Furthermore, in a finite Desarguesian plane:

1. A set of *q* + 1 points, no three collinear in PG(2, *q*) is called an *oval*. If *q* is odd, by [Segre's theorem](wikipedia://en/49713321), an oval in PG(2, *q*) is a conic, in sense 3 above.
1. An *Ostrom conic* is based on a generalization of harmonic sets.

Artzy has given an example of a Steiner conic in a Moufang plane which is not a von Staudt conic.[^o18] Garner gives an example of a von Staudt conic that is not an Ostrom conic in a finite semifield plane.[^o16]
