# Equilateral dimension

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equilateral dimension"}

*Image: Simplexes.jpg.* Regular [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) of dimensions 0 through 3. The vertices of these shapes give the largest possible equally-spaced point sets for the [Euclidean distances](wikipedia://en/53932) in those dimensions

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **equilateral dimension** of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is the maximum size of any subset of the space whose points are all at equal distances to each other.[^o2] Equilateral dimension has also been called "[metric dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_dimension_%28disambiguation%29)", but the term "metric dimension" also has many other inequivalent usages.[^o2] The equilateral dimension of $d$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is $d+1$, achieved by the vertices of a regular [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781), and the equilateral dimension of a $d$-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with the [Chebyshev distance](wikipedia://en/1503963) ($L^\infty$ norm) is $2^d$, achieved by the vertices of a [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783). However, the equilateral dimension of a space with the [Manhattan distance](wikipedia://en/408354) ($L^1$ norm) is not known. **Kusner's conjecture**, named after [Robert B. Kusner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_B._Kusner), states that it is exactly $2d$, achieved by the vertices of a [cross polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_polytope).[^o3]

:::

## Lebesgue spaces {#Lebesgue_spaces}

The equilateral dimension has been particularly studied for [Lebesgue spaces](wikipedia://en/45194), finite-dimensional [normed vector spaces](wikipedia://en/21538) with the $L^p$ norm

$$\ \|x\|_p=\bigl(|x_1|^p+|x_2|^p+\cdots+|x_d|^p\bigr)^{1/p}.$$

The equilateral dimension of $L^p$ spaces of dimension $d$ behaves differently depending on the value of $p$:

::: {#o5 .problem cue=unsolved}

How many equidistant points exist in spaces with [Manhattan distance](wikipedia://en/408354)?

:::

- For $p=1$, the $L^p$ norm gives rise to [Manhattan distance](wikipedia://en/408354). In this case, it is possible to find $2d$ equidistant points, the vertices of an axis-aligned [cross polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_polytope). The equilateral dimension is known to be exactly $2d$ for $d\le 4$,[^o6] and to be upper bounded by $O(d\log d)$ for all $d$.[^o7] Robert B. Kusner suggested in 1983 that the equilateral dimension for this case should be exactly $2d$;[^o9] this suggestion (together with a related suggestion for the equilateral dimension when $p>2$) has come to be known as **Kusner's conjecture**.
- For $1<p<2$, the equilateral dimension is at least $(1+\varepsilon)d$ where $\varepsilon$ is a constant that depends on $p$.[^o8]
- For $p=2$, the $L^p$ norm is the familiar [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932). The equilateral dimension of $d$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is $d+1$: the $d+1$ vertices of an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285), [regular tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/5242521), or higher-dimensional regular [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) form an equilateral set, and every equilateral set must have this form.[^o9]
- For $2<p<\infty$, the equilateral dimension is at least $d+1$: for instance the $d$ [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) of the vector space together with another vector of the form $(-x,-x,\dots)$ for a suitable choice of $x$ form an equilateral set. Kusner's conjecture states that in these cases the equilateral dimension is exactly $d+1$. Kusner's conjecture has been proven for the special case that $p=4$.[^o8] When $p$ is an odd integer the equilateral dimension is upper bounded by $O(d\log d)$.[^o7]
- For $p=\infty$ (the limiting case of the $L^p$ norm for finite values of $p$, in the limit as $p$ grows to infinity) the $L^p$ norm becomes the [Chebyshev distance](wikipedia://en/1503963), the maximum absolute value of the differences of the coordinates. For a $d$-dimensional vector space with the Chebyshev distance, the equilateral dimension is $2^d$: the $2^d$ vertices of an axis-aligned [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) are at equal distances from each other, and no larger equilateral set is possible.[^o9]

## Normed vector spaces {#Normed_vector_spaces}

Equilateral dimension has also been considered for [normed vector spaces](wikipedia://en/21538) with norms other than the $L^p$ norms. The problem of determining the equilateral dimension for a given norm is closely related to the [kissing number problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kissing_number_problem): the kissing number in a normed space is the maximum number of disjoint translates of a unit ball that can all touch a single central ball, whereas the equilateral dimension is the maximum number of disjoint translates that can all touch each other.

For a normed vector space of dimension $d$, the equilateral dimension is at most $2^d$; that is, the $L^\infty$ norm has the highest equilateral dimension among all normed spaces.[^o11] (Petty 1971) asked whether every normed vector space of dimension $d$ has equilateral dimension at least $d+1$, but this remains unknown. There exist normed spaces in any dimension for which certain sets of four equilateral points cannot be extended to any larger equilateral set[^o11] but these spaces may have larger equilateral sets that do not include these four points. For norms that are sufficiently close in [Banach–Mazur distance](wikipedia://en/20021256) to an $L^p$ norm, Petty's question has a positive answer: the equilateral dimension is at least $d+1$.[^o13]

It is not possible for high-dimensional spaces to have bounded equilateral dimension: for any integer $k$, all normed vector spaces of sufficiently high dimension have equilateral dimension at least $k$.[^o14] more specifically, according to a variation of [Dvoretzky's theorem](wikipedia://en/9118440) by (Alon Milman 1983), every $d$-dimensional normed space has a $k$-dimensional subspace that is close either to a Euclidean space or to a Chebyshev space, where

$$k\ge\exp(c\sqrt{\log d})$$

for some constant $c$. Because it is close to a Lebesgue space, this subspace and therefore also the whole space contains an equilateral set of at least $k+1$ points. Therefore, the same superlogarithmic dependence on $d$ holds for the lower bound on the equilateral dimension of $d$-dimensional space.[^o13]

## Riemannian manifolds {#Riemannian_manifolds}

For any $d$-dimensional [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) the equilateral dimension is at least $d+1$.[^o9] For a $d$-dimensional [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), the equilateral dimension is $d+2$, the same as for a Euclidean space of one higher dimension into which the sphere can be embedded.[^o9] At the same time as he posed Kusner's conjecture, Kusner asked whether there exist Riemannian metrics with bounded dimension as a manifold but arbitrarily high equilateral dimension.[^o9]
