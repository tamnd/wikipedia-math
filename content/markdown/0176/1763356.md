# Laplace–Beltrami operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Laplace–Beltrami operator"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the **Laplace–Beltrami operator** is a generalization of the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706) to functions defined on [submanifolds](wikipedia://en/525095) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) and, even more generally, on [Riemannian](wikipedia://en/144652) and [pseudo-Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/287555). It is named after [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783) and [Eugenio Beltrami](wikipedia://en/543760).

:::

For any twice-[differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) real-valued function *f* defined on Euclidean space **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, the Laplace operator (also known as the *Laplacian*) takes *f* to the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of its [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) vector field, which is the sum of the *n* pure second derivatives of *f* with respect to each vector of an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) for **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. Like the Laplacian, the Laplace–Beltrami operator is defined as the divergence of the gradient, and is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) taking functions into functions. The operator can be extended to operate on tensors as the divergence of the covariant derivative. Alternatively, the operator can be generalized to operate on [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) using the divergence and [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782). The resulting operator is called the Laplace–de Rham operator (named after [Georges de Rham](wikipedia://en/398699)).

## Details {#Details}

The Laplace–Beltrami operator, like the Laplacian, is the (Riemannian) [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of the (Riemannian) [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461):

$$\Delta f = {\rm div}( \nabla f).$$

An explicit formula in [local coordinates](wikipedia://en/304139) is possible.

Suppose first that *M* is an [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_manifold) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652). The orientation allows one to specify a definite [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811) on *M*, given in an oriented coordinate system *x*<sup>*i*</sup> by

$$\operatorname{vol}_n := \sqrt{|g|} \;dx^1\wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$$

where |*g*| := |det(*g<sub>ij</sub>*)| is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795), and the *dx<sup>i</sup>* are the [1-forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-form) forming the [dual frame](wikipedia://en/3131766) to the frame

$$\partial_i := \frac {\partial}{\partial x^i}$$

of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) $TM$ and $\wedge$ is the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product).

The divergence of a vector field $X$ on the manifold is then defined as the scalar function $\nabla \cdot X$ with the property

$$(\nabla \cdot X) \operatorname{vol}_n := L_X \operatorname{vol}_n$$

where *L<sub>X</sub>* is the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437) along the [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) *X*. In local coordinates, one obtains

$$\nabla \cdot X = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}} \partial_i \left(\sqrt {|g|} X^i\right)$$

where here and below the [Einstein notation](wikipedia://en/195407) is implied, so that the repeated index *i* is summed over.

The gradient of a scalar function ƒ is the vector field grad *f* that may be defined through the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ on the manifold, as

$$\langle \operatorname{grad} f(x) , v_x \rangle = df(x)(v_x)$$

for all vectors *v<sub>x</sub>* anchored at point *x* in the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) *T<sub>x</sub>M* of the manifold at point *x*. Here, *d*ƒ is the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of the function ƒ; it is a 1-form taking argument *v<sub>x</sub>*. In local coordinates, one has

$$\left(\operatorname{grad} f\right)^i = \partial^i f = g^{ij} \partial_j f$$

where *g<sup>ij</sup>* are the components of the inverse of the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795), so that *g<sup>ij</sup>g<sub>jk</sub>* = δ<sup>*i*</sup><sub>*k*</sub> with δ<sup>*i*</sup><sub>*k*</sub> the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890).

Combining the definitions of the gradient and divergence, the formula for the Laplace–Beltrami operator applied to a scalar function ƒ is, in local coordinates

$$\Delta f = \frac{1}{\sqrt {|g|}} \partial_i \left(\sqrt{|g|} g^{ij} \partial_j f \right).$$

If *M* is not oriented, then the above calculation carries through exactly as presented, except that the volume form must instead be replaced by a [volume element](wikipedia://en/1920694) (a [density](wikipedia://en/3428935) rather than a form). Neither the gradient nor the divergence actually depends on the choice of orientation, and so the Laplace–Beltrami operator itself does not depend on this additional structure.

## Formal self-adjointness {#Formal_self-adjointness}

The exterior derivative $d$ and $- \nabla$ are formal adjoints, in the sense that for a compactly supported function $f$

$$\int_M df(X) \operatorname{vol}_n = - \int_M f \nabla \cdot X \operatorname{vol}_n$$

where the last equality is an application of [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497). Dualizing gives

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\int_M f \,\Delta h\,\operatorname{vol}_n = -\int_M \langle df, dh\rangle\, \operatorname{vol}_n \tag{2}$$

:::

for all compactly supported functions $f$ and $h$. Conversely, ((2)) characterizes the Laplace–Beltrami operator completely, in the sense that it is the only operator with this property.

As a consequence, the Laplace–Beltrami operator is negative and formally self-adjoint, meaning that for compactly supported functions $f$ and $h$,

$$\int_M f\,\Delta h \operatorname{vol}_n = -\int_M \langle d f, d h \rangle \operatorname{vol}_n = \int_M h\,\Delta f \operatorname{vol}_n.$$

Because the Laplace–Beltrami operator, as defined in this manner, is negative rather than positive, often it is defined with the opposite sign.

## Eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator (Lichnerowicz–Obata theorem) {#Eigenvalues_of_the_Laplace–Beltrami_operator_(Lichnerowicz–Obata_theorem)}

Let M denote a compact Riemannian manifold without boundary. We want to consider the eigenvalue equation,

$$-\Delta u=\lambda u,$$

where $u$ is the [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) associated with the eigenvalue $\lambda$. It can be shown using the self-adjointness proved above that the eigenvalues $\lambda$ are real. The compactness of the manifold $M$ allows one to show that the eigenvalues are discrete and furthermore, the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of eigenfunctions associated with a given eigenvalue $\lambda$, i.e. the [eigenspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) are all finite-dimensional. Notice by taking the [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861) as an eigenfunction, we get $\lambda=0$ is an eigenvalue. Also since we have considered $-\Delta$ an integration by parts shows that $\lambda\geq 0$. More precisely if we multiply the eigenvalue equation through by the eigenfunction $u$ and integrate the resulting equation on $M$ we get (using the notation $dV=\operatorname{vol}_n$):

$$-\int_M \Delta u\ u\ dV=\lambda\int_Mu^2\ dV$$

Performing an integration by parts or what is the same thing as using the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864) on the term on the left, and since $M$ has no boundary we get

$$-\int_M\Delta u\ u\ dV=\int_M|\nabla u|^2\ dV$$

Putting the last two equations together we arrive at

$$\int_M|\nabla u|^2\ dV=\lambda\int_Mu^2\ dV$$

We conclude from the last equation that $\lambda\geq 0$.

A fundamental result of [André Lichnerowicz](wikipedia://en/2389188)[^o7] states that: Given a compact *n*-dimensional Riemannian manifold with no boundary with $n\geq 2$. Assume the [Ricci curvature](wikipedia://en/276582) satisfies the lower bound:

$$\operatorname{Ric}(X,X)\geq \kappa g(X,X),\kappa>0,$$

where $g(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the metric tensor and $X$ is any tangent vector on the manifold $M$. Then the first positive eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ of the eigenvalue equation satisfies the lower bound:

$$\lambda_1\geq \frac{n}{n-1}\kappa.$$

This lower bound is sharp and achieved on the sphere $\mathbb{S}^n$. In fact on $\mathbb{S}^2$ the eigenspace for $\lambda_1$ is three dimensional and spanned by the restriction of the coordinate functions $x_1,x_2,x_3$ from $\mathbb{R}^3$ to $\mathbb{S}^2$. Using spherical coordinates $(\theta,\phi)$, on $\mathbb{S}^2$ the two dimensional sphere, set

$$x_3=\cos\phi=u_1,$$

we see easily from the formula for the spherical Laplacian displayed below that

$$-\Delta _{\mathbb{S}^2}u_1=2u_1$$

Thus the lower bound in Lichnerowicz's theorem is achieved at least in two dimensions.

Conversely it was proved by [Morio Obata](wikipedia://en/80481508),[^o8] that if the *n*-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold without boundary were such that for the first positive eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ one has,

$$\lambda_1=\frac{n}{n-1}\kappa,$$

then the manifold is isometric to the *n*-dimensional sphere $\mathbb{S}^n\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{\kappa}}\bigg)$, the sphere of radius $\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{\kappa}}$. Proofs of all these statements may be found in the book by Isaac Chavel.[^o9] Analogous sharp bounds also hold for other geometries and for certain degenerate Laplacians associated with these geometries like the [Kohn Laplacian](wikipedia://en/3131794#The_tangential_Cauchy–Riemann_complex_%28Kohn_Laplacian,_Kohn–Rossi_complex%29) (after [Joseph J. Kohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_J._Kohn)) on a compact [CR manifold](wikipedia://en/3131794). Applications there are to the global embedding of such CR manifolds in $\mathbb{C}^n.$[^o10]

## Tensor Laplacian {#Tensor_Laplacian}

The Laplace–Beltrami operator can be written using the [trace (or contraction)](wikipedia://en/202722) of the iterated [covariant derivative](wikipedia://en/431848) associated with the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176). The [Hessian (tensor)](wikipedia://en/412108#Generalizations_to_Riemannian_manifolds) of a function $f$ is the symmetric 2-tensor

$\displaystyle \mbox{Hess} f \in \mathbf \Gamma(\mathsf T^*M \otimes \mathsf T^*M),$ $\mbox{Hess} f := \nabla^2 f \equiv \nabla \nabla f \equiv \nabla \mathrm df,$

where *df* denotes the [(exterior) derivative](wikipedia://en/220782#Definition) of a function *f*.

Let *X*<sub>i</sub> be a basis of tangent vector fields (not necessarily induced by a coordinate system). Then the components of *Hess f* are given by

$$(\mbox{Hess} f)_{ij} = \mbox{Hess} f(X_i, X_j) = \nabla_{X_i}\nabla_{X_j} f - \nabla_{\nabla_{X_i}X_j} f$$

This is easily seen to transform tensorially, since it is linear in each of the arguments *X*<sub>i</sub>, *X*<sub>j</sub>. The Laplace–Beltrami operator is then the trace (or [contraction](wikipedia://en/202722)) of the Hessian with respect to the metric:

$$\displaystyle \Delta f := \mathrm{tr} \nabla \mathrm df \in C^\infty(M).$$

More precisely, this means

$$\displaystyle \Delta f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla \mathrm df(X_i,X_i),$$

or in terms of the metric

$$\Delta f = \sum_{ij} g^{ij} (\mbox{Hess} f)_{ij}.$$

In [abstract indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_indices), the operator is often written

$$\Delta f = \nabla^a \nabla_a f$$

provided it is understood implicitly that this trace is in fact the trace of the Hessian *tensor*.

Because the covariant derivative extends canonically to arbitrary [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965), the Laplace–Beltrami operator defined on a tensor *T* by

$$\Delta T = g^{ij}\left( \nabla_{X_i}(\nabla_{X_j} T) - \nabla_{\nabla_{X_i}X_j} T\right)$$

is well-defined.

## Laplace–de Rham operator {#Laplace–de_Rham_operator}

More generally, one can define a Laplacian [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) on sections of the bundle of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) on a [pseudo-Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555). On a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) it is an [elliptic operator](wikipedia://en/550137), while on a [Lorentzian manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold) it is [hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_operator). The **Laplace–de Rham operator** is defined by

$$\Delta = \mathrm{d}\delta + \delta\mathrm{d} = (\mathrm{d}+\delta)^2,\;$$

where d is the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) or differential and *δ* is the [codifferential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codifferential), acting as (−1)<sup>*kn*+*n*+1</sup>∗d∗ on *k*-forms, where ∗ is the [Hodge star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_star). The first order operator $\mathrm{d}+\delta$ is the Hodge–Dirac operator.[^o14]

When computing the Laplace–de Rham operator on a scalar function *f*, we have *δf* = 0, so that

$$\Delta f = \delta \, \mathrm df.$$

Up to an overall sign, the Laplace–de Rham operator is equivalent to the previous definition of the Laplace–Beltrami operator when acting on a scalar function; see the proof for details. On functions, the Laplace–de Rham operator is actually the negative of the Laplace–Beltrami operator, as the conventional normalization of the [codifferential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codifferential) assures that the Laplace–de Rham operator is (formally) [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_operator), whereas the Laplace–Beltrami operator is typically negative. The sign is merely a convention, and both are common in the literature.

The Laplace–de Rham operator differs more significantly from the tensor Laplacian restricted to act on skew-symmetric tensors. Apart from the incidental sign, the two operators differ by a [Weitzenböck identity](wikipedia://en/5468083) that explicitly involves the [Ricci curvature tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_curvature_tensor).

## Examples {#Examples}

Many examples of the Laplace–Beltrami operator can be worked out explicitly.

### Euclidean space {#Euclidean_space}

In the usual (orthonormal) [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) *x*<sup>*i*</sup> on [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the metric is reduced to the Kronecker delta, and one therefore has $|g| = 1$. Consequently, in this case

$$\Delta f = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}} \partial_i \sqrt{|g|}\partial^i f = \partial_i \partial^i f$$

which is the ordinary Laplacian. In [curvilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/755300), such as [spherical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) or [cylindrical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates), one obtains [alternative expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian#Coordinate_expressions).

Similarly, the Laplace–Beltrami operator corresponding to the [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric) with [signature](wikipedia://en/463225) (− + + +) is the [d'Alembertian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Alembertian).

### Spherical Laplacian {#Spherical_Laplacian}

The spherical Laplacian is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the (*n* − 1)-sphere with its canonical metric of constant [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) 1. It is convenient to regard the sphere as isometrically embedded into **R**<sup>*n*</sup> as the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) centred at the origin. Then for a function *f* on *S*<sup>*n*−1</sup>, the spherical Laplacian is defined by

$$\Delta _{S^{n-1}}f(x) = \Delta f(x/|x|)$$

where *f*(*x*/|*x*|) is the degree zero homogeneous extension of the function *f* to **R**<sup>*n*</sup> − {0}, and $\Delta$ is the Laplacian of the ambient Euclidean space. Concretely, this is implied by the well-known formula for the Euclidean Laplacian in spherical polar coordinates:

$$\Delta f = r^{1-n}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{n-1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right) + r^{-2}\Delta _{S^{n-1}}f.$$

More generally, one can formulate a similar trick using the [normal bundle](wikipedia://en/2370200) to define the Laplace–Beltrami operator of any Riemannian manifold isometrically embedded as a hypersurface of Euclidean space.

One can also give an intrinsic description of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the sphere in a [normal coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7679762). Let (*ϕ*, *ξ*) be spherical coordinates on the sphere with respect to a particular point *p* of the sphere (the "north pole"), that is geodesic polar coordinates with respect to *p*. Here *ϕ* represents the latitude measurement along a unit speed geodesic from *p*, and *ξ* a parameter representing the choice of direction of the geodesic in *S*<sup>*n*−1</sup>. Then the spherical Laplacian has the form:

$$\Delta _{S^{n-1}} f(\xi,\phi) = (\sin\phi)^{2-n} \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi}\left((\sin\phi)^{n-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi}\right) + (\sin\phi)^{-2}\Delta _\xi f$$

where $\Delta _\xi$ is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the ordinary unit (*n* − 2)-sphere. In particular, for the ordinary 2-sphere using standard notation for polar coordinates we get:

$$\Delta _{S^{2}} f(\theta,\phi) = (\sin\phi)^{-1} \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi}\left(\sin\phi\frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi}\right) + (\sin\phi)^{-2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2}f$$

### Hyperbolic space {#Hyperbolic_space}

A similar technique works in [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742). Here the hyperbolic space *H*<sup>*n*−1</sup> can be embedded into the *n* dimensional [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space), a real vector space equipped with the quadratic form

$$q(x) = x_1^2 - x_2^2-\cdots - x_n^2.$$

Then *H*<sup>*n-1*</sup> is the subset of the future null cone in Minkowski space given by

$$H^{n-1} = \{ x \mid q(x) = 1, x_1>1\}. \,$$

Then

$$\Delta _{H^{n-1}} f = \left. \Box f\left(x/q(x)^{1/2}\right) \right| _{H^{n-1}}$$

Here $f(x/q(x)^{1/2})$ is the degree zero homogeneous extension of *f* to the interior of the future null cone and $\square$ is the [wave operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_operator)

$$\Box = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_1^2} - \cdots - \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_n^2}.$$

The operator can also be written in polar coordinates. Let (*t*, *ξ*) be spherical coordinates on the sphere with respect to a particular point *p* of *H*<sup>*n*−1</sup> (say, the center of the [Poincaré disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_disc)). Here *t* represents the hyperbolic distance from *p* and *ξ* a parameter representing the choice of direction of the geodesic in *S*<sup>*n*−2</sup>. Then the hyperbolic Laplacian has the form:

$$\Delta _{H^{n-1}} f(t,\xi) = \sinh(t)^{2-n} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\sinh(t)^{n-2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right) + \sinh(t)^{-2}\Delta _\xi f$$

where $\Delta _\xi$ is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the ordinary unit (*n* − 2)-sphere. In particular, for the hyperbolic plane using standard notation for polar coordinates we get:

$$\Delta _{H^{2}} f(r,\theta) = \sinh(r)^{-1} \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\sinh(r)\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right) + \sinh(r)^{-2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2}f$$
