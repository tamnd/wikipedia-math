# Wedderburn's little theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wedderburn's little theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Wedderburn's little theorem** states that every [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603); thus, every finite domain is a field. In other words, for [finite rings](wikipedia://en/23706953), there is no distinction between [domains](wikipedia://en/487627), [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067) and fields.

:::

The [Artin–Zorn theorem](wikipedia://en/14818089) generalizes the theorem to [alternative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_ring): every finite alternative division ring is a field.[^o2]

## History {#History}

The original proof was given by [Joseph Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494) in 1905,[^o4] who went on to prove the theorem in two other ways. Another proof was given by [Leonard Eugene Dickson](wikipedia://en/1790247) shortly after Wedderburn's original proof, and Dickson acknowledged Wedderburn's priority. However, as noted in (Parshall 1983), Wedderburn's first proof was incorrect – it had a gap – and his subsequent proofs appeared only after he had read Dickson's correct proof. On this basis, Parshall argues that Dickson should be credited with the first correct proof.

A simplified version of the proof was later given by [Ernst Witt](wikipedia://en/2619911).[^o4] Witt's proof is sketched below. Alternatively, the theorem is a consequence of the [Skolem–Noether theorem](wikipedia://en/693453) by the following argument.[^o6] Let $D$ be a finite [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) with [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) $k$. Let $[D:k]=n^{2}$ and $q$ denote the cardinality of $k$. Every maximal subfield of $D$ has $q^{n}$ elements; so they are isomorphic and thus are conjugate by Skolem–Noether. But a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) (the multiplicative group of $D$ in our case) cannot be a union of conjugates of a proper subgroup; hence, $n = 1$.

A later "[group-theoretic](wikipedia://en/41890)" proof was given by [Ted Kaczynski](wikipedia://en/18621887) in 1964.[^o7] This proof, Kaczynski's first published piece of mathematical writing, was a short, two-page note which also acknowledged the earlier historical proofs.

## Relationship to the Brauer group of a finite field {#Relationship_to_the_Brauer_group_of_a_finite_field}

The theorem is essentially equivalent to saying that the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) of a finite field is trivial. In fact, this characterization immediately yields a proof of the theorem as follows: let *K* be a finite field. Since the [Herbrand quotient](wikipedia://en/6043076) vanishes by finiteness, $\operatorname{Br}(K) = H^2(K^{\text{al}}/K)$ coincides with $H^1(K^{\text{al}}/K)$, which in turn vanishes by [Hilbert 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_theorem_90).

The triviality of the Brauer group can also be obtained by direct computation, as follows. Let $|K| = q,$ and let $L/K$ be a finite extension of degree $n,$ so that $|L|=q^n.$ Then $\mathrm{Gal}(L/K)$ is a cyclic group of order $n,$ and the standard method of computing [cohomology of finite cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/248635#Group_cohomology_of_a_finite_cyclic_group) shows that

$$H^2(L/K) = K^{\times}/N_{L/K}(L^{\times}),$$

where the norm map $N_{L/K}:L^{\times} \to K^{\times}$ is given by

$$N_{L/K}(\alpha) = \prod_{\sigma \in \mathrm{Gal}(L/K)} \sigma(\alpha) = \alpha \cdot \alpha^{q} \cdot \alpha^{q^2} \cdots \alpha^{q^{n-1}} = \alpha^{\frac{q^n-1}{q-1}}.$$

Taking $\alpha$ to be a generator of the cyclic group $L^{\times},$ we find that $N_{L/K}(\alpha)$ has order $q-1,$ and therefore it must be a generator of $K^{\times}$. This implies that $N_{L/K}$ is surjective, and therefore $H^{2}(L/K)$ is trivial.

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Let $A$ be a finite domain. For each nonzero $b$ in $A$, the two maps

$$a \mapsto ab, a \mapsto ba: A \to A$$

are injective by the [cancellation property](wikipedia://en/490646), and thus, surjective by counting. It follows from elementary group theory[^o11] that the nonzero elements of $A$ form a group under multiplication. Thus, $A$ is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067).

Since the [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) $Z(A)$ of $A$ is a field, $A$ is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over $Z(A)$ with finite dimension $n$. Our objective is then to show $n = 1$. If $q$ is the order of $Z(A)$, then $A$ has order ${q}^{n}$. Note that because $Z(A)$ contains the distinct elements $0$ and $1$, $q>1$. For each $b$ in $A$ that is not in the center, the [centralizer](wikipedia://en/145661) ${Z}_{b}$ of $b$ is a vector space over $Z(A)$, hence it has order ${q}^{d}$ where $d$ is less than $n$. Viewing ${Z(A)}^{*}$, $A^{*}$, and ${Z}^{*}_{b}$ as groups under multiplication, we can write the [class equation](wikipedia://en/49176#Conjugacy_class_equation)

$$q^n - 1 = q - 1 + \sum {q^n - 1 \over q^d - 1}$$

where the sum is taken over the conjugacy classes not contained within ${Z(A)}^{*}$, and the $d$ are defined so that for each conjugacy class, the order of ${Z}^{*}_{b}$ for any $x$ in the class is ${q}^{d} - 1$. In particular, the fact that ${Z}^{*}_{b}$ is a subgroup of $A^{*}$ implies that $q^d-1$ divides $q^n-1$, whence $d$ divides $n$ by [elementary algebra](wikipedia://en/9710).

${q}^{n} - 1$ and $q^{d} - 1$ both admit [polynomial factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_factorization) in terms of [cyclotomic polynomials](wikipedia://en/171992) $\Phi_f(q)$. The cyclotomic polynomials on $\mathbb{Q}$ are in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, and satisfy the identities

$x^n-1 = \prod_{m\mid n} \Phi_m(x)$ and $x^d-1 = \prod_{m\mid d} \Phi_m(x)$.

Since each $d$ is a proper divisor of $n$,

$\Phi_n(x)$ divides both ${x}^{n} - 1$ and each ${x^n - 1 \over x^d - 1}$ in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$,

thus by the class equation above, $\Phi_n(q)$ must divide $q - 1$, and therefore by taking the [norms](wikipedia://en/990534),

$$|\Phi_n(q)| \leq q-1.$$

To see that this forces $n$ to be $1$, we will show

$$|\Phi_n(q)| > q-1$$

for $n>1$ using factorization over the complex numbers. In the polynomial identity

$$\Phi_n(x) = \prod (x - \zeta),$$

where $\zeta$ runs over the primitive $n$-th roots of unity, set $x$ to be $q$ and then take absolute values

$$|\Phi_n(q)| = \prod |q - \zeta|.$$

For $n>1$, we see that for each primitive $n$-th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) $\zeta$,

$$|q-\zeta| > |q-1|$$

because of the location of $q$, $1$, and $\zeta$ in the complex plane. Since $q \geq 2$, the term $q-1$ is at least $1$. Thus

$$|\Phi_n(q)| > q-1.$$

:::
