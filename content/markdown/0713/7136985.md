# Vectorization (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vectorization"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and [matrix theory](wikipedia://en/20556859), the **vectorization** of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) which converts the matrix into a [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956). Specifically, the vectorization of a *m* × *n* matrix *A*, denoted vec(*A*), is the *mn* × 1 column vector obtained by stacking the columns of the matrix *A* on top of one another:

$$\operatorname{vec}(A) = [a_{1,1}, \ldots, a_{m,1}, a_{1,2}, \ldots, a_{m,2}, \ldots, a_{1,n}, \ldots, a_{m,n}]^\mathrm{\top}$$

Here, $a_{i,j}$ represents the element in the *i*-th row and *j*-th column of *A*, and the superscript ${}^\mathrm{\top}$ denotes the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844). In other words, vec(*A*) is a vector containing the entries of *A* in [column-major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row-_and_column-major_order) order.

:::

Vectorization expresses, through coordinates, the [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) $\mathbf{R}^{m \times n} \cong \mathbf{R}^{mn}$ between these (i.e., of matrices and vectors) as vector spaces.

For example, for the 2×2 matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$, the vectorization is $\operatorname{vec}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \\ b \\ d \end{bmatrix}$.

The connection between the vectorization of *A* and the vectorization of its transpose is given by the [commutation matrix](wikipedia://en/7133473).

## Compatibility with Kronecker products {#Compatibility_with_Kronecker_products}

The vectorization is frequently used together with the [Kronecker product](wikipedia://en/712430) to express [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) as a linear transformation on matrices. In particular,

$$\operatorname{vec}(ABC) = (C^\mathrm{\top}\otimes A) \operatorname{vec}(B)$$

for matrices *A*, *B*, and *C* of dimensions *k*×*l*, *l*×*m*, and *m*×*n*.[^o3] For example, if $\operatorname{ad}_A(X) = AX-XA$ (the [adjoint endomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_endomorphism) of the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) gl(*n*, **C**) of all *n*×*n* matrices with [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) entries), then $\operatorname{vec}(\operatorname{ad}_A(X)) = (A \otimes I_n - I_n \otimes A^\mathrm{\top}) \text{vec}(X)$, where $I_n$ is the *n*×*n* [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

There are two other useful formulations:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{vec}(ABC) &= (I_n\otimes AB)\operatorname{vec}(C) = (C^\mathrm{\top}B^\mathrm{\top}\otimes I_k) \operatorname{vec}(A) \\ \operatorname{vec}(AB) &= (I_m \otimes A) \operatorname{vec}(B) = (B^\mathrm{\top}\otimes I_k) \operatorname{vec}(A) \end{aligned}$$

If *B* is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) (i.e., $B = \operatorname{diag}(b_1, \dots, b_n)$), the vectorization can be written using the column-wise Kronecker product $\ast$ (see [Khatri-Rao product](wikipedia://en/64530953)) and the [main diagonal](wikipedia://en/194990) $b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1, \dots, b_n \end{bmatrix}^\mathrm \top$ of *B*:

$$\operatorname{vec}(ABC) = (C^\mathrm{\top} \ast A) b$$

More generally, it has been shown that vectorization is a [self-adjunction](wikipedia://en/53991) in the monoidal closed structure of any category of matrices.[^o4]

## Compatibility with Hadamard products {#Compatibility_with_Hadamard_products}

Vectorization is an [algebra homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) from the space of *n* × *n* matrices with the [Hadamard](wikipedia://en/34260496) (entrywise) product to **C**<sup>*n*<sup>2</sup></sup> with its Hadamard product:

$$\operatorname{vec}(A \circ B) = \operatorname{vec}(A) \circ \operatorname{vec}(B) .$$

## Compatibility with inner products {#Compatibility_with_inner_products}

Vectorization is a [unitary transformation](wikipedia://en/236007) from the space of *n*×*n* matrices with the [Frobenius](wikipedia://en/1543735#Frobenius_norm) (or [Hilbert–Schmidt](wikipedia://en/976673)) [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) to **C**<sup>*n*<sup>2</sup></sup>:

$$\operatorname{tr}(A^\dagger B) = \operatorname{vec}(A)^\dagger \operatorname{vec}(B),$$

where the superscript <sup>†</sup> denotes the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548).

## Vectorization as a linear sum {#Vectorization_as_a_linear_sum}

The matrix vectorization operation can be written in terms of a linear sum. Let **X** be an *m* × *n* matrix that we want to vectorize, and let **e**<sub>*i*</sub> be the *i*-th canonical basis vector for the *n*-dimensional space, that is $\mathbf{e}_i=\left[0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0\right]^\mathrm{T}$. Let **B**<sub>*i*</sub> be a (*mn*) × *m* block matrix defined as follows:

$$\mathbf{B}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{I}_m \\ \mathbf{0} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{e}_i \otimes \mathbf{I}_m$$

**B**<sub>*i*</sub> consists of *n* block matrices of size *m* × *m*, stacked column-wise, and all these matrices are all-zero except for the *i*-th one, which is a *m* × *m* identity matrix **I**<sub>*m*</sub>.

Then the vectorized version of **X** can be expressed as follows:

$$\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{B}_i \mathbf{X} \mathbf{e}_i$$

Multiplication of **X** by **e**<sub>*i*</sub> extracts the *i*-th column, while multiplication by **B**<sub>*i*</sub> puts it into the desired position in the final vector.

Alternatively, the linear sum can be expressed using the [Kronecker product](wikipedia://en/712430):

$$\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{X}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{e}_i \otimes \mathbf{X} \mathbf{e}_i$$

## Half-vectorization {#Half-vectorization}

For a [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) *A*, the vector vec(*A*) contains more information than is strictly necessary, since the matrix is completely determined by the symmetry together with the [lower triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_triangular_matrix) portion, that is, the *n*(*n* + 1)/2 entries on and below the [main diagonal](wikipedia://en/194990). For such matrices, the **half-vectorization** is sometimes more useful than the vectorization. The half-vectorization, vech(*A*), of a symmetric *n* × *n* matrix *A* is the *n*(*n* + 1)/2 × 1 column vector obtained by vectorizing only the lower triangular part of *A*:

$$\operatorname{vech}(A) = [A_{1,1}, \ldots, A_{n,1}, A_{2,2}, \ldots, A_{n,2}, \ldots, A_{n-1,n-1}, A_{n,n-1}, A_{n,n}]^\mathrm{\top}.$$

For example, for the 2×2 matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & d \end{bmatrix}$, the half-vectorization is $\operatorname{vech}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ d \end{bmatrix}$.

There exist unique matrices transforming the half-vectorization of a matrix to its vectorization and vice versa called, respectively, the [duplication matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duplication_matrix) and the [elimination matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elimination_matrix).

:::

## Programming language {#Programming_language}

Programming languages that implement matrices may have easy means for vectorization. In [Matlab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matlab)/[GNU Octave](wikipedia://en/48707) a matrix `A` can be vectorized by `A(:)`. [GNU Octave](wikipedia://en/48707) also allows vectorization and half-vectorization with `vec(A)` and `vech(A)` respectively. [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29) has the `vec(A)` function as well. In [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) [NumPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NumPy) arrays implement the `flatten` method,[^o3] while in [R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_programming_language) the desired effect can be achieved via the `c()` or `as.vector()` functions or, more efficiently, by removing the dimensions attribute of a matrix `A` with `dim(A) <- NULL`. In [R](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_programming_language), function `vec()` of package 'ks' allows vectorization and function `vech()` implemented in both packages 'ks' and 'sn' allows half-vectorization.[^o11][^o12][^o13]

::: {#o15 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Vectorization is used in [matrix calculus](wikipedia://en/1765852) and its applications in establishing e.g., moments of random vectors and matrices, asymptotics, as well as Jacobian and Hessian matrices.[^o16] It is also used in local sensitivity and statistical diagnostics.[^o17]

:::
