# PrimeGrid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="PrimeGrid"}

**PrimeGrid** is a [volunteer computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volunteer_computing) project that searches for very large (up to world-record size) [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) whilst also aiming to solve long-standing [mathematical conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_conjecture). It uses the [Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Open_Infrastructure_for_Network_Computing) (BOINC) platform. PrimeGrid offers a number of subprojects for prime-number sieving and discovery. Some of these are available through the [BOINC client](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOINC_client–server_technology), others through the PRPNet client. Some of the work is manual, i.e. it requires manually starting work units and uploading results. Different subprojects may run on different operating systems, and may have executables for CPUs, GPUs, or both; while running the [Lucas–Lehmer–Riesel test](wikipedia://en/15371861#LLR_software), CPUs with [Advanced Vector Extensions](wikipedia://en/16779711) and [Fused Multiply-Add](wikipedia://en/23095212) instruction sets will yield the fastest results for non-GPU accelerated workloads.

:::

PrimeGrid awards badges to users in recognition of achieving certain defined levels of credit for work done. The badges have no intrinsic value but are valued by many as a sign of achievement. The issuing of badges should also benefit PrimeGrid by evening out the participation in the less popular sub projects. The easiest of the badges can often be obtained in less than a day by a single computer, whereas the most challenging badges will require far more time and computing power.

## History {#History}

section PrimeGrid started in June 2005[^o3] under the name Message@home and tried to decipher text fragments hashed with [MD5](wikipedia://en/18826). Message@home was a test to port the BOINC scheduler to [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl) to obtain greater portability. After a while the project attempted the [RSA factoring challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_factoring_challenge) trying to factor RSA-640. After RSA-640 was factored by an outside team in November 2005, the project moved on to RSA-768. With the chance to succeed too small, it discarded the RSA challenges, was renamed to PrimeGrid, and started generating a list of the first prime numbers. At 210,000,000,000[^o8] the primegen subproject was stopped.

In June 2006, dialog started with [Riesel Sieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesel_Sieve) to bring their project to the BOINC community. PrimeGrid provided PerlBOINC support and Riesel Sieve was successful in implementing their sieve as well as a prime finding ([LLR](wikipedia://en/15371861)) application. With collaboration from Riesel Sieve, PrimeGrid was able to implement the LLR application in partnership with another prime finding project, [Twin Prime Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Prime_Search) (TPS). In November 2006, the TPS LLR application was officially released at PrimeGrid. Less than two months later, January 2007, the record twin was found by the original manual project. TPS has since been completed, and the search for [Sophie Germain primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Germain_prime) was suspended in 2024.

In the summer of 2007, the [Cullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cullen_prime) and [Woodall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodall_prime) prime searches were launched. In the Fall, more prime searches were added through partnerships with the [Prime Sierpinski Problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_number#Prime_Sierpiński_problem) and [3\*2^n-1 Search](https://www.mersenneforum.org/forumdisplay.php?f=14) projects. Additionally, two sieves were added: the Prime Sierpinski Problem combined sieve which includes supporting the Seventeen or Bust sieve and the combined Cullen/Woodall sieve. In the fall of the same year, PrimeGrid migrated its systems from PerlBOINC to standard [BOINC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOINC) software.

Since September 2008, PrimeGrid is also running a [Proth prime](wikipedia://en/62508931) sieving subproject.[^o9]

In January 2010 the subproject Seventeen or Bust (for solving the [Sierpinski problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_problem)) was added.[^o10] The calculations for the [Riesel problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesel_problem) followed in March 2010.

### 321 Prime Search {#321_Prime_Search}

## Accomplishments {#Accomplishments}

### AP26 {#AP26}

One of PrimeGrid projects was AP26 Search which searched for a record 26 [primes in arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/11740178). The search was successful in April 2010 with the finding of the first known AP26:

$43142746595714191 + 23681770 \cdot 23\# \cdot n$ is prime for $n = 0, ..., 25$.[^o72]

$23\# = 2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17\cdot19\cdot23 = 223092870$, or 23 [primorial](wikipedia://en/422786), is the product of all primes up to 23.

### AP27 {#AP27}

Next target of the project was AP27 Search which searched for a record 27 [primes in arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/11740178). The search was successful in September 2019 with the finding of the first known AP27:

$224584605939537911 + 81292139 \cdot 23\# \cdot n$ is prime for $n = 0, ..., 26$.[^o74]

$23\# = 2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17\cdot19\cdot23 = 223092870$, or 23 [primorial](wikipedia://en/422786), is the product of all primes up to 23.

### Cullen prime search {#Cullen_prime_search}

PrimeGrid is also running a search for [Cullen prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cullen_prime) numbers, yielding the two largest known Cullen primes. The first one being the 14th largest known prime at the time of discovery, and the second one was PrimeGrid's largest prime found $6679881 \cdot 2^{6679881} + 1$ at over 2 million digits.[^o76]

### Generalized Fermat prime search {#Generalized_Fermat_prime_search}

On 24 September 2022, PrimeGrid discovered the largest known [Generalized Fermat prime](wikipedia://en/91127#Generalized_Fermat_numbers) to date, $1963736^{1048576} + 1$. This prime is 6,598,776 digits long and is only the second Generalized Fermat prime found for $n = 20$. It ranks as the 13th largest known prime overall.[^o78]

### Riesel Problem {#Riesel_Problem}

, PrimeGrid has eliminated 18 values of *k* from the [Riesel problem](wikipedia://en/321963)[^o80] and is continuing the search to eliminate the 43 remaining numbers. 3 values of *k* are found by independent searchers.

### Twin prime search {#Twin_prime_search}

Primegrid worked with the [Twin Prime Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Prime_Search) to search for a record-sized [twin prime](wikipedia://en/41997) at approximately 58,700 digits. The new world's largest known twin prime $2003663613 \times 2^{195000} \pm\,1$ was eventually discovered on January 15, 2007 (sieved by Twin Prime Search and tested by PrimeGrid). The search continued for another record twin prime at just above 100,000 digits. It was completed in August 2009 when PrimeGrid found $65516468355 \times 2^{333333} \pm\,1$. Continued testing for twin primes in conjunction with the search for a [Sophie Germain prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Germain_prime) yielded a new record twin prime in September 2016 upon finding the number $2996863034895 \times 2^{1290000} \pm\,1$ composed of 388,342 digits.

### Woodall prime search {#Woodall_prime_search}

, the project has discovered the four largest [Woodall primes](wikipedia://en/321962) known to date.[^o83] The largest of these is $17016602 \times 2^{17016602} - 1$ and was found on 21 March 2018. The search continues for an even bigger Woodall prime. PrimeGrid also found the largest known generalized Woodall prime,[^o84] $563528 \times 13^{563528} - 1$.

## Media coverage {#Media_coverage}

PrimeGrid's author Rytis Slatkevičius has been featured as a young entrepreneur in *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist)*.[^o86]

PrimeGrid has also been featured in an article by [Francois Grey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francois_Grey) in the *[CERN Courier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN_Courier)* and a talk about citizen cyberscience in [TEDx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TEDx) Warwick conference.[^o87][^o88]

In the first [Citizen Cyberscience Summit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizen_Cyberscience_Summit), Rytis Slatkevičius gave a talk as a founder of PrimeGrid, named *Finding primes: from digits to digital technology*,[^o89] relating mathematics and volunteering and featuring the history of the project.[^o90]
