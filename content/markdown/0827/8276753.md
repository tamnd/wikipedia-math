# Duncan Sommerville

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Duncan Sommerville"}

**Duncan MacLaren Young Sommerville** (1879–1934) was a Scottish mathematician and astronomer. He compiled a bibliography on [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) and also wrote a leading textbook in that field. He also wrote *Introduction to the Geometry of N Dimensions*, advancing the study of [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471). He was a co-founder and the first secretary of the [New Zealand Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Astronomical_Society).

:::

Sommerville was also an accomplished watercolourist, producing a series of New Zealand landscapes.

The middle name 'MacLaren' is spelt using the old [orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthography) **M'Laren** in some sources, for example the records of the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh).[^o3]

## Early life {#Early_life}

Sommerville was born on 24 November 1879 in [Beawar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beawar) in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India), where his father, the Rev Dr James Sommerville, was employed as a missionary by the [United Presbyterian Church of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Presbyterian_Church_of_Scotland). His father had been responsible for establishing the hospital at [Jodhpur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jodhpur), [Rajputana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajputana).

The family returned home to [Perth, Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perth,_Scotland), where Duncan spent 4 years at a private school, before completing his education at [Perth Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perth_Academy). His father died in his youth. He lived with his mother at 12 Rose Terrace.[^o5] Despite his father's death, he won a scholarship, allowing him to continue his studies to university level.[^o6]

He then studied mathematics at the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews) in [Fife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fife), graduating MA in 1902. He then began as an assistant lecturer at the university. In 1905 he gained his doctorate ([DSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science)) for his thesis *Networks of the Plane in Absolute Geometry* and was promoted to lecturer. He continued teaching mathematics at St Andrews until 1915.[^o7]

In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) the method of [Cayley–Klein metrics](wikipedia://en/30794512) had been used in the 19th century to model [non-euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-euclidean_geometry). In 1910 Sommerville wrote "Classification of geometries with projective metrics".[^o8] The classification is described by Daniel Corey as follows:

He classifies them into 9 types of plane geometries, 27 in dimension 3, and more generally 3<sup>n</sup> in dimension n. A number of these geometries have found applications, for instance in physics.

In 1910 Sommerville reported[^o10] to the [British Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Association) on the need for a [bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliography) on [non-euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-euclidean_geometry), noting that the field had no International Association like the [Quaternion Association](wikipedia://en/16416512) to sponsor it.

In 1911 Sommerville published his compiled bibliography of works on non-euclidean geometry, and it received favorable reviews.[^o11][^o12] In 1970 [Chelsea Publishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea_Publishing) issued a second edition which referred to [collected works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthology) then available of some of the cited authors.

Sommerville was elected a Fellow of the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh) in 1911. His proposers were [Peter Redford Scott Lang](wikipedia://en/53442033), [Robert Alexander Robertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Alexander_Robertson), [William Peddie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Peddie) and [George Chrystal](wikipedia://en/14008803).[^o14]

## Family {#Family}

In 1912 he married Louisa Agnes Beveridge.

## Work in New Zealand {#Work_in_New_Zealand}

*Image: Duncan MacLaren Young Sommerville 2.jpg.* Duncan MacLaren Young Sommerville

In 1915 Sommerville went to [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand) to take up the Chair of Pure and Applied Mathematics at the [Victoria College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Wellington) of Wellington.

He became interested in [honeycombs](wikipedia://en/6008654) and wrote "Division of space by congruent triangles and tetrahedra" in 1923.[^o17] The following year he extended results to *n*-dimensional space.[^o18]

He also discovered the [Dehn–Sommerville equations](wikipedia://en/8276156) for the number of faces of [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885).

Sommerville used geometry to describe the [voting theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_theory) of a [preferential ballot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preferential_ballot).[^o19] He addressed [Nanson's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanson's_method) where *n* candidates are ordered by voters into a sequence of preferences. Sommerville shows that the outcomes lie in *n* ! [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) that cover the surface of an *n* − 2 dimensional spherical space.

When his *Introduction to Geometry of N Dimensions* appeared in 1929, it received a positive review from B. C. Wong in the [American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly).[^o20]

Sommerville was co-founder and first secretary of the [New Zealand Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand_Astronomical_Society) (1920). He was President of Section A of the [Australasian Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasian_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science) meeting, Adelaide (1924). In 1926 he became a fellow of the [Royal Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Astronomical_Society).

He died in New Zealand on 31 January 1934.
