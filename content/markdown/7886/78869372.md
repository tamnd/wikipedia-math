# Tree-like curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tree-like curve"}

*Image: Tree-like curve.svg.* A tree-like curve with finitely many marked double points

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a **tree-like curve** is a [generic](wikipedia://en/383736) [immersion](wikipedia://en/6993953) $c: S^1 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ with the property that removing any [double point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_point) splits the curve into exactly two [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29). This property gives these curves a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560)-like structure, hence their name. They were first systematically studied by Russian [mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematicians) [Boris Shapiro](wikipedia://en/46545446) and [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490) in the 1990s.[^o2][^o3]

:::

For generic curves interpreted as the shadows of [knots](wikipedia://en/153008) (that is, [knot diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_diagram) from which the over-under relations at each crossing have been erased), the tree-like curves can only be shadows of the [unknot](wikipedia://en/60075). As knot diagrams, these represent [connected sums](wikipedia://en/490134) of [figure-eight curves](wikipedia://en/5210590). Each figure-eight is unknotted and their connected sum remains unknotted. Random curves with few crossings are likely to be tree-like, and therefore random knot diagrams with few crossings are likely to be unknotted.[^o4]
