# Arnold invariants

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arnold invariants"}

*Image: Vladimir Arnold-1.jpg.* Mathematician [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490)

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008), **Arnold invariants** are [invariants](wikipedia://en/166898) introduced by [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490) in 1994[^o2] for studying the topology and geometry of [plane curves](wikipedia://en/971691). The three main invariants—$J^+$, $J^-$, and $St$—provide ways to classify and understand how curves can be deformed while preserving certain properties.[^o3]

:::

## Background {#Background}

The fundamental context for Arnold invariants comes from the [Whitney–Graustein theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney–Graustein_theorem), which states that any two [immersed](wikipedia://en/6993953) loops (smooth curves in the plane) with the same [rotation number](wikipedia://en/3928569) can be [deformed](wikipedia://en/679351) into each other through a series of [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [transformations](wikipedia://en/1904373).[^o5] These transformations can be broken down into three elementary types: direct *self-tangency moves* (where two portions of the curve become tangent with aligned directions, either creating or eliminating two self-intersection points), *inverse self-tangency moves* (similar to direct moves, but the tangent directions are opposite), and *triple point moves* (where three portions of the curve intersect at a single point).[^o6]

## *J<sup>±</sup>* invariants {#J±_invariants}

The $J^+$ and $J^-$ invariants keep track of how curves change under these transformations and deformations. The $J^+$ invariant increases by 2 when a direct self-tangency move creates new self-intersection points (and decreases by 2 when such points are eliminated), while $J^-$ decreases by 2 when an inverse self-tangency move creates new intersections (and increases by 2 when they are eliminated). Neither invariant changes under triple point moves. A fundamental relationship between these invariants is that their difference equals the total number of self-intersection points in the curve. That is,

$J^+(c) - J^-(c) = \text{number of self-intersection points of }c$.[^o8]

Mathematicians [Oleg Viro](wikipedia://en/14465687) and Eugene Gutkin discovered an [explicit formula](wikipedia://en/585143) for calculating $J^-$:

$J^-(c) = 1 - \sum_R \text{wind}(c,R)^2 + \sum_q \text{meanwind}(c,q)^2$[^o8]

where $R$ ranges over the regions into which $c$ divides the plane, $\text{wind}(c,R)$ is the [winding number](wikipedia://en/86113) around a point in region $R$, and $\text{meanwind}(c,q)$ is the mean winding number at each self-intersection point $q$. For example, a curve with $k$ curls in standard form has $J^+ = -2k$ and $J^- = -3k$, while a simple circle has $J^+ = J^- = 0$.[^o6]

## Bridges and channels {#Bridges_and_channels}

In 2002, mathematicians Catarina Mendes de Jesus and Maria Carmen Romero Fuster introduced the concepts of *bridges and channels* for plane curves to facilitate the calculation of Arnold invariants.[^o10] A *bridge* consists of introducing a rectangle in the [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_complement) of the curve in the plane while respecting [orientations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29), decomposing a given curve into two smaller curves with known invariants. The invariant of the original curve can then be obtained as a function of the invariants of these two component curves and the index of the bridge relative to the original curve. This decomposition technique is particularly powerful for analyzing curves with double points.

An important theorem regarding this decomposition states that a curve with $n$ double points is a [tree-like curve](wikipedia://en/78869372) if and only if it admits a decomposition into exactly n curves of types $K_0$ and $K_2$ with bridges having no double points, or a decomposition into exactly $n+1$ curves of type $K_1$ ([isotopic](wikipedia://en/212250#Isotopy) to the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220)) with bridges having double points.[^o11] This result proved a conjecture originally proposed by Arnold regarding the formulas for families of tree-like curves. The bridge and channel technique provides a systematic method for computing Arnold invariants for plane curves in terms of simpler curves with at most one double point.
