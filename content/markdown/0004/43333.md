# Measurable space

Measure space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Measurable space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **measurable space** or **Borel space**[^o10] is a basic object in [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory). It consists of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) and a [σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra), which defines the [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) that will be measured.

:::

It captures and generalises intuitive notions such as length, area, and volume with a set $X$ of 'points' in the space, but *regions* of the space are the elements of the [σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra), since the intuitive measures are not usually defined for points. The algebra also captures the relationships that might be expected of regions: that a region can be defined as an intersection of other regions, a union of other regions, or the space with the exception of another region.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Consider a set $X$ and a [σ-algebra](wikipedia://en/29586) $\mathcal F$ on $X.$ Then the tuple $(X, \mathcal F)$ is called a measurable space.[^o12] The elements of $\mathcal F$ are called **measurable sets** within the measurable space.

Note that in contrast to a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270), no [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) is needed for a measurable space.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Look at the set:

$$X = \{1,2,3\}.$$

One possible $\sigma$-algebra would be:

$$\mathcal {F}_1 = \{X, \varnothing\}.$$

Then $\left(X, \mathcal{F}_1 \right)$ is a measurable space. Another possible $\sigma$-algebra would be the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) on $X$:

$$\mathcal{F}_2 = \mathcal P(X).$$

With this, a second measurable space on the set $X$ is given by $\left(X, \mathcal F_2\right).$

:::

## Common measurable spaces {#Common_measurable_spaces}

If $X$ is finite or countably infinite, the $\sigma$-algebra is most often the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) on $X,$ so $\mathcal{F} = \mathcal P(X).$ This leads to the measurable space $(X, \mathcal P(X)).$

If $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), the $\sigma$-algebra is most commonly the [Borel $\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra) $\mathcal B,$ so $\mathcal{F} = \mathcal B(X).$ This leads to the measurable space $(X, \mathcal B(X))$ that is common for all topological spaces such as the real numbers $\mathbb{R}.$

## Ambiguity with Borel spaces {#Ambiguity_with_Borel_spaces}

The term Borel space is used for different types of measurable spaces. It can refer to

- any measurable space, so it is a synonym for a measurable space as defined above [^o10]
- a measurable space that is [Borel isomorphic](wikipedia://en/33605690) to a measurable subset of the real numbers (again with the Borel $\sigma$-algebra)[^o11]
