# Exponential distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Exponential distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **exponential distribution** or **negative exponential distribution** is the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the distance between events in a [Poisson point process](wikipedia://en/41597450), i.e., a process in which events occur continuously and independently at a constant average rate; the distance parameter could be any meaningful mono-dimensional measure of the process, such as time between production errors, or length along a roll of fabric in the weaving manufacturing process.[^o2] It is a particular case of the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079). It is the continuous analogue of the [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922), and it has the key property of being [memoryless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoryless).[^o3] In addition to being used for the analysis of Poisson point processes it is found in various other contexts.[^o4]

:::

The exponential distribution is not the same as the class of [exponential families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_families) of distributions. This is a large class of probability distributions that includes the exponential distribution as one of its members, but also includes many other distributions, such as the [normal](wikipedia://en/21462), [binomial](wikipedia://en/3876), [gamma](wikipedia://en/207079), and [Poisson](wikipedia://en/23009144) distributions.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Probability density function {#Probability_density_function}

The [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) (pdf) of an exponential distribution is

$$f(x;\lambda) = \begin{cases} \lambda e^{ - \lambda x} & x \ge 0, \\ 0 & x < 0. \end{cases}$$

Here *λ* > 0 is the parameter of the distribution, often called the *rate parameter*. The distribution is supported on the interval 0, ∞. If a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* has this distribution, we write $X \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)$.

The exponential distribution exhibits [infinite divisibility](wikipedia://en/13588803).

### Cumulative distribution function {#Cumulative_distribution_function}

The [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) is given by

$$F(x;\lambda) = \begin{cases} 1-e^{-\lambda x} & x \ge 0, \\ 0 & x < 0. \end{cases}$$

### Alternative parametrization {#Alternative_parametrization}

The exponential distribution is sometimes parametrized in terms of the [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503) $\beta = 1/\lambda$, which is also the mean:

$$f(x;\beta) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\beta} e^{-x/\beta} & x \ge 0, \\ 0 & x < 0. \end{cases} \qquad\qquad F(x;\beta) = \begin{cases} 1- e^{-x/\beta} & x \ge 0, \\ 0 & x < 0. \end{cases}$$

## Properties {#Properties}

### Mean, variance, moments, and median {#Mean,_variance,_moments,_and_median}

*Image: Mean exp.svg.* The mean is the probability mass centre, that is, the [first moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_moment).

*Image: Median exp.svg.* The median is the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) *F*<sup>−1</sup>(1/2).

The mean or [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of an exponentially distributed random variable *X* with rate parameter *λ* is given by

$$\operatorname{E}[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda}.$$

In light of the examples given below, this makes sense; a person who receives an average of two telephone calls per hour can expect that the time between consecutive calls will be 0.5 hour, or 30 minutes.

The [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of *X* is given by

$$\operatorname{Var}[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda^2},$$

so the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) is equal to the mean.

The [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) of *X*, for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ are given by

$$\operatorname{E}\left[X^n\right] = \frac{n!}{\lambda^n}.$$

The [central moments](wikipedia://en/19983) of *X*, for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ are given by

$$\mu_n = \frac{!n}{\lambda^n} = \frac{n!}{\lambda^n}\sum^n_{k=0}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}.$$

where !*n* is the [subfactorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subfactorial) of *n*.

The [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of *X* is given by

$$\operatorname{m}[X] = \frac{\ln(2)}{\lambda} < \operatorname{E}[X],$$

where $\ln$ refers to the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476). Thus the [absolute difference](wikipedia://en/28021681) between the mean and median is

$$\left|\operatorname{E}\left[X\right] - \operatorname{m}\left[X\right]\right| = \frac{1 - \ln(2)}{\lambda} < \frac{1}{\lambda} = \operatorname{\sigma}[X],$$

in accordance with the [median-mean inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median-mean_inequality).

### Memorylessness property of exponential random variable {#Memorylessness_property_of_exponential_random_variable}

An exponentially distributed random variable *T* obeys the relation

$$\Pr \left (T > s + t \mid T > s \right ) = \Pr(T > t), \qquad \forall s, t \ge 0.$$

This can be seen by considering the [complementary cumulative distribution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_cumulative_distribution_function):

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr\left(T > s + t \mid T > s\right) &= \frac{\Pr\left(T > s + t \cap T > s\right)}{\Pr\left(T > s\right)} \\[4pt] &= \frac{\Pr\left(T > s + t \right)}{\Pr\left(T > s\right)} \\[4pt] &= \frac{e^{-\lambda(s + t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} \\[4pt] &= e^{-\lambda t} \\[4pt] &= \Pr(T > t). \end{aligned}$$

When *T* is interpreted as the waiting time for an event to occur relative to some initial time, this relation implies that, if *T* is conditioned on a failure to observe the event over some initial period of time *s*, the distribution of the remaining waiting time is the same as the original unconditional distribution. For example, if an event has not occurred after 30 seconds, the [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) that occurrence will take at least 10 more seconds is equal to the unconditional probability of observing the event more than 10 seconds after the initial time.

The exponential distribution and the [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922) are [the only memoryless probability distributions](wikipedia://en/772441).

The exponential distribution is consequently also necessarily the only continuous probability distribution that has a constant [failure rate](wikipedia://en/1336960).

### Quantiles {#Quantiles}

*Image: Tukey anomaly criteria for Exponential PDF.png.* Tukey criteria for anomalies.

The [quantile function](wikipedia://en/10167616) (inverse cumulative distribution function) for Exp(*λ*) is

$$F^{-1}(p;\lambda) = \frac{-\ln(1-p)}{\lambda},\qquad 0 \le p < 1$$

The [quartiles](wikipedia://en/25170) are therefore:

- first quartile: ln(4/3)/*λ*
- [median](wikipedia://en/18837): ln(2)/*λ*
- third quartile: ln(4)/*λ*

And as a consequence the [interquartile range](wikipedia://en/14812) is ln(3)/*λ*.

### Conditional Value at Risk (Expected Shortfall) {#Conditional_Value_at_Risk_(Expected_Shortfall)}

The conditional value at risk (CVaR) also known as the [expected shortfall](wikipedia://en/8307819) or superquantile for Exp(*λ*) is derived as follows:[^o16]

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{q}_\alpha (X) &= \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \int_{\alpha}^{1} q_p (X) dp \\ &= \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)} \int_{\alpha}^{1} \frac{-\ln (1 - p )}{\lambda} dp \\ &= \frac{-1}{\lambda(1-\alpha)} \int_{1-\alpha}^{0} -\ln (y ) dy \\ &= \frac{-1}{\lambda(1-\alpha)} \int_{0}^{1 - \alpha} \ln (y ) dy \\ &= \frac{-1}{\lambda(1-\alpha)} [ ( 1-\alpha) \ln(1-\alpha) - (1-\alpha) ] \\ &= \frac{ - \ln(1-\alpha) + 1 } { \lambda} \\ \end{aligned}$$

### Buffered Probability of Exceedance (bPOE) {#Buffered_Probability_of_Exceedance_(bPOE)}

The buffered probability of exceedance is one minus the probability level at which the CVaR equals the threshold $x$. It is derived as follows:[^o16]

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{p}_x (X) &= \{ 1 - \alpha | \bar{q}_\alpha (X) = x \} \\ &= \{ 1 - \alpha |\frac{ - \ln(1-\alpha) + 1 } { \lambda} = x \} \\ &= \{ 1 - \alpha | \ln(1-\alpha) = 1-\lambda x \} \\ &= \{ 1 - \alpha | e^{\ln(1-\alpha)} = e^{1-\lambda x} \} = \{ 1 - \alpha | 1-\alpha = e^{1-\lambda x} \} = e^{1-\lambda x} \end{aligned}$$

### Kullback–Leibler divergence {#Kullback–Leibler_divergence}

The directed [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) in [nats](wikipedia://en/3070481) of $e^\lambda$ ("approximating" distribution) from $e^{\lambda_0}$ ('true' distribution) is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta(\lambda_0 \parallel \lambda) &= \mathbb{E}_{\lambda_0}\left( \log \frac{p_{\lambda_0}(x)}{p_\lambda(x)}\right)\\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\lambda_0}\left( \log \frac{\lambda_0 e^{\lambda_0 x}}{\lambda e^{\lambda x}}\right)\\ &= \log(\lambda_0) - \log(\lambda) - (\lambda_0 - \lambda)E_{\lambda_0}(x)\\ &= \log(\lambda_0) - \log(\lambda) + \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0} - 1. \end{aligned}$$

### Maximum entropy distribution {#Maximum_entropy_distribution}

Among all continuous probability distributions with [support](wikipedia://en/381013#In_probability_and_measure_theory) 0, ∞ and mean *μ*, the exponential distribution with *λ* = 1/*μ* has the largest [differential entropy](wikipedia://en/3504168). In other words, it is the [maximum entropy probability distribution](wikipedia://en/1813193) for a [random variate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variate) *X* which is greater than or equal to zero and for which E\[*X*\] is fixed.[^o20]

### Multiplication with a constant {#Multiplication_with_a_constant}

For a random variable $X\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ with parameter $\lambda$, the variable multiplied with a constant $c>0$ is again exponentially distributed:

$$cX\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda/c).$$

### Minimum of exponential random variables {#Minimum_of_exponential_random_variables}

Let *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> be [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_random_variables) exponentially distributed random variables with rate parameters *λ*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *λ<sub>n</sub>*. Then[^o23]

$$\min\left\{X_1, \dotsc, X_n \right\}$$

is also exponentially distributed, with parameter

$$\lambda = \lambda_1 + \dotsb + \lambda_n.$$

This can be seen by considering the [complementary cumulative distribution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_cumulative_distribution_function):

$$\begin{aligned} &\Pr\left(\min\{X_1, \dotsc, X_n\} > x\right) \\ ={} &\Pr\left(X_1 > x, \dotsc, X_n > x\right) \\ ={} &\prod_{i=1}^n \Pr\left(X_i > x\right) \\ ={} &\prod_{i=1}^n \exp\left(-x\lambda_i\right) = \exp\left(-x\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\right). \end{aligned}$$

The index of the variable which achieves the minimum is distributed according to the categorical distribution

$$\Pr\left(X_k = \min\{X_1, \dotsc, X_n\}\right) = \frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_1 + \dotsb + \lambda_n}.$$

A proof can be seen by letting $I = \operatorname{argmin}_{i \in \{1, \dotsb, n\}}\{X_1, \dotsc, X_n\}$. Then,

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr (I = k) &= \int_{0}^{\infty} \Pr(X_k = x) \Pr(\forall_{i\neq k}X_{i} > x ) \,dx \\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty} \lambda_k e^{- \lambda_k x} \left(\prod_{i=1, i\neq k}^{n} e^{- \lambda_i x}\right) dx \\ &= \lambda_k \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{- \left(\lambda_1 + \dotsb +\lambda_n\right) x} dx \\ &= \frac{\lambda_k}{\lambda_1 + \dotsb + \lambda_n}. \end{aligned}$$

In contrast, $\max\{X_1, \dotsc, X_n\}$ is not exponentially distributed, if *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> do not all have parameter 0.[^o24]

### Joint moments of i.i.d. exponential order statistics {#Joint_moments_of_i.i.d._exponential_order_statistics}

Let $X_1, \dotsc, X_n$ be $n$ [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed) exponential random variables with rate parameter *λ*. Let $X_{(1)}, \dotsc, X_{(n)}$ denote the corresponding [order statistics](wikipedia://en/160986). For $i < j$ , the joint moment $\operatorname E\left[X_{(i)} X_{(j)}\right]$ of the order statistics $X_{(i)}$ and $X_{(j)}$ is given by

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname E\left[X_{(i)} X_{(j)}\right] &= \sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{1}{(n - k)\lambda} \operatorname E\left[X_{(i)}\right] + \operatorname E\left[X_{(i)}^2\right] \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{1}{(n - k)\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{1}{(n - k)\lambda} + \sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{1}{((n - k)\lambda)^2} + \left(\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{1}{(n - k)\lambda}\right)^2. \end{aligned}$$

This can be seen by invoking the [law of total expectation](wikipedia://en/312397) and the memoryless property:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname E\left[X_{(i)} X_{(j)}\right] &= \int_0^\infty \operatorname E\left[X_{(i)} X_{(j)} \mid X_{(i)}=x\right] f_{X_{(i)}}(x) \, dx \\ &= \int_{x=0}^\infty x \operatorname E\left[X_{(j)} \mid X_{(j)} \geq x\right] f_{X_{(i)}}(x) \, dx &&\left(\textrm{since}~X_{(i)} = x \implies X_{(j)} \geq x\right) \\ &= \int_{x=0}^\infty x \left[ \operatorname E\left[X_{(j)}\right] + x \right] f_{X_{(i)}}(x) \, dx &&\left(\text{by the memoryless property}\right) \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{1}{(n - k)\lambda} \operatorname E\left[X_{(i)}\right] + \operatorname E\left[X_{(i)}^2\right]. \end{aligned}$$

The first equation follows from the [law of total expectation](wikipedia://en/312397). The second equation exploits the fact that once we condition on $X_{(i)} = x$, it must follow that $X_{(j)} \geq x$. The third equation relies on the memoryless property to replace $\operatorname E\left[ X_{(j)} \mid X_{(j)} \geq x\right]$ with $\operatorname E\left[X_{(j)}\right] + x$.

### Sum of two independent exponential random variables {#Sum_of_two_independent_exponential_random_variables}

The probability distribution function (PDF) of a sum of two independent random variables is the [convolution of their individual PDFs](wikipedia://en/32251002). If $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent exponential random variables with respective rate parameters $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2,$ then the probability density of $Z=X_1+X_2$ is given by

$$\begin{aligned} f_Z(z) &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_{X_1}(x_1) f_{X_2}(z - x_1)\,dx_1\\ &= \int_0^z \lambda_1 e^{-\lambda_1 x_1} \lambda_2 e^{-\lambda_2(z - x_1)} \, dx_1 \\ &= \lambda_1 \lambda_2 e^{-\lambda_2 z} \int_0^z e^{(\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)x_1}\,dx_1 \\ &= \begin{cases} \dfrac{\lambda_1 \lambda_2}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1} \left(e^{-\lambda_1 z} - e^{-\lambda_2 z}\right) & \text{ if } \lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2 \\[4 pt] \lambda^2 z e^{-\lambda z} & \text{ if } \lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda. \end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

The entropy of this distribution is available in closed form: assuming $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2$ (without loss of generality), then

$$\begin{aligned} H(Z) &= 1 + \gamma + \ln \left( \frac{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}{\lambda_1 \lambda_2} \right) + \psi \left( \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2} \right) , \end{aligned}$$

where $\gamma$ is the [Euler-Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler-Mascheroni_constant), and $\psi(\cdot)$ is the [digamma function](wikipedia://en/406935).[^o27]

In the case of equal rate parameters, the result is an [Erlang distribution](wikipedia://en/199028) with shape 2 and parameter $\lambda,$ which in turn is a special case of [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079).

The sum of n independent Exp(*λ)* exponential random variables is Gamma(n, *λ)* distributed.

## Related distributions {#Related_distributions}

In this section, the following conventions are used:

- $U(a,b)$ for the [(continuous) uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/1699223) on the interval $(a,b)$ with $a<b$.
- $\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)$ for the [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) with parameters $a,b>0$.
- $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ for the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with parameter $\lambda>0$.
- $\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\lambda)$ for the [Gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) with shape parameter $\alpha>0$ and *rate* parameter $\lambda>0$.
- $\mathrm{Erlang}(k,\lambda)$ for the [Erlang distribution](wikipedia://en/199028) with shape parameter $k\in\mathbb{N}$ and rate parameter $\lambda>0$.
- $\chi^2_n$ for the [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) with $n$ degrees of freedom.
- $\mathrm{Pareto}(x_m,\alpha)$ the Pareto distribution with scale parameter $x_m>0$ and shape parameter $\alpha>0$.
- $\mathrm{Laplace}(\mu,b)$ for the [Laplace distribution](wikipedia://en/1106213) with location $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ and scale parameter $b>0$.
- $\mathrm{SkewLogistic}(\theta)$ for the [type I generalized logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/15841869#Type_I) with parameter $\theta>0$.
- $\mathrm{Reighley}(\sigma)$ for the [Reyleigh distribution](wikipedia://en/262296) with parameter $\sigma>0$.
- $\mathrm{Weibull}(\lambda,k)$ for the [Weibull distribution](wikipedia://en/206948) with scale and shape parameters $\lambda,k>0$.
- $\mathrm{Gumbel}(\mu,\beta)$ for the [Gumbel distribution](wikipedia://en/270827) with location parameter $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ and scale parameter $\beta>0$.
- $\mathrm{Logistic}(\mu,\beta)$ for the [logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/542666) with location parameter $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ and scale parameter $\beta>0$.
- $\mathrm{Geometric}_0(p)$ the [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922) with success probability $p\in(0,1]$ and support $\mathbb{N}_0=\{0,1,2,\dots\}$.
- $\mathrm{Geometric}_1(p)$ the [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922) with success probability $p\in(0,1]$ and support $\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}$.
- $\mathrm{Lomax}(\alpha,\lambda)$ for the [Lomax distribution](wikipedia://en/32253318) with shape parameter $\alpha>0$ and scale parameter $\lambda>0$.
- $\mathrm{GEV}(\mu,\sigma,\xi)$ for the [generalized extreme value distribution](wikipedia://en/1494479) with location parameter $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$, scale parameter $\sigma>0$ and shape parameter $\xi\in\mathbb{R}$.
- $\mathrm{BenktanderWeibull}(a,b)$ for the [Benktander type II distribution](wikipedia://en/31101129) with parameters $a>0$ and $b\in(0,1]$.

### Special cases of other distributions {#Special_cases_of_other_distributions}

- The exponential distribution is a special case of type 3 [Pearson distribution](wikipedia://en/1804023).
- For any $\lambda>0$: $\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\sim \operatorname{Weibull}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}, 1\right)$$=\mathrm{Gamma}(1,\lambda)$$=\mathrm{Erlang}(1,\lambda)$.[^o31]
- $$\mathrm{Exp}(1/2)=\chi^2_2.$$

### Functional relationships to other distributions {#Functional_relationships_to_other_distributions}

- If *X* ~ [Laplace](wikipedia://en/1106213)(μ, β${}^{-1}$), then |*X* − μ| ~ Exp(β).[^o31] On the other hand, if *X*, *Y* ~ Exp(λ) are [independent](wikipedia://en/27593), then *X*-*Y* ~ [Laplace](wikipedia://en/1106213)(0, λ${}^{-1}$). Also, if *X* ~ Exp(λ${}_{1}$) and *Y* ~ Exp(λ${}_{2}$) are independent, then λ${}_{1}$*X* - λ${}_{2}$*Y* ~ [Laplace](wikipedia://en/1106213)(0, 1).
- If *X* ~ [Beta(λ, 1)](wikipedia://en/207074), then −log(*X*) ~ Exp(λ). Equivalently, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then *e<sup>−Y</sup>* ~ [Beta(λ, 1)](wikipedia://en/207074).[^o31]
- As a special case of the above: If *X* ~ [*U*(0, 1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) then −log(*X*)/λ ~ Exp(λ). Equivalently, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then *e<sup>−λY</sup>* ~ [*U*(0, 1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29). This is a direct consequence of the [probability integral transform](wikipedia://en/8405353).
- If *X* ~ [Pareto(k, λ)](wikipedia://en/53057), then log(*X*/k) ~ Exp(λ). Equivalently, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then *ke<sup>Y</sup>* ~ [Pareto(*k*, λ)](wikipedia://en/53057).[^o31]
- If *X* ~ [SkewLogistic(θ)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-logistic_distribution), then $\log\left(1 + e^{-X}\right) \sim \operatorname{Exp}(\theta)$. Equivalently, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then −log(e${}^{Y}$−1) ~ [SkewLogistic(λ)](wikipedia://en/15841869).
- If *X* ~ [Rayleigh(σ)](wikipedia://en/262296), then $X^2\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1/(2\sigma^2))$. Equivalently, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then $\sqrt{Y} \sim \operatorname{Rayleigh} \left(1/\sqrt{2\lambda}\right)$.[^o31]
- If *X* ~ [Weibull(λ, k)](wikipedia://en/206948), then *X*${}^{k}$ ~ Exp(1/λ${}^{k}$).[^o32] In particular, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then *Y*${}^{2}$ ~ [Weibull(1/λ${}^{2}$, 1/2)](wikipedia://en/206948).[^o31]
- If *X* ~ [Gumbel(μ, β)](wikipedia://en/270827), then exp((*X*-μ)/β) ~ Exp(1). Equivalently, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then μ − β log(λ*X*) ∼ [Gumbel](wikipedia://en/270827)(μ, β) = [GEV(μ, σ, 0)](wikipedia://en/1494479).
- If *X* ~ [BenktanderWeibull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benktander_Weibull_distribution)(λ, 1), then *X* - 1 ~ Exp(λ). Equivalently, if *Y* ~ Exp(λ), then 1 + *Y* ~ [BenktanderWeibull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benktander_Weibull_distribution)(λ, 1), which reduces to a truncated exponential distribution.
- If *X* ~ Exp(λ), then $\lfloor X\rfloor \sim \operatorname{Geometric}_0\left(1-e^{-\lambda}\right)$, $\lceil X\rceil \sim \operatorname{Geometric}_1\left(1-e^{-\lambda}\right)$ and $\tfrac{1}{k}$*e*${}^{X}$ ~ [PowerLaw](wikipedia://en/24522)(*k*, λ).
- If *X* ~ Exp(1), then $\mu - \beta\log\left(\frac{e^{-X}}{1 - e^{-X}}\right) \sim \operatorname{Logistic}(\mu, \beta)$.
- If *X*, *Y* ~ Exp(1) are independent, then *X*/*Y* ~ [Lomax](wikipedia://en/32253318)(1, 1) and $\mu - \beta\log\left(\frac{X}{Y}\right) \sim \operatorname{Logistic}(\mu, \beta)$. The former can be used to obtain a [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) for quotients of rates $\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_j}$.
- If *X* ~ Exp(1) and $Y \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\left(\alpha, \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)$ are independent, then $\sqrt{XY} \sim \operatorname{K}(\alpha, \beta)$.
- If *X* ~ Exp(λ) and λ ~ [Gamma](wikipedia://en/207079)(*k*, Λ), then the marginal distribution of *X* is [Lomax](wikipedia://en/32253318)(*k*, Λ), the gamma [mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_distribution).
- If *X* ~ Exp(λ) and $Y \mid X$ ~ [Poisson](wikipedia://en/23009144)(*X*), then $Y \sim \operatorname{Geometric}_0\left(\frac{1}{1 + \lambda^{-1}}\right)$.
- If *Y<sub>i</sub>* ~ [*U*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29)(0, 1), then $$n \min \left(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\right) \ \overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\ \operatorname{Exp}(1).$$ Since $\min \left(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\right)\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,n)$, the exponential distribution is therefore a ([weak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_convergence_of_measures)) limit of a scaled [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074): $$n \operatorname{Beta}(1, n) \ \overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\ \operatorname{Exp}(1).$$
- The [Hoyt distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoyt_distribution) can be obtained from exponential distribution and [arcsine distribution](wikipedia://en/26400022).
- The exponential distribution is a limit of the [*κ*-exponential distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaniadakis_Exponential_distribution) in the $\kappa = 0$ case.
- Exponential distribution is a limit of the κ-Generalized Gamma distribution in the $\alpha = 1$ and $\nu = 1$ cases:

$$\lim_{(\alpha,\nu)\to(0,1)} p_\kappa(x) = (1+\kappa\nu)(2\kappa)^\nu \frac{\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2\kappa}+\frac{\nu}{2}\Big)}{\Gamma\Big(\frac{1}{2\kappa}-\frac{\nu}{2}\Big)} \frac{\alpha \lambda^\nu}{\Gamma(\nu)} x^{\alpha\nu-1}\exp_\kappa(-\lambda x^\alpha) = \lambda e^{ - \lambda x}.$$

Indirectly related distributions:

- [Hyper-exponential distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyper-exponential_distribution) – the distribution whose density is a weighted sum of exponential densities.
- [Hypoexponential distribution](wikipedia://en/1873318) – the distribution of a general sum of exponential random variables.[^o31]
- [exGaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExGaussian_distribution) – the sum of an exponential distribution and a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462).

### Sequences of exponential variables {#Sequences_of_exponential_variables}

In the following, let *X, X*${}_{1}$, ..., *X*${}_{n}$ ~ Exp(λ) be all [independent](wikipedia://en/27593).

- $X_1 + \cdots + X_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \sim$ [Erlang](wikipedia://en/199028)(*n*, λ) = [Gamma](wikipedia://en/207079)(*n*, λ).[^o34]
- If $T = X_1 + \cdots + X_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$, then $2 \lambda T \sim \chi^2_{2n}$.
- $\frac{X}{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i}\sim$ [Lomax](wikipedia://en/32253318)(*n*, 1) and $\frac{X}{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i}+1\sim$ [Pareto](wikipedia://en/53057)(1, *n*).
- $\frac{X}{X + \sum_{i=1}^n X_i}\sim$ [Beta](wikipedia://en/207074)(1, *n*).[^o35] In particular, $\frac{X}{X + X_1}\sim$ *[U](wikipedia://en/1699223)*(0,1).

## Statistical inference {#Statistical_inference}

Below, suppose random variable *X* is exponentially distributed with rate parameter λ, and $x_1, \dotsc, x_n$ are *n* independent samples from *X*, with sample mean $\bar{x}$.

### Parameter estimation {#Parameter_estimation}

#### Maximum Likelihood estimator {#Maximum_Likelihood_estimator}

The [maximum likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood) estimator for λ is constructed as follows.

The [likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968) for λ, given an [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed) sample *x* = (*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>) drawn from the variable, is:

$$L(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n\lambda\exp(-\lambda x_i) = \lambda^n\exp\left(-\lambda \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\right) = \lambda^n\exp\left(-\lambda n\overline{x}\right),$$

where:

$$\overline{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$$

is the sample mean.

The derivative of the likelihood function's logarithm is:

$$\frac{d}{d\lambda} \ln L(\lambda) = \frac{d}{d\lambda} \left( n \ln\lambda - \lambda n\overline{x} \right) = \frac{n}{\lambda} - n\overline{x}\ \begin{cases} > 0, & 0 < \lambda < \frac{1}{\overline{x}}, \\[8pt] = 0, & \lambda = \frac{1}{\overline{x}}, \\[8pt] < 0, & \lambda > \frac{1}{\overline{x}}. \end{cases}$$

Consequently, the [maximum likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood) estimate for the rate parameter is:

$$\widehat{\lambda}_\text{mle} = \frac{1}{\overline{x}} = \frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}$$

This is not an [unbiased estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiased_estimator) of $\lambda,$ although $\overline{x}$ is an unbiased[^o39] MLE[^o40] of $1/\lambda$ and the distribution mean. There are methods to reduce the bias of $\widehat{\lambda}_\text{mle}$ yielding a [bias-corrected maximum likelihood estimator](wikipedia://en/140806#Second-order_efficiency_after_correction_for_bias).

An approximate minimizer of the [mean squared error](wikipedia://en/201816) (see also: [bias–variance tradeoff](wikipedia://en/40678189)) can be found, assuming a sample size greater than two, with a correction factor to the MLE:

$$\widehat{\lambda} = \left(\frac{n - 2}{n}\right) \left(\frac{1}{\bar{x}}\right) = \frac{n - 2}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}$$

This is derived from the mean and variance of the [inverse-gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/1653565), $\mbox{Inv-Gamma}(n, \lambda)$.[^o41]

#### UMVUE {#UMVUE}

The [uniformly minimum-variance unbiased estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_minimum-variance_unbiased_estimator) for $\lambda$ is

$$\widehat\lambda_{\mathrm{umvue}} = \frac{n-1}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i},$$

which, in contrast to the MLE, is unbiased by definition.

### Fisher information {#Fisher_information}

The [Fisher information](wikipedia://en/598971), denoted $\mathcal{I}(\lambda)$, for an estimator of the rate parameter $\lambda$ is given as:

$$\mathcal{I}(\lambda) = \operatorname{E} \left[\left. \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda} \log f(x;\lambda)\right)^2\right|\lambda\right] = \int \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda} \log f(x;\lambda)\right)^2 f(x; \lambda)\,dx$$

Plugging in the distribution and solving gives:

$$\mathcal{I}(\lambda) = \int_{0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda} \log \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\right)^2 \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx = \int_{0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{\lambda} - x\right)^2 \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx = \lambda^{-2}.$$

This determines the amount of information each independent sample of an exponential distribution carries about the unknown rate parameter $\lambda$.

### Confidence intervals {#Confidence_intervals}

An exact 100(1 − α)% confidence interval for the rate parameter of an exponential distribution is given by:[^o45]

$$\frac{2n}{\widehat{\lambda}_{\textrm{mle}} \chi^2_{\frac{\alpha}{2},2n} }< \frac{1}{\lambda} < \frac{2n}{\widehat{\lambda}_{\textrm{mle}} \chi^2_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2},2n}}\,,$$

which is also equal to

$$\frac{2n\overline{x}}{\chi^2_{\frac{\alpha}{2},2n}} < \frac{1}{\lambda} < \frac{2n\overline{x}}{\chi^2_{1-\frac{\alpha}{2},2n}}\,,$$

where $\chi^{2}_{p,v}$ is the $100(p)$ [percentile](wikipedia://en/354907) of the [chi squared distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi_squared_distribution) with *v* [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606), n is the number of observations and x-bar is the sample average. A simple approximation to the exact interval endpoints can be derived using a normal approximation to the $\chi^{2}_{p,v}$ distribution. This approximation gives the following values for a 95% confidence interval:

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda_\text{lower} &= \widehat{\lambda}\left(1 - \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \\ \lambda_\text{upper} &= \widehat{\lambda}\left(1 + \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{n}}\right) \end{aligned}$$

### Bayesian inference with a conjugate prior {#Bayesian_inference_with_a_conjugate_prior}

The [conjugate prior](wikipedia://en/846412) for the exponential distribution is the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) (of which the exponential distribution is a special case). The following parameterization of the gamma probability density function is useful:

$$\operatorname{Gamma}(\lambda; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \lambda^{\alpha-1} \exp(-\lambda\beta).$$

The [posterior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_distribution) *p* can then be expressed in terms of the likelihood function defined above and a gamma prior:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\lambda) &\propto L(\lambda) \Gamma(\lambda; \alpha, \beta) \\ &= \lambda^n \exp\left(-\lambda n\overline{x}\right) \frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \lambda^{\alpha-1} \exp(-\lambda \beta) \\ &\propto \lambda^{(\alpha+n)-1} \exp(-\lambda \left(\beta + n\overline{x}\right)). \end{aligned}$$

Now the posterior density *p* has been specified up to a missing normalizing constant. Since it has the form of a gamma pdf, this can easily be filled in, and one obtains:

$$p(\lambda) = \operatorname{Gamma}(\lambda; \alpha + n, \beta + n\overline{x}).$$

Here the [hyperparameter](wikipedia://en/11641585) *α* can be interpreted as the number of prior observations, and *β* as the sum of the prior observations. The posterior mean here is:

$$\frac{\alpha + n}{\beta + n\overline{x}}.$$

### Bayesian inference with a calibrating prior {#Bayesian_inference_with_a_calibrating_prior}

The exponential distribution is one of a number of statistical distributions with [group](wikipedia://en/19447) structure. As a result of the group structure, the exponential has an associated [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645), which is $1/\lambda.$The use of the [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) as the [prior](wikipedia://en/472877) (known as the Haar prior) in a Bayesian prediction gives probabilities that are perfectly calibrated, for any underlying true parameter values.[^o48][^o49][^o50] Perfectly calibrated probabilities have the property that the predicted probabilities match the frequency of out-of-sample events exactly. For the exponential, there is an exact expression for Bayesian predictions generated using the Haar prior, given by

$$p_{\rm Haar-prior}(x_{n+1} \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \frac{ n^{n+1} \left( \overline{x} \right)^n }{ \left( n \overline{x} + x_{n+1} \right)^{n+1} }.$$

This is an example of calibrating prior prediction, in which the prior is chosen to improve calibration (and, in this case, to make the calibration perfect). Calibrating prior prediction for the exponential using the Haar prior is implemented in the [R](wikipedia://en/376707) software package fitdistcp.<https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/fitdistcp/index.html>

The same prediction can be derived from a number of other perspectives, as discussed in the [prediction](wikipedia://en/45906#Prediction) section below.

## Occurrence and applications {#Occurrence_and_applications}

### Occurrence of events {#Occurrence_of_events}

The exponential distribution occurs naturally when describing the lengths of the inter-arrival times in a homogeneous [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process).

The exponential distribution may be viewed as a continuous counterpart of the [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922), which describes the number of [Bernoulli trials](wikipedia://en/102653) necessary for a *discrete* process to change state. In contrast, the exponential distribution describes the time for a continuous process to change state.

In real-world scenarios, the assumption of a constant rate (or probability per unit time) is rarely satisfied. For example, the rate of incoming phone calls differs according to the time of day. But if we focus on a time interval during which the rate is roughly constant, such as from 2 to 4 p.m. during work days, the exponential distribution can be used as a good approximate model for the time until the next phone call arrives. Similar caveats apply to the following examples which yield approximately exponentially distributed variables:

- The time until a radioactive [particle decays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_decay), or the time between clicks of a [Geiger counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger_counter)
- The time between receiving one telephone call and the next
- The time until default (on payment to company debt holders) in reduced-form credit risk modeling

Exponential variables can also be used to model situations where certain events occur with a constant probability per unit length, such as the distance between [mutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation) on a [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) strand, or between [roadkills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roadkill) on a given road.

In [queuing theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queuing_theory), the service times of agents in a system (e.g. how long it takes for a bank teller etc. to serve a customer) are often modeled as exponentially distributed variables. (The arrival of customers for instance is also modeled by the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) if the arrivals are independent and distributed identically.) The length of a process that can be thought of as a sequence of several independent tasks follows the [Erlang distribution](wikipedia://en/199028) (which is the distribution of the sum of several independent exponentially distributed variables).

[Reliability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliability_theory) and [reliability engineering](wikipedia://en/1724836) also make extensive use of the exponential distribution. Because of the memoryless property of this distribution, it is well-suited to model the constant [hazard rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_rate) portion of the [bathtub curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathtub_curve) used in reliability theory. It is also very convenient because it is so easy to add [failure rates](wikipedia://en/1336960) in a reliability model. The exponential distribution is however not appropriate to model the overall lifetime of organisms or technical devices, because the "failure rates" here are not constant: more failures occur for very young and for very old systems.

*Image: FitExponDistr.tif.* Fitted cumulative exponential distribution to annually maximum 1-day rainfalls

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), if you observe a [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) at a fixed [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) and [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) in a uniform [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field), the heights of the various molecules also follow an approximate exponential distribution, known as the [Barometric formula](wikipedia://en/861530). This is a consequence of the entropy property mentioned below.

In [hydrology](wikipedia://en/13435), the exponential distribution is used to analyze extreme values of such variables as monthly and annual maximum values of daily rainfall and river discharge volumes.[^o53]

The rainfall data are represented by [plotting positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotting_position) as part of the [cumulative frequency analysis](wikipedia://en/45064451).

In operating-rooms management, the distribution of surgery duration for a category of surgeries with [no typical work-content](wikipedia://en/67412446) (like in an emergency room, encompassing all types of surgeries).

### Prediction {#Prediction}

Having observed a sample of *n* data points from an unknown exponential distribution a common task is to use these samples to make predictions about future data from the same source. A common predictive distribution over future samples is the so-called plug-in distribution, formed by plugging a suitable estimate for the rate parameter *λ* into the exponential density function. A common choice of estimate is the one provided by the principle of maximum likelihood, and using this yields the predictive density over a future sample *x*<sub>*n*+1</sub>, conditioned on the observed samples *x* = (*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x<sub>n</sub>*) given by

$$p_{\rm ML}(x_{n+1} \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \left( \frac1{\overline{x}} \right) \exp \left( - \frac{x_{n+1}}{\overline{x}} \right).$$

The Bayesian approach provides a predictive distribution which takes into account the uncertainty of the estimated parameter, although this may depend crucially on the choice of prior.

A predictive distribution free of the issues of choosing priors that arise under the subjective Bayesian approach is

$$p_{\rm CNML}(x_{n+1} \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \frac{ n^{n+1} \left( \overline{x} \right)^n }{ \left( n \overline{x} + x_{n+1} \right)^{n+1} },$$

which can be considered as

1. a frequentist [confidence distribution](wikipedia://en/31722662), obtained from the distribution of the pivotal quantity ${x_{n+1}}/{\overline{x}}$;[^o55]
1. a profile predictive likelihood, obtained by eliminating the parameter *λ* from the joint likelihood of *x*<sub>*n*+1</sub> and *λ* by maximization;[^o56]
1. an objective Bayesian predictive posterior distribution, obtained using the non-informative [Jeffreys prior](wikipedia://en/1901158) 1/*λ,* which is equal to the right Haar prior in this case. Predictions generated using the right Haar prior are guaranteed to give perfectly calibrated probabilities.[^o57][^o58]
1. the Conditional Normalized Maximum Likelihood (CNML) predictive distribution, from information theoretic considerations.[^o59]

The accuracy of a predictive distribution may be measured using the distance or divergence between the true exponential distribution with rate parameter, *λ*<sub>0</sub>, and the predictive distribution based on the sample *x*. The [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) is a commonly used, parameterisation free measure of the difference between two distributions. Letting Δ(*λ*<sub>0</sub>||*p*) denote the Kullback–Leibler divergence between an exponential with rate parameter *λ*<sub>0</sub> and a predictive distribution *p* it can be shown that

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}_{\lambda_0} \left[ \Delta(\lambda_0\parallel p_{\rm ML}) \right] &= \psi(n) + \frac{1}{n-1} - \log(n) \\ \operatorname{E}_{\lambda_0} \left[ \Delta(\lambda_0\parallel p_{\rm CNML}) \right] &= \psi(n) + \frac{1}{n} - \log(n) \end{aligned}$$

where the expectation is taken with respect to the exponential distribution with rate parameter *λ*<sub>0</sub> ∈ (0, ∞), and ψ( · ) is the digamma function. It is clear that the CNML predictive distribution is strictly superior to the maximum likelihood plug-in distribution in terms of average Kullback–Leibler divergence for all sample sizes *n* > 0.

## Random variate generation {#Random_variate_generation}

A conceptually very simple method for generating exponential [variates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variate) is based on [inverse transform sampling](wikipedia://en/45705): Given a random variate *U* drawn from the [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) on the unit interval 0, 1, the variate

$$T = F^{-1}(U)$$

has an exponential distribution, where *F*${}^{-1}$ is the [quantile function](wikipedia://en/10167616), defined by

$$F^{-1}(p)=\frac{-\ln(1-p)}{\lambda}.$$

Moreover, if *U* is uniform on (0, 1), then so is 1 − *U*. This means one can generate exponential variates as follows:

$$T = \frac{-\ln(U)}{\lambda}.$$

Other methods for generating exponential variates are discussed by Knuth[^o61] and Devroye.[^o62]

A fast method for generating a set of ready-ordered exponential variates without using a sorting routine is also available.[^o62]
