# Atlas (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Atlas"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), an **atlas** is a concept used to describe a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). An atlas consists of individual *charts* that, roughly speaking, describe individual regions of the manifold. In general, the notion of atlas underlies the formal definition of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) and related structures such as [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524) and other [fiber bundles](wikipedia://en/276526).

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Chart (topology)"}

## Charts {#Charts}

The definition of an atlas depends on the notion of a *chart*. A **chart** for a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *M* is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) $\varphi$ from an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) *U* of *M* to an open subset of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). The chart is traditionally recorded as the ordered pair $(U, \varphi)$.[^o4]

When a coordinate system is chosen in the Euclidean space, this defines coordinates on $U$: the coordinates of a point $P$ of $U$ are defined as the coordinates of $\varphi(P).$ The pair formed by a chart and such a coordinate system is called a **local coordinate system**, **coordinate chart**, **coordinate patch**, **coordinate map**, or **local frame**.

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition of atlas"}

## Formal definition of atlas {#Formal_definition_of_atlas}

An **atlas** for a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $M$ is an [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) $\{(U_{\alpha}, \varphi_{\alpha}) : \alpha \in I\}$ of charts on $M$ which [covers](wikipedia://en/317552) $M$ (that is, $\bigcup_{\alpha\in I} U_{\alpha} = M$). If for some fixed *n*, the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of each chart is an open subset of *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), then $M$ is said to be an *n*-dimensional [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470).

The plural of atlas is *atlases*, although some authors use *atlantes*.[^o7][^o8]

An atlas $\left( U_i, \varphi_i \right)_{i \in I}$ on an $n$-dimensional manifold $M$ is called an **adequate atlas** if the following conditions hold:

- The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of each chart is either $\mathbb{R}^n$ or $\mathbb{R}_+^n$, where $\mathbb{R}_+^n$ is the [closed half-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_half-space),
- $\left( U_i \right)_{i \in I}$ is a [locally finite](wikipedia://en/11611098) open cover of $M$, and
- $M = \bigcup_{i \in I} \varphi_i^{-1}\left( B_1 \right)$, where $B_1$ is the open ball of radius 1 centered at the origin.

Every [second-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-countable) manifold admits an adequate atlas.[^o9] Moreover, if $\mathcal{V} = \left( V_j \right)_{j \in J}$ is an open covering of the second-countable manifold $M$, then there is an adequate atlas $\left( U_i, \varphi_i \right)_{i \in I}$ on $M$, such that $\left( U_i\right)_{i \in I}$ is a [refinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refinement_of_a_cover) of $\mathcal{V}$.[^o9]

:::

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Transition map"}

## Transition maps {#Transition_maps}

A transition map provides a way of comparing two charts of an atlas. To make this comparison, we consider the composition of one chart with the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of the other. This composition is not well-defined unless we restrict both charts to the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of their [domains](wikipedia://en/50263) of definition. (For example, if we have a chart of Europe and a chart of Russia, then we can compare these two charts on their overlap, namely the European part of Russia.)

To be more precise, suppose that $(U_{\alpha}, \varphi_{\alpha})$ and $(U_{\beta}, \varphi_{\beta})$ are two charts for a manifold *M* such that $U_{\alpha} \cap U_{\beta}$ is [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566). The **transition map** $\tau_{\alpha,\beta}: \varphi_{\alpha}(U_{\alpha} \cap U_{\beta}) \to \varphi_{\beta}(U_{\alpha} \cap U_{\beta})$ is the map defined by

$$\tau_{\alpha,\beta} = \varphi_{\beta} \circ \varphi_{\alpha}^{-1}.$$

Note that since $\varphi_{\alpha}$ and $\varphi_{\beta}$ are both homeomorphisms, the transition map $\tau_{\alpha, \beta}$ is also a homeomorphism.

:::

## More structure {#More_structure}

One often desires more structure on a manifold than simply the topological structure. For example, if one would like an unambiguous notion of [differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_%28mathematics%29) of functions on a manifold, then it is necessary to construct an atlas whose transition functions are [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206). Such a manifold is called [differentiable](wikipedia://en/2119219). Given a differentiable manifold, one can unambiguously define the notion of [tangent vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_vectors) and then [directional derivatives](wikipedia://en/487859).

If each transition function is a [smooth map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map), then the atlas is called a [smooth atlas](wikipedia://en/1542238), and the manifold itself is called [smooth](wikipedia://en/2119219#Definition). Alternatively, one could require that the transition maps have only *k* continuous derivatives in which case the atlas is said to be $C^k$.

Very generally, if each transition function belongs to a [pseudogroup](wikipedia://en/1255458) $\mathcal G$ of homeomorphisms of Euclidean space, then the atlas is called a $\mathcal G$-atlas. If the transition maps between charts of an atlas preserve a [local trivialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_trivialization), then the atlas defines the structure of a fibre bundle.
