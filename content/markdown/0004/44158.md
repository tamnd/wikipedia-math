# Conservative force

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conservative force"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a **conservative force** is a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) with the property that the total [work](wikipedia://en/149861) done by the force in moving a particle between two points is independent of the path taken.[^o2] Equivalently, if a particle travels in a closed loop, the total work done (the sum of the force acting along the path multiplied by the [displacement](wikipedia://en/859275)) by a conservative force is zero.[^o3]

:::

A conservative force depends only on the position of the object. If a force is conservative, it is possible to assign a numerical value for the [potential](wikipedia://en/461654) at any point and conversely, when an object moves from one location to another, the force changes the [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) of the object by an amount that does not depend on the path taken, contributing to the [mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy) and the overall [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088). If the force is not conservative, then defining a [scalar potential](wikipedia://en/461654) is not possible, because taking different paths would lead to conflicting potential differences between the start and end points.

[Gravitational force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation) is an example of a conservative force, while [frictional force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frictional_force) is an example of a non-conservative force.

Other examples of conservative forces are: [force in elastic spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law), [force due to liquid pressure acting on a surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure#Liquid_pressure), [electrostatic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_force) between two electric charges, and [magnetic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_force) between two magnetic poles. The last two forces are called central forces as they act along the line joining the centres of two charged/magnetized bodies. A central force is conservative if and only if it is spherically symmetric.[^o4]

For conservative forces,

$$\mathbf{F_c} = - \frac{\textit{dU}}{d\mathbf{s}}$$

where $F_c$ is the conservative force, $U$ is the potential energy, and $s$ is the position.[^o5]

## Informal definition {#Informal_definition}

Informally, a conservative force can be thought of as a force that *conserves* [mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy). Suppose a particle starts at point A, and there is a force *F* acting on it. Then the particle is moved around by other forces, and eventually ends up at A again. Though the particle may still be moving, at that instant when it passes point A again, it has traveled a closed path. If the net work done by *F* at this point is 0, then *F* passes the closed path test. Any force that passes the closed path test for all possible closed paths is classified as a conservative force.

The [gravitational force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_force), [spring force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law), [magnetic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_force) (according to some definitions, see below) and [electric force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_force) (at least in a time-independent magnetic field, see [Faraday's law of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction) for details) are examples of conservative forces, while [friction](wikipedia://en/11062) and [air drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_drag) are classical examples of non-conservative forces.

For non-conservative forces, the mechanical energy that is lost (not conserved) has to go somewhere else, by [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088). Usually the energy is turned into [heat](wikipedia://en/19593167), for example the heat generated by friction. In addition to heat, friction also often produces some [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) energy. The water drag on a moving boat converts the boat's mechanical energy into not only heat and [sound energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_energy), but also wave energy at the edges of its [wake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_%28physics%29). These and other energy losses are irreversible because of the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/133017).

## Path independence {#Path_independence}

*Image: Conservative Force Gravity Example.svg.*

A direct consequence of the closed path test is that the work done by a conservative force on a particle moving between any two points does not depend on the path taken by the particle.

This is illustrated in the figure to the right: The work done by the gravitational force on an object depends only on its change in height because the [gravitational force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_force) is conservative. The work done by a conservative force is equal to the negative of change in potential energy during that process. For a proof, imagine two paths 1 and 2, both going from point A to point B. The variation of energy for the particle, taking path 1 from A to B and then path 2 backwards from B to A, is 0; thus, the work is the same in path 1 and 2, i.e., the work is independent of the path followed, as long as it goes from A to B.

For example, if a child slides down a frictionless slide, the work done by the gravitational force on the child from the start of the slide to the end is independent of the shape of the slide; it only depends on the vertical displacement of the child.

## Mathematical description {#Mathematical_description}

A [force field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_field_%28physics%29) *F*, defined everywhere in space (or within a [simply-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply-connected) volume of space), is called a *conservative force* or *[conservative vector field](wikipedia://en/461454)* if it meets any of these three *equivalent* conditions:

1. The [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) of *F* is the zero vector: $$\mathbf{\nabla} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}.$$ where in two dimensions this reduces to: $$\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y} = 0$$
1. There is zero net [work](wikipedia://en/149861) (*W*) done by the force when moving a particle through a trajectory that starts and ends in the same place: $$W \equiv \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf r = 0.$$
1. The force can be written as the negative [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of a [potential](wikipedia://en/23703), $\Phi$: $$\mathbf{F} = -\mathbf{\nabla} \Phi.$$

::: {#o9 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof that these three conditions are equivalent when F is a force field"}

::: {#o12 .entry cue=term name="3 implies 1"}

::: {#o11 .entry cue=term name="2 implies 3"}

::: {#o10 .entry cue=term name="1 implies 2"}

**Proof that these three conditions are equivalent when *F* is a [force field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_field_%28physics%29).**

**1 implies 2**

Let *C* be any simple closed path (i.e., a path that starts and ends at the same point and has no self-intersections), and consider a surface *S* of which *C* is the boundary. Then [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497) says that

$$\int_S \left(\mathbf{\nabla} \times \mathbf{F}\right) \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{a} = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}$$

If the curl of **F** is zero the left hand side is zero – therefore statement 2 is true.

:::

**2 implies 3**

Assume that statement 2 holds. Let *c* be a simple curve from the origin to a point $x$ and define a function

$$\Phi(x) = -\int_c \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}.$$

The fact that this function is well-defined (independent of the choice of *c*) follows from statement 2. Anyway, from the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696), it follows that

$$\mathbf{F} = -\mathbf{\nabla} \Phi .$$

So statement 2 implies statement 3 ([see full proof](wikipedia://en/5337437#Converse_of_the_gradient_theorem)).

:::

**3 implies 1**

Finally, assume that the third statement is true. A well-known vector calculus identity states that the curl of the gradient of any function is 0. (See [proof](wikipedia://en/3114930#Curl_of_gradient_is_zero).) Therefore, if the third statement is true, then the first statement must be true as well.

This shows that statement 1 implies 2, 2 implies 3, and 3 implies 1. Therefore, all three are equivalent, [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)

(The equivalence of 1 and 3 is also known as (one aspect of) [Helmholtz's theorem](wikipedia://en/820253).)

:::

:::

The term *conservative force* comes from the fact that when a conservative force exists, it conserves mechanical energy. The most familiar conservative forces are [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), the [electric force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_force) (in a time-independent magnetic field, see [Faraday's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_law_of_induction)), and [spring force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law).

Many forces (particularly those that depend on velocity) are not [force *fields*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_field_%28physics%29). In these cases, the above three conditions are not mathematically equivalent. For example, the [magnetic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_force) satisfies condition 2 (since the work done by a magnetic field on a charged particle is always zero), but does not satisfy condition 3, and condition 1 is not even defined (the force is not a vector field, so one cannot evaluate its curl). Accordingly, some authors classify the magnetic force as conservative,[^o13] while others do not.[^o14] The magnetic force is an unusual case; most velocity-dependent forces, such as [friction](wikipedia://en/11062), do not satisfy any of the three conditions, and therefore are unambiguously nonconservative.

## Non-conservative force {#Non-conservative_force}

Despite conservation of total energy, non-conservative forces can arise in classical physics due to neglected [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29) or from time-dependent potentials.[^o16] Many non-conservative forces may be perceived as macroscopic effects of small-scale conservative forces.[^o17] For instance, friction may be treated without violating conservation of energy by considering the motion of individual molecules; however, that means every molecule's motion must be considered rather than handling it through statistical methods. For macroscopic systems the non-conservative approximation is far easier to deal with than millions of degrees of freedom.

Examples of non-conservative forces are [friction](wikipedia://en/11062) and non-elastic material [stress](wikipedia://en/228107). Friction has the effect of transferring some of the energy from the large-scale motion of the bodies to small-scale movements in their interior, and therefore appear non-conservative on a large scale.[^o17] [General relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) is non-conservative, as seen in the [anomalous precession](wikipedia://en/1784313#Perihelion_precession_of_Mercury) of Mercury's orbit. However, general relativity does conserve a [stress–energy–momentum pseudotensor](wikipedia://en/5529638).
