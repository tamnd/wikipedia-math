# Upper and lower bounds

*Image: Illustration of supremum.svg.* A set with upper bounds and its least upper bound

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Upper and lower bounds"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), an **upper bound** or **majorant**[^o14] of a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of some [preordered set](wikipedia://en/23582) $(K, \le)$ is an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) of $K$ that is [greater than or equal to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_than_or_equal_to) every element of $S$.[^o13][^o2] [Dually](wikipedia://en/600618), a **lower bound** or **minorant** of $S$ is defined to be an element of $K$ that is less than or equal to every element of $S$. A set with an upper (respectively, lower) bound is said to be **bounded from above** or **majorized**[^o14] (respectively **bounded from below** or **minorized**) by that bound. The terms **bounded above** (**bounded below**) are also used in the mathematical literature for sets that have upper (respectively lower) bounds.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, $5$ is a lower bound for the set $S = \{5, 8, 42, 34, 13934\}$ (as a subset of the [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) or of the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), etc.), and so is $4$. On the other hand, $6$ is not a lower bound for $S$ since it is not smaller than every element in $S$. $13934$ and other numbers *x* such that $x \ge 13934$ would be an upper bound for *S*.

The set $S = \{42\}$ has $42$ as both an upper bound and a lower bound; all other numbers are either an upper bound or a lower bound for that $S$.

Every subset of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) has a lower bound since the natural numbers have a least element (0 or 1, depending on convention). An infinite subset of the natural numbers cannot be bounded from above. An infinite subset of the integers may be bounded from below or bounded from above, but not both. An infinite subset of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) may or may not be bounded from below, and may or may not be bounded from above.

Every finite subset of a non-empty [totally ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_set) has both upper and lower bounds.

:::

## Bounds of functions {#Bounds_of_functions}

The definitions can be generalized to [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) and even to sets of functions.

Given a function $f$ with [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) $D$ and a preordered set $(K, \le)$ as [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264), an element $y$ of $K$ is an upper bound of $f$ if $y \ge f(x)$ for each $x$ in $D$. The upper bound is called *[sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_jargon#sharp)* if equality holds for at least one value of $x$. It indicates that the constraint is optimal, and thus cannot be further reduced without invalidating the inequality.

Similarly, a function $g$ defined on domain $D$ and having the same codomain $(K, \le)$ is an upper bound of $f$, if $g(x) \ge f(x)$ for each $x$ in $D$. The function $g$ is further said to be an upper bound of a set of functions, if it is an upper bound of *each* function in that set.

The notion of lower bound for (sets of) functions is defined analogously, by replacing ≥ with ≤.

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Tight lower bound"}

## Tight bounds {#Tight_bounds}

An upper bound is said to be a *tight upper bound*, a *least upper bound*, or a *[supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum)*, if no smaller value is an upper bound. Similarly, a lower bound is said to be a *tight lower bound*, a *greatest lower bound*, or an *[infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum)*, if no greater value is a lower bound.

:::

## Exact upper bounds {#Exact_upper_bounds}

An upper bound $u$ of a subset $S$ of a preordered set $(K, \le)$ is said to be an *exact upper bound* for $S$ if every element of $K$ that is strictly majorized by $u$ is also majorized by some element of $S$. Exact upper bounds of [reduced products](wikipedia://en/2174269) of [linear orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_order) play an important role in [PCF theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCF_theory).[^o10]
