# Semidirect product

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semidirect product"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the concept of a **semidirect product** is a generalization of a [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015). It is usually denoted with the symbol $\rtimes$. There are two closely related concepts of semidirect product:

:::

- an *inner* semidirect product is a particular way in which a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) can be made up of two [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011), one of which is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918).
- an *outer* semidirect product is a way to construct a new group from two given groups by using the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) as a set and a particular multiplication operation.

As with direct products, there is a natural equivalence between inner and outer semidirect products, and both are commonly referred to simply as *semidirect products*.

For [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707), the [Schur–Zassenhaus theorem](wikipedia://en/8113769) provides a sufficient condition for the existence of a decomposition as a semidirect product (also known as [splitting extension](wikipedia://en/345807#Classifying_split_extensions)).

## Inner semidirect product definitions {#Inner_semidirect_product_definitions}

Given a group $G$ with [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) $e$, a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $H$, and a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) $N \triangleleft G$, the following statements are equivalent:

- $G$ is the [product of subgroups](wikipedia://en/24734#Product_of_subgroups), $G = NH$, and these subgroups have trivial intersection: $N \cap H = \{e\}$.
- For every $g \in G$, there are unique $n \in N$ and $h \in H$ such that $g = nh$.
- The [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) $\pi \circ i$ of the natural embedding $i : H \to G$ with the natural projection $\pi : G \to G/N$ induces an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) between $H$ and the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $G/N$.
- There exists a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $G \to H$ that is the [identity](wikipedia://en/15069) [on](wikipedia://en/1589032) $H$ and whose [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is $N$. In other words, there is a split [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) $$1 \to N \to G \to H \to 1$$ of groups (which is also known as a [split extension](wikipedia://en/345807#Classifying_split_extensions) of $H$ by $N$).

If any of these statements holds (and hence all of them hold, by their equivalence), we say $G$ is the **semidirect product** of $N$ and $H$, written $G = N \rtimes H$ or $G = H \ltimes N$. The symbol $\rtimes$ is a combination of $\triangleleft$ and $\times$, oriented so that $N \triangleleft (N \rtimes H)$, i.e. so that the semidirect product symbol points towards the normal subgroup.[^o3][^o4] Another way of expressing this is that $G$ *splits* over $N$; one also says that $G$ is a semidirect product of $H$ *acting on* $N$, or even a semidirect product of $H$ and $N$ (though to avoid ambiguity, it is advisable to specify which is the normal subgroup).

If $G = N \rtimes H$, then there is a group homomorphism $\varphi : H\rightarrow \mathrm{Aut} (N)$ given by $\varphi_h(n)=hnh^{-1}$, and for $g=nh,g'=n'h'$, we have $gg'=nhn'h' = nhn'h^{-1}hh' = n\varphi_{h}(n')hh' = n^* h^*$.

## Inner and outer semidirect products {#Inner_and_outer_semidirect_products}

### Inner semidirect product {#Inner_semidirect_product}

Let us first consider the inner semidirect product. In this case, for a group $G$, consider a normal subgroup $N$ and another subgroup $H$ (not necessarily normal). Assume that the conditions on the list above hold. Let $\operatorname{Aut}(N)$ denote the group of all [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/56661772) of $N$, which is a group under composition. Construct a group homomorphism $\varphi : H \to \operatorname{Aut}(N)$ defined by conjugation,

$\varphi_h(n) = hnh^{-1}$, for all $h$ in $H$ and $n$ in $N$.

In this way we can construct a group $G'=(N,H)$ with group operation defined as

$(n_1, h_1) \cdot (n_2, h_2) = (n_1 \varphi_{h_1}(n_2),\, h_1 h_2)$ for $n_{1}, n_{2}$ in $N$ and $h_{1}, h_{2}$ in $H$.

The subgroups $N$ and $H$ determine $G$ [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism, as we will show later. In this way, we can construct the group $G$ from its subgroups. This kind of construction is called an **inner semidirect product** (also known as internal semidirect product[^o7]).

### Outer semidirect product {#Outer_semidirect_product}

Let us now consider the outer semidirect product. Given any two groups $N$ and $H$ and a group homomorphism $\varphi : H \to \operatorname{Aut}(N)$, we can construct a new group $N \rtimes_{\varphi} H$, called the **outer semidirect product** of $N$ and $H$ with respect to $\varphi$, defined as follows:[^o9] The underlying set is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $N \times H$.

This defines a group in which the identity element is $(e_{N}, e_{H})$ and the inverse of the element $(n, h)$ is $(\varphi_{h^{-1}}(n^{-1}), h^{-1})$. Pairs $(n, e_{H})$ form a normal subgroup isomorphic to $N$, while pairs $(e_{N}, h)$ form a subgroup isomorphic to $H$. The full group is a semidirect product of those two subgroups in the sense given earlier.

Conversely, suppose that we are given a group $G$ with a normal subgroup $N$ and a subgroup $H$, such that every element $g$ of $G$ may be written uniquely in the form $g = nh$ where $n$ lies in $N$ and $h$ lies in $H$. Let $\varphi : H \to \operatorname{Aut}(N)$ be the homomorphism (written $\varphi(h) = \varphi_{h}$) given by

$$\varphi_h(n) = hnh^{-1}$$

for all $n \in N, h \in H$.

Then $G$ is isomorphic to the semidirect product $N \rtimes_{\varphi} H$. The isomorphism $\lambda : G \to N \rtimes_{\varphi} H$ is well defined by $\lambda(a) = \lambda(nh) = (n, h)$ due to the uniqueness of the decomposition $a = nh$.

In $G$, we have

$$(n_1 h_1)(n_2 h_2) = n_1 h_1 n_2(h_1^{-1}h_1) h_2 = (n_1 \varphi_{h_1}(n_2))(h_1 h_2)$$

Thus, for $a = n_{1}h_{1}$ and $b = n_{2}h_{2}$ we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda(ab) & = \lambda(n_1 h_1 n_2 h_2) = \lambda(n_1 \varphi_{h_1} (n_2) h_1 h_2) = (n_1 \varphi_{h_1} (n_2), h_1 h_2) = (n_1, h_1) \bullet (n_2, h_2) \\[5pt] & = \lambda(n_1 h_1) \bullet \lambda(n_2 h_2) = \lambda(a) \bullet \lambda(b), \end{aligned}$$

which [proves](wikipedia://en/82285) that $\lambda$ is a homomorphism. Since $\lambda$ is obviously an epimorphism and monomorphism, then it is indeed an isomorphism. This also explains the definition of the multiplication rule in $N \rtimes_{\varphi} H$.

The direct product is a special case of the semidirect product. To see this, let $\varphi$ be the trivial homomorphism (i.e., sending every element of $H$ to the identity automorphism of $N$) then $N \rtimes_{\varphi} H$ is the direct product $N \times H$.

A version of the [splitting lemma](wikipedia://en/138378) for groups states that a group $G$ is isomorphic to a semidirect product of the two groups $N$ and $H$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there exists a [short exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215#Short_exact_sequence)

$$1 \longrightarrow N \,\overset{\beta}{\longrightarrow}\, G \,\overset{\alpha}{\longrightarrow}\, H \longrightarrow 1$$

and a group homomorphism $\gamma : H \to G$ such that $\alpha\,\circ\,\gamma = \operatorname{id}_{H}$, the identity map on $H$. In this case, $\varphi : H \to \operatorname{Aut}(N)$ is given by $\varphi(h) = \varphi_{h}$, where

$$\varphi_h(n) = \beta^{-1}(\gamma(h)\beta(n)\gamma(h^{-1})).$$

## Examples {#Examples}

### Dihedral group {#Dihedral_group}

The [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) $D_{n}$ with $2n$ elements is isomorphic to a semidirect product of the [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) $C_{n}$ and $C_{2}$, i.e., $D_{n} = C_{n} \rtimes C_{2}$ .[^o13] Here, the non-identity element of $C_{2}$ acts on $C_{n}$ by inverting elements; this is an automorphism since $C_{n}$ is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974). The [presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation) for this group is:

$$\langle a,\;b \mid a^2 = e,\; b^n = e,\; aba^{-1} = b^{-1}\rangle.$$

#### Cyclic groups {#Cyclic_groups}

More generally, a semidirect product of any two cyclic groups $C_{m}$ with generator $a$ and $C_{n}$ with generator $b$ is given by one extra relation, $aba^{-1} = b^{k}$, with $k$ and $n$ [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime), and $k^m\equiv 1 \pmod{n}$;[^o13] that is, the presentation:[^o13]

$$\langle a,\;b \mid a^m = e,\;b^n = e,\;aba^{-1} = b^k\rangle.$$

If $r$ and $m$ are coprime, $a^{r}$ is a generator of $C_{m}$ and $a^{r}ba^{-r} = b^{k^{r}}$, hence the presentation:

$$\langle a,\;b \mid a^m = e,\;b^n = e,\;aba^{-1} = b^{k^{r}}\rangle$$

gives a group isomorphic to the previous one.

### Symmetric group {#Symmetric_group}

The [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$ where $n \geq 2$ is a semidirect product of the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_{n}$ and any 2-element subgroup generated by a single transposition.

### Holomorph of a group {#Holomorph_of_a_group}

One canonical example of a group expressed as a semidirect product is the [holomorph](wikipedia://en/2841008) of a group. This is defined as

> $$\operatorname{Hol}(G)=G\rtimes \operatorname{Aut}(G)$$

where $\text{Aut}(G)$ is the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of a group $G$ and the structure map $\varphi$ comes from the right action of $\text{Aut}(G)$ on $G$. In terms of multiplying elements, this gives the group structure

> $$(g,\alpha)(h,\beta)=(g(\varphi(\alpha)\cdot h),\alpha\beta).$$

### Fundamental group of the Klein bottle {#Fundamental_group_of_the_Klein_bottle}

The [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412) can be presented in the form

$$\langle a,\;b \mid aba^{-1} = b^{-1}\rangle.$$

and is therefore a semidirect product of the group of integers with addition, $\mathbb{Z}$, with $\mathbb{Z}$. The corresponding homomorphism $\varphi:\mathbb{Z} \rightarrow \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z})$ is given by $\varphi(h)(n)=(-1)^{h}n$.

### Upper triangular matrices {#Upper_triangular_matrices}

The group $\mathbb{T}_n$ of upper [triangular matrices](wikipedia://en/374222) with non-zero [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) in an arbitrary field, that is with non-zero entries on the [diagonal](wikipedia://en/194990), has a decomposition into the semidirect product $\mathbb{T}_n \cong \mathbb{U}_n \rtimes \mathbb{D}_n$[^o19] where $\mathbb{U}_n$ is the subgroup of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with only $1$s on the diagonal, which is called the upper [unitriangular matrix](wikipedia://en/374222) group, and $\mathbb{D}_n$ is the subgroup of [diagonal matrices](wikipedia://en/174080). The group action of $\mathbb{D}_n$ on $\mathbb{U}_n$ is induced by matrix multiplication. If we set

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & x_2 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & x_n \end{bmatrix}$$

and

$$B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & a_{12} & a_{13} & \cdots & a_{1n} \\ 0 & 1 & a_{23} & \cdots & a_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

then their [matrix product](wikipedia://en/125280) is

$$AB = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & x_1a_{12} & x_1a_{13} & \cdots & x_1a_{1n} \\ 0 & x_2 & x_2a_{23} & \cdots & x_2a_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & x_n \end{bmatrix}.$$

This gives the induced group action $m:\mathbb{D}_n\times \mathbb{U}_n \to \mathbb{U}_n$

$$m(A,B) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_1a_{12} & x_1a_{13} & \cdots & x_1a_{1n} \\ 0 & 1 & x_2a_{23} & \cdots & x_2a_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

A matrix in $\mathbb{T}_n$ can be represented by matrices in $\mathbb{U}_n$ and $\mathbb{D}_n$. Hence $\mathbb{T}_n \cong \mathbb{U}_n \rtimes \mathbb{D}_n$.

### Group of isometries on the plane {#Group_of_isometries_on_the_plane}

The [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164) of all rigid motions ([isometries](wikipedia://en/254777)) of the plane (maps $f:\mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ such that the Euclidean distance between $x$ and $y$ equals the distance between $f(x)$ and $f(y)$ for all $x$ and $y$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$) is isomorphic to a semidirect product of the abelian group $\mathbb{R}^2$ (which describes translations) and the group $\mathrm O(2)$ of [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/105620) $2 \times 2$ matrices (which describes rotations and reflections that keep the origin fixed). Applying a translation and then a rotation or reflection has the same effect as applying the rotation or reflection first and then a translation by the rotated or reflected translation vector (i.e. applying the [conjugate](wikipedia://en/2977884) of the original translation). This shows that the group of translations is a normal subgroup of the Euclidean group, that the Euclidean group is a semidirect product of the translation group and $\mathrm O(2)$, and that the corresponding homomorphism $\varphi:\mathrm O(2) \rightarrow \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ is given by [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280): $\varphi(h)(n) = hn$.

### Orthogonal group O(*n*) {#Orthogonal_group_O(n)}

The [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $O(n)$ of all orthogonal [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) $n \times n$ matrices (intuitively the set of all rotations and reflections of $n$-dimensional space that keep the origin fixed) is isomorphic to a semidirect product of the group $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ (consisting of all orthogonal matrices with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $1$, intuitively the rotations of $n$-dimensional space) and $C_{2}$. If we represent $C_{2}$ as the multiplicative group of matrices $\{I, R\}$, where $R$ is a reflection of $n$-dimensional space that keeps the origin fixed (i.e., an orthogonal matrix with determinant $-1$ representing an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450)), then $\varphi : C_{2} \to \operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{SO}(n))$ is given by $\varphi(H)(N) = HNH^{-1}$ for all *H* in $C_{2}$ and $N$ in $\operatorname{SO}(n)$. In the non-trivial case ($H$ is not the identity) this means that $\varphi(H)$ is conjugation of operations by the reflection (in 3-dimensional space a rotation axis and the direction of rotation are replaced by their "mirror image").

### Semi-linear transformations {#Semi-linear_transformations}

The group of [semilinear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semilinear_transformation) on a vector space $V$ over a field $K$, often denoted $\Gamma L(V)$, is isomorphic to a semidirect product of the [linear group](wikipedia://en/3983656) $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ (a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $\Gamma L(V)$), and the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of $K$.

## Non-examples {#Non-examples}

Of course, no [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) can be expressed as a semidirect product (because they do not have nontrivial normal subgroups), but there are a few common counterexamples of groups containing a non-trivial normal subgroup that nonetheless cannot be expressed as a semidirect product. Note that although not every group $G$ can be expressed as a split extension of $H$ by $A$, it turns out that such a group can be embedded into the [wreath product](wikipedia://en/146625) $A\wr H$ by the [universal embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/61229758).

### Z<sub>4</sub> {#Z4}

The cyclic group $\mathrm{Z}_4$ is not a simple group since it has a subgroup of order 2, namely $\{0,2\} \cong \mathrm{Z}_2$ is a subgroup and their quotient is $\mathrm{Z}_2$, so there is an extension

> $$0 \to \mathrm{Z}_2 \to \mathrm{Z}_4 \to \mathrm{Z}_2 \to 0$$

If instead this extension is [split](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_extension), then the group $G$ in

> $$0 \to \mathrm{Z}_2 \to G \to \mathrm{Z}_2 \to 0$$

would be isomorphic to $\mathrm{Z}_2\times\mathrm{Z}_2$.

### Q<sub>8</sub> {#Q8}

The [group of the eight quaternions](wikipedia://en/153130) $\{\pm 1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}$ where $ijk = -1$ and $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$, is another example of a group[^o27] which has non-trivial normal subgroups yet is still not split. For example, the subgroup generated by $i$ is isomorphic to $\mathrm{Z}_4$ and is normal. It also has a subgroup of order $2$ generated by $-1$. This would mean $\mathrm{Q}_8$ would have to be a split extension in the following *hypothetical* exact sequence of groups:

> $$0 \to \mathrm{Z}_4 \to \mathrm{Q}_8 \to \mathrm{Z}_2 \to 0,$$

but such an exact sequence does not exist. This can be shown by computing the first group cohomology group of $\mathrm{Z}_2$ with coefficients in $\mathrm{Z}_4$, so $H^1(\mathrm{Z}_2,\mathrm{Z}_4) \cong \mathrm{Z}/2$ and noting the two groups in these extensions are $\mathrm{Z}_2\times\mathrm{Z}_4$ and the dihedral group $\mathrm{D}_8$. But, as neither of these groups is isomorphic with $\mathrm{Q}_8$, the quaternion group is not split. This non-existence of isomorphisms can be checked by noting the trivial extension is abelian while $\mathrm{Q}_8$ is non-abelian, and noting the only normal subgroups are $\mathrm{Z}_2$ and $\mathrm{Z}_4$, but $\mathrm{Q}_8$ has three subgroups isomorphic to $\mathrm{Z}_4$. With this not-split-ness, $\mathrm{Q}_8$ can not be expressed as $N \ltimes H$ with normal subgroup $\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{Z}_4=\{\pm 1,\pm i\}$ (or two other isomorphic subgroups) and $\mathrm{H}=\mathrm{Z}_2$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

If $G$ is the semidirect product of the normal subgroup $N$ and the subgroup $H$, and both $N$ and $H$ are finite, then the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) of $G$ equals the product of the orders of $N$ and $H$. This follows from the fact that $G$ is of the same order as the outer semidirect product of $N$ and $H$, whose underlying set is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $N \times H$.

### Relation to direct products {#Relation_to_direct_products}

Suppose $G$ is a semidirect product of the normal subgroup $N$ and the subgroup $H$. If $H$ is also normal in $G$, or equivalently, if there exists a homomorphism $G \to N$ that is the identity on $N$ with kernel $H$, then $G$ is the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of $N$ and $H$.

The direct product of two groups $N$ and $H$ can be thought of as the semidirect product of $N$ and $H$ with respect to $\varphi(h) = \operatorname{id}_{N}$ for all $h$ in $H$.

Note that in a direct product, the order of the factors is not important, since $N \times H$ is isomorphic to $H \times N$. This is not the case for semidirect products, as the two factors play different roles.

Furthermore, the result of a (proper) semidirect product by means of a non-trivial homomorphism is never an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), even if the factor groups are abelian.

### Non-uniqueness of semidirect products (and further examples) {#Non-uniqueness_of_semidirect_products_(and_further_examples)}

As opposed to the case with the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015), a semidirect product of two groups is not, in general, unique; if $G$ and $G'$ are two groups that both contain isomorphic copies of $N$ as a normal subgroup and $H$ as a subgroup, and both are a semidirect product of $N$ and $H$, then it does *not* follow that $G$ and $G'$ are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) because the semidirect product also depends on the choice of an action of $H$ on $N$.

For example, there are four non-isomorphic groups of order 16 that are semidirect products of $C_{8}$ and $C_{2}$; in this case, $C_{8}$ is necessarily a normal subgroup because it has index 2. One of these four semidirect products is the direct product, while the other three are non-abelian groups:

- the dihedral group of order 16
- the [quasidihedral group](wikipedia://en/1050057) of order 16
- the [Iwasawa group](wikipedia://en/16355158) of order 16

If a given group is a semidirect product, then there is no guarantee that this decomposition is unique. For example, there is a group of order 24 (the only one containing six elements of order 4 and six elements of order 6) that can be expressed as semidirect product in the following ways: $(D_{8} \ltimes C_{3}) \cong (C_{2} \ltimes Q_{12}) \cong (C_{2} \ltimes D_{12}) \cong (D_{6} \ltimes V)$.[^o32]

### Existence {#Existence}

In general, there is no known characterization (i.e., a necessary and sufficient condition) for the existence of semidirect products in groups. However, some sufficient conditions are known, which guarantee existence in certain cases. For finite groups, the [Schur–Zassenhaus theorem](wikipedia://en/8113769) guarantees existence of a semidirect product when the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of the normal subgroup is [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to the order of the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526).

For example, the Schur–Zassenhaus theorem implies the existence of a semidirect product among groups of order 6; there are two such products, one of which is a direct product, and the other a dihedral group. In contrast, the Schur–Zassenhaus theorem does not say anything about groups of order 4 or groups of order 8 for instance.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Within group theory, the construction of semidirect products can be pushed much further. The [Zappa–Szép product](wikipedia://en/7094111) of groups is a generalization that, in its internal version, does not assume that either subgroup is normal.

There is also a construction in [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), the [crossed product of rings](wikipedia://en/2749495). This is constructed in the natural way from the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) for a semidirect product of groups. The ring-theoretic approach can be further generalized to the [semidirect sum of Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/45603435#By_semidirect_sum).

For geometry, there is also a crossed product for [group actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450); unfortunately, it is in general non-commutative even if the group is abelian. In this context, the semidirect product is the *space of orbits* of the group action. The latter approach has been championed by [Alain Connes](wikipedia://en/340) as a substitute for approaches by conventional topological techniques; cf. [noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917).

The semidirect product is a special case of the [Grothendieck construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_construction) in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869). Specifically, an action of $H$ on $N$ (respecting the group, or even just monoid structure) is the same thing as a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987)

$$F : BH \to Cat$$

from the [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) $BH$ associated to *H* (having a single object \*, whose endomorphisms are *H*) to the category of categories such that the unique object in $BH$ is mapped to $BN$. The Grothendieck construction of this functor is equivalent to $B(H \rtimes N)$, the (groupoid associated to) semidirect product.[^o36]

### Groupoids {#Groupoids}

Another generalization is for groupoids. This occurs in topology because if a group $G$ acts on a space $X$ it also acts on the [fundamental groupoid](wikipedia://en/55264190) $\pi_{1}(X)$ of the space. The semidirect product $\pi_{1}(X) \rtimes G$ is then relevant to finding the fundamental groupoid of the [orbit space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_space) $X/G$. For full details see Chapter 11 of the book referenced below, and also some details in semidirect product[^o38] in [ncatlab](wikipedia://en/27226892).

### Abelian categories {#Abelian_categories}

Non-trivial semidirect products do *not* arise in [abelian categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_categories), such as the [category of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812). In this case, the [splitting lemma](wikipedia://en/138378) shows that every semidirect product is a direct product. Thus the existence of semidirect products reflects a failure of the category to be abelian.

## Notation {#Notation}

Usually the semidirect product of a group $H$ acting on a group $N$ (in most cases by conjugation as subgroups of a common group) is denoted by $N \rtimes H$ or $H \ltimes N$. However, some sources[^o42] may use this symbol with the opposite meaning. In case the action $\varphi : H \to \operatorname{Aut}(N)$ should be made explicit, one also writes $N \rtimes_{\varphi} H$. One way of thinking about the $N \rtimes H$ symbol is as a combination of the symbol for normal subgroup ($\text{◁}$) and the symbol for the product ($\times$). [Barry Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Simon), in his book on group representation theory,[^o43] employs the unusual notation $N\mathbin{\circledS_{\varphi}}H$ for the semidirect product.

[Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) lists four variants:[^o44]
