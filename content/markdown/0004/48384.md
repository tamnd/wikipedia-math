# Equatorial coordinate system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equatorial coordinate system"}

*Image: Celestial Sphere - Equatorial Coordinate System.png.* Model of the equatorial coordinate system. [Declination](wikipedia://en/8612) (vertical arcs, degrees) and [hour angle](wikipedia://en/48838) (horizontal arcs, hours) is shown. For hour angle, [right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073) (horizontal arcs, degrees) can be used as an alternative.

The **equatorial coordinate system** is a [celestial coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system) widely used to specify the positions of [celestial objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object). It may be implemented in [spherical](wikipedia://en/29181) or [rectangular](wikipedia://en/7706) coordinates, both defined by an [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) at the centre of [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), a [fundamental plane](wikipedia://en/8081066) consisting of the [projection](wikipedia://en/243849) of Earth's [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) onto the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) (forming the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681)), a primary direction towards the [March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox) [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox_%28celestial_coordinates%29), and a [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule) convention.[^o2][^o3]

:::

The origin at the centre of Earth means the coordinates are *[geocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_model)*, that is, as seen from the centre of Earth as if it were [transparent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transparency_and_translucency).[^o4] The fundamental plane and the primary direction mean that the coordinate system, while aligned with Earth's [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) and [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_pole), does not rotate with the Earth, but remains relatively fixed against the background [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_stars). A right-handed convention means that coordinates increase northward from and eastward around the fundamental plane.

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Primary direction"}

## Primary direction {#Primary_direction}

This description of the [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) of the reference frame is somewhat simplified; the orientation is not quite fixed. A slow motion of Earth's axis, [precession](wikipedia://en/72576), causes a slow, continuous turning of the coordinate system westward about the poles of the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264), completing one circuit in about 26,000 years. Superimposed on this is a smaller motion of the ecliptic, and a small oscillation of the Earth's axis, [nutation](wikipedia://en/52297430).[^o7]

In order to fix the exact primary direction, these motions necessitate the specification of the [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) of a particular date, known as an [epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29), when giving a position. The three most commonly used are:

**Mean equinox of a standard epoch (usually [J2000.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29), but may include B1950.0, B1900.0, etc.)**

is a fixed standard direction, allowing positions established at various dates to be compared directly.

**Mean equinox of date**

is the intersection of the ecliptic of "date" (that is, the ecliptic in its position at "date") with the *mean* equator (that is, the equator rotated by precession to its position at "date", but free from the small periodic oscillations of nutation). Commonly used in planetary [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) calculation.

**True equinox of date**

is the intersection of the ecliptic of "date" with the *true* equator (that is, the mean equator plus nutation). This is the actual intersection of the two planes at any particular moment, with all motions accounted for.

A position in the equatorial coordinate system is thus typically specified *true equinox and equator of date*, *mean equinox and equator of J2000.0*, or similar. Note that there is no "mean ecliptic", as the ecliptic is not subject to small periodic oscillations.[^o8]

## Spherical coordinates {#Spherical_coordinates}

### Use in astronomy {#Use_in_astronomy}

*Image: Equatorial\_and\_horizontal\_celestial\_coordinates\_E.svg.* Equatorial (red) and horizontal (blue) celestial coordinates

A [star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star)'s spherical coordinates are often expressed as a pair, [right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073) and [declination](wikipedia://en/8612), without a [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) coordinate. The direction of sufficiently distant objects is the same for all observers, and it is convenient to specify this direction with the same coordinates for all. In contrast, in the [horizontal coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48386), a star's position differs from observer to observer based on their positions on the Earth's surface, and is continuously changing with the Earth's rotation.

[Telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope) equipped with [equatorial mounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_mount) and [setting circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setting_circles) employ the equatorial coordinate system to find objects. Setting circles in conjunction with a [star chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart) or [ephemeris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephemeris) allow the telescope to be easily pointed at known objects on the celestial sphere.

### Declination {#Declination}

The declination symbol $\delta$, (lower case "delta", abbreviated DEC) measures the angular distance of an object perpendicular to the celestial equator, positive to the north, negative to the south. For example, the north celestial pole has a declination of +90°. The origin for declination is the celestial equator, which is the projection of the Earth's equator onto the celestial sphere. Declination is analogous to terrestrial [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616).[^o13][^o14][^o15]

### Right ascension {#Right_ascension}

*Image: Hour angle still1.png.* As seen from above the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s [north pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_pole), a star's cyan[local hour angle](wikipedia://en/48838) (LHA) for an redobserver near New York. Also depicted are the star's green[right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073) and blueGreenwich hour angle (GHA), the magenta[local mean sidereal time](wikipedia://en/48837) (LMST) and purple[Greenwich mean sidereal time](wikipedia://en/48837) (GMST). The symbol ♈︎ identifies the [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) direction.

The right ascension symbol $\alpha$, (lower case "alpha", abbreviated RA) measures the angular distance of an object eastward along the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) from the March [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) to the [hour circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hour_circle) passing through the object. The March equinox point is one of the two points where the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) intersects the celestial equator. Right ascension is usually measured in [sidereal](wikipedia://en/48837) hours, minutes and seconds instead of degrees, a result of the method of measuring right ascensions by [timing the passage of objects across the meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_circle) as the [Earth rotates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_rotation). There are $\tfrac{360^{\circ}}{24^{h}}$ = 15° in one hour of right ascension, and 24<sup>h</sup> of right ascension around the entire [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681).[^o13][^o17][^o18]

When used together, right ascension and declination are usually abbreviated RA/Dec.

### Hour angle {#Hour_angle}

Alternatively to [right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073), [hour angle](wikipedia://en/48838) (abbreviated HA or LHA, *local hour angle*), a left-handed system, measures the angular distance of an object westward along the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) from the observer's [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28astronomy%29) to the [hour circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hour_circle) passing through the object. Unlike right ascension, hour angle is always increasing with the [rotation of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_of_Earth). Hour angle may be considered a means of measuring the time since upper [culmination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culmination), the moment when an object contacts the meridian overhead.

A culminating star on the observer's meridian is said to have a zero hour angle (0<sup>h</sup>). One [sidereal hour](wikipedia://en/48837) (approximately 0.9973 [solar hours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time)) later, Earth's rotation will carry the star to the west of the meridian, and its hour angle will be 1<sup>h</sup>. When calculating [topocentric](wikipedia://en/48386) phenomena, right ascension may be converted into hour angle as an intermediate step.[^o20][^o21][^o22]

## Rectangular coordinates: geocentric equatorial coordinates {#Rectangular_coordinates:_geocentric_equatorial_coordinates}

*Image: Ra and dec rectangular.png.* Geocentric equatorial coordinates. The [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) is the centre of the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228). The fundamental [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) is the plane of the Earth's equator. The primary direction (the $x$ axis) is the March [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox). A [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-handed) convention specifies a $y$ axis 90° to the east in the fundamental plane; the $z$ axis is the north polar axis. The reference frame does not rotate with the Earth, rather, the Earth rotates around the $z$ axis.

There are a number of [rectangular](wikipedia://en/7706) variants of equatorial coordinates. All have:

- The [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) at the centre of the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228).
- The fundamental [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) in the plane of the Earth's equator.
- The primary direction (the $x$ axis) toward the March [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox), that is, the place where the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) crosses the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) in a northward direction in its annual apparent circuit around the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264).
- A [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-handed) convention, specifying a $y$ axis 90° to the east in the fundamental plane and a $z$ axis along the north polar axis.

The reference frames do not rotate with the Earth (in contrast to [Earth-centred, Earth-fixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECEF) frames), remaining always directed toward the [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox), and drifting over time with the motions of [precession](wikipedia://en/72576) and [nutation](wikipedia://en/52297430).

- In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy):[^o24]
  - The [position of the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_of_the_Sun) is often specified in the geocentric equatorial rectangular coordinates $X$, $Y$, $Z$ and a fourth distance coordinate, $R$ $(= X2 + Y2 + Z2)$, in units of the [astronomical unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit).
  - The positions of the [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planets) and other [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) bodies are often specified in the geocentric equatorial rectangular coordinates $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ and a fourth distance coordinate, $\Delta$ (equal to $\xi2 + \eta2 + \zeta2$), in units of the [astronomical unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit).These rectangular coordinates are related to the corresponding spherical coordinates by $$\begin{aligned} \frac{X}{R} = \frac{\xi}{\mathit{\Delta}} &= \cos \delta \cos \alpha \\ \frac{Y}{R} = \frac{\eta}{\mathit{\Delta}} &= \cos \delta \sin \alpha \\ \frac{Z}{R} = \frac{\zeta}{\mathit{\Delta}} &= \sin \delta \end{aligned}$$
- In [astrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrodynamics):[^o25]
  - The positions of artificial Earth [satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite) are specified in *geocentric equatorial* coordinates, also known as *geocentric equatorial inertial (GEI)*, *[Earth-centred inertial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth-centered_inertial) (ECI)*, and *conventional inertial system (CIS)*, all of which are equivalent in definition to the astronomical geocentric equatorial rectangular frames, above. In the geocentric equatorial frame, the $x$, $y$ and $z$ axes are often designated $I$, $J$ and $K$, respectively, or the frame's [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) is specified by the [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) $\text{Î}$, $\text{Ĵ}$ and $K\text{̂}$.
  - The *Geocentric Celestial Reference Frame (GCRF)* is the geocentric equivalent of the [International Celestial Reference Frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Celestial_Reference_Frame) (ICRF). Its primary direction is the [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) of [J2000.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29), and does not move with [precession](wikipedia://en/72576) and [nutation](wikipedia://en/52297430), but it is otherwise equivalent to the above systems.

## Generalization: heliocentric equatorial coordinates {#Generalization:_heliocentric_equatorial_coordinates}

In astronomy, there is also a heliocentric [rectangular](wikipedia://en/7706) variant of equatorial coordinates, designated $x$, $y$, $z$, which has:

- The [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) at the centre of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun).
- The fundamental [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) in the plane of the Earth's equator.
- The primary direction (the $x$ axis) toward the March [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox).
- A [right-handed](wikipedia://en/32703814) convention, specifying a $y$ axis 90° to the east in the fundamental plane and a $z$ axis along [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s north polar axis.

This frame is similar to the $\xi$, $\eta$, $\zeta$ frame above, except that the origin is removed to the centre of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun). It is commonly used in planetary orbit calculation. The three astronomical rectangular coordinate systems are related by[^o27]

$$\begin{aligned} \xi &= x + X \\ \eta &= y + Y \\ \zeta &= z + Z \end{aligned}$$
