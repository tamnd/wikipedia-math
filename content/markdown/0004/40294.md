# Stephen Smale

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stephen Smale"}

**Stephen Smale** (born July 15, 1930) is an American mathematician, known for his research in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) and [mathematical economics](wikipedia://en/18967255). He was awarded the [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) in 1966[^o4] and spent more than three decades on the mathematics faculty of the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley) (1960–1961 and 1964–1995), where he currently is a professor emeritus, with research interests in [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms), [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [global analysis](wikipedia://en/42027498).[^o5]

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Smale was born in [Flint, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint,_Michigan) and entered the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) in 1948.[^o7][^o8] Initially, he was placed into an honors [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) sequence taught by [Bob Thrall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Thrall) and earned himself A's. However, his [sophomore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophomore_year) and junior years were marred with mediocre grades, mostly Bs, Cs and even an F in [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics). Smale obtained his Bachelor of Science degree in 1952. Despite his grades, Smale was accepted as a graduate student at the University of Michigan's mathematics department. Yet again, Smale performed poorly in his first years, earning a C average as a graduate student. When the department chair, [Hildebrandt](wikipedia://en/36561562), threatened to kick Smale out, he began to take his studies more seriously.[^o9] Smale finally earned his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in 1957, under [Raoul Bott](wikipedia://en/337457), beginning his career as an instructor at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago).

Early in his career, Smale was involved in controversy over remarks he made regarding his work habits while proving the higher-dimensional Poincaré conjecture. He said that his best work had been done "on the beaches of Rio."[^o10][^o11] He has been politically active in various movements in the past, such as the [Free Speech movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Speech_movement) and member of the [Fair Play for Cuba Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Play_for_Cuba_Committee).[^o12] In 1966, having travelled to Moscow under an [NSF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation) grant to accept the Fields Medal, he held a press conference there to denounce the [American position in Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War), [Soviet intervention in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary–Soviet_Union_relations) and Soviet maltreatment of intellectuals. After his return to the US, he was unable to renew the grant.[^o13] At one time he was [subpoenaed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subpoena)[^o14] by the [House Un-American Activities Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Un-American_Activities_Committee).

In 1960, Smale received a [Sloan Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Research_Fellowship) and was appointed to the [Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley) mathematics faculty, moving to a professorship at [Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) the following year. In 1964 he returned to a professorship at Berkeley, where he has spent the main part of his career. He became a professor emeritus at Berkeley in 1995 and took up a post as professor at the [City University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_Hong_Kong). He also amassed over the years one of the finest private mineral collections in existence. Many of Smale's mineral specimens can be seen in the book *The Smale Collection: Beauty in Natural Crystals*.[^o15]

From 2003 to 2012, Smale was a professor at the [Toyota Technological Institute at Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Technological_Institute_at_Chicago);[^o16] starting August 1, 2009, he became a Distinguished University Professor at the [City University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_Hong_Kong).[^o17]

In 1988, Smale was the recipient of the [Chauvenet Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvenet_Prize)[^o3] of the [MAA](wikipedia://en/198772). In 2007, Smale was awarded the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize) in mathematics.[^o18]

## Research {#Research}

Smale proved that the [oriented diffeomorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism_group) of the two-dimensional sphere has the same [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type) as the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) of $3 \times 3$ matrices. Smale's theorem has been reproved and extended a few times, notably to higher dimensions in the form of the [Smale conjecture](wikipedia://en/34785639),[^o20] as well as to other topological types.[^o21]

In another early work, he studied the [immersions](wikipedia://en/6993953) of the two-dimensional sphere into Euclidean space. By relating immersion theory to the [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) of [Stiefel manifolds](wikipedia://en/1392495), he was able to fully clarify when two immersions can be deformed into one another through a family of immersions. Directly from his results it followed that the standard immersion of the sphere into three-dimensional space can be deformed (through immersions) into its negation, which is now known as [sphere eversion](wikipedia://en/478672). He also extended his results to higher-dimensional spheres, and his doctoral student [Morris Hirsch](wikipedia://en/32523108) extended his work to immersions of general [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold).[^o22] Along with [John Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Forbes_Nash)'s work on [isometric immersions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_immersion), the Hirsch–Smale immersion theory was highly influential in [Mikhael Gromov](wikipedia://en/771562)'s early work on development of the [h-principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-principle), which abstracted and applied their ideas to contexts other than that of immersions.[^o23]

In the study of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087), Smale introduced what is now known as a [Morse–Smale system](wikipedia://en/23024252). For these dynamical systems, Smale was able to prove [Morse inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_inequality) relating the [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) of the underlying space to the dimensions of the [(un)stable manifolds](wikipedia://en/5466649). Part of the significance of these results is from Smale's theorem asserting that the [gradient flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradient_flow) of any [Morse function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_function) can be arbitrarily well approximated by a Morse–Smale system without closed orbits. Using these tools, Smale was able to construct *self-indexing* Morse functions, where the value of the function equals its [Morse index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_index) at any critical point.[^o24] Using these self-indexing Morse functions as a key tool, Smale resolved the [generalized Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/8536216) in every dimension greater than four. Building on these works, he also established the more powerful [h-cobordism theorem](wikipedia://en/681049) the following year, together with the full classification of [simply-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply-connected) smooth five-dimensional manifolds.[^o24]

Smale also introduced the [horseshoe map](wikipedia://en/449364), inspiring much subsequent research. He also outlined a research program carried out by many others. Smale is also known for injecting [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) into mathematical [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), as well as recent explorations of various theories of [computation](wikipedia://en/5926).

In 1998 he compiled a list of 18 problems in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) to be solved in the 21st century, known as [Smale's problems](wikipedia://en/8294746). This list was compiled in the spirit of [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s [famous list of problems](wikipedia://en/154584) produced in 1900. In fact, Smale's list contains some of the original Hilbert problems, including the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125) and the second half of [Hilbert's sixteenth problem](wikipedia://en/381750), both of which are still unsolved. Other famous problems on his list include the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798) (now a theorem, proved by [Grigori Perelman](wikipedia://en/225266)), the [P = NP problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_=_NP_problem), and the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/6013654), all of which have been designated [Millennium Prize Problems](wikipedia://en/27599234) by the [Clay Mathematics Institute](wikipedia://en/7655).
