# Logarithmic spiral

*Image: Logarithmic Spiral Pylab.svg.* Logarithmic spiral ([pitch](wikipedia://en/75407072) 10°)

*Image: Mandel zoom 04 seehorse tail.jpg.* A section of the [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562) following a logarithmic spiral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logarithmic spiral"}

A **logarithmic spiral**, **equiangular spiral**, or **growth spiral** is a [self-similar](wikipedia://en/28782) [spiral](wikipedia://en/61563) [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) that often appears in nature. The first to describe a logarithmic [spiral](wikipedia://en/61563) was [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer) (1525) who called it an "eternal line" ("ewige Linie").[^o2][^o3] More than a century later, the curve was discussed by [Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) (1638), and later extensively investigated by [Jacob Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/64740), who called it *Spira mirabilis*, "the marvelous spiral".

:::

The logarithmic spiral is distinct from the [Archimedean spiral](wikipedia://en/61559) in that the distances between the turnings of a logarithmic spiral increase in a [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670), whereas for an Archimedean spiral these distances are constant.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) $(r, \varphi)$ the [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/17860) spiral can be written as[^o6]

$$r = ae^{k\varphi},\quad \varphi \in \mathbb{R},$$

or

$$\varphi = \frac{1}{k} \ln \frac{r}{a},$$

with [$e$](wikipedia://en/9633) being the base of [natural logarithms](wikipedia://en/21476), and $a > 0$, $k\ne 0$ being real constants.

:::

## In Cartesian coordinates {#In_Cartesian_coordinates}

The logarithmic spiral with the polar equation

$$r = a e^{k\varphi}$$

can be represented in Cartesian coordinates $(x=r\cos\varphi,\, y=r\sin\varphi)$ by

$$x = a e^{k\varphi}\cos \varphi, \qquad y = a e^{k\varphi}\sin \varphi.$$

In the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) $(z=x+iy,\, e^{i\varphi}=\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi)$:

$$z=ae^{(k+i)\varphi}.$$

## *Spira mirabilis* and Jacob Bernoulli {#Spira_mirabilis_and_Jacob_Bernoulli}

***Spira mirabilis***, [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for "miraculous spiral", is another name for the logarithmic spiral. Although this curve had already been named by other mathematicians, the specific name ("miraculous" or "marvelous" spiral) was given to this curve by [Jacob Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/64740), because he was fascinated by one of its unique mathematical properties: the size of the spiral increases but its shape is unaltered with each successive curve, a property known as [self-similarity](wikipedia://en/28782). Possibly as a result of this unique property, the spira mirabilis has evolved in nature, appearing in certain growing forms such as [nautilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautilus) shells and [sunflower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower) heads. Jacob Bernoulli wanted such a spiral engraved on his [headstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headstone) along with the phrase "[Eadem mutata resurgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eadem_mutata_resurgo)" ("Although changed, I shall arise the same."), but, by error, an [Archimedean spiral](wikipedia://en/61559) was placed there instead.[^o9][^o10]

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Spiral-log-st-se.svg.* Definition of slope angle and sector

*Image: Logspiral.gif.* Animation showing the constant angle between an intersecting circle centred at the origin and a logarithmic spiral.

The logarithmic spiral $r=a e^{k\varphi} \;,\; k\ne 0,$ has the following properties (see [Spiral](wikipedia://en/61563)):

- **[Pitch angle](wikipedia://en/75407072)**: Constant value $\tan\alpha=k\quad$ with pitch angle $\alpha$ (see diagram and animation).(In case of $k=0$ angle $\alpha$ would be 0 and the curve a circle with radius $a$.)
- **Curvature**: $\kappa=\frac{1}{r\sqrt{1+k^2}}=\frac{\cos \alpha}{r}$
- **Arc length**: $L(\varphi_1,\varphi_2)=\frac{\sqrt{k^2+1}}{k}\big(r(\varphi_2)-r(\varphi_1)\big)= \frac{r(\varphi_2)-r(\varphi_1)}{\sin \alpha}$Especially: $\ L(-\infty,\varphi_2)=\frac{r(\varphi_2)}{\sin \alpha}\quad\;$ is finite if $k > 0$. This property was first realized by [Evangelista Torricelli](wikipedia://en/210413) even before [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) had been invented.[^o13]
- **Sector area:** $A=\frac{r(\varphi_2)^2-r(\varphi_1)^2}{4k}$
- **Inversion:** [Circle inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_inversion) ($r\to 1/r$) maps the logarithmic spiral $r=a e^{k\varphi}$ onto the logarithmic spiral $r=\tfrac{1}{a} e^{-k\varphi} \, .$

*Image: Spiral-log-a-1-5.svg.* Examples for $a= 1,2,3,4,5$

- **Rotating, scaling**: Rotating the spiral by angle $\varphi_0$ yields the spiral $r=ae^{-k\varphi_0}e^{k\varphi}$, which is the original spiral uniformly scaled (at the origin) by $e^{-k\varphi_0}$. Scaling by $\;e^{kn2\pi}\; , n=\pm 1,\pm2,...,\;$ gives the *same* curve.
- **[Self-similarity](wikipedia://en/28782)**: A result of the previous property: A scaled logarithmic spiral is [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) (by rotation) to the original curve. *Example:* The diagram shows spirals with slope angle $\alpha=20^\circ$ and $a=1,2,3,4,5$. Hence they are all scaled copies of the red one. But they can also be generated by rotating the red one by angles $-109^\circ,-173^\circ,-218^\circ,-253^\circ$ resp.. All spirals have no points in common (see property on *complex exponential function*).
- **Relation to other curves:** Logarithmic spirals are congruent to their own [involutes](wikipedia://en/840704), [evolutes](wikipedia://en/842387), and the [pedal curves](wikipedia://en/845864) based on their centers.
- **Complex exponential function**: The [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) exactly maps all lines not parallel with the real or imaginary axis in the complex plane, to all logarithmic spirals in the complex plane with centre at $0$: $$z(t)=\underbrace{(kt+b)\; +it}_{\text{line}}\quad \to\quad e^{z(t)}=e^{kt+b}\cdot e^{it}= \underbrace{e^b e^{kt}(\cos t+i\sin t)}_{\text{log. spiral}}$$ The pitch angle $\alpha$ of the logarithmic spiral is the angle between the line and the imaginary axis.

:::

## Special cases and approximations {#Special_cases_and_approximations}

The [golden spiral](wikipedia://en/219370) is a logarithmic spiral that grows outward by a factor of the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) for every 90 degrees of rotation (pitch angle about 17.03239 degrees). It can be approximated by a "Fibonacci spiral", made of a sequence of quarter circles with radii proportional to [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number).

## In nature {#In_nature}

*Image: Nautilus Cutaway with Logarithmic Spiral.png.* Cutaway of a [nautilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautilus) shell showing the chambers arranged in an approximately logarithmic spiral. The plotted spiral (dashed blue curve) is based on growth rate parameter $b = 0.1759$, resulting in a pitch of $\arctan b \approx 10^\circ$.

In several natural phenomena one may find curves that are close to being logarithmic spirals. Here follow some examples and reasons:

- The approach of a [hawk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawk) to its prey in [classical pursuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_pursuit), assuming the prey travels in a straight line. Their sharpest view is at an angle to their direction of flight; this angle is the same as the spiral's pitch.[^o16]
- The approach of an insect to a light source. They are used to having the light source at a constant angle to their flight path. Usually the Sun (or Moon for nocturnal species) is the only light source and flying that way will result in a practically straight line.[^o17] In the same token, a [rhumb line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhumb_line) approximates a logarithmic spiral close to a pole.
- The arms of spiral [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy).[^o18] The [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way) galaxy has several spiral arms, each of which is roughly a logarithmic spiral with pitch of about 12 degrees.[^o19] However, although spiral galaxies have often been modeled as logarithmic spirals, [Archimedean spirals](wikipedia://en/61559), or [hyperbolic spirals](wikipedia://en/183045), their pitch angles vary with distance from the galactic center, unlike logarithmic spirals (for which this angle does not vary), and also at variance with the other mathematical spirals used to model them.[^o20]
- The nerves of the [cornea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornea) (this is, corneal nerves of the subepithelial layer terminate near superficial epithelial layer of the cornea in a logarithmic spiral pattern).[^o21]
- The [bands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainband) of [tropical cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone), such as hurricanes.[^o22]
- Many [biological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) structures including the shells of [mollusks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mollusca).[^o23] In these cases, the reason may be construction from expanding similar shapes, as is the case for [polygonal](wikipedia://en/23621) figures.
- [Logarithmic spiral beaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral_beaches) can form as the result of wave refraction and diffraction by the coast. [Half Moon Bay (California)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Moon_Bay_%28California%29) is an example of such a type of beach.[^o24]

## In engineering applications {#In_engineering_applications}

- Logarithmic [spiral antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_antenna) are frequency-independent antennas, that is, antennas whose radiation pattern, impedance and polarization remain largely unmodified over a wide bandwidth.[^o27]
- When manufacturing mechanisms by subtractive fabrication machines (such as [laser cutters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_cutting)), there can be a loss of precision when the mechanism is fabricated on a different machine due to the difference of material removed (that is, the [kerf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerf)) by each machine in the cutting process. To adjust for this variation of kerf, the self-similar property of the logarithmic spiral has been used to design a kerf cancelling mechanism for laser cutters.[^o28]
- Logarithmic [spiral bevel gears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_bevel_gear) are a type of spiral bevel gear whose gear tooth centerline is a logarithmic spiral. A logarithmic spiral has the advantage of providing equal angles between the tooth centerline and the radial lines, which gives the meshing transmission more stability.[^o29]
- In [rock climbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_climbing), [spring-loaded camming devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring-loaded_camming_device) are made from metal cams whose outer gripping surfaces are shaped as arcs of logarithmic spirals. When the device is inserted into a rock crack, the rotation of these cams expands their combined width to match the width of the crack, while maintaining a constant angle against the surface of the rock (relative to the center of the spiral, where force is applied). The pitch angle of the spiral is chosen to optimize the friction of the device against the rock.[^o30]
- Soft robots based on the logarithmic spiral were designed for scalable and efficient 3D printing. Using cable-driven actuation, they mimic octopus-like movements for stable and versatile object manipulation.[^o31]
