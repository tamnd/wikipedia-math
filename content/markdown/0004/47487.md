# Azimuth

*Image: Azimuth-Altitude schematic.svg.* The azimuth is the angle formed between a reference direction (in this example, north) and a [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sightline) from the observer to a point of interest projected on the same plane as the reference direction orthogonal to the [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Azimuth"}

An **azimuth** (; from اَلسُّمُوت)[^o2] is the [horizontal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) [angle](wikipedia://en/1196#Measuring_angles) from a [cardinal direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_direction), most commonly [north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North), in a local or observer-centric [spherical coordinate system](wikipedia://en/29181).

:::

Mathematically, the [relative position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_position) [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28physics_and_mathematics%29) from an observer ([origin](wikipedia://en/1313432)) to a point of interest is [projected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_projection) [perpendicularly](wikipedia://en/76944) onto a [reference plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_plane) (the [horizontal plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_plane)); the angle between the projected vector and a reference vector on the reference plane is called the azimuth.

When used as a [celestial coordinate](wikipedia://en/48386), the azimuth is the [horizontal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) direction of a [star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) or other [astronomical object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object) in the [sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky). The star is the point of interest, the reference plane is the local area (e.g. a circular area with a 5 km radius at [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level)) around an observer on [Earth's surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_surface), and the reference vector points to [true north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_north). The azimuth is the angle between the north vector and the star's vector on the [horizontal plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_plane).

Azimuth is usually measured in [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294) (°), in the positive range 0° to 360° or in the signed range -180° to +180°. The concept is used in [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrometry), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), [mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map), [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608), [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining), and [ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word *azimuth* originates from medieval Arabic السموت (*al-sumūt*, pronounced *as-sumūt*), meaning "the directions" (plural of Arabic السمت *al-samt* = "the direction"). The Arabic word entered late [Medieval Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Latin) in an [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) context and, in particular, in the use of the Arabic version of the [astrolabe](wikipedia://en/73664) astronomy instrument. Its first recorded use in English is in the 1390s in [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer)'s *[A Treatise on the Astrolabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_on_the_Astrolabe)*. The first known record in any Western language is in Spanish in the 1270s in an astronomy book that was largely derived from Arabic sources, the *[Libros del saber de astronomía](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libros_del_saber_de_astronomía)*, commissioned by [King Alfonso X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Alfonso_X) of Castile.[^o5]

## In astronomy {#In_astronomy}

In the [horizontal coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48386), used in [celestial navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_navigation), azimuth is one of the two [coordinates](wikipedia://en/81931).[^o7] The other is *[altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_%28astronomy%29)*, sometimes called elevation above the horizon. It is also used for [satellite dish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_dish) installation (see also: [sat finder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sat_finder)). In modern [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), azimuth is nearly always measured from the north.

## In navigation {#In_navigation}

*Image: True North Mount Allen.fw.png.* Azimuth marker, Mount Allen ([Sandstone Peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone_Peak)), Southern California, U.S.

In land navigation, azimuth is usually denoted [alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha), *α*, and defined as a horizontal angle measured [clockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clockwise_and_counterclockwise) from a north base line or *[meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29)*.[^o9][^o10] *Azimuth* has also been more generally defined as a horizontal angle measured clockwise from any fixed reference plane or easily established base direction line.[^o11][^o12][^o13]

Today, the reference plane for an azimuth is typically [true north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_north), measured as a 0° azimuth, though other angular units ([grad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grad_%28angle%29), [mil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_mil)) can be used. Moving clockwise on a 360-degree circle, east has azimuth 90°, south 180°, and west 270°. There are exceptions: some navigation systems use south as the reference vector. Any direction can be the reference vector, as long as it is clearly defined.

Quite commonly, azimuths or compass bearings are stated in a system in which either north or south can be the zero, and the angle may be measured clockwise or anticlockwise from the zero. For example, a bearing might be described as "(from) south, (turn) thirty degrees (toward the) east" (the words in brackets are usually omitted), abbreviated "S30°E", which is the bearing 30 degrees in the eastward direction from south, i.e. the bearing 150 degrees clockwise from north. The reference direction, stated first, is always north or south, and the turning direction, stated last, is east or west. The directions are chosen so that the angle, stated between them, is positive, between zero and 90 degrees. If the bearing happens to be exactly in the direction of one of the [cardinal points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_point), a different notation, e.g. "due east", is used instead.

## In geodesy {#In_geodesy}

*Image: Bearing and azimuth along the geodesic.png.* The azimuth between [Cape Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Town) and [Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne) along the [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) (the shortest route) changes from 141° to 42°. [Azimuthal orthographic projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthographic_projection_in_cartography) and [Miller cylindrical projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_cylindrical_projection).

We are standing at latitude $\varphi_1$, longitude zero; we want to find the azimuth from our viewpoint to Point 2 at latitude $\varphi_2$, longitude *L* (positive eastward). We can get a fair approximation by assuming the Earth is a sphere, in which case the azimuth *α* is given by

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{\sin L}{\cos\varphi_1 \tan\varphi_2 - \sin\varphi_1 \cos L}$$

A better approximation assumes the Earth is a slightly squashed sphere (an *[oblate spheroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblate_spheroid)*); *azimuth* then has at least two very slightly different meanings. **[Normal-section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_normal_section) azimuth** is the angle measured at our viewpoint by a [theodolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodolite) whose axis is perpendicular to the surface of the spheroid; **geodetic azimuth** (or **geodesic azimuth**) is the angle between north and the [ellipsoidal geodesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipsoidal_geodesic) (the shortest path on the surface of the spheroid from our viewpoint to Point 2). The difference is usually negligible: less than 0.03 arc second for distances less than 100 km.[^o17]

Normal-section azimuth can be calculated as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} e^2 &= f(2 - f) \\ 1 - e^2 &= (1 - f)^2 \\ \Lambda &= \left(1 - e^2\right) \frac{\tan\varphi_2}{\tan\varphi_1} + e^2 \sqrt{\frac{1 + \left(1 - e^2\right)\left(\tan\varphi_2\right)^2} {1 + \left(1 - e^2\right)\left(\tan\varphi_1\right)^2}} \\ \tan\alpha &= \frac{\sin L}{(\Lambda - \cos L)\sin\varphi_1} \end{aligned}$$

where *f* is the flattening and *e* the eccentricity for the chosen spheroid (e.g., for [WGS84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System)). If *φ*<sub>1</sub> = 0 then

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{\sin L}{\left(1 - e^2\right)\tan\varphi_2}$$

To calculate the azimuth of the Sun or a star given its [declination](wikipedia://en/8612) and [hour angle](wikipedia://en/48838) at a specific location, modify the formula for a spherical Earth. Replace *φ*<sub>2</sub> with declination and longitude difference with hour angle, and change the sign (since the hour angle is positive westward instead of east).

## In cartography {#In_cartography}

*Image: Brunton.JPG.* A standard Brunton Geo [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass), commonly used by geologists and surveyors to measure azimuth

The **cartographical azimuth** or **grid azimuth** (in decimal degrees) can be calculated when the coordinates of two points are known in a flat plane ([cartographical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_reference_system)):

$$\alpha = \frac{180}{\pi} \operatorname{atan2}(X_2 - X_1, Y_2 - Y_1)$$

Remark that the reference axes are swapped relative to the (counterclockwise) mathematical [polar coordinate system](wikipedia://en/25120) and that the azimuth is clockwise relative to the north. This is why the X and Y axis in the above formula are swapped. If the azimuth becomes negative, one can always add 360°.

The formula in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) would be slightly easier:

$$\alpha = \operatorname{atan2}(X_2 - X_1, Y_2 - Y_1)$$

Note the swapped $(x, y)$ in contrast to the normal $(y, x)$ [atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703) input order.

The opposite problem occurs when the coordinates (*X*<sub>1</sub>, *Y*<sub>1</sub>) of one point, the distance *D*, and the azimuth *α* to another point (*X*<sub>2</sub>, *Y*<sub>2</sub>) are known, one can calculate its coordinates:

$$\begin{aligned} X_2 &= X_1 + D \sin\alpha \\ Y_2 &= Y_1 + D \cos\alpha \end{aligned}$$

This is typically used in [triangulation](wikipedia://en/207174) and azimuth identification (AzID), especially in [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) applications.

### Map projections {#Map_projections}

There is a wide variety of [azimuthal map projections](wikipedia://en/51784#Azimuthal_.28projections_onto_a_plane.29). They all have the property that directions (the azimuths) from a central point are preserved. Some navigation systems use south as the reference plane. However, any direction can serve as the plane of reference, as long as it is clearly defined for everyone using that system.

## Related coordinates {#Related_coordinates}

### Right ascension {#Right_ascension}

If, instead of measuring from and along the horizon, the angles are measured from and along the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681), the angles are called [right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073) if referenced to the [Vernal Equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernal_Equinox), or hour angle if referenced to the [celestial meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_meridian).

### Polar coordinate {#Polar_coordinate}

In mathematics, the azimuth angle of a point in [cylindrical coordinates](wikipedia://en/176733) or [spherical coordinates](wikipedia://en/29181) is the anticlockwise [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between the positive *x*-axis and the projection of the [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) onto the *xy*-[plane](wikipedia://en/19469852). A special case of an azimuth angle is the angle in [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) of the component of the vector in the *xy*-plane, although this angle is normally measured in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) rather than degrees and denoted by *θ* rather than *φ*.

## Other uses {#Other_uses}

For [magnetic tape drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_drive), *azimuth* refers to the angle between the tape head(s) and tape.

In [sound localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_localization) experiments and literature, the *azimuth* refers to the angle the sound source makes compared to the imaginary straight line that is drawn from within the head through the area between the eyes.

An [azimuth thruster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azimuth_thruster) in [shipbuilding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipbuilding) is a [propeller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propeller) that can be rotated horizontally.
