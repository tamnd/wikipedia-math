# Up to

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Up to"}

*Image: Partitions of 6-set with 3 singletons.svg.* *Top:* In a [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) vertex set there are 20 [partitions](wikipedia://en/340240) which have one three-element subset (green) and three single-element subsets (uncolored). *Bottom:* Of these, there are 4 partitions up to rotation, and 3 partitions up to rotation and reflection.

Two [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) $a$ and $b$ are called "equal **up to** an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $R$" if $a$ and $b$ are related by $R$, or, in notation, if $aRb$ holds. Since $a$ is related to $b$ if and only if they have the same [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260), it is also the case that $a$ and $b$ are equal up to $R$ if and only if the equivalence classes of $a$ and $b$ with respect to $R$ are equal.

:::

This [figure of speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure_of_speech) is mostly used in connection with expressions derived from equality, such as uniqueness or count. For example, "$x$ is unique up to $R$" means that all objects $x$ under consideration are in the same equivalence class with respect to the relation $R$.

Moreover, the equivalence relation $R$ is often designated rather implicitly by a generating condition or transformation.

- For example, the statement "an integer's prime factorization is unique up to ordering" is a concise way to say that any two lists of prime factors of a given integer are equivalent with respect to the relation $R$ that relates two lists if one can be obtained by reordering ([permuting](wikipedia://en/44027)) the other.[^o2]
- As another example, the statement "the solution to an indefinite integral is $\sin(x)$, up to addition of a constant" tacitly employs the equivalence relation $R$ between functions, defined by $fRg$ if the [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) difference $f-g$ is a [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861), and means that the solution and the function $\sin(x)$ are equal up to this $R$.
- In the picture, "there are 4 partitions up to rotation" means that the set $P$ has 4 equivalence classes with respect to $R$ defined by $aRb$ if $b$ can be obtained from $a$ by rotation; one representative from each class is shown in the bottom left picture part.

Equivalence relations are often used to disregard possible differences of objects, so "up to $R$" can be understood informally as "ignoring the same subtleties as $R$ ignores". In the factorization example, "up to ordering" means "ignoring the particular ordering".

Further examples include "up to isomorphism", "up to permutations", and "up to rotations", which are described in the [Examples section](wikipedia://en/44787#Examples).

In informal contexts, mathematicians often use the word **[modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_%28jargon%29)** (or simply **mod**) for similar purposes, as in "modulo isomorphism".

Objects that are distinct up to an equivalence relation defined by a group action, such as rotation, reflection, or permutation, can be counted using [Burnside's lemma](wikipedia://en/251900) or its generalization, [Pólya enumeration theorem](wikipedia://en/4396962).

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Tetris {#Tetris}

*Image: Tetrominoes IJLO STZ Worlds.svg.* Tetris pieces I, J, L, O, S, T, Z

Consider the seven [Tetris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris) pieces (I, J, L, O, S, T, Z), known mathematically as the [tetrominoes](wikipedia://en/30411). If all the possible rotations of these pieces are treated as distinct — for example, "I" oriented vertically instead of horizontally — then there are 19 distinct possible shapes to be displayed on the screen. (These 19 are the so-called "fixed" tetrominoes.) But if rotations are not considered distinct — so that both "I vertically" and "I horizontally" are treated indifferently as "I" — then there are only seven. We say that "there are seven [tetrominoes](wikipedia://en/30411), up to rotation". One could also say that "there are five tetrominoes, up to rotation and reflection", which accounts for the fact that L reflected gives J, and S reflected gives Z.

### Eight queens {#Eight_queens}

*Image: Skak 8dronning.png.* A solution of the eight queens problem

In the [eight queens puzzle](wikipedia://en/10474), if the queens are considered to be distinct (e.g. if they are colored with eight different colors), then there are 3709440 distinct solutions. Normally, however, the queens are considered to be interchangeable, and one usually says "there are $3,709,440 / 8! = 92$ unique solutions up to [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of the queens", or that "there are 92 solutions modulo the names of the queens", signifying that two different arrangements of the queens are considered equivalent if the queens have been permuted, as long as the set of occupied squares remains the same.

If, in addition to treating the queens as identical, [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) and [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829) of the board were allowed, we would have only 12 distinct solutions "up to [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) and the naming of the queens". For more, see Eight queens puzzle#Solutions.

### Polygons {#Polygons}

The [regular $n$-gon](wikipedia://en/333306), for a fixed $n$, is unique up to [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377). In other words, the "similarity" equivalence relation over the regular $n$-gons (for a fixed $n$) has only one equivalence class; it is impossible to produce two regular $n$-gons which are not similar to each other.

### Group theory {#Group_theory}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), one may have a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on a set $X$, in which case, one might say that two elements of $X$ are equivalent "up to the group action"—if they lie in the same [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29).

Another typical example is the statement that "there are two different [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of order 4 up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)", or "modulo isomorphism, there are two groups of order 4". This means that, if one considers [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) groups "equivalent", there are only two equivalence classes of groups of order 4, prototypically the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 4 and the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928).

### Nonstandard analysis {#Nonstandard_analysis}

A [hyperreal](wikipedia://en/51429) $x$ and its [standard part](wikipedia://en/19145800) $\operatorname{st}(x)$ are equal up to an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) difference.
