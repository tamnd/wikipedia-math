# Arago spot

*Image: A photograph of the Arago spot.png.* Photo of the Arago spot in a shadow of a 5.8 mm circular obstacle.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arago spot"}

In [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), the **Arago spot**, **Poisson spot**,[^o2][^o3] or **Fresnel spot**[^o4] is a bright point that appears at the center of a circular object's [shadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow) due to [Fresnel diffraction](wikipedia://en/2649192).[^o27][^o28][^o29][^o30] This spot played an important role in the discovery of the [wave nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_nature) of [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) and is a common way to demonstrate that light behaves as a wave.

:::

The basic experimental setup requires a point source, such as an illuminated pinhole or a diverging [laser beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_beam). The dimensions of the setup must comply with the requirements for [Fresnel diffraction](wikipedia://en/2649192). Namely, the [Fresnel number](wikipedia://en/2938800) must satisfy

$$F = \frac{d^2}{\ell \lambda} \gtrsim 1,$$

where

- $d$ is the diameter of the circular object,
- $\ell$ is the distance between the object and the screen, and
- $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the source.

Finally, the edge of the circular object must be sufficiently smooth. With the [laser sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser) available today the Arago-spot can be demonstrated.[^o5]

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), the Arago spot can also be observed in the strongly defocussed image of a [star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) in a [Newtonian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_telescope). There, the star provides an almost ideal [point source](wikipedia://en/7622892) at infinity, and the [secondary mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_mirror) of the telescope constitutes the circular obstacle.

*Image: Arago spot formation.webm.* Formation of the Arago spot by the interfering edge waves

When light shines on the circular obstacle, [Huygens' principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens'_principle) says that every point in the plane of the obstacle acts as a new point source of light. The light coming from points on the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of the obstacle and going to the center of the shadow travels exactly the same distance, so all the light passing close by the object arrives at the screen in [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) and constructively [interferes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_%28wave_propagation%29). This results in a bright spot at the shadow's center, where [geometrical optics](wikipedia://en/731780) and [particle theories of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_theory_of_light) predict that there should be no light at all.[^o46]

## History {#History}

At the beginning of the 19th century, the idea that light does not simply propagate along straight lines gained traction. [Thomas Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_%28scientist%29) published his [double-slit experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young's_double-slit_interferometer) in 1807.[^o31] The original Arago spot experiment was carried out a decade later and was the deciding experiment on the question of whether light is a particle or a wave. It is thus an example of an *[experimentum crucis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimentum_crucis)*.

At that time, many favored [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s [corpuscular theory of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpuscular_theory_of_light), among them the theoretician [Siméon Denis Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670).[^o32] In 1818 the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) launched a competition to explain the properties of light, where Poisson was one of the members of the judging committee. The civil engineer [Augustin-Jean Fresnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin-Jean_Fresnel) entered this competition by submitting a new [wave theory of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_theory_of_light).[^o33]

Poisson studied Fresnel's theory in detail and, being a supporter of the particle theory of light, looked for a way to prove it wrong. Poisson thought that he had found a flaw when he argued that a consequence of Fresnel's theory was that there would exist an on-axis bright spot in the shadow of a circular obstacle, where there should be complete darkness according to the particle theory of light. This prediction was seen as an absurd consequence of the wave theory, and the failure of that prediction should be a strong argument to reject Fresnel's theory.

However, the head of the committee, [François Arago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Arago), decided to actually perform the experiment. He molded a 2 mm metallic disk to a glass plate with wax.[^o34] He succeeded in observing the predicted spot, confirming Fresnel's prediction in 1819.[^o7][^o39][^o8]

Arago later noted[^o9] that the phenomenon (later known as "Poisson's spot" or the "spot of Arago") had already been observed by [Joseph-Nicolas Delisle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Nicolas_Delisle)[^o35] and by [Giacomo F. Maraldi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giacomo_F._Maraldi)[^o36] a century earlier.

Although Arago's experimental result was overwhelming evidence in favor of the wave theory, a century later, in conjunction with the birth of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) (and first suggested in one of [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s [*Annus Mirabilis* papers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annus_Mirabilis_papers)), it became understood that light (as well as all forms of matter and energy) must be described as both a particle and a wave ([wave–particle duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave–particle_duality)). However the particle associated with electromagnetic waves, the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon), has nothing in common with the particles imagined in the corpuscular theory that had been dominant before the rise of the wave theory and Arago's powerful demonstration. Before the advent of quantum theory in the late 1920s, only the wave nature of light could explain phenomena such as [diffraction](wikipedia://en/8603) and [interference](wikipedia://en/15112). Today it is known that a diffraction pattern appears through the mosaic-like buildup of bright spots caused by single photons, as predicted by Dirac's quantum theory. With increasing light intensity the bright dots in the mosaic diffraction pattern just assemble faster. In contrast, the wave theory predicts the formation of an extended continuous pattern whose overall brightness increases with light intensity.

## Theory {#Theory}

*Image: huygensfresnelintegral.jpg.* Notation for calculating the wave amplitude at point P<sub>1</sub> from a spherical point source at P<sub>0</sub>.

At the heart of Fresnel's wave theory is the [Huygens–Fresnel principle](wikipedia://en/14359), which states that every unobstructed point of a wavefront becomes the source of a secondary spherical [wavelet](wikipedia://en/50903) and that the amplitude of the optical field *E* at a point on the screen is given by the superposition of all those secondary wavelets taking into account their relative phases.[^o37] This means that the field at a point P<sub>1</sub> on the screen is given by a [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867):

$$U(P_1) = \frac{A e^{\mathbf{i} k r_0}}{r_0} \int_S \frac{e^{\mathbf{i} k r_1}}{r_1} K(\chi) \, dS,$$

where the inclination factor $K(\chi)$ which ensures that the secondary wavelets do not propagate backwards is given by

$$K(\chi) = \frac{\mathbf{i}}{2 \lambda} (1 + \cos(\chi))$$

and

- *A* is the amplitude of the source wave
- $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ is the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber)
- *S* is the unobstructed surface.

The first term outside of the integral represents the oscillations from the source wave at a distance *r*<sub>0</sub>. Similarly, the term inside the integral represents the oscillations from the secondary wavelets at distances *r*<sub>1</sub>.

In order to derive the intensity behind the circular obstacle using this integral one assumes that the experimental parameters fulfill the requirements of the [near-field diffraction](wikipedia://en/2649192) regime (the size of the circular obstacle is large compared to the wavelength and small compared to the distances *g* = P<sub>0</sub>C and *b* = CP<sub>1</sub>). Going to [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) then yields the integral for a circular object of radius *a* (see for example Born and Wolf[^o39]):

$$U(P_1) = - \frac{\mathbf{i}}{\lambda} \frac{A e^{\mathbf{i} k (g + b)}}{g b} 2\pi \int_a^\infty e^{\mathbf{i} k \frac{1}{2} \left(\frac{1}{g} + \frac{1}{b}\right) r^2} r \, dr.$$

*Image: poissonspotintensity.jpg.* The on-axis intensity at the center of the shadow of a small circular obstacle converges to the unobstructed intensity.

This integral can be solved numerically (see below). If *g* is large and *b* is small so that the angle $\chi$ is not negligible one can write the integral for the on-axis case (P<sub>1</sub> is at the center of the shadow) as (see Sommerfeld[^o38]):

$$U(P_1) = \frac{A e^{\mathbf{i} k g}}{g} \frac{b}{\sqrt{b^2 + a^2}} e^{\mathbf{i} k \sqrt{b^2 + a^2}}.$$

The source [intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensity_%28physics%29), which is the square of the field amplitude, is $I_0 = \left|\frac{1}{g} A e^{\mathbf{i} k g}\right|^2$ and the intensity at the screen $I = \left| U(P_1) \right|^2$. The on-axis intensity as a function of the distance *b* is hence given by:

$$I = \frac{b^2}{b^2 + a^2} I_0.$$

This shows that the on-axis intensity at distances *b* much greater than the diameter of the circular obstacle is the same as the source intensity, as if the circular object was not present at all. However at larger distances *b*, it turns out that the *size* of the bright spot (as can be seen in the simulations below where *b/a* is increased in successive images) is larger therefore making the spot easier to discern.

### Calculation of diffraction images {#Calculation_of_diffraction_images}

To calculate the full diffraction image that is visible on the screen one has to consider the surface integral of the previous section. One cannot exploit [circular symmetry](wikipedia://en/2217599) anymore, because the line between the source and an arbitrary point on the screen does not pass through the center of the circular object. With the aperture function $g(r,\theta)$ which is 1 for transparent parts of the object plane and 0 otherwise (i.e. It is 0 if the direct line between source and the point on the screen passes through the blocking circular object.) the integral that needs to be solved is given by:

$$U(P_1) \propto \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^\infty g(r,\theta) e^{\frac{\mathbf{i} \pi \rho^2}{\lambda} \left( \frac{1}{g} + \frac{1}{b} \right)} \rho \, d\rho \, d\theta.$$

Numerical calculation of the integral using the [trapezoidal rule](wikipedia://en/573452) or [Simpson's rule](wikipedia://en/219962) is not efficient and becomes numerically unstable especially for configurations with large [Fresnel number](wikipedia://en/2938800). However, it is possible to solve the radial part of the integral so that only the integration over the azimuth angle remains to be done numerically.[^o40] For a particular angle one must solve the [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) for the ray with origin at the intersection point of the line P<sub>0</sub>P<sub>1</sub> with the circular object plane. The contribution for a particular ray with azimuth angle $\theta_1$ and passing a transparent part of the object plane from $r = s$ to $r = t$ is:

$$R(\theta_1) \propto e^{\frac{\pi}{2} \mathbf{i} s^2} - e^{\frac{\pi}{2} \mathbf{i} t^2}.$$

So for each angle one has to compute the intersection point(*s*) of the ray with the circular object and then sum the contributions $I(\theta_1)$ for a certain number of angles between 0 and $2\pi$. Results of such a calculation are shown in the following images.

*Image: poissonspot simulation d4mm.jpg.*

*Image: poissonspot simulation d2mm.jpg.*

*Image: poissonspot simulation d1mm.jpg.*

The images are simulations of the Arago spot in the shadow of discs of diameter 4 mm, 2 mm, and 1 mm, imaged 1 m behind each disc. The disks are illuminated by light of wavelength of 633 nm, diverging from a point 1 m in front of each disc. Each image is 16 mm wide.

The Arago spot can also be visualized using lines of average energy flow calculated numerically by averaging the [Poynting vector](wikipedia://en/49718) of the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field).[^o13][^o12]

## Experimental aspects {#Experimental_aspects}

### Intensity and size {#Intensity_and_size}

*Image: poissonspot setup treisinger.jpg.* Arago spot experiment. A point source illuminates a circular object, casting a shadow on a screen. At the shadow's center a bright spot appears due to [diffraction](wikipedia://en/8603), contradicting the prediction of [geometric optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_optics).

For an ideal [point source](wikipedia://en/7622892), the intensity of the Arago spot equals that of the undisturbed [wave front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_front). Only the width of the Arago spot intensity peak depends on the distances between source, circular object and screen, as well as the source's wavelength and the diameter of the circular object. This means that one can compensate for a reduction in the source's [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) by increasing the distance between the circular object and screen or reducing the circular object's diameter.

The lateral intensity distribution on the screen has in fact the shape of a squared [zeroth Bessel function of the first kind](wikipedia://en/4700) when close to the [optical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_axis) and using a [plane wave source](wikipedia://en/41559) (point source at infinity):[^o41]

$$U(P_1, r) \propto J_0^2 \left(\frac{\pi r d}{\lambda b}\right)$$

where

- *r* is the distance of the point *P*<sub>1</sub> on the screen from the optical axis
- *d* is the diameter of circular object
- *λ* is the wavelength
- *b* is the distance between circular object and screen.

The following images show the radial intensity distribution of the simulated Arago spot images above:

*Image: poissonspot simulation d2mm lateral.jpg.*

*Image: poissonspot simulation d1mm lateral.jpg.*

The red lines in these three graphs correspond to the simulated images above, and the green lines were computed by applying the corresponding parameters to the squared Bessel function given above.

### Finite source size and spatial coherence {#Finite_source_size_and_spatial_coherence}

The main reason why the Arago spot is hard to observe in circular shadows from conventional light sources is that such light sources are bad approximations of point sources. If the wave source has a finite size *S* then the Arago spot will have an extent that is given by *Sb*/*g*, as if the circular object acted like a lens.[^o37] At the same time the intensity of the Arago spot is reduced with respect to the intensity of the undisturbed wave front. Defining the relative intensity $I_\text{rel}$as the intensity divided by the intensity of the undisturbed wavefront, the relative intensity for an extended circular source of diameter w can be expressed exactly using the following equation:[^o17]

$$I_\text{rel}(w) = J_0^2\left(\frac{w R \pi}{g \lambda}\right) + J_1^2\left(\frac{w R \pi}{g \lambda}\right)$$

where $J_0$and $J_1$are the Bessel functions of the first kind. $R$ is the radius of the disc casting the shadow, $\lambda$ the wavelength and $g$ the distance between source and disc. For large sources the following asymptotic approximation applies:[^o17]

$$I_\text{rel}(w) \approx \frac{2 g \lambda }{\pi^2 w R}$$

### Deviation from circularity {#Deviation_from_circularity}

If the cross-section of the circular object deviates slightly from its circular shape (but it still has a sharp edge on a smaller scale) the shape of the point-source Arago spot changes. In particular, if the object has an ellipsoidal cross-section the Arago spot has the shape of an [evolute](wikipedia://en/842387).[^o42] Note that this is only the case if the source is close to an ideal point source. From an extended source the Arago spot is only affected marginally, since one can interpret the Arago spot as a [point-spread function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-spread_function). Therefore, the image of the extended source only becomes washed out due to the convolution with the point-spread function, but it does not decrease in overall intensity.

### Surface roughness of circular object {#Surface_roughness_of_circular_object}

The Arago spot is very sensitive to small-scale deviations from the ideal circular cross-section. This means that a small amount of surface roughness of the circular object can completely cancel out the bright spot. This is shown in the following three diagrams which are simulations of the Arago spot from a 4 mm diameter disc (*g* = *b* = 1 m):

*Image: poissonspot simulation d4mm lateral cor10.jpg.*

*Image: poissonspot simulation d4mm lateral cor50.jpg.*

*Image: poissonspot simulation d4mm lateral cor100.jpg.*

The simulation includes a regular sinusoidal corrugation of the circular shape of amplitude 10 μm, 50 μm and 100 μm, respectively. Note, that the 100 μm edge corrugation almost completely removes the central bright spot.

This effect can be best understood using the [Fresnel zone concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresnel_zone). The field transmitted by a radial segment that stems from a point on the obstacle edge provides a contribution whose phase is tight to the position of the edge point relative to Fresnel zones. If the variance in the radius of the obstacle are much smaller than the width of Fresnel zone near the edge, the contributions form radial segments are approximately in phase and [interfere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_%28wave_propagation%29) constructively. However, if random edge corrugation have amplitude comparable to or greater than the width of that adjacent Fresnel zone, the contributions from radial segments are no longer in phase and cancel each other reducing the Arago spot intensity.

The adjacent Fresnel zone is approximately given by:[^o43]

$$\Delta r \approx \sqrt{r^2 + \lambda \frac{g b}{g + b}} - r.$$

The edge corrugation should not be much more than 10% of this width to see a close to ideal Arago spot. In the above simulations with the 4 mm diameter disc the adjacent Fresnel zone has a width of about 77 μm.

## Arago spot with matter waves {#Arago_spot_with_matter_waves}

In 2009, the Arago spot experiment was demonstrated with a supersonic expansion beam of [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium) molecules (an example of neutral [matter waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_waves)).[^o43] Material particles behaving like waves is known from [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). The wave nature of particles actually dates back to [de Broglie's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_de_Broglie) hypothesis[^o44] as well as [Davisson and Germer's experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davisson–Germer_experiment).[^o45] An Arago spot of electrons, which also constitute matter waves, can be observed in [transmission electron microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_electron_microscope) when examining circular structures of a certain size.

The observation of an Arago spot with large molecules, thus proving their wave-nature, is a topic of current research.[^o43]

## Other applications {#Other_applications}

Beside the demonstration of wave-behavior, the Arago spot also has a few other applications. One of the ideas is to use the Arago spot as a straight line reference in alignment systems.[^o22] Another is to probe aberrations in laser beams by using the spot's sensitivity to beam [aberrations](wikipedia://en/2704).[^o41] Finally, the [aragoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aragoscope) has been proposed as a method for dramatically improving the diffraction-limited resolution of space-based telescopes.[^o23][^o24]
