# Adrien-Marie Legendre

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Adrien-Marie Legendre"}

*Image: Legendre Adrien-Marie (croquis Barral).png.* Portrait based on an anonymous and undated sketch, allegedly given by Legendre to [François Arago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Arago) in 1829, according to Arago's son.

*Image: Blason Adrien-Marie Legendre.svg.* Coat of Arms of Adrien-Marie Legendre, as he was knighted in 1811

**Adrien-Marie Legendre** (;[^o5] ; 18 September 1752 – 9 January 1833) was a French [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who made numerous contributions to [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). Well-known and important concepts such as the [Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/100349) and [Legendre transformation](wikipedia://en/390273) are named after him. He is also known for his contributions to the [method of least squares](wikipedia://en/82359), and was the first to officially publish on it, though [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) had discovered it before him.[^o6][^o7]

:::

## Life {#Life}

Adrien-Marie Legendre was born in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989) on 18 September 1752 to a wealthy family. He received his education at the [Collège Mazarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_Mazarin) in Paris, and defended his thesis in physics and mathematics in 1770. He taught at the [École Militaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Militaire) in Paris from 1775 to 1780 and at the [École Normale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure) from 1795. At the same time, he was associated with the [Bureau des Longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_des_Longitudes). In 1782, the [Berlin Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences) awarded Legendre a prize for his treatise on projectiles in resistant media. This treatise also brought him to the attention of [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange).

The *[Académie des sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_des_sciences)* made Legendre an adjoint member in 1783 and an associate in 1785. In 1789, he was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society).[^o10]

He assisted with the [Anglo-French Survey (1784–1790)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-French_Survey_%281784–1790%29) to calculate the precise distance between the [Paris Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Observatory) and the [Royal Greenwich Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Greenwich_Observatory) by means of [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261). To this end in 1787 he visited Dover and London together with [Dominique, comte de Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominique,_comte_de_Cassini) and [Pierre Méchain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Méchain). The three also visited [William Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Herschel), the discoverer of the planet [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus).

Legendre lost his private fortune in 1793 during the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution). That year, he also married Marguerite-Claudine Couhin, who helped him put his affairs in order. In 1795, Legendre became one of six members of the mathematics section of the reconstituted Académie des Sciences, renamed the Institut National des Sciences et des Arts. Later, in 1803, Napoleon reorganized the Institut National, and Legendre became a member of the Geometry section. From 1799 to 1812, Legendre served as mathematics examiner for graduating artillery students at the École Militaire and from 1799 to 1815 he served as permanent mathematics examiner for the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique).[^o11] In 1824, Legendre's pension from the École Militaire was stopped because he refused to vote for the government candidate at the Institut National. In 1831, he was made an officer of the [Légion d'Honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'Honneur).[^o9]

Legendre died in Paris on 9 January 1833, after a long and painful illness, and Legendre's widow carefully preserved his belongings to memorialize him. Upon her death in 1856, she was buried next to her husband in the village of [Auteuil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auteuil,_Paris), where the couple had lived, and left their last country house to the village. Legendre's name is one of the [72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_72_names_on_the_Eiffel_Tower).

*Image: Tombe Adrien-Marie Legendre, Cimetière d'Auteuil, Paris.jpg.* Legendre's grave at the Auteuil cemetery

## Mathematical work {#Mathematical_work}

[Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Abel)'s work on [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939) was built on Legendre's, and some of [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125)'s work in statistics and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) completed that of Legendre. He developed, and first communicated to his contemporaries before Gauss, the [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) method [^o13] which has broad application in [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386), [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), statistics, and [curve fitting](wikipedia://en/555425); this was published in 1806 as an appendix to his book on the paths of comets. Today, the term "least squares method" is used as a direct translation from the French "méthode des moindres carrés".

His major work is *Exercices de Calcul Intégral*, published in three volumes in 1811, 1817 and 1819. In the first volume he introduced the basic properties of elliptic integrals, [beta functions](wikipedia://en/245660) and [gamma functions](wikipedia://en/12316), introducing the symbol Γ and normalizing it to Γ(n+1) = n!. Further results on the beta and gamma functions along with their applications to mechanics – such as the rotation of the earth, and the attraction of ellipsoids – appeared in the second volume.[^o14] In 1830, he gave a proof of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) for exponent *n* = 5, which was also proven by [Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) in 1828.[^o14]

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), he conjectured the [quadratic reciprocity](wikipedia://en/25272) law, subsequently proved by Gauss; in connection to this, the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567) is named after him. He also did pioneering work on the distribution of [primes](wikipedia://en/23666), and on the application of analysis to number theory. His 1798 conjecture of the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692) was rigorously proved by [Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675) and [de la Vallée-Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jean_de_la_Vallée-Poussin) in 1896.

Legendre did an impressive amount of work on [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939), including the classification of [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960), but it took [Abel](wikipedia://en/21573)'s study of the inverses of [Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jakob_Jacobi)'s [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi's_elliptic_functions) to solve the problem completely.

He is known for the [Legendre transformation](wikipedia://en/390273), which is used to go from the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) to the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) formulation of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586). In [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) it is also used to obtain the [enthalpy](wikipedia://en/10274) and the [Helmholtz](wikipedia://en/255447) and [Gibbs](wikipedia://en/238181) [(free) energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy) from the [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757). He is also the namesake of the [Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/100349), solutions to Legendre's differential equation, which occur frequently in physics and engineering applications, such as [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics).

Legendre is best known as the author of *Éléments de géométrie*, which was published in 1794 and was the leading elementary text on the topic for around 100 years. This text greatly rearranged and simplified many of the propositions from [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107) to create a more effective textbook.

## Mistaken portrait {#Mistaken_portrait}

For two centuries, until the discovery of the error in 2005, books, paintings and articles had incorrectly shown a profile portrait of the obscure French politician [Louis Legendre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Legendre) (1752–1797) as a portrait of the mathematician. The error arose from the fact that the sketch was labelled simply "Legendre" and appeared in a book along with contemporary mathematicians such as Lagrange. One of only two known portraits of Legendre, rediscovered in 2008, is found in the 1820 book *Album de 73 portraits-charge aquarellés des membres de I'Institut*, a book of caricatures of seventy-three members of the Institut de France in Paris by the French artist [Julien-Léopold Boilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julien-Léopold_Boilly) as shown below.[^o20][^o4] The other portrait is from the book *Le Panthéon scientifique de la tour Eiffel*.[^o21]
