# Pseudorandom noise

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudorandom noise"}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), **pseudorandom noise** (**PRN**[^o2]) is a [signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal) similar to [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_%28physics%29) which satisfies one or more of the standard tests for [statistical randomness](wikipedia://en/2551773). Although it seems to lack any definite [pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern), pseudorandom noise consists of a deterministic [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [pulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_%28signal_processing%29) that will repeat itself after its period.

:::

In [cryptographic devices](wikipedia://en/18934432), the pseudorandom noise pattern is determined by a [key](wikipedia://en/53039) and the repetition period can be very long, even millions of digits.

Pseudorandom noise is used in some [electronic musical instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthesizer), either by itself or as an input to [subtractive synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtractive_synthesis), and in many [white noise machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_noise_machine).

In [spread-spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread-spectrum) systems, the receiver [correlates](wikipedia://en/157057) a locally generated signal with the received [signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_%28information_theory%29). Such spread-spectrum systems require a set of one or more "codes" or "sequences" such that

- Like random noise, the local sequence has a very low correlation with any other sequence in the set, or with the same sequence at a significantly different time offset, or with narrow band interference, or with thermal noise.
- Unlike random noise, it must be easy to generate exactly the same sequence at both the transmitter and the receiver, so the receiver's locally generated sequence has a very high correlation with the transmitted sequence.

In a [direct-sequence spread spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-sequence_spread_spectrum) system, each bit in the [pseudorandom binary sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom_binary_sequence) is known as a *[chip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chip_%28CDMA%29)* and the *inverse* of its period as *[chip rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chip_rate)*; *compare [bit rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_rate) and [symbol rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol_rate).*

In a [frequency-hopping spread spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-hopping_spread_spectrum) sequence, each value in the pseudorandom sequence is known as a *channel number* and the *inverse* of its period as the *hop rate*. [FCC Part 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FCC_Part_15) mandates at least 50 different channels and at least a 2.5 Hz hop rate for narrow band frequency-hopping systems.

GPS satellites broadcast data at a rate of 50 data bits per second – each satellite modulates its data with one PN bit stream at 1.023 million [chips per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chips_per_second) and the same data with another PN bit stream at 10.23 million chips per second. [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS) receivers correlate the received PN bit stream with a local reference to measure distance. GPS is a receive-only system that uses relative timing measurements from several satellites (and the known positions of the satellites) to determine receiver position.

Other [range-finding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range-finding) applications involve two-way transmissions. A local station generates a pseudorandom bit sequence and transmits it to the remote location (using any modulation technique). Some object at the remote location echoes this PN signal back to the location station – either passively, as in some kinds of radar and sonar systems, or using an active transponder at the remote location, as in the Apollo [Unified S-band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unified_S-band) system.[^o4] By correlating a (delayed version of) the transmitted signal with the received signal, a precise round trip time to the remote location can be determined and thus the distance.

## PN code {#PN_code}

A **pseudo-noise code** (**PN code**) or **pseudo-random-noise code** (**PRN code**) is one that has a spectrum similar to a [random sequence](wikipedia://en/4257548) of bits but is [deterministically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_computation) generated. The most commonly used sequences in [direct-sequence spread spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-sequence_spread_spectrum) systems are [maximal length sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_length_sequence), [Gold codes](wikipedia://en/50567), [Kasami codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasami_code), and [Barker codes](wikipedia://en/4507652).[^o7]
