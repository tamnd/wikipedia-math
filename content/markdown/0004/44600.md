# Carnelian

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Carnelian"}

**Carnelian** (also spelled **cornelian**)[^o5] is a brownish-red [mineral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) commonly used as a [semiprecious stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gemstone). Similar to carnelian is **sard**, which is generally harder and darker; the difference is not rigidly defined, and the two names are often used interchangeably. Both carnelian and sard are varieties of the [silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica) mineral [chalcedony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedony) colored by impurities of [iron oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron%28III%29_oxide). The color can vary greatly, ranging from pale orange to an intense almost-black coloration. Significant localities include [Yanacodo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanacodo), Peru and [Ratnapura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratnapura), Sri Lanka.[^o3] It has been found in [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia), [Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India), [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran), [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) ([Siberia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siberia)), and [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany).[^o6]

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Korwin signet-ring1.PNG.* Polish [signet ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signet_ring) in light-orange carnelian [intaglio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraved_gem) showing [Korwin coat of arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korwin_coat_of_arms)

The red variety of chalcedony has been known to be used as beads since the Early Neolithic in Bulgaria. The first faceted (with constant 16+16=32 facets on each side of the bead) carnelian beads are described from the Varna [Chalcolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcolithic) necropolis (middle of the 5th millennium BC).[^n1] The [bow drill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_drill) was used to drill holes into carnelian in [Mehrgarh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehrgarh) in the 4th–5th millennium BC.[^o9] [Etched carnelian beads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etched_carnelian_beads) were manufactured by the Indus Valley Civilization during the 3rd millennium BCE. Carnelian was recovered from [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age) [Minoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoan_civilization) layers at [Knossos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knossos) on [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete) in a form that demonstrated its use in decorative arts;[^o10] this use dates to approximately 1800 [BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_the_Common_Era). Carnelian was used widely during Roman times to make [engraved gems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraved_gems) for signet or [seal rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_ring) for imprinting a seal with wax on correspondence or other important documents, as hot wax does not stick to carnelian.[^o11] Sard was used for [Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyria) [cylinder seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_seal), [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) and [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia) [scarabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarab_%28artifact%29), and early [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) and Etruscan gems. The [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) *odem* (also translated as *sardius*), was the first stone in the [High Priest's breastplate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoshen), a red stone, probably sard but perhaps red [jasper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasper).[^o12] In [Revelation 4:3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation_4), the One seated on the heavenly throne seen in the vision of [John the Apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Apostle) is said to "look like jasper and σαρδίῳ (*sardius* transliterated)." And likewise it is in Revelation 21:20 as one of the precious stones in the foundations of the wall of the heavenly city.

*Image: Carnelian intaglio Prolemaic queen CdM Paris.jpg.* Carnelian [intaglio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engraved_gem) with a [Ptolemaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_dynasty) queen, Hellenistic artwork, [Cabinet des Médailles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_des_Médailles), [BnF Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BnF_Museum), Paris

There is a [Neo-Assyrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Assyrian) seal made of carnelian in the Western Asiatic Seals collection of the [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum) that shows [Ishtar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ishtar)-[Gula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gula_%28goddess%29) as a star goddess. She is holding a ring of royal authority and is seated on a throne. She is shown with the spade of [Marduk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marduk) (his symbol), Sibbiti (seven) gods, the stylus of [Nabu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabu) and a worshiper. An 8th century BC carnelian seal from the collection of the [Ashmolean Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashmolean_Museum) in Oxford shows Ishtar-Gula with her dog facing the spade of Marduk and his red dragon.[^o14]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

Although now the more common term, "carnelian" is a 16th-century corruption of the 14th-century word "cornelian" (and its associated orthographies corneline and cornalyn).[^o16] Cornelian, cognate with similar words in several [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages), comes from the [Mediaeval Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediaeval_Latin) corneolus, itself derived from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) word *cornum*, the [cornel cherry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Cornel), whose translucent red fruits resemble the stone. The *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* calls "carnelian" a perversion of "cornelian," by subsequent analogy with the Latin word *caro, carnis* ("flesh"). According to [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder), sard derived its name from the city of [Sardis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sardis) in [Lydia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia) from which it came, and according to others, may ultimately be related to the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language) word *سرد* (*sered*, "yellowish-red").[^o12] Another possible derivation is from the Greek *σάρξ* (*sarx*, "flesh"); compare the surer etymology of *[onyx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onyx)*, which comes from Greek *ὄνυξ* (*onyx*, "claw, fingernail"), presumably because onyx with flesh-colored and white bands can resemble a fingernail.[^o18]

## Distinction between carnelian and sard {#Distinction_between_carnelian_and_sard}

The names carnelian and sard are often used interchangeably, but they can also be used to describe distinct subvarieties. The general differences are as follows:[^o12]

*Comparison of carnelian and sard*

::: {#o20 .table cue=table name="Comparison of carnelian and sard"}

| Aspect | Carnelian | Sard |
|---|---|---|
| Color | Lighter, with shades ranging from orange to reddish brown | Darker, with shades ranging from a deep reddish brown to almost black |
| Hardness | Softer | Harder and tougher |
| Fracture | Uneven, splintery and conchoidal | Like carnelian, but duller and more hackly (having the appearance of something that has been hacked, i.e. jagged) |

:::

All of these properties vary across a continuum, so the boundary between carnelian and sard is inherently blurry.
