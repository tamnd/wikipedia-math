# Demography

the academic discipline

*Image: Global Population-Pyramid-1950-to-2100.jpg.* The Demography of the World Population from 1950 to 2100. Data source: United Nations — World Population Prospects 2017

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Demography"}

**Demography** (grc) is the [statistical study](wikipedia://en/26685) of human [populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_%28human_biology%29): their size, composition (e.g., [ethnic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group), age), and how they change through the interplay of fertility (births), mortality (deaths), and [migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration).[^o3]

:::

**Formal demography** limits its object of study to the measurement of population processes, while the broader field of **social demography** (or population studies) also analyses the relationships among economic, social, institutional, cultural, and biological processes that influence a population.[^o4] Educational institutions usually treat demography as a field of [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology), although independent demography departments do exist.[^o5][^o6]

**Demographic analysis** (usually abbreviated to DA) examines and measures the dimensions and [dynamics](wikipedia://en/501466) of populations, including whole societies or groups defined by criteria such as [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education), [nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationality), [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion), and [ethnicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnicity).[^o7] The methods of DA have primarily been developed to study human populations, but have also been used in a variety of areas where researchers want to know how other non-human populations of [social actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_actions) can change across time through processes of birth, death, and [migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration).

Demographic analysis is used in a wide variety of contexts. In the [labor force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_force), DA is used to estimate sizes and flows of populations of workers; in [population ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_ecology) the focus is on the birth, death, emigration and immigration of individuals in a population of living organisms; in the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science) this could involve the movement of firms and [institutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution) forms; in business planning DA is often used to describe the population in a business's geographic area.[^o8]

In the context of [human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human) biological populations, demographic analysis uses [administrative records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_records) to develop an independent [estimate](wikipedia://en/336271) of the population.[^o9] For example, **patient demographics** such as [date of birth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_of_birth), [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender), date of [death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death), postal code, ethnicity, [blood type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_type), emergency contact information, family doctor, insurance provider data, [allergies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergies), major diagnoses, and major medical history form the core of the data for any medical institution, allowing the identification and categorization of a patient for the purpose of statistical analysis.[^o10]

Demographic analysis estimates are often considered a reliable standard for judging the accuracy of [census](wikipedia://en/6889) information. For example, the [U.S. Census Bureau](wikipedia://en/57070) expanded its DA categories for the 2010 U.S. Census to include comparative analysis between independent housing estimates, and differences between census address lists at different key times.[^o7]

## History {#History}

Demographic thought traces back to antiquity, and was present in many civilizations and cultures like [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), [Ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome), [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) and [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India).[^o13] Made up of the prefix *demo-* and the suffix *[-graphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-graphy)*, the term *demography* refers to the overall study of population.[^o14]

Demography is discussed in the works of Greek thinkers such as [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus), [Thucydides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thucydides), [Hippocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocrates), [Epicurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurus), [Protagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protagoras), [Polus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polus), [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) and [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), and Romans such as [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero), [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger), [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder), [Marcus Aurelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius), [Epictetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epictetus), [Cato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_the_Elder), and [Columella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Columella).[^o13]

In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christians) thinkers devoted much time to refuting the Classical ideas on demography. Important contributors to the field were [William of Conches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Conches),[^o15] [Bartholomew of Lucca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomew_of_Lucca),[^o15] [William of Auvergne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Auvergne,_Bishop_of_Paris),[^o15] [William of Pagula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Pagula),[^o15] and [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims) sociologists like [Ibn Khaldun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Khaldun).[^o16]

One of the earliest demographic studies in the modern period was *Natural and Political Observations Made upon the Bills of Mortality* (1662) by [John Graunt](wikipedia://en/30875381), which contains a primitive form of [life table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuarial_table). Among the study's findings were that one-third of the children in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London) died before their sixteenth birthday. Mathematicians, such as [Edmond Halley](wikipedia://en/36858805), developed the life table as the basis for life insurance mathematics. [Richard Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Price) was credited with the first textbook on life contingencies published in 1771,[^o17] followed later by [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289), *On the Application of Probabilities to Life Contingencies* (1838).[^o18]

In 1755, [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin) published his essay *[Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind, Peopling of Countries, etc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observations_Concerning_the_Increase_of_Mankind,_Peopling_of_Countries,_etc.)*, projecting [exponential growth](wikipedia://en/191933) in [British colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_colonies).[^o19] His work influenced [Thomas Robert Malthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Robert_Malthus),[^o20] who, writing at the end of the 18th century, feared that, if unchecked, population growth would tend to outstrip growth in food production, leading to ever-increasing famine and poverty (see [Malthusian catastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malthusian_catastrophe)). Malthus is seen as the intellectual father of ideas of [overpopulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_overpopulation) and the limits to growth. Later, more sophisticated and realistic models were presented by [Benjamin Gompertz](wikipedia://en/501523) and [Verhulst](wikipedia://en/491402).

In 1855, [Belgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium) scholar Achille Guillard defined demography as the natural and social history of the human species or the mathematical knowledge of populations, of their general changes, and of their physical, civil, intellectual, and moral condition.[^o21]

Newell (1988, pp. 4-5) claims that the first major developments in the 20th century, in what was to become formal demography, were made in three papers by [Alfred J. Lotka](wikipedia://en/484120) (1907, 1911 (with F.R. Sharpe), and 1922, which developed a Stable Population Model. This model was similar to [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)'s earlier, overlooked modeling, which showed how a population with constant fertility and mortality might grow geometrically using a difference equation. Under this geometric growth model, Euler also examined relationships among various demographic indices, showing how they could be used to produce estimates when data were missing. Lotka and Sharpe showed that a closed population, assuming constant both age-specific mortality and fertility, developed along a path leading to a fixed age structure - The Stable Population.[^o22][^o23][^o24][^o25][^o26]

## Methods {#Methods}

*Image: 1888-01-22, La Ilustración Española y Americana, El censo de población, Alcázar.jpg.* Early censuses and surveys provided demographic data.

Demographic [data collection](wikipedia://en/11501746) can be direct, meaning the desired statistics are explicitly recorded, or indirect, meaning statistics must be estimated via proxy data.[^o29]

### Direct methods {#Direct_methods}

Direct data comes from [vital statistics registries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_registration) that track births and deaths; certain changes in legal status such as marriage and divorce; and registration of place of residence, which may be used to track migration. In [developed countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developed_country), registry statistics are the most accurate method for estimating the number of births and deaths.[^o29]

A [census](wikipedia://en/6889) is a common direct method for collecting demographic data. A government, commonly a national government, conducts a census and attempts to enumerate every person in a country[^o31]. In contrast to vital statistics data, which are typically collected continuously and summarized annually, censuses occur only every 10 years or so and thus are not usually the best source of data on births and deaths. Analyses are conducted after a census to estimate the extent of over- or undercounting. These analyses compare the [sex ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_ratio) from the census data to those estimated from natural values and mortality data.

Censuses also typically collect information about families or households, in addition to individual characteristics such as age, sex, marital status, literacy/education, employment status, occupation, and geographical location. They may also collect data on migration (or place of birth or of previous residence), language, religion, nationality, ethnicity/race, and citizenship. In countries in which the vital registration system may be incomplete, the censuses are also used as a direct source of information about fertility and mortality; for example, the censuses of the [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_China) gather information on births and deaths that occurred in the 18 months immediately preceding the census.

### Indirect methods {#Indirect_methods}

Indirect methods of data collection are required in countries and periods where full data are unavailable, as is the case in much of the developing world and in most of [historical demography](wikipedia://en/10817881). One of these techniques in contemporary demography is the sister method, in which survey researchers ask women how many of their sisters have died or had children, and at what ages. With these surveys, researchers can then indirectly estimate birth or death rates for the entire population. Other indirect methods in contemporary demography include asking people about siblings, parents, and children. Other indirect methods are necessary in historical demography.

There are a variety of demographic methods for modelling population processes. They include models of mortality (including the [life table](wikipedia://en/1614128), [Gompertz models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gompertz_curve), [hazards models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_hazards_models), Cox proportional hazards models, [multiple decrement life tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decrement_table), Brass relational logits), [fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility) (Hermes model, [Coale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ansley_J._Coale)-Trussell models, [parity progression ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_progression_ratios)), marriage (Singulate Mean at Marriage, Page model), disability ([Sullivan's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sullivan's_method), multistate life tables), [population projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_projections) ([Lee-Carter model](wikipedia://en/28975424), the [Leslie Matrix](wikipedia://en/1545816)), and [population momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_momentum) ([Keyfitz](wikipedia://en/38349113)).

The United Kingdom has a series of four national birth cohort studies, the first three spaced apart by 12 years: the [1946 National Survey of Health and Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Survey_of_Health_&_Development), the 1958 [National Child Development Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Child_Development_Study),[^o35] the [1970 British Cohort Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_British_Cohort_Study),[^o36] and the [Millennium Cohort Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Cohort_Study_%28United_Kingdom%29), begun much more recently in 2000. These have followed the lives of samples of people (typically beginning with around 17,000 participants in each study) for many years and are still ongoing. As the samples have been drawn in a nationally representative way, inferences can be drawn from these studies about the differences between four distinct generations of British people in terms of their health, education, attitudes, childbearing and employment patterns.[^o37]

Indirect standardization is used when a population is small enough that the number of events (births, deaths, etc.) is also small. In this case, methods must be used to produce a standardized [mortality rate](wikipedia://en/191305) (SMR) or standardized incidence rate (SIR).[^o38][^o39]

## Standardization of population numbers {#Standardization_of_population_numbers}

For a comparison to be [statistically significant](wikipedia://en/160995), numbers must be adjusted for the size of the population being studied. For example, the [fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_rate) is calculated as the ratio of births among women of childbearing age to the total number of women in that age range. If these adjustments were not made, we would not know whether a nation with a higher birth or death rate has a population with more women of childbearing age or more births per eligible woman.

Within the category of [standardization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardization), there are two major approaches: direct standardization and indirect standardization.[^o41]

## Common rates and ratios {#Common_rates_and_ratios}

- The **crude [birth rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_rate)**, the annual number of live births per 1,000 people.
- The **general [fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_rate)**, the annual number of live births per 1,000 women of childbearing age (often taken to be from 15 to 49 years old, but sometimes from 15 to 44).
- The **age-specific fertility** rates, the annual number of live births per 1,000 women in particular age groups (usually age 15–19, 20–24, etc.)
- The **crude [death rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_rate)**, the annual number of deaths per 1,000 people.
- The **[infant mortality rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality_rate)**, the annual number of deaths of children less than 1 year old per 1,000 live births.
- The **expectation of life** (or [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669)), the number of years that an individual at a given age could expect to live at present mortality levels.
- The **[total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate)**, the number of live births per woman completing her reproductive life, if her childbearing at each age reflected current age-specific fertility rates.
- The **[replacement level fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replacement-level_fertility)**, the average number of children women must have to replace the population for the next generation. For example, the replacement level fertility in the US is 2.11.[^o43]
- The **[gross reproduction rate](wikipedia://en/12983678)**, the number of daughters who would be born to a woman completing her reproductive life at current age-specific fertility rates.
- The **[net reproduction ratio](wikipedia://en/12983357)** is the expected number of daughters, per newborn prospective mother, who may or may not survive to and through the ages of childbearing.
- A **stable population**, one that has had constant crude birth and death rates for such a long period of time that the percentage of people in every age class remains constant, or equivalently, the [population pyramid](wikipedia://en/989011) has an unchanging structure.[^o43]
- A **stationary population**, one that is both stable and unchanging in size (the difference between crude birth rate and crude death rate is zero).[^o43]
- Measures of centralisation are concerned with the extent to which an area's population is concentrated in its [urban centres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area).[^o44][^o45]

A stable population does not necessarily remain fixed in size. It can be expanding or shrinking.[^o43]

The crude death rate, as defined above and applied to a whole population, can give a misleading impression. For example, the number of deaths per 1,000 people can be higher in developed nations than in less-developed countries, despite standards of health being better in developed countries. This is because developed countries have proportionally more older people, who are more likely to die in a given year, so that the overall mortality rate can be higher even if the mortality rate at any given age is lower. A more complete picture of mortality is given by a [life table](wikipedia://en/1614128), which summarizes mortality separately at each age. A life table is necessary to give a good estimate of life expectancy.

## Basic equation regarding development of a population {#Basic_equation_regarding_development_of_a_population}

Suppose that a country (or other entity) contains *Population<sub>t</sub>* persons at time *t*. What is the size of the population at time *t* + 1?

$$\text{Population}_{t+1} = \text{Population}_t + \text{Natural Increase}_t + \text{Net Migration}_t$$

Natural increase from time *t* to *t* + 1:

$$\text{Natural Increase}_t = \text{Births}_t - \text{Deaths}_t$$

[Net migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_migration_rate) from time *t* to *t* + 1:

$$\text{Net Migration}_t = \text{Immigration}_t - \text{Emigration}_t$$

These basic equations can also be applied to subpopulations. For example, the population size of ethnic groups or nationalities within a given society or country is subject to the same sources of change. When dealing with ethnic groups, however, "net migration" might have to be subdivided into physical migration and ethnic reidentification ([assimilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_assimilation)). Individuals who change their ethnic self-labels or whose ethnic classification in government statistics changes over time may be thought of as migrating or moving from one population subcategory to another.[^o47]

More generally, while the basic demographic equation holds by definition, in practice, the recording and counting of events (births, deaths, immigration, [emigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emigration)) and the enumeration of the total population are subject to error. So allowance needs to be made for errors in the underlying statistics when accounting for population size or change.

The figure in this section shows the latest (2004) UN ([United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)) [WHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization) projections of [world population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population) out to the year 2150 (red = high, orange = medium, green = low). The UN "medium" projection shows world population reaching an approximate equilibrium of 9 billion by 2075. Working independently, demographers at the [International Institute for Applied Systems Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Institute_for_Applied_Systems_Analysis) in [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria) expect world population to peak at 9 billion by 2070.[^o48] Throughout the 21st century, the average age of the population is likely to continue to rise.

### Science of population {#Science_of_population}

Populations can change through three processes: fertility, mortality, and migration. Fertility refers to the number of children a woman has and should be contrasted with [fecundity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecundity) (a woman's childbearing potential).[^o50] Mortality is the study of the causes, consequences, and measurement of processes affecting death in members of the population. Demographers most commonly study mortality using the [life table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_Table), a statistical device that provides information about the mortality conditions (most notably the life expectancy) in the population.[^o51]

Migration refers to the movement of persons from a locality of origin to a destination place across a predefined political boundary. Migration researchers do not designate movements 'migrations' unless they are somewhat permanent. Thus, demographers do not consider tourists and travellers to be migrating. While demographers who study migration typically rely on census data on place of residence, indirect data sources, including tax forms and labour force surveys, are also important.[^o52]

Demography is widely taught today at many universities around the world, attracting students with initial training in the social sciences, statistics, or health studies. Being at the crossroads of several disciplines such as [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [epidemiology](wikipedia://en/66997), [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography), [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology) and [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History), demography offers tools to approach a large range of population issues by combining a more technical quantitative approach that represents the core of the discipline with many other methods borrowed from social or other sciences. Demographic research is conducted in universities, research institutes, statistical departments, and several international agencies. Population institutions are part of the [CICRED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_for_International_Cooperation_in_National_Research_in_Demography) (International Committee for Coordination of Demographic Research) network while most individual scientists engaged in demographic research are members of the [International Union for the Scientific Study of Population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_for_the_Scientific_Study_of_Population),[^o53] or a national association such as the [Population Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_Association_of_America) in the United States,[^o54] or affiliates of the Federation of Canadian Demographers in [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada).[^o55]

## Population composition {#Population_composition}

*Image: Population by broad age group projected to 2100, OWID.svg.* World demography by age composition from 1950 to 2100 (projected).[^o57]

**Population composition** is the description of a population defined by characteristics such as age, [race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Race_%28classification_of_humans%29),[^o58] sex, or [marital status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marital_status). These descriptions can be necessary for understanding the [social dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_dynamics) from historical and comparative research. These data are often compared using a [population pyramid](wikipedia://en/989011).

Population composition is also a very important part of historical research. Information ranging back hundreds of years is not always worthwhile because the number of people for whom data are available may not provide the information that is important (such as [population size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_size)). A lack of information about the original data-collection procedures may prevent an accurate evaluation of data quality.

## Population change {#Population_change}

*Image: US Race by Hispanic origin age demographics from 1990 to 2020.gif.* Change in ethnic population composition from 1990 to 2020 in the USA.[^o58]

Population change is analyzed by measuring the difference between two population sizes. [Global population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_population) continues to rise, which makes population change an essential component of demographics. This is calculated by subtracting the population size from an earlier [census](wikipedia://en/6889) from the current population size. The best way to measure population change is to use the intercensal percentage change. The intercensal percentage change is the absolute change in population between the censuses divided by the population size in the earlier census. Next, multiply this by a hundredfold to receive a [percentage](wikipedia://en/64493). When this statistic is achieved, the [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth) between two or more [nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation) of different sizes can be accurately measured and examined.[^o60][^o61] The population can change in terms of population composition.[^o58]

## Effects {#Effects}

The long-term sustainability of a population is described by the demographic [sustainability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sustainability).[^o63] The sustainability of contemporary [pay-as-you-go pensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pension#Pay-as-you-go) depends on sustainable demographics and [total fertility rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate).[^o64]

### Turnover and in internal labor markets {#Turnover_and_in_internal_labor_markets}

People decide to leave organizations for many reasons, such as better job opportunities, dissatisfaction, and family concerns. The causes of turnover can be split into two factors: one linked to the organization's culture, and the other to all other factors. People who do not fully accept a culture might leave voluntarily. Or, some individuals might leave because they fail to fit in or change within a particular organization. [^o66]

### Organizational ecology {#Organizational_ecology}

A basic definition of population ecology is the study of the distribution and abundance of organisms. In relation to organizations and demography, organizations face various [liabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_liability) that affect their continued survival. Hospitals, like all other large and complex organizations, are impacted by the environment in which they work. For example, a study examined the closure of acute care hospitals in Florida during a specific period. The study examined the effect size, age, and niche density of these particular hospitals. A population theory says that organizational outcomes are mostly determined by [environmental factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_factor). Among the theory's several factors, four apply to the hospital closure example: size, age, the density of niches in which organizations operate, and the density of niches in which organizations are established.

Problems in which demographers may be called upon to assist business organizations include determining the best prospective location for a branch store or service outlet, predicting demand for a new product, and analysing certain dynamics of a company's workforce. Choosing a new location for a branch of a bank, choosing the area in which to start a new supermarket, consulting a bank [loan officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loan_officer) that a particular location would be a beneficial site to start a car wash, and determining what shopping area would be best to buy and be redeveloped in metropolis area are types of problems in which demographers can be called upon. Standardization is a useful demographic technique for analysing a business. It can be used as an interpretive and analytical tool for comparing different markets.
