# Standard Model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Standard Model"}

The **Standard Model** of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics) is the [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_theory) describing three of the four known [fundamental forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_force) ([electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), [weak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction) and [strong interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction) – excluding [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity)) in the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) and classifying all known [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle). It was developed in stages throughout the latter half of the 20th century, through the work of many scientists worldwide,[^o2] with the current formulation being finalized in the mid-1970s upon [experimental confirmation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_confirmation) of the existence of [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark). Since then, proof of the [top quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_quark) (1995), the [tau neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_neutrino) (2000), and the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson) (2012) have added further credence to the Standard Model. In addition, the Standard Model has predicted with great accuracy the various properties of [weak neutral currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_neutral_current) and the [W and Z bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_and_Z_bosons).

:::

Although the Standard Model is believed to be theoretically self-consistentThere are mathematical issues regarding quantum field theories still under debate (see e.g. [Landau pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau_pole)), but the predictions extracted from the Standard Model by current methods applicable to current experiments are all self-consistent.[^o3] and has demonstrated some success in providing [experimental predictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_prediction), it leaves some [physical phenomena unexplained](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_beyond_the_Standard_Model) and so falls short of being a [complete theory of fundamental interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_everything).[^o4] For example, it does not fully explain [why there is more matter than anti-matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon_asymmetry), incorporate the full [theory of gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_gravitation)[^o5] as described by [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), or account for the [universe's accelerating expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerating_expansion_of_the_universe) as possibly described by [dark energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy). The model does not contain any viable [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter) particle that possesses all of the required properties deduced from observational [cosmology](wikipedia://en/5378). The Standard Model without modifications also does not incorporate [neutrino oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation) and their non-zero masses, but extensions have been proposed that can account for these features.

The development of the Standard Model was driven by [theoretical](wikipedia://en/19594028) and [experimental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_physics) particle physicists alike. The Standard Model is a paradigm of a [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) for theorists, exhibiting a wide range of phenomena, including [spontaneous symmetry breaking](wikipedia://en/297466), [anomalies](wikipedia://en/364731), and non-perturbative behavior. It is used as a basis for building more exotic models that incorporate [hypothetical particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothetical_particle), [extra dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra_dimensions), and elaborate symmetries (such as [supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/224636)) to explain experimental results at variance with the Standard Model, such as the existence of [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter) and [neutrino oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation).

## Historical background {#Historical_background}

In 1928, [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742) introduced the [Dirac equation](wikipedia://en/39407), which implied the existence of [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter).[^o7] In 1954, [Yang Chen-Ning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Chen-Ning) and [Robert Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mills_%28physicist%29) extended the concept of [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155) for [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), e.g. [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268), to [nonabelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonabelian_group) to provide an explanation for [strong interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction).[^o8] In 1957, [Chien-Shiung Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chien-Shiung_Wu) demonstrated [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29) was not conserved in the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction).[^o9] In 1961, [Sheldon Glashow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheldon_Glashow) combined the [electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) and weak interactions.[^o10] In 1964, [Murray Gell-Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Gell-Mann) and [George Zweig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Zweig) introduced [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark) and that same year [Oscar W. Greenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_W._Greenberg) implicitly introduced [color charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge) of quarks.[^o11] In 1967 [Steven Weinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Weinberg)[^o12] and [Abdus Salam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdus_Salam)[^o13] incorporated the [Higgs mechanism](wikipedia://en/644550)[^o14][^o15][^o16] into Glashow's [electroweak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_interaction), giving it its modern form.

In 1970, Sheldon Glashow, John Iliopoulos, and Luciano Maiani introduced the [GIM mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GIM_mechanism), predicting the [charm quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charm_quark).[^o17] In 1973 Gross, Wilczek and Politzer independently discovered that non-Abelian gauge theories, like the color theory of the strong force, have [asymptotic freedom](wikipedia://en/648008).[^o17] In 1971, [Gerard 't Hooft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_'t_Hooft) showed that [gauge theories](wikipedia://en/23912155), including [Yang–Mills](wikipedia://en/672202), could be [renormalized](wikipedia://en/291453).[^o18] In 1976, Martin Perl discovered the [tau lepton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_lepton) at the [SLAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLAC_National_Accelerator_Laboratory).[^o19][^o20] In 1977, a team led by Leon Lederman at Fermilab discovered the bottom quark.[^o21]

The Higgs mechanism is believed to give rise to the [masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of all the [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) in the Standard Model. This includes the masses of the [W and Z bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_and_Z_bosons), and the masses of the [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion), i.e. the [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark) and [leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton).

After the [neutral weak currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_current) caused by [Z boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subatomic_particle) exchange [were discovered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gargamelle) at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN) in 1973,[^o22][^o23][^o24][^o25] the electroweak theory became widely accepted and Glashow, Salam, and Weinberg shared the 1979 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) for discovering it. The W<sup>±</sup> and Z<sup>0</sup> [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) were discovered experimentally in 1983; and the ratio of their masses was found to be as the Standard Model predicted.[^o26]

The theory of the [strong interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction) (i.e. [quantum chromodynamics](wikipedia://en/25264), QCD), to which many contributed, acquired its modern form in 1973–74 when [asymptotic freedom](wikipedia://en/648008) was proposed[^o27][^o28] (a development that made QCD the main focus of theoretical research)[^o29] and experiments confirmed that the [hadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadron) were composed of fractionally charged quarks.[^o30][^o31]

The term "Standard Model" was introduced by [Abraham Pais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Pais) and [Sam Treiman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Treiman) in 1975,[^o32] with reference to the electroweak theory with four quarks.[^o33] [Steven Weinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Weinberg) has since claimed priority, explaining that he chose the term Standard [Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model) out of a sense of modesty[^o34][^o35][^o36] and used it in 1973 during a talk in Aix-en-Provence in France.[^o37]

## Particle content {#Particle_content}

The Standard Model includes members of several classes of elementary particles, which in turn can be distinguished by other characteristics, such as [color charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge).

All particles can be summarized as follows:

### Fermions {#Fermions}

The Standard Model includes 12 [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) of [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) $\tfrac{1}{2}$, known as [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion).[^o40] Fermions respect the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669), meaning that two [identical fermions](wikipedia://en/15352) cannot simultaneously occupy the same quantum state in the same atom.[^o41] Each fermion has a corresponding [antiparticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiparticle), which are particles that have corresponding properties with the exception of [opposite](wikipedia://en/228312) [charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_%28physics%29).[^o42] Fermions are classified based on how they interact, which is determined by the charges they carry, into two groups: [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark) and [leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton). Within each group, pairs of particles that exhibit similar physical behaviors are then grouped into [generations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_%28particle_physics%29) (see the table). Each member of a generation has a greater mass than the corresponding particle of generations prior. Thus, there are three generations of quarks and leptons.[^o43] As first-generation particles do not decay, they comprise all of ordinary ([baryonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon)) matter.[^o44] Specifically, all atoms consist of electrons orbiting around the [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus), ultimately constituted of up and down quarks. On the other hand, second- and third-generation charged particles decay with very short [half-lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life) and can only be observed in high-energy environments. Neutrinos of all generations also do not decay, and pervade the universe, but rarely interact with baryonic matter.

There are six quarks: [up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_quark), [down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down_quark), [charm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charm_quark), [strange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_quark), [top](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_quark), and [bottom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottom_quark).[^o40][^o43] Quarks carry [color charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge), and hence interact via the [strong interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction). The [color confinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_confinement) phenomenon results in quarks being strongly bound together such that they form color-neutral composite particles called [hadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadron); quarks cannot individually exist and must always bind with other quarks. Hadrons can contain either a quark-antiquark pair ([mesons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson)) or three quarks ([baryons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon)).[^o45] The lightest baryons are the [nucleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon): the [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) and [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron). Quarks also carry [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) and [weak isospin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_isospin), and thus interact with other fermions through [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) and [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction). The six leptons consist of the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), [electron neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_neutrino), [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon), [muon neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon_neutrino), [tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_%28particle%29), and [tau neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_neutrino). The leptons do not carry color charge, and do not respond to strong interaction. The charged leptons carry an [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) of −1 *[e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge)*, while the three [neutrinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino) carry zero electric charge. Thus, the neutrinos' motions are influenced by only the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction) and [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), making them difficult to observe.

### Gauge bosons {#Gauge_bosons}

*Image: Standard Model – All Feynman diagram vertices.svg.* Interactions in the Standard Model. All Feynman diagrams in the model are built from combinations of these vertices. *q* is any quark, *g* is a gluon, *X* is any charged particle, $\gamma$ is a photon, *f* is any fermion, *m* is any particle with mass (with the possible exception of the neutrinos), *m*<sub>B</sub> is any boson with mass. In diagrams with multiple particle labels separated by '/', one particle label is chosen. In diagrams with particle labels separated by '\|', the labels must be chosen in the same order. For example, in the four boson electroweak case the valid diagrams are WWWW, WWZZ, WWγγ, WWZγ. The conjugate of each listed vertex (reversing the direction of arrows) is also allowed.[^o47]

The Standard Model includes 4 kinds of [gauge bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_boson) of [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) 1,[^o40] with bosons being quantum particles containing an integer spin. The gauge bosons are defined as [force carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_carrier), as they are responsible for mediating the [fundamental interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction). The Standard Model explains the four fundamental forces as arising from the interactions, with fermions [exchanging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_forces_and_virtual-particle_exchange) [virtual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_particle) force carrier particles, thus mediating the forces. At a macroscopic scale, this manifests as a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force).[^o48] As a result, they do not follow the Pauli exclusion principle that constrains fermions; bosons do not have a theoretical limit on their [spatial density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_number_density). The types of gauge bosons are described below.

**[Electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism)**

[Photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) mediate the [electromagnetic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_force), responsible for interactions between electrically charged particles. The photon is massless and is described by the theory of [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) (QED).

**[Strong interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction)**

[Gluons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon) mediate the strong interactions, which binds quarks to each other by influencing the [color charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge), with the interactions being described in the theory of [quantum chromodynamics](wikipedia://en/25264) (QCD). They have no mass, and there are eight distinct gluons, with each being denoted through a color-anticolor charge combination (e.g. red–antigreen).Although nine color–anticolor combinations mathematically exist, gluons form color octet particles. As one color-symmetric combination is linear and forms a color singlet particles, there are eight possible gluons.[^o49] As gluons have an effective color charge, they can also interact amongst themselves.

**[Weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction)**

The [W boson+, W boson-, and Z boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_and_Z_bosons) gauge bosons mediate the weak interactions between all [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion), being responsible for [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay). They contain mass, with the Z boson having more mass than the W boson+-. The weak interactions involving the W boson+- act only on [*left-handed* particles and *right-handed* antiparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_%28physics%29) respectively. The W boson+- carries an electric charge of +1 and −1 and couples to the electromagnetic interaction. The electrically neutral Z boson boson interacts with both left-handed particles and right-handed antiparticles. These three gauge bosons along with the photons are grouped together, as collectively mediating the [electroweak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak) interaction.

**[Gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation)**

It is currently unexplained in the Standard Model, as the hypothetical mediating particle [graviton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton) has been proposed, but not observed.[^o50] This is due to the incompatibility of quantum mechanics and [Einstein's theory of general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), regarded as being the best explanation for gravity. In general relativity, gravity is explained as being the geometric curving of spacetime.[^o51]

The [Feynman diagram](wikipedia://en/11617) calculations, which are a graphical representation of the [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/297069) approximation, invoke "force mediating particles", and when applied to analyze [high-energy scattering experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator) are in reasonable agreement with the data. However, perturbation theory (and with it the concept of a "force-mediating particle") fails in other situations. These include low-energy quantum chromodynamics, [bound states](wikipedia://en/696912), and [solitons](wikipedia://en/51654). The interactions between all the particles described by the Standard Model are summarized by the diagrams on the right of this section.

### Higgs boson {#Higgs_boson}

The Higgs particle is a massive [scalar](wikipedia://en/5173456) elementary particle theorized by [Peter Higgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Higgs) ([and others](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_PRL_symmetry_breaking_papers)) in 1964, when he showed that Goldstone's 1962 theorem (generic continuous symmetry, which is spontaneously broken) provides a third polarization of a massive vector field. Hence, Goldstone's original scalar doublet, the massive spin-zero particle, was [proposed as the Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_PRL_symmetry_breaking_papers), and is a key building block in the Standard Model.[^o53] It has no intrinsic [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), and for that reason is classified as a [boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) with spin-0.[^o40]

The Higgs boson plays a unique role in the Standard Model, by explaining why the other elementary particles, except the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) and [gluon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon), are massive. In particular, the Higgs boson explains why the photon has no mass, while the [W and Z bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_and_Z_bosons) are very heavy. Elementary-particle masses and the differences between [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) (mediated by the photon) and the [weak force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_force) (mediated by the W and Z bosons) are critical to many aspects of the structure of microscopic (and hence macroscopic) matter. In [electroweak theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_interaction), the Higgs boson generates the masses of the leptons (electron, muon, and tau) and quarks. As the Higgs boson is massive, it must interact with itself.

Because the Higgs boson is a very massive particle and also decays almost immediately when created, only a very high-energy [particle accelerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator) can observe and record it. Experiments to confirm and determine the nature of the Higgs boson using the [Large Hadron Collider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Hadron_Collider) (LHC) at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN) began in early 2010 and were performed at [Fermilab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermilab)'s [Tevatron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevatron) until its closure in late 2011. Mathematical consistency of the Standard Model requires that any mechanism capable of generating the masses of elementary particles must become visible at energies above \1.4 TeV;[^o54] therefore, the LHC (designed to collide two 7 TeV proton beams) was built to answer the question of whether the Higgs boson actually exists.[^o55]

On 4 July 2012, two of the experiments at the LHC ([ATLAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATLAS_experiment) and [CMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Muon_Solenoid)) both reported independently that they had found a new particle with a mass of about 125 GeV/c2 (about 133 proton masses, on the order of × 10<sup>\-25</sup> kg), which is "consistent with the Higgs boson".[^o56][^o57] On 13 March 2013, it was confirmed to be the searched-for Higgs boson.[^o58][^o59]

Technically, [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) provides the mathematical framework for the Standard Model, in which a [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/40752010) controls the dynamics and kinematics of the theory. Each kind of particle is described in terms of a dynamical [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29) that pervades [space-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-time).[^o63] The construction of the Standard Model proceeds following the modern method of constructing most field theories: by first postulating a set of symmetries of the system, and then by writing down the most general [renormalizable](wikipedia://en/291453) Lagrangian from its particle (field) content that observes these symmetries.

The [global](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_symmetry) [Poincaré symmetry](wikipedia://en/230487) is postulated for all relativistic quantum field theories. It consists of the familiar [translational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry), [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) and the inertial reference frame invariance central to the theory of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962). The [local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_symmetry) SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1) [gauge symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_symmetry) is an [internal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_symmetries) that essentially defines the Standard Model. Roughly, the three factors of the gauge symmetry give rise to the three fundamental interactions. The fields fall into different [representations](wikipedia://en/292786) of the various symmetry groups of the Standard Model (see table). Upon writing the most general Lagrangian, one finds that the dynamics depends on 19 parameters, whose numerical values are established by experiment. The parameters are summarized in the table (made visible by clicking "show") above.

#### Quantum chromodynamics sector {#Quantum_chromodynamics_sector}

The quantum chromodynamics (QCD) sector defines the interactions between quarks and gluons, which is a [Yang–Mills gauge theory](wikipedia://en/672202) with SU(3) symmetry, generated by $T^a = \lambda^a/2$. Since leptons do not interact with gluons, they are not affected by this sector. The Dirac Lagrangian of the quarks coupled to the gluon fields is given by

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{QCD} = \overline{\psi} i\gamma^\mu D_{\mu} \psi - \frac{1}{4} G^a_{\mu\nu} G^{\mu\nu}_a,$$

where $\psi$ is a three component column vector of [Dirac spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_spinor), each element of which refers to a quark field with a specific [color charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge) (i.e. red, blue, and green) and summation over [flavor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavour_%28particle_physics%29) (i.e. up, down, strange, etc.) is implied.

The gauge covariant derivative of QCD is defined by $D_{\mu} \equiv \partial_\mu - i g_\text{s}\frac{1}{2}\lambda^a G_\mu^a$, where

- $\gamma\mu$ are the [Dirac matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_matrices),
- $G^{a}_{\mu}$ is the 8-component ($a = 1, 2, \dots, 8$) SU(3) gauge field,
- $\lambda^{a}$ are the 3 × 3 [Gell-Mann matrices](wikipedia://en/563694), generators of the SU(3) color group,
- $G^{a}_{\mu\nu}$ represents the [gluon field strength tensor](wikipedia://en/38184895), and
- $g_{s}$ is the strong coupling constant.

The QCD Lagrangian is invariant under local SU(3) gauge transformations; i.e., transformations of the form $\psi \rightarrow \psi' = U\psi$, where $U = e^{-i g_\text{s}\lambda^a \phi^{a}(x)}$ is 3 × 3 unitary matrix with determinant 1, making it a member of the group SU(3), and $\phi^{a}(x)$ is an arbitrary function of spacetime.

#### Electroweak sector {#Electroweak_sector}

The electroweak sector is a [Yang–Mills gauge theory](wikipedia://en/672202) with the symmetry group U(1) × SU(2)<sub>L</sub>,

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{EW} = \overline{Q}_{\text{L}j} i\gamma^\mu D_{\mu} Q_{\text{L}j} + \overline{u}_{\text{R}j} i\gamma^\mu D_{\mu} u_{\text{R}j} + \overline{d}_{\text{R}j} i\gamma^\mu D_{\mu} d_{\text{R}j} + \overline{\ell}_{\text{L}j} i\gamma^\mu D_{\mu} \ell_{\text{L}j} + \overline{e}_{\text{R}j} i\gamma^\mu D_{\mu} e_{\text{R}j} - \tfrac{1}{4} W_a^{\mu\nu} W_{\mu\nu}^a - \tfrac{1}{4} B^{\mu\nu} B_{\mu\nu},$$

where the subscript $j$ sums over the three generations of fermions; $Q_\text{L}, u_\text{R}$, and $d_\text{R}$ are the left-handed doublet, right-handed singlet up type, and right handed singlet down type quark fields; and $\ell_\text{L}$ and $e_\text{R}$ are the left-handed doublet and right-handed singlet lepton fields.

The electroweak [gauge covariant derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_covariant_derivative) is defined as $D_\mu \equiv \partial_\mu - ig' \tfrac12 Y_\text{W} B_\mu - ig \tfrac{1}{2} \vec\tau_\text{L} \vec W_\mu$, where

- $B_{\mu}$ is the U(1) gauge field,
- $Y_{W}$ is the [weak hypercharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_hypercharge) – the generator of the U(1) group,
- $\vec{W}_{\mu}$ is the 3-component SU(2) gauge field,
- $\operatorname{lh}=0.5_{L}$ are the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) – infinitesimal generators of the SU(2) group – with subscript L to indicate that they only act on *left*-chiral fermions,
- $g'$ and $g$ are the U(1) and SU(2) coupling constants respectively,
- $W^{a\mu\nu}$ ($a = 1, 2, 3$) and $B^{\mu\nu}$ are the [field strength tensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_strength_tensor) for the weak isospin and weak hypercharge fields.

Notice that the addition of fermion mass terms into the electroweak Lagrangian is forbidden, since terms of the form $m\overline\psi\psi$ do not respect U(1) × SU(2)<sub>L</sub> gauge invariance. Neither is it possible to add explicit mass terms for the U(1) and SU(2) gauge fields. The Higgs mechanism is responsible for the generation of the gauge boson masses, and the fermion masses result from Yukawa-type interactions with the Higgs field.

#### Higgs sector {#Higgs_sector}

In the Standard Model, the [Higgs field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_field) is an SU(2)${}_{L}$ doublet of complex [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_%28physics%29) fields with four degrees of freedom:

$$\varphi = \begin{pmatrix} \varphi^+ \\ \varphi^0 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} \varphi_1 + i\varphi_2 \\ \varphi_3 + i\varphi_4 \end{pmatrix},$$

where the superscripts + and 0 indicate the electric charge $Q$ of the components. The weak hypercharge $Y_\text{W}$ of both components is 1. Before symmetry breaking, the Higgs Lagrangian is

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{H} = \left(D_{\mu}\varphi\right)^{\dagger} \left(D^{\mu}\varphi \right) - V(\varphi),$$

where $D_{\mu}$ is the electroweak gauge covariant derivative defined above and $V(\varphi)$ is the potential of the Higgs field. The square of the covariant derivative leads to three and four point interactions between the electroweak gauge fields $W^{a}_{\mu}$ and $B_{\mu}$ and the scalar field $\varphi$. The scalar potential is given by

$$V(\varphi) = -\mu^2\varphi^{\dagger}\varphi + \lambda \left( \varphi^{\dagger}\varphi \right)^2,$$

where $\mu^2>0$, so that $\varphi$ acquires a non-zero [vacuum expectation value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_expectation_value), which generates masses for the Electroweak gauge fields (the Higgs mechanism), and $\lambda>0$, so that the potential is bounded from below. The quartic term describes self-interactions of the scalar field $\varphi$.

The minimum of the potential is degenerate with an infinite number of equivalent [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state) solutions, which occurs when $\varphi^{\dagger}\varphi = \tfrac{\mu^2}{2\lambda}$. It is possible to perform a [gauge transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_gauge) on $\varphi$ such that the ground state is transformed to a basis where $\varphi_1 = \varphi_2 = \varphi_4 = 0$ and $\varphi_3 = \tfrac{\mu}{\sqrt{\lambda}} \equiv v$. This breaks the symmetry of the ground state. The expectation value of $\varphi$ now becomes

$$\langle \varphi \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v \end{pmatrix},$$

where $v$ has units of mass and sets the scale of electroweak physics. This is the only dimensional parameter of the Standard Model and has a measured value of ~246 GeV/c2.

After symmetry breaking, the masses of the W and Z are given by $m_\text{W}=\frac{1}{2}gv$ and $m_\text{Z}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}v$, which can be viewed as predictions of the theory. The photon remains massless. The mass of the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson) is $m_\text{H}=\sqrt{2\mu^2}=\sqrt{2\lambda}v$. Since $\mu$ and $\lambda$ are free parameters, the Higgs's mass could not be predicted beforehand and had to be determined experimentally.

#### Yukawa sector {#Yukawa_sector}

The [Yukawa interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukawa_interaction) terms are:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{Yukawa} = (Y_\text{u})_{mn}(\bar{Q}_\text{L})_m \tilde{\varphi}(u_\text{R})_n + (Y_\text{d})_{mn}(\bar{Q}_\text{L})_m \varphi(d_\text{R})_n + (Y_\text{e})_{mn}(\bar{\ell}_\text{L})_m {\varphi}(e_\text{R})_n + \mathrm{h.c.}$$

where $Y_\text{u}$, $Y_\text{d}$, and $Y_\text{e}$ are $3\,\times\,3$ matrices of Yukawa couplings, with the $mn$ term giving the coupling of the generations $m$ and $n$, and h.c. means Hermitian conjugate of preceding terms. The fields $Q_\text{L}$ and $\ell_\text{L}$ are left-handed quark and lepton doublets. Likewise, $u_\text{R}, d_\text{R}$ and $e_\text{R}$ are right-handed up-type quark, down-type quark, and lepton singlets. Finally $\varphi$ is the Higgs doublet and $\tilde{\varphi} = i\tau_2\varphi^{*}$ is its charge conjugate state.

The Yukawa terms are invariant under the SU(2)${}_{L}$ × U(1)${}_{Y}$ gauge symmetry of the Standard Model and generate masses for all fermions after spontaneous symmetry breaking.

## Fundamental interactions {#Fundamental_interactions}

The Standard Model describes three of the four fundamental interactions in nature; only gravity remains unexplained. In the Standard Model, such an interaction is described as an exchange of [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) between the objects affected, such as a [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) for the electromagnetic force and a [gluon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon) for the strong interaction. Those particles are called [force carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_carrier) or messenger [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particles).[^o69]

### Gravity {#Gravity}

*Image: Fundamental Interactions.png.* Fundamental Interactions of the Standard Model altered to add the hypothetical graviton

Despite being perhaps the most familiar fundamental interaction, gravity is not described by the Standard Model, due to contradictions that arise when combining general relativity—the modern theory of gravity—and quantum mechanics.[^o73][^o74] However, gravity is so weak at microscopic scales, that it is essentially unmeasurable. The [graviton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton) is postulated to be the mediating particle, but has not yet been proved to exist.[^o75]

### Electromagnetism {#Electromagnetism}

Electromagnetism is the only long-range force in the Standard Model. It is mediated by photons and couples to electric charge.[^o69] Electromagnetism is responsible for a wide range of phenomena including [atomic electron shell structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_shell), [chemical bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bonds), [electric circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_circuit) and [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics). Electromagnetic interactions in the Standard Model are described by quantum electrodynamics.

### Weak interaction {#Weak_interaction}

The weak interaction is responsible for various forms of [particle decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_decay), such as [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay). It is weak and short-range, due to the fact that the weak mediating particles, W and Z bosons, have mass. W bosons have electric charge and mediate interactions that change the particle type (referred to as flavor) and charge. Interactions mediated by W bosons are [charged current interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_current_interaction). Z bosons are neutral and mediate neutral current interactions, which do not change particle flavor. Thus Z bosons are similar to the photon, aside from them being massive and interacting with the neutrino. The weak interaction is also the only interaction to violate [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_violation) and [CP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CP_violation). Parity violation is maximal for charged current interactions, since the W boson interacts exclusively with left-handed fermions and right-handed antifermions.

In the Standard Model, the weak force is understood in terms of the electroweak theory[^o78], which states that the weak and electromagnetic interactions become united into a single *electroweak* interaction at high energies.

### Strong interaction {#Strong_interaction}

The strong interaction is responsible for hadronic and [nuclear binding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_binding_energy). It is mediated by gluons, which couple to color charge. Since gluons themselves have color charge, the strong force exhibits [confinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_confinement) and [asymptotic freedom](wikipedia://en/648008). Confinement means that only color-neutral particles can exist in isolation, therefore quarks can only exist in hadrons and never in isolation, at low energies. Asymptotic freedom means that the strong force becomes weaker, as the energy scale increases. The strong force overpowers the [electrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic) repulsion of protons and quarks in nuclei and hadrons respectively, at their respective scales.

While quarks are bound in hadrons by the fundamental strong interaction, which is mediated by gluons, nucleons are bound by an emergent phenomenon termed the *residual strong force* or *[nuclear force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_force)*. This interaction is mediated by mesons, such as the [pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion). The color charges inside the nucleon cancel out, meaning most of the gluon and quark fields cancel out outside of the nucleon. However, some residue is "leaked", which appears as the exchange of virtual mesons, which result in an effective attractive force between nucleons. The (fundamental) strong interaction is described by quantum chromodynamics, which is a component of the Standard Model.

## Tests and predictions {#Tests_and_predictions}

The Standard Model predicted the existence of the [W and Z bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_and_Z_bosons), [gluon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon), [top quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_quark) and [charm quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charm_quark), and predicted many of their properties before these particles were observed. The predictions were experimentally confirmed with good precision.[^o81]

The Standard Model also predicted the existence of the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson), which was found in 2012 at the [Large Hadron Collider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Hadron_Collider), the final fundamental particle predicted by the Standard Model to be experimentally confirmed.[^o82]

## Challenges {#Challenges}

::: {#o84 .problem cue=unsolved}

What gives rise to the Standard Model of particle physics?

Why do particle masses and [coupling constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_constant) have the values that we measure?

Why are there three [generations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_%28particle_physics%29) of particles?

Why is there more matter than [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter) in the universe?

Where does [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter) fit into the model? Does it even consist of one or more new particles?

:::

Self-consistency of the Standard Model (currently formulated as a non-[abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) gauge theory quantized through path-integrals) has not been mathematically proved. While regularized versions useful for approximate computations (for example [lattice gauge theory](wikipedia://en/671804)) exist, it is not known whether they converge (in the sense of S-matrix elements) in the limit that the regulator is removed. A key question related to the consistency is the [Yang–Mills existence and mass gap](wikipedia://en/2393975) problem.

Experiments indicate that [neutrinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrinos) have [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass), which the classic Standard Model did not allow.[^o85] To accommodate this finding, the classic Standard Model can be modified to include neutrino mass, although it is not obvious exactly how this should be done.

If one insists on using only Standard Model particles, this can be achieved by adding a non-renormalizable interaction of leptons with the Higgs boson.[^o86] On a fundamental level, such an interaction emerges in the [seesaw mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seesaw_mechanism) where heavy right-handed neutrinos are added to the theory. This is natural in the [left-right symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-right_symmetry) extension of the Standard Model[^o87][^o88] and in certain [grand unified theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_unified_theory).[^o89] As long as new physics appears below or around 10<sup>14</sup> [GeV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronvolt), the neutrino masses can be of the right order of magnitude.

Theoretical and experimental research has attempted to extend the Standard Model into a [unified field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unified_field_theory) or a [theory of everything](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_everything), a complete theory explaining all physical phenomena including constants. Inadequacies of the Standard Model that motivate such research include:

- The model does not explain [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation), although physical confirmation of a theoretical particle known as a [graviton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton) would account for it to a degree. Though it addresses strong and electroweak interactions, the Standard Model does not consistently explain the canonical theory of gravitation, [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), in terms of [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267). The reason for this is, among other things, that quantum field theories of gravity generally break down before reaching the [Planck scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_scale). As a consequence, we have no reliable theory for the very early universe.
- Some physicists consider it to be *ad hoc* and inelegant, requiring 19 numerical constants whose values are unrelated and arbitrary.[^o90] Although the Standard Model, as it now stands, can explain why neutrinos have masses, the specifics of neutrino mass are still unclear. It is believed that explaining neutrino mass will require an additional 7 or 8 constants, which are also arbitrary parameters.[^o91]
- The Higgs mechanism gives rise to the [hierarchy problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchy_problem) if some new physics (coupled to the Higgs) is present at high energy scales. In these cases, in order for the weak scale to be much smaller than the [Planck scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_scale), severe fine tuning of the parameters is required; there are, however, other scenarios that include [quantum gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_safety_in_quantum_gravity) in which such fine tuning can be avoided.[^o92]
- The model is inconsistent with the emerging [Lambda-CDM model](wikipedia://en/985963) of cosmology. Contentions include the absence of an explanation in the Standard Model of particle physics for the observed amount of [cold dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_dark_matter) (CDM) and its contributions to [dark energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy), which are many orders of magnitude too large. It is also difficult to accommodate the observed predominance of matter over antimatter ([matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter)/[antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter) [asymmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon_asymmetry)). The [isotropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic) and [homogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneity_%28physics%29) of the visible universe over large distances seems to require a mechanism like cosmic [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_%28cosmology%29), which would also constitute an extension of the Standard Model.

Currently, no proposed [theory of everything](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_everything) has been widely accepted or verified.
