# Euler's identity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euler's identity"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Euler's identity** (also known as **Euler's equation**) is the [equality](wikipedia://en/90446)

$$e^{i \pi} + 1 = 0$$

where

:::

- $e$ is [Euler's number](wikipedia://en/9633), the base of [natural logarithms](wikipedia://en/21476),
- $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358), which by definition satisfies $i^2 = -1$, and
- $\pi$ is [pi](wikipedia://en/23601), the ratio of the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of a circle to its [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007).

Euler's identity is named after the Swiss mathematician [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902). It is a special case of [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613) $e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x$ when evaluated for $x = \pi$. Euler's identity is considered an exemplar of [mathematical beauty](wikipedia://en/18974136), as it shows a profound connection between the most fundamental numbers in mathematics.

## Mathematical beauty {#Mathematical_beauty}

Euler's identity is often cited as an example of deep [mathematical beauty](wikipedia://en/18974136).[^o5] Three of the basic [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) operations occur exactly once each: [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), and [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491). The identity also links five fundamental [mathematical constants](wikipedia://en/48779551):[^o6]

- The [number $0$](wikipedia://en/34513), the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225)
- The [number $1$](wikipedia://en/77591639), the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity)
- The [number $\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) ($\pi = 3.14159...$), the fundamental [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) constant
- The [number $e$](wikipedia://en/9633) ($e = 2.71828...$), also known as Euler's number, which occurs widely in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396)
- The [number $i$](wikipedia://en/52358), the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) such that $i^{2} = -1$

The equation is often given in the form of an expression set equal to zero, which is common practice in several areas of mathematics.

[Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) mathematics professor [Keith Devlin](wikipedia://en/574895) has said, "like a Shakespearean [sonnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonnet) that captures the very essence of love, or a painting that brings out the beauty of the human form that is far more than just skin deep, Euler's equation reaches down into the very depths of existence".[^o7] [Paul Nahin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nahin), a professor emeritus at the [University of New Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_Hampshire) who wrote a book dedicated to [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613) and its applications in [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659), said Euler's identity is "of exquisite beauty".[^o8]

Mathematics writer [Constance Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constance_Reid) has said that Euler's identity is "the most famous formula in all mathematics".[^o9] [Benjamin Peirce](wikipedia://en/364270), a 19th-century American philosopher, mathematician, and professor at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University), after proving Euler's identity during a lecture, said that it "is absolutely paradoxical; we cannot understand it, and we don't know what it means, but we have proved it, and therefore we know it must be the truth".[^o10]

A 1990 poll of readers by *[The Mathematical Intelligencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Intelligencer)* named Euler's identity the "most beautiful theorem in mathematics".[^o11] In a 2004 poll of readers by *[Physics World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_World)*, Euler's identity tied with [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) (of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism)) as the "greatest equation ever".[^o12]

At least three books in [popular mathematics](wikipedia://en/2069923) have been published about Euler's identity:

- *Dr. Euler's Fabulous Formula: Cures Many Mathematical Ills*, by [Paul Nahin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nahin) (2011)[^o13]
- *A Most Elegant Equation: Euler's formula and the beauty of mathematics*, by David Stipp (2017)[^o14]
- *Euler's Pioneering Equation: The most beautiful theorem in mathematics*, by [Robin Wilson](wikipedia://en/2715277) (2018)[^o15]

## Explanations {#Explanations}

### Imaginary exponents {#Imaginary_exponents}

*Image: ExpIPi.gif.* In this animation $N$ takes various increasing values from 1 to 100. The computation of $(1 + \tfrac{i\pi}{N})^{N}$ is displayed as the combined effect of $N$ repeated multiplications in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), with the final point being the actual value of $(1 + \tfrac{i\pi}{N})^{N}$. It can be seen that as $N$ gets larger $(1 + \tfrac{i\pi}{N})^{N}$ approaches a limit of −1.

Euler's identity asserts that $e^{i\pi}$ is equal to −1. The expression $e^{i\pi}$ is a special case of the expression $e^z$, where $z$ is any [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826). In general, $e^z$ is defined for complex $z$ by extending one of the [definitions of the exponential function](wikipedia://en/853175) from real exponents to complex exponents. For example, one common definition is:

$$e^z = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left(1+\frac z n \right)^n.$$

Euler's identity therefore states that the limit, as $n$ approaches infinity, of $(1 + \tfrac {i\pi}{n})^n$ is equal to −1. This limit is illustrated in the animation to the right.

*Image: Euler's formula.svg.* Euler's formula for a general angle

Euler's identity is a [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) of [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613), which states that for any [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x$,

$$e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x$$

where the inputs of the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/18717261) sine and cosine are given in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003).

In particular, when $x = \pi$,

$$e^{i \pi} = \cos \pi + i\sin \pi.$$

Since

$$\cos \pi = -1$$

and

$$\sin \pi = 0,$$

it follows that

$$e^{i \pi} = -1 + 0 i,$$

which yields Euler's identity:

$$e^{i \pi} +1 = 0.$$

### Geometric interpretation {#Geometric_interpretation}

Any complex number $z = x + iy$ can be represented by the point $(x, y)$ on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). This point can also be represented in [polar coordinates](wikipedia://en/5826#Polar_complex_plane) as $(r, \theta)$, where $r$ is the absolute value of $z$ (distance from the origin), and $\theta$ is the argument of $z$ (angle counterclockwise from the positive *x*-axis). By the definitions of sine and cosine, this point has cartesian coordinates of $(r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta)$, implying that $z = r(\cos \theta + i \sin \theta)$. According to Euler's formula, this is equivalent to saying $z = r e^{i\theta}$.

Euler's identity says that $-1 = e^{i\pi}$. Since $e^{i\pi}$ is $r e^{i\theta}$ for $r$ = 1 and $\theta = \pi$, this can be interpreted as a fact about the number −1 on the complex plane: its distance from the origin is 1, and its angle from the positive *x*-axis is $\pi$ radians.

Additionally, when any complex number $z$ is [multiplied](wikipedia://en/5826#Multiplication_and_division_in_polar_form) by $e^{i\theta}$, it has the effect of rotating $z$ counterclockwise by an angle of $\theta$ on the complex plane. Since multiplication by −1 reflects a point across the origin, Euler's identity can be interpreted as saying that rotating any point $\pi$ radians around the origin has the same effect as reflecting the point across the origin. Similarly, setting $\theta$ equal to $2\pi$ yields the related equation $e^{2\pi i} = 1$, which can be interpreted as saying that rotating any point by one [turn](wikipedia://en/855329) around the origin returns it to its original position.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Euler's identity is also a special case of the more general identity that the $n$th [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity), for $n > 1$, add up to 0:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{2 \pi i \frac{k}{n}} = 0 .$$

Euler's identity is the case where $n = 2$.

A similar identity also applies to [quaternion exponential](wikipedia://en/51440#Exponential,_logarithm,_and_power_functions): let $\{i, j, k\}$ be the basis [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440); then,

$$e^{\frac{1}{\sqrt 3}(i \pm j \pm k)\pi} + 1 = 0.$$

More generally, let $q$ be a quaternion with a zero real part and a norm equal to 1; that is, $q=ai+bj+ck,$ with $a^2+b^2+c^2=1.$ Then one has

$$e^{q\pi} + 1 = 0.$$

The same formula applies to [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436), with a zero real part and a norm equal to 1. These formulas are a direct generalization of Euler's identity, since $i$ and $-i$ are the only complex numbers with a zero real part and a norm (absolute value) equal to 1.

:::

## History {#History}

Euler's identity is a direct result of [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613), first published in his monumental 1748 work of mathematical analysis, *[Introductio in analysin infinitorum](wikipedia://en/19777721)*,[^o22] but it is questionable whether the particular concept of linking five fundamental constants in a compact form can be attributed to Euler himself, as he may never have expressed it.[^o23]
