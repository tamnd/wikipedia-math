# Subalgebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subalgebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **subalgebra** is a subset of an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788), closed under all its operations, and carrying the induced operations.

:::

"[Algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_%28disambiguation%29)", when referring to a structure, often means a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) or [module](wikipedia://en/276410) equipped with an additional [bilinear](wikipedia://en/4365) operation. Algebras in [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) are far more general: they are a common generalisation of *all* [algebraic structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_structures). "Subalgebra" can refer to either case.

## Subalgebras for algebras over a ring or field {#Subalgebras_for_algebras_over_a_ring_or_field}

*Image: Common\_associative\_algebras\_over\_the\_reals.svg.* Algebras are ordered by inclusion as in this [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) of associative algebras. There is a corresponding lattice of groups generated by basis elements, an example of a [Galois correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_correspondence).

A **subalgebra** of an [algebra over a commutative ring or field](wikipedia://en/191788) is a [vector subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace) which is closed under the multiplication of vectors. The restriction of the algebra multiplication makes it an algebra over the same ring or field. This notion also applies to most specializations, where the multiplication must satisfy additional properties, e.g. to [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112) or to [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944). Only for [unital algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) is there a stronger notion, of **unital subalgebra**, for which it is also required that the unit of the subalgebra be the unit of the bigger algebra.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

The 2×2-matrices over the reals **R**, with [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280), form a four-dimensional unital algebra M(2,**R**). The 2×2-matrices for which all entries are zero, except for the first one on the diagonal, form a subalgebra. It is also unital, but it is not a unital subalgebra.

The [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of M(2,**R**) is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) I, so the unital subalgebras contain the line of [diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_matrices) {*x* I : *x* in **R**}. For two-dimensional subalgebras, consider

$$E^2 = \begin{pmatrix}a & c \\ b & -a \end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}a^2+bc & 0 \\ 0 & bc+a^2 \end{pmatrix} = p I \ \ \text{where}\ \ p = a^2 + bc .$$

When *p* = 0, then E is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) and the subalgebra { *x* I + *y* E : *x, y* in **R** } is a copy of the [dual number](wikipedia://en/42169) plane. When *p* is negative, take *q* = 1/√−*p*, so that (*q* E)<sup>2</sup> = − I, and subalgebra { *x* I + *y* (*q*E) : *x,y* in **R** } is a copy of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). Finally, when *p* is positive, take *q* = 1/√*p*, so that (*q*E)<sup>2</sup> = I, and subalgebra { *x* I + *y* (*q*E) : *x,y* in **R** } is a copy of the plane of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559). By the [law of trichotomy](wikipedia://en/373956), these are the only planar subalgebras of M(2,**R**).

[L. E. Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._E._Dickson) noted in 1914, the "Equivalence of [complex quaternion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_quaternion) and complex matric algebras", meaning M(2,**C**), the 2x2 complex matrices.[^o5] But he notes also, "the real quaternion and real matric sub-algebras are not \[isomorphic\]." The difference is evident as there are the three [isomorphism classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class) of planar subalgebras of M(2,**R**), while real quaternions have only one isomorphism class of planar subalgebras as they are all isomorphic to **C**.

:::

## Subalgebras in universal algebra {#Subalgebras_in_universal_algebra}

In [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), a **subalgebra** of an [algebra](wikipedia://en/4055928) *A* is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) *S* of *A* that also has the structure of an algebra of the same type when the algebraic operations are restricted to *S*. If the axioms of a kind of [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) is described by [equational laws](wikipedia://en/1021753), as is typically the case in universal algebra, then the only thing that needs to be checked is that *S* is [*closed*](wikipedia://en/293802) under the operations.

Some authors consider algebras with [partial functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_functions). There are various ways of defining subalgebras for these. Another generalization of algebras is to allow relations. These more general algebras are usually called [structures](wikipedia://en/4055928), and they are studied in [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) and in [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392). For structures with relations there are notions of weak and of induced [substructures](wikipedia://en/2057747).

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

For example, the standard signature for [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) in universal algebra is (•, <sup>−1</sup>, 1). (Inversion and unit are needed to get the right notions of homomorphism and so that the group laws can be expressed as equations.) Therefore, a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of a group *G* is a subset *S* of *G* such that:

- the identity *e* of *G* belongs to *S* (so that *S* is closed under the identity constant operation);
- whenever *x* belongs to *S*, so does *x*<sup>−1</sup> (so that *S* is closed under the inverse operation);
- whenever *x* and *y* belong to *S*, so does *x* • *y* (so that *S* is closed under the group's multiplication operation).

:::
