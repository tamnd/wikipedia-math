# Isomorphism theorems

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isomorphism theorems"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **isomorphism theorems** (also known as **Noether's isomorphism theorems**) are [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977) that describe the relationship among [quotients](wikipedia://en/310953), [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564), and [subobjects](wikipedia://en/540476). Versions of the theorems exist for [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944), and other [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364). In [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), the isomorphism theorems can be generalized to the context of algebras and [congruences](wikipedia://en/48167).

:::

## History {#History}

The isomorphism theorems were formulated in some generality for homomorphisms of modules by [Emmy Noether](wikipedia://en/149896) in her paper *Abstrakter Aufbau der Idealtheorie in algebraischen Zahl- und Funktionenkörpern*, which was published in 1927 in [Mathematische Annalen](wikipedia://en/1423381). Three years later, [B. L. van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390) published *[Moderne Algebra](wikipedia://en/37252648)*, an influential early [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) textbook that helped standardize the structural treatment of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), and [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) in which these theorems appear prominently.[^n1][^n2]

## Groups {#Groups}

We first present the isomorphism theorems of the [groups](wikipedia://en/19447).

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem A (groups)"}

### Theorem A (groups) {#Theorem_A_(groups)}

*Image: First-isomorphism-theorem.svg.* Diagram of the fundamental theorem on homomorphisms

Let $G$ and $H$ be groups, and let $f : G \rightarrow H$ be a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396). Then:

1. The [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240#Group_homomorphisms) of $f$ is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $G$,
1. The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of $f$ is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $H$, and
1. The image of $f$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $G / \ker f$.

In particular, if $f$ is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) then $H$ is isomorphic to $G / \ker f$.

This theorem is usually called the *first isomorphism theorem*.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem B (groups)"}

### Theorem B (groups) {#Theorem_B_(groups)}

*Image: Diagram for the First Isomorphism Theorem.png.* Diagram for theorem B4. The two quotient groups (dotted) are isomorphic.

Let $G$ be a group. Let $S$ be a subgroup of $G$, and let $N$ be a normal subgroup of $G$. Then the following hold:

1. The [product](wikipedia://en/24734) $SN$ is a subgroup of $G$,
1. The subgroup $N$ is a normal subgroup of $SN$,
1. The [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) $S \cap N$ is a normal subgroup of $S$, and
1. The quotient groups $(SN)/N$ and $S/(S\cap N)$ are isomorphic.

Technically, it is not necessary for $N$ to be a normal subgroup, as long as $S$ is a subgroup of the [normalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer) of $N$ in $G$. In this case, $N$ is not a normal subgroup of $G$, but $N$ is still a normal subgroup of the product $SN$.

This theorem is sometimes called the *second isomorphism theorem*,[^o27] *diamond theorem*[^o10] or the *parallelogram theorem*.[^o11]

An application of the second isomorphism theorem identifies [projective linear groups](wikipedia://en/382667): for example, the group on the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line) starts with setting $G = \operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$, the group of [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) 2 × 2 [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), $S = \operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})$, the subgroup of [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) 1 matrices, and $N$ the normal subgroup of scalar matrices $\mathbb{C}^{\times}\!I = \left\{\left( \begin{smallmatrix} a & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{smallmatrix} \right) : a \in \mathbb{C}^{\times} \right\}$, we have $S \cap N = \{\pm I\}$, where $I$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), and $SN = \operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb{C})$. Then the second isomorphism theorem states that:

$$\operatorname{PGL}_2(\mathbb{C}) := \operatorname{GL}_2 \left(\mathbb{C})/(\mathbb{C}^{\times}\!I\right) \cong \operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{C})/\{\pm I\} =: \operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})$$

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem C (groups)"}

### Theorem C (groups) {#Theorem_C_(groups)}

Let $G$ be a group, and $N$ a normal subgroup of $G$. Then

1. If $K$ is a subgroup of $G$ such that $N \subseteq K \subseteq G$, then $G/N$ has a subgroup isomorphic to $K/N$.
1. Every subgroup of $G/N$ is of the form $K/N$ for some subgroup $K$ of $G$ such that $N \subseteq K \subseteq G$.
1. If $K$ is a normal subgroup of $G$ such that $N \subseteq K \subseteq G$, then $G/N$ has a normal subgroup isomorphic to $K/N$.
1. Every normal subgroup of $G/N$ is of the form $K/N$ for some normal subgroup $K$ of $G$ such that $N \subseteq K \subseteq G$.
1. If $K$ is a normal subgroup of $G$ such that $N \subseteq K \subseteq G$, then the quotient group $(G/N)/(K/N)$ is isomorphic to $G/K$.

The last statement is sometimes referred to as the *third isomorphism theorem*. The first four statements are often subsumed under Theorem D below, and referred to as the *lattice theorem*, *correspondence theorem*, or *fourth isomorphism theorem*.

:::

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem D (groups)"}

### Theorem D (groups) {#Theorem_D_(groups)}

Let $G$ be a group, and $N$ a normal subgroup of $G$. The canonical projection homomorphism $G\rightarrow G/N$ defines a bijective correspondence between the set of subgroups of $G$ containing $N$ and the set of (all) subgroups of $G/N$. Under this correspondence normal subgroups correspond to normal subgroups.

This theorem is sometimes called the [*correspondence theorem*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_theorem_%28group_theory%29), the *lattice theorem*, and the *fourth isomorphism theorem*.

The [Zassenhaus lemma](wikipedia://en/877209) (also known as the butterfly lemma) is sometimes called the fourth isomorphism theorem.[^o16]

:::

### Discussion {#Discussion}

The first isomorphism theorem can be expressed in [category theoretical](wikipedia://en/5869) language by saying that the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092) is (normal epi, mono)-factorizable; in other words, the [normal epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/142626) and the [monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) form a [factorization system](wikipedia://en/7149012) for the [category](wikipedia://en/53759). This is captured in the [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484) in the margin, which shows the [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) and [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) whose existence can be deduced from the morphism $f : G \rightarrow H$. The diagram shows that every morphism in the category of groups has a [kernel](wikipedia://en/142616) in the category theoretical sense; the arbitrary morphism *f* factors into $\iota \circ \pi$, where *ι* is a monomorphism and *π* is an epimorphism (in a [conormal category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conormal_category), all epimorphisms are normal). This is represented in the diagram by an object $\ker f$ and a monomorphism $\kappa: \ker f \rightarrow G$ (kernels are always monomorphisms), which complete the [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) running from the lower left to the upper right of the diagram. The use of the [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) convention saves us from having to draw the [zero morphisms](wikipedia://en/320638) from $\ker f$ to $H$ and $G / \ker f$.

If the sequence is right split (i.e., there is a morphism *σ* that maps $G / \operatorname{ker} f$ to a $\pi$-preimage of itself), then *G* is the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of the normal subgroup $\operatorname{im} \kappa$ and the subgroup $\operatorname{im} \sigma$. If it is left split (i.e., there exists some $\rho: G \rightarrow \operatorname{ker} f$ such that $\rho \circ \kappa = \operatorname{id}_{\text{ker} f}$), then it must also be right split, and $\operatorname{im} \kappa \times \operatorname{im} \sigma$ is a [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) decomposition of *G*. In general, the existence of a right split does not imply the existence of a left split; but in an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) (such as [that of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748)), left splits and right splits are equivalent by the [splitting lemma](wikipedia://en/138378), and a right split is sufficient to produce a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/106341) decomposition $\operatorname{im} \kappa \oplus \operatorname{im} \sigma$. In an abelian category, all monomorphisms are also normal, and the diagram may be extended by a second short exact sequence $0 \rightarrow G / \operatorname{ker} f \rightarrow H \rightarrow \operatorname{coker} f \rightarrow 0$.

In the second isomorphism theorem, the product *SN* is the [join](wikipedia://en/3956618) of *S* and *N* in the [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) of *G*, while the intersection *S* ∩ *N* is the [meet](wikipedia://en/3956618).

The third isomorphism theorem is generalized by the [nine lemma](wikipedia://en/454673) to [abelian categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_categories) and more general maps between objects.

### Note on numbers and names {#Note_on_numbers_and_names}

Below we present four theorems, labelled A, B, C and D. They are often numbered as "First isomorphism theorem", "Second..." and so on; however, there is no universal agreement on the numbering. Here we give some examples of the group isomorphism theorems in the literature. Notice that these theorems have analogs for rings and modules.

*Comparison of the names of the group isomorphism theorems*

::: {#o19 .table cue=table name="Comparison of the names of the group isomorphism theorems"}

| Comment | Author | Theorem A | Theorem B | Theorem C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No "third" theorem | Jacobson[^o20] | Fundamental theorem of homomorphisms | (*Second isomorphism theorem*) | "often called the first isomorphism theorem" |
|  | van der Waerden,[^o21] Durbin | Fundamental theorem of homomorphisms | First isomorphism theorem | Second isomorphism theorem |
|  | Knapp[^o22] | (*No name*) | Second isomorphism theorem | First isomorphism theorem |
|  | Grillet[^o23] | Homomorphism theorem | Second isomorphism theorem | First isomorphism theorem |
| Three numbered theorems | (*Other convention per Grillet*) | First isomorphism theorem | Third isomorphism theorem | Second isomorphism theorem |
|  | Rotman[^o24] | First isomorphism theorem | Second isomorphism theorem | Third isomorphism theorem |
|  | Fraleigh[^o25] | Fundamental homomorphism theorem or first isomorphism theorem | Second isomorphism theorem | Third isomorphism theorem |
|  | Dummit & Foote[^o26] | First isomorphism theorem | Second or Diamond isomorphism theorem | Third isomorphism theorem |
| No numbering | Milne[^o27] | Homomorphism theorem | Isomorphism theorem | Correspondence theorem |
|  | Scott[^o28] | Homomorphism theorem | Isomorphism theorem | Freshman theorem |

:::

It is less common to include the Theorem D, usually known as the *[lattice theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theorem)* or the *correspondence theorem*, as one of isomorphism theorems, but when included, it is the last one.

## Rings {#Rings}

The statements of the theorems for [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) are similar, with the notion of a normal subgroup replaced by the notion of a [two-sided ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_ideal). All statements hold if the rings involved are assumed to have a multiplicative identity, with homomorphisms preserving the identity, or if rings are not assumed to have an identity.

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem A (rings)"}

### Theorem A (rings) {#Theorem_A_(rings)}

Let $R$ and $S$ be rings, and let $\varphi:R\rightarrow S$ be a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411). Then:

1. The [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240#Ring_homomorphisms) of $\varphi$ is a two-sided ideal of $R$,
1. The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of $\varphi$ is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of $S$, and
1. The image of $\varphi$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) to the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $R/\ker\varphi$.

In particular, if $\varphi$ is surjective then $S$ is isomorphic to $R/\ker\varphi$.[^o32]

:::

::: {#o34 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem B (rings)"}

### Theorem B (rings) {#Theorem_B_(rings)}

Let $R$ be a ring. Let $S$ be a subring of $R$, and let $I$ be a two-sided ideal of $R$. Then:

1. The [sum](wikipedia://en/25977#Ideal_operations) $S+I=\{s+i\mid s\in S,i\in I\}$ is a subring of $R$,
1. The intersection $S\cap I$ is a two-sided ideal of $S$, and
1. The quotient rings $(S+I)/I$ and $S/(S\cap I)$ are isomorphic.

:::

::: {#o36 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem C (rings)"}

### Theorem C (rings) {#Theorem_C_(rings)}

Let $R$ be a ring, and $I$ a two-sided ideal of $R$. Then

1. If $A$ is a subring of $R$ such that $I \subseteq A \subseteq R$, then $A/I$ is a subring of $R/I$.
1. Every subring of $R/I$ is of the form $A/I$ for some subring $A$ of $R$ such that $I \subseteq A \subseteq R$.
1. If $J$ is a two-sided ideal of $R$ such that $I \subseteq J \subseteq R$, then $J/I$ is a two-sided ideal of $R/I$.
1. Every left, right, or two-sided ideal of $R/I$ is of the form $J/I$ for some left, right, or two-sided ideal $J$ of $R$ such that $I \subseteq J \subseteq R$.
1. If $J$ is a two-sided ideal of $R$ such that $I \subseteq J \subseteq R$, then the quotient ring $(R/I)/(J/I)$ is isomorphic to $R/J$.

:::

::: {#o38 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem D (rings)"}

### Theorem D (rings) {#Theorem_D_(rings)}

Let $I$ be a two-sided ideal of $R$. The correspondence $A\leftrightarrow A/I$ is an [inclusion](wikipedia://en/27631)-preserving [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the set of subrings $A$ of $R$ that contain $I$ and the set of subrings of $R/I$.[^o39]

:::

## Modules {#Modules}

The statements of the isomorphism theorems for [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) are particularly simple, since it is possible to form a [quotient module](wikipedia://en/364820) from any [submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule). The isomorphism theorems for [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) (modules over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603)) and [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) (modules over $\mathbb{Z}$) are special cases of these. For [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) vector spaces, all of these theorems follow from the [rank–nullity theorem](wikipedia://en/330310).

In the following, "module" will mean either "left *R*-module" or "right *R*-module" for some fixed ring *R*.

::: {#o42 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem A (modules)"}

### Theorem A (modules) {#Theorem_A_(modules)}

Let $M$ and $N$ be modules, and let $\varphi:M\rightarrow N$ be a [module homomorphism](wikipedia://en/474118). Then:

1. The [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of $\varphi$ is a submodule of $M$,
1. The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of $\varphi$ is a submodule of $N$, and
1. The image of $\varphi$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/474118#Terminology) to the [quotient module](wikipedia://en/364820) $M/\ker\varphi$.

In particular, if $\varphi$ is surjective then $N$ is isomorphic to $M/\ker\varphi$.

:::

::: {#o44 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem B (modules)"}

### Theorem B (modules) {#Theorem_B_(modules)}

Let $M$ be a module, and let $S$ and $T$ be submodules of $M$. Then:

1. The sum $S+T=\{s+t\mid s\in S,t\in T\}$ is a submodule of $M$,
1. The intersection $S\cap T$ is a submodule of $M$, and
1. The quotient modules $(S+T)/T$ and $S/(S\cap T)$ are isomorphic.

:::

::: {#o46 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem C (modules)"}

### Theorem C (modules) {#Theorem_C_(modules)}

Let *M* be a module, *T* a submodule of *M*.

1. If $S$ is a submodule of $M$ such that $T \subseteq S \subseteq M$, then $S/T$ is a submodule of $M/T$.
1. Every submodule of $M/T$ is of the form $S/T$ for some submodule $S$ of $M$ such that $T \subseteq S \subseteq M$.
1. If $S$ is a submodule of $M$ such that $T \subseteq S \subseteq M$, then the quotient module $(M/T)/(S/T)$ is isomorphic to $M/S$.

:::

::: {#o48 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem D (modules)"}

### Theorem D (modules) {#Theorem_D_(modules)}

Let $M$ be a module, $N$ a submodule of $M$. There is a bijection between the submodules of $M$ that contain $N$ and the submodules of $M/N$. The correspondence is given by $A\leftrightarrow A/N$ for all $A\supseteq N$. This correspondence commutes with the processes of taking sums and intersections (i.e., is a [lattice isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_isomorphism) between the lattice of submodules of $M/N$ and the lattice of submodules of $M$ that contain $N$).[^o49]

:::

## Universal algebra {#Universal_algebra}

To generalise this to [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), normal subgroups need to be replaced by [congruence relations](wikipedia://en/48167).

A **congruence** on an [algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) $A$ is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\Phi\subseteq A \times A$ that forms a subalgebra of $A \times A$ considered as an algebra with componentwise operations. One can make the set of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) $A/\Phi$ into an algebra of the same type by defining the operations via representatives; this will be [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) since $\Phi$ is a subalgebra of $A \times A$. The resulting structure is the [quotient algebra](wikipedia://en/310953).

::: {#o52 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem A (universal algebra)"}

### Theorem A (universal algebra) {#Theorem_A_(universal_algebra)}

Let $f:A \rightarrow B$ be an algebra [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564). Then the image of $f$ is a subalgebra of $B$, the relation given by $\Phi:f(x)=f(y)$ (i.e. the [kernel](wikipedia://en/330095) of $f$) is a congruence on $A$, and the algebras $A/\Phi$ and $\operatorname{im} f$ are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic). (Note that in the case of a group, $f(x)=f(y)$ [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) $f(xy^{-1}) = 1$, so one recovers the notion of kernel used in group theory in this case.)

:::

::: {#o54 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem B (universal algebra)"}

### Theorem B (universal algebra) {#Theorem_B_(universal_algebra)}

Given an algebra $A$, a subalgebra $B$ of $A$, and a congruence $\Phi$ on $A$, let $\Phi_B = \Phi \cap (B \times B)$ be the trace of $\Phi$ in $B$ and $[B]^\Phi=\{K \in A/\Phi: K \cap B \neq\emptyset\}$ the collection of equivalence classes that intersect $B$. Then

1. $\Phi_B$ is a congruence on $B$,
1. $\ [B]^\Phi$ is a subalgebra of $A/\Phi$, and
1. the algebra $[B]^\Phi$ is isomorphic to the algebra $B/\Phi_B$.

:::

::: {#o56 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem C (universal algebra)"}

### Theorem C (universal algebra) {#Theorem_C_(universal_algebra)}

Let $A$ be an algebra and $\Phi, \Psi$ two congruence relations on $A$ such that $\Psi \subseteq \Phi$. Then $\Phi/\Psi = \{ ([a']_\Psi,[a'']_\Psi): (a',a'')\in \Phi\} = [\ ]_\Psi \circ \Phi \circ [\ ]_\Psi^{-1}$ is a congruence on $A/\Psi$, and $A/\Phi$ is isomorphic to $(A/\Psi)/(\Phi/\Psi).$

:::

::: {#o58 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem D (universal algebra)"}

### Theorem D (universal algebra) {#Theorem_D_(universal_algebra)}

Let $A$ be an algebra and denote $\operatorname{Con}A$ the set of all congruences on $A$. The set $\operatorname{Con}A$ is a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) ordered by inclusion.[^o59] If $\Phi\in\operatorname{Con}A$ is a congruence and we denote by $\left[\Phi,A\times A\right]\subseteq\operatorname{Con}A$ the set of all congruences that contain $\Phi$ (i.e. $\left[\Phi,A\times A\right]$ is a principal [filter](wikipedia://en/19719) in $\operatorname{Con}A$, moreover it is a sublattice), then the map $\alpha:\left[\Phi,A\times A\right]\to\operatorname{Con}(A/\Phi),\Psi\mapsto\Psi/\Phi$ is a lattice isomorphism.[^o60][^o61]

:::
