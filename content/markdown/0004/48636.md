# Partition of unity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Partition of unity"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **partition of unity** on a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $R$ of [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) from $X$ to the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) \[0,1\] such that for every point $x\in X$:

:::

- there is a [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $x$ where all but a [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) number of the functions of $R$ are zero,[^o2] and
- the sum of all the function values at $x$ is 1, i.e., $\sum_{\rho\in R} \rho(x) = 1.$

*Image: Partition of unity illustration.svg.* A partition of unity on a circle with four functions. The circle is unrolled to a line segment (the bottom solid line) for graphing purposes. The dashed line on top is the sum of the functions in the partition.

Partitions of unity are useful because they often allow one to extend local constructions to the whole space. They are also important in the [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) of data, in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), and the theory of [spline functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spline_function).

## Existence {#Existence}

The existence of partitions of unity assumes two distinct forms:

1. Given any [open cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) $\{ U_i \}_{i \in I}$ of a space, there exists a partition of unity $\{ \rho_i \}_{i \in I}$ indexed *over the same set* $I$ such that [supp](wikipedia://en/381013) $\rho_i \subseteq U_i.$ Such a partition is said to be **subordinate to the open cover** $\{ U_i \}_i.$
1. Given any open cover $\{ U_i \}_{i \in I}$ of a [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) space, there exists a partition of unity $\{ \rho_j \}_{j \in J}$ indexed over a possibly distinct index set $J$ such that each $\rho_j$ has [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) and for each $j \in J$ there is an $i \in I$ with supp $\rho_j \subseteq U_i$.

Thus one chooses either to have the [supports](wikipedia://en/381013) indexed by the open cover, or compact supports. If the space is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), then there exist partitions satisfying both requirements.

A finite open cover always has a continuous partition of unity subordinate to it, provided the space is locally compact and Hausdorff.[^o4] [Paracompactness](wikipedia://en/48631) of the space is a necessary condition to guarantee the existence of a partition of unity [subordinate to any open cover](wikipedia://en/48631). Depending on the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) to which the space belongs, this may also be a sufficient condition.[^o5] In particular, a compact set in the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) admits a smooth partition of unity subordinate to any finite open cover. The construction uses [mollifiers](wikipedia://en/1596063) (bump functions), which exist in continuous and [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifolds), but not necessarily in [analytic manifolds](wikipedia://en/3183798). Thus for an open cover of an analytic manifold, an analytic partition of unity subordinate to that open cover generally does not exist. *See* [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842).

If $R$ and $T$ are partitions of unity for spaces $X$ and $Y$ respectively, then the set of all pairs $\{ \rho\otimes\tau :\ \rho \in R,\ \tau \in T \}$ is a partition of unity for the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) space $X \times Y$. The tensor product of functions act as $(\rho \otimes \tau )(x,y) = \rho(x)\tau(y).$

## Example {#Example}

Let $p$ and $q$ be antipodal points on the circle $S^1$. We can construct a partition of unity on $S^1$ by looking at a chart on the complement of the point $p \in S^1$ that sends $S^1 -\{p\}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ with center $q \in S^1$. Now let $\Phi$ be a [bump function](wikipedia://en/815799) on $\mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$\Phi(x) = \begin{cases} \exp\left(\frac{1}{x^2-1}\right) & x \in (-1,1) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

then, both this function and $1 - \Phi$ can be extended uniquely onto $S^1$ by setting $\Phi(p) = 0$. Then, the pair of functions $\{ (S^1 - \{p\}, \Phi), (S^1 - \{q\}, 1-\Phi) \}$ forms a partition of unity over $S^1$.

:::

## Variant definitions {#Variant_definitions}

Sometimes a less restrictive definition is used: the sum of all the function values at a particular point is only required to be positive, rather than 1, for each point in the space. However, given such a set of functions $\{ \psi_i \}_{i=1}^\infty$ one can obtain a partition of unity in the strict sense by dividing by the sum; the partition becomes $\{ \sigma^{-1}\psi_i \}_{i=1}^\infty$ where $\sigma(x) := \sum_{i=1}^\infty \psi_i(x)$, which is well defined since at each point only a finite number of terms are nonzero. Even further, some authors drop the requirement that the supports be locally finite, requiring only that $\sum_{i = 1}^\infty \psi_i(x) < \infty$ for all $x$.[^o9]

In the field of [operator algebras](wikipedia://en/455987), a partition of unity is composed of projections[^o10] $p_i=p_i^*=p_i^2$. In the case of [$\mathrm{C}^*$-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184), it can be shown that the entries are pairwise [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221):[^o11]

$$p_ip_j=\delta_{i,j}p_i\qquad (p_i,\,p_j\in R).$$

Note it is *not* the case that in a general [\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/293426) that the entries of a partition of unity are pairwise orthogonal.[^o12]

If $a$ is a [normal](wikipedia://en/21454688) element of a unital $\mathrm{C}^*$-algebra $A$, and has finite [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) $\sigma(a)=\{\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N\}$, then the projections in the [spectral decomposition](wikipedia://en/123495):

$$a=\sum_{i=1}^N\lambda_i\,P_i,$$

form a partition of unity.[^o13]

In the field of [compact quantum groups](wikipedia://en/3852696), the rows and columns of the fundamental representation $u\in M_N(C)$ of a quantum permutation group $(C,u)$ form partitions of unity.[^o14]

## Applications {#Applications}

A partition of unity can be used to define the integral (with respect to a [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811)) of a function defined over a manifold: one first defines the integral of a function whose support is contained in a single coordinate patch of the manifold; then one uses a partition of unity to define the integral of an arbitrary function; finally one shows that the definition is independent of the chosen partition of unity.

A partition of unity can be used to show the existence of a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) on an arbitrary manifold.

[Method of steepest descent](wikipedia://en/27082137#The_case_of_multiple_non-degenerate_saddle_points) employs a partition of unity to construct asymptotics of integrals.

[Linkwitz–Riley filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linkwitz–Riley_filter) is an example of practical implementation of partition of unity to separate input signal into two output signals containing only high- or low-frequency components.

The [Bernstein polynomials](wikipedia://en/340136) of a fixed degree *m* are a family of *m*+1 linearly independent single-variable polynomials that are a partition of unity for the unit interval $[0,1]$.

The weak [Hilbert Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215) asserts that if $f_1,\ldots, f_r\in \mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ are polynomials with no common vanishing points in $\mathbb{C}^n$, then there are polynomials $a_1, \ldots, a_r$ with $a_1f_1+\cdots+a_r f_r = 1$. That is, $\rho_i = a_i f_i$ form a polynomial partition of unity subordinate to the [Zariski-open](wikipedia://en/241744) cover $U_i = \{x\in \mathbb{C}^n \mid f_i(x)\neq 0\}$.

Partitions of unity are used to establish global smooth approximations for [Sobolev](wikipedia://en/611964) functions in bounded domains.[^o17]
