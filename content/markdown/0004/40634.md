# Convex hull

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Convex hull"}

*Image: Extreme points.svg.* The convex hull of the red set is the blue and red [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292).

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **convex hull**, **convex envelope** or **convex closure** of a shape is the smallest [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) that contains it. The convex hull may be defined either as the intersection of all convex sets containing a given subset of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), or equivalently as the set of all [convex combinations](wikipedia://en/794534) of points in the subset. For a [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) subset of the plane, the convex hull may be visualized as the shape enclosed by a rubber band stretched around the subset.

:::

Convex hulls of [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) are open, and convex hulls of [compact sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) are compact. Every compact convex set is the convex hull of its [extreme points](wikipedia://en/454968). The convex hull operator is an example of a [closure operator](wikipedia://en/483120), and every [antimatroid](wikipedia://en/667063) can be represented by applying this closure operator to finite sets of points. The [algorithmic](wikipedia://en/775) problems of finding the convex hull of a finite set of points in the plane or other low-dimensional Euclidean spaces, and its [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_duality) problem of intersecting [half-spaces](wikipedia://en/647297), are fundamental problems of [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927). They can be solved in time $O(n\log n)$ for two or three dimensional point sets, and in time matching the worst-case output complexity given by the [upper bound theorem](wikipedia://en/40964551) in higher dimensions.

As well as for finite point sets, convex hulls have also been studied for [simple polygons](wikipedia://en/1059530), [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436), [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve), and [epigraphs of functions](wikipedia://en/5744061). Convex hulls have wide applications in mathematics, statistics, combinatorial optimization, economics, geometric modeling, and ethology. Related structures include the [orthogonal convex hull](wikipedia://en/7847685), [convex layers](wikipedia://en/52621851), [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) and [Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/177668), and [convex skull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_skull).

## Definitions {#Definitions}

*Image: ConvexHull.svg.* Convex hull of a bounded planar set: rubber band analogy

A set of points in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is defined to be [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) if it contains the line segments connecting each pair of its points. The convex hull of a given set $X$ may be defined as[^n1]

1. The (unique) minimal convex set containing $X$
1. The intersection of all convex sets containing $X$
1. The set of all [convex combinations](wikipedia://en/794534) of points in $X$
1. The union of all [simplices](wikipedia://en/39781) with vertices in $X$

For [bounded sets](wikipedia://en/48258) in the Euclidean plane, not all on one line, the boundary of the convex hull is the [simple closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_closed_curve) with minimum [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) containing $X$. One may imagine stretching a [rubber band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_band) so that it surrounds the entire set $S$ and then releasing it, allowing it to contract; when it becomes taut, it encloses the convex hull of $S$.[^n2] This formulation does not immediately generalize to higher dimensions: for a finite set of points in three-dimensional space, a neighborhood of a [spanning tree](wikipedia://en/455770) of the points encloses them with arbitrarily small surface area, smaller than the surface area of the convex hull.[^o6] However, in higher dimensions, variants of the [obstacle problem](wikipedia://en/19487617) of finding a minimum-energy surface above a given shape can have the convex hull as their solution.[^n3]

For objects in three dimensions, the first definition states that the convex hull is the smallest possible convex [bounding volume](wikipedia://en/657106) of the objects. The definition using intersections of convex sets may be extended to [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610), and the definition using convex combinations may be extended from Euclidean spaces to arbitrary [real vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) or [affine spaces](wikipedia://en/298834); convex hulls may also be generalized in a more abstract way, to [oriented matroids](wikipedia://en/24338041).[^n4]

### Equivalence of definitions {#Equivalence_of_definitions}

*Image: 3D\_Convex\_Hull.tiff.* 3D convex hull of 120 point cloud

It is not obvious that the first definition makes sense: why should there exist a unique minimal convex set containing $X$, for every $X$? However, the second definition, the intersection of all convex sets containing $X$, is well-defined. It is a subset of every other convex set $Y$ that contains $X$, because $Y$ is included among the sets being intersected. Thus, it is exactly the unique minimal convex set containing $X$. Therefore, the first two definitions are equivalent.[^n1]

Each convex set containing $X$ must (by the assumption that it is convex) contain all convex combinations of points in $X$, so the set of all convex combinations is contained in the intersection of all convex sets containing $X$. Conversely, the set of all convex combinations is itself a convex set containing $X$, so it also contains the intersection of all convex sets containing $X$, and therefore the second and third definitions are equivalent.[^o11]

In fact, according to [Carathéodory's theorem](wikipedia://en/892014), if $X$ is a subset of a $d$-dimensional Euclidean space, every convex combination of finitely many points from $X$ is also a convex combination of at most $d+1$ points in $X$. The set of convex combinations of a $(d+1)$-tuple of points is a [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781); in the plane it is a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) and in three-dimensional space it is a tetrahedron. Therefore, every convex combination of points of $X$ belongs to a simplex whose vertices belong to $X$, and the third and fourth definitions are equivalent.[^o11]

### Upper and lower hulls {#Upper_and_lower_hulls}

In two dimensions, the convex hull is sometimes partitioned into two parts, the upper hull and the lower hull, stretching between the leftmost and rightmost points of the hull. More generally, for convex hulls in any dimension, one can partition the boundary of the hull into upward-facing points (points for which an upward ray is disjoint from the hull), downward-facing points, and extreme points. For three-dimensional hulls, the upward-facing and downward-facing parts of the boundary form topological disks.[^o13]

## Topological properties {#Topological_properties}

### Closed and open hulls {#Closed_and_open_hulls}

The *closed convex hull* of a set is the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of the convex hull, and the *open convex hull* is the [interior](wikipedia://en/55610) (or in some sources the [relative interior](wikipedia://en/8612907)) of the convex hull.[^n5]

The closed convex hull of $X$ is the intersection of all closed [half-spaces](wikipedia://en/647297) containing $X$. If the convex hull of $X$ is already a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) itself (as happens, for instance, if $X$ is a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) or more generally a [compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set)), then it equals the closed convex hull. However, an intersection of closed half-spaces is itself closed, so when a convex hull is not closed it cannot be represented in this way.[^n6]

If the open convex hull of a set $X$ is $d$-dimensional, then every point of the hull belongs to an open convex hull of at most $2d$ points of $X$. The sets of vertices of a square, regular octahedron, or higher-dimensional [cross-polytope](wikipedia://en/716401) provide examples where exactly $2d$ points are needed.[^o18]

### Preservation of topological properties {#Preservation_of_topological_properties}

*Image: Versiera007.svg.* The [witch of Agnesi](wikipedia://en/407764). The points on or above the red curve provide an example of a closed set whose convex hull is open (the open [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826)).

Topologically, the convex hull of an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) is always itself open, and (in Euclidean spaces) the convex hull of a compact set is always itself compact. However, there exist closed sets for which the convex hull is not closed.[^o20] For instance, the closed set

$$\left \{ (x,y) \mathop{\bigg|} y\ge \frac{1}{1+x^2}\right\}$$

(the set of points that lie on or above the [witch of Agnesi](wikipedia://en/407764)) has the open [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) as its convex hull.[^o21]

Convex hulls can be defined more generally in infinite-dimensional [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752), but they may not preserve compactness in these spaces. Instead, the compactness of convex hulls of compact sets, in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, is generalized by the [Krein–Smulian theorem](wikipedia://en/64247707), according to which the closed convex hull of a weakly compact subset of a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) (a subset that is compact under the [weak topology](wikipedia://en/33662)) is weakly compact.[^n7]

### Extreme points {#Extreme_points}

An [extreme point](wikipedia://en/454968) of a convex set is a point in the set that does not lie on any open line segment between any other two points of the same set. For a convex hull, every extreme point must be part of the given set, because otherwise it cannot be formed as a convex combination of given points. According to the [Krein–Milman theorem](wikipedia://en/3206654), every compact convex set in a Euclidean space (or more generally in a [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844)) is the convex hull of its extreme points.[^o24] However, this may not be true for convex sets that are not compact; for instance, the whole Euclidean plane and the open unit ball are both convex, but neither one has any extreme points. [Choquet theory](wikipedia://en/3655598) extends this theory from finite convex combinations of extreme points to infinite combinations (integrals) in more general spaces.[^n8]

## Geometric and algebraic properties {#Geometric_and_algebraic_properties}

### Closure operator {#Closure_operator}

The convex-hull operator has the characteristic properties of a [closure operator](wikipedia://en/483120):[^n9]

- It is *extensive*, meaning that the convex hull of every set $X$ is a superset of $X$.
- It is *[non-decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function#Monotonicity_in_order_theory)*, meaning that, for every two sets $X$ and $Y$ with $X\subseteq Y$, the convex hull of $X$ is a subset of the convex hull of $Y$.
- It is *[idempotent](wikipedia://en/14972)*, meaning that for every $X$, the convex hull of the convex hull of $X$ is the same as the convex hull of $X$.

When applied to a finite set of points, this is the closure operator of an [antimatroid](wikipedia://en/667063), the shelling antimatroid of the point set. Every antimatroid can be represented in this way by convex hulls of points in a Euclidean space of high-enough dimension.[^n10]

### Minkowski sum {#Minkowski_sum}

The operations of constructing the convex hull and taking the [Minkowski sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_sum) commute with each other, in the sense that the Minkowski sum of convex hulls of sets gives the same result as the convex hull of the Minkowski sum of the same sets. This provides a step towards the [Shapley–Folkman theorem](wikipedia://en/29237460) bounding the distance of a Minkowski sum from its convex hull.[^o31]

### Projective duality {#Projective_duality}

The [projective dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_dual) operation to constructing the convex hull of a set of points is constructing the intersection of a family of closed halfspaces that all contain the origin (or any other designated point).[^n11]

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

### Finite point sets {#Finite_point_sets}

*Image: Convex hull.png.* Convex hull of points in the plane

The convex hull of a finite point set $S \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ forms a [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019) when $d=2$, or more generally a [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) in $\mathbb{R}^d$. Each extreme point of the hull is called a [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417), and (by the Krein–Milman theorem) every convex polytope is the convex hull of its vertices. It is the unique convex polytope whose vertices belong to $S$ and that encloses all of $S$.[^n2] For sets of points in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736), the convex hull is a [simplicial polytope](wikipedia://en/8853373).[^n12]

According to the [upper bound theorem](wikipedia://en/40964551), the number of faces of the convex hull of $n$ points in $d$-dimensional Euclidean space is $O(n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor})$.[^n13] In particular, in two and three dimensions the number of faces is at most linear in $n$.[^n14]

### Simple polygons {#Simple_polygons}

*Image: Convex hull of a simple polygon.svg.* Convex hull (in blue and yellow) of a simple polygon (in blue)

The convex hull of a [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530) encloses the given polygon and is partitioned by it into regions, one of which is the polygon itself. The other regions, bounded by a [polygonal chain](wikipedia://en/12056032) of the polygon and a single convex hull edge, are called *pockets*. Computing the same decomposition recursively for each pocket forms a hierarchical description of a given polygon called its *convex differences tree*.[^n15] Reflecting a pocket across its convex hull edge expands the given simple polygon into a polygon with the same perimeter and larger area, and the [Erdős–Nagy theorem](wikipedia://en/20852937) states that this expansion process eventually terminates.[^n16]

### Brownian motion {#Brownian_motion}

The curve generated by [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) in the plane, at any fixed time, has probability 1 of having a convex hull whose boundary forms a [continuously differentiable curve](wikipedia://en/493403). However, for any angle $\theta$ in the range $\pi/2<\theta<\pi$, there will be times during the Brownian motion where the moving particle touches the boundary of the convex hull at a point of angle $\theta$. The [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) of this set of exceptional times is (with high probability) $1-\pi/2\theta$.[^n17]

### Space curves {#Space_curves}

*Image: Oloid structure.svg.* An [oloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oloid), the convex hull of two circles in 3d space

For the convex hull of a [space curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve) or finite set of space curves in general position in three-dimensional space, the parts of the boundary away from the curves are [developable](wikipedia://en/2878111) and [ruled surfaces](wikipedia://en/705158).[^n18] Examples include the [oloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oloid), the convex hull of two circles in perpendicular planes, each passing through the other's center,[^n19] the [sphericon](wikipedia://en/572813), the convex hull of two semicircles in perpendicular planes with a common center, and D-forms, the convex shapes obtained from [Alexandrov's uniqueness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandrov's_uniqueness_theorem) for a surface formed by gluing together two planar convex sets of equal perimeter.[^n20]

### Functions {#Functions}

The convex hull or [lower convex envelope](wikipedia://en/3165509) of a function $f$ on a real vector space is the function whose [epigraph](wikipedia://en/5744061) is the lower convex hull of the epigraph of $f$. It is the unique maximal [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568) majorized by $f$.[^n21] The definition can be extended to the convex hull of a set of functions (obtained from the convex hull of the union of their epigraphs, or equivalently from their [pointwise minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointwise_minimum)) and, in this form, is dual to the [convex conjugate](wikipedia://en/1001490) operation.[^n22]

## Computation {#Computation}

In [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), a number of algorithms are known for computing the convex hull for a finite set of points and for other geometric objects. Computing the convex hull means constructing an unambiguous, efficient [representation](wikipedia://en/8519) of the required convex shape. Output representations that have been considered for convex hulls of point sets include a list of [linear inequalities](wikipedia://en/19575563) describing the [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647) of the hull, an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) of facets and their adjacencies, or the full [face lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_lattice) of the hull.[^n23] In two dimensions, it may suffice more simply to list the points that are vertices, in their cyclic order around the hull.[^n2]

For convex hulls in two or three dimensions, the complexity of the corresponding algorithms is usually estimated in terms of $n$, the number of input points, and $h$, the number of points on the convex hull, which may be significantly smaller than $n$. For higher-dimensional hulls, the number of faces of other dimensions may also come into the analysis. [Graham scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_scan) can compute the convex hull of $n$ points in the plane in time $O(n\log n)$. For points in two and three dimensions, more complicated [output-sensitive algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Output-sensitive_algorithm) are known that compute the convex hull in time $O(n\log h)$. These include [Chan's algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan's_algorithm) and the [Kirkpatrick–Seidel algorithm](wikipedia://en/11699089).[^n24] For dimensions $d>3$, the time for computing the convex hull is $O(n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor})$, matching the worst-case output complexity of the problem.[^o58] The convex hull of a simple polygon in the plane can be constructed in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time).[^o59]

[Dynamic convex hull](wikipedia://en/11543042) data structures can be used to keep track of the convex hull of a set of points undergoing insertions and deletions of points,[^n25] and [kinetic convex hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_convex_hull) structures can keep track of the convex hull for points moving continuously.[^n26] The construction of convex hulls also serves as a tool, a building block for a number of other computational-geometric algorithms such as the [rotating calipers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotating_calipers) method for computing the [width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Width) and [diameter](wikipedia://en/78733484) of a point set.[^n27]

## Related structures {#Related_structures}

Several other shapes can be defined from a set of points in a similar way to the convex hull, as the minimal superset with some property, the intersection of all shapes containing the points from a given family of shapes, or the union of all combinations of points for a certain type of combination. For instance:

- The [affine hull](wikipedia://en/3416209) is the smallest affine subspace of a Euclidean space containing a given set, or the union of all affine combinations of points in the set.[^n28]
- The [linear hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_hull) is the smallest linear subspace of a vector space containing a given set, or the union of all linear combinations of points in the set.[^n28]
- The [conical hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conical_hull) or positive hull of a subset of a vector space is the set of all positive combinations of points in the subset.[^n28]
- The [visual hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_hull) of a three-dimensional object, with respect to a set of viewpoints, consists of the points $p$ such that every ray from a viewpoint through $p$ intersects the object. Equivalently it is the intersection of the (non-convex) cones generated by the outline of the object with respect to each viewpoint. It is used in [3D reconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_reconstruction) as the largest shape that could have the same outlines from the given viewpoints.[^n29]
- The circular hull or alpha-hull of a subset of the plane is the intersection of all disks with a given radius $1/\alpha$ that contain the subset.[^n30]
- The [relative convex hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_convex_hull) of a subset of a two-dimensional [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530) is the intersection of all relatively convex supersets, where a set within the same polygon is relatively convex if it contains the [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) between any two of its points.[^n31]
- The [orthogonal convex hull](wikipedia://en/7847685) or rectilinear convex hull is the intersection of all orthogonally convex and connected supersets, where a set is orthogonally convex if it contains all axis-parallel segments between pairs of its points.[^n32]
- The orthogonal convex hull is a special case of a much more general construction, the [hyperconvex hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperconvex_hull), which can be thought of as the smallest [injective metric space](wikipedia://en/7840768) containing the points of a given [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018).[^n33]
- The [holomorphically convex hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphically_convex_hull) is a generalization of similar concepts to [complex analytic manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analytic_manifold), obtained as an intersection of sublevel sets of [holomorphic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_functions) containing a given set.[^n34]

The [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) of a point set and its [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_%28mathematics%29), the [Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/177668), are mathematically related to convex hulls: the Delaunay triangulation of a point set in $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be viewed as the projection of a convex hull in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.$[^n35] The [alpha shapes](wikipedia://en/33029175) of a finite point set give a nested family of (non-convex) geometric objects describing the shape of a point set at different levels of detail. Each of alpha shape is the union of some of the features of the Delaunay triangulation, selected by comparing their [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) to the parameter alpha. The point set itself forms one endpoint of this family of shapes, and its convex hull forms the other endpoint.[^n30] The [convex layers](wikipedia://en/52621851) of a point set are a nested family of convex polygons, the outermost of which is the convex hull, with the inner layers constructed recursively from the points that are not vertices of the convex hull.[^n36]

The [convex skull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_skull) of a polygon is the largest convex polygon contained inside it. It can be found in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time), but the exponent of the algorithm is high.[^n37]

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: CIE1931xy\_gamut\_comparison.svg.* The convex hull of the primary colors in each [color space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_space) on a [CIE 1931](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIE_1931) xy [chromaticity diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromaticity_diagram) defines the space's [gamut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamut) of possible colors.

Convex hulls have wide applications in many fields. Within mathematics, convex hulls are used to study [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), matrix [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue), and [unitary elements](wikipedia://en/20402624), and several theorems in [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468) involve convex hulls. They are used in [robust statistics](wikipedia://en/2885691) as the outermost contour of [Tukey depth](wikipedia://en/34627868), are part of the [bagplot](wikipedia://en/40745990) visualization of two-dimensional data, and define risk sets of [randomized decision rules](wikipedia://en/54431035). Convex hulls of [indicator vectors](wikipedia://en/41904209) of solutions to combinatorial problems are central to [combinatorial optimization](wikipedia://en/420555) and [polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519). In economics, convex hulls can be used to apply methods of [convexity in economics](wikipedia://en/30643278) to non-convex markets. In geometric modeling, the convex hull property [Bézier curves](wikipedia://en/4664) helps find their crossings, and convex hulls are part of the measurement of boat hulls. And in the study of animal behavior, convex hulls are used in a standard definition of the [home range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_range).

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

[Newton polygons](wikipedia://en/742477) of univariate [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) and [Newton polytopes](wikipedia://en/31041066) of multivariate polynomials are convex hulls of points derived from the exponents of the terms in the polynomial, and can be used to analyze the [asymptotic](wikipedia://en/641995) behavior of the polynomial and the valuations of its roots.[^o80] Convex hulls and polynomials also come together in the [Gauss–Lucas theorem](wikipedia://en/9397319), according to which the [roots](wikipedia://en/264210) of the derivative of a polynomial all lie within the convex hull of the roots of the polynomial.[^n38]

*Image: Tverberg heptagon.svg.* Partition of seven points into three subsets with intersecting convex hulls, guaranteed to exist for any seven points in the plane by [Tverberg's theorem](wikipedia://en/3115869)

In [spectral analysis](wikipedia://en/506713), the [numerical range](wikipedia://en/15581094) of a [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366) is the convex hull of its [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue).[^n39] The [Russo–Dye theorem](wikipedia://en/20403479) describes the convex hulls of [unitary elements](wikipedia://en/20402624) in a [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184).[^n40] In [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468), both [Radon's theorem](wikipedia://en/1406385) and [Tverberg's theorem](wikipedia://en/3115869) concern the existence of partitions of point sets into subsets with intersecting convex hulls.[^n41]

The definitions of a convex set as containing line segments between its points, and of a convex hull as the intersection of all convex supersets, apply to [hyperbolic spaces](wikipedia://en/318742) as well as to Euclidean spaces. However, in hyperbolic space, it is also possible to consider the convex hulls of sets of [ideal points](wikipedia://en/4995922), points that do not belong to the hyperbolic space itself but lie on the boundary of a model of that space. The boundaries of convex hulls of ideal points of three-dimensional hyperbolic space are analogous to [ruled surfaces](wikipedia://en/705158) in Euclidean space, and their metric properties play an important role in the [geometrization conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642) in [low-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/621774).[^n42] Hyperbolic convex hulls have also been used as part of the calculation of [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) [triangulations](wikipedia://en/1440970) of [hyperbolic manifolds](wikipedia://en/1589135), and applied to determine the equivalence of [knots](wikipedia://en/366808).[^n43]

See also the section on Brownian motion for the application of convex hulls to this subject, and the section on space curves for their application to the theory of [developable surfaces](wikipedia://en/2878111).

### Statistics {#Statistics}

*Image: Bagplot.png.* A [bagplot](wikipedia://en/40745990). The outer shaded region is the convex hull, and the inner shaded region is the 50% Tukey depth contour.

In [robust statistics](wikipedia://en/2885691), the convex hull provides one of the key components of a [bagplot](wikipedia://en/40745990), a method for visualizing the spread of two-dimensional sample points. The contours of [Tukey depth](wikipedia://en/34627868) form a nested family of convex sets, with the convex hull outermost, and the bagplot also displays another polygon from this nested family, the contour of 50% depth.[^n44]

In statistical [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216), the risk set of a [randomized decision rule](wikipedia://en/54431035) is the convex hull of the risk points of its underlying deterministic decision rules.[^n45]

### Combinatorial optimization {#Combinatorial_optimization}

In [combinatorial optimization](wikipedia://en/420555) and [polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519), central objects of study are the convex hulls of [indicator vectors](wikipedia://en/41904209) of solutions to a combinatorial problem. If the facets of these polytopes can be found, describing the polytopes as intersections of halfspaces, then algorithms based on [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) can be used to find optimal solutions.[^o91] In [multi-objective optimization](wikipedia://en/10251864), a different type of convex hull is also used, the convex hull of the weight vectors of solutions. One can maximize any [quasiconvex combination](wikipedia://en/5781226) of weights by finding and checking each convex hull vertex, often more efficiently than checking all possible solutions.[^n46]

### Economics {#Economics}

In the [Arrow–Debreu model](wikipedia://en/1239556) of [general economic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_equilibrium), agents are assumed to have convex [budget sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budget_set) and [convex preferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_preferences). These assumptions of [convexity in economics](wikipedia://en/30643278) can be used to prove the existence of an equilibrium. When actual economic data is [non-convex](wikipedia://en/30618217), it can be made convex by taking convex hulls. The [Shapley–Folkman theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapley–Folkman_theorem) can be used to show that, for large markets, this approximation is accurate, and leads to a "quasi-equilibrium" for the original non-convex market.[^o94]

### Geometric modeling {#Geometric_modeling}

In [geometric modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_modeling), one of the key properties of a [Bézier curve](wikipedia://en/4664) is that it lies within the convex hull of its control points. This so-called "convex hull property" can be used, for instance, in quickly detecting intersections of these curves.[^n47]

In the geometry of boat and ship design, [chain girth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_girth) is a measurement of the size of a sailing vessel, defined using the convex hull of a cross-section of the [hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_%28watercraft%29) of the vessel. It differs from the [skin girth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_girth), the perimeter of the cross-section itself, except for boats and ships that have a convex hull.[^n48]

### Ethology {#Ethology}

The convex hull is commonly known as the minimum convex polygon in [ethology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethology), the study of animal behavior, where it is a classic, though perhaps simplistic, approach in estimating an animal's [home range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_range) based on points where the animal has been observed.[^o99] [Outliers](wikipedia://en/160951) can make the minimum convex polygon excessively large, which has motivated relaxed approaches that contain only a subset of the observations, for instance by choosing one of the convex layers that is close to a target percentage of the samples,[^n49] or in the [local convex hull](wikipedia://en/6385832) method by combining convex hulls of [neighborhoods](wikipedia://en/1775388) of points.[^n50]

### Quantum physics {#Quantum_physics}

In [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics), the [state space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space) of any quantum system — the set of all ways the system can be prepared — is a convex hull whose extreme points are [positive-semidefinite operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-semidefinite_matrix) known as pure states and whose interior points are called mixed states.[^n51] The [Schrödinger–HJW theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger–HJW_theorem) proves that any mixed state can in fact be written as a convex combination of pure states in multiple ways.[^n52]

### Thermodynamics {#Thermodynamics}

*Image: Mg–C convex hull.png.* Convex hull of [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium)–[carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) compounds.[^n53] Mg<sub>2</sub>C<sub>3</sub> is expected to be unstable as it lies above the lower hull.

A convex hull in [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) was identified by [Josiah Willard Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) (1873),[^n54] although the paper was published before the convex hull was so named. In a set of energies of several [stoichiometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometry) of a material, only those measurements on the lower convex hull will be stable. When removing a point from the hull and then calculating its distance to the hull, its distance to the new hull represents the degree of stability of the phase.[^o108]

## History {#History}

The lower convex hull of points in the plane appears, in the form of a Newton polygon, in a letter from [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) to [Henry Oldenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Oldenburg) in 1676.[^o110] The term "convex hull" itself appears as early as the work of (txt), and the corresponding term in [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) appears earlier, for instance in [Hans Rademacher](wikipedia://en/1864916)'s review of (txt). Other terms, such as "convex envelope", were also used in this time frame.[^o113] By 1938, according to [Lloyd Dines](wikipedia://en/47924017), the term "convex hull" had become standard; Dines adds that he finds the term unfortunate, because the colloquial meaning of the word "hull" would suggest that it refers to the surface of a shape, whereas the convex hull includes the interior and not just the surface.[^n55]
