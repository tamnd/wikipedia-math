# Union (set theory)

*Image: Venn0111.svg.* Union of two sets:$~A \cup B$

*Image: Venn 0111 1111.svg.* Union of three sets:$~A \cup B \cup C$

*Image: Example of a non pairwise disjoint family of sets.svg.* The union of A, B, C, D, and E is everything except the white area.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Union"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), the **union** (denoted by ∪) of a collection of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) is the set of all [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28set_theory%29) in the collection.[^o2] It is one of the fundamental operations through which sets can be combined and related to each other. A **nullary union** refers to a union of [zero ($0$)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) sets and it is by definition equal to the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566).

:::

For explanation of the symbols used in this article, refer to the [table of mathematical symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematical_symbols).

## Union of two sets {#Union_of_two_sets}

The union of two sets *A* and *B* is the set of elements which are in *A*, in *B*, or in both *A* and *B*.[^o4] In [set-builder notation](wikipedia://en/220089),

$A \cup B = \{ x: x \in A \text{ or } x \in B\}$.[^o5]

For example, if *A* = {1, 3, 5, 7} and *B* = {1, 2, 4, 6, 7} then *A* ∪ *B* = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7}. A more elaborate example (involving two infinite sets) is:

*A* = $\{x\text{ is }\text{ an }\text{ even } \operatorname{integer} \operatorname{greater} \operatorname{than} 1\}$

*B* = $\{x\text{ is }\text{ an }\text{ odd } \operatorname{integer} \operatorname{greater} \operatorname{than} 1\}$

$$A \cup B = \{2,3,4,5,6, \dots\}$$

As another example, the number 9 is *not* contained in the union of the set of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) $\{2, 3, 5, 7, 11, ...\}$ and the set of [even numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) $\{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, ...\}$, because 9 is neither prime nor even.

Sets cannot have duplicate elements,[^o5][^o6] so the union of the sets $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and $\{2, 3, 4\}$ is $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$.

### Finite unions {#Finite_unions}

One can take the union of several sets simultaneously. For example, the union of three sets *A*, *B*, and *C* contains all elements of *A*, all elements of *B*, and all elements of *C*, and nothing else. Thus, *x* is an element of *A* ∪ *B* ∪ *C* if and only if *x* is in at least one of *A*, *B*, and *C*.

A **finite union** is the union of a finite number of sets; the phrase does not imply that the union set is a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742).[^o8][^o9]

## Notation {#Notation}

The notation for the general concept can vary considerably. For a finite union of sets $S_1, S_2, S_3, \dots , S_n$ one often writes $S_1 \cup S_2 \cup S_3 \cup \dots \cup S_n$ or $\bigcup_{i=1}^n S_i$. Various common notations for arbitrary unions include $\bigcup \mathbf{M}$, $\bigcup_{A\in\mathbf{M}} A$, and $\bigcup_{i\in I} A_{i}$. The last of these notations refers to the union of the collection $\left\{A_i : i \in I\right\}$, where *I* is an [index set](wikipedia://en/393055) and $A_i$ is a set for every $i \in I$. In the case that the index set *I* is the set of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), one uses the notation $\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_{i}$, which is analogous to that of the [infinite sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sum) in series.[^o16]

When the symbol "∪" is placed before other symbols (instead of between them), it is usually rendered as a larger size.

### Notation encoding {#Notation_encoding}

In [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode), union is represented by the character 222A.[^o13] In [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX), $\cup$ is rendered from `\cup` and $\bigcup$ is rendered from `\bigcup`; In Typst, `union` renders $\cup$, where `union.big` renders $\bigcup$.

## Arbitrary union {#Arbitrary_union}

The most general notion is the union of an arbitrary collection of sets, sometimes called an *infinitary union*. If **M** is a set or [class](wikipedia://en/20696) whose elements are sets, then *x* is an element of the union of **M** [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there is [at least one](wikipedia://en/91420) element *A* of **M** such that *x* is an element of *A*.[^o16] In symbols:

$$x \in \bigcup \mathbf{M} \iff \exists A \in \mathbf{M},\ x \in A.$$

This idea subsumes the preceding sections—for example, *A* ∪ *B* ∪ *C* is the union of the collection $\{A, B, C\}$. Also, if **M** is the empty collection, then the union of **M** is the empty set.

### Formal derivation {#Formal_derivation}

In [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) (ZFC) and other set theories, the ability to take the arbitrary union of any sets is granted by the [axiom of union](wikipedia://en/52553), which states that, given any set of sets $A$, there exists a set $B$, whose elements are exactly those of the elements of $A$. Sometimes this axiom is less specific, where there exists a $B$ which contains the elements of the elements of $A$, but may be larger. For example if $A = \{ \{1\}, \{2\} \},$ then it may be that $B = \{ 1, 2, 3\}$ since $B$ contains 1 and 2. This can be fixed by using the [axiom of specification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_specification) to get the subset of $B$ whose elements are exactly those of the elements of $A$. Then one can use the [axiom of extensionality](wikipedia://en/52376) to show that this set is unique. For readability, define the binary [predicate](wikipedia://en/285109) $\operatorname{Union}(X,Y)$ meaning "$X$ is the union of $Y$" or "$X = \bigcup Y$" as:

$$\operatorname{Union}(X,Y) \iff \forall x (x \in X \iff \exists y \in Y ( x \in y))$$

Then, one can prove the statement "for all $Y$, there is a unique $X$, such that $X$ is the union of $Y$":

$$\forall Y \, \exists ! X (\operatorname{Union}(X,Y))$$

Then, one can use an [extension by definition](wikipedia://en/12219849) to add the union operator $\bigcup A$ to the [language of ZFC](wikipedia://en/152214#Formal_language) as:

$$\begin{aligned} B = \bigcup A & \iff \operatorname{Union}(B,A) \\ & \iff \forall x (x \in B \iff \exists y \in Y(x \in y)) \end{aligned}$$

or equivalently:

$$x \in \bigcup A \iff \exists y \in A \, (x \in y)$$

After the union operator has been defined, the binary union $A \cup B$ can be defined by showing there exists a unique set $C = \{A,B\}$ using the [axiom of pairing](wikipedia://en/52385), and defining $A \cup B = \bigcup \{A,B\}$. Then, finite unions can be defined inductively as:

$$\bigcup _ {i=1} ^ 0 A_i = \varnothing \text{, and } \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i = \left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1} A_i \right) \cup A_n$$

## Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

Binary union is an [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) operation; that is, for any sets $A, B, \text{ and } C$,

$$A \cup (B \cup C) = (A \cup B) \cup C.$$

Thus, the parentheses may be omitted without ambiguity: either of the above can be written as $A \cup B \cup C$. Also, union is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), so the sets can be written in any order.[^o20] The [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for the operation of union. That is, $A \cup \varnothing = A$, for any set $A$. Also, the union operation is idempotent: $A \cup A = A$. All these properties follow from analogous facts about [logical disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962).

Intersection distributes over union

$$A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B)\cup(A \cap C)$$

and union distributes over intersection[^o4]

$$A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C).$$

The [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of a set $U$, together with the operations given by union, [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429), and [complementation](wikipedia://en/54347), is a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959). In this Boolean algebra, union can be expressed in terms of intersection and complementation by the formula

$$A \cup B = ( A^\complement \cap B^\complement )^\complement,$$

where the superscript ${}^\complement$ denotes the complement in the [universal set](wikipedia://en/296838) $U$. Alternatively, intersection can be expressed in terms of union and complementation in a similar way: $A \cap B = ( A^\complement \cup B^\complement )^\complement$. These two expressions together are called [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669).[^o21][^o22][^o23]

:::

## History and etymology {#History_and_etymology}

The english word *union* comes from the term in [middle French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_French) meaning "coming together", which comes from the [post-classical Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-Classical_Latin_language) *unionem*, "oneness".[^o25] The original term for union in set theory was *Vereinigung* (in german), which was introduced in 1895 by [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216).[^o26] The english use of *union* of two sets in mathematics began to be used by at least 1912, used by [James Pierpont](wikipedia://en/2594993).[^o27][^o28] The symbol $\cup$ used for union in mathematics was introduced by [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) in his *[Arithmetices principia](wikipedia://en/51988929)* in 1889, along with the notations for intersection $\cap$, set membership $\in$, and subsets $\subset$.[^o29]
