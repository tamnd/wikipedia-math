# Unit interval

*Image: Unit-interval.svg.* The unit interval as a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unit interval"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **unit interval** is the [closed interval](wikipedia://en/49172) 0,1, that is, the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) that are greater than or equal to 0 and less than or equal to 1. It is often denoted *$I$* (capital letter I). In addition to its role in [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), the unit interval is used to study [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) in the field of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954).

:::

In the literature, the term "unit interval" is sometimes applied to the other shapes that an interval from 0 to 1 could take: 0,1, 0,1, and 0,1. However, the notation *$I$* is most commonly reserved for the closed interval 0,1.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The unit interval is a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832), [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to the [extended real number line](wikipedia://en/51698). As a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), it is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible), [path connected](wikipedia://en/87141) and [locally path connected](wikipedia://en/6959754). The [Hilbert cube](wikipedia://en/216389) is obtained by taking a [topological product](wikipedia://en/24910) of countably many copies of the unit interval.

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the unit interval is a [one-dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) analytical [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) whose boundary consists of the two points 0 and 1. Its standard [orientation](wikipedia://en/187446) goes from 0 to 1.

The unit interval is a [totally ordered set](wikipedia://en/30330) and a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) (every subset of the unit interval has a [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) and an [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum)).

### Cardinality {#Cardinality}

The *size* or *[cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174)* of a set is the number of elements it contains.

The unit interval is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$. However, it has the same size as the whole set: the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901). Since the real numbers can be used to represent points along an [infinitely long line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), this implies that a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) of length 1, which is a part of that line, has the same number of points as the whole line. Moreover, it has the same number of points as a square of [area](wikipedia://en/1209) 1, as a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) of [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) 1, and even as an unbounded *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ (see [Space filling curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_filling_curve)).

The number of elements (either real numbers or points) in all the above-mentioned sets is [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248), as it is strictly greater than the number of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474).

### Orientation {#Orientation}

The unit interval is a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246). The open interval (0,1) is a subset of the [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) and inherits an orientation from them. The [orientation](wikipedia://en/3633706) is reversed when the interval is entered from 1, such as in the integral $\int_1^x \frac{dt}{t}$ used to define [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) for *x* in the interval, thus yielding negative values for logarithm of such *x*. In fact, this integral is evaluated as a [signed area](wikipedia://en/39433938) yielding *negative area* over the unit interval due to reversed orientation there.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The interval -1,1, with length two, demarcated by the positive and negative units, occurs frequently, such as in the [range](wikipedia://en/275991) of the [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) sine and cosine and the [hyperbolic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) tanh. This interval may be used for the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of [inverse functions](wikipedia://en/14907). For instance, when is restricted to −π/2, π/2 then $\sin\theta$ is in this interval and arcsine is defined there.

Sometimes, the term "unit interval" is used to refer to objects that play a role in various branches of mathematics analogous to the role that 0,1 plays in homotopy theory. For example, in the theory of [quivers](wikipedia://en/355100), the (analogue of the) unit interval is the graph whose vertex set is $\{0,1\}$ and which contains a single edge *e* whose source is 0 and whose target is 1. One can then define a notion of [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) between quiver [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) analogous to the notion of homotopy between [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) maps.

:::

## Fuzzy logic {#Fuzzy_logic}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), the unit interval 0,1 can be interpreted as a generalization of the [Boolean domain](wikipedia://en/3954077) {0,1}, in which case rather than only taking values 0 or 1, any value between and including 0 and 1 can be assumed. Algebraically, [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) (NOT) is replaced with $1 - x$; [conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) (AND) is replaced with multiplication ($xy$); and [disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) (OR) is defined, per [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669), as $1 - (1 - x)(1 - y)$.

Interpreting these values as logical [truth values](wikipedia://en/161711) yields a [multi-valued logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-valued_logic), which forms the basis for [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180) and [probabilistic logic](wikipedia://en/5017608). In these interpretations, a value is interpreted as the "degree" of truth – to what extent a proposition is true, or the probability that the proposition is true.
