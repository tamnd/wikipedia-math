# Ecliptic coordinate system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ecliptic coordinate system"}

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), the **ecliptic coordinate system** is a [celestial coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system) commonly used for representing the [apparent positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_place), [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit), and [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poles_of_astronomical_bodies) orientations[^o26] of [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) objects. Because most [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) (except [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29)) and many [small Solar System bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_Solar_System_body) have orbits with only slight [inclinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) to the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264), using it as the [fundamental plane](wikipedia://en/8081066) is convenient. The system's [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) can be the center of either the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) or [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), its primary direction is towards the [March](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox) [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox_%28celestial_coordinates%29), and it has a [right-hand convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule). It may be implemented in [spherical](wikipedia://en/29181) or [rectangular coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706).[^o2]

## Primary direction {#Primary_direction}

*Image: Ecliptic vs equator small.gif.* The apparent motion of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) along the ecliptic (red) as seen on the inside of the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239). Ecliptic coordinates appear in (red). The [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) (blue) and the [equatorial coordinates](wikipedia://en/48384) (blue), being inclined to the ecliptic, appear to wobble as the Sun advances.

The [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) and the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) are slowly moving relative to the distant stars due to [perturbing forces](wikipedia://en/1490148) on the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), therefore the [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) of the primary direction, their intersection at the [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox), is not quite fixed. A slow motion of Earth's axis, [precession](wikipedia://en/72576), causes a slow, continuous turning of the coordinate system westward about the poles of the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264), completing one circuit in about 26,000 years. Superimposed on this is a smaller motion of the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264), and a small oscillation of the Earth's axis, [nutation](wikipedia://en/52297430).[^o5][^o6]

In order to reference a coordinate system which can be considered as fixed in space, these motions require specification of the [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) of a particular date, known as an [epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29), when giving a position in ecliptic coordinates. The three most commonly used are:

**Mean equinox of a standard epoch**

(usually the [J2000.0 epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29), but may include B1950.0, B1900.0, etc.) is a fixed standard direction, allowing positions established at various dates to be compared directly.

**Mean equinox of date**

is the intersection of the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) of "date" (that is, the ecliptic in its position at that "date") with the *mean* equator (that is, the equator rotated by [precession](wikipedia://en/72576) to its position at "date", but free from the small periodic oscillations of [nutation](wikipedia://en/52297430)). Commonly used in planetary [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) calculation.

**True equinox of date**

is the intersection of the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) of "date" with the *true* equator (that is, the mean equator plus [nutation](wikipedia://en/52297430)). This is the actual intersection of the two planes at any particular moment, with all motions accounted for.

A position in the ecliptic coordinate system is thus typically specified *true equinox and ecliptic of date*, *mean equinox and ecliptic of J2000.0*, or similar. Note that there is no "mean ecliptic", as the ecliptic is not subject to small periodic oscillations.[^o7]

**Ecliptic longitude**

*Ecliptic longitude* or *celestial longitude* (symbols: heliocentric $\ell$, geocentric $\lambda$) measures the angular distance of an object along the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) from the primary direction. Like [right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073) in the [equatorial coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48384), the primary direction (0° ecliptic longitude) points from the Earth towards the Sun at the [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox). Because it is a right-handed system, ecliptic longitude is measured positive eastwards in the fundamental plane (the ecliptic) from 0°–360° . Because of [axial precession](wikipedia://en/72576), the ecliptic longitude of most "fixed stars" (referred to the equinox of date) increases by about 50.3 [arcseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcsecond) per year, or 83.8 [arcminutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcminute) per century, the speed of general precession.[^o13][^o14] However, for stars near the ecliptic poles, the rate of change of ecliptic longitude is dominated by the slight movement of the ecliptic (that is, of the plane of the Earth's orbit), so the rate of change may be anything from minus infinity to plus infinity depending on the exact position of the star.

**Ecliptic latitude**

*Ecliptic latitude* or *celestial latitude* (symbols: heliocentric $b$, geocentric $\beta$), measures the angular distance of an object from the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) towards the north (positive) or south (negative) [ecliptic pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_pole). For example, the [north ecliptic pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_pole) has a celestial latitude of +90°. Ecliptic latitude for "fixed stars" is not affected by precession.

**Distance**

*Distance* is also necessary for a complete spherical position (symbols: heliocentric $r$, geocentric $\Delta$). Different distance units are used for different objects. Within the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), [astronomical units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit) (au) are used, and for objects near the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), [Earth radii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius), [Lunar distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance), or thousands of [kilometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilometer) are used.

### Historical use {#Historical_use}

From antiquity through the 18th century, ecliptic longitude was commonly measured using twelve [zodiacal signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological_sign), each of 30° longitude, a practice that continues in modern [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology). The signs are approximately the positions on the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264), relative to the position of the Sun on the [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox), where [constellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation) were on the ecliptic in the year 200 ce ; however the stars of those [constellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation) have [precessed](wikipedia://en/24714#Astronomy) eastward and the stars are now shifted by about one-and-a-half 30° signs from the locations of their nominal zodiacal signs. Longitudes were specified in signs, degrees, minutes, and seconds.

For an example of historical use, a longitude of ♌ 19°5558 is 19.933° east of the start of the sign [Leo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_%28astrology%29) (♌). Since Leo begins 120° from the [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox), the longitude in modern form is 139° 55 58.[^o16]

In China, ecliptic longitude is measured using 24 [Solar terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_term), each of 15° longitude, and are used by [Chinese lunisolar calendars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_calendar) to stay synchronized with the seasons, which is crucial for agrarian societies.

## Rectangular coordinates {#Rectangular_coordinates}

*Image: Heliocentric rectangular ecliptic.png.* [Heliocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentrism) ecliptic coordinates. The [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) is the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun)'s center, the [plane of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_of_reference) is the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) plane, and the primary direction (the $x$-axis) is the March [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox_%28celestial_coordinates%29). A [right-handed rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-handed_rule) specifies a $y$-axis 90° to the east on the fundamental plane. The $z$-axis points toward the north [ecliptic pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_pole). The reference frame is relatively stationary, aligned with the March equinox.

A [rectangular variant](wikipedia://en/7706) of ecliptic coordinates is often used in [orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) calculations and simulations. It has its [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) at the center of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) (or at the [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter) of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System)), its [fundamental plane](wikipedia://en/8081066) on the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) plane, and the $x$-axis toward the March [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox_%28celestial_coordinates%29). The coordinates have a [right-handed convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-handed_rule), that is, if one extends their right thumb upward, it simulates the $z$-axis, their extended index finger the $x$-axis, and the curl of the other fingers points generally in the direction of the $y$-axis.[^o18]

These rectangular coordinates are related to the corresponding spherical coordinates by

$$\begin{aligned} x &= r \cos b \cos \ell \\ y &= r \cos b \sin \ell \\ z &= r \sin b \end{aligned}$$

## Conversion between celestial coordinate systems {#Conversion_between_celestial_coordinate_systems}

### Converting Cartesian vectors {#Converting_Cartesian_vectors}

#### Conversion from ecliptic coordinates to equatorial coordinates {#Conversion_from_ecliptic_coordinates_to_equatorial_coordinates}

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_\text{equatorial} \\ y_\text{equatorial} \\ z_\text{equatorial} \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos \varepsilon & -\sin \varepsilon \\ 0 & \sin \varepsilon & \cos \varepsilon \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_\text{ecliptic} \\ y_\text{ecliptic} \\ z_\text{ecliptic} \\ \end{bmatrix}$$

[^o22]

#### Conversion from equatorial coordinates to ecliptic coordinates {#Conversion_from_equatorial_coordinates_to_ecliptic_coordinates}

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_\text{ecliptic} \\ y_\text{ecliptic} \\ z_\text{ecliptic} \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos \varepsilon & \sin \varepsilon \\ 0 & -\sin \varepsilon & \cos \varepsilon \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_\text{equatorial} \\ y_\text{equatorial} \\ z_\text{equatorial} \\ \end{bmatrix}$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the [obliquity of the ecliptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obliquity_of_the_ecliptic).
