# Arity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arity"}

Adicity In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), **arity** () is the number of [arguments](wikipedia://en/32842052) or [operands](wikipedia://en/22656) taken by a [function](wikipedia://en/185427), [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) or [relation](wikipedia://en/19509). In mathematics, arity may also be called rank,[^o2][^o3] but this word can have many other meanings. In logic and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), arity may also be called **adicity** and **degree**.[^o4][^o5] In [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), it is usually named [valency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valency_%28linguistics%29).[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In general, functions or operators with a given arity follow the naming conventions of *n*-based [numeral systems](wikipedia://en/21170), such as [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) and [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263). A [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) prefix is combined with the -ary suffix. For example:

- A nullary function takes no arguments.
  - Example: $f()=2$
- A [unary function](wikipedia://en/161006) takes one argument.
  - Example: $f(x)=2x$
- A [binary function](wikipedia://en/3948) takes two arguments.
  - Example: $f(x,y)=2xy$
- A [ternary function](wikipedia://en/459413) takes three arguments.
  - Example: $f(x,y,z)=2xyz$
- An *n*-ary function takes *n* arguments.
  - Example: $f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)=2\prod_{i=1}^n x_i$

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="0-ary function"}

### Nullary {#Nullary}

A [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132) can be treated as the output of an operation of arity 0, called a *nullary operation*.

Also, outside of [functional programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming), a function without arguments can be meaningful and not necessarily constant (due to [side effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side_effect_%28computer_science%29)). Such functions may have some *hidden input*, such as [global variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_variable) or the whole state of the system (time, free memory, etc.).

:::

### Unary {#Unary}

Examples of [unary operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unary_operator) in mathematics and in programming include the [unary minus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unary_minus) and plus, the increment and decrement operators in [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29)-style languages (not in logical languages), and the [successor](wikipedia://en/325637), [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606), [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940), [floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function), [ceiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceiling_function), [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532), [sign](wikipedia://en/505449), [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991), [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) (the principal square root), [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) (unary of "one" [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826), that however has two parts at a lower level of abstraction), and [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) functions in mathematics. In programming the [two's complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two's_complement), [address reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_%28computer_science%29), and the [logical NOT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_NOT) operators are examples of unary operators.

All functions in [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203) and in some [functional programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming_language) (especially those descended from [ML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ML_%28programming_language%29)) are technically unary, but see n-ary below.

According to [Quine](wikipedia://en/33833), the Latin distributives being *singuli*, *bini*, *terni*, and so forth, the term "singulary" is the correct adjective, rather than "unary".[^o12] [Abraham Robinson](wikipedia://en/46024) follows Quine's usage.[^o13]

In philosophy, the adjective *monadic* is sometimes used to describe a [one-place relation](wikipedia://en/8015680) such as 'is square-shaped' as opposed to a [two-place relation](wikipedia://en/3931) such as 'is the sister of'.

### Binary {#Binary}

Most operators encountered in programming and mathematics are of the [binary](wikipedia://en/3948) form. For both programming and mathematics, these include the [multiplication operator](wikipedia://en/976642), the radix operator, the often omitted [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) operator, the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) operator, the [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) operator, and the [division](wikipedia://en/53696) operator. Logical predicates such as *[OR](wikipedia://en/7962)*, *[XOR](wikipedia://en/105979)*, *[AND](wikipedia://en/18152)*, *IMP* are typically used as binary operators with two distinct operands. In [CISC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_instruction_set_computing) architectures, it is common to have two source operands (and store result in one of them).

### Ternary {#Ternary}

Most modern [CPUs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_processing_unit) provide a ternary [fused multiply–add](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_multiply–add) (FMA) instruction for floating-point numbers, which multiplies two numbers and adds the result to a third number, only rounding once at the end. This avoids the inaccuracy resulting from rounding the multiplication and addition operations separately.

The computer programming language [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29) and its various descendants (including [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++), [C#](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_Sharp_%28programming_language%29), [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29), [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29), [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl), and others) provide the [ternary conditional operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternary_conditional_operator) `?:`. The first operand (the condition) is evaluated, and if it is true, the result of the entire expression is the value of the second operand, otherwise it is the value of the third operand. This operator has a lazy or 'shortcut' [evaluation strategy](wikipedia://en/2977119) that does not evaluate whichever of the second and third arguments is not used. Some functional programming languages, such as [Agda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agda_%28programming_language%29), have such an evaluation strategy for all functions and consequently implement if...then...else as an ordinary function; several others, such as [Haskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskell), can do this but for syntactic, performance or historical reasons choose to define keywords instead.

The [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) language has a ternary conditional expression, x if C else y. In [Elixir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elixir_%28programming_language%29) the equivalent would be if(C, do: x, else: y).

The [Forth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forth_%28programming_language%29) language also contains a ternary operator, `*/`, which multiplies the first two (one-cell) numbers, dividing by the third, with the intermediate result being a double cell number. This is used when the intermediate result would overflow a single cell.

The Unix [dc calculator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dc_%28computer_program%29) has several ternary operators, such as `|`, which will pop three values from the stack and efficiently compute $x^y \bmod z$ with [arbitrary precision](wikipedia://en/600892).

Many ([RISC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_instruction_set_computing)) [assembly language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assembly_language) instructions are ternary (as opposed to only two operands specified in CISC); or higher, such as

```asm
MOV %AX, (%BX, %CX)
```

, which will load (MOV) into register AX the contents of a calculated memory location that is the sum (parenthesis) of the registers BX and CX.

### *n*-ary {#n-ary}

The [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of *n* real numbers is an *n*-ary function: $\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\left (\sum_{i=1}^n{x_i}\right) = \frac{x_1+x_2+\dots+x_n}{n}$

Similarly, the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of *n* [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) is an *n*-ary function: $\left(\prod_{i=1}^n a_i\right)^\frac{1}{n} = \ \sqrt[n]{a_1 a_2 \cdots a_n} .$ Note that a [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of the geometric mean is the arithmetic mean of the logarithms of its *n* arguments

From a mathematical point of view, a function of *n* arguments can always be considered as a function of a single argument that is an element of some [product space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space). However, it may be convenient for notation to consider *n*-ary functions, as for example [multilinear maps](wikipedia://en/221525) (which are not linear maps on the product space, if *n* ≠ 1).

The same is true for programming languages, where functions taking several arguments could always be defined as functions taking a single argument of some [composite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_composition) such as a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729), or in languages with [higher-order functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_function), by [currying](wikipedia://en/6600).

### Varying arity {#Varying_arity}

In computer science, a function that accepts a variable number of arguments is called *[variadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variadic_function)*. In logic and philosophy, predicates or relations accepting a variable number of arguments are called *[multigrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multigrade_predicate)*, anadic, or variably polyadic.[^o18]

## Terminology {#Terminology}

[Latinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) names are commonly used for specific arities, primarily based on Latin [distributive numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_number) meaning "in group of *n*", though some are based on Latin [cardinal numbers](wikipedia://en/6173) or [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932). For example, 1-ary is based on cardinal *unus*, rather than from distributive *singulī* that would result in *singulary*.

*n*-*ary* means having *n* operands (or parameters), but is often used as a synonym of "polyadic".

These words are often used to describe anything related to that number (e.g., undenary chess is a [chess variant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_variant) with an 11×11 board, or the [Millenary Petition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millenary_Petition) of 1603).

The arity of a [relation](wikipedia://en/19509) (or [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29)) is the dimension of the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) in the corresponding [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095). (An operation of arity *n* thus has arity *n*+1 considered as a relation.) "Since an n-ary operation is a special case of an (n+1)-ary relation, we see that algebras may be regarded as a special case of relational structures."[^o22]

In [computer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming), there is often a [syntactical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax_%28programming_languages%29) distinction between [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28programming%29) and [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_%28computer_science%29); syntactical operators usually have arity 1, 2, or 3 (the [ternary operator](wikipedia://en/459413) ?: is also common). Functions vary widely in the number of arguments, though large numbers can become unwieldy. Some programming languages also offer support for [variadic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variadic_functions), i.e., functions syntactically accepting a variable number of arguments.
