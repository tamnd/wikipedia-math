# Alternating group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alternating group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **alternating group** is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of [even permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_permutation) of a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742). The alternating group on a set of $n$ elements is called the **alternating group of degree $n$**, or the **alternating group on $n$ letters** and denoted by $A_{n}$ or $\operatorname{Alt}(n).$

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

For *n* > 1, the group A<sub>*n*</sub> is the [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) S<sub>*n*</sub> with [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 2 and has therefore [*n*!](wikipedia://en/10606)/2 elements. It is the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the signature [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) sgn : S<sub>*n*</sub> → $\{1, -1\}$ explained under [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901).

The group A<sub>*n*</sub> is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *n* ≤ 3 and [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) if and only if *n* = 3 or *n* ≥ 5. A<sub>5</sub> is the smallest non-abelian [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600), having [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) 60, and thus the smallest non-[solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012).

The group A<sub>4</sub> has the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) V as a proper [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918), namely the identity and the double transpositions $\{(), (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}$, that is the kernel of the [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection) of A<sub>4</sub> onto A<sub>3</sub> ≅ Z<sub>3</sub>. We have the [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) V → A<sub>4</sub> → A<sub>3</sub> = Z<sub>3</sub>. In [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), this map, or rather the corresponding map S<sub>4</sub> → S<sub>3</sub>, corresponds to associating the [Lagrange resolvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_resolvent) cubic to a quartic, which allows the [quartic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_polynomial) to be solved by radicals, as established by [Lodovico Ferrari](wikipedia://en/18720).

:::

## Conjugacy classes {#Conjugacy_classes}

As in the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901), any two elements of A<sub>*n*</sub> that are conjugate by an element of A<sub>*n*</sub> must have the same [cycle shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_shape). The converse is not necessarily true, however. If the cycle shape consists only of cycles of odd length with no two cycles the same length, where cycles of length one are included in the cycle type, then there are exactly two conjugacy classes for this cycle shape (Scott 1987, §11.1, p299).

Examples:

- The two [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) (123) and (132) are not conjugates in A<sub>3</sub>, although they have the same cycle shape, and are therefore conjugate in S<sub>3</sub>.
- The permutation (123)(45678) is not conjugate to its inverse (132)(48765) in A<sub>8</sub>, although the two permutations have the same cycle shape, so they are conjugate in S<sub>8</sub>.

## Relation with symmetric group {#Relation_with_symmetric_group}

*See [Symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901#Relation_with_alternating_group)*.

As finite symmetric groups are the groups of all permutations of a set with finite elements, and the alternating groups are groups of even permutations, alternating groups are subgroups of finite symmetric groups. For n>4, along with the group itself, and the trivial subgroup, the alternating group is the only other [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) a symmetric group has.

## Generators and relations {#Generators_and_relations}

For *n* ≥ 3, A<sub>*n*</sub> is generated by 3-cycles, since 3-cycles can be obtained by combining pairs of transpositions. This generating set is often used to prove that A<sub>*n*</sub> is simple for *n* ≥ 5.

## Automorphism group {#Automorphism_group}

::: {#o9 .table cue=table}

| *n* | Aut(A<sub>*n*</sub>) | Out(A<sub>*n*</sub>) |
|---|---|---|
| *n* ≥ 4, *n* ≠ 6 | S<sub>*n*</sub> | Z<sub>2</sub> |
| *n* = 1, 2 | Z<sub>1</sub> | Z<sub>1</sub> |
| *n* = 3 | Z<sub>2</sub> | Z<sub>2</sub> |
| *n* = 6 | S<sub>6</sub> ⋊ Z<sub>2</sub> | V = Z<sub>2</sub> × Z<sub>2</sub> |

:::

For *n* > 3, except for *n* = 6, the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of A<sub>*n*</sub> is the symmetric group S<sub>*n*</sub>, with [inner automorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_automorphism_group) A<sub>*n*</sub> and [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) Z<sub>2</sub>; the outer automorphism comes from conjugation by an odd permutation.

For *n* = 1 and 2, the automorphism group is trivial. For *n* = 3 the automorphism group is Z<sub>2</sub>, with trivial inner automorphism group and outer automorphism group Z<sub>2</sub>.

The outer automorphism group of A<sub>6</sub> is [the Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) V = Z<sub>2</sub> × Z<sub>2</sub>, and is related to [the outer automorphism of S<sub>6</sub>](wikipedia://en/28901#Automorphism_group). The extra outer automorphism in A<sub>6</sub> swaps the 3-cycles (like (123)) with elements of shape 3<sup>2</sup> (like (123)(456)).

## Exceptional isomorphisms {#Exceptional_isomorphisms}

There are some [exceptional isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/25262819) between some of the small alternating groups and small [groups of Lie type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groups_of_Lie_type), particularly [projective special linear groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group). These are:

- A<sub>4</sub> is isomorphic to PSL<sub>2</sub>(3)[^o11] and the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of chiral [tetrahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2904472).
- A<sub>5</sub> is isomorphic to PSL<sub>2</sub>(4), PSL<sub>2</sub>(5), and the symmetry group of chiral [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844). (See[^o11] for an indirect isomorphism of PSL<sub>2</sub>(F<sub>5</sub>) → A<sub>5</sub> using a classification of simple groups of order 60, and [here](wikipedia://en/382667#Action_on_p_points) for a direct proof).
- A<sub>6</sub> is isomorphic to PSL<sub>2</sub>(9) and PSp<sub>4</sub>(2)'.
- A<sub>8</sub> is isomorphic to PSL<sub>4</sub>(2).

More obviously, A<sub>3</sub> is isomorphic to the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) Z<sub>3</sub>, and A<sub>0</sub>, A<sub>1</sub>, and A<sub>2</sub> are isomorphic to the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) (which is also SL<sub>1</sub>(*q*) = PSL<sub>1</sub>(*q*) for any *q*).

### A<sub>5</sub> as a subgroup of 3-space rotations {#A5_as_a_subgroup_of_3-space_rotations}

*Image: A5\_in\_SO(3).gif.* A<sub>5</sub> \< SO<sub>3</sub>(**R**) gray yellow purple green red

*Image: Compound of five tetrahedra.png.* Compound of five tetrahedra. A<sub>5</sub> acts on the dodecahedron by permuting the 5 inscribed tetrahedra. Even permutations of these tetrahedra are exactly the symmetric rotations of the dodecahedron and characterizes the A<sub>5</sub> \< SO<sub>3</sub>(**R**) correspondence.

A<sub>5</sub> is the group of isometries of a dodecahedron in 3-space, so there is a representation A<sub>5</sub> → SO<sub>3</sub>(**R**).

In this picture the vertices of the polyhedra represent the elements of the group, with the center of the sphere representing the identity element. Each vertex represents a rotation about the axis pointing from the center to that vertex, by an angle equal to the distance from the origin, in radians. Vertices in the same polyhedron are in the same conjugacy class. Since the conjugacy class equation for A<sub>5</sub> is 1 + 12 + 12 + 15 + 20 = 60, we obtain four distinct (nontrivial) polyhedra.

The vertices of each polyhedron are in bijective correspondence with the elements of its conjugacy class, with the exception of the conjugacy class of (2,2)-cycles, which is represented by an icosidodecahedron on the outer surface, with its antipodal vertices identified with each other. The reason for this redundancy is that the corresponding rotations are by $\pi$ radians, and so can be represented by a vector of length $\pi$ in either of two directions. Thus the class of (2,2)-cycles contains 15 elements, while the icosidodecahedron has 30 vertices.

The two conjugacy classes of twelve 5-cycles in A<sub>5</sub> are represented by two icosahedra, of radii 2$\pi$/5 and 4$\pi$/5, respectively. The nontrivial [outer automorphism](wikipedia://en/628466) in Out(A<sub>5</sub>) ≃ Z<sub>2</sub> interchanges these two classes and the corresponding icosahedra.

### The 15 puzzle {#The_15_puzzle}

*Image: 15-puzzle magical.svg.* A [15 puzzle](wikipedia://en/432213).

It can be proved that the [15 puzzle](wikipedia://en/432213), a famous example of the [sliding puzzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sliding_puzzle), can be represented by the alternating group A<sub>15</sub>,[^o18] because the combinations of the 15 puzzle can be generated by [3-cycles](wikipedia://en/44027#Definition). In fact, any 2*k* − 1 sliding puzzle with square tiles of equal size can be represented by A<sub>2*k*−1</sub>.

## Subgroups {#Subgroups}

A<sub>4</sub> is the smallest group demonstrating that the converse of [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150) is not true in general: given a finite group *G* and a divisor *d* of $\lvert G\rvert$, there does not necessarily exist a subgroup of *G* with order *d*: the group *G* = A<sub>4</sub>, of order 12, has no subgroup of order 6. A subgroup of three elements (generated by a cyclic rotation of three objects) with any distinct nontrivial element generates the whole group.

For all *n* > 4, A<sub>*n*</sub> has no nontrivial (that is, proper) [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918). Thus, A<sub>*n*</sub> is a [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) for all *n* > 4. A<sub>5</sub> is the smallest [non-solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012).

## Group homology {#Group_homology}

The [group homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homology) of the alternating groups exhibits stabilization, as in [stable homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/2946655): for sufficiently large *n*, it is constant. However, there are some low-dimensional exceptional homology. Note that the [homology of the symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901#Homology) exhibits similar stabilization, but without the low-dimensional exceptions (additional homology elements).

### *H*<sub>1</sub>: Abelianization {#H1:_Abelianization}

The first [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) coincides with [abelianization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelianization), and (since A<sub>*n*</sub> is [perfect](wikipedia://en/585271), except for the cited exceptions) is thus:

*H*<sub>1</sub>(A<sub>*n*</sub>, Z) = Z<sub>1</sub> for *n* = 0, 1, 2;

*H*<sub>1</sub>(A<sub>3</sub>, Z) = A${}^{\operatorname{ab}}_{3}$ = A<sub>3</sub> = Z<sub>3</sub>;

*H*<sub>1</sub>(A<sub>4</sub>, Z) = A${}^{\operatorname{ab}}_{4}$ = Z<sub>3</sub>;

*H*<sub>1</sub>(A<sub>*n*</sub>, Z) = Z<sub>1</sub> for *n* ≥ 5.

This is easily seen directly, as follows. A<sub>*n*</sub> is generated by 3-cycles – so the only non-trivial abelianization maps are A<sub>*n*</sub> → Z<sub>3</sub>, since order-3 elements must map to order-3 elements – and for *n* ≥ 5 all 3-cycles are conjugate, so they must map to the same element in the abelianization, since conjugation is trivial in abelian groups. Thus a 3-cycle like (123) must map to the same element as its inverse (321), but thus must map to the identity, as it must then have order dividing 2 and 3, so the abelianization is trivial.

For *n* \< 3, A<sub>*n*</sub> is trivial, and thus has trivial abelianization. For A<sub>3</sub> and A<sub>4</sub> one can compute the abelianization directly, noting that the 3-cycles form two conjugacy classes (rather than all being conjugate) and there are non-trivial maps A<sub>3</sub> ↠ Z<sub>3</sub> (in fact an isomorphism) and A<sub>4</sub> ↠ Z<sub>3</sub>.

### *H*<sub>2</sub>: Schur multipliers {#H2:_Schur_multipliers}

The [Schur multipliers](wikipedia://en/1121587) of the alternating groups A<sub>*n*</sub> (in the case where *n* is at least 5) are the cyclic groups of order 2, except in the case where *n* is either 6 or 7, in which case there is also a triple cover. In these cases, then, the Schur multiplier is (the cyclic group) of order 6.[^o23] These were first computed in (Schur 1911).

*H*<sub>2</sub>(A<sub>*n*</sub>, Z) = Z<sub>1</sub> for *n* = 1, 2, 3;

*H*<sub>2</sub>(A<sub>*n*</sub>, Z) = Z<sub>2</sub> for *n* = 4, 5;

*H*<sub>2</sub>(A<sub>*n*</sub>, Z) = Z<sub>6</sub> for *n* = 6, 7;

*H*<sub>2</sub>(A<sub>*n*</sub>, Z) = Z<sub>2</sub> for *n* ≥ 8.
