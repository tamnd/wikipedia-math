# Scale (ratio)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Scale"}

*Image: Maßstabsleiste.png.* Graphical scale bar in combination with a scale expressed as a ratio and a conversion help.

The **scale ratio** of a [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_model) represents the [proportional](wikipedia://en/81863) ratio of a linear dimension of the model to the same feature of the original. Examples include a 3-dimensional [scale model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_model) of a building or the scale drawings of the elevations or plans of a building.[^o2] In such cases the scale is [dimensionless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_number) and **exact** throughout the model or drawing.

:::

The scale can be expressed in four ways: in words (a lexical scale), as a ratio, as a fraction and as a [graphical (bar) scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_scale). Thus on an architect's drawing one might read 'one centimeter to one meter', 1:100, 1/100, or $\tfrac{1}{100}$. A bar scale would also normally appear on the drawing. [Colon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_%28punctuation%29) may also be substituted with a specific, slightly raised ratio symbol 2236, ie. "1∶100".

## General representation {#General_representation}

Generally, a representation may involve more than one scale at the same time. For example, a drawing showing a new road in elevation might use different horizontal and vertical scales. An elevation of a bridge might be annotated with arrows with a length proportional to a force loading, as in 1 cm to 1000 newtons: this is an example of a dimensional scale. A weather map at some scale may be annotated with wind arrows at a dimensional scale of 1 cm to 20 mph.

## In maps {#In_maps}

Map scales require careful discussion. A town plan may be constructed as an exact scale drawing, but for larger areas a [map projection](wikipedia://en/51784) is necessary and no projection can represent the Earth's surface at a uniform scale. In general, the scale of a projection depends on position and direction. The variation of scale may be considerable in small scale maps which may cover the globe. In large scale maps of small areas, the variation of scale may be insignificant for most purposes, but it is always present. The scale of a map projection must be interpreted as a nominal scale. (The usage *large* and *small* in relation to map scales relates to their expressions as fractions. The fraction 1/10,000 used for a local map is much *larger* than the 1/100,000,000 used for a global map. There is no fixed dividing line between small and large scales.)

## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the idea of geometric [scaling](wikipedia://en/651752) can be generalized. The scale between two mathematical objects need not be a fixed ratio but may vary in some systematic way; this is part of mathematical [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097), which generally defines a point by point relationship between two mathematical objects. (Generally, these may be mathematical [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) and may not represent geometric objects.)
