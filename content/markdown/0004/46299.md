# Menelaus

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Menelaus"}

In [Greek mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mythology), **Menelaus**[^o3] (; Μενέλαος[^o4]) was a king of [Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta). According to the *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)*, the [Trojan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War) began as a result of Menelaus' wife, [Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy), fleeing to Troy with the Trojan prince [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_%28mythology%29). Menelaus was a central figure in the Trojan War, leading the Spartan contingent of the Greek army, under his elder brother [Agamemnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon), king of [Mycenae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae). Prominent in both the *Iliad* and *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)*, Menelaus was also popular in Greek vase painting and [Greek tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_tragedy), the latter more as a hero of the Trojan War than as a member of the doomed House of [Atreus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atreus).

:::

## Description {#Description}

*Image: Menelaus captures Helen in Troy (cropped).jpg.* Menelaus captures [Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy) in Troy, detail of fresco in [Pompeii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompeii)

In the account of [Dares the Phrygian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dares_Phrygius), Menelaus was described as "of moderate stature, auburn-haired, and handsome. He had a pleasing personality."[^o6]

[Homer uses the epithet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epithets_in_Homer) "ξανθός," transliterated as "grc," to describe Menelaus, a word often translated as fair-haired.[^o7][^o8]

## Family {#Family}

Menelaus was a descendant of [Pelops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelops) son of [Tantalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantalus).[^o10] He was the younger brother of [Agamemnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon), and the husband of [Helen of Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy). According to the usual version of the story, followed by the *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)* and *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey)* of [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer), Agamemnon and Menelaus were the sons of [Atreus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atreus), king of [Mycenae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae), and [Aerope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerope), daughter of the [Cretan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan) king [Catreus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catreus).[^o11] However, according to another tradition, Agamemnon and Menelaus were the sons of Atreus's son [Pleisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleisthenes), with their mother being Aerope, [Cleolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleolla), or Eriphyle. According to this tradition Pleisthenes died young, with Agamemnon and Menelaus being raised by Atreus.[^o12] Agamemnon and Menelaus had a sister [Anaxibia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaxibia) (or [Astyoche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astyoche)) who married [Strophius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strophius), the son of [Crisus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisus).[^o13]

According to the *Odyssey*, Menelaus had only one child by Helen, a daughter named [Hermione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermione_%28mythology%29); and an illegitimate son, [Megapenthes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megapenthes_%28son_of_Menelaus%29), by a slave.[^o14] Other sources mention other sons of Menelaus by either Helen, or slaves. A scholiast on [Sophocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophocles)'s *[Electra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electra_%28Sophocles_play%29)* quotes [Hesiod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesiod) as saying that after Hermione, Helen also bore Menelaus a son [Nicostratus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicostratus_%28mythology%29),[^o15] while according to a *[Cypria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cypria)* fragment, Menelaus and Helen had a son [Pleisthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleisthenes).[^o16] The mythographer [Apollodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliotheca_%28Pseudo-Apollodorus%29), tells us that Megapenthes's mother was a slave "[Pieris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieris_%28mythology%29), an Aetolian, or, according to [Acusilaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acusilaus), ... [Tereis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tereis)", and that Menelaus had another illegitimate son Xenodamas by another slave girl, [Cnossia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cnossia),[^o17] while according to the geographer [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_%28geographer%29), Megapenthes *and* Nicostratus were sons of Menelaus by a slave.[^o18] The scholiast on *Iliad* 3.175 mentions Nicostratus and [Aethiolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aethiolas) as two sons of Helen (by Menelaus?) worshipped by the [Lacedaemonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacedaemonians) and another son of Helen by Menelaus, Maraphius, from whom descended the Persian Maraphions.[^o19]

## Mythology {#Mythology}

### Accession and reign {#Accession_and_reign}

Although early authors, such as [Aeschylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeschylus), refer in passing to Menelaus's early life, detailed sources are quite late, post-dating 5th-century BC Greek [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy).[^o22] According to these sources, Menelaus's father, [Atreus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atreus), had been feuding with his brother [Thyestes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thyestes) over the throne of [Mycenae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenae). After a back-and-forth struggle that featured [adultery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adultery), [incest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest), and [cannibalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_cannibalism), Thyestes gained the throne after his son [Aegisthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegisthus) murdered [Atreus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atreus). As a result, Atreus's sons, Menelaus and [Agamemnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon), went into exile. They first stayed with King [Polypheides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polypheides) of [Sicyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicyon), and later with King [Oeneus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeneus) of [Calydon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calydon). But when they thought the time was ripe to dethrone Mycenae's hostile ruler, they returned. Assisted by King [Tyndareus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyndareus) of [Sparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta), they drove Thyestes away, and Agamemnon took the [throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Throne) for himself.

When it was time for [Tyndareus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyndareus)'s stepdaughter [Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy) to marry, [many kings and princes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suitors_of_Helen) came to seek her hand. Among the contenders were [Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus), [Menestheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menestheus), [Ajax the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajax_the_Great), [Patroclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patroclus), and [Idomeneus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idomeneus). Most offered opulent gifts. Tyndareus would accept none of the gifts, nor would he send any of the suitors away for fear of offending them and giving grounds for a quarrel. Odysseus promised to solve the problem in a satisfactory manner if Tyndareus would support him in his courting of Tyndareus's niece [Penelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope), the daughter of [Icarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icarius_%28Spartan%29). Tyndareus readily agreed, and Odysseus proposed that, before the decision was made, all the suitors should swear a most solemn oath to defend the chosen husband in any quarrel. Then it was decreed that straws were to be drawn for Helen's hand. The suitor who won was Menelaus (Tyndareus, not to displease the mighty Agamemnon offered him another of his daughters, [Clytaemnestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clytaemnestra)).[^o23] The rest of the suitors swore their oaths, and Helen and Menelaus were married, Menelaus becoming a ruler of Sparta with Helen after Tyndareus and [Leda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leda_%28mythology%29) abdicated the thrones.

Their supposed palace (ἀνάκτορον) has been discovered (the excavations started in 1926 and continued until 1995) in [Pellana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pellana), [Laconia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laconia), to the north-west of modern (and classical) Sparta.[^o24] Other archaeologists consider that [Pellana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pellana) is too far away from other Mycenaean centres to have been the "capital of Menelaus".[^o25]

According to tradition Menelaus founded the port-city [Menelai Portus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelai_Portus) on the coast of [Marmarica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marmarica) in Northern Africa.[^o26]

### Trojan War {#Trojan_War}

*Image: Helen Menelaus Louvre G424.jpg.* Menelaus regains Helen, detail of an Attic red-figure crater, c. 450–440 BC, found in [Gnatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnatia) (now Egnazia, [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy)).

According to legend, in return for awarding her a golden apple inscribed "to the fairest," [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite) promised [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_%28mythology%29) the most beautiful woman in all the world. After concluding a diplomatic mission to Sparta during the latter part of which Menelaus was absent to attend the funeral of his maternal grandfather [Catreus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catreus) in [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete), Paris ran off to Troy with Helen despite his brother [Hector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector)'s prohibition. Invoking the oath of [Tyndareus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyndareus), Menelaus and [Agamemnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon) raised a fleet of a thousand ships and went to Troy to secure Helen's return; the Trojans refused, providing a *[casus belli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casus_belli)* for the [Trojan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War).

[Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer)'s *[Iliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliad)* is the most comprehensive source for Menelaus's exploits during the Trojan War. In Book 3, Paris challenges Menelaus to a duel for Helen's return. Menelaus soundly beats Paris, but before he can kill him and claim victory, Aphrodite spirits Paris away inside the walls of Troy. In Book 4, while the Greeks and Trojans squabble over the duel's winner, [Athena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athena) inspires the Trojan [Pandarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandarus) to shoot Menelaus with his bow and arrow. However, Athena never intended for Menelaus to die and she protects him from the arrow of Pandarus.[^o28] Menelaus is wounded in the abdomen, and the fighting resumes. Later, in Book 17, Homer gives Menelaus an extended *[aristeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristeia)* as the hero retrieves the corpse of Patroclus from the battlefield.

According to [Hyginus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Julius_Hyginus), Menelaus killed eight men in the war, and was one of the Greeks hidden inside the [Trojan Horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_Horse). During the sack of Troy, Menelaus killed [Deiphobus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deiphobus), who had married Helen after the death of Paris.

There are four versions of Menelaus's and Helen's reunion on the night of the sack of Troy:

- Menelaus sought out Helen in the conquered city. Raging at her infidelity, he raised his sword to kill her, but as he saw her weeping at his feet, begging for her life, Menelaus's wrath instantly left him. He took pity on her and decided to take her back as his wife.
- Menelaus resolved to kill Helen, but her irresistible beauty prompted him to drop his sword and take her back to his ship "to punish her at Sparta", as he claimed.[^o29]
- According to the *[Bibliotheca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliotheca_%28Pseudo-Apollodorus%29)*, Menelaus raised his sword in front of the [temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple) in the central square of Troy to kill her, but his wrath went away when he saw her rending her clothes in anguish, revealing her naked breasts.
- A similar version by [Stesichorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stesichorus) in "Ilion's Conquest" narrated that Menelaus surrendered her to his soldiers to stone her to death, but when she ripped the front of her robes, the Achaean warriors were stunned by her beauty and the stones fell harmlessly from their hands as they stared at her.

### After the war {#After_the_war}

*Image: Patroclus corpse MAN Firenze.jpg.* Menelaus and [Meriones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meriones_%28mythology%29) lifting [Patroclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patroclus)'s corpse on a cart while [Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus) looks on; [alabaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabaster) urn, [Etruscan artwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_art) from [Volterra](wikipedia://en/548647), 2nd century BC

Book 4 of the *Odyssey* provides an account of Menelaus's return from Troy and his homelife in Sparta. When visited by Odysseus's son [Telemachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telemachus), Menelaus recounts his voyage home. As happened to many Greeks, Menelaus's homebound fleet was blown by storms to [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete) and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) where they were becalmed, unable to sail away. They trapped [Proteus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteus) and forced him to reveal how to make the voyage home.

In [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus)' account, it was not Helen but a [phantom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eidolon) who went to Troy. She had spent the war in Egypt and was there reunited with her husband. Contrary winds detained the couple, leading Menelaus to sacrifice two Egyptian children in order to escape.[^o31]

Once back in Sparta, he and Helen appear to have a harmonious married life—he holding no grudge at her having run away with a lover and she feeling no restraint in telling anecdotes of her life inside besieged Troy. However, some scholars detect underlying conflict. Menelaus undercuts Helen's tale of loyalty to Greece with a story of his own, where she tries to expose the soldiers hidden inside the [Trojan horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_horse).[^o32]

Menelaus does seem to be pained that he and Helen have no male heir, and is shown to be fond of [Megapenthes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megapenthes_%28son_of_Menelaus%29) and [Nicostratus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicostratus_%28mythology%29), his sons by slave women.

Menelaus and Helen were worshipped at the [Menelaion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaion) in [Therapne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapne). Of all the ancient authors, only [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_%28geographer%29) mentions that Menelaus and Helen were buried there after their deaths. According to another legend recorded by [Ptolemy Chennus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_Chennus) and [Photios I of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photios_I_of_Constantinople), both of them were sacrificed by [Iphigenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iphigenia) to Artemis in [Taurus of Scythia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Crimea).[^o33] A Rhodian legend recorded by Pausanias states that after Menelaus' death, Helen was driven out by Megapenthes and Nicostratus, forcing her to flee to Rhodes where she was murdered by [Polyxo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyxo_%28Rhodes%29).[^o34]

According to Homer's *Odyssey* on the other hand, it was ordained that Menelaus would not die and would instead be taken to the [Elysium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elysium).[^o33] Per [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides)' *[Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_%28play%29)*, Menelaus will spend his afterlife on the [Isle of the Blessed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortunate_Isles).[^o35]

The [Lindos Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindos_Chronicle) describes dedications attributed to Menelaus: a leather cap associated with Paris and a dagger, which Menelaus supposedly dedicated at the [temple to Athena at Lindos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Athena_Lindia).[^o36]

## In vase painting {#In_vase_painting}

Menelaus appears in Greek vase painting in the 6th to 4th centuries BC, such as: Menelaus's reception of Paris at Sparta; his retrieval of Patroclus's corpse; and his reunion with Helen.[^o38]

## In Greek tragedy {#In_Greek_tragedy}

Menelaus appears as a character in a number of 5th-century Greek tragedies: [Sophocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophocles)'s *[Ajax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajax_%28Sophocles%29)*, and [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides)'s *[Andromache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromache_%28play%29)*, *[Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_%28play%29)*, *[Orestes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orestes_%28play%29)*, *[Iphigenia at Aulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iphigenia_at_Aulis)*, and *[The Trojan Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trojan_Women)*.
