# Abelian category

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abelian category"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **abelian category** is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) in which [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) and [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) can be added and in which [kernels](wikipedia://en/142616) and [cokernels](wikipedia://en/279715) exist and have desirable properties.

:::

The motivating prototypical example of an abelian category is the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748), $\mathbf{Ab}$.

Abelian categories are very *stable* categories; for example they are [regular](wikipedia://en/9790950) and they satisfy the [snake lemma](wikipedia://en/87089). The [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of abelian categories is closed under several categorical constructions, for example, the category of [chain complexes](wikipedia://en/138404) of an abelian category, or the category of [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) from a [small category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category) to an abelian category are abelian as well. These stability properties make them inevitable in [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410) and beyond; the theory has major applications in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) and pure [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869).

[Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574)[^n1] says [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042)[^n2] defined abelian categories in 1957, but there is a reference[^n3] that says [Samuel Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501)'s disciple, [David Buchsbaum](wikipedia://en/30781131), had proposed the concept in his 1955 PhD thesis,[^n4] and Grothendieck popularized it under the name "abelian category".

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A category is **abelian** if it is *[preadditive](wikipedia://en/59521)* and

- it has a [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object),
- it has all binary [biproducts](wikipedia://en/59574),
- it has all [kernels](wikipedia://en/142616) and [cokernels](wikipedia://en/279715), and
- all [monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) and [epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/59539) are [normal](wikipedia://en/142626).

This definition is equivalent[^n5] to the following "piecemeal" definition:

- A category is *[preadditive](wikipedia://en/59521)* if it is [enriched](wikipedia://en/142622) over the [monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604) $\mathbf{Ab}$ of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974). This means that all [hom-sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom-set) are abelian groups and the composition of morphisms is [bilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_operator).
- A preadditive category is *[additive](wikipedia://en/60854)* if every [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of objects has a [biproduct](wikipedia://en/59574). This means that we can form finite [direct sums](wikipedia://en/58899) and [direct products](wikipedia://en/8887). Some authors[^n6] require that an additive category have a zero object (empty biproduct).
- An additive category is *[preabelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preabelian_category)* if every morphism has both a [kernel](wikipedia://en/142616) and a [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715).
- Finally, a preabelian category is **abelian** if every [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) and every [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) is [normal](wikipedia://en/142626). This means that every monomorphism is a kernel of some morphism, and every epimorphism is a cokernel of some morphism.

Note that the enriched structure on [hom-sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom-set) is a *consequence* of the first three [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) of the first definition. This highlights the foundational relevance of the category of [Abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) in the theory and its canonical nature.

The concept of [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) arises naturally in this setting, and it turns out that [exact functors](wikipedia://en/453945), i.e. the functors preserving exact sequences in various senses, are the relevant functors between abelian categories. This *exactness* concept has been axiomatized in the theory of [exact categories](wikipedia://en/10987985), forming a very special case of [regular categories](wikipedia://en/9790950).

## Examples {#Examples}

- As mentioned above, the category of all abelian groups is an abelian category. The category of all [finitely generated abelian groups](wikipedia://en/58527) is also an abelian category, as is the category of all finite abelian groups.
- If *R* is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), then the category of all left (or right) [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over *R* is an abelian category. In fact, it can be shown that any small abelian category is equivalent to a [full subcategory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_subcategory) of such a category of modules (*[Mitchell's embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/453755)*).
- If *R* is a left-[noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129), then the category of [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) left modules over *R* is abelian. In particular, the category of finitely generated modules over a noetherian [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) is abelian; in this way, abelian categories show up in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990).
- As special cases of the two previous examples: the category of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a fixed [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k* is abelian, as is the category of finite-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_dimension) vector spaces over *k*.
- If *X* is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), then the category of all (real or complex) [vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundles) on *X* is not usually an abelian category, as there can be monomorphisms that are not kernels.
- If *X* is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), then the category of all [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) of abelian groups on *X* is an abelian category. More generally, the category of sheaves of abelian groups on a [Grothendieck site](wikipedia://en/12910) is an abelian category. In this way, abelian categories show up in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997).
- If **C** is a [small category](wikipedia://en/53759#Small_and_large_categories) and **A** is an abelian category, then the [category of all functors](wikipedia://en/407039) from **C** to **A** forms an abelian category. If **C** is small and [preadditive](wikipedia://en/59521), then the category of all [additive functors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_functor) from **C** to **A** also forms an abelian category. The latter is a generalization of the *R*-module example, since a ring can be understood as a preadditive category with a single object.

## Grothendieck's axioms {#Grothendieck's_axioms}

In his [Tōhoku article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tōhoku_article), Grothendieck listed four additional axioms (and their duals) that an abelian category **A** might satisfy. These axioms are still in common use to this day. They are the following:

- AB3) For every indexed family (*A*<sub>*i*</sub>) of objects of **A**, the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) \**A*<sub>i</sub> exists in **A** (i.e. **A** is [cocomplete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocomplete)).
- AB4) **A** satisfies AB3), and the coproduct of a family of monomorphisms is a monomorphism.
- [AB5](wikipedia://en/30051646)) **A** satisfies AB3), and [filtered colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtered_colimit) of [exact sequences](wikipedia://en/138215) are exact.

and their duals

- AB3\*) For every indexed family (*A*<sub>*i*</sub>) of objects of **A**, the [product](wikipedia://en/237868) P*A*<sub>*i*</sub> exists in **A** (i.e. **A** is [complete](wikipedia://en/62781)).
- AB4\*) **A** satisfies AB3\*), and the product of a family of epimorphisms is an epimorphism.
- AB5\*) **A** satisfies AB3\*), and [filtered limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtered_limit) of exact sequences are exact.

Axioms AB1) and AB2) were also given. They are what make an additive category abelian. Specifically:

- AB1) Every morphism has a kernel and a cokernel.
- AB2) For every morphism *f*, the canonical morphism from coim *f* to im *f* is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828).

Grothendieck also gave axioms AB6) and AB6\*).

- AB6) **A** satisfies AB3), and given a family of filtered categories $I_j, j\in J$ and functors $A_j : I_j \to$ **A**, we have $\prod_{j\in J} \lim_{I_j} A_j = \lim_{I_j, \forall j\in J} \prod_{j\in J} A_j$, where lim denotes the filtered colimit.
- AB6\*) **A** satisfies AB3\*), and given a family of cofiltered categories $I_j, j\in J$ and functors $A_j : I_j \to$ **A**, we have $\sum_{j\in J} \lim_{I_j} A_j = \lim_{I_j, \forall j\in J} \sum_{j\in J} A_j$, where lim denotes the cofiltered limit.

:::

## Elementary properties {#Elementary_properties}

Given any pair *A*, *B* of objects in an abelian category, there is a special [zero morphism](wikipedia://en/320638) from *A* to *B*. This can be defined as the [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) element of the [hom-set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom-set) Hom(*A*,*B*), since this is an abelian group. Alternatively, it can be defined as the unique composition *A* → 0 → *B*, where 0 is the [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object) of the abelian category.

In an abelian category, every morphism *f* can be written as the composition of an epimorphism followed by a monomorphism. This epimorphism is called the *[coimage](wikipedia://en/632685)* of *f*, while the monomorphism is called the *[image](wikipedia://en/342899)* of *f*.

[Subobjects](wikipedia://en/540476) and [quotient objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_object) are [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved) in abelian categories. For example, the [poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) of subobjects of any given object *A* is a [bounded lattice](wikipedia://en/310878).

Every abelian category **A** is a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over the monoidal category of finitely generated abelian groups; that is, we can form a [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of a finitely generated abelian group *G* and any object *A* of **A**. The abelian category is also a [comodule](wikipedia://en/1200537); Hom(*G*,*A*) can be interpreted as an object of **A**. If **A** is [complete](wikipedia://en/62781), then we can remove the requirement that *G* be finitely generated; most generally, we can form [finitary](wikipedia://en/404456) [enriched limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enriched_limit) in **A**.

Given an object $A$ in an abelian category, **flatness** refers to the idea that $- \otimes A$ is an [exact functor](wikipedia://en/453945). See [flat module](wikipedia://en/492616) or, for more generality, [flat morphism](wikipedia://en/542520).

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

Abelian categories are the most general setting for [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410). All of the constructions used in that field are relevant, such as exact sequences, and especially [short exact sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence), and [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875). Important theorems that apply in all abelian categories include the [five lemma](wikipedia://en/138214) (and the [short five lemma](wikipedia://en/130280) as a special case), as well as the [snake lemma](wikipedia://en/87089) (and the [nine lemma](wikipedia://en/454673) as a special case).

### Semi-simple abelian categories {#Semi-simple_abelian_categories}

An abelian category $\mathbf{A}$ is called **semi-simple** if there is a collection of objects $\{X_i\}_{i \in I} \in \text{Ob}(\mathbf{A})$ called **simple objects** (meaning the only sub-objects of any $X_i$ are the zero object $0$ and itself) such that an object $X \in \text{Ob}(\mathbf{A})$ can be decomposed as a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) (denoting the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) of the abelian category)

$$X \cong \bigoplus_{i \in I} X_i$$

This technical condition is rather strong and excludes many natural examples of abelian categories found in nature. For example, most module categories over a ring $R$ are not semi-simple; in fact, this is the case if and only if $R$ is a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring).

::: {#o19 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

Some abelian categories found in nature are semi-simple, such as

- The category of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) $\text{Vect}(k)$ over a fixed field $k$.
- By [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007) the category of representations $\text{Rep}_k(G)$ of a finite group $G$ over a field $k$ whose characteristic does not divide $|G|$ is a semi-simple abelian category.
- The category of [coherent sheaves](wikipedia://en/603780) on a [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/2783891) [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) is semi-simple if and only if $X$ is a finite disjoint union of irreducible points. This is equivalent to a finite coproduct of categories of vector spaces over different fields. Showing this is true in the forward direction is equivalent to showing all $\text{Ext}^1$ groups vanish, meaning the [cohomological dimension](wikipedia://en/9880197) is 0. This only happens when the skyscraper sheaves $k_x$ at a point $x \in X$ have [Zariski tangent space](wikipedia://en/510207) equal to zero, which is isomorphic to $\text{Ext}^1(k_x,k_x)$ using [local algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_algebra) for such a scheme.[^o20]

:::

::: {#o22 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

#### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

There do exist some natural counter-examples of abelian categories which are not semi-simple, such as certain categories of [representations](wikipedia://en/19378200). For example, the category of representations of the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $(\mathbb{R},+)$ has the representation

$$a \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} 1 & a \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

which only has one subrepresentation of dimension $1$. In fact, this is true for any [unipotent group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unipotent_group).[^n7]

:::

## Subcategories of abelian categories {#Subcategories_of_abelian_categories}

There are numerous types of (full, additive) subcategories of abelian categories that occur in nature, as well as some conflicting terminology.

Let **A** be an abelian category, **C** a full, additive subcategory, and *I* the inclusion functor.

- **C** is an exact subcategory if it is itself an [exact category](wikipedia://en/10987985) and the inclusion *I* is an [exact functor](wikipedia://en/453945). This occurs if and only if **C** is closed under [pullbacks](wikipedia://en/841689) of epimorphisms and [pushouts](wikipedia://en/582024) of monomorphisms. The exact sequences in **C** are thus the exact sequences in **A** for which all objects lie in **C**.
- **C** is an abelian subcategory if it is itself an abelian category and the inclusion *I* is an [exact functor](wikipedia://en/453945). This occurs if and only if **C** is closed under taking kernels and cokernels. Note that there are examples of full subcategories of an abelian category that are themselves abelian but where the inclusion functor is not exact, so they are not abelian subcategories (see below).
- **C** is a thick subcategory if it is closed under taking direct summands and satisfies the 2-out-of-3 property on short exact sequences; that is, if $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ is a short exact sequence in **A** such that two of $M',M,M''$ lie in **C**, then so does the third. In other words, **C** is closed under kernels of epimorphisms, cokernels of monomorphisms, and extensions. Note that P. Gabriel used the term *thick subcategory* to describe what we here call a *Serre subcategory*.
- **C** is a topologizing subcategory if it is closed under [subquotients](wikipedia://en/1639294).
- **C** is a [Serre subcategory](wikipedia://en/44974120) if, for all short exact sequences $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$ in **A** we have *M* in **C** if and only if both $M',M''$ are in **C**. In other words, **C** is closed under extensions and [subquotients](wikipedia://en/1639294). These subcategories are precisely the kernels of exact functors from **A** to another abelian category.
- **C** is a [localizing subcategory](wikipedia://en/44974120) if it is a Serre subcategory such that the quotient functor $Q\colon\mathbf A \to \mathbf A/\mathbf C$ admits a [right adjoint](wikipedia://en/53991).
- There are two competing notions of a wide subcategory. One version is that **C** contains every object of **A** (up to isomorphism); for a full subcategory this is obviously not interesting. (This is also called a [lluf](wikipedia://en/310959#Types_of_subcategories) subcategory.) The other version is that **C** is closed under extensions.

Here is an explicit example of a full, additive subcategory of an abelian category that is itself abelian but the inclusion functor is not exact. Let *k* be a field, $T_n$ the algebra of upper-triangular $n\times n$ matrices over *k*, and $\mathbf A_n$ the category of finite-dimensional $T_n$-modules. Then each $\mathbf A_n$ is an abelian category and we have an inclusion functor $I\colon\mathbf A_2 \to \mathbf A_3$ identifying the simple projective, simple injective and indecomposable projective-injective modules. The essential image of *I* is a full, additive subcategory, but *I* is not exact.

## History {#History}

Abelian categories were introduced by Buchsbaum[^n4] (under the name of "exact category") and Grothendieck[^n2] in order to unify various cohomology theories. At the time, there was a cohomology theory for [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466), and a cohomology theory for [groups](wikipedia://en/19447). The two were defined differently, but they had similar properties. In fact, much of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) was developed as a language to study these similarities. Grothendieck unified the two theories: they both arise as [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) on abelian categories; the abelian category of sheaves of abelian groups on a topological space, and the abelian category of [*G*-modules](wikipedia://en/26068776) for a given group *G*.
