# Dual number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dual number"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the **dual numbers** are a [quadratic algebra](wikipedia://en/23565253) first introduced in the 19th century. They are [expressions](wikipedia://en/609125) of the form $a + b\varepsilon$, where $a$ and $b$ are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), and $\varepsilon$ is a symbol taken to satisfy $\varepsilon^2 = 0$ with $\varepsilon\neq 0$.

:::

Dual numbers can be added component-wise, and multiplied by the formula

$$(a+b\varepsilon)(c+d\varepsilon) = ac + (ad+bc)\varepsilon,$$

which follows from the property $\varepsilon^{2} = 0$ and the fact that multiplication is a [bilinear operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_operation).

The dual numbers form a [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29) of [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) two over the reals, and also an [Artinian local ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artinian_local_ring). They are one of the simplest examples of a ring that has nonzero [nilpotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element).

## History {#History}

Dual numbers were introduced in 1873 by [William Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820), and were used at the beginning of the twentieth century by the German mathematician [Eduard Study](wikipedia://en/663997), who used them to represent the dual angle which measures the relative position of two [skew lines](wikipedia://en/1975821) in space. Study defined a dual angle as $\theta + d\varepsilon$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the directions of two lines in three-dimensional space and $d$ is a distance between them. The $n$-dimensional generalization, the [Grassmann number](wikipedia://en/3117887), was introduced by [Hermann Grassmann](wikipedia://en/99242) in the late 19th century.

## Modern definition {#Modern_definition}

In modern [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the algebra of dual numbers is often defined as the [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) of a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) over the real numbers $(\mathbb{R})$ by the [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126) generated by the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of the [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29),[^o4] that is

$$\mathbb{R}[X]/\left\langle X^2 \right\rangle.$$

It may also be defined as the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) of a one-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with $\varepsilon$ as its basis element.

## Division {#Division}

Division of dual numbers is defined when the real part of the denominator ($c$ in the equations below) is [non-zero](wikipedia://en/185663). The division process is analogous to [complex division](wikipedia://en/5826) in that the denominator is multiplied by its conjugate in order to cancel the non-real parts.

Therefore, to evaluate an expression of the form

$$\frac{a + b\varepsilon}{c + d\varepsilon}$$

we multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{a + b\varepsilon}{c + d\varepsilon} &= \frac{(a + b\varepsilon)(c - d\varepsilon)}{(c + d\varepsilon)(c - d\varepsilon)}\\[5pt] &= \frac{ac - ad\varepsilon + bc\varepsilon - bd\varepsilon^2}{c^2 + cd\varepsilon - cd\varepsilon - d^2\varepsilon^2}\\[5pt] &= \frac{ac - ad\varepsilon + bc\varepsilon - 0}{c^2 - 0}\\[5pt] &= \frac{ac + \varepsilon(bc - ad)}{c^2}\\[5pt] &= \frac{a}{c} + \frac{bc - ad}{c^2}\varepsilon \end{aligned}$$

If, on the other hand, $c$ is zero while $d$ is not, then the equation

$${a + b\varepsilon = (x + y\varepsilon) d\varepsilon} = {xd\varepsilon + 0}$$

1. has no solution if $a$ is nonzero
1. is otherwise solved by any dual number of the form $\tfrac{b}{d} + y\varepsilon$.

This means that the non-real part of the "quotient" is arbitrary and division is therefore not defined for purely nonreal dual numbers. Indeed, they are (trivially) [zero divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_divisors) and clearly form an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of the associative [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) (and thus [ring](wikipedia://en/48404)) of the dual numbers.

## Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

The dual number $a + b \varepsilon$ can be represented by the [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) $\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix}$. In this representation the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ squares to the zero matrix, corresponding to the dual number $\varepsilon$.

Generally, if $\varepsilon$ is a [nilpotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element) matrix, then *B* = {*x* I + *y* $\varepsilon$: *x, y* real} is a [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) isomorphic to the algebra of dual numbers. In the case of 2x2 real matrices M(2,**R**), $\varepsilon$ can be taken as any matrix of the form $\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ c & -a \end{pmatrix}$ with *p* = *a*<sup>2</sup> + *bc* = 0.

The dual numbers are one of three isomorphism classes of real 2-algebras in M(2,**R**). When *p* > 0 the subalgebra *B* is isomorphic to [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559), and when *p* \< 0, *B* is isomorphic to the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).

## Automatic differentiation {#Automatic_differentiation}

One application of dual numbers is [automatic differentiation](wikipedia://en/734787). Any polynomial

$$P(x) = p_0 + p_1x + p_2x^2 + \cdots + p_nx^n$$

with real coefficients can be extended to a function of a dual-number-valued argument,

$$\begin{aligned} P(a + b\varepsilon) &= p_0 + p_1(a + b\varepsilon) + \cdots + p_n(a + b\varepsilon)^n \\[2mu] &= p_0 + p_1 a + p_2 a^2 + \cdots + p_n a^n + p_1 b\varepsilon + 2 p_2 a b\varepsilon + \cdots + n p_n a^{n-1} b\varepsilon \\[5mu] &= p_0 + p_1 a + p_2 a^2 + \cdots + p_n a^n + b (p_1 + 2 p_2 a + \cdots + n p_n a^{n-1}) \varepsilon\\[5mu] &= P(a) + bP'(a)\varepsilon, \end{aligned}$$

where $P'$ is the derivative of $P.$

More generally, any (analytic) real function can be extended to the dual numbers via its [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448):

$$f(a + b\varepsilon) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(n)} (a)b^n \varepsilon^n}{n!} = f(a) + bf'(a)\varepsilon,$$

since all terms involving $\varepsilon^{2}$ or greater powers are trivially $0$ by the definition of $\varepsilon$.

By computing compositions of these functions over the dual numbers and examining the coefficient of $\varepsilon$ in the result we find we have automatically computed the derivative of the composition.

A similar method works for polynomials of $n$ variables, using the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) of an $n$-dimensional vector space.

## Geometry {#Geometry}

The "unit circle" of dual numbers consists of those with $a = \pm1$ since these satisfy $zz* = 1$ where $z* = a - b\varepsilon$. However, note that

$$e^{b \varepsilon} = \sum^\infty_{n=0} \frac{\left(b\varepsilon\right)^n}{n!} = 1 + b \varepsilon,$$

so the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) applied to the $\varepsilon$-axis covers only half the "circle".

Let $z = a + b\varepsilon$. If $a \ne 0$ and $m = \tfrac{b}{a}$, then $z = a(1 + m\varepsilon)$ is the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241#Alternative_planar_decompositions) of the dual number $z$, and the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) $m$ is its angular part. The concept of a *rotation* in the dual number plane is equivalent to a vertical [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405) since $(1 + p\varepsilon)(1 + q\varepsilon) = 1 + (p + q)\varepsilon$.

In [absolute space and time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_space_and_time) the [Galilean transformation](wikipedia://en/295183)

$$\left(t', x'\right) = (t, x)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & v \\0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\,,$$

that is

$$t' = t,\quad x' = vt + x,$$

relates the resting coordinates system to a moving frame of reference of [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) $v$. With dual numbers $t + x\varepsilon$ representing [events](wikipedia://en/24651576) along one space dimension and time, the same transformation is effected with multiplication by $1 + v\varepsilon$.

### Cycles {#Cycles}

Given two dual numbers $p$ and $q$, they determine the set of $z$ such that the difference in slopes ("Galilean angle") between the lines from $z$ to $p$ and $q$ is constant. This set is a **cycle** in the dual number plane; since the equation setting the difference in slopes of the lines to a constant is a [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) in the real part of $z$, a cycle is a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231). The "cyclic rotation" of the dual number plane occurs as a motion of its projective line. According to [Isaak Yaglom](wikipedia://en/11606627),[^o21] the cycle $Z = \{z : y = \alpha x^{2}\}$ is invariant under the composition of the shear

$$x_1 = x ,\quad y_1 = vx + y$$

with the [translation](wikipedia://en/204682)

$$x' = x_1 = \frac{v}{2a} ,\quad y' = y_1 + \frac{v^2}{4a}.$$

## Applications in mechanics {#Applications_in_mechanics}

Dual numbers find applications in [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), notably for kinematic synthesis. For example, the dual numbers make it possible to transform the input/output equations of a four-bar spherical linkage, which includes only rotoid joints, into a four-bar spatial mechanism (rotoid, rotoid, rotoid, cylindrical). The dualized angles are made of a primitive part, the angles, and a dual part, which has units of length.[^o11] See [screw theory](wikipedia://en/1318037) for more.

## Algebraic geometry {#Algebraic_geometry}

In modern [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), the dual numbers over a field $k$ (by which we mean the ring $k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$) may be used to define the [tangent vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_vectors) to the points of a $k$-[scheme](wikipedia://en/364754).[^o13] Since the field $k$ can be chosen intrinsically, it is possible to speak simply of the tangent vectors to a scheme. This allows notions from [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) to be imported into algebraic geometry.

In detail: The ring of dual numbers may be thought of as the ring of functions on the "first-order neighborhood of a point" – namely, the $k$-[scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) $\operatorname{Spec} (k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2))$.[^o13] Then, given a $k$-scheme $X$, $k$-points of the scheme are in 1-1 correspondence with maps $\operatorname{Spec} k \to X$, while tangent vectors are in 1-1 correspondence with maps $\operatorname{Spec} (k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)) \to X$.

The field $k$ above can be chosen intrinsically to be a [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680). To wit: Given a point $x$ on a scheme $S$, consider the [stalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalk_%28mathematics%29) $S_x$. Observe that $S_x$ is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) with a unique [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164), which is denoted $\mathfrak m_x$. Then simply let $k = S_x / \mathfrak m_x$.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

This construction can be carried out more generally: for a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $R$ one can define the dual numbers over $R$ as the [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) of the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[X]$ by the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) $(X^{2})$: the image of $X$ then has square equal to zero and corresponds to the element $\varepsilon$ from above.

### Arbitrary module of elements of zero square {#Arbitrary_module_of_elements_of_zero_square}

There is a more general construction of the dual numbers. Given a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $R$ and a module $M$, there is a ring $R[M]$ called the ring of dual numbers which has the following structures:

It is the $R$-module $R \oplus M$ with the multiplication defined by $(r, m) \cdot \left(r', m'\right) = \left(rr', rm' + r'm\right)$ for $r, r' \in R$ and $m, m' \in M.$

The algebra of dual numbers is the special case where $M = R$ and $\varepsilon = (0, 1).$

:::

## Superspace {#Superspace}

Dual numbers find applications in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), where they constitute one of the simplest non-trivial examples of a [superspace](wikipedia://en/432630). Equivalently, they are [supernumbers](wikipedia://en/3117887) with just one generator; supernumbers generalize the concept to $n$ distinct generators $\varepsilon$, each anti-commuting, possibly taking $n$ to infinity. Superspace generalizes supernumbers slightly, by allowing multiple commuting dimensions.

The motivation for introducing dual numbers into physics follows from the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669) for fermions. The direction along $\varepsilon$ is termed the "fermionic" direction, and the real component is termed the "bosonic" direction. The fermionic direction earns this name from the fact that [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) obey the Pauli exclusion principle: under the exchange of coordinates, the quantum mechanical wave function changes sign, and thus vanishes if two coordinates are brought together; this physical idea is captured by the algebraic relation $\varepsilon^{2} = 0$.

## Projective line {#Projective_line}

The idea of a projective line over dual numbers was advanced by Grünwald[^o19] and [Corrado Segre](wikipedia://en/2422061).[^o20]

Just as the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) needs a north pole [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) to close up the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line), so a [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142) succeeds in closing up the plane of dual numbers to a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29).[^o21]p. 149–153

Suppose $D$ is the ring of dual numbers $x + y\varepsilon$ and $U$ is the subset with $x \ne 0$. Then $U$ is the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of $D$. Let $B = \{(a, b) \in D \times D : a \in U\text{ or } b \in U\}$. A [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) is defined on B as follows: $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ when there is a $u$ in $U$ such that $ua = c$ and $ub = d$. This relation is in fact an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). The points of the projective line over $D$ are [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) in $B$ under this relation: $P(D) = B/\sim$. They are represented with [projective coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_coordinates) $[a, b]$.

Consider the [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) $D \to P(D)$ by $z \to [z, 1]$. Then points $[1, n]$, for $n^{2} = 0$, are in $P(D)$ but are not the image of any point under the embedding. $P(D)$ is mapped onto a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) by [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097): Take a cylinder tangent to the double number plane on the line $\{y\varepsilon : y \in \mathbf{R}\}$, $\varepsilon^{2} = 0$. Now take the opposite line on the cylinder for the axis of a [pencil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pencil_%28mathematics%29) of planes. The planes intersecting the dual number plane and cylinder provide a correspondence of points between these surfaces. The plane parallel to the dual number plane corresponds to points $[1, n]$, $n^{2} = 0$ in the projective line over dual numbers.
