# Divisor

*Image: Cuisenaire ten.JPG.* The divisors of 10 illustrated with [Cuisenaire rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisenaire_rods): 1, 2, 5, and 10

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Divisor"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **divisor** of an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n,$ also called a **factor** of $n,$ is an integer $m$ that may be multiplied by some integer to produce $n.$[^n1] In this case, one also says that $n$ is a *[multiple](wikipedia://en/3912709)* of $m.$ An integer $n$ is **divisible** or **evenly divisible** by another integer $m$ if $m$ is a divisor of $n$; this implies dividing $n$ by $m$ leaves no remainder.

:::

The concept of a divisor is extended, with the same definition, to elements of any [ring](wikipedia://en/48404); see [Divisibility (ring theory)](wikipedia://en/32981716).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$ is divisible by a nonzero integer $m$ if there exists an integer $k$ such that $n=km.$ This is written as

$$m\mid n.$$

This may be read as that $m$ divides $n,$ $m$ is a divisor of $n,$ $m$ is a factor of $n,$ or $n$ is a multiple of $m.$ If $m$ does not divide $n,$ then the notation is $m\not\mid n.$[^n2][^n3]

There are two conventions, distinguished by whether $m$ is permitted to be zero:

- With the convention without an additional constraint on $m,$ $m \mid 0$ for every integer $m.$[^n2][^n3]
- With the convention that $m$ be nonzero, $m \mid 0$ for every nonzero integer $m.$[^n4][^n5]

:::

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Non-trivial divisor"}

## General {#General}

Divisors can be [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) as well as positive, although often the term is restricted to positive divisors. For example, there are six divisors of 4; they are 1, 2, 4, −1, −2, and −4, but only the positive ones (1, 2, and 4) would usually be mentioned.

1 and −1 divide (are divisors of) every integer. Every integer (and its negation) is a divisor of itself. Integers divisible by 2 are called [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_numbers), and integers not divisible by 2 are called [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_numbers).

1, −1, $n$ and $-n$ are known as the **trivial divisors** of $n.$ A divisor of $n$ that is not a trivial divisor is known as a **non-trivial divisor** (or strict divisor). A nonzero integer with at least one non-trivial divisor is known as a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289), while the [units](wikipedia://en/289666) −1 and 1 and [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) have no non-trivial divisors.

There are [divisibility rules](wikipedia://en/991210) that allow one to recognize certain divisors of a number from the number's digits.

## Further notions and facts {#Further_notions_and_facts}

There are some elementary rules:

- If $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c,$ then $a \mid c;$ that is, divisibility is a [transitive relation](wikipedia://en/200463).
- If $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a,$ then $a = b$ or $a = -b.$ (That is, $a$ and $b$ are [associates](wikipedia://en/32981716).)
- If $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c,$ then $a \mid (b + c)$ holds, as does $a \mid (b - c).$ However, if $a \mid b$ and $c \mid b,$ then $(a + c) \mid b$ does *not* always hold (for example, $2\mid6$ and $3 \mid 6$ but 5 does not divide 6).
- $a \mid b \iff ac \mid bc$ for nonzero $c$. This follows immediately from writing $ka = b \iff kac = bc$.

If $a \mid bc,$ and $\gcd(a, b) = 1,$ then $a \mid c.$ This is called [Euclid's lemma](wikipedia://en/826617).

If $p$ is a prime number and $p \mid ab$ then $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b.$

A positive divisor of $n$ that is different from $n$ is called a **proper divisor** or an **aliquot part** of $n$ (for example, the proper divisors of 6 are 1, 2, and 3). A number that does not evenly divide $n$ but leaves a remainder is sometimes called an **aliquant part** of $n.$

An integer $n > 1$ whose only proper divisor is 1 is called a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). Equivalently, a prime number is a positive integer that has exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself.

Any positive divisor of $n$ is a product of [prime divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) of $n$, each raised to some power. This is a consequence of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556).

A number $n$ is said to be [perfect](wikipedia://en/23670) if it equals the sum of its proper divisors, [deficient](wikipedia://en/321843) if the sum of its proper divisors is less than $n,$ and [abundant](wikipedia://en/321831) if this sum exceeds $n.$

The total number of positive divisors of $n$ is a [multiplicative function](wikipedia://en/20059) $d(n),$ meaning that when two numbers $m$ and $n$ are [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime), then $d(mn)=d(m)\times d(n).$ For instance, $d(42) = 8 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = d(2) \times d(3) \times d(7)$; the eight divisors of 42 are 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 21 and 42. However, the number of positive divisors is not a totally multiplicative function: if the two numbers $m$ and $n$ share a common divisor, then it might not be true that $d(mn)=d(m)\times d(n).$ The sum of the positive divisors of $n$ is another multiplicative function $\sigma (n)$ (for example, $\sigma (42) = 96 = 3 \times 4 \times 8 = \sigma (2) \times \sigma (3) \times \sigma (7) = 1+2+3+6+7+14+21+42$). Both of these functions are examples of [divisor functions](wikipedia://en/407249).

If the [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) of $n$ is given by

$$n = p_1^{\nu_1} \, p_2^{\nu_2} \cdots p_k^{\nu_k}$$

then the number of positive divisors of $n$ is

$$d(n) = (\nu_1 + 1) (\nu_2 + 1) \cdots (\nu_k + 1),$$

and each of the divisors has the form

$$p_1^{\mu_1} \, p_2^{\mu_2} \cdots p_k^{\mu_k}$$

where $0 \le \mu_i \le \nu_i$ for each $1 \le i \le k.$

For every natural $n,$ $d(n) < 2 \sqrt{n}.$

Also,[^n6]

$$d(1)+d(2)+ \cdots +d(n) = n \ln n + (2 \gamma -1) n + O(\sqrt{n}),$$

where $\gamma$ is [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant). One interpretation of this result is that a randomly chosen positive integer *n* has an average number of divisors of about $\ln n.$ However, this is a result from the contributions of [numbers with "abnormally many" divisors](wikipedia://en/208732).

:::

## Division lattice {#Division_lattice}

In definitions that allow the divisor to be 0, the relation of divisibility turns the set $\mathbb{N}$ of [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) integers into a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) that is a [complete distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/469304). The largest element of this lattice is 0 and the smallest is 1. The meet operation **∧** is given by the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) and the join operation **∨** by the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961). This lattice is isomorphic to the [dual](wikipedia://en/600618) of the [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) of the infinite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) Z.
