# Horizontal coordinate system

*Image: Azimuth-Altitude schematic.svg.* Horizontal coordinates use a [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) centered on the observer. [Azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) is measured [eastward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East) from the [north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_north) point (sometimes from the [south](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South) point) of the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon); altitude is the angle above the horizon.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Horizontal coordinate system"}

The **horizontal coordinate system** is a [celestial coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system) that uses the observer's local [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) as the [fundamental plane](wikipedia://en/8081066) to define two angles of a [spherical coordinate system](wikipedia://en/29181): **altitude** and *[azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487)*. Therefore, the horizontal coordinate system is sometimes called the **az/el system**,[^o11] the **alt/az system**, or the **alt-azimuth system**, among others. In an [altazimuth mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altazimuth_mount) of a [telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope), the instrument's two axes follow altitude and azimuth.[^o9]

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

This [celestial coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system) divides the [sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky) into two [hemispheres](wikipedia://en/27859#Hemisphere): The upper hemisphere, where objects are above the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) and are visible, and the lower hemisphere, where objects are below the horizon and cannot be seen, since the Earth obstructs views of them. The [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) separating the hemispheres is the *celestial horizon*, which is defined as the great circle on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) whose plane is normal to the local [gravity vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_vector) (the [vertical direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_direction)).[^o10] In practice, the horizon can be defined as the plane [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to a quiet, liquid surface, such as a pool of [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28element%29), or by using a [bull's eye level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bull's_eye_level).[^o13] The pole of the upper hemisphere is called the *[zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith)* and the pole of the lower hemisphere is called the *[nadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadir)*.[^o12]

The following are two independent horizontal [angular coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_coordinate):

- Altitude (alt. or altitude angle), sometimes referred to as *elevation* (el. or *elevation angle*) or apparent height, is the angle between the object and the observer's local horizon. For visible objects, it is an angle between 0° and 90°.
- [Azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) (az.) is the angle of the object around the horizon, usually measured from [true north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_north) and increasing eastward. Exceptions are, for example, [ESO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Southern_Observatory)'s [FITS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FITS) convention where it is measured from the south and increasing westward, or the [FITS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FITS) convention of the [Sloan Digital Sky Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Digital_Sky_Survey) where it is measured from the south and increasing eastward.

A horizontal coordinate system should not be confused with a *[topocentric coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topocentric_coordinate_system)*. Horizontal coordinates define the observer's [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mental%29), but not location of the origin, while topocentric coordinates define the origin location, on the Earth's surface, in contrast to a [geocentric celestial system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_Celestial_Reference_System).

:::

## General features {#General_features}

The horizontal coordinate system is fixed to a location on Earth, not the stars. Therefore, the altitude and azimuth of an object in the sky changes with time, as the object appears to drift across the sky with [Earth's rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_rotation). In addition, since the horizontal system is defined by the observer's local horizon, the same object viewed from different locations on Earth at the same time will have different values of altitude and azimuth.

The [cardinal points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_direction) on the horizon have specific values of azimuth that are helpful references.

*Azimuth values for the cardinal directions*

::: {#o5 .table cue=table name="Azimuth values for the cardinal directions"}

| Cardinal point | Azimuth |
|---|---|
| North | 0° |
| East | 90° |
| South | 180° |
| West | 270° |

:::

Horizontal coordinates are very useful for determining the rise and set times of an object in the sky. When an object's altitude is 0°, it is on the horizon. If at that moment its altitude is increasing, it is rising, but if its altitude is decreasing, it is setting. However, all objects on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) are subject to [diurnal motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diurnal_motion), which always appears to be westward.

A northern observer can determine whether altitude is increasing or decreasing by instead considering the azimuth of the celestial object:

- If the azimuth is between 0° and 180° (north–east–south), the object is rising.
- If the azimuth is between 180° and 360° (south–west–north), the object is setting.

There are the following special cases:

- All directions are south when viewed from the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole), and all directions are north when viewed from the [South Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole), so the azimuth is undefined in both locations. When viewed from either pole, a star (or any object with fixed [equatorial coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_coordinates)) has constant altitude and thus [never rises or sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumpolar_star). The [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun), [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), and [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planets) can rise or set over the span of a year when viewed from the poles because their [declinations](wikipedia://en/8612) are constantly changing.
- When viewed from the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator), objects on the celestial poles stay at fixed points, perched on the horizon.
