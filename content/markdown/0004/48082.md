# Great circle

*Image: Great circle, axis, and poles.svg.* The great circle $g$ (green) lies in a plane through the sphere's center $O$ (black). The perpendicular line $a$ (purple) through the center is called the *axis* of $g$, and its two intersections with the sphere, $P$ and $P$ (red), are the *poles* of $g$. Any great circle $s$ (blue) through the poles is *secondary* to $g$.

*Image: Great circle hemispheres.png.* A great circle divides the sphere in two equal hemispheres.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Great circle"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **great circle** or **orthodrome** is the [circular](wikipedia://en/6220) [intersection](wikipedia://en/41604289) of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) and a **diametral plane**, which is a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) [passing through](wikipedia://en/387224) the sphere's [center point](wikipedia://en/4850775).[^o2][^o3]

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

Any [arc](wikipedia://en/5578920) of a great circle is a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) of the sphere, so that great circles in [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) are the natural analog of [straight lines](wikipedia://en/946975) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). For any pair of distinct non-[antipodal](wikipedia://en/367577) [points](wikipedia://en/593693) on the sphere, there is a unique great circle passing through both. (Every great circle through any point also passes through its antipodal point, so there are infinitely many great circles through two antipodal points.) The shorter of the two great-circle arcs between two distinct points on the sphere is called the *minor arc*, and is the shortest surface-path between them. Its [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) is the [great-circle distance](wikipedia://en/379733) between the points (the [intrinsic distance](wikipedia://en/475335) on a sphere), and is proportional to the [measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_measure) of the [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) formed by the two points and the center of the sphere.

A great circle is the largest circle that can be drawn on any given sphere. Any [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of any great circle coincides with a diameter of the sphere, and therefore every great circle is [concentric](wikipedia://en/19049087) with the sphere and shares the same [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815). Any other [circle of the sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_a_sphere) is called a [small circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_circle), and is the intersection of the sphere with a plane not passing through its center. Small circles are the spherical-geometry analog of circles in Euclidean space.

Every circle in Euclidean 3-space is a great circle of exactly one sphere.

The [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) bounded by a great circle is called a *great disk*: it is the intersection of a [ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_%28geometry%29) and a plane passing through its center. In higher dimensions, the great circles on the [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) are the intersection of the *n*-sphere with 2-planes that pass through the origin in the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{R}^{n + 1}$.

Half of a great circle may be called a *great [semicircle](wikipedia://en/617831)* (e.g., as in parts of a [meridian in astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28astronomy%29)).

## Derivation of shortest paths {#Derivation_of_shortest_paths}

To prove that the minor arc of a great circle is the shortest path connecting two points on the surface of a sphere, one can apply [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) to it.

Consider the class of all regular paths from a point $p$ to another point $q$. Introduce [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) so that $p$ coincides with the north pole. Any curve on the sphere that does not intersect either pole, except possibly at the endpoints, can be parametrized by

$$\theta = \theta(t),\quad \phi = \phi(t),\quad a\le t\le b$$

provided $\phi$ is allowed to take on arbitrary real values. The infinitesimal arc length in these coordinates is

$$ds=r\sqrt{\theta'^2+\phi'^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\, dt.$$

So the length of a curve $\gamma$ from $p$ to $q$ is a [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) of the curve given by

$$S[\gamma]=r\int_a^b\sqrt{\theta'^2+\phi'^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\, dt.$$

According to the [Euler–Lagrange equation](wikipedia://en/294995), $S[\gamma]$ is minimized if and only if

$$\frac{\sin^2\theta\phi'}{\sqrt{\theta'^2+\phi'^2\sin^2\theta}}=C,$$

where $C$ is a $t$-independent constant, and

$$\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta\phi'^2}{\sqrt{\theta'^2+\phi'^2\sin^2\theta}}=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\theta'}{\sqrt{\theta'^2+\phi'^2\sin^2\theta}}.$$

From the first equation of these two, it can be obtained that

$$\phi'=\frac{C\theta'}{\sin\theta\sqrt{\sin^2\theta-C^2}}.$$

Integrating both sides and considering the boundary condition, the real solution of $C$ is zero. Thus, $\phi'=0$ and $\theta$ can be any value between 0 and $\theta_0$, indicating that the curve must lie on a meridian of the sphere. In a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), this is

$$x\sin\phi_0 - y\cos\phi_0 = 0$$

which is a plane through the origin, i.e., the center of the sphere.

## Applications {#Applications}

Some examples of great circles on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) include the [celestial horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_horizon), the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681), and the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264). Great circles are also used as rather accurate approximations of [geodesics](wikipedia://en/36495578) on the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s surface for air or sea [navigation](wikipedia://en/4151763) (although it [is not a perfect sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_of_the_Earth)), as well as on spheroidal [celestial bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_bodies).

The [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) of the idealized earth is a great circle and any meridian and its opposite meridian form a great circle. Another great circle is the one that divides the [land and water hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_and_water_hemispheres). A great circle divides the earth into two [hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemisphere_of_the_Earth) and if a great circle passes through a point it must pass through its [antipodal point](wikipedia://en/367577).

The [Funk transform](wikipedia://en/23253927) integrates a function along all great circles of the sphere.
