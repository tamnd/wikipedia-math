# Tree (graph theory)

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Tree"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **tree** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) in which every pair of distinct [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) is connected by exactly one [path](wikipedia://en/638889), or equivalently, a [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph) [acyclic](wikipedia://en/168609) undirected graph.[^n1] A **forest** is an undirected graph in which any two vertices are connected by at most one path, or equivalently an acyclic undirected graph, or equivalently a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/9938459) of trees.[^n2]

:::

A directed tree,[^n3] oriented tree,[^o6][^o7] [polytree](wikipedia://en/1731488),[^o8] or singly connected network[^o9] is a [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002) (DAG) whose underlying undirected graph is a tree. A polyforest (or directed forest or oriented forest) is a directed acyclic graph whose underlying undirected graph is a forest.

The various kinds of [data structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_structures) referred to as [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_%28data_structure%29) in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) have [underlying graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying_graph) that are trees in graph theory, although such data structures are generally [rooted trees](wikipedia://en/48560#Rooted_tree). A rooted tree may be directed, called a directed rooted tree,[^o10][^o11] either making all its edges point away from the root—in which case it is called an [arborescence](wikipedia://en/13251077)[^n3][^o13] or out-tree[^n4][^o15]—or making all its edges point towards the root—in which case it is called an anti-arborescence[^o16] or in-tree.[^n4][^o18] A rooted tree itself has been defined by some authors as a directed graph.[^o19][^o20][^o21] A rooted forest is a disjoint union of rooted trees. A rooted forest may be directed, called a directed rooted forest, either making all its edges point away from the root in each rooted tree—in which case it is called a [branching](wikipedia://en/13251077) or out-forest—or making all its edges point towards the root in each rooted tree—in which case it is called an anti-branching or in-forest.

The term tree was coined in 1857 by the British mathematician [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034).[^o22]

::: {#o24 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Tree {#Tree}

A *tree* is an undirected graph $G$ that satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions:

- $G$ is [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph) and [acyclic](wikipedia://en/168609) (contains no cycles).
- $G$ is acyclic, and a simple cycle is formed if any [edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) is added to $G$.
- $G$ is connected, but would become [disconnected](wikipedia://en/1614492#Connected_graph) if any single edge is removed from $G$.
- $G$ is connected and the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{3}$ is not a [minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_%28graph_theory%29) of $G$.
- Any two vertices in $G$ can be connected by a unique [simple path](wikipedia://en/638889).

If $G$ has finitely many vertices, say $n$ of them, then the above statements are also equivalent to any of the following conditions:

- $G$ is connected and has $n - 1$ edges.
- $G$ is connected, and every [subgraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgraph_%28graph_theory%29) of $G$ includes at least one vertex with zero or one incident edges. (That is, $G$ is connected and [1-degenerate](wikipedia://en/27545816).)
- $G$ has no simple cycles and has $n - 1$ edges.

As elsewhere in graph theory, the [order-zero graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-zero_graph) (graph with no vertices) is generally not considered to be a tree: while it is vacuously connected as a graph (any two vertices can be connected by a path), it is not [0-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-connected) (or even (−1)-connected) in algebraic topology, unlike non-empty trees, and violates the "one more vertex than edges" relation. It may, however, be considered as a forest consisting of zero trees.

An internal vertex (or inner vertex) is a vertex of [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) at least 2. Similarly, an external vertex (or outer vertex, terminal vertex or leaf) is a vertex of degree 1. A branch vertex in a tree is a vertex of degree at least 3.[^o26]

An irreducible tree (or series-reduced tree) is a tree in which there is no vertex of degree 2 (enumerated at sequence in the [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OEIS)).[^n5]

::: {#o29 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Forest (graph theory)"}

### Forest {#Forest}

A forest is an undirected acyclic graph or equivalently a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/9938459) of trees. Trivially so, each connected component of a forest is a tree. As special cases, the order-zero graph (a forest consisting of zero trees), a single tree, and an edgeless graph, are examples of forests. Since for every tree $V - E = 1$, we can easily count the number of trees that are within a forest by subtracting the difference between total vertices and total edges. $V - E =$ number of trees in a forest.

:::

### Polytree {#Polytree}

A polytree[^o8] (or *directed tree*[^n3] or *oriented tree*[^o6][^o7] or *singly connected network*[^o9]) is a [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002) (DAG) whose underlying undirected graph is a tree. In other words, if we replace its directed edges with undirected edges, we obtain an undirected graph that is both connected and acyclic.

Some authors restrict the phrase "directed tree" to the case where the edges are all directed towards a particular vertex, or all directed away from a particular vertex (see [arborescence](wikipedia://en/13251077)).[^o32][^o33][^o34]

### Polyforest {#Polyforest}

A polyforest (or directed forest or oriented forest) is a directed acyclic graph whose underlying undirected graph is a forest. In other words, if we replace its directed edges with undirected edges, we obtain an undirected graph that is acyclic.

As with directed trees, some authors restrict the phrase "directed forest" to the case where the edges of each connected component are all directed towards a particular vertex, or all directed away from a particular vertex (see [branching](wikipedia://en/13251077)).[^o33]

::: {#o37 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Locally finite rooted tree"}

### Rooted tree {#Rooted_tree}

A rooted tree is a tree in which one vertex has been designated the root.[^n6] The edges of a rooted tree can be assigned a natural orientation, either away from or towards the root, in which case the structure becomes a directed rooted tree. When a directed rooted tree has an orientation away from the root, it is called an *arborescence*[^n3] or *out-tree*;[^n4] when it has an orientation towards the root, it is called an *anti-arborescence* or *in-tree*.[^n4] The tree-order is the [partial ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_ordering) on the vertices of a tree with $u < v$ only if the unique path from the root to $v$ passes through $u$. A rooted tree $T$ that is a [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraphs) of some graph $G$ is a [normal tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_tree) if the ends of every $T$-path in $G$ are comparable in this tree-order (Diestel 2005, 15). Rooted trees, often with an additional structure such as an ordering of the neighbors at each vertex, are a key data structure in computer science; see [tree data structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_data_structure).

In a context where trees typically have a root, a tree without any designated root is called a *free tree*.

A *labeled tree* is a tree in which each vertex is given a unique label. The vertices of a labeled tree on $n$ vertices (for nonnegative integers $n$) are typically given the labels $1, 2, \ldots, n$. A *[recursive tree](wikipedia://en/5140949)* is a labeled rooted tree where the vertex labels respect the tree order (i.e., if $u < v$ for two vertices $u$ and $v$, then the label of $u$ is smaller than the label of $v$).

In a rooted tree, the **parent** of a vertex $v$ is the vertex connected to $v$ on the [path](wikipedia://en/638889) to the root; every vertex has a unique parent, except the root has no parent.[^n6] A *child* of a vertex $v$ is a vertex of which $v$ is the parent.[^n6] An *ascendant* of a vertex $v$ is any vertex that is either the parent of $v$ or is (recursively) an ascendant of a parent of $v$. A *descendant* of a vertex $v$ is any vertex that is either a child of $v$ or is (recursively) a descendant of a child of $v$. A *sibling* to a vertex $v$ is any other vertex on the tree that shares a parent with $v$.[^n6] A *leaf* is a vertex with no children.[^n6] An *internal vertex* is a vertex that is not a leaf.[^n6]

The *height* of a vertex in a rooted tree is the length of the longest downward path to a leaf from that vertex. The *height* of the tree is the height of the root. The *depth* of a vertex is the length of the path to its root (*root path*). The depth of a rooted tree is the maximum depth of any vertex, which is the same as the height of the tree. Depth is commonly needed in the manipulation of the various self-balancing trees, [AVL trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AVL_tree) in particular. The root has depth zero, leaves have height zero, and a tree with only a single vertex (hence both a root and leaf) has depth and height zero. Conventionally, an empty tree (a tree with no vertices, if such are allowed) has depth and height −1.

A *[$k$-ary tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-ary_tree)* (for nonnegative integers $k$) is a rooted tree in which each vertex has at most $k$ children.[^o48] 2-ary trees are often called *[binary trees](wikipedia://en/4321)*, while 3-ary trees are sometimes called *[ternary trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternary_tree)*.

:::

### Ordered tree {#Ordered_tree}

An *ordered tree* (alternatively, *plane tree* or *positional tree*[^o50]) is a rooted tree in which an ordering is specified for the children of each vertex.[^n6][^o52] This is called a "plane tree" because an ordering of the children is equivalent to an embedding of the tree in the plane, with the root at the top and the children of each vertex lower than that vertex. Given an embedding of a rooted tree in the plane, if one fixes a direction of children, say left to right, then an embedding gives an ordering of the children. Conversely, given an ordered tree, and conventionally drawing the root at the top, then the child vertices in an ordered tree can be drawn left-to-right, yielding an essentially unique planar embedding.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- Every tree is a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431). A graph is bipartite if and only if it contains no cycles of odd length. Since a tree contains no cycles at all, it is bipartite.
- Every tree with only [countably](wikipedia://en/6026) many vertices is a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314).
- Every connected graph *G* admits a [spanning tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanning_tree_%28mathematics%29), which is a tree that contains every vertex of *G* and whose edges are edges of *G*. More specific types spanning trees, existing in every connected finite graph, include [depth-first search](wikipedia://en/97034) trees and [breadth-first search](wikipedia://en/97026) trees. Generalizing the existence of depth-first-search trees, every connected graph with only [countably](wikipedia://en/6026) many vertices has a [Trémaux tree](wikipedia://en/30247317).[^n7] However, some [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248)-[order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_graph) graphs do not have such a tree.[^n8]
- Every finite tree with *n* vertices, with *n* > 1, has at least two terminal vertices (leaves). This minimal number of leaves is characteristic of [path graphs](wikipedia://en/4587190); the maximal number, *n* − 1, is attained only by [star graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_graph). The number of leaves is at least the maximum vertex degree.
- For any three vertices in a tree, the three paths between them have exactly one vertex in common. More generally, a vertex in a graph that belongs to three shortest paths among three vertices is called a median of these vertices. Because every three vertices in a tree have a unique median, every tree is a [median graph](wikipedia://en/16970848).
- Every tree has a [center](wikipedia://en/10447275) consisting of one vertex or two adjacent vertices. The center is the middle vertex or middle two vertices in every longest path. Similarly, every *n*-vertex tree has a centroid consisting of one vertex or two adjacent vertices. In the first case removal of the vertex splits the tree into subtrees of fewer than *n*/2 vertices. In the second case, removal of the edge between the two centroidal vertices splits the tree into two subtrees of exactly *n*/2 vertices.
- The maximal cliques of a tree are precisely its edges, implying that the class of trees has [few cliques](wikipedia://en/75118858).

:::

## Enumeration {#Enumeration}

::: {#o60 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Labeled tree"}

### Labeled trees {#Labeled_trees}

[Cayley's formula](wikipedia://en/1266713) states that there are $n^{n-2}$ trees on $n$ labeled vertices. A classic proof uses [Prüfer sequences](wikipedia://en/1650168), which naturally show a stronger result: the number of trees with vertices $1, 2, \ldots, n$ of degrees $d_{1}, d_{2}, \ldots, d_{n}$ respectively, is the [multinomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/597998)

$${n - 2 \choose d_1 - 1, d_2 - 1, \ldots, d_n - 1}.$$

A more general problem is to count [spanning trees](wikipedia://en/455770) in an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph), which is addressed by the [matrix tree theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_tree_theorem). (Cayley's formula is the special case of spanning trees in a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816).) The similar problem of counting all the subtrees regardless of size is [\#P-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-P-complete) in the general case ((Jerrum 1994)).

:::

### Unlabeled trees {#Unlabeled_trees}

Counting the number of unlabeled free trees is a harder problem. No closed formula for the number $t(n)$ of trees with $n$ vertices [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [graph isomorphism](wikipedia://en/247577) is known. The first few values of $t(n)$ are

1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 3, 6, 11, 23, 47, 106, 235, 551, 1301, 3159, … .

(Otter 1948) proved the asymptotic estimate

$$t(n) \sim C \alpha^n n^{-5/2} \quad\text{as } n\to\infty,$$

with $C \approx 0.534949606...$ and $\alpha \approx 2.95576528565...$ . Here, the $\sim$ symbol means that

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{t(n)}{C \alpha^n n^{-5/2} } = 1.$$

This is a consequence of his asymptotic estimate for the number $r(n)$ of unlabeled rooted trees with $n$ vertices:

$$r(n) \sim D\alpha^n n^{-3/2} \quad\text{as } n\to\infty,$$

with $D \approx 0.43992401257...$ and the same $\alpha$ as above (cf. (Knuth 1997), chap. 2.3.4.4 and (Flajolet Sedgewick 2009), chap. VII.5, p. 475).

The first few values of $r(n)$ are[^o66]

1, 1, 2, 4, 9, 20, 48, 115, 286, 719, 1842, 4766, 12486, 32973, … .

These values are generated by the [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806):

$$r(n+1) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left( \sum_{j|k} j r(j) \right) r(n-k+1),$$

with $r(1)=1$, and where the inner sum is taken over all divisors $j$ of $k$.

::: {#o67 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof of recurrence relation for rooted unlabelled trees"}

This is given as an exercise by (Flajolet Sedgewick 2009), chap. I.5.2, p. 72, and is related to a similar recursion for the [partition function](wikipedia://en/312250). First, the [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) for the number of rooted unlabeled trees is given by a recursive [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303),

$$A(z) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n z^n = z\prod_{k=1}^\infty (1-z^k)^{-A_k},$$

defining $A_k\equiv r(k)$. Taking the logarithm, differentiating, and multiplying by $z$, we have

$$z\frac{A'(z)}{A(z)} = 1+\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{k A_k z^{k}}{1-z^k}.$$

Notice that the sum on the right-hand side is a [Lambert series](wikipedia://en/1367040), and thus

$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{k A_k z^{k-1}}{1-z^k} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \left( \sum_{j|k} j A_j \right) z^k.$$

By rearranging, we find

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty n A_n z^n = \sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n z^n +\left[\sum_{n=1}^\infty A_n z^n\right]\left[\sum_{k=1}^\infty \left( \sum_{j|k} j A_j \right) z^k\right].$$

Using the [Cauchy product](wikipedia://en/863791) and matching powers of $z$ immediately yields the desired expression,

$$(n-1)A_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \left( \sum_{j|k} j A_j \right) A_{n-k}.$$

:::

:::

## Types of trees {#Types_of_trees}

- A *[path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190)* (or *linear graph*) consists of $n$ vertices arranged in a line, so that vertices $i$ and $i + 1$ are connected by an edge for $i = 1, \ldots, n - 1$.
- A *[starlike tree](wikipedia://en/53828785)* consists of a central vertex called *root* and several path graphs attached to it. More formally, a tree is starlike if it has exactly one vertex of degree greater than 2.
- A *[star tree](wikipedia://en/19310129)* is a tree which consists of a single internal vertex (and $n - 1$ leaves). In other words, a star tree of order $n$ is a tree of order $n$ with as many leaves as possible.
- A *[caterpillar tree](wikipedia://en/27258886)* is a tree in which all vertices are within distance 1 of a central path subgraph.
- A *[lobster tree](wikipedia://en/8997770#Lobster)* is a tree in which all vertices are within distance 2 of a central path subgraph.
- A *regular tree* of degree $d$ is the infinite tree with $d$ edges at each vertex. These arise as the [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) of [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735), and in the theory of [Tits buildings](wikipedia://en/275015). In [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) they are known as *[Bethe lattices](wikipedia://en/1410431)*.
