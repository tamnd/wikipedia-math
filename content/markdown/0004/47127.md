# Baby boomers

2

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Baby boomers"}

**Baby boomers**, often shortened to **boomers**,[^o2] are the demographic [cohort](wikipedia://en/1509191) preceded by the [Silent Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Generation) and followed by [Generation X](wikipedia://en/11973). The [generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation) is often defined as people born from 1946 to 1964. These years span the [mid-20th-century baby boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-20th-century_baby_boom) that followed the end of World War II. The dates, the demographic context, and the cultural identifiers may vary by country.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o38] Most boomers are the parents of Generation X, [Millennials](wikipedia://en/149183) and [older Generation Z](wikipedia://en/67889883).[^o6][^o7]

:::

In [the West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world), boomers' childhoods in the 1950s and 1960s had significant reforms in education, both as part of the ideological confrontation that was the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War),[^o105][^o106] and as a continuation of the [interwar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period).[^o108][^o98] Theirs was a time of [economic prosperity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post–World_War_II_economic_expansion)[^o8] and rapid technological progress,[^o83] and many grew up expecting the world to improve with time.[^o9]

As this relatively large number of young people entered their teens and young adulthood, they, and those around them, created a very specific [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447) around their cohort,[^o10] and social movements brought about by their size in numbers. This impacted society's perception of the boomers and further popularized generational thinking, which was a relatively new phenomenon at the time.[^o185] In many countries, this period was one of deep political instability due to the postwar youth bulge.[^o151][^o115] In Europe and North America, older boomers came of age during the [counterculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture_of_the_1960s) of the mid-1960s to early 1970s[^o11] and its backlash.[^o151] In the U.S., younger boomers (or [Generation Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_Jones)) came of age in the ["malaise" years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973–1975_recession) of the mid-1970s to early 1980s.[^o12] In China, boomers lived through the [Cultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution) and were subject to the [one-child policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-child_policy) as adults.[^o13]

In the early 21st century, baby boomers in some developed countries are the single biggest cohort in their societies due to [sub-replacement fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-replacement_fertility) and population aging.[^o14] In the United States, despite their advancing age, they remain the second-largest age demographic after the millennials.[^o15]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The term *[baby boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baby_boom)* refers to a noticeable increase in the birth rate. The post-World War II population increase was described as a "boom" by various newspaper reporters, including Sylvia F. Porter in a column in the May 4, 1951, edition of the *[New York Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Post)*, based on the increase of 19,161,229, a 14.5% increase, in the population of the U.S. from census 1940 to census 1950.[^o17]

The first recorded use of "baby boomer" is in a January 1963 *[Daily Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_Press_%28Virginia%29)* article by Leslie J. Nason, describing a massive surge of college enrollments approaching as the oldest boomers were coming of age.[^o18][^o19] The *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* dates the modern meaning of the term to a January 23, 1970, article in *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post)*.[^o20]

## Date range and definitions {#Date_range_and_definitions}

*Image: 1910- Fertility rate - United States.svg.* The baby boom birth years from the mid 1940s through the mid 1960s extended an early-1940s increase in live births following lows in the Great Depression.[^o22]

A significant degree of consensus exists around the date range of the baby boomer cohort, with the generation considered to cover those born from 1946 to 1964 by organizations including the [Merriam-Webster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merriam-Webster) Online Dictionary,[^o23] [Pew Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center),[^o24] U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics,[^o25][^o26] [Federal Reserve Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Board),[^o27] [Australian Bureau of Statistics](wikipedia://en/4732815),[^o28] [Gallup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallup_%28company%29),[^o29] [YouGov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouGov),[^o30] Australia's Social Research Center,[^o31] and the [United States Census Bureau](wikipedia://en/57070),[^o32][^o33] as well as [Landon Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landon_Jones) in his 1980 book *Great Expectations: America and the Baby Boom Generation*.[^o34]

Among those who have delimited the baby boom period differently, authors [William Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Strauss) and [Neil Howe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Howe), in their 1991 book *Generations*, define the generation as those born from 1943 to 1960, who were too young to have any personal memory of World War II but old enough to remember the [postwar American High](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_United_States_%281945–1964%29) before [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy)'s [assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_John_F._Kennedy).[^o35]

[David Foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Foot), author of *Boom, Bust and Echo: Profiting from the Demographic Shift in the 21st Century* (1997), defined a Canadian boomer as someone born from 1947 to 1966, the years in which more than 400,000 babies were born. He acknowledges, though, that this is a demographic definition, and that culturally, it may not be as clear-cut.[^o36] Doug Owram argues that the Canadian boom took place from 1946 to 1962, but that culturally, boomers everywhere were born between the late war years and about 1955 or 1956. Those born in the 1960s might feel disconnected from the cultural identifiers of the earlier boomers.[^o37]

French politician [Michèle Delaunay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michèle_Delaunay) in her book *Le Fabuleux Destin des Baby-Boomers* (2019), places the baby-boom generation in France between 1946 and 1973, and in Spain between 1958 and 1975.[^o38] Another French academic, Jean-François Sirinelli, in an earlier study, *Les Baby-Boomers: Une génération 1945-1969* (2007) denotes the generation span between 1945 and 1969.[^o39]

The [Office for National Statistics](wikipedia://en/324029) has described the UK as having had two baby booms in the middle of the 20th century, one in the years immediately after World War II and one around the 1960s with a noticeably lower birth rate (but still significantly higher than that seen in the 1930s or later in the '70s) during part of the 1950s.[^o40] [Bernard Salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Salt) places the Australian baby boom between 1946 and 1961.[^o5][^o41]

In the US, the generation can be segmented into two broadly defined cohorts: the "leading-edge boomers" (or "early boomers") are individuals born between 1946 and 1955, those who came of age during the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War) and Civil Rights eras.[^o42] This group represents slightly more than half of the generation, or roughly 38,002,000 people. Roughly 1 in 10 baby boomer men served in the [military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Armed_Forces), and some of them were deployed to Vietnam.[^o43][^o44] The other half of the generation, usually called "[Generation Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_Jones)" (also called "late boomers" or "trailing-edge boomers"), was born between 1956 and 1964, and came of age during the [Watergate scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watergate_scandal) and [economic struggles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973–1975_recession) of the 1970s,[^o42][^o45][^o46][^o47][^o48] partly caused by energy shortages.[^o49] This second cohort includes about 37,818,000 people.[^o50] Others use the term Generation Jones to refer to a [cusp generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cusp_generation), which includes those born in the latter half of the Baby Boomers to the early years of Generation X, with a typical range of 1954 to 1965.[^o51][^o52][^o53]

## Demographics {#Demographics}

### Asia {#Asia}

During the time of the [Great Leap Forward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward), the [Chinese Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party) (CCP) encouraged couples to have as many children as possible because it believed a growing labor force was needed for national development along socialist lines.[^o148] China's baby-boom cohort is the largest in the world. According to journalist and photographer Howard French, who spent many years in China, many Chinese neighborhoods were, by the mid-2010s, disproportionately filled with the elderly, to whom the Chinese themselves referred as a "lost generation", who grew up during the Cultural Revolution, when higher education was discouraged and large numbers of people were sent to the countryside for political reasons. As China's baby boomers retire in the late-2010s and onward, the people replacing in the workforce will be a much smaller cohort due to the one-child policy. Consequently, China's central government faces a stark economic trade-off between "cane and butter"—how much to spend on social welfare programs such as state pensions to support the elderly and how much to spend in the military to achieve the nation's geopolitical objectives.[^o13]

According to the [National Development Council of Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Development_Council_%28Taiwan%29), the nation's population could start shrinking by 2022 and the number of people of working age could fall 10% by 2027. About half of Taiwanese would be aged 50 or over by 2034.[^o56] At the current rate, Taiwan is set to transition from an aged to super-aged society, where 21% of the population is over 65 years of age, in eight years, compared to seven years for Singapore, eight years for South Korea, 11 years for Japan, 14 for the United States, 29 for France, and 51 for the United Kingdom.[^o57]

Japan at present has one of the oldest populations in the world and persistently subreplacement fertility, currently 1.4 per woman. Japan's population peaked in 2017. Forecasts suggest that the elderly will make up 35% of Japan's population by 2040.[^o58] As of 2018, Japan was already a super-aged society,[^o59] with 27% of its people being older than 65 years.[^o60] According to government data, Japan's total fertility rate was 1.43 in 2017.[^o61] According to the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, University of Washington, Japan has one of the oldest populations in the world, with a median age of 47 years in 2017.[^o62]

A baby boom occurred in the aftermath of the Korean War, and the government subsequently encouraged people to have no more than two children per couple. Although South Korean fertility remained above replacement well in to the 1970s,[^o63] its fertility has since been declining due to declining economic prospects for young people, and women's liberation. In recent times, the South Korean government has since made many efforts to increase the national fertility rate through subsidies; however, these efforts have failed, and South Korea retains one of the world's lowest fertility rates, with a [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) of less than 1 child per woman.[^o64]

### Europe {#Europe}

From about 1750 to 1950, Western Europe transitioned from having both high birth and death rates to low birth and death rates. By the late 1960s or 1970s, the average woman had fewer than two children, and although demographers at first expected a "correction", such a rebound never came. Despite a bump in the total fertility rates of some European countries in the very late 20th century (the 1980s and 1990s), especially France and Scandinavia, they never returned to replacement level; the bump was largely due to older women realizing their dreams of motherhood. Member states of the [European Economic Community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Economic_Community) saw a steady increase in not just divorce and out-of-wedlock births between 1960 and 1985 but also falling fertility rates. In 1981, a survey of countries across the industrialized world found that while more than half of people aged 65 and over thought that women needed children to be fulfilled, only 35% of those between the ages of 15 and 24 (younger baby boomers and older generation Xers) agreed. Falling fertility was due to urbanization and decreased infant mortality rates, which diminished the benefits and increased the costs of raising children. In other words, investing more in fewer children became more economically sensible, as economist [Gary Becker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Becker) argued. (This is the first demographic transition.) By the 1960s, people began moving from traditional and communal values towards more expressive and individualistic outlooks due to access to and aspiration of higher education, and to the spread of lifestyle values once practiced only by a tiny minority of cultural elites. (This is the [second demographic transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographic_transition#Second_demographic_transition).)[^o66]

At the start of the 21st century, Europe has an [aging population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_ageing). This problem is especially acute in Eastern Europe, whereas in Western Europe, it is alleviated by international immigration.[^o67] Researches by demographers and political scientists [Eric Kaufmann](wikipedia://en/52270193), [Roger Eatwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Eatwell), and [Matthew Goodwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Goodwin_%28author%29) suggest that such immigration-induced ethnodemographic change is one of the key reasons behind public backlash in the form of [national populism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-wing_populism) across the rich liberal democracies, an example of which is the [2016 United Kingdom European Union membership referendum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_Kingdom_European_Union_membership_referendum) (Brexit).[^o68]

In 2018, 19.70% of the population of the European Union (EU) were 65 or older.[^o60] The median age was 43 in 2019, and was about 29 in the 1950s. Europe had significant population growth in the late 20th century. However, Europe's growth is projected to halt by the early 2020s due to falling fertility rates and an aging population. In 2015, a woman living in the EU had on average 1.5 children, down from 2.6 in 1960. Although the EU continues to experience a net influx of immigrants, this is not enough to balance out the low fertility rates.[^o69] In 2017, the median age was 53.1 years in Monaco, 45 in Germany and Italy, and 43 in Greece, Bulgaria, and Portugal, making them some of the oldest countries in the world besides Japan and Bermuda. They are followed by Austria, Croatia, Latvia, Lithuania, Slovenia, and Spain, whose median age was 43.[^o62]

### North America {#North_America}

By the mid-2010s, sub-replacement fertility and growing life expectancy meant that Canada had an aging population.[^o71] Statistics Canada reported in 2015 that for the first time in Canadian history, more people were aged 65 and over than people below the age of 15. One in six Canadians was above the age of 65 in July 2015.[^o72] Projections by Statistics Canada suggest this gap will only increase in the next 40 years. Economist and demographer David Foot from the University of Toronto told CBC that policymakers have ignored this trend for decades. With the massive baby-boom generation entering retirement, economic growth will be slower and demand for social support will rise. This will significantly alter the Canadian economy. Nevertheless, Canada remained the second-youngest G7 nation, as of 2015.[^o71]

*Image: US living adult generations.png.* U.S. adult demographic cohorts in 2019

American parents of the postwar era married earlier and had more children than their predecessors.[^o85] Pediatrician [Benjamin Spock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Spock) published his *[The Common Sense Book of Baby and Child Care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Common_Sense_Book_of_Baby_and_Child_Care)* in 1946, just in time of the coming population boom.[^o85] In addition, the ideals of universalism and cosmopolitanism became institutionalized after the Second World War, and ethnic minorities started moving towards institutional parity with the once-dominant Anglo-Protestants.[^o73] The [Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_and_Nationality_Act_of_1965) (also known as the Hart–Celler Act), passed at the urging of President Lyndon B. Johnson, abolished national quotas for immigrants, and replaced it with a system that admits a fixed number of persons per year based in qualities such as skills and the need for refuge. Immigration subsequently surged from elsewhere in North America (especially Canada and Mexico), Asia, Central America, and the West Indies.[^o74] By the mid-1980s, most immigrants originated from Asia and Latin America. Some were refugees from Vietnam, Cuba, Haiti, and other parts of the Americas, while others came illegally by crossing the long and largely undefended U.S.-Mexican border. Although Congress offered amnesty to "undocumented immigrants" who had been in the country for a long time and attempted to penalize employers who recruited them, their influx continued. At the same time, the postwar baby boom and subsequently falling fertility rate seemed to jeopardize America's Social Security system as the baby boomers retire in the early 21st century.[^o75]

Using their own definition of baby boomers as people born between 1946 and 1964 and U.S. census data, the Pew Research Center estimated 71.6 million boomers were in the United States as of 2019.[^o76] The age wave theory suggests an [economic slowdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_slowdown) when the boomers started retiring during 2007–2009.[^o77] In 2018, though, 29% of people aged 65–72 in the United States remained active in the labor force, according to the Pew Research Center. This trend follows from the general expectation of Americans to work after the age of 65. The baby boomers who chose to remain in the work force after the age of 65 tended to be university graduates, whites, and residents of the big cities. That the boomers maintained a relatively high labor participation rate made economic sense because the longer they postpone retirement, the more Social Security benefits they could claim, once they finally retire.[^o212]

## In childhood and youth {#In_childhood_and_youth}

### Standards of living and economic prospects {#Standards_of_living_and_economic_prospects}

After the Second World War, the United States offered massive financial assistance to Western European nations in the form of the [Marshall Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Plan) to rebuild themselves and to extend U.S. economic and political influence. The Soviet Union did the same for Eastern Europe with the [Council for Mutual Economic Assistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comecon).[^o81][^o14] Full employment was reached on both sides of the Atlantic by the 1960s. The [automobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car), already a common sight in North America, became so in Western Europe, and to a lesser extent, Eastern Europe and Latin America. At the same time, governments around the world undertook the construction or expansion of [public transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_transport) networks at a rate never before seen.[^o89]

Many items previously deemed luxurious, such as the [laundry machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washing_machine), the [dishwasher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dishwasher), the [refrigerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigerator), and the [telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone), entered mass production for the average consumer. The average person could live like the upper class in the previous generation. Technological advances made before, during, and after the war, such as plastics, television, [magnetic tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_tape), [transistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor), [integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit), and [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser), played a key role in the tremendous improvements in the standards of living for the average citizen in the developed world.[^o89][^o82] This was a time of optimism, economic prosperity, and a growing middle class.[^o83] In some instances, the rate of technological change was so rapid even when compared to optimistic projections, so much so that some social theorists of the day warned of boredom for the [housewife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housewife).[^o82] In reality, it paved the way for a more individualistic culture and women's emancipation, something the Baby Boomers would push for when they came of age during the late 1960s and 1970s. It was also one of the reasons why the baby boom lasted for as long as it did; housekeeping and child-rearing became less onerous for women.[^o83] Nevertheless, after 1945, because [child labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_labour) had been virtually eradicated in the West, married women from families of modest means had to join the work force.[^o95] As historians [Louise Tilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_A._Tilly) and [Joan Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Wallach_Scott) explain, "in the past children had worked so that their mothers could remain at home fulfilling domestic and reproductive responsibilities. Now when families needed additional income, mothers worked instead of children."[^o84]

Demand for housing exploded. Governments both in the East and the West massively subsidized housing with many [public housing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_housing) projects in urban areas in the form of high-rise apartment buildings. In many cases, this came at the cost of destroying historical sites.[^o89] The United States saw the beginning of mass [suburbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburbanization), a phenomenon not seen in European democracies, as the long commutes were not affordable.[^o85] [suburban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suburb) communities began developing their own entertainment quarters and [shopping malls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shopping_mall).[^o95]

Public health improved, too, with vaccination programs playing an important role. In the United Kingdom, for example, the introduction of vaccines against [poliomyelitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poliomyelitis), [measles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measles), and [pertussis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whooping_cough) (whooping cough) in the 1950s and 1960s caused infection rates to plummet, albeit with some upticks due to [vaccine hesitancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccine_hesitancy).[^o86] In the United States, vaccination against measles resulted in not only falling childhood mortality rates but also other positive life outcomes such as rising family income.[^o87] In the West, average life expectancy increased by about seven years between the 1930s and 1960s.[^o89] In developed countries, boomers reached puberty at an earlier age than previous generations.[^o185]

The enormous baby boom enabled a vast consumer market and popular culture centered about the preferences and desires of the young.[^o88] Prosperity was taken for granted. Indeed, for older boomers who came of age in the 1960s, the interwar experience of mass unemployment and stable or falling prices was confined to the history books, and [full employment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_employment) and inflation were the norm. Those with higher standards of living and educational levels were often the most demanding of betterment.[^o151][^o89] The new-found wealth allowed many Western governments to finance generous welfare programs. By the 1970s, all industrialized capitalist nations had become welfare states. But when the 'Golden Age' came to an end, such government largess proved problematic.[^o89]

The 'Golden Age' finally petered out in the 1970s,[^o14] as automation started eating away jobs at the low to medium skill levels,[^o95] and as younger boomers ([Generation Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_Jones)) entered the workplace en masse.[^o90] In the United States, at least, the onset of a recession—as defined by the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Economic_Research)—typically occurred within a few years of a peak in the rate of change of the young-adult population, both positive and negative, and indeed, the recession of the mid-1970s took place shortly after older boomers' growth peak in the late 1960s.[^o90] Western capitalist nations slid into recessions during the mid-1970s to early 1980s. Although the collective GDP of these nations continued to grow until the early 1990s, so much so that they became much wealthier and more productive by that date, unemployment, especially youth unemployment, exploded in many of them. In the European Community, the average unemployment rate stood at 9.2% by the late 1980s, despite the deceleration of population growth. Generous welfare programs alleviated the potential of social unrest, though Western governments found themselves squeezed by a combination of falling tax revenue and high state spending.[^o91] Members of the [Silent Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Generation) found themselves in an abundance of employment opportunities as they entered the workforce in the 1950s. In fact, they could expect to achieve parity with their fathers' wages at the entrance level. This, however, was not the case for Jonesers. By the mid-1980s, younger boomers could only expect to make a third of what their fathers made as new entrants to the labor force.[^o90]

The pace of economic growth in the 1960s was understood to be unprecedented. In the long-term view, though, it was just another upswing in the [Kondratiev cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kondratiev_wave) (see figure), much like the mid-[Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) boom or the *[Belle Époque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belle_Époque)* from around 1850 to 1873 in Britain and France, respectively. Globally, agricultural output doubled between the early 1950s and early 1980s—more than that in North America, Western Europe, and East Asia—while the fishing industry tripled its catches.[^o89]

*Image: Kondratieff Wave-HQ.png.* Schematic diagram of the Kondratiev wave

Communist nations, especially the Soviet Union and the Eastern European states, grew considerably, too. Heretofore agrarian states such as Bulgaria and Romania began to industrialize. But by the 1960s, their growth faltered compared to the capitalist industrialized countries.[^o89] By the 1980s, the economies of the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe became stagnant. This was, however, not the case in the newly industrializing economies such as China or South Korea, whose process of industrialization began much later, nor was it in Japan.[^o91]

The developing world achieved significant growth during the 1950s and 1960s, though it never quite reached the level of affluence of industrialized societies. The populations of Africa, Asia, and Latin America boomed between 1950 and 1975. Food production comfortably outpaced population growth. As a consequence, this period saw no major famines other than cases due to armed conflict and politics, which did happen in Communist China.[^o89] People who faced the [Great Famine of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Chinese_Famine) (1958–1961) as toddlers were noticeably shorter than those who did not. The Great Famine killed up to 30 million people and massively reduced China's economic output.[^o92] Chairman [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong) introduced a plan for the rapid industrialization of his country, the [Great Leap Forward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Leap_Forward). Monetary income was replaced by six basic services: food, healthcare, education, haircuts, funerals, and movies. Mao's plan was quickly abandoned, not just because it failed, but also because of the Great Famine. Yet despite the disastrous results of [Maoist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maoism) policies, by the standards of the developing world, China was not doing so poorly. By the mid-1970s, China's food consumption measured in calories was just above the global median and the nation's life expectancy grew steadily, interrupted only by the famine years.[^o93]

Between 1960 and 1975, the Chinese mainland's growth was fast, but lagged behind the [growth of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_economic_miracle) and the rise of the [Four Asian Tigers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Asian_Tigers) ([South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korea), [Taiwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiwan), [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong), and [Singapore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore)) grew even faster.[^o93]

### Education {#Education}

Universal literacy was a major goal for practically all governments in the developing world and many made significant progress towards this end, even if their 'official' statistics were questionably optimistic.[^o95] In the 1980s, [James R. Flynn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Flynn_%28academic%29) examined psychometric data and discovered evidence that the [IQ scores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IQ_score) of Americans were increasing significantly between the early 1930s and late 1970s. On average, younger cohorts scored higher than their elders. This was confirmed by later studies and on data in other countries; the discovery became known as the [Flynn effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flynn_effect).[^o96] During the postwar era, the importance of modern mathematics—especially mathematical logic, optimization, and numerical analysis—was acknowledged for its usefulness during the war. From this sprang proposals for reforms in mathematics education. The international movement to bring about such reforms was launched in the late 1950s, with heavy French influence. In France, they also grew out of a desire to bring the subject as it was taught in schools closer to the research done by pure mathematicians, particularly the [Nicholas Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) school, which emphasized an austere and abstract style of doing mathematics, [axiomatization](wikipedia://en/188401).[^o97] Up until the 1950s, the purpose of primary education was to prepare students for life and future careers. This changed in the 1960s. A commission headed by [André Lichnerowicz](wikipedia://en/2389188) was established to work out the details of the desired reforms in mathematics education. At the same time, the French government mandated that the same courses be taught to all schoolchildren, regardless of their career prospects and aspirations. Thus the same highly abstract courses in mathematics were taught to not just those willing and able to pursue university studies but also those who left school early to join the workforce. From elementary school to the [French Baccalaureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baccalauréat), [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) and [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) were de-emphasized in favor of [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) and [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). This conception of mass public education was inherited from the interwar period and was taken for granted; the model for the elites was to be applied to all segments of society. But by the early 1970s, the Commission ran into problems. Mathematicians, physicists, members of professional societies, economists, and industrial leaders criticized the reforms as being suitable for neither schoolteachers nor students. Many teachers were ill-prepared and ill-equipped. One member of the Lichnerowicz Commission asked, "Should we teach outdated mathematics to less intelligent children?" Lichnerowicz resigned and the commission was disbanded in 1973.[^o98] In the United States, the "[New Math](wikipedia://en/404988)" initiative—under which students received lessons in set theory, which is what mathematicians actually use to construct the set of real numbers, something advanced undergraduates learned in a course on [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478),[^o99] and arithmetic with bases other than ten[^o100]—was similarly unsuccessful,[^o106] and was widely criticized by not just parents,[^o106] but also STEM experts.[^o101][^o102][^o103] Nevertheless, the influence of the Bourbaki school in mathematics education lived on, as the Soviet mathematician [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490) recalled in a 1995 interview.[^o104]

Before World War II, the share of university-educated people in even the most advanced of industrialized nations, except the United States, a world leader in post-secondary education, was negligible. After the war, the number of university students skyrocketed, not just in the West, but also among developing countries as well. In Europe, between 1960 and 1980, the number of university students increased by a factor of four to five in West Germany, Ireland, and Greece, a factor of five to seven in Finland, Iceland, Sweden and Italy, and a factor of seven to nine in Spain and Norway.[^o95] In West Germany, the number of university students steadily grew in the 1960s despite the construction of the Berlin Wall, which prevented East German students from coming. By 1966, West Germany had a grand total of 400,000 students, up from 290,000 in 1960.[^o160] In the Republic of Korea (South Korea), the number of university students as a share of the population grew from around 0.8% to 3% between 1975 and 1983. Families typically considered higher education to be the gateway towards a higher social status and higher income, or, in short, a better life; as such they pushed their children to university whenever possible. In general, the postwar economic expansion made it possible for a larger percentage of the population to send their children to university as full-time students. Moreover, many Western welfare states, starting with U.S. government subsidies to military veterans who wished to attend university, provided financial aid in one form or another to university students, though they were still expected to live frugally. In most countries, with the notable exceptions of Japan and the U.S., universities were more likely to be public rather than private. The total number of universities worldwide more than doubled in the 1970s. The rise of university campuses and university towns was a culturally and politically novel phenomenon, and one that would usher in the political turbulence of the late 1960s around the world.[^o95]

After World War I, the goal of primary education in the United States shifted from using schools to realize social change to employing them to promote emotional development. While it might have helped students improve their mental welfare, critics pointed to the de-emphasizing of traditional academic subjects leading to poor work habits and plain ignorance. Such a system became less and less tenable because society increasingly demanded rigorous education. In his book *[The American High School Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American_High_School_Today)* (1959), former Harvard president [James B. Conant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_B._Conant) laid out his critique of the status quo. In particular, he pointed to the failure of English classes in teaching proper grammar and composition, the neglect of foreign languages, and the inability to meet the needs of gifted and slow students alike. People like Conant rose to prominence due to the successful launch of the *[Sputnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1)* satellite by the Soviet Union in 1957.[^o108] As a matter of fact, the passages of the artificial satellite were recorded by the Boston newspapers and viewed with the naked eye from rooftops.[^o105]

*Image: University of Chicago Law School, 1955-63. Law School - 00441v.jpg.* Large numbers of Americans pursued higher education after World War II. *Pictured*: the [University of Chicago Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago_Law_School) (1955–63)

This surprising Soviet success demonstrated to the Americans that their education system had fallen behind.[^o106] *Life* magazine reported that three quarters of American high-school students took no physics at all. The U.S. government realized it needed thousands of scientists and engineers to match the might of its ideological rival. On President [Dwight D. Eisenhower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwight_D._Eisenhower)'s direct orders, science education underwent major reforms and the federal government started pouring enormous sums of money into not just education but also research and development. Private institutions, such as the Carnegie Corporation and the Ford Foundation provided funding for education, too.[^o108][^o107] Authors felt inspired to cater to the physics textbook market, and one of the results was the *[Berkeley Physics Course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Physics_Course)*, a series for undergraduates influenced by MIT's [Physical Science Study Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Science_Study_Committee), formed right before the launch of *Sputnik*. One of the most famous of textbooks from the *Berkeley* series is *[Electricity and Magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_and_Magnetism_%28book%29)* by Nobel laureate [Edward Mills Purcell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Mills_Purcell), which has gone through multiple editions and remains in print in the twenty-first century.[^o105]

In any case, academic performance reclaimed its importance in the United States. At the same time, large numbers of young people desired to go to college due to population growth and the needs of society for specialized skills. Prestigious institutions were able to handpick the very best of students from massive pools of applications and consequently became the training centers for a growing class of cognitive elites. Indeed, the share of college graduates among 23-year-olds steadily rose after World War II, first due to veterans returning to civilian life and later due to people born after the war. In 1950, there were 2.6 million students in American institutions of higher learning. By 1970, that number was 8.6 million, and by 1980, it became 12 million.[^o108] In the 1970s, there was a seemingly infinite number of Baby Boomers applying for admissions at institutions of higher learning in the U.S., so much so that many schools became extremely difficult to get into. This cooled off by the 1980s, though.[^o109] In the end, about a quarter of Baby Boomers had at least a bachelor's degree.[^o110] More women earned university degrees than ever before, and became professionals at an unprecedented rate.[^o111] Because so many Baby Boomers pursued higher education, costs started to rise, making the Silent Generation the last cohort to benefit from tuition-free public universities anywhere in the United States.[^o83] The number of women pursuing higher education grew in other countries, too, including those on the other side of the Iron Curtain.[^o95]

American physicist [Herbert Callen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Callen) observed that even though a survey conducted by the American Physical Society Committee on the Applications of Physics reported (in the *Bulletin of the APS*) in 1971 that industry leaders desired a greater emphasis on more practical subjects, such as thermodynamics as opposed to the more abstract statistical mechanics, academia subsequently went the other way.[^o112] British physicist [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742), who had relocated to the United States in the 1970s, opined to his colleagues he doubted the wisdom of educating so many undergraduates in science when so many of them had neither the interest nor the aptitude.[^o113] Having a youth bulge can be seen as one factor among many in explaining social unrest and uprisings in society.[^o114] Quantitative historian [Peter Turchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Turchin) noted intensifying competition among graduates, whose numbers were larger than what the economy could absorb, a phenomenon he termed [elite overproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elite_overproduction), led to political polarization, social fragmentation, and even violence as many became disgruntled with their dim prospects despite having attained a high level of education. Income inequality, stagnating or declining real wages, and growing public debt were contributory factors. Turchin argued that having a [youth bulge](wikipedia://en/989011#Youth_bulge) and massive young population with university degrees were the key reasons for the instability of the 1960s and 1970s and predicted that the 2020s would see the pattern repeat itself.[^o115]

Because the baby boomers were a huge demographic cohort, when they entered the workforce they took up all the jobs they could find, including those below their skill levels. As a result, wages were depressed and many households needed two streams of income in order to pay their bills.[^o14] In China, even though the Central Government made plans for increasing the people's access to education, school attendance, including at the elementary level, dropped by 25 million due to the Great Famine, and another fifteen million due to the Cultural Revolution. Yet despite all this, by the mid-1970s almost all Chinese children went to elementary school (96%), up six times from the early 1950s. Although Chinese figures for the people deemed illiterate or semi-literate appeared high—a quarter of Chinese over twelve years of age fell into these categories in 1984—the peculiarities of the Chinese language made direct comparisons with other countries difficult.[^o93]

Ignoring the skepticism of his comrades, Chairman Mao introduced the [Hundred Flowers Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Flowers_Campaign) of 1956-57 encouraging intellectuals and elites from the old era to share their thoughts freely with the slogan, "Let a hundred flowers bloom, let a hundred school of thoughts contend". Mao thought that his revolution had already transformed Chinese society for good. The result was an outburst of ideas deemed unacceptable by the CCP and above all, Mao himself, which fueled his distrust of intellectuals. Mao responded with the Cultural Revolution, which saw the intelligentsia being sent to the countryside for manual labor. Post-secondary education was almost completely abolished in mainland China. There existed only 48,000 university students in China in 1970, including 4,260 in the natural sciences and 90 in the social sciences, 23,000 technical school students in 1969, and 15,000 teachers in training in 1969. Data on post-graduate students was not available, presumably because there were no such students. China had a population of around 830 million in 1970.[^o93]

In China, the baby boomers grew up during the Cultural Revolution, when institutions of higher learning were closed. As a consequence, when China introduced some elements of capitalist reforms in the late 1970s, most of this cohort found itself at a severe disadvantage as people were unable to take the various jobs that became vacant.[^o13]

### Cultural and sociopolitical identities {#Cultural_and_sociopolitical_identities}

#### Popular culture {#Popular_culture}

*Image: Family watching television 1958 cropped2.jpg.* An American family gathering to watch television (1958)

In the United States, a new doll named [Barbie Millicent Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbie) was brought to market in 1959, proving to be an icon of girlhood.[^o85] The [television set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_set) was available for sale right after the Second World War, and quickly became a major source of entertainment for families.[^o85] [Soap operas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_opera)—characterized by melodramatic plots focused on interpersonal affairs and cheap production value—are a genre that was named after being sponsored by soap and detergent companies. They proved to be popular in the 1930s on radio and migrated to television in the 1950s. Again successful in the new broadcast environment, many of their viewers from the 1950s and 1960s grew old with them and introduced them to their children and grandchildren. In the United States, soap operas often dealt with the various social issues of the day, such as abortion, race relations, sexual politics, and inter-generational conflicts,[^o118] and they often took positions that were, by the standards of their day, progressive.[^o119] In Europe, and especially in the United Kingdom, the top soap operas typically featured working- or middle-class people, and most soap operas promoted post-war [social-democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democracy) values.[^o120]

Following the Second World War, the United States was not just a land of peace and prosperity but also of anxiety and fear of cultural deviancy and ideological subversion. And one victim of said [moral panic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_panic) was comic books.[^o124] Some well-established fictional characters were attacked directly; Batman and Robin (Dick Grayson) were accuse of homosexuality and Wonder Woman of [sexual deviancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_deviancy).[^o127] Comic books were blamed for the rise in [juvenile delinquency in that country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juvenile_delinquency_in_the_United_States) because a number of juvenile offenders admitted to reading them.[^o121] This culminated in the book *[Seduction of the Innocent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seduction_of_the_Innocent)* (1954) by [Fredric Wertham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredric_Wertham),[^o121] causing a decline in the comics industry.[^o121] To address public concerns, in 1954 the [Comics Code Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comics_Code_Authority) was created to regulate and curb violence in comics, marking the start of a new era, the [Silver Age of American comics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Age_of_Comic_Books), which lasted until the early 1970s.[^o122] Unlike those of the Golden Age, stories from the Silver Age moved away from horror, and crime.[^o123] Plots shifted towards romance and [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction), deemed acceptable by the Code.[^o124] For a variety of stories and characters, scientific-sounding concepts replaced magic and gods.[^o125] Initially, many plots were [escapist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escapism) fantasies and reflected the cultural zeitgeist of the day, featuring traditional [family values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_values) (with an emphasis on gender roles and marriage) as well as gender equality,[^o126] But later, in parallel with the fomenting counterculture of the 1960s, comic books began addressing the social issues of the day, such as the civil-rights movement.[^o127] Originally aimed at children, they soon attracted growing numbers of young adults; that the younger Baby Boomers had their imaginations fed by superhero comics ensured that this genre of fiction would remain in American popular culture well into the twenty-first century.[^o127] A number of fictional characters invented during the Silver Age—such as Batgirl (Barbara Gordon), Spider-Man (Peter Parker), and the X-Men—continue to be popular decades later.[^o127]

[J. D. Salinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._D._Salinger)'s *[The Catcher in the Rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Catcher_in_the_Rye)* (1951) attracted the attention of adolescent readers even though it was written for adults. The themes of adolescent angst and alienation in the novel have become synonymous with [young-adult literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_adult_literature).[^o128] But according to [Michael Cart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Cart), it was the 1960s that saw the maturing of novels for teenagers and young adults.[^o129] One early example of this genre was [S. E. Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._E._Hinton)'s *[The Outsiders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Outsiders_%28novel%29)* (1967). The novel features a truer, darker side of adolescent life that was not often represented in works of fiction of the time.[^o130][^o131] Written during high school and written when Hinton was only 16,[^o132] *The Outsiders* also lacked the nostalgic tone common in books about adolescents written by adults.[^o133] *The Outsiders* remains one of the best-selling young-adult novels of all time.[^o133] *[Are You There God? It's Me, Margaret.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Are_You_There_God?_It's_Me,_Margaret.)* (1970) by [Judy Blume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judy_Blume) was another major success.[^o134][^o135] Blume was one of the first novelists who focused on such controversial topics as masturbation, menstruation, teen sex, birth control, and death.[^o136][^o137]

#### Cultural influences {#Cultural_influences}

*Image: Beauvoir Sartre - Che Guevara -1960 - Cuba.jpg.* (Left to right) [Simone de Beauvoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_de_Beauvoir), [Jean-Paul Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre), and Ernesto Che Guevara meeting in Cuba, 1960. They were some of the radical icons of the 1960s.

In the West, those born in the years before the actual boom were often the most influential people among boomers. Some of these people were musicians such as [The Beatles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beatles), [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan), and [The Rolling Stones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rolling_Stones); writers like [Jack Kerouac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Kerouac), [Allen Ginsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Ginsberg), [Betty Friedan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Friedan), [Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Solzhenitsyn), [Herbert Marcuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Marcuse), and other authors of the [Frankfurt School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_School) of Social Theory; and political leaders such as Mao Zedong, [Fidel Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro), and [Che Guevara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Che_Guevara).[^o151][^o37][^o165][^o180] As romanticists and idealists safe from political retribution, radical youths did not care for competency or results as much as ideology. To this end, revolutionary icons such as Mao, Castro, or Guevara proved to be potent symbols.[^o165][^o139] Kerouac, Ginsberg, and the poet [Sylvia Plath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvia_Plath) encouraged their readers to question cultural norms.[^o85] Parents, by contrast, saw their influence greatly diminished. This was a time of rapid change, and what the parents could teach their children was less important than what the children knew and what their parents did not. For young people, life was vastly different from what their parents experienced during the interwar and war years. Economic depression, mass unemployment, war, and chaos were a distant memory; full employment and material comfort were the norm. Such a drastic difference in outlook and experience created a rift between the generations. As for the peers, they did have a significant influence on young people, for while the *modus operandi* of youth culture at the time was to be oneself and to disregard the opinions of others, in practice, [peer pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_pressure) ensured conformity and uniformity, at least within a given subculture.[^o185]

*Image: Betty Friedan 1960.jpg.* Betty Friedan's *[The Feminine Mystique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Feminine_Mystique)* (1963) triggered the [second wave of feminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_wave_of_feminism) from the 1960s to the 1980s[^o140] by questioning the era's social norms, in particular its insistence on traditional gender roles.[^o83]

In the United States, while the 1950s may be remembered by some as an orderly and tranquil decade, dissent and rebellion were just beneath the surface, ready to erupt.[^o85] Indeed, the generational gap became apparent in the late 1960s.[^o85] Moreover, the Baby Boomers lived through a period of dramatic cultural cleavage between the left-leaning proponents of change and the more conservative individuals.[^o141] Analysts believe this cleavage has played out politically from the time of the Vietnam War to the present day,[^o141] to some extent defining the divided political landscape in the country.[^o142][^o143] Leading-edge boomers are often associated with the counterculture of the 1960s, the later years of the [civil rights movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement), and the [second wave of feminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-wave_feminism) in the 1970s.[^o144][^o145] Conversely, many trended in moderate to conservative directions opposite to the counterculture, especially those making professional careers in the military (officer and enlisted), law enforcement, business, blue-collar trades, and Republican Party politics.[^o144][^o145] On the other hand, trailing-edge boomers (also known as [Generation Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_Jones)) came of age in the "[malaise era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaise_era)" of the 1970s with events such as the [Watergate scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watergate_scandal), the [1973–1975 recession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973–1975_recession), the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis), the [United States Bicentennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Bicentennial) (1976), and the [Iranian hostage crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran_hostage_crisis) (1979). Politically, early boomers in the United States tend to be Democrats, while later boomers tend to be Republicans.[^o146]

*Image: Listening to is Yes' self-titled debut album from 1969 (cropped).jpg.* An American university student listening to a [British rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_rock) record on campus while sporting newly popularized blue jeans, 1970

During the 1960s and 1970s, the music industry made a fortune selling rock records to people between the ages of fourteen and twenty-five. This era was home to many youthful stars—people like [Brian Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Jones) of the Rolling Stones, [Jimi Hendrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimi_Hendrix), [Jim Morrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Morrison) of [the Doors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Doors), or [Janis Joplin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janis_Joplin)—who had lifestyles that all but guaranteed [their early deaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/27_Club).[^o185][^o147]

Across the Anglosphere, and increasingly in many other countries, middle- and upper-class youths started adopting the popular culture of the lower-classes, in stark contrast with previous generations. In the United Kingdom, for instance, young people from wealthy families changed their accents to approximate how working-class people spoke, and were not averse to the occasional use of profanities.[^o185] In France, the fashion industry discovered that trousers could outsell skirts in the mid-1960s.[^o185] Blue jeans, made popular by the likes of actor [James Dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Dean), steadily became a common sight across the Western world, even outside of college campuses.[^o185]

*Image: Londons Carnaby Street, 1966.jpg.* Young people in London (c. 1966). Prosperity played a role in shaping the youth culture of the 1960s.

A remarkable characteristic of youth culture from this period is its internationalism. Whereas previous generations typically preferred cultural products from their own countries, those who came of age during the 1960s and 1970s readily consumed the music of other countries, above all the United States, the cultural hegemon of the era. English-language music was normally left untranslated. Musical styles from the Caribbean, Latin America, and later, Africa also proved popular.[^o185]

In Roman Catholic countries such as Ireland and Italy, the 1960s and 1970s saw a schism between the Church and the young on issues such as divorce or abortion. In the Canadian province of Quebec, religious attendance plummeted in the same period.[^o185]

In China, despite the passage of the National Marriage Law in 1950, which prohibited having concubines, allowed women to file for divorce, and banned arranged marriages, arranged marriages in fact remained common, and the notion of marrying for [romantic love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_%28love%29) was considered a capitalist invention to be opposed during the period of the Cultural Revolution.[^o148]

#### Counterculture {#Counterculture}

*Image: Prenez vos désirs pour des réalités - 1968.jpg.* A graffiti telling students to "take your desires for reality" at [the Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris), May 1968

In the decades following the Second World War, cultural rebellion became a common feature in urbanized and industrialized societies, both East and West. In the context of the ideological competition of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War), governments sought to improve the material standards of living of their own citizens but also to encourage them to seek meaning in their daily lives. However, young people felt a sense of alienation and sought to assert their own individuality, freedom, and authenticity.[^o151] Journalist [Tom Wolfe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Wolfe) coined the phrase "Me Decade" to describe the 1960s.[^o85] By the early 1960s, elements of the [counterculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture_of_the_1960s) had already entered public consciousness on both sides of the Atlantic, but were not yet viewed as a threat. But even then, West German Chancellor [Konrad Adenauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Adenauer) acknowledged that the "most important problem of our epoch" was what many youths viewed as the empty [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_materialism) and superficiality of modern life. In the Soviet Union, the official youth periodical, *[Komsomol'skaia pravda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komsomolskaya_Pravda)*, called for attention to the "psychology of contemporary young people". By 1968, counterculture was considered a serious threat. In the United States, the [Central Intelligence Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Intelligence_Agency) (CIA) reported to the President that counterculture was a highly disruptive force not just in the nation but also abroad. In the CIA's view, it undermined societies East and West, from U.S. allies like West Germany, Japan, and South Korea to Communist nations like Poland, the Soviet Union, and China.[^o151] Long-time director of the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation) (FBI) J. Edgar Hoover suspected that students' protests and counterculture were instigated by Communist agents. However, the CIA subsequently found no evidence of foreign subversion.[^o150] Counterculture also affected Third World nations—those that chose to remain unaligned in the Cold War. In the Soviet Union, director of the Committee for State Security ([KGB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KGB)) [Yuri Andropov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Andropov) became paranoid about the internal security. Under General Secretary [Leonid Brezhnev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Brezhnev), the KGB amplified its efforts to suppress politically dissident voices, though the Soviet Union never quite returned to [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin)'s style of governance.[^o151]

With hindsight, the CIA's assessments proved overly pessimistic. These youth movements had a bark that was worse than their bite. Despite sounding radical, the proponents of counterculture did not exactly demand the complete destruction of society in order to build it anew; they only wanted to work within the confines of the *[status quo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_quo)* to bring about the change they desired. Changes, if they came, were less well-organized than the activists themselves. Moreover, the loudest and most visible participants of counterculture often came from privileged backgrounds—with heretofore unheard-of access to higher education, material comfort, and leisure—which allowed them to feel secure enough in their activism. Counterculture was therefore not about material desires.[^o151][^o152]

Counterculture did, however, come with an entire pharmacopeia, including [marijuana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_%28drug%29), amphetamines (such as "[purple hearts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexamyl)"), and [magic mushrooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psilocybin_mushroom). But perhaps the most notorious was a substance known as "acid" or [lysergic acid diethylamide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LSD) (LSD). Synthesized in 1938 by chemist [Albert Hoffmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Hofmann) in his quest to cure migraine, its use as a psychedelic drug was promoted in the 1960s by psychologist [Timothy Leary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Leary). Attempts to ban it in 1966 made the substance [even more popular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streisand_effect). A number of cultural icons of the late 1960s, such as poet Allen Ginsberg were known for using this drug.[^o150]

During the 1960s, conservative students objected to the counterculture and found ways to celebrate their conservative ideals by reading books such as [J. Edgar Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Edgar_Hoover)'s *A Study of Communism*, joining student organizations like the [College Republicans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Republicans), and organizing [Greek events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraternities_and_sororities) which reinforced [gender norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_role).[^o153] While historians disagree over the influence of the [countercultural movements of the 1960s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterculture_of_the_1960s) in American politics and society, they tend to describe it in similar terms. For instance, sociologist [Todd Gitlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Todd_Gitlin) calls it self-indulgent, childish, irrational, narcissistic, and even dangerous.[^o154] Moreover, it is possible that this movement did no more than creating new marketing segments for the specific sectors of the population, the "hip" crowd.[^o155][^o156]

#### Protests and riots {#Protests_and_riots}

When they came of age during the late 1960s and 1970s, Baby Boomers immediately became politically active and made themselves heard due to the sheer size of their demographic cohort.[^o111] Violent crime and protests increased markedly in the late 1960s and early 1970s.[^o151] University students in three continents revolted.[^o85] Many proponents of counterculture idealized violence and armed struggle against what they considered oppression, drawing inspiration from conflicts in the Third World and from the [Cultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution) in Communist China, a creation of [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong) intended to thoroughly sever the ties of society to its history, with deadly results. Some young men and women simply refused to engage in dialogue with mainstream society and instead believed that violence was [a sign of their status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_symbol) as resistance fighters.[^o151]

In [May 1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_68), French youths launched massive protest demanding social and educational reforms, while labor unions simultaneously initiated a general strike, prompting countermeasures by the government. This led to a general mayhem in a manner similar to a civil war, especially in Paris. Finally, the government acquiesced to the demands of the students and workers; [Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle) stepped down as president in 1969.[^o158]

In the Federal Republic of Germany ([West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany)), the 1950s was a period of strong economic growth and prosperity. But like so many other Western nations, it soon faced severe political polarization thanks to youth revolts. By the 1960s there was a general feeling of stagnation, which stimulated the creation of the primarily student-backed [Extra-parliamentary Opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra-parliamentary_opposition) (APO). One of the goals of the APO was reforms to the university system of admissions and registration. One of the most prominent APO activists was [Rudi Dutschke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudi_Dutschke), who declared "the [long march through the institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_march_through_the_institutions)" in the context of recruitment for the civil service. Another major student movement of this era was the [Red Army Faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army_Faction) (RAF), a militant [Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism) group most active in the 1970s and the 1980s. Members of the RAF believed the West German economic and political systems to be inhumane and [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism); they looted stores, robbed banks, and kidnapped or assassinated West German businessmen, politicians, and judges. The RAF's reign of terror lasted until around 1993. It disbanded itself in 1998.[^o159] The RAF turned out to be deadlier than its American counterpart, the [Weather Underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_Underground), which declared itself a "movement that fights, not just talks about fighting."[^o151]

Many West German youths in the late 1960s were suspicious of authority. Student demonstrators protested West Germany's rearmament, [NATO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO) membership, refusal to recognize the Democratic Republic of Germany ([East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germany)), and the role of the United States in the Vietnam War. On the other hand, the construction of the [Berlin Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Wall) (starting in August 1961, after the [Berlin standoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Crisis_of_1961)) by East Germany boosted anti-communist sentiments in the West, where there was growing demands for high academic standards and opposition to communist indoctrination. A sort of civil war erupted in German academia. The [Free University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_University_of_Berlin) was the heart of West German student movements. Many leading professors left because of the asphyxiating political atmosphere. However, by the mid-1970s, things calmed down. Students were more interested in academics and career preparation.[^o160] Indeed, counterculture had by this time invited stern public backlash. Resistance to change heightened. Major governments around the world implemented various policies intended to ensure "[law and order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_and_order_%28politics%29)".[^o151]

Some well-known slogans among youth rebels were, "When I think of revolution I want to make love," "I take my desires for reality and I believe in the reality of my desires," and "We want everything and we want it now!" These were evidently not what would normally be recognized as political slogans; rather, they were subjective expressions of the private individual.[^o185]

In general, though, no major government was overthrown by the protests and riots of the 1960s; indeed, governments proved rather stable during this turbulent period in history. Growing demands for change stimulated resistance to change.[^o151] Frustrated with the lack of revolutionary results despite their protests, which some skeptical observers such as [Raymond Aron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Aron) dismissed as no more than '[psychodrama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_fiction)' and '[street theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_theatre)', some students became radicalized and opted for violence and even terrorism to achieve their goals. Nevertheless, other than publicity they achieved little. Doing one's "revolutionary service"—as the joke goes in Peru—[did wonders for one's future career](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Careerism), though. From Latin America to France, students were aware that the civil service recruited university graduates and in fact, many had a successful career working for the government after leaving radical groups (and in some cases, becoming completely apolitical). Governments understood that people became less rebellious as they aged.[^o95]

In the United States, protests against American participation in the War in Vietnam shook college campuses and cities across the nation, even though the U.S. Armed Forces were deployed for similar (anti-communist) reasons they were during the Korean War, a conflict that provoked little political headwind. This was a time of growing focus on the self and mistrust of authority. Young people were no longer interested in military service the way their forebears were. [Draft evasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draft_evasion_in_the_Vietnam_War) grew, and many took to the streets to demand the abolition of [conscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_in_the_United_States), which became a reality. Some of these student demonstrations grew violent, with fatal consequences.[^o111] Among counties that saw only peaceful demonstrations, the chances of the Democratic Party winning elections were unharmed. But in those that had riots, the Republican Party was able to attract new votes by appealing to the desire for security and stability. In fact, the backlash against the civil unrest of the late 1960s and early 1970s was so strong that politicians in the 1990s like [Bill Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton) had decided to endorse tough policies regarding public security in order to win elections.[^o161] High-profile protestors such as the hippies who confronted the police were the target of public hostility and condemnation.[^o162] Meanwhile, many American university students disapproved of their rebellious peers and concentrated on their studies.[^o85]

In Mexico, authorities deployed security forces to quell a youth protest in preparation for the [1968 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Summer_Olympics) in Mexico City.[^o85]

As a group, Western left-wing activists and radicals of the 1960s were intellectuals, and this was reflected in the ways their variants of political action and beliefs, primarily drawn from the experience in the classroom rather than on the factory floor. Many of these remained in academia and consequently became an unprecedentedly large cadre of cultural and political radicals on campus.[^o163]

One side effect of the student revolts of the late-1960s was that it made unions and workers realize they could demand more from their employers. Nevertheless, after so many years of full employment and growing wages and benefits, the working class was simply uninterested in starting a revolution.[^o95] To the contrary, some Americans even took to the streets to denounce counterculture and anti-war activists in the context of the War in Vietnam.[^o164]

In China, Chairman Mao in 1965 created the [Red Guards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Guards), which initially consisted mainly of students, to purge dissident CCP officials and intellectuals in general, as part of the Cultural Revolution. The result was general mayhem. Mao eventually opted to deploy the People's Liberation Army against his own Red Guards to restore public order.[^o93] Mao disposed of millions of Red Guards by sending them to the countryside.[^o165]

#### Hippies and the Hippie Trail {#Hippies_and_the_Hippie_Trail}

The youthful proponents of counterculture, known as the [hippies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippie), disapproved of the modern world so much they sought refuge from it in [communes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentional_community) and [mystical religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism). During the 1960s and 1970s, large groups of them could be found in any major European or American city. Male hippies wore long hair and grew beards, while female hippies eschewed anything that women traditionally wore to make themselves attractive, such as [makeup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmetics) and [bras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bra). Hippies were iconoclasts to varying degrees and rejected the traditional work ethic. They preferred love to money, feelings to facts, and natural things to manufactured items. They engaged in [casual sex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casual_sex) and used various [hallucinogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucinogen). They were generally pacifists and pessimists. Many disliked politics and activism, though they were influenced by the political atmosphere of the time.[^o167] A significant cultural event of this era was the [Woodstock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock) Festival in August 1969, which drew huge crowds despite bad weather and a general lack of facilities.[^o167] Although it is commonly asserted that some half a million people attended, the actual figure is difficult to determine, even with aerial photography, as crowd experts would attest.[^o168]

The so-called [Hippie Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippie_trail) probably started in the mid-1950s, as expeditions of wealthy tourists and students traveling in small groups. They started from the United Kingdom, heading eastwards. As [Western European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe) economies grew, so did demand for international travel; many bus services sprang up to serve tourists. The first hippies—initially used to refer to men with long hair—joined the trail in the late 1960s. Many young people were beguiled by [Eastern religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_religions) and mysticism, and they wanted to visit Asia to learn more. Others wanted to escape the conventional lifestyles of their home countries or saw opportunities for profit. Some smoked [marijuana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_%28drug%29) and wished to visit the Middle East and [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia), where their favored products came from. But air travel was in its infancy at this time in history and was beyond the reach of most. For those seeking an adventure, traveling by long-distance bus and trains from Western Europe to Asia became an affordable alternative. But not all who traversed the Hippie Trail were from Europe. In fact, many hailed from Canada, the United States, Australia, and New Zealand. Visas were easily obtained and in some cases were not required at all.[^o171] Many young and naive Western tourists fell victim to scammers, tricksters, and even murderers, taking advantage of the nascent global drug culture at the time.[^o169][^o170] The Hippie Trail ended in 1979 with the [Iranian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_Revolution) and the start of the [Soviet–Afghan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet–Afghan_War) (1979–1989).[^o171]

#### Sexual revolution and feminism {#Sexual_revolution_and_feminism}

In the United Kingdom, the [Lady Chatterley trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_v_Penguin_Books_Ltd) (1959) and the first long-play of the Beatles, *[Please Please Me](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Please_Please_Me)* (1963) were to begin the process of altering public perception of [human mating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mating_strategies), a cause subsequently taken up by young people seeking personal liberation.[^o185][^o173]

In the United States, the [Food and Drugs Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_and_Drug_Administration) (FDA) in May 1960 approved the first contraceptive pill, a drug that has had a huge impact on the nation's history.[^o174] Invented by [Gregory Pincus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Goodwin_Pincus) in 1956, [the pill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combined_oral_contraceptive_pill), as it is commonly called, marked the first time in human history when sexuality and reproduction can be reliably separated.[^o175] The pill and antibiotics capable of curing various venereal diseases eliminated two leading arguments against extra-marital sex, paving the way for the [sexual revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_revolution).[^o178] But the revolution did not happen right away; it was not until the 1980s that the pill become widely available in the United States and other Western nations.[^o175] In the case of the U.S., contraception outside of married remained illegal until the 1970s.[^o85] In fact, the first wave of Baby Boomers married and had children early, following the footsteps of their parents.[^o83] Eventually, though, political attitudes towards human sexuality altered dramatically in the late 1960s because of young people. Although the behavior of most Americans did not change overnight, the heretofore mainstream beliefs on issues such as [premarital sex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premarital_sex), birth control, abortion, homosexuality, and pornography were openly challenged and no longer considered automatically valid. Individuals no longer feared social consequences when they expressed deviant ideas.[^o178] Indeed, the 1960s and 1970s were the most consequential in the history of human sexuality. While there was a backlash on moral or religious grounds, the sexual mores in Western societies were forever changed.[^o175] Nevertheless, there were concerns over possible abandonment or disrespect among (older) teenage girls and young women if they were to offer themselves to their boyfriends, a sentiment captured by the song "[Will You Love Me Tomorrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_You_Love_Me_Tomorrow)?" from 1960.[^o111] [Going steady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Going_steady)—the practice of dating one person exclusively (as opposed to "playing the field")—rose in popularity among young Americans following the Second World War[^o176] and was part of mainstream youth culture through the 1980s, with teenagers beginning to go steady at progressively earlier ages.[^o177]

Sexologist [Alfred C. Kinsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Kinsey)'s books, *Sexual Behavior in the Human Male* (1948) and *Sexual Behavior in the Human Female* (1958), employed confidential interviews to proclaim that sexual behaviors previously deemed unusual were more common than people thought. Despite triggering a storm of criticisms, the [Kinsey Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinsey_Reports) earned him the nickname the "Marx of the sexual revolution" due to their revolutionary influence. Many men and women celebrated their newfound freedom and had their satisfactions, but the sexual revolution also paved the way to new problems.[^o178] Many young people were under peer pressure to enter relationships they felt they were ill-prepared for, with serious psychological consequences. Illegitimate births ballooned, as did sexually transmitted diseases. Public health officials raised the alarm on an epidemic of [gonorrhea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonorrhea) and the emergence of the lethal acquired immune deficiency syndrome ([AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS)). Because many had strong opinions on various subjects relating to sexuality, the sexual revolution exacerbated sociopolitical stratification.[^o178]

Coupled with the sexual revolution was a new wave of feminism, as the relaxation of traditional views heightened women's awareness of what they might be able to change. Competition in the job market led many to demand equal pay for equal work and government-funded daycare services. Some groups, such as the [National Organization for Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Organization_for_Women) (NOW) equated women's rights with civil rights and copied the tactics of black activists, demanding an [Equal Rights Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_Rights_Amendment), changes to the divorce laws making them more favorable to women, and the legalization of abortion.[^o180]

In her book *The Feminine Mystique*, Betty Friedan discussed what she called "the problem that has no name"—namely, that married women with children were unhappy as housewives because "There is no other way for a woman to dream of creation or of the future. There is no way she can even dream about herself, except as her children's mother, her husband's wife."[^o179] "[The personal is political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_personal_is_political)" became the motto for the [second wave of feminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-wave_feminism).[^o140] But the feminist movement splintered, because some became radicalized and thought that groups such as NOW were not enough. Such [radical feminists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_feminism) believed that people should start using [gender-neutral language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender-neutral_language), marriage should be abolished, and that the traditional family unit was "a decadent, energy-absorbing, destructive, wasteful institution," rejected [heterosexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterosexuality) as a matter of principle, and attacked "not just capitalism, but men." At the other extreme, staunch [social conservatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_conservatism) launched a major backlash, for example, by starting [anti-abortion movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-abortion_movement) after the [Supreme Court of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) declared abortion constitutional in *[Roe v. Wade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roe_v._Wade)* (1973). Yet despite their best efforts, mainstream American society changed. Many women entered the workforce, taking a variety of jobs and thus altered the balance of power between the sexes.[^o180]

Although the new feminist movement germinated in the United States in the 1960s, initially to address the concerns of middle-class women, thanks to the appearance of the word 'sex' in the [Civil Rights Act of 1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Rights_Act_of_1964), which was primarily intended to prohibit racial discrimination, it quickly spread to other Western nations in the 1970s and especially the 1980s. More women realized how much power they had as a group and they made use of it immediately, as can be seen in reforms regarding divorce and abortion laws in Italy, for example.[^o95]

Women flooded the workforce, and by the early 1980s, many sectors of the economy were feminized, though men continued to monopolize manual labor. Due to the [law of supply and demand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_supply_and_demand), such a surge in the number of workers diminished the prestige and income of those jobs. For many middle-class married women, joining the workforce made little economic sense, after accounting for all the extra costs, such as paid childcare and house work, but many chose to work anyway in order to achieve financial independence. But as the desire to send one's children to university became ever more common, middle-class women worked outside the home for the same reason their poorer counterparts did: making ends meet. Nevertheless, at least among middle-class intellectuals, men became much more reluctant to disrupt the careers of their wives, who were not as willing to follow their husbands wherever their jobs led them as was the case for previous generations.[^o95]

Although, in practice, the practitioners of feminism did not necessarily form an ideologically coherent group, they did succeed in creating a collective consciousness for women, something left-wing politicians in democracies were keen to acknowledge in order to court their votes. By the 1970s and 1980s, the traditional solidarity of the working class was fading away as different segments of this group began experience divergent economic prospects due to the continuation of industrial automation.[^o95]

Following the advent of a new atmosphere of sexual freedom, in the 1970s and 1980s, homosexuals were increasingly willing to demand acceptance by society and full legal rights.[^o175][^o178] It proved difficult to object to what consenting adults practiced in private.[^o178] Stating one's commitment to a heretofore prohibited or ostracized way of life, that is, 'coming out', was important to [this movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT_social_movements). Homosexuality was decriminalized in England, Wales, and the United States in the late 1960s.[^o185] The world's first Gay Pride parade took place in 1969.[^o175] However, the [HIV/AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS) epidemic acted as a brake on this social trend when its first victims were identified as such in the early 1980s. Writer [Randy Shilts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randy_Shilts), himself a gay man, noted, "HIV is certainly character-building. It's made me see all the shallow things we cling to, like ego and [vanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity). Of course, I'd rather have a few more [T-cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T_cell) and a little less character."[^o181]

#### Marriage and family {#Marriage_and_family}

*Image: Families US.svg.* By the final third of the twentieth century, the nuclear family was no longer the most common type of household in the United States and other Western nations.

Marriage in many developed countries became much less stable in the 1980s, when Baby Boomers were getting married. In the West, this manifested in rising divorce rates. But this pattern was not seen in East Asia.[^o185] Instead, the region saw declining marriage rates, particularly in Japan, where social changes led to the deterioration of Japanese men's social standing after World War II.[^o183] Men in East Asia were more likely to be unmarried than men in the West.[^o184]

Between 1970 and 1985, the number of divorces per thousand people doubled in Denmark and Norway and tripled in France, Belgium, and the Netherlands. In England and Wales, while there was only one divorce per fifty-two weddings in 1938, that number became one every 2.2 fifty years later; this trend accelerated in the 1960s. During the 1970s, Californian women visiting their doctors showed a marked decline in the desire for marriage and children. In all Western nations, the number of single-person households steadily rose. In the major metropolitan areas, half the population lived alone. Meanwhile, the "traditional" [nuclear family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_family)—a phrase first coined in 1924—was in decline. In Canada, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and West Germany, only a minority of households consisted of two parents and their children by the 1980s, down from half or more than half in 1960. In Sweden, such a family unit fell from 37% to 25% in the same period; in fact, more than half of all children in Sweden in the mid-1980s were born to unmarried women.[^o185]

In the United States, what set the Boomers apart from their parents was not their marriage rates, or that they married in their early 20s, but rather that they were much more likely to be divorced. Divorced Boomers were less likely to remarry than their counterparts from the Silent Generation.[^o111] Statistically, marrying before the age of 25 increased the probability of divorce.[^o83] The nuclear family fell from 44% of all households in 1960 to 29% in 1980. But for African Americans, the figure was higher. In 1991, single mothers had given birth to the majority of children (70%) and headed a majority of households (58%).[^o185] As women's labor participation rose, rumors that career-oriented women were unhappy and unfulfilled or that mothers regretted returning to work began to spread. Mothers-in-law and medical doctors began urging women to forgo their ambitions in order to have children early to avoid problems infertility later on. Popular media frequently exhorted working women—if implicitly—to quit their jobs.[^o111] In a 1978 column for the *Washington Post*, Richard Cohen coined the term "biological clock" in the context of women's fertility decline.[^o186] Media outlets frequently glamorized women who pursued both demanding careers and had children before it was too late for them to do so. However, as psychologist Jean Twenge pointed out, statistics concerning women's fertility decline as a function of age used at the time were misleading because they relied on French birth records between 1670 and 1830, "a time before electricity, antibiotics, or fertility treatment."[^o187] Nevertheless, the share of American adults who rejected traditional gender roles steadily rose during the 1970s and 1980s.[^o111] As more and more women became financially independent, the traditional reason for women to marry faded away.[^o188] Marriage became increasingly viewed as an option, rather than an obligation.[^o111] In fact, since the 1960s, marriage has stopped being primarily focused on having and raising children but instead, the fulfillment of adults.[^o189] Married men were noticeably less willing to disrupt the careers of their wives.[^o185] Unmarried women were no longer deemed as "sick" or "immoral" the way they were in the 1950s. In addition, neither working mothers nor [single parenthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_parent) (what used to be called [illegitimacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_%28family_law%29)) was socially ostracized the way they used to be.[^o111]

*Image: Ms. magazine Cover - Spring 1972.jpg.* [*Ms.* magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ms._%28magazine%29) cover, spring 1972

Parts of the reason why marriages were delayed or avoided were economic. People who entered the workforce during the 1970s and 1980s made less than their fathers did in the 1950s. Fertility rates fell as a result.[^o90] The rise in the number of cohabitating couples was also a factor. These couples claimed [cohabitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohabitation) helped them assess the suitability of a mate before marriage.[^o190]

In Italy, divorce was legalized in 1970 and confirmed by referendum in 1974. Abortion went through the same process in 1978 and 1981, respectively.[^o95] In the Canadian province of Quebec, the birth rate fell considerably during the 1960s and 1970s as the influence of the Catholic Church declined.[^o185]

While sociologists had known for a long time that humans tend to select mates sharing many similar characteristics, a phenomenon known as [assortative mating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_mating_strategies#Assortative_mating), during the 1970s and 1980s, an even stronger correlation between the educational levels of a married couple was observed in the United States. In fact, both the age at (first) marriage and the amount of time spent in school increased in the 1970s. Higher rates of university attendance and workforce participation by women affected the marital expectations of both sexes in that both men and women became more symmetrical in what they desired in a mate.[^o191]

Part of the reason why people increasingly married their socioeconomic and educational peers was economic in nature. Innovations that became commercially available in the late twentieth century such as the washing machine and frozen food reduced the amount of time people needed to spend on housework, which diminished the importance of domestic skills.[^o192] Moreover, it was no longer possible for a couple with one spouse having no more than a high-school diploma to earn about the national average; on the other hand, couples both of whom had at least a bachelor's degree could expect to make a significant amount above the national average. People thus had a clear economic incentive to seek out a mate with at least as high a level of education in order to maximize their potential income. A societal outcome of this was that as household gender equality improved because women had more choices, income inequality widened.[^o193]

## In midlife {#In_midlife}

### Economic power {#Economic_power}

By the early 2000s, the Baby Boomers reached middle age and were starting to save for retirement, though not necessarily enough. Seeking to increase their income and thus savings, many started investing, pushing interest rates to the floor. Borrowing became so cheap that some investors made rather risky decisions in order to get better returns. Financial analysts call this the "seeking yield" problem. But even the United States was not enough to absorb all these investments, so the capital flowed overseas, helping to fuel the considerable economic growth of various developing countries.[^o14] Steve Gillon has suggested that one thing that sets the Baby Boomers apart from other generational groups is the fact that "almost from the time they were conceived, boomers were dissected, analyzed, and pitched to by modern [marketers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketing), who reinforced a sense of generational distinctiveness".[^o196]

This is supported by the articles of the late 1940s identifying the increasing number of babies as an economic boom, such as a 1948 *[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek)* article whose title proclaimed "Babies Mean Business",[^o197] or a 1948 *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_%28magazine%29)* magazine article called "Baby Boom".[^o198]

From 1979 to 2007, those receiving the highest one percentile of incomes saw their already large incomes increase by 278% while those in the middle at the 40th–60th percentiles saw a 35% increase. Since 1980, after the vast majority of Baby Boomer college goers graduated, the cost of college has been increased by over 600% (inflation-adjusted).[^o199]

After the CCP opened up their nation's economy in the late 1970s, because so many Baby Boomers did not have access to higher education, they were simply left behind as the Chinese economy grew enormously thanks to said reforms.[^o13]

### Family values {#Family_values}

*Image: Tony Martin Cyd Charisse and family 1956.JPG.* Nuclear families such as this one (pictured 1956) were in retreat by the last third of the twentieth century.

Among Americans aged 50 and up, the divorce rate per 1,000 married persons went from 5 in 1990 to 10 in 2015; that among those in the 65 and higher age group tripled to 6 per 1,000 during the same period. Marriage instability in early adulthood contributed to their high rate of divorce. For comparison, the divorce rate for that same period for people aged 25 to 39 went from 30 down to 24, and those aged 40 to 49 increased from 18 to 21 per 1,000 married persons.[^o201]

Because the Baby Boomers came of age during a time of reliable contraception and legal abortion, their children—the Millennials—were wanted and planned. Boomers typically had fewer children than previous cohorts and invested more in each child. By the 1980s and 1990s, parenting in the United States had become more competitive.[^o202]

Among Frenchwomen born in the early 1960s, only a third had at least three children. Yet they were responsible for more than half of the next generation because so many of their contemporaries had just one or even none at all. In the United States, one-fifth of women born in the 1950s ended their fertile years without giving birth. 17% of women from the Baby Boom Generation had only one child each and were responsible for only 8% of the next generation. On the other hand, 11% of Baby Boomer women gave birth to at least four children each, for a grand total of one-quarter of the Millennial generation. Those who had many children were likely religious (especially practicing Christians) while those who [chose to remain childless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voluntary_childlessness) were often members of countercultural or feminist movements of the 1960s and 1970s.[^o203]

Due to the one-child policy introduced in the late 1970s, one-child households have become the norm in China, leading to rapid population aging, especially in the cities where the costs of living are much higher than in the countryside.[^o204]

### Attitude towards religion {#Attitude_towards_religion}

In the United States, radical activists of the 1960s prompted a backlash by religious leaders, who advocated a return to basic "family values". Evangelical Christians grew considerably in numbers in the 1970s. This movement became politically active, resulting in the fusion between [Christian fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_fundamentalism) and [neoconservatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoconservatism) in the late 1970s and 1980s and the election of [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan) as president.[^o151]

In 1993, *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_%28magazine%29)* magazine reported on the religious affiliations of Baby Boomers. Citing [Wade Clark Roof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wade_Clark_Roof), a [sociologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociologist) at the [University of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California) at [Santa Barbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Barbara,_California), the articles stated that about 42% of Baby Boomers had already left formal religion, 33% had never strayed from church, and 25% of boomers were returning to religious practice. The boomers returning to religion were "usually less tied to tradition and less dependable as church members than the loyalists. They are also more liberal, which deepens rifts over issues like [abortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abortion) and [homosexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality)."[^o206]

## In later years {#In_later_years}

### Work and retirement {#Work_and_retirement}

Baby boomers started entering retirement in the mid-2000s and have already begun withdrawing their investments. Any economic activities that depend on the cheap capital courtesy of the Baby Boomers will cease to be.[^o14] From a demographic point of view, the aging of the American workforce is inevitable due to the sheer number of the Baby Boomers.[^o209] Economists expected the United States to face a labor crunch in the 2010s as more and more Baby Boomers retired.[^o210] However, in 2018, a large portion of the older Baby Boomers (65–72 years of age) in the United States remained active in the labor force (29%), compared to the Silent Generation (21%) and the Greatest Generation (19%) when they were the same age, the Pew Research Center found by analyzing official labor statistics. The labor participation rate of women aged 65 to 72 in 2018 was 25% and of men of the same age group was 35%. This trend follows from the general expectation of Americans to work after the age of 65,[^o212] as life expectancy is higher than for previous generations.[^o211] The Baby Boomers who chose to remain in the work force after the age of 65 are mostly university graduates, whites, and residents of the big cities. That the Boomers maintained a relatively high labor participation rate made economic sense because the longer they postpone retirement, the more Social Security benefits they could claim, once they finally retire.[^o212] Many of these Boomers work past the age of retirement not because they cannot afford to retire, but because they enjoy their work.[^o211] Large numbers prefer semi-retirement arrangements or flexible work schedules.[^o213] There is an ongoing shift away from pension plans in favor of [401(k)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/401%28k%29) or similar options, which do not mandate retirement at a certain age.[^o214] Among the Boomers who have retired, a significant portion opts to live in the suburbs, where the Millennials are also moving to in large numbers as they have children of their own. These confluent trends increase the level of economic activities in the American suburbs.[^o215] Moreover, during the late 2010s, Millennials and seniors in the U.S. were driving up demand for affordable housing outside the major cities; to prevent another housing bubble, banks and regulators have restricted lending to filter out speculators and those with bad credit.[^o216]

According to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), elders in nations where people typically retire late like Sweden and Switzerland tended to experience memory loss at a rate twice as slow as their counterparts from countries where people usually retire early, such as France. Evidence suggests that those who remain mentally active are more likely to maintain their faculties.[^o217]

By the mid-2010s, it has already become apparent that China was facing a serious demographic crisis as the population of retirees boomed while the number of working-age people shrank. This poses serious challenges for any attempts to implement social support for the elderly and imposes constraints on China's future economic prospects.[^o204] In 2018, about 17.8% of China's population, or around 250 million people, was at least 60 years old. The Central Government has been considering raising the retirement age, like many other countries have done, though this is controversial among the Chinese public, who dislike postponing their pensions. As of 2020, China's retirement age is 60 for men and 55 for women working for the government or in other white-collar jobs. But China's demography is such that the nation's pension funds will be "insolvent" by 2035 if current trends continue, according to an official report.[^o218]

*Image: Work Force Participation Rate by Age Group.webp.* In the United States, the COVID-19 pandemic has prompted a number of older workers to retire early.

The [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) may have spurred some Baby Boomers to exit the workforce. In the United Kingdom, data collected by the Institute for Fiscal Studies in June and July 2020 showed that 6% of those aged 66–70 and 11% of those aged 71 and older who were working immediately before the crisis had retired, half of whom had not previously planned to do so.[^o219] In the United States, Pew Research Center reported that the number of Baby Boomers in retirement had increased by 2.3 million in 2020 the largest annual increase since the oldest Baby Boomers turned 65 during 2011.[^o220] This surge in the number of retirees contributed to the labor shortage as the country recovered from the pandemic.[^o210] In fact, the retiring Boomers contributed to an economic phenomenon dubbed the [Great Resignation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Resignation) of the early 2020s.[^o111]

Countries facing population aging around the world have accelerated automation in order to cope with labor shortages[^o221][^o222][^o223] and to help care for the elderly.[^o224][^o225] In general, an aging population and a labor shortage accelerate industrial automation, not just to replace the "missing" workers but also to cut costs,[^o226][^o227] though these technologies still have much room for improvement.[^o224][^o228]

### Financial position {#Financial_position}

In the United Kingdom, three quarters of Baby Boomers were home owners and the values of their properties have more than doubled over the between the late 1990s and late 2010s upon their deaths, according to the [Resolution Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_Foundation). For this reason, the transfer of wealth between the baby boomers and their children, the millennials, will prove highly beneficial to the latter compared to previous cohorts, especially those who came from high-income families.[^o230]

According to economists William G. Gale, Hilary Gelfond, Jason J. Fichtner, and Benjamin H. Harris, while the Great Recession has diminished the wealth of all age groups in the United States in the short run, a longitudinal analysis reveals that older generations, including the Baby Boomers, have been able to acquire more wealth whereas Millennials have gotten poorer overall.[^o235] Data from the [Federal Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Board) indicates that American Boomers held approximately \$83 trillion in assets by 2025, representing more than half of all U.S. household wealth despite constituting roughly 20 percent of the population.[^o236] [Bank of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_America) estimated that government policy from the 1980s onward—including rising public debt, falling interest rates, and asset price inflation—facilitated a transfer of approximately \$129 trillion into Boomer and older households' collective net worth.[^o237] A survey found that nearly a third of Baby Boomer multimillionaires polled in the US would prefer to pass on their [inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance) to [charities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charity_%28practice%29) rather than pass it down to their children. Of these Boomers, 57% believed it was important for each generation to earn their own money; 54% believed it was more important to invest in their children while they were growing up.[^o238] Bank of America Merrill Lynch estimated in 2014 that the '[silver economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_economy)' would be worth US\$15 trillion in 2020, up from US\$8 trillion ten years prior. This dramatic growth is due not just to baby boomers retiring en masse, but also to their spending habits. While previous generations generally preserved their wealth and passed it onto their children, many baby boomers prefer to spend their money on their own long retirement.[^o239] Nevertheless, some American politicians have warned that many senior citizens did not have enough savings for retirement.[^o240]

The heirs of American Baby Boomers are projected to inherit approximately US\$84.4 trillion in assets through 2045. But the "[Great Wealth Transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wealth_Transfer)" heavily concentrated among the wealthiest households, and that end-of-life healthcare costs may substantially reduce the amounts ultimately inherited by many families.[^o241] Similarly, in Canada, during the 2020s, an estimated C\$1 trillion is being transferred from the Baby Boomers to their heirs, mostly Millennials, but only a select few are going to inherit a substantial sum from their parents.[^o242]

### Leisure and other habits {#Leisure_and_other_habits}

As younger generations move towards getting more of their entertainment via the internet, traditional television has held up better in the leisure time of baby boomers. Research from 2018 into British viewing habits suggested that individuals aged 65 to 74 (mainly older baby boomers) viewed an average of 333 minutes of broadcast TV each day higher than any younger age group and only nine minutes less than in 2010. In the case of younger baby boomers the figures suggested that 55 to 64 year olds consumed an average 277 minutes of broadcast TV each day in 2018 (still higher than any age range their junior) with a sharper decline since 2010 of 44 minutes.[^o244] In the same year, three main cable news stations in the United States all had average viewer ages' within the Baby boomer range.[^o245]

In 2019, advertising platform Criteo conducted a survey of 1,000 U.S. consumers which showed baby boomers were less likely than [millennials](wikipedia://en/149183) to purchase groceries online. Of the baby boomers surveyed, 30 percent said they used some form of online grocery delivery service.[^o246]

### Life expectancy and health {#Life_expectancy_and_health}

*Image: Median age by country, 2016.svg.* Median age by country in 2016. Many countries are aging due to rising life expectancy and falling fertility.

A 2020 study by sociologist Hui Zheng suggests that early US baby boomers (born between the late-1940s and early-1950s) and middle baby boomers (born between the mid- to late-1950s) had significant cognitive decline at age 50 and over compared to their elders, though the generations born before and during the Second World War had increasing cognitive scores from one generation to the next when they were at the same age.[^o248]

Around the world, people are living longer than ever before. Global life expectancy has increased from 47 years in the 1950s to over 72 years in 2016.[^o249] As a result, the number of people older than 65 years of age has gone up over the decades, as has their share of the global population. However, the rate of population aging in the developing world is faster than among developed nations. Asia, South America, and the Caribbean are all aging rapidly. Globally, the ratio of the number of working-age people (15–64) to those aged 65 and over—the support ratio—has fallen from 11.75 in 1950 to 8.5 in 2012 and is on course to drop even further in the upcoming decades. These developments will fundamentally change the patterns of consumption in the global economy. The global disease burden will also change, with conditions affecting the elderly, such as [dementia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dementia), becoming more common.[^o239]

As countries around the world face the problem of a 'boomer dense' population, they find their tax revenues and what they can spend on elderly support in decline. Some countries, such as Japan, South Korea, and Singapore, have invested considerable sums of money on a variety of novel medical devices, robotics, and other gadgets to assist the elderly in their sunset years. Others, such as Austria and the Netherlands, have created specialized services for the elderly, among them dementia-friendly villages decorated with items and music from the 1950s and 1960s to help residents feel at home. Meanwhile, Hong Kong, Taiwan, and India passed legislation to incentivize people to provide more financial support for their elders.[^o239] In the United States, the sheer number of baby boomers can put a strain on [Medicare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicare_%28United_States%29). According to the [American Medical Student Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Medical_Student_Association), the population of individuals over the age of 65 will increase by 73 percent between 2010 and 2030, meaning one in five Americans will be a senior citizen.[^o250]

## Political evolution {#Political_evolution}

In Europe, the period between the middle to the late twentieth century could be described as an era of mass politics, meaning people were generally loyal to a chosen political party. Political debates were mostly about economic questions, such as wealth redistribution, taxation, jobs, and the role of government. But as countries transitioned from having industrial economies to a post-industrial and globalized world, and as the twentieth century became the twenty-first, topics of political discourse changed to other questions and polarization due to competing values intensified.[^o253]

Scholars such as [Ronald Inglehart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Inglehart) traced the roots of this new 'culture conflict' all the way back to the 1960s, which witnessed the emergence of the Baby Boomers, who were generally university-educated middle-class voters. Whereas their predecessors in the twentieth century—the Lost Generation, the Greatest Generation, and the Silent Generation—had to endure severe poverty and world wars, focused on economic stability or simple survival, the Baby Boomers benefited from an economically secure, if not affluent, upbringing and as such tended to be drawn to 'post-materialist' values. Major topics for political discussion at that time were things like the sexual revolution, civil rights, nuclear weaponry, ethnocultural diversity, environmental protection, European integration, and the concept of 'global citizenship'. Some mainstream parties, especially the social democrats, moved to the left in order to accommodate these voters. In the twenty-first century, supporters of post-materialism lined up behind causes such as LGBT rights, climate change, multiculturalism, and various [political campaigns on social media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_activism). Inglehart called this the "Silent Revolution". But not everyone approved, giving rise to what Piero Ignazi called the "Silent Counter-Revolution".[^o253] The university-educated and non-degree holders have very different upbringing, live very different lives, and as such hold very different values.[^o254] Education plays a role in this 'culture conflict' as national populism appeals most strongly to those who finished high school but did not graduate from university while the experience of higher education has been shown to be linked to having a socially liberal mindset. Degree holders tend to favor tolerance, individual rights, and group identities whereas non-degree holders lean towards [conformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformity), and maintaining order, customs, and traditions.[^o255] While the number of university-educated Western voters continues to grow, in many democracies non-degree holders still form a large share of the electorate. According to the OECD, in 2016, the average share of voters between the ages of 25 and 64 without tertiary education in the European Union was 66% of the population. In Italy, it exceeded 80%. In many major democracies, such as France, although the representation of women and ethnic minorities in the corridors of power has increased, the same cannot be said for the working-class and non-degree holders.[^o254]

In the United Kingdom, political scientists James Tilley and Geoffrey Evans conducted a longitudinal analysis of the electoral behavior of the same cohort between 1964 and 2010 and found that the average likelihood of a person voting for the right-leaning Conservative Party increased by 0.38% each year. Previous research suggests that aging and key life events—such as seeking employment, marriage, rearing children, and retirement—all make a person more skeptical of change and more conservative.[^o255][^o256]

In the United States, the baby boomers have been described as "the largest and most powerful generation in US history."[^o257] Even though the Baby Boomers were responsible for the cultural revolution of the late 1960s and 1970s, their political views shifted quickly and decisively during the 1980s. This was not because they wanted to reverse the changes they have brought about, but rather because they wanted to preserve the status quo. In fact, the level of support for conservatism among Boomers was higher than older generations when they were at the same age.[^o111] Compared to younger generations coming of age in the early twenty-first century, boomers are more conservative as well.[^o257] In addition, especially since the 1970s, working-class voters, who had previously formed the backbone of support for the [New Deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Deal) introduced by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, have been turning away from the left-leaning Democratic Party in favor of the right-leaning Republican Party.[^o253] As the Democratic Party attempted to make itself friendlier towards the university-educated and women during the 1990s, more blue-collar workers and non-degree holders left.[^o253]

*Image: US\_political\_leanings\_by\_age\_(Gallup\_2018).png.*

In 2018, Gallup conducted a survey of almost 14,000 Americans from all 50 states and the District of Columbia aged 18 and over on their political sympathies. They found that overall, younger adults tended to lean liberal while older adults tilted conservative. More specifically, groups with strong conservative leanings included the elderly, residents of the Midwest and the South, and people with some or no college education. Groups with strong liberal leanings were adults with advanced degrees, whereas those with moderate liberal leanings included younger adults (18 to 29 and 30 to 49), women, and residents of the East. Gallup found little variations by income groups compared to the national average. Among people between the ages of 50 and 64—older Generation X and younger Baby Boomers—Gallup found that 23% identified as liberals, 32% as moderates, and 41% as conservatives. Among those aged 65 and over—older Baby Boomers—they found that 22% considered themselves liberals, 30% moderates, and 43% conservatives. (See above.)[^o258]

In both Europe and the United States, older voters are the primary support base for the rise of nationalist and populist movements, though there are pockets of support among young people as well.[^o253] During the 2010s, a consistent trend in many Western countries is that older people are more likely to vote than their younger countrymen, and they tend to vote for more right-leaning (or conservative) candidates.[^o259][^o260][^o261] Due to the continued aging of these countries and the long life expectancy of their voters, right-leaning parties will continue to have a strong base of support.[^o255][^o256]

When the 'Golden Age' of economic boom finally came to a grinding halt in the 1970s, various reforms were introduced. High-skilled workers earned even more than before whereas low-skilled workers saw their fortunes melt away and consequently became dependent on welfare. This created a schism between the two groups, with the upper-end of the working class now supporting the political Right upon realizing that it was they, viewing themselves as hard-working taxpayers, who were the ones financing welfare, something they would rather do without except in cases of emergency. The fact that some governments incautiously made such welfare programs quite generous during the boom years exacerbated resentment for 'the underclass'. Public housing complexes came to be viewed as nothing more than residences for those who were socially problematic and chronically dependent on welfare.[^o95]

## Generational milestones {#Generational_milestones}

According to journalist and satirist [P. J. O'Rourke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._J._O'Rourke), "You're not a baby boomer if you don't have a visceral recollection of a Kennedy and a King assassination, a Beatles breakup, a U.S. defeat in Vietnam, and a [Watergate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watergate)."[^o111] In a 1985 study of U.S. generational cohorts by Schuman and Scott, a broad sample of adults was asked, "What world events over the past 50 years were especially important to you?"[^o263] For the baby boomers, the results were divided into two groups:

- Baby Boomer cohort number one (born 1946–1955), the cohort who epitomized the cultural change of the 1960s
  - Memorable events: the early Cold War (and associated [Red Scare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Scare)); the [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis); the assassinations of President John F. Kennedy, Robert Kennedy, and Martin Luther King Jr.; political unrest; the [Apollo program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program); the War in Vietnam; sexual liberation; drug experimentation; the civil rights movement; environmentalism; the second wave of feminism; and the Woodstock Festival.
- [Baby Boomer cohort number two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_Jones) (born 1956–1964), the cohort who came of age in the "malaise" years of the 1970s
  - Memorable events: the Cold War; the Cuban Missile Crisis; the assassinations of political icons; the War in Vietnam; the Moon-landing mission; the Watergate scandal and Richard Nixon's resignation; the lowering of the legal drinking age to 18 in many states from 1970 to 1976 (restored to 21 in the mid-1980s as a result of congressional lobbying by [Mothers Against Drunk Driving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mothers_Against_Drunk_Driving) (MADD)); the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis); [stagflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagflation_of_the_1970s); President Jimmy Carter's re-imposition of registration for the draft; the Iranian hostage crisis; the election of Ronald Reagan as president; and [Live Aid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Live_Aid).

## Legacy {#Legacy}

"For better or for worse, the world today is committed to accelerating change: radical, wrenching, erosive of both traditions and old values. Its Inheritors have grown up with rapid change, are better prepared to accommodate it than any in history, indeed embrace change as a virtue in itself. With his skeptical yet humanistic outlook, his disdain for fanaticism and his scorn for the spurious, the Man of the Year suggests that he will infuse the future with a new sense of morality, a transcendent and contemporary ethic that could infinitely enrich the ’empty society.'"In recognition of the historical significance of this demographic cohort, *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_%28magazine%29)* magazine designated the Baby Boomers "[Man of the Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_Person_of_the_Year)" in 1966.[^o268] The face most prominently seen on the cover representing the generation, called "The Inheritors" by *Time*, was that of [Thomas M. McLaughlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_M._McLaughlin), an alumnus of Yale University and a decorated U.S. Air Force pilot who served in Vietnam.[^o265][^o266] Due to their number, the Baby Boomers have been a dominant factor in American life since the time they were born.[^o111] While they were being born and growing up from the late 1940s to 1960s, American culture was centered around child-rearing. As young adults during the late 1960s and 1970s, the Boomers instigated a period of serious social and political turbulence.[^o111] While building their careers during the 1980s and 1990s, the Baby Boomers insisted on stability. When the youngest among them reached retirement in the early 2020s, they left behind a labor shortage.[^o111] In fact, retiring Baby Boomers were a contributing factor to the [Great Resignation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Resignation).[^o267] As Claire Raines points out in *Beyond Generation X* (1997), "never before in history had youth been so idealized as they were at this moment." According to her, when [Generation&nbsp;X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation&nbsp;X) came along, it had much to live up to.[^o268][^o269]

Although the fortunes of their nation may wax and wane, as individuals, American Baby Boomers have been quite ambitious in what they expect from life.[^o270] In spite of the fact that the seeds of expressive individualism had already been sown by the Silent Generation, it was the Baby Boomers who pushed for the social and cultural changes that echoed down the decades, many of which taken for granted by subsequent cohorts.[^o111][^o270] For instance, young women in the 2000s could pursue careers their grandmothers could only dream about, and young people of color could take advantage of opportunities not accessible to their ancestors.[^o270]

While older Boomers who attended colleges and universities were interested in learning new things, later Boomers and Generation X were more likely to say they wanted to make more money. In fact, by the late 2010s, this has become the dominant justification for higher education. Later Boomers and Generation X were also the first group to treat higher education as a commodity and to consider themselves as customers who were entitled to high grades.[^o271] Thanks to the Baby Boomers, materialism became the norm in American culture by the time Generation X came of age.[^o271]

The experimentation with marijuana and psychedelic drugs spearheaded by some of the baby boomers in their youth has continued to present day, leading in some countries to re-evaluation of these substances as useful medicinal and psychotherapeutic tools.[^o272][^o273]

Just as Paul Erlich's *[The Population Bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Population_Bomb)* (1968) was released, feminist movements were spreading all across the Western world. As access to education improved and contraception became readily available, women during the 1970s and 1980s became a lot more willing to delay or eschew marriage and to reduce the number of children, if any, they had. Because so many women during this period seized the opportunity to control their fertility, they have had a significant impact on the trajectory of human history.[^o274]

This intentional reduction of fertility occurred not just in Western countries, but also in places like India and Iran. Consequently, the predictions of Erlich failed to match reality. This development paved the way for the phenomenon of population aging observed in many countries around the world in the early twenty-first century.[^o274] Geopolitical analyst [Peter Zeihan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Zeihan) predicted that this demographic trend would result in "accelerating population falls unparalleled in speed and depth by any peacetime event in human history with the singular exception of the [Black Plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death)." He noted, however, that baby boomers in Australia, New Zealand, Cyprus, Ireland, Iceland, and the United States had enough children so that their nations were not aging as rapidly as other developed and even some developing countries.[^o14]

According to the United Nations, between 2015 and 2040, East Asia would be the world's fastest aging region on Earth, with a forecasted increase in median age of 9.5 years. U.N. data also shows that the region has been aging faster than the global average since the mid-1970s. On one hand, this reflects dramatic improvements in standards of living compared to the 1960s; on the other hand, many East Asian polities, such as South Korea, are facing a demographic time bomb.[^o275]
