# Ideal on a set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ideal on a set"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **ideal** on a set is a [family](wikipedia://en/723043) of subsets that is closed under subsets and finite unions. Informally, sets that belong to the ideal are considered "small" or "negligible".

:::

The concept is generalized both by [ideals on a partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/530559) (an ideal on a set $X$ is an ideal on the powerset $\mathcal{P}(X)$ partially ordered by inclusion), and by [ideals on rings](wikipedia://en/25977) (an ideal on $X$ is an ideal on the [Boolean ring](wikipedia://en/54356) $\mathcal{P}(X)$). The notion dual to ideals is [filters](wikipedia://en/12646549).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a set $X$, an **ideal** $\mathcal{I}$ on $X$ is a set of subsets of $X$ such that:

- $\mathcal{I}$ is [downwards-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downwards-closed_set): If $A, B \subseteq X$ are such that $A \in \mathcal{I}$ and $B \subseteq A$ then $B \in \mathcal{I}$,
- $\mathcal{I}$ is closed under finite unions: $\varnothing \in I$, and if $A \in \mathcal{I}$ and $B \in \mathcal{I}$ then $A \cup B \in \mathcal{I}$.

A *proper ideal* is an ideal that is proper as a subset of the powerset $\mathcal{P}(X)$. By contrast, $\mathcal{P}(X)$ itself, consisting of all possible subsets, is called the *improper ideal*. By downwards-closure, an ideal is proper if and only if it does not contain $X$. Some authors adopt the convention that an ideal must be proper by definition.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

An element of an ideal $I$ is said to be $I$-null or $I$-negligible, or simply null or negligible if the ideal $I$ is understood from context. If $I$ is an ideal on $X,$ then a subset of $X$ is said to be $I$-positive (or just positive) if it is not an element of $I.$ The collection of all $I$-positive subsets of $X$ is denoted $I^+.$

If $I$ is a proper ideal on $X$ and for every $A \subseteq X$ either $A \in I$ or $X \setminus A \in I,$ then $I$ is a **prime ideal**.

:::

## Examples of ideals {#Examples_of_ideals}

### General examples {#General_examples}

- For any set $X$ and any arbitrarily chosen subset $B \subseteq X,$ the subsets of $B$ form an ideal on $X.$ For finite $X,$ all ideals are of this form.
- The [finite subsets](wikipedia://en/11742) of any set $X$ form an ideal on $X.$
- For any [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270), subsets of sets of measure zero.
- For any [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270), sets of [finite measure](wikipedia://en/4316185). This encompasses finite subsets (using [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786)) and small sets below.
- A [bornology](wikipedia://en/9584635) on a set $X$ is an ideal that [covers](wikipedia://en/317552) $X.$
- A non-empty family $\mathcal{B}$ of subsets of $X$ is a proper ideal on $X$ if and only if its dual in $X,$ which is denoted and defined by $X \setminus \mathcal{B} := \{X \setminus B : B \in \mathcal{B}\},$ is a proper [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549) on $X$ (a filter is proper if it is not equal to $\wp(X)$). The dual of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) $\wp(X)$ is itself; that is, $X \setminus \wp(X) = \wp(X).$ Thus a non-empty family $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \wp(X)$ is an ideal on $X$ if and only if its dual $X \setminus \mathcal{B}$ is a [dual ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_ideal) on $X$ (which by definition is either the power set $\wp(X)$ or else a proper filter on $X$).

### Ideals on the natural numbers {#Ideals_on_the_natural_numbers}

- The ideal of all finite sets of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) is denoted Fin.
- The summable ideal on the natural numbers, denoted $\mathcal{I}_{1/n},$ is the collection of all sets $A$ of natural numbers such that the sum $\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n+1}$ is finite. See [small set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_set_%28combinatorics%29).
- The ideal of asymptotically zero-density sets on the natural numbers, denoted $\mathcal{Z}_0,$ is the collection of all sets $A$ of natural numbers such that the fraction of natural numbers less than $n$ that belong to $A,$ tends to zero as $n$ tends to infinity. (That is, the [asymptotic density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_density) of $A$ is zero.)

### Ideals on the real numbers {#Ideals_on_the_real_numbers}

- The measure ideal is the collection of all sets $A$ of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) such that the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) of $A$ is zero.
- The meager ideal is the collection of all [meager sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meager_set) of real numbers.

### Ideals on other sets {#Ideals_on_other_sets}

- If $\lambda$ is an [ordinal number](wikipedia://en/26547932) of uncountable [cofinality](wikipedia://en/6693), the nonstationary ideal on $\lambda$ is the collection of all subsets of $\lambda$ that are not [stationary sets](wikipedia://en/1679067). This ideal has been studied extensively by [W. Hugh Woodin](wikipedia://en/2275071).

## Operations on ideals {#Operations_on_ideals}

Given ideals $I$ and $J$ on underlying sets $X$ and $Y$ respectively, one forms the *skew* or *Fubini* product $I \times J$, an ideal on the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $X \times Y,$ as follows: For any subset $A \subseteq X \times Y,$

$$A \in I \times J \quad \text{ if and only if } \quad \{ x \in X \; : \; \{y : \langle x, y \rangle \in A\} \not\in J \} \in I$$

That is, a set lies in the product ideal if only a negligible collection of $x$-coordinates correspond to a non-negligible slice of $A$ in the $y$-direction. (Perhaps clearer: A set is positive in the product ideal if positively many $x$-coordinates correspond to positive slices.)

An ideal $I$ on a set $X$ induces an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on $\wp(X),$ the powerset of $X$, considering $A$ and $B$ to be equivalent (for $A, B$ subsets of $X$) if and only if the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of $A$ and $B$ is an element of $I$. The [quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set) of $\wp(X)$ by this equivalence relation is a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959), denoted $\wp(X) / I$ (read "P of $X$ mod $I$").

To every ideal there is a corresponding [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549), called its dual filter. If $I$ is an ideal on $X$, then the dual filter of $I$ is the collection of all sets $X \setminus A,$ where $A$ is an element of $I$. Here $X \setminus A$ denotes the [relative complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_complement) of $A$ in $X$; that is, the collection of all elements of $X$ that are not in $A$.

## Relationships among ideals {#Relationships_among_ideals}

If $I$ and $J$ are ideals on $X$ and $Y$ respectively, $I$ and $J$ are Rudin–Keisler isomorphic if they are the same ideal except for renaming of the elements of their underlying sets (ignoring negligible sets). More formally, the requirement is that there be sets $A$ and $B,$ elements of $I$ and $J$ respectively, and a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) $\varphi : X \setminus A \to Y \setminus B,$ such that for any subset $C \subseteq X,$ $C \in I$ if and only if the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of $C$ under $\varphi \in J.$

If $I$ and $J$ are Rudin–Keisler isomorphic, then $\wp(X) / I$ and $\wp(Y) / J$ are isomorphic as Boolean algebras. Isomorphisms of quotient Boolean algebras induced by Rudin–Keisler isomorphisms of ideals are called trivial isomorphisms.
