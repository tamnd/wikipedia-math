# CA-group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="CA-group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the realm of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is said to be a **CA-group** or **centralizer abelian group** if the [centralizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) of any non-[identity](wikipedia://en/14962) element is an [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011). [Finite](wikipedia://en/323707) CA-groups are of historical importance as an early example of the type of classifications that would be used in the [Feit–Thompson theorem](wikipedia://en/1656473) and the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). Several important infinite groups are CA-groups, such as [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735), [Tarski monsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski_monster), and some [Burnside groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnside_group), and the [locally finite](wikipedia://en/4939270) CA-groups have been classified explicitly. CA-groups are also called **commutative-transitive groups** (or **CT-groups** for short) because commutativity is a [transitive relation](wikipedia://en/200463) amongst the non-identity elements of a group if and only if the group is a CA-group.

:::

## History {#History}

Locally finite CA-groups were classified by several mathematicians from 1925 to 1998. First, finite CA-groups were shown to be [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) or [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) in (Weisner 1925). Then in the [Brauer–Suzuki–Wall theorem](wikipedia://en/29594411) (Brauer Suzuki Wall 1958), finite CA-groups of even [order](wikipedia://en/493527) were shown to be [Frobenius groups](wikipedia://en/1716123), abelian groups, or 2-dimensional [projective special linear groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) of even order, PSL(2, 2<sup>*f*</sup>) for *f* ≥ 2. Finally, finite CA-groups of odd order were shown to be Frobenius groups or abelian groups in (Suzuki 1957), and so in particular, are never nonabelian simple.

CA-groups were important in the context of the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). [Michio Suzuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michio_Suzuki_%28mathematician%29) showed that every finite, simple, nonabelian CA-group is of even order. This result was first extended to the Feit–Hall–Thompson theorem showing that finite, simple, nonabelian [CN-groups](wikipedia://en/5745210) had even order, and then to the [Feit–Thompson theorem](wikipedia://en/1656473) which states that every finite, simple, nonabelian group is of even order. A textbook exposition of the classification of finite CA-groups is given as example 1 and 2 in (Suzuki 1986, 291–305). A more detailed description of the Frobenius groups appearing is included in (Wu 1998), where it is shown that a finite, solvable CA-group is a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of an abelian group and a fixed-point-free automorphism, and that conversely every such semidirect product is a finite, solvable CA-group. Wu also extended the classification of Suzuki et al. to [locally finite groups](wikipedia://en/4939270).

## Examples {#Examples}

Every abelian group is a CA-group, and a group with a non-[trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) [center](wikipedia://en/7125) is a CA-group if and only if it is abelian. The finite CA-groups are classified: the solvable ones are semidirect products of abelian groups by [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) such that every non-trivial element acts fixed-point-freely and include groups such as the [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267) of order 4*k*+2, and the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) on 4 points of order 12, while the nonsolvable ones are all simple and are the 2-dimensional projective special linear groups PSL(2, 2<sup>*n*</sup>) for *n* ≥ 2. Infinite CA-groups include free groups, [PSL(2, **R**)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSL2%28R%29), and [Burnside groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnside_group) of large [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) exponent, (Lyndon Schupp 2001, 10). Some more recent results in the infinite case are included in (Wu 1998), including a classification of locally finite CA-groups. Wu also observes that [Tarski monsters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski_monster) are obvious examples of infinite simple CA-groups.
