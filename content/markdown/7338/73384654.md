# Chessboard complex

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chessboard complex"}

A **chessboard complex** is a particular kind of [abstract simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/723105), which has various applications in [topological graph theory](wikipedia://en/1631772) and [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).[^o2][^o3] Informally, the (*m*, *n*)-chessboard complex contains all sets of positions on an *m*-by-*n* [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard), where [rooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rook_%28chess%29) can be placed without attacking each other. Equivalently, it is the [matching complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matching_complex) of the (*m*, *n*)-[complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320), or the [independence complex](wikipedia://en/64659151) of the *m*-by-*n* [rook's graph](wikipedia://en/7324959).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

For any two positive integers *m* and *n*, the **(*m, n*)-chessboard complex** $\Delta_{m,n}$ is the [abstract simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/723105) with vertex set $[m]\times [n]$ that contains all subsets *S* such that, if $(i_1,j_1)$ and $(i_2,j_2)$ are two distinct elements of *S*, then both $i_1\neq i_2$ and $j_1\neq j_2$. The vertex set can be viewed as a two-dimensional grid (a "chessboard"), and the complex contains all subsets *S* that do *not* contain two cells in the same row or in the same column. In other words, all subset *S* such that rooks can be placed on them without taking each other.

The chessboard complex can also be defined succinctly using [deleted join](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deleted_join). Let *D<sub>m</sub>* be a set of *m* discrete points. Then the chessboard complex is the *n*-fold 2-wise [deleted join](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deleted_join) of *D<sub>m</sub>*, denoted by *$(D_m)^{*n}_{\Delta(2)}$*.p. 176

Another definition is the set of all [matchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matching_%28cards%29) in the complete [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) $K_{m,n}$.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In any (*m*,*n*)-chessboard complex, the neighborhood of each vertex has the structure of a (*m* − 1,*n* − 1)-chessboard complex. In terms of chess rooks, placing one rook on the board eliminates the remaining squares in the same row and column, leaving a smaller set of rows and columns where additional rooks can be placed. This allows the topological structure of a chessboard to be studied hierarchically, based on its lower-dimensional structures. An example of this occurs with the (4,5)-chessboard complex, and the (3,4)- and (2,3)-chessboard complexes within it:[^o9]

- The (2,3)-chessboard complex is a [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733), consisting of six vertices (the six squares of the chessboard) connected by six edges (pairs of non-attacking squares).
- The (3,4)-chessboard complex is a triangulation of a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), with 24 triangles (triples of non-attacking squares), 36 edges, and 12 vertices. Six triangles meet at each vertex, in the same hexagonal pattern as the (2,3)-chessboard complex.
- The (4,5)-chessboard complex forms a three-dimensional [pseudomanifold](wikipedia://en/28247411): in the neighborhood of each vertex, 24 tetrahedra meet, in the pattern of a torus, instead of the spherical pattern that would be required of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). If the vertices are removed from this space, the result can be given a geometric structure as a cusped [hyperbolic 3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1237700), topologically equivalent to the [link complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link_complement) of a 20-component [link](wikipedia://en/1161784).

## Properties {#Properties}

Every facet of $\Delta_{m,n}$ contains $\min(m,n)$ elements. Therefore, the dimension of $\Delta_{m,n}$ is $\min(m,n)-1$.

The [homotopical connectivity](wikipedia://en/6519310) of the chessboard complex is at least $\min\left(m, n, \frac{m+n+1}{3}\right)-2$ (so $\eta \geq \min\left(m, n, \frac{m+n+1}{3}\right)$).[^o2]

The [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262) $b_{r - 1}$ of chessboard complexes are zero if and only if $(m - r)(n - r) > r$.[^o12]p. 200 The eigenvalues of the [combinatorial Laplacians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_Laplacian) of the chessboard complex are integers.[^o12]p. 193

The chessboard complex is $(\nu_{m, n} - 1)$-connected, where $\nu_{m, n} := \min\{m, n, \lfloor\frac{m + n + 1}{3}\rfloor \}$.[^o13]p. 527 The [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) $H_{\nu_{m, n}}(M_{m, n})$ is a [3-group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-group) of exponent at most 9, and is known to be exactly the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) on 3 elements when $m + n \equiv 1\pmod{3}$.[^o13]p. 543–555

The $(\lfloor\frac{n + m + 1}{3}\rfloor - 1)$-skeleton of chessboard complex is *[vertex decomposable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_decomposable)* in the sense of Provan and Billera (and thus [shellable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shellable)), and the entire complex is vertex decomposable if $n\geq 2m - 1$.[^o14]p. 3 As a corollary, any position of *k* rooks on a *m*-by-*n* chessboard, where $k\leq\lfloor\frac{m + n + 1}{3}\rfloor$, can be transformed into any other position using at most $mn - k$ single-rook moves (where each intermediate position is also not rook-taking).[^o14]p. 3

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The complex $\Delta_{n_1,\ldots,n_k}$ is a "chessboard complex" defined for a *k*-dimensional chessboard. Equivalently, it is the set of [matchings](wikipedia://en/63030703) in a complete *k*-partite [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535). This complex is at least $(\nu - 2)$-connected, for $\nu := \min\{n_1, \lfloor\frac{n_1 + n_2 + 1}{3}\rfloor, \dots, \lfloor\frac{n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_k + 1}{2k + 1}\rfloor\}$ [^o2]p. 33

:::
