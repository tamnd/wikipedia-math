# Essentially unique

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Essentially unique"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the term **essentially unique** is used to describe a weaker form of uniqueness, where an object satisfying a property is "unique" only in the sense that all objects satisfying the property are equivalent to each other. The notion of essential uniqueness presupposes some form of "sameness", which is often formalized using an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259).

:::

A related notion is a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245), where an object is not only essentially unique, but unique *[up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a unique [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)*[^o2] (meaning that it has trivial [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772)). In general there can be more than one isomorphism between examples of an essentially unique object.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Set theory {#Set_theory}

At the most basic level, there is an essentially unique set of any given [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174), whether one labels the elements $\{1,2,3\}$ or $\{a,b,c\}$. In this case, the non-uniqueness of the isomorphism (e.g., match 1 to $a$ or 1 to *$c$*) is reflected in the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901).

On the other hand, there is an essentially unique [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered) set of any given finite cardinality that is unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) unique isomorphism: if one writes $\{1 < 2 < 3\}$ and $\{a< b< c\}$, then the only [order-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-preserving) isomorphism is the one which maps 1 to *$a$,* 2 to *$b$,* and 3 to *$c$.*

### Number theory {#Number_theory}

The [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556) establishes that the [factorization](wikipedia://en/82341) of any positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) into [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) is essentially unique, i.e., unique up to the ordering of the prime [factors](wikipedia://en/49492).[^o7][^o8]

### Group theory {#Group_theory}

In the context of classification of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), there is an essentially unique group containing exactly 2 elements.[^o8] Similarly, there is also an essentially unique group containing exactly 3 elements: the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order three. In fact, regardless of how one chooses to write the three elements and denote the group operation, all such groups can be shown to be [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to each other, and hence are "the same".

On the other hand, there does not exist an essentially unique group with exactly 4 elements, as there are in this case two non-isomorphic groups in total: the cyclic group of order 4 and the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928).[^o10]

### Measure theory {#Measure_theory}

There is an essentially unique measure that is [translation](wikipedia://en/204682)-[invariant](wikipedia://en/7635201), [strictly positive](wikipedia://en/6607051) and [locally finite](wikipedia://en/6621919) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line). In fact, any such measure must be a constant multiple of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), specifying that the measure of the unit interval should be 1—before determining the solution uniquely.

### Topology {#Topology}

There is an essentially unique two-dimensional, [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470): the [2-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-sphere). In this case, it is unique up to [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660).

In the area of topology known as [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008), there is an analogue of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic: the decomposition of a knot into a sum of [prime knots](wikipedia://en/315030) is essentially unique.[^o13]

### Lie theory {#Lie_theory}

A [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726) of a [semisimple Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) may not be unique, but is unique up to [conjugation](wikipedia://en/49176#Conjugacy_of_subgroups_and_general_subsets).

### Category theory {#Category_theory}

An object that is the [limit](wikipedia://en/39248) or colimit over a given diagram is essentially unique, as there is a *unique* isomorphism to any other limiting/colimiting object.[^o16]

### Coding theory {#Coding_theory}

Given the task of using 24-[bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) words to store 12 bits of information in such a way that 4-bit errors can be detected and 3-bit errors can be corrected, the solution is essentially unique: the [extended binary Golay code](wikipedia://en/344971).[^o18]
