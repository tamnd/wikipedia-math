# Involutory matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Involutory matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **involutory matrix** is a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) that is its own [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_matrix). That is, multiplication by the matrix $\mathbf A_{n \times n}$ is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) if and only if $\mathbf A^2 = \mathbf I,$ where $\mathbf I$ is the $n \times n$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). Involutory matrices are all [square roots](wikipedia://en/2929701) of the identity matrix. This is a consequence of the fact that any [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122) multiplied by its inverse is the identity.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The $2\times2$ [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a & b \\ c & -a \end{pmatrix}$ is involutory provided that $a^2 + bc = 1 .$[^o5]

The [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) in $M(2,\mathbb{C})$ are involutory:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_1 = \sigma_x &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\ \sigma_2 = \sigma_y &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\ \sigma_3 = \sigma_z &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

One of the three classes of [elementary matrix](wikipedia://en/5897031) is involutory, namely the row-interchange elementary matrix. A special case of another class of elementary matrix, that which represents multiplication of a row or column by −1, is also involutory; it is in fact a trivial example of a [signature matrix](wikipedia://en/7887913), all of which are involutory.

Some simple examples of involutory matrices are shown below.

$$\begin{array}{cc} \mathbf{I} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} ; & \mathbf{I}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \\ \\ \mathbf{R} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} ; & \mathbf{R}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \\ \\ \mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} +1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} ; & \mathbf{S}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} +1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\ \end{array}$$

where

- $\mathbf{I}$ is the 3 × 3 identity matrix (which is trivially involutory);
- $\mathbf{R}$ is the 3 × 3 identity matrix with a pair of interchanged rows;
- $\mathbf{S}$ is a [signature matrix](wikipedia://en/7887913).

Any [block-diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block-diagonal_matrix) constructed from involutory matrices will also be involutory, as a consequence of the linear independence of the blocks.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Symmetry"}

## Symmetry {#Symmetry}

An involutory matrix which is also [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) is an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620), and thus represents an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) (a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) which preserves [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932)). Conversely every orthogonal involutory matrix is symmetric.[^o8] As a special case of this, every [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28linear_algebra%29) and 180° [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005) is involutory.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

An involution is [non-defective](wikipedia://en/5302952), and each [eigenvalue](wikipedia://en/2161429) equals $\pm 1$, so an involution [diagonalizes](wikipedia://en/246325) to a signature matrix.

A [normal](wikipedia://en/170366) involution is [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) (complex) or symmetric (real) and also [unitary](wikipedia://en/129222) (complex) or orthogonal (real).

The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of an involutory matrix over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is ±1.[^o11]

If $\mathbf{A}$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, then $\mathbf{A}$ is involutory if and only if $\mathbf P_+ = (\mathbf I + \mathbf A)/2$ is [idempotent](wikipedia://en/2974863). This relation gives a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between involutory matrices and idempotent matrices.[^o11] Similarly, $\mathbf{A}$ is involutory if and only if $\mathbf P_- = (\mathbf I - \mathbf A)/2$ is [idempotent](wikipedia://en/2974863). These two operators form the symmetric and antisymmetric projections $v_\pm = \mathbf P_\pm v$ of a vector $v = v_+ + v_-$ with respect to the involution $\mathbf{A}$, in the sense that $\mathbf Av_\pm = \pm v_\pm$, or $\mathbf{A P}_\pm = \pm \mathbf P_\pm$. The same construct applies to any [involutory function](wikipedia://en/293450), such as the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) (real and imaginary parts), [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) (symmetric and antisymmetric matrices), and [Hermitian adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) ([Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) and [skew-Hermitian](wikipedia://en/509788) matrices).

If $\mathbf{A}$ is an involutory matrix in $M(n, \mathbb{R}),$ which is a [matrix algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_algebra) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), and $\mathbf{A}$ is not a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{I}$, then the [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) $\{x \mathbf I + y \mathbf A: x y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ [generated by](wikipedia://en/3171371) $\mathbf{A}$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559).

If $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are two involutory matrices which [commute](wikipedia://en/25264990) with each other (i.e. $\mathbf{AB} = \mathbf{BA}$) then $\mathbf{AB}$ is also involutory.

If $\mathbf{A}$ is an involutory matrix then every [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) [power](wikipedia://en/125280#Powers_of_a_matrix) of $\mathbf{A}$ is involutory. In fact, $\mathbf{A}^{n}$ will be equal to $\mathbf{A}$ if $n$ is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) and $\mathbf{I}$ if $n$ is [even](wikipedia://en/143135).
