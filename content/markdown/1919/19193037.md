# Regular skew polyhedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular skew polyhedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **regular skew polyhedra** are generalizations to the set of [regular polyhedra](wikipedia://en/333299) which include the possibility of nonplanar [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) or [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719). [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter) looked at skew vertex figures which created new 4-dimensional regular polyhedra, and much later [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) looked at regular skew faces.[^o2]

:::

Infinite regular skew polyhedra that span 3-space or higher are called [regular skew apeirohedra](wikipedia://en/45592828).

## History {#History}

According to [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter), in 1926 [John Flinders Petrie](wikipedia://en/23915649) generalized the concept of [regular skew polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon) (nonplanar polygons) to *regular skew polyhedra*.

Coxeter offered a modified [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) $\{l,m|n\}$ for these figures, with $\{l,m\}$ implying the [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719), $m$ $l$-gons around a vertex, and $n$-gonal holes. Their vertex figures are [skew polygons](wikipedia://en/6545461), zig-zagging between two planes.

The regular skew polyhedra, represented by $\{l,m|n\}$, follow this equation:

$$2 \cos \frac{\pi}{l} \cos \frac{\pi}{m} = \cos \frac{\pi}{n}$$

A first set $\{l,m|n\}$, repeats the five convex [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905), and one nonconvex [Kepler–Poinsot solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler–Poinsot_solid):

[Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter) also enumerated the a larger set of finite regular polyhedra in his paper "regular skew polyhedra in three and four dimensions, and their topological analogues".

Just like the infinite skew polyhedra represent manifold surfaces between the cells of the [convex uniform honeycombs](wikipedia://en/2961), the finite forms all represent manifold surfaces within the cells of the [uniform 4-polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polychoron).

Polyhedra of the form {2p, 2q | r} are related to [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) symmetry of \[(p,r,q,r)\], which reduces to the linear \[r,p,r\] when q is 2. Coxeter gives these symmetry as \[\[(*p*,*r*,*q*,*r*)\]<sup>\+</sup>\] which he says is isomorphic to his [abstract group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_group) (2*p*,2*q*|2,*r*). The related honeycomb has the extended symmetry \[\[(*p*,*r*,*q*,*r*)\]\].[^o8]

{2p,4|r} is represented by the {2p} faces of the [bitruncated](wikipedia://en/6792316) {r,p,r} [uniform 4-polytope](wikipedia://en/1645899), and {4,2p|r} is represented by square faces of the [runcinated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runcination) {r,p,r}.

{4,4|n} produces a *n*-*n* [duoprism](wikipedia://en/2114155), and specifically {4,4|4} fits inside of a {4}x{4} [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112).

## Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

Regular skew polyhedra can also be constructed in dimensions higher than 4 as [embeddings](wikipedia://en/52119) into regular polytopes or honeycombs. For example, the regular icosahedron can be embedded into the vertices of the [6-demicube](wikipedia://en/10275945); this was named the **regular skew icosahedron** by [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328). The dodecahedron can be similarly embedded into the [10-demicube](wikipedia://en/18335434).[^o17]
