# Allegory (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Allegory"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), an **allegory** is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) that has some of the structure of the category **Rel** of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) and [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931) between them. Allegories can be used as an abstraction of categories of relations, and in this sense the theory of allegories is a generalization of [relation algebra](wikipedia://en/4363670) to relations between different sorts. Allegories are also useful in defining and investigating certain constructions in category theory, such as [exact](wikipedia://en/9790950) completions.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An allegory is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) in which

- every morphism $R : X\to Y$ is associated with an **anti-involution**, i.e. a morphism $R^\circ : Y\to X$ with $R^{\circ\circ} = R$ and $(RS)^\circ = S^\circ R^\circ\text{;}$ and
- every pair of morphisms $R,S : X\to Y$ with common domain/codomain is associated with an **intersection**, i.e. a morphism $R \cap S : X\to Y$

all such that

- intersections are [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent): $R\cap R = R,$ [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative): $R\cap S = S\cap R,$ and [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative): $(R\cap S)\cap T = R\cap (S\cap T);$
- anti-involution [distributes](wikipedia://en/103118) over intersection: $(R\cap S)^\circ = R^\circ \cap S^\circ;$
- composition is semi-distributive over intersection: $R(S\cap T) \subseteq RS\cap RT$ and $(R\cap S)T \subseteq RT\cap ST;$ and
- the modularity law is satisfied: $RS \cap T \subseteq (R\cap TS^\circ)S.$

Here, the order defined by the intersection $R \subseteq S$ is used as an abbreviation of $R = R\cap S.$

A first example of an allegory is the [category of sets and relations](wikipedia://en/5930730). The [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) of this allegory are sets, and a morphism $X \to Y$ is a binary relation between $X$ and $Y$. Composition of morphisms is [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170), and the anti-involution of $R$ is the [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020) $R^\circ$; we have $y R^\circ x$ if and only if $xRy$. Intersection of morphisms is (set-theoretic) [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of relations.

:::

## Regular categories and allegories {#Regular_categories_and_allegories}

### Allegories of relations in regular categories {#Allegories_of_relations_in_regular_categories}

In a category $C$, a **relation** between objects $X$ and $Y$ is a [span](wikipedia://en/10318351) of morphisms $X\gets R\to Y$ that is jointly [monic](wikipedia://en/59538). Two such spans $X\gets S\to Y$ and $X\gets T\to Y$ are considered equivalent when there is an isomorphism between $S$ and $T$ that make everything commute; strictly speaking, relations are only defined up to equivalence (one may formalise this either by using [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) or by using [bicategories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicategory)). If the category $C$ has products, a relation between $X$ and $Y$ is the same thing as a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) into $X\times Y$ (or an equivalence class of such). In the presence of [pullbacks](wikipedia://en/841689) and a proper [factorization system](wikipedia://en/7149012), one can define the composition of relations. The composition $X\gets R\to Y\gets S\to Z$ is found by first pulling back the cospan $R\to Y\gets S$ and then taking the jointly-monic image of the resulting span $X\gets R\gets\bullet\to S\to Z$.

Composition of relations will be associative if the factorization system is appropriately stable. In this case, one can consider a category $\operatorname{Rel}(C)$, with the same objects as $C$, but where morphisms are relations between the objects. The identity relations are the diagonals $X \to X\times X.$

A [regular category](wikipedia://en/9790950) (a category with finite limits and images in which covers are stable under pullback) has a stable regular epi/mono factorization system. The category of relations for a regular category is always an allegory. Anti-involution is defined by turning the source/target of the relation around, and intersections are intersections of [subobjects](wikipedia://en/540476), computed by pullback.

### Maps in allegories, and tabulations {#Maps_in_allegories,_and_tabulations}

A morphism $R$ in an allegory $A$ is called a **map** if it is entire $(1\subseteq R^\circ R)$ and deterministic $(RR^\circ \subseteq 1).$ Another way of saying this is that a map is a morphism that has a [right adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) in $A$ when $A$ is considered, using the local order structure, as a [2-category](wikipedia://en/2740708). Maps in an allegory are closed under identity and composition. Thus, there is a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) $\operatorname{Map}(A)$ of $A$ with the same objects but only the maps as morphisms. For a regular category $C$, there is an isomorphism of categories $C \cong \operatorname{Map}(\operatorname{Rel}(C)).$ In particular, a morphism in $\operatorname{Map}(\operatorname{Rel}(\mathbf{Set}))$ is just an ordinary [set function](wikipedia://en/185427).

In an allegory, a morphism $R : X\to Y$ is **tabulated** by a pair of maps $f : Z\to X$ and $g : Z\to Y$ if $gf^\circ = R$ and $f^\circ f \cap g^\circ g = 1$. An allegory is called **tabular** if every morphism has a tabulation. For a regular category $C$, the allegory $\operatorname{Rel}(C)$ is always tabular. On the other hand, for any tabular allegory $A$, the category $\operatorname{Map}(A)$ of maps is a locally regular category: it has pullbacks, [equalizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equalizer_%28mathematics%29), and images that are stable under pullback. This is enough to study relations in $\operatorname{Map}(A)$, and in this setting, $A\cong \operatorname{Rel}(\operatorname{Map}(A)).$

### Unital allegories and regular categories of maps {#Unital_allegories_and_regular_categories_of_maps}

A **unit** in an allegory is an object $U$ for which the identity is the largest morphism $U\to U$, and such that from every other object, there is an entire relation to $U$. An allegory with a unit is called **unital**. Given a tabular allegory $A$, the category $\operatorname{Map}(A)$ is a regular category (it has a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object)) if and only if $A$ is unital.

### More sophisticated kinds of allegory {#More_sophisticated_kinds_of_allegory}

Additional properties of allegories can be axiomatized. **Distributive allegories** have a [union](wikipedia://en/47949)-like operation that is suitably well-behaved, and **division allegories** have a generalization of the division operation of [relation algebra](wikipedia://en/4363670). **Power allegories** are distributive division allegories with additional [powerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerset)-like structure. The connection between allegories and regular categories can be developed into a connection between power allegories and [toposes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toposes).
