# Texas Instruments signing key controversy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Texas Instruments signing key controversy"}

*Image: Ti83plus.jpg.* A TI-83+ graphing calculator displaying a [sine wave](wikipedia://en/324749)

The **Texas Instruments signing key controversy** resulted from [Texas Instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Instruments)' (TI) response to a project to [factorize](wikipedia://en/1544741) the 512-[bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29) [cryptographic keys](wikipedia://en/53039) needed to write custom [firmware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firmware) to TI devices.

:::

## Project {#Project}

In July 2009, Benjamin Moody, a United-TI [forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_forum) user, published the [factors](wikipedia://en/15491) of a 512-bit [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29) key used to sign the [TI-83+ series](wikipedia://en/439710) graphing calculator. The discovery of the private key would allow end users to [flash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flash_memory) their own [operating systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_system) onto the device without having to use any special hardware. Moody used two [free software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_software) implementations of the [general number field sieve](wikipedia://en/152734), msieve and ggnfs; the computation took 73 days on a 1.9 GHz dual-core processor. This demonstrates the progress of hardware development: the factorization of the similar 512-bit [RSA-155](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA-155) in 1999 using the same algorithm required a large dedicated research group, 8000 MIPS-years of computing time, and a [Cray C916](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cray_C90) supercomputer.[^o3]

In response, members of the wider TI graphing calculators community (at yAronet) set up a [BOINC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOINC)-based [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing) project, RSA Lattice Siever (RSALS for short), that quickly factored the other keys.[^o4] RSA Lattice Siever remained active for nearly three years after outliving its initial purpose, by factoring other integers for the mathematical community. After factoring over 400 integers,[^o5] RSALS moved to RSALS-inspired NFS@home[^o6] at the end of August 2012.

## Legal response {#Legal_response}

Texas Instruments began by sending out two initial [Digital Millennium Copyright Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Millennium_Copyright_Act) (DMCA) take-down requests to the hackers, referring to sites or forum posts that they controlled.[^o8][^o9] The hackers responded by removing the keys, without consulting an attorney.[^o10] TI then sent further DMCA notices to a variety[^o11] of [websites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Website) displaying the keys, including United-TI, [Reddit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reddit), and [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia).[^o12] Texas Instruments' efforts then became subject to the [Streisand effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streisand_effect),[^o13] and the keys were mirrored on a number of sites, including [WikiLeaks](wikipedia://en/8877168).[^o14] In September 2009, Dan Goodin from *[The Register](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Register)* alerted the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation) (EFF) to TI's actions, and the EFF agreed to take on the case *[pro bono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_bono)*, representing three people who had received DMCA notices.

On October 13, 2009, the EFF sent a letter to TI warning them that the posting of the keys did not violate the DMCA, and that it might be liable for [misrepresentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misrepresentation).[^o15] Despite the letter by the EFF, TI continued to send DMCA notices to websites that posted the keys, but stopped doing so after late 2009. The EFF filed a [DMCA Section 512 counter-notice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DMCA_512) on behalf of three of the bloggers who received DMCA notices. When the EFF did not receive a response by the deadline, the bloggers reposted the content that had been taken down.[^o16]

## Cryptographic keys {#Cryptographic_keys}

The public (or verifying) [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29) parameters of the original [TI-83+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-83+) / TI-83+ Silver Edition OS signing key factored by Benjamin Moody are the following 512-bit modulus *n* and exponent *e* (specified in [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263)):[^o18]

n = 82EF4009ED7CAC2A5EE12B5F8E8AD9A0AB9CC9F4F3E44B7E8BF2D57A2F2BEACE83424E1CFF0D2A5A7E2E53CB926D61F347DFAA4B35B205B5881CEB40B328E58F e = 11

By factoring *n*, Moody obtained the factors *p* (252 bits) and *q* (260 bits), which can be used in turn to quickly compute the 512-bit private (or [signing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Signature)) key $d=e^{-1} \bmod (p-1)(q-1)$:

p = B709D3A0CD2FEC08EAFCCF540D8A100BB38E5E091D646ADB7B14D021096FFCD q = B7207BD184E0B5A0B89832AA68849B29EDFB03FBA2E8917B176504F08A96246CB d = 4D0534BA8BB2BFA0740BFB6562E843C7EC7A58AE351CE11D43438CA239DD99276CD125FEBAEE5D2696579FA3A3958FF4FC54C685EAA91723BC8888F292947BA1

The value *d* can then be used to sign arbitrary OS software.

The keys factored by RSA Lattice Siever (the [TI-92+](wikipedia://en/354102#TI-92_Plus), [TI-73](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-73), [TI-89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-89), [Voyage 200](wikipedia://en/354102#Voyage_200), [TI-89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-89) Titanium, [TI-84+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-84+) / TI-84 Silver Edition OS signing and date-stamp signing keys) are similar but with different values of *n*, *p*, *q*, and *d*. A single date-stamp signing key is shared by all models.
