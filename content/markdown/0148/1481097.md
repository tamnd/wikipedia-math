# History of the separation axioms

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="History of the separation axioms"}

The **history of the [separation axioms](wikipedia://en/40117302)** in [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649) has been convoluted, with many meanings competing for the same terms and many terms competing for the same concept.

:::

## Origins {#Origins}

Before the current general definition of [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), there were many definitions offered, some of which assumed (what we now think of as) some separation axioms. For example, the definition given by [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989) in 1914 is equivalent to the modern definition plus the [Hausdorff separation axiom](wikipedia://en/13637).

The separation axioms, as a group, became important in the study of [metrisability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrisability): the question of which topological spaces can be given the [structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_%28mathematics%29) of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). Metric spaces satisfy all of the separation axioms; but in fact, studying spaces that satisfy only *some* axioms helps build up to the notion of full metrisability.

The separation axioms that were first studied together in this way were the axioms for [accessible spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accessible_space), [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637), [regular spaces](wikipedia://en/53058), and [normal spaces](wikipedia://en/48629). Topologists assigned these classes of spaces the names T<sub>1</sub>, T<sub>2</sub>, T<sub>3</sub>, and T<sub>4</sub>. Later this system of numbering was extended to include [T<sub>0</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T0_space), [T<sub>2½</sub>](wikipedia://en/327166), [T<sub>3½</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_regular_space) (or T<sub>π</sub>), [T<sub>5</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_normal_space), and [T<sub>6</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfectly_normal_space).

But this sequence had its problems. The idea was supposed to be that every T<sub>*i*</sub> space is a special kind of T<sub>*j*</sub> space if *i* > *j*. But this is not necessarily true, as definitions vary. For example, a regular space (called T<sub>3</sub>) does not have to be a Hausdorff space (called T<sub>2</sub>), at least not according to the simplest definition of regular spaces.

## Different definitions {#Different_definitions}

Every author agreed on T<sub>0</sub>, T<sub>1</sub>, and T<sub>2</sub>. For the other axioms, however, different authors could use significantly different definitions, depending on what they were working on. These differences could develop because, if one assumes that a topological space satisfies the T<sub>1</sub> axiom, then the various definitions are (in most cases) equivalent. Thus, if one is going to make that assumption, then one would want to use the simplest definition. But if one did not make that assumption, then the simplest definition might not be the right one for the most useful concept; in any case, it would destroy the (transitive) [entailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entailment) of T<sub>*i*</sub> by T<sub>*j*</sub>, allowing (for example) non-Hausdorff regular spaces.

Topologists working on the metrisation problem generally *did* assume T<sub>1</sub>; after all, all metric spaces are T<sub>1</sub>. Thus, they used the simplest definitions for the T<sub>*i*</sub>. Then, for those occasions when they did *not* assume T<sub>1</sub>, they used words ("regular" and "normal") for the more complicated definitions, in order to contrast them with the simpler ones. This approach was used as late as 1970 with the publication of *[Counterexamples in Topology](wikipedia://en/915449)* by [Lynn A. Steen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_A._Steen) and [J. Arthur Seebach, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Arthur_Seebach,_Jr.)

In contrast, [general topologists](wikipedia://en/178649), led by [John L. Kelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Kelley) in 1955, usually did not assume T<sub>1</sub>, so they studied the separation axioms in the greatest generality from the beginning. They used the more complicated definitions for T<sub>*i*</sub>, so that they would always have a nice property relating T<sub>*i*</sub> to T<sub>*j*</sub>. Then, for the simpler definitions, they used words (again, "regular" and "normal"). Both conventions could be said to follow the "original" meanings; the different meanings are the same for T<sub>1</sub> spaces, which was the original context. But the result was that different authors used the various terms in precisely opposite ways. Adding to the confusion, some literature will observe a nice distinction between an axiom and the space that satisfies the axiom, so that a T<sub>3</sub> *space* might need to satisfy the *axioms* T<sub>3</sub> and T<sub>0</sub> (e.g., in the *[Encyclopedic Dictionary of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/29902421)*, 2nd ed.).

Since 1970, the general topologists' terms have been growing in popularity, including in other branches of mathematics, such as [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_%28math%29). But usage is still not consistent.

## Completely Hausdorff, Urysohn, and T<sub>2$\tfrac{1}{2}$</sub> spaces {#Completely_Hausdorff,_Urysohn,_and_T2_spaces}

Steen and Seebach define a Urysohn space as "a space with a Urysohn function for any two points". Willard calls this a completely Hausdorff space. Steen & Seebach define a completely Hausdorff space or T<sub>2$\tfrac{1}{2}$</sub> space as a space in which every two points are separated by closed neighborhoods, which Willard calls a Urysohn space or T<sub>2$\tfrac{1}{2}$</sub> space.
