# Normal family

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Normal family"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), with special application to [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a *normal family* is a [pre-compact](wikipedia://en/596600) subset of the space of [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29). Informally, this means that the [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) in the family are not widely spread out, but rather stick together in a somewhat "clustered" manner. Note that a compact family of continuous functions is automatically a normal family. Sometimes, if each function in a normal family *F* satisfies a particular property (e.g. is [holomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic)), then the property also holds for each [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of the set *F*.

:::

More formally, let *X* and *Y* be [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450). The set of continuous functions $f: X \to Y$ has a natural [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) called the [compact-open topology](wikipedia://en/726335). A **normal family** is a [pre-compact](wikipedia://en/596600) subset with respect to this topology.

If *Y* is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), then the compact-open topology is equivalent to the topology of [compact convergence](wikipedia://en/7442564),[^o2] and we obtain a definition which is closer to the classical one: A collection *F* of continuous functions is called a **normal family** if every [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of functions in *F* contains a [subsequence](wikipedia://en/236098) which [converges uniformly on compact subsets](wikipedia://en/7442564) of *X* to a continuous function from *X* to *Y*. That is, for every sequence of functions in *F*, there is a subsequence $f_n(x)$ and a continuous function $f(x)$ from *X* to *Y* such that the following holds for every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subset *K* contained in *X*:

$$\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \sup_{x\in K} d_Y(f_n(x),f(x)) = 0$$

where $d_Y$ is the [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) of *Y*.

## Normal families of holomorphic functions {#Normal_families_of_holomorphic_functions}

The concept arose in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), that is the study of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110). In this case, *X* is an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), *Y* is the complex plane, and the metric on *Y* is given by $d_Y(y_1,y_2) = |y_1-y_2|$. As a consequence of [Cauchy's integral theorem](wikipedia://en/61190), a sequence of holomorphic functions that converges uniformly on compact sets must converge to a holomorphic function. That is, each [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of a normal family is holomorphic.

Normal families of holomorphic functions provide the quickest way of proving the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215).[^o4]

More generally, if the spaces *X* and *Y* are [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), and *Y* is equipped with the metric coming from the [uniformization theorem](wikipedia://en/288270), then each limit point of a normal family of holomorphic functions $f: X \to Y$ is also holomorphic.

For example, if *Y* is the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), then the metric of uniformization is the [spherical distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_distance). In this case, a holomorphic function from *X* to *Y* is called a [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857), and so each limit point of a normal family of meromorphic functions is a meromorphic function.

## Criteria {#Criteria}

In the classical context of holomorphic functions, there are several criteria that can be used to establish that a family is normal: [Montel's theorem](wikipedia://en/1484228) states that a family of locally bounded holomorphic functions is normal. The [Montel-Caratheodory](wikipedia://en/1484228) theorem states that the family of meromorphic functions that omit three distinct values in the [extended complex plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_complex_plane) is normal. For a family of holomorphic functions, this reduces to requiring two values omitted by viewing each function as a meromorphic function omitting the value infinity.

[Marty's theorem](wikipedia://en/64337173)[^o6] provides a criterion equivalent to normality in the context of meromorphic functions: A family $F$ of meromorphic functions from a [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643) $U \subset \mathbb{C}$ to the complex plane is a normal family if and only if for each compact subset *K* of *U* there exists a constant *C* so that for each $f \in F$ and each *z* in *K* we have

$$\frac{2|f'(z)|}{1 + |f(z)|^2} \leq C.$$

Indeed, the expression on the left is the formula for the [pull-back](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pull-back) of the [arclength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arclength) element on the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) to the complex plane via the inverse of [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431).

## History {#History}

[Paul Montel](wikipedia://en/1796347) first coined the term "normal family" in 1911.[^o8][^o9] Because the concept of a normal family has continually been very important to complex analysis, Montel's terminology is still used to this day, even though from a modern perspective, the phrase *pre-compact subset* might be preferred by some mathematicians. Note that though the notion of compact open topology generalizes and clarifies the concept, in many applications the original definition is more practical.
