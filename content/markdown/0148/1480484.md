# Menelaus's theorem

*Image: Menelaus' theorem 1.svg.* Menelaus's theorem, case 1: line $DEF$ passes inside triangle $\triangle ABC$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Menelaus's theorem"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), **Menelaus's theorem**, named for [Menelaus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/46301), is a proposition about [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) in [plane geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_geometry). Suppose we have a triangle $\triangle ABC$, and a [transversal](wikipedia://en/13295107) line that crosses $BC, AC, AB$ at points $D, E, F$ respectively, with $D, E, F$ distinct from $A, B, C$. A weak version of the theorem states that

:::

$$\left|\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}\right| \times \left|\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}\right| \times \left|\frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}\right| = 1,$$

where "| |" denotes [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) (i.e., all segment lengths are positive).

The theorem can be strengthened to a statement about [signed lengths of segments](wikipedia://en/22634860#Directed_line_segment), which provides some additional information about the relative order of collinear points. Here, the length $\overline{AB}$ is taken to be positive or negative according to whether $A$ is to the left or right of $B$ in some fixed orientation of the line; for example, $\tfrac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}$ is defined as having positive value when $F$ is between $A$ and $B$ and negative otherwise. The signed version of Menelaus's theorem states

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}} \times \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}} \times \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}} = - 1.$$

Equivalently,[^o2]

$$\overline{AF} \times \overline{BD} \times \overline{CE} = - \overline{FB} \times \overline{DC} \times \overline{EA}.$$

Some authors organize the factors differently and obtain the seemingly different relation[^o3]

$$\frac{\overline{FA}}{\overline{FB}} \times \frac{\overline{DB}}{\overline{DC}} \times \frac{\overline{EC}}{\overline{EA}} = 1,$$

but as each of these factors is the negative of the corresponding factor above, the relation is seen to be the same.

The [converse](wikipedia://en/30977#Converse) is also true: If points $D, E, F$ are chosen on $BC, AC, AB$ respectively so that

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}} \times \frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}} \times \frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}} = -1,$$

then $D, E, F$ are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear). The converse is often included as part of the theorem. (Note that the converse of the weaker, unsigned statement is not necessarily true.)

The theorem is very similar to [Ceva's theorem](wikipedia://en/152428) in that their equations differ only in sign. By re-writing each in terms of [cross-ratios](wikipedia://en/401767), the two theorems may be seen as [projective duals](wikipedia://en/539925).[^o4]

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proofs"}

## Proofs {#Proofs}

*Image: Menelaos' theorem 2.svg.* Menelaus's theorem, case 2: line $DEF$ is entirely outside triangle $\triangle ABC$

### A standard proof {#A_standard_proof}

A proof given by John Wellesley Russell uses [Pasch's axiom](wikipedia://en/5751973) to consider cases where a line does or does not meet a triangle.[^o8] First, the sign of the [left-hand side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-hand_side) will be negative since either all three of the ratios are negative, the case where the line $DEF$ misses the triangle (see diagram), or one is negative and the other two are positive, the case where $DEF$ crosses two sides of the triangle.

To check the magnitude, construct perpendiculars from $A, B, C$ to the line $DEF$ and let their lengths be $a, b, c$ respectively. Then by [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) triangles it follows that

$$\left|\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}\right| = \left|\frac{a}{b}\right|, \quad \left|\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}\right| = \left|\frac{b}{c}\right|, \quad \left|\frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}\right| = \left|\frac{c}{a}\right|.$$

Therefore,

$$\left|\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}\right| \times \left|\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}\right| \times \left|\frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}\right| = \left| \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{b}{c} \times \frac{c}{a} \right| = 1.$$

For a simpler, if less symmetrical way to check the magnitude,[^o9] draw $\overline{CK}$ parallel to $\overline{AB}$ where $DEF$ meets $\overline{CK}$ at $K$. Then by similar triangles

$$\left|\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}\right| = \left|\frac{\overline{BF}}{\overline{CK}}\right|, \quad \left|\frac{\overline{AE}}{\overline{EC}}\right| = \left|\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{CK}}\right|,$$

and the result follows by eliminating $\overline{CK}$ from these equations.

The converse follows as a corollary.[^o10] Let $D, E, F$ be given on the lines $BC, AC, AB$ so that the equation holds. Let $F'$ be the point where $DE$ crosses $AB$. Then by the theorem, the equation also holds for $D, E, F'$. Comparing the two,

$$\frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}} = \frac{\overline{AF'}}{\overline{F'B}}\ .$$

But at most one point can cut a segment in a given ratio so $F = F'.$

### A proof using homotheties {#A_proof_using_homotheties}

*Image: Menelaus-thm.png.* Homothetie centers D, E F are colinear iff the composition is identity.

The following proof[^o12] uses only notions of [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890), notably [homotheties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation). Whether or not $D, E, F$ are collinear, there are three homotheties with centers $D, E, F$ that respectively send $B$ to $C$, $C$ to $A$, and $A$ to $B$. The composition of the three then is an element of the group of homothety-translations that fixes $B$, so it is a homothety with center $B$, possibly with ratio 1 (in which case it is the identity). This composition fixes the line $DE$ if and only if $F$ is collinear with $D, E$ (since the first two homotheties certainly fix $DE$, and the third does so only if $F$ lies on $DE$). Therefore $D, E, F$ are collinear if and only if this composition is the identity, which means that the magnitude of the product of the three ratios is 1:

$$\frac{\overrightarrow{DC}}{\overrightarrow{DB}} \times \frac{\overrightarrow{EA}}{\overrightarrow{EC}} \times \frac{\overrightarrow{FB}}{\overrightarrow{FA}} = 1,$$

which is equivalent to the given equation.

## History {#History}

It is uncertain who actually discovered the theorem; however, the oldest extant exposition appears in *Spherics* by Menelaus. In this book, the plane version of the theorem is used as a lemma to prove a spherical version of the theorem.[^o14]

In *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*, [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) applies the theorem on a number of problems in spherical astronomy.[^o15] During the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age), Muslim scholars devoted a number of works that engaged in the study of Menelaus's theorem, which they referred to as "the proposition on the secants" (*shakl al-qatta**). The [complete quadrilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_quadrilateral) was called the "figure of secants" in their terminology.[^o15] [Al-Biruni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Biruni)'s work,*****The Keys of Astronomy*, lists a number of those works, which can be classified into studies as part of commentaries on Ptolemy's*Almagest*as in the works of [al-Nayrizi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nayrizi) and [al-Khazin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khazin) where each demonstrated particular cases of Menelaus's theorem that led to the [sine rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_rule),[^o16] or works composed as independent treatises such as:***

- The "Treatise on the Figure of Secants" (*Risala fi shakl al-qatta**) by [Thabit ibn Qurra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thabit_ibn_Qurra).[^o15]***
- [Husam al-Din al-Salar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husam_al-Din_al-Salar)'s *Removing the Veil from the Mysteries of the Figure of Secants* (Kashf al-qina' 'an asrar al-shakl al-qatta'), also known as "The Book on the Figure of Secants" (*Kitab al-shakl al-qatta**) or in Europe as*****The Treatise on the Complete Quadrilateral*. The lost treatise was referred to by [Sharaf al-Din al-Tusi](wikipedia://en/1768580) and [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](wikipedia://en/33642424).[^o15]***
- Work by [al-Sijzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Sijzi).[^o16]
- *Tahdhib* by [Abu Nasr ibn Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Nasr_ibn_Iraq).[^o16]
- [Roshdi Rashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roshdi_Rashed) and [Athanase Papadopoulos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athanase_Papadopoulos), Menelaus' Spherics: Early Translation and al-Mahani'/al-Harawi's version (Critical edition of Menelaus' Spherics from the Arabic manuscripts, with historical and mathematical commentaries), De Gruyter, Series: Scientia Graeco-Arabica, 21, 2017, 890 pages.

In the West the theorem was rediscovered by the Italian mathematician [Giovanni Ceva](wikipedia://en/152426).
