# Change of basis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Change of basis"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an [ordered basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_basis) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of finite [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) $n$ allows representing uniquely any element of the vector space by a [coordinate vector](wikipedia://en/879358), which is a [finite sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_sequence) of $n$ [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) called [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates). If two different bases are considered, the coordinate vector that represents a vector $v$ on one basis is, in general, different from the coordinate vector that represents $v$ on the other basis. A **change of basis** consists of converting every assertion expressed in terms of coordinates relative to one basis into an assertion expressed in terms of coordinates relative to the other basis.[^o2][^o3][^o4]

:::

Such a conversion results from the *change-of-basis formula*, which expresses the coordinates relative to one basis in terms of the coordinates relative to the other basis. Using [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), this formula can be written

$$\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{old} = A ~ \mathbf{x}_\mathrm{new},$$

where $\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{old}$ and $\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{new}$ are the [column vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector) of the coordinates of the same vector on the "old" (initially defined) and "new" (other) bases. $A$ is the **change-of-basis matrix** (also called **transition matrix**), which is the matrix whose columns are the coordinates of the "new" [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) on the "old" basis.

*Image: Change-of-basis\_coordinate\_mapping\_(matrix\_representation).png.* The matrix $\mathrm{ A = \mathbf{[} \overrightarrow{a} \! _1 \mathbf{|} \overrightarrow{a} \! _2 \mathbf{]} }$ maps coordinate vectors in the basis $\mathrm{ ( \overrightarrow{a} \! _1, \overrightarrow{a} \! _2 ) }$ to coordinate vectors in the standard basis: $\mathrm{ \overrightarrow{x} = A \overrightarrow{x} \! _A }.$

*Image: Change-of-basis\_coordinate\_transformation\_between\_bases.png.* Geometric illustration of change of basis: the same vector expressed in two non-canonical bases and the standard basis, showing the relationship between coordinate representations.

A change of basis is sometimes called a *change of coordinates*, although it excludes many [coordinate transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_transformation). For applications in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and specially in [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), a change of basis often involves the transformation of an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063), understood as a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) in [physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_space), thus excluding [translations](wikipedia://en/204682).

This article deals mainly with finite-dimensional vector spaces. However, many of the presented principles are also valid for infinite-dimensional vector spaces.

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Change-of-basis formula"}

## Change-of-basis formula {#Change-of-basis_formula}

Let $B_\mathrm{old} = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)$ be a basis of a [finite-dimensional vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-dimensional_vector_space) $V$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$.

For $j = 1, ..., n$, one can define a vector $w_{j}$ by its coordinates $a_{i,j}$ over $B_\mathrm{old} \colon$

$$w_j = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{i,j} v_i.$$

Let

$$A = \left( a_{i,j} \right)_{i,j}$$

be the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) whose $j$-th column is formed by the coordinates of $w_{j}$. (Here and in what follows, the index $i$ refers always to the rows of $A$ and the $v_i,$ while the index $j$ refers always to the columns of $A$ and the $w_j;$ such a convention is useful for avoiding errors in explicit computations.)

Setting $B_\mathrm{new} = (w_1, \ldots, w_n),$ one has that $B_\mathrm{new}$ is a basis of $V$ if and only if the matrix $A$ is [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122), or equivalently, if it has a nonzero [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468). In this case, $A$ is said to be the *change-of-basis matrix* from the basis $B_\mathrm{old}$ to the basis $B_\mathrm{new}.$

Given a vector $u \in V,$ let $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ be the coordinates of $u$ over $B_\mathrm{old},$ and $(y_1, \ldots, y_n)$ its coordinates over $B_\mathrm{new};$ that is

$$u = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i v_i = \sum_{j=1}^n y_j w_j.$$

(One could take the same summation index for the two sums, but choosing systematically the indexes $i$ for the old basis and $j$ for the new one makes clearer the formulas that follows, and helps avoiding errors in proofs and explicit computations.)

The *change-of-basis formula* expresses the coordinates over the old basis in terms of the coordinates over the new basis. With above notation, it is

$$x_i = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{i,j} y_j \qquad \text{for } i=1, \ldots, n.$$

In terms of matrices, the change-of-basis formula is

$$\mathbf{x} = A ~ \mathbf{y},$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are the column vectors of the coordinates of $u$ over $B_\mathrm{old}$ and $B_\mathrm{new},$ respectively. (This reverse terminology is confusing, but internationally adopted.)

*Proof:* Using the above definition of the change-of-basis matrix, one has

$$\begin{aligned} u &= \sum_{j=1}^n y_j w_j\\ &= \sum_{j=1}^n \left( y_j \sum_{i=1}^n a_{i,j} v_i \right)\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n \left( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{i,j} y_j \right) v_i. \end{aligned}$$

As $u = \textstyle \sum_{i=1}^n x_i v_i,$ the change-of-basis formula results from the uniqueness of the decomposition of a vector over a basis.

:::

## Example {#Example}

Consider the [Euclidean vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector_space) $\mathbb{R}^2$ and its [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005), consisting of the vectors $v_1$ and $v_2$ with column vectors

$\mathbf{v_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad$ and $\quad \mathbf{v_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.$

[Rotating](wikipedia://en/410009) them by an angle of $t$ gives a "new" basis, formed by the vectors $w_1$ and $w_2$ with column vectors

$\mathbf{w_1} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos t\\ \sin t \end{bmatrix} \quad$ and $\quad \mathbf{w_2} = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin t\\ \cos t \end{bmatrix}.$

So, the change-of-basis matrix is

$$\begin{bmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t \end{bmatrix}.$$

The change-of-basis formula asserts that, for a vector with "old" coordinates $(x_1, x_2)$ and "new" coordinates $(y_1, y_2),$ one has

$$\begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t \end{bmatrix} ~ \begin{bmatrix} y_1\\ y_2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

That is,

$$\begin{cases} x_1 = y_1 \cos t - y_2 \sin t\\ x_2 = y_1 \sin t + y_2 \cos t. \end{cases}$$

This may be shown by writing

$$\begin{aligned} y_1 w_1 + y_2 w_2 &= y_1 \Big( (\cos t) ~ v_1 + (\sin t) ~ v_2 \Big) + y_2 \Big( \! -(\sin t) ~ v_1 + (\cos t) ~ v_2 \Big)\\ &= \big( y_1 \cos t - y_2 \sin t \big) v_1 + \big( y_1 \sin t + y_2 \cos t \big) v_2\\ &= x_1 v_1 + x_2 v_2. \end{aligned}$$

:::

## In terms of linear maps {#In_terms_of_linear_maps}

Usually, a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) represents a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102), and the product of a matrix and a column vector represents the [function application](wikipedia://en/20576545) of the corresponding linear map to the vector whose coordinates form the column vector. The change-of-basis formula is a specific case of this general principle, although this is not immediately clear from its definition and proof.

When one says that a matrix *represents* a linear map, one refers implicitly to [bases](wikipedia://en/18420) of implied vector spaces, and to the fact that the choice of a basis induces a [linear isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isomorphism) between a vector space and $F^n$, where $F$ is the ground field of scalars. When only one basis is considered for each vector space, it is convenient to leave this isomorphism implicit, and to work [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an isomorphism. As several bases of the same vector space are considered here, a more accurate wording is required.

Let $F$ be a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), the set $F^n$ of the [$n$-tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) is an $F$-vector space whose addition and scalar multiplication are defined component-wise. Its [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) is the basis that has as its $i$-th element the tuple with all components equal to $0$ except the $i$-th one, equal to $1$.

A basis $B = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)$ of an $F$-vector space $V$ defines a linear isomorphism $\varphi \colon F^n \to V$ by

$$\varphi(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i v_i.$$

Conversely, such a linear isomorphism defines a basis, which is the image by $\varphi$ of the standard basis of $F^n.$

Let $B_\mathrm{old} = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)$ be the "old" basis of a change of basis, and $\varphi_\mathrm{old}$ the associated isomorphism. Given a change-of-basis ([invertible](wikipedia://en/217122)) matrix $A$, one could consider it the matrix of an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) (a [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective_linear_map) [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569)) $\psi_A$ of $F^n.$ Finally, define

$$\varphi_\mathrm{new} = \varphi_\mathrm{old} \circ \psi_A$$

(where $\circ$ denotes [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947)), and

$$B_\mathrm{new} = \varphi_\mathrm{new} \left( \varphi_\mathrm{old}^{-1}(B_\mathrm{old}) \right).$$

A straightforward verification shows that this definition of $B_\mathrm{new}$ is the same as that in the preceding section.

Now, by composing the equation $\varphi_\mathrm{new} = \varphi_\mathrm{old} \circ \psi_A$ with $\varphi_\mathrm{old}^{-1}$ on the left and $\varphi_\mathrm{new}^{-1}$ on the right, one gets

$$\varphi_\mathrm{old}^{-1} = \psi_A \circ \varphi_\mathrm{new}^{-1}.$$

It follows that, for $v \in V,$ one has

$$\varphi_\mathrm{old}^{-1}(v) = \psi_A \left( \varphi_\mathrm{new}^{-1}(v) \right),$$

which is the change-of-basis formula expressed in terms of linear maps instead of coordinates.

## Function defined on a vector space {#Function_defined_on_a_vector_space}

A [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that has a vector space as its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is commonly specified as a [multivariate function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_function) whose variables are the coordinates on some basis of the vector on which the function is [applied](wikipedia://en/20576545).

When the basis is changed, the [expression](wikipedia://en/609125) of the function is changed. This change can be computed by substituting the "old" coordinates for their expressions in terms of the "new" coordinates. More precisely, if $f(\mathbf{x})$ is the expression of the function in terms of the "old" coordinates, and if $\mathbf{x} = A\mathbf{y}$ is the change-of-basis formula, then $f(A\mathbf{y})$ is the expression of the same function in terms of the "new" coordinates.

The fact that the change-of-basis formula expresses the "old" coordinates in terms of the "new" ones may seem unnatural, but appears as useful, because no [matrix inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inversion) is needed here.

As the change-of-basis formula involves only [linear functions](wikipedia://en/152111), many function properties are kept by a change of basis. This allows defining these properties as properties of functions of a variable vector that are not related to any specific basis. So, a function whose domain is a vector space or a subset of it is

- a linear function,
- a [polynomial function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function),
- a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122),
- a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206),
- a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function),
- an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478),

if the multivariate function that represents it on some basis$\text{—}$and thus on every basis$\text{—}$has the same property.

This is specially useful in the theory of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), as this allows extending the concepts of continuous, differentiable, smooth, and analytic functions to functions that are defined on a manifold.

## Linear maps {#Linear_maps}

Consider a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $L: V \to W$ from a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ of dimension $n$ to a vector space $W$ of dimension $m$. It is represented on "old" bases of $V$ and $W$ by an $m\times n$ matrix $M$. A change of basis is defined by an $n\times n$ change-of-basis matrix $P$ for $V$, and an $m\times m$ change-of-basis matrix $Q$ for $W$.

On the "new" bases, the matrix representation of $L$ is

$$M' = Q^{-1}MP.$$

This is a straightforward consequence of the change-of-basis formula.

### Endomorphisms {#Endomorphisms}

[Endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) are linear maps from a vector space $V$ to itself. For a change of basis, the formula of the preceding section applies, with the same change-of-basis matrix on both sides of the formula. That is, if $M$ is the [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) of an endomorphism of $V$ on an "old" basis, and $P$ is a change-of-basis matrix, then the matrix of the endomorphism on the "new" basis is

$$M' = P^{-1}MP.$$

As every [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122) can be used as a change-of-basis matrix, this implies that two matrices are [similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_matrices) if and only if they represent the same endomorphism on two different bases.

## Bilinear forms {#Bilinear_forms}

A *[bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477)* on a vector space $V$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$ is a function $\varPhi \colon V \! \times V \to F$ which is [linear](wikipedia://en/18102) in both arguments. That is, $\varPhi$ is bilinear if the maps $v \mapsto \varPhi(v, w)$ and $v \mapsto \varPhi(w, v)$ are linear for every fixed $w \in V.$

The matrix $\mathbf{\Phi}$ of a bilinear form $\varPhi$ on a basis $(v_1, \ldots, v_n)$ (the "old" basis) is the matrix whose entry of the $i$-th row and $j$-th column is $\varPhi(v_i, v_j)$. It follows that for two vectors $v$ and $w$ with coordinate column vectors $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$, one has

$$\varPhi(v, w) = \mathbf{v}^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{\Phi} \mathbf{w},$$

where $\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf T}$ denotes the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of the column vector $\mathbf{v}$.

For a change of basis with matrix $P$, a straightforward computation shows that the matrix of the bilinear form on the "new" basis is

$$\mathbf{\Phi'} = P^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{\Phi} P.$$

A [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) is a bilinear form $S$ such that $S(v,w) = S(w,v)$ for every $v$ and $w$ in $V$. It follows that the matrix of $S$ on any basis is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474). This implies that the property of being a symmetric matrix must be kept by the above change-of-basis formula. One can also check this by noting that the transpose of a matrix product is the product of the transposes computed in the reverse order. Thus,

$$(P^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{S} P)^{\mathsf T} = P^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{S}^{\mathsf T} P;$$

finally, since the matrix $\mathbf{S}$ is symmetric,

$$(P^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{S} P)^{\mathsf T} = P^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{S} P.$$

If the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of the ground field $F$ is not two, then for every symmetric bilinear form, there is a basis for which the matrix is [diagonal](wikipedia://en/174080). Moreover, the resulting nonzero entries on the diagonal are defined up to the multiplication by a square. So, if the ground field is the field $\mathbb{R}$ of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), these nonzero entries can be chosen to be either $1$ or $-1$. [Sylvester's law of inertia](wikipedia://en/1127460) is a theorem that asserts that the numbers of $1$ and of $-1$ depend only on the bilinear form, and not on the change of basis.

Symmetric bilinear forms over the reals are often encountered in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), typically in the study of [quadrics](wikipedia://en/145570) and of the [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia) of a [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769). In these cases, [orthonormal bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal_bases) are specially useful; this means that one generally prefers to restrict changes of basis to those that have an [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/105620) change-of-basis matrix, that is, a matrix such that $P^{\mathsf T} = P^{-1}.$ Such matrices have the fundamental property that the change-of-basis formula is the same for a symmetric bilinear form and the endomorphism that is represented by the same symmetric matrix. The [Spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) asserts that, given such a symmetric matrix, there is an orthogonal change of basis such that the resulting matrix (of both the bilinear form and the endomorphism) is a diagonal matrix with the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of the initial matrix on the diagonal. It follows that, over the reals, if the matrix of an endomorphism is symmetric, then it is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325).
