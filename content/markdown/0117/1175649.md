# Complement graph

*Image: Petersen graph complement.svg.* The [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) (on the left) and its complement graph (on the right)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complement graph"}

In the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **complement** or **inverse** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ is a graph $H$ on the same [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) such that two distinct vertices are adjacent (connected) in $H$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are not adjacent in $G$. That is, to generate the complement of a graph, one fills in all the missing [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) required to form a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816), and removes all the edges that were previously there.[^o2]

:::

The complement of the graph is not the [set complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of the graph: only the edges are complemented.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $G = (V, E)$ be a [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) undirected graph and let $P$ consist of all pairs of distinct vertices in $V$. Then the simple [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) $H = (V, P \backslash E)$ is the complement of $G$,[^o5] where $P \backslash E$ is the [relative complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_complement) of $E$ in $P$.

Let $G = (V, A)$ be a [simple directed graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_directed_graph) and let $O$ consist of all [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) of distinct vertices in $V$. Then the simple directed graph $H = (V, O \backslash A)$ is the complement of $G$.

Let $G$ be a simple undirected / directed graph, let $K$ be the [complete](wikipedia://en/85816) simple undirected / directed graph on the same number of vertices (i.e., all entries are unity except the diagonal entries which are zero), let $\mathbb{A}(G)$ and $\mathbb{A}(K)$ respectively be the adjacency matrices of $G$ and $K$. Then the [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) of the complement $H$ of $G$ is: $\mathbb{A}(H) = \mathbb{A}(K) - \mathbb{A}(G)$.

The complement is not defined for [multigraphs](wikipedia://en/1774970).

For graphs that allow [self-loops](wikipedia://en/1449083) (but not multiple adjacencies), the complement of a graph $G$ may be defined by adding a self-loop to every vertex that does not have one in $G$, removing its self-loop from every vertex that has one in $G$, and otherwise using the same formula as above. However, this operation is different from the one for simple graphs, since applying it to a graph with no self-loop results in a graph with self-loops on all vertices.

:::

## Applications and examples {#Applications_and_examples}

Several graph-theoretic concepts are related to each other via complementation:

- The complement of an [edgeless graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgeless_graph) is a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816), and vice versa.
- Any [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) of the complement graph of a graph $G$ is the complement of the corresponding induced subgraph in $G$.
- An [independent set](wikipedia://en/524501) in a graph is a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) in the complement graph, and vice versa. This is a special case of the previous two properties, as an independent set is an edgeless induced subgraph and a clique is a complete induced subgraph.
- The [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186) group of a graph is the automorphism group of its complement.
- The complement of every [triangle-free graph](wikipedia://en/7667996) is a [claw-free graph](wikipedia://en/12415907),[^o7] but the reverse is not true.

## Self-complementary graphs and graph classes {#Self-complementary_graphs_and_graph_classes}

*Image: Self-complementary NZ graph.svg.* The four-vertex path is self-complementary.

A [self-complementary graph](wikipedia://en/15093884) is a graph that is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to its own complement.[^o2] Examples include the four-vertex [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190) and five-vertex [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962). There is no known characterization of self-complementary graphs.

Several classes of graphs are self-complementary, in the sense that the complement of any graph in one of these classes is another graph in the same class.

- [Perfect graphs](wikipedia://en/670531) are the graphs in which, for every induced subgraph, the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) equals the size of the maximum clique. The fact that the complement of a perfect graph is also perfect is the [perfect graph theorem](wikipedia://en/670602) of [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938).[^o9]
- [Cographs](wikipedia://en/1175666) are defined as the graphs that can be built up from single vertices by [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) and complementation operations. They form a self-complementary family of graphs: the complement of any cograph is another, different, cograph. For cographs of more than one vertex, exactly one graph in each complementary pair is connected, and one equivalent definition of cographs is that each of their connected [induced subgraphs](wikipedia://en/1655188) has a disconnected complement. Another, self-complementary, definition is that cographs are the graphs with no four-vertex path as an induced subgraph.[^o10]
- Another self-complementary class of graphs is the class of [split graphs](wikipedia://en/9928681), the graphs in which the vertices can be partitioned into a clique and an independent set. The same partition gives an independent set and a clique in the complement graph.[^o11]
- The [threshold graphs](wikipedia://en/11503485) are the graphs formed by repeatedly adding either an independent vertex (one with no neighbors) or a [universal vertex](wikipedia://en/51352780) (adjacent to all previously added vertices). These two operations are complementary and they generate a self-complementary class of graphs.[^o12]

## Algorithmic aspects {#Algorithmic_aspects}

In the [analysis of algorithms](wikipedia://en/2230) on graphs, the distinction between a graph and its complement is an important one, because a [sparse graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparse_graph) (one with a small number of edges compared to the number of pairs of vertices) will in general not have a sparse complement, and so an algorithm that takes time proportional to the number of edges on a given graph may take a much larger amount of time if the same algorithm is run on an explicit representation of the complement graph. Therefore, researchers have studied algorithms that perform standard graph computations on the complement of an input graph, using an [implicit graph](wikipedia://en/24109545) representation that does not require the explicit construction of the complement graph. In particular, it is possible to simulate either [depth-first search](wikipedia://en/97034) or [breadth-first search](wikipedia://en/97026) on the complement graph, in an amount of time that is linear in the size of the given graph, even when the complement graph may have a much larger size.[^o14] It is also possible to use these simulations to compute other properties concerning the connectivity of the complement graph.[^o14][^o15]
