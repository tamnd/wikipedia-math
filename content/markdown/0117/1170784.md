# Stars (M. C. Escher)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stars"}

***Stars*** is a [wood engraving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_engraving) [print](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_master_print) created by the Dutch artist [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) in 1948, depicting two [chameleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chameleon) in a [polyhedral](wikipedia://en/23470) cage floating through space.

:::

The [compound of three octahedra](wikipedia://en/33808818) used for the central cage in *Stars* had been studied before in mathematics, and Escher likely learned of it from the book *Vielecke und Vielflache* by [Max Brückner](wikipedia://en/48703370). Escher used similar compound polyhedral forms in several other works, including *Crystal* (1947), *Study for Stars* (1948), *Double Planetoid* (1949), and *Waterfall* (1961).

The design for *Stars* was likely influenced by Escher's own interest in both geometry and astronomy, by a long history of using geometric forms to model the heavens, and by a drawing style used by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci). Commentators have interpreted the cage's compound shape as a reference to double and triple stars in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), or to twinned crystals in [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography). The image contrasts the celestial order of its polyhedral shapes with the more chaotic forms of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology).

Prints of *Stars* belong to the permanent collections of major museums including the [Rijksmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijksmuseum), the [National Gallery of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Art), and the [National Gallery of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Canada).

## Description {#Description}

*Stars* is a [wood engraving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood_engraving) print; that is, it was produced by carving the artwork into the end grain of a block of wood (unlike a [woodcut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut) which uses the side grain), and then using this block to print the image. It was created by Escher in October 1948.[^o4][^o5] Although most published copies of *Stars* are [monochromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochromatic), with white artwork against a black background, the copy in the [National Gallery of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Canada) is tinted in different shades of turquoise, yellow, green, and pale pink.[^o27]

The print depicts a hollowed-out [compound of three octahedra](wikipedia://en/33808818), a [polyhedral compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedral_compound) composed of three interlocking [regular octahedra](wikipedia://en/80177635), floating in space. Numerous other [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) and polyhedral compounds float in the background; the four largest are, on the upper left, the [compound of cube and octahedron](wikipedia://en/8010874); on the upper right, the [stella octangula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stella_octangula); on the lower left, a [compound of two cubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_of_two_cubes); and on the lower right, a solid version of the same octahedron 3-compound. The smaller polyhedra visible within the print also include all of the five [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) and the [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631).[^o18][^o9] In order to depict polyhedra accurately, Escher made models of them from cardboard.[^o5]

Two [chameleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chameleon) are contained within the cage-like shape of the central compound; Escher writes that they were chosen as its inhabitants "because they are able to cling by their legs and tails to the beams of their cage as it swirls through space".[^o6] The chameleon on the left sticks out his tongue, perhaps in commentary; [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) observes that the tongue has an unusual [spiral](wikipedia://en/61563)-shaped tip.[^o9]

## Influences {#Influences}

*Image: Leonardo polyhedra.png.* A [rhombicuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/26213) drawn by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), 1509, four centuries before Escher

Escher's interest in geometry is well known, but he was also an avid amateur astronomer, and in the early 1940s he became a member of the Dutch Association for Meteorology and Astronomy. He owned a 6 cm [refracting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope), and recorded several observations of [binary stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star).[^o5]

The use of polyhedra to model heavenly bodies can be traced back to [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954), who in the *[Timaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_%28dialogue%29)* identified the [regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) with the shape of the heavens and its 12 faces with the [constellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation) of the [zodiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiac).[^o8] Later, [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) theorized that the distribution of distances of the planets from the sun could be explained by the shapes of the five [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905), nested within each other. Escher kept a model of this system of nested polyhedra, and regularly depicted polyhedra in his artworks relating to astronomy and other worlds.[^o5]

Escher learned his wood engraving technique from [Samuel Jessurun de Mesquita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Jessurun_de_Mesquita).[^o6] He illustrated the octahedral compound of *Stars* in the beveled [wire-frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire-frame_model) style that had been used by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) in his illustrations for [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473)'s 1509 book, *[De divina proportione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_divina_proportione)*.[^o18][^o9][^o10]

The [stella octangula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stella_octangula) (Latin for "eight-pointed star") in the upper right of *Stars* was first described by Pacioli, and later rediscovered by Kepler, who gave it its astronomical name.[^o11] [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) reports that the shape of the central chameleon cage in *Stars* had previously been described in 1900 by [Max Brückner](wikipedia://en/48703370), whose book *Vielecke und Vielflache* includes a photograph of a model of the same shape. Coxeter, believing that Escher was not aware of this reference, wrote "It is remarkable that Escher, without any knowledge of algebra or analytic geometry, was able to rediscover this highly symmetrical figure."[^o9] However, [George W. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Hart) has documented that Escher was familiar with Brückner's book and based much of his knowledge of stellated polyhedra and polyhedral compounds on it.[^o12]

## Analysis {#Analysis}

Martin Beech interprets the many [polyhedral compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedral_compound) within *Stars* as corresponding to [double stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_star) and [triple star systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_star) in astronomy.[^o5] Beech writes that, for Escher, the mathematical orderliness of polyhedra depicts the "stability and timeless quality" of the heavens, and similarly Marianne L. Teuber writes that *Stars* "celebrates Escher's identification with Johannes Kepler's neo-Platonic belief in an underlying mathematical order in the universe".[^o14]

Alternatively, Howard W. Jaffe interprets the polyhedral forms in *Stars* [crystallographically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography), as "brilliantly faceted jewels" floating through space, with its compound polyhedra representing [crystal twinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_twinning).[^o15] However, R. A. Dunlap points out the contrast between the order of the polyhedral forms and the more chaotic biological nature of the chameleons inhabiting them.[^o19] In the same vein, Beech observes that the stars themselves convey tension between order and chaos: despite their symmetric shapes, the stars are scattered apparently at random, and vary haphazardly from each other.[^o5] As Escher himself wrote about the central chameleon cage, "I shouldn't be surprised if it wobbles a bit."[^o5]

## Related works {#Related_works}

[Tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606) 3. [Rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631) 5. [Regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968) 6. [Octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) 7. [Cube](wikipedia://en/6285) 8. [Stella octangula](wikipedia://en/2601866) 9. [Regular dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) 10. Two compound [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285)

A closely related woodcut, *Study for Stars*, completed in August 1948,[^o5][^o17] depicts wireframe versions of several of the same polyhedra and polyhedral compounds, floating in black within a square composition, but without the chameleons. The largest polyhedron shown in *Study for Stars*, a [stellated rhombic dodecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellated_rhombic_dodecahedron), is also one of two polyhedra depicted prominently in Escher's 1961 print *[Waterfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall_%28M._C._Escher%29)*.[^o18]

The [stella octangula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stella_octangula), a compound of two tetrahedra that appears in the upper right of *Stars*, also forms the central shape of another of Escher's astronomical works, *Double Planetoid* (1949).[^o9] The [compound of cube and octahedron](wikipedia://en/8010874) in the upper left was used earlier by Escher, in *Crystal* (1947).[^o11]

Escher's later work *Four Regular Solids (Stereometric Figure)* returned to the theme of polyhedral compounds, depicting a more explicitly Keplerian form in which the compound of the cube and octahedron is nested within the compound of the dodecahedron and icosahedron.[^o19]

## Collections and publications {#Collections_and_publications}

*Stars* was used as [cover art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cover_art) for the 1962 [anthology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthology) *Best Fantasy Stories* edited by [Brian Aldiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Aldiss),[^o21] and for a 1971 Italian edition of occult guidebook *[The Morning of the Magicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Morning_of_the_Magicians)*.[^o22] It also formed the frontispiece for a 1996 textbook on [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography).[^o15]

As well as being exhibited in the [Escher Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escher_Museum), copies of *Stars* are in the permanent collections of the [Rijksmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijksmuseum),[^o23] [National Gallery of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Art),[^o24] [Mildred Lane Kemper Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mildred_Lane_Kemper_Art_Museum),[^o25] [Boston Public Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Public_Library),[^o26] and the [National Gallery of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Canada).[^o27]
