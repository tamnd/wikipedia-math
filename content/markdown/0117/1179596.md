# Nelson Goodman

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Nelson Goodman"}

**Henry Nelson Goodman** (7 August 1906 – 25 November 1998) was an American [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher), known for his work on [counterfactuals](wikipedia://en/491578), [mereology](wikipedia://en/375380), the [problem of induction](wikipedia://en/177456), [irrealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrealism_%28philosophy%29), and [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics).

:::

## Life and career {#Life_and_career}

Goodman was born in [Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville,_Massachusetts), [Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts), the son of Sarah Elizabeth (née Woodbury) and Henry Lewis Goodman.[^o5] He was of [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish) origins.[^o6] He graduated from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University), [AB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts), *[magna cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_cum_laude)* (1928). During the 1930s, he ran an [art gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_gallery) in [Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston), Massachusetts, while studying for a Harvard [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), which he completed in 1941.[^o7] His experience as an [art dealer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_dealer) helps explain his later turn towards [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics), where he became better known than in [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211). During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), he served as a [psychologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychologist) in the [US Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army).[^o8]

He taught at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania), 1946–1964, where his students included [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky), [Sidney Morgenbesser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Morgenbesser), [Stephen Stich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Stich), and [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233). He was a [research fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_fellow) at the Harvard Center for Cognitive Studies from 1962 to 1963 and was a professor at [Brandeis University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandeis_University) from 1964 to 1967, before being appointed Professor of Philosophy at Harvard in 1968.[^o9]

In 1967, at the Harvard Graduate School of Education, he was the founding director of Harvard Project Zero, a basic research project in artistic cognition and artistic education. He remained the director for four years and served as an informal adviser for many years thereafter.[^o10]

Goodman died in [Needham, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Needham,_Massachusetts).[^o11]

## Philosophical work {#Philosophical_work}

### Induction and "grue" {#Induction_and_"grue"}

In his book *[Fact, Fiction, and Forecast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact,_Fiction,_and_Forecast)*, Goodman introduced the "[new riddle of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_riddle_of_induction)", so-called by analogy with [Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume)'s classical [problem of induction](wikipedia://en/177456). He accepted Hume's observation that [inductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/393736) (i.e. inferring from past experience about events in the future) was based solely on human habit and regularities to which our day-to-day existence has accustomed us. Goodman argued, however, that Hume overlooked the fact that some regularities establish habits (a given piece of copper conducting electricity increases the credibility of statements asserting that other pieces of copper conduct electricity) while some do not (the fact that a given man in a room is a third son does not increase the credibility of statements asserting that other men in this room are third sons).

[Hempel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Hempel)'s [confirmation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_theory) argued that the solution is to differentiate between hypotheses, which apply to all things of a certain class, and [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence) statements, which apply to only one thing. Goodman's famous counterargument was to introduce the predicate [grue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grue_and_bleen), which applies to all things examined before a certain time *t* just in case they are green, but also to other things just in case they are blue and not examined before time *t*. If we examine emeralds before time *t* and find that emerald *a* is green, emerald *b* is green, and so forth, each will confirm the hypothesis that all emeralds are green. However, emeralds *a, b, c,*..etc. also confirm the hypothesis that all emeralds are [grue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grue_and_bleen). Thus, before time *t*, the apparently law-like statements "All emeralds are green" and "All emeralds are grue" are equally well confirmed by observation, but obviously "All emeralds are grue" is not a law-like statement.

Goodman's example showed that the difficulty in determining what constitutes law-like statements is far greater than previously thought, and that once again we find ourselves facing the initial [dilemma](wikipedia://en/1618182) that "anything can confirm anything".

### Nominalism and mereology {#Nominalism_and_mereology}

Goodman, along with [Stanislaw Lesniewski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislaw_Lesniewski), is the founder of the contemporary variant of [nominalism](wikipedia://en/21176), which argues that [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) should dispense with [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553). Goodman's nominalism was driven purely by [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology) considerations. After a long and difficult 1947 paper coauthored with [W. V. O. Quine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._O._Quine), Goodman ceased to trouble himself with finding a way to reconstruct mathematics while dispensing with [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) – discredited as sole [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358) as of 1913 ([Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) and [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395), in *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*).

The program of [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) to reconstruct it from logical axioms was proven futile in 1931 by [Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736). Because of this and other failures of seemingly fruitful lines of research, Quine soon came to believe that such a reconstruction was impossible, but Goodman's Penn colleague [Richard Milton Martin](wikipedia://en/2860251) argued otherwise, writing a number of papers suggesting ways forward.

According to [Thomas Tymoczko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Tymoczko)'s *afterword* in *New directions in the philosophy of mathematics*, Quine had "urged that we abandon ad hoc devices distinguishing mathematics from science and just accept the resulting assimilation", putting the "key burden on the theories (networks of sentences) that we accept, not on the individual sentences whose significance can change dramatically depending on their theoretical context." In so doing, Tymoczko claimed, [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439) and [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science) were merged into [quasi-empiricism](wikipedia://en/159731): the emphasis of [mathematical practice](wikipedia://en/159730) as effectively part of the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method), an emphasis on method over result.

The Goodman–Leonard (1940) calculus of individuals is the starting point for the American variant of [mereology](wikipedia://en/375380). While the exposition in Goodman and Leonard invoked a bit of naive set theory, the variant of the calculus of individuals that grounds Goodman's 1951 *The Structure of Appearance*, a revision and extension of his PhD thesis, makes no mention of the notion of set (while his PhD thesis still did).[^o15] Simons (1987) and Casati and [Varzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achille_Varzi_%28philosopher%29) (1999) show that the calculus of individuals can be grounded in either a bit of set theory, or monadic predicates, schematically employed. Mereology is accordingly "ontologically neutral" and retains some of Quine's pragmatism (which Tymoczko in 1998 carefully qualified as [American Pragmatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Pragmatism)).
