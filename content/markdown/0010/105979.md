# Exclusive or

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Exclusive or"}

*Image: Venn 0110 1001.svg.* [Venn diagram](wikipedia://en/61701) of $A \oplus B \oplus C$

**Exclusive or**, **exclusive disjunction**, **exclusive alternation**, **logical non-equivalence**, or [logical inequality](wikipedia://en/3092370#Inequality) is a [logical operator](wikipedia://en/18153) whose negation is the [logical biconditional](wikipedia://en/228783). With two inputs, XOR is true [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the inputs differ (one is true, one is false). With multiple inputs, XOR is true if and only if the number of true inputs is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135).[^o3]

:::

It gains the name "exclusive or" because the meaning of "or" is ambiguous when both [operands](wikipedia://en/22656) are true. XOR *excludes* that case. Some informal ways of describing XOR are "one or the other but not both", "either one or the other", and "A or B, but not A and B".

It is [symbolized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_logic_symbols) by the prefix operator $J$[^o4]p. 16 and by the [infix operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infix_operator) **XOR** ( or ), **EOR**, **EXOR**, $\dot{\vee}$, $\overline{\vee}$, $\underline{\vee}$, **⩛**, $\oplus$, $\nleftrightarrow$, $\not\equiv$, and ^.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Equivalences, elimination, and introduction {#Equivalences,_elimination,_and_introduction}

Exclusive disjunction essentially means 'either one, but not both nor none'. In other words, the statement is true [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) one is true and the other is false. For example, if two horses are racing, then one of the two will win the race, but not both of them. The exclusive disjunction $p\nleftrightarrow q$, also denoted by $p\operatorname{?}q$ or $Jpq$, can be expressed in terms of the [logical conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) ("logical and", $\land$), the [disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction) ("logical or", $\vee$), and the [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) ($\neg$) as follows:

$$\begin{matrix} p\nleftrightarrow q & = & (p\vee q)\land\neg(p\land q) \end{matrix}$$

The exclusive disjunction $p \nleftrightarrow q$ can also be expressed in the following way:

$$\begin{matrix} p \nleftrightarrow q & = & (p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land q) \end{matrix}$$

This representation of XOR may be found useful when constructing a circuit or network, because it has only one $\lnot$ operation and small number of $\land$ and $\lor$ operations. A proof of this identity is given below:

$$\begin{matrix} p \nleftrightarrow q & = & (p \land \lnot q) & \lor & (\lnot p \land q) \\[3pt] & = & ((p \land \lnot q) \lor \lnot p) & \land & ((p \land \lnot q) \lor q) \\[3pt] & = & ((p \lor \lnot p) \land (\lnot q \lor \lnot p)) & \land & ((p \lor q) \land (\lnot q \lor q)) \\[3pt] & = & (\lnot p \lor \lnot q) & \land & (p \lor q) \\[3pt] & = & \lnot (p \land q) & \land & (p \lor q) \end{matrix}$$

It is sometimes useful to write $p \nleftrightarrow q$ in the following way:

$$\begin{matrix} p \nleftrightarrow q & = & \lnot ((p \land q) \lor (\lnot p \land \lnot q)) \end{matrix}$$

or:

$$\begin{matrix} p \nleftrightarrow q & = & (p \lor q) \land (\lnot p \lor \lnot q) \end{matrix}$$

This equivalence can be established by applying [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669) twice to the fourth line of the above proof.

The exclusive or is also equivalent to the negation of a [logical biconditional](wikipedia://en/228783), by the rules of material implication (a [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808) is equivalent to the disjunction of the negation of its [antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755) and its consequence) and [material equivalence](wikipedia://en/14922).

In summary, we have, in mathematical and in engineering notation:

$$\begin{matrix} p \nleftrightarrow q & = & (p \land \lnot q) & \lor & (\lnot p \land q) & = & p\overline{q} + \overline{p}q \\[3pt] & = & (p \lor q) & \land & (\lnot p \lor \lnot q) & = & (p + q)(\overline{p} + \overline{q}) \\[3pt] & = & (p \lor q) & \land & \lnot (p \land q) & = & (p + q)(\overline{pq}) \end{matrix}$$

## Negation of the operator {#Negation_of_the_operator}

By applying the spirit of [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669), we get:

$$\neg(p \nleftrightarrow q)\equiv\neg p\nleftrightarrow q\equiv p\nleftrightarrow\neg q.$$

## Relation to modern algebra {#Relation_to_modern_algebra}

Although the [operators](wikipedia://en/4140245) $\wedge$ ([conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152)) and $\lor$ ([disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962)) are very useful in logic systems, they fail a more generalizable structure in the following way:

The systems $(\{T, F\}, \wedge)$ and $(\{T, F\}, \lor)$ are [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652), but neither is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). This unfortunately prevents the combination of these two systems into larger structures, such as a [mathematical ring](wikipedia://en/48404).

However, the system using exclusive or $(\{T, F\}, \oplus)$ *is* an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974). The combination of operators $\wedge$ and $\oplus$ over elements $\{T, F\}$ produce the well-known [two-element field $\mathbb{F}_2$](wikipedia://en/11137263). This field can represent any logic obtainable with the system $(\land, \lor)$ and has the added benefit of the arsenal of algebraic analysis tools for fields.

More specifically, if one associates $F$ with 0 and $T$ with 1, one can interpret the logical "AND" operation as multiplication on $\mathbb{F}_2$ and the "XOR" operation as addition on $\mathbb{F}_2$:

$$\begin{matrix} r = p \land q & \Leftrightarrow & r = p \cdot q \pmod 2 \\[3pt] r = p \oplus q & \Leftrightarrow & r = p + q \pmod 2 \\ \end{matrix}$$

The description of a [Boolean function](wikipedia://en/753349) as a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in $\mathbb{F}_2$, using this basis, is called the function's [algebraic normal form](wikipedia://en/1048680).[^o10]

## Exclusive or in natural language {#Exclusive_or_in_natural_language}

Disjunction is often understood exclusively in [natural languages](wikipedia://en/21173). In English, the disjunctive word "or" is often understood exclusively, particularly when used with the particle "either". The English example below would normally be understood in conversation as implying that Mary is not both a singer and a poet.[^o12][^o13]

\1. Mary is a singer or a poet.

However, disjunction can also be understood inclusively, even in combination with "either". For instance, the first example below shows that "either" can be [felicitously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felicity_%28pragmatics%29) used in combination with an outright statement that both disjuncts are true. The second example shows that the exclusive inference vanishes away under [downward entailing](wikipedia://en/1986315) contexts. If disjunction were understood as exclusive in this example, it would leave open the possibility that some people ate both rice and beans.[^o12]

\2. Mary is either a singer or a poet or both.

\3. Nobody ate either rice or beans.

Examples such as the above have motivated analyses of the exclusivity inference as [pragmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics) [conversational implicatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversational_implicature) calculated on the basis of an inclusive [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_semantics_%28linguistics%29). Implicatures are typically [cancellable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancellable_%28linguistics%29) and do not arise in downward entailing contexts if their calculation depends on the [Maxim of Quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooperative_principle#Maxim_of_quantity_%28content_length_and_depth%29). However, some researchers have treated exclusivity as a bona fide semantic [entailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entailment) and proposed nonclassical logics which would validate it.[^o12]

This behavior of English "or" is also found in other languages. However, many languages have disjunctive constructions which are robustly exclusive such as French *soit... soit*.[^o12]

## Alternative symbols {#Alternative_symbols}

The symbol used for exclusive disjunction varies from one field of application to the next, and even depends on the properties being emphasized in a given context of discussion. In addition to the abbreviation "XOR", any of the following symbols may also be seen:

- $+$ was used by [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298) in 1847.[^o15] Although Boole used $+$ mainly on classes, he also considered the case that $x,y$ are propositions in $x+y$, and at the time $+$ is a connective. Furthermore, Boole used it exclusively. Although such use does not show the relationship between inclusive disjunction (for which $\vee$ is almost fixedly used nowadays) and exclusive disjunction, and may also bring about confusions with its other uses, some classical and modern textbooks still keep such use.[^o16][^o17]
- $\overline{\vee}$ was used by [Christine Ladd-Franklin](wikipedia://en/9397829) in 1883.[^o18] Strictly speaking, Ladd used $A\operatorname{\overline{\vee}}B$ to express "$A$ is-not $B$" or "No $A$ is $B$", i.e., used $\overline{\vee}$ as exclusions, while implicitly $\overline{\vee}$ has the meaning of exclusive disjunction since the article is titled as "On the Algebra of Logic".
- $\not=$, denoting the negation of [equivalence](wikipedia://en/228783), was used by [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015) in 1890,[^o19]p. 307 Although the usage of $=$ as equivalence could be dated back to [George Boole](wikipedia://en/38298) in 1847,[^o15] during the 40 years after Boole, his followers, such as [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117), [Hugh MacColl](wikipedia://en/3380850), [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) and so on, did not use $\not=$ as non-equivalence literally which is possibly because it could be defined from negation and equivalence easily.
- $\circ$ was used by [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) in 1894: "$a\circ b=a-b\,\cup\,b-a$. The sign $\circ$ corresponds to Latin *aut*; the sign $\cup$ to *vel*."[^o20]p. 10 Note that the Latin word "aut" means "exclusive or" and "vel" means "inclusive or", and that Peano use $\cup$ as inclusive disjunction.
- $\vee\vee$ was used by Izrail Solomonovich Gradshtein (Израиль Соломонович Градштейн) in 1936.[^o21]p. 76
- $\oplus$ was used by [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) in 1938.[^o22] Shannon borrowed the symbol as exclusive disjunction from [Edward Vermilye Huntington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Vermilye_Huntington) in 1904.[^o23] Huntington borrowed the symbol from [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) in 1890 (the original date is not definitely known, but almost certainly it is written after 1685; and 1890 is the publishing time).[^o24] While both Huntington in 1904 and Leibniz in 1890 used the symbol as an algebraic operation. Furthermore, Huntington in 1904 used the symbol as inclusive disjunction (logical sum) too, and in 1933 used $+$ as inclusive disjunction.[^o25]
- $\not\equiv$, also denoting the negation of [equivalence](wikipedia://en/228783), was used by [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731) in 1944.[^o26]
- $J$ (as a [prefix operator](wikipedia://en/25056), $J\phi\psi$) was used by [Józef Maria Bocheński](wikipedia://en/1029376) in 1949.[^o4]p. 16 Somebody[^o27] may mistake that it is [Jan Łukasiewicz](wikipedia://en/305064) who is the first to use $J$ for exclusive disjunction (it seems that the mistake spreads widely), while neither in 1929[^o28] nor in other works did Łukasiewicz make such use. In fact, in 1949 Bocheński introduced a system of [Polish notation](wikipedia://en/25056) that names all 16 binary [connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) of classical logic which is a compatible extension of the notation of Łukasiewicz in 1929, and in which $J$ for exclusive disjunction appeared at the first time. Bocheński's usage of $J$ as exclusive disjunction has no relationship with the Polish "alternatywa rozłączna" of "exclusive or" and is an accident for which see the table on page 16 of the book in 1949.
- `^`, the [caret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caret), has been used in several [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) to denote the [bitwise](wikipedia://en/264399) exclusive or operator, beginning with [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29)[^o29] and also including [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++), [C#](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_Sharp_%28programming_language%29), [D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_%28programming_language%29), [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29), [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl), [Ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_%28programming_language%29), [PHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHP), [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) and [Rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_%28programming_language%29).
- The [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of two sets $S$ and $T$, which may be interpreted as their elementwise exclusive or, has variously been denoted as $S\ominus T$, $S\mathop{\triangledown} T$, or $S\mathop{\vartriangle} T$.

## Computer science {#Computer_science}

*Image: XOR ANSI Labelled.svg.* Traditional symbolic representation of an XOR [logic gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gate)

### Bitwise operation {#Bitwise_operation}

*Image: Z2^4; Cayley table; binary.svg.* [Nimber](wikipedia://en/164501) addition is the *exclusive or* of [nonnegative integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative_integer) in binary representation. This is also the vector addition in $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^4$.

Exclusive disjunction is often used for bitwise operations. Examples:

- 1 XOR 1 = 0
- 1 XOR 0 = 1
- 0 XOR 1 = 1
- 0 XOR 0 = 0
- 1110 XOR 1001 = 0111 (this is equivalent to addition without [carry](wikipedia://en/1629687))

As noted above, since exclusive disjunction is identical to addition modulo 2, the bitwise exclusive disjunction of two *n*-bit strings is identical to the standard vector of addition in the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^n$.

In computer science, exclusive disjunction has several uses:

- It tells whether two bits are unequal.
- It is a controllable bit-flipper (the control input chooses whether or not to invert the data input).
- It tells whether there is an [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) number of 1 bits ($A \oplus B \oplus C \oplus D \oplus E$ is true [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) an odd number of the variables are true), which is equal to the [parity bit](wikipedia://en/194467) returned by a [parity function](wikipedia://en/22399369).

In logical circuits, a simple [adder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adder_%28electronics%29) can be made with an [XOR gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR_gate) to add the numbers, and a series of AND, OR and NOT gates to create the carry output.

On some computer architectures, it is more efficient to store a zero in a register by XOR-ing the register with itself (bits XOR-ed with themselves are always zero) than to load and store the value zero.

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), XOR is sometimes used as a simple, self-inverse mixing function, such as in [one-time pad](wikipedia://en/22210) or [Feistel network](wikipedia://en/352709) systems. XOR is also heavily used in block ciphers such as AES (Rijndael) or Serpent and in block cipher implementation (CBC, CFB, OFB or CTR).

In simple threshold-activated [artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network), modeling the XOR function requires a second layer because XOR is not a [linearly separable](wikipedia://en/523173) function.

Similarly, XOR can be used in generating [entropy pools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_pool) for [hardware random number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardware_random_number_generator). The XOR operation preserves randomness, meaning that a random bit XORed with a non-random bit will result in a random bit. Multiple sources of potentially random data can be combined using XOR, and the unpredictability of the output is guaranteed to be at least as good as the best individual source.[^o45]

XOR is used in [RAID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAID) 3–6 for creating parity information. For example, RAID can "back up" bytes 10011100 and 01101100 from two (or more) hard drives by XORing the just mentioned bytes, resulting in (11110000) and writing it to another drive. Under this method, if any one of the three hard drives are lost, the lost byte can be re-created by XORing bytes from the remaining drives. For instance, if the drive containing 01101100 is lost, 10011100 and 11110000 can be XORed to recover the lost byte.[^o46]

XOR is also used to detect an overflow in the result of a signed binary arithmetic operation. If the leftmost retained bit of the result is not the same as the infinite number of digits to the left, then that means overflow occurred. XORing those two bits will give a "1" if there is an overflow.

XOR can be used to swap two numeric variables in computers, using the [XOR swap algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR_swap_algorithm); however this is regarded as more of a curiosity and not encouraged in practice.

[XOR linked lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XOR_linked_list) leverage XOR properties in order to save space to represent [doubly linked list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_linked_list) data structures.

In [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics), XOR-based drawing methods are often used to manage such items as [bounding boxes](wikipedia://en/657106) and [cursors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursor_%28computers%29) on systems without [alpha channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_compositing) or overlay planes.
