# Florence Nightingale

::: {#o6 .subject cue=lead name="Florence Nightingale"}

**Florence Nightingale** () (12 May 1820 – 13 August 1910) was an English [social reformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_movement), [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661) and the founder of modern [nursing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing). Nightingale came to prominence while serving as a manager and trainer of nurses during the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War), in which she organised care for wounded soldiers at [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople).[^o7] She significantly reduced death rates by improving hygiene and living standards. Nightingale gave nursing a favourable reputation and became an icon of [Victorian culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_culture), especially in the persona of "**The Lady with the Lamp**" making [ward rounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary_bedside_rounds) for wounded soldiers at night.[^o8][^o9]

:::

Recent commentators have asserted that Nightingale's Crimean War achievements were exaggerated by the media at the time, but critics agree on the importance of her later work in professionalising nursing roles for women. In 1860, she laid the foundation of professional nursing with the establishment of [her nursing school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Faculty_of_Nursing_and_Midwifery) at St Thomas' Hospital in London. It was the first secular nursing school in the world and is now part of [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London).[^o10] In recognition of her pioneering work in nursing, the [Nightingale Pledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightingale_Pledge) taken by new nurses, and the [Florence Nightingale Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Medal), the highest international distinction a nurse can achieve, were named in her honour, and the annual [International Nurses Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Nurses_Day) is celebrated on her birthday.[^o137] Her social reforms included improving healthcare for all sections of British society, advocating better hunger relief in India, helping to [abolish prostitution laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_%28prostitution%29) that were harsh for women, and expanding the acceptable forms of female participation in the workforce.[^o11][^o12]

Nightingale was an innovator in statistics; she represented her analysis in graphical forms to ease drawing conclusions and actionables from data. She is famous for usage of the [polar area diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_area_diagram), also called the Nightingale rose diagram, which is equivalent to a modern circular [histogram](wikipedia://en/13266) or pie chart. This diagram is still regularly used in [data visualisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_visualisation).[^o73][^o13]

Nightingale was a prodigious and versatile writer. In her lifetime, much of her published work was concerned with spreading medical knowledge. Some of her tracts were written in [simple English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_English) so that they could easily be understood by those with poor literary skills. Much of her writing, including her extensive work on religion and [mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism), has only been published posthumously.[^o14]

## Early life {#Early_life}

*Image: Embley Park.jpg.* [Embley Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embley_Park) in Hampshire, now a school, one of the family homes of [William Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Nightingale), seen in 2007

Florence Nightingale was born on 12 May 1820 at Villa Colombaia, near the Porta Romana in [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence), where her parents, [William Edward Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Nightingale) and Frances "Fanny" ( Smith), were staying during a European tour.[^n1][^n2] Like her older sister [Frances Parthenope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Parthenope_Verney), who had been named partly after [Parthenope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenope_%28Naples%29), the Greek settlement on the site of Naples, Nightingale was named after her birthplace.[^n3] The family returned to England in 1821, when Nightingale was a year old.[^n4] [Lea Hurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lea_Hurst) in Derbyshire became one of her early homes, though the family first lived at Kynsham Court, Presteigne, while Lea Hurst was being completed.[^n5][^n4] From 1825, after William Nightingale purchased [Embley Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embley_Park) in Hampshire, the family divided its time chiefly between Embley and Lea Hurst, with some time also spent in London.[^n5][^n6]

William Edward Nightingale had been born William Edward Shore, and assumed the Nightingale name after inheriting the Lea estate from Peter Nightingale.[^n7] Fanny Nightingale was the daughter of the [abolitionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abolitionism_in_the_United_Kingdom) and [Unitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarianism) [William Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Smith_%28abolitionist%29).[^n8] William Nightingale held views on women's intellectual education that were advanced for his time.[^n9] Although the daughters had a governess in their early years, William supervised their education and provided much of their later instruction himself.[^n10] Their curriculum included French, German, Italian, Latin, Greek, mathematics, constitutional history, grammar, composition, English literature and music. Nightingale's study of Latin had practical value in later life, as it sometimes enabled communication with abbots and monks she encountered while travelling.[^n11][^n12]

In 1838, William Nightingale took the family on another European tour, during which Nightingale met the English-born Parisian hostess [Mary Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Elizabeth_Mohl).[^n13] Clarke became close to the Nightingale family, especially Florence, whom she treated as an intellectual equal.[^n14] Clarke disliked the social conventions that constrained upper-class young women, reportedly saying that, if forced to choose, she would rather be "a galley-slave" than a woman.[^n14] Her independence and intellectual circle helped show Nightingale a world in which women could participate in intellectual life alongside men.[^n15]

## Nursing vocation and training {#Nursing_vocation_and_training}

On 7 February 1837, at the age of sixteen, she experienced what she later described as the first of several calls from God to service, though the call did not specify what form that service should take.[^n16] It took several years for her vocation to become focused on nursing, and resistance from her mother and sister, Parthenope, delayed her attempts to act on it.[^n17] Despite the anger and distress of her mother and sister, she rejected the expected role for a woman of her status to become a wife and mother. Nightingale worked hard to educate herself in the art and science of nursing, in the face of opposition from her family and the restrictive social code for affluent young English women.[^o37] As a young woman, Nightingale was described as attractive, slender, and graceful. While her demeanour was often severe, she was said to be very charming and to possess a radiant smile. Her most persistent suitor was the politician and poet [Richard Monckton Milnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Monckton_Milnes), but after a nine-year courtship, she rejected him, convinced that marriage would interfere with her ability to follow her calling to nursing.[^o37]

In Rome in 1847, she met [Sidney Herbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Herbert,_1st_Baron_Herbert_of_Lea), a politician who had been [Secretary at War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secretary_at_War) (1845–1846) who was on his honeymoon. He and Nightingale became lifelong close friends. Herbert would be Secretary of War again during the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War) when he and his wife would be instrumental in facilitating Nightingale's nursing work in Crimea. She became Herbert's key adviser throughout his political career, though she was accused by some of having hastened Herbert's death from [Bright's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bright's_disease) in 1861 because of the pressure her programme of reform placed on him. Nightingale also much later had strong relations with academic [Benjamin Jowett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Jowett), who may have wanted to marry her.[^o38]

Nightingale continued her travels (now with Charles and [Selina Bracebridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selina_Bracebridge)) as far as Greece and Egypt. While in Athens, Greece, Nightingale rescued a juvenile [little owl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_owl) from a group of children who were tormenting it, and she named the owl Athena. Nightingale often carried the owl in her pocket, until the pet died (shortly before Nightingale left for Crimea).[^o39]

Her writings on Egypt, in particular, are testimony to her learning, literary skill, and philosophy of life. Sailing up the Nile as far as Abu Simbel in January 1850, she wrote of the [Abu Simbel temples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Simbel_temples), "Sublime in the highest style of intellectual beauty, intellect without effort, without suffering ... not a feature is correct — but the whole effect is more expressive of spiritual grandeur than anything I could have imagined. It makes the impression upon one that thousands of voices do, uniting in one unanimous simultaneous feeling of enthusiasm or emotion, which is said to overcome the strongest man."[^o40]

*Image: Florence Nightingale by Augustus Egg.jpg.* Painting of Nightingale by [Augustus Egg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus_Egg), c. 1840s

At Thebes, she wrote of being "called to God", while a week later near Cairo she wrote in her diary (as distinct from her far longer letters that her elder sister Parthenope was to print after her return): "God called me in the morning and asked me would I do good for him alone without reputation."[^o40] Later in 1850, she visited the [Lutheran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheranism) religious community at [Kaiserswerth-am-Rhein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Düsseldorf-Kaiserswerth) in Germany, where she observed Pastor [Theodor Fliedner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Fliedner), [Friederike Fliedner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friederike_Fliedner) and the [deaconesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deaconess) working for the sick and the deprived. She regarded the experience as a turning point in her life and issued her findings anonymously in 1851; *The Institution of Kaiserswerth on the Rhine, for the Practical Training of Deaconesses, etc.* was her first published work.[^n18] She also received four months of medical training at the institute, which formed the basis for her later care.

On 22 August 1853, Nightingale took the post of superintendent at the [Institute for the Care of Sick Gentlewomen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightingale_Hospital_%28Marylebone%29) in [Upper Harley Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Harley_Street), London, a position she held until October 1854.[^o42] Her father had given her an annual income of 500 (UK), which allowed her to live comfortably and to pursue her career.[^o43]

## Crimean War {#Crimean_War}

*Image: Nightingale jewel awarded to Florence Nightingale .JPG.* A jewel awarded to Nightingale by [Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria) for her services to the soldiers in the war

Florence Nightingale's most famous contribution came during the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War), which became her central focus when reports got back to Britain about the horrific conditions for the wounded at the military hospital on the Asiatic side of the [Bosporus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosporus), opposite [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople), at Scutari (modern-day [Üsküdar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Üsküdar) in [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul)). Britain and France entered the war against Russia on the side of the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire). On 21 October 1854, she and the staff of 38 women volunteer nurses including her head nurse [Eliza Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eliza_Roberts_%28British_nurse%29) and her aunt Mai Smith,[^o45] and 15 Catholic nuns (mobilised by [Henry Edward Manning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Edward_Manning))[^o46] were sent (under the authorisation of Sidney Herbert) to the Ottoman Empire. On the way, Nightingale was assisted in Paris by her friend [Mary Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Elizabeth_Mohl).[^o47] The volunteer nurses worked about 295 nmi away from the main British camp across the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea) at [Balaklava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balaklava), in the [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimea).

Nightingale arrived at [Selimiye Barracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selimiye_Barracks) in Scutari early in November 1854. Her team found that poor care for wounded British soldiers was being delivered by overworked medical staff in the face of official indifference. Medicines were in short supply, [hygiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hygiene) was being neglected, and mass infections were common, many of them fatal. There was no equipment to process food for the patients:

After Nightingale sent a plea to *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* for a government solution to the poor condition of the facilities, the British Government commissioned [Isambard Kingdom Brunel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isambard_Kingdom_Brunel) to design a [prefabricated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefabricated_building) hospital that could be built in England and shipped to the [Dardanelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dardanelles). The result was [Renkioi Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renkioi_Hospital), a civilian facility that, under the management of [Edmund Alexander Parkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Alexander_Parkes), had a death rate less than one tenth of that of Scutari.[^o49]

[Stephen Paget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Paget) in the *[Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography)* asserted that Nightingale reduced the death rate from 42% to 2%, either by making improvements in hygiene herself, or by calling for the Sanitary Commission.[^o50] For example, Nightingale implemented [handwashing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwashing) in the hospital where she worked.[^o51]

During her first winter at Scutari, 4,077 British soldiers died there. Ten times more soldiers died from illnesses such as [typhus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhus), [typhoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoid), [cholera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholera), and [dysentery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysentery) than from battle wounds. With overcrowding, defective [sewers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitary_sewer) and lack of ventilation, the Sanitary Commission had to be sent out by the British government to Scutari in March 1855, almost six months after Nightingale had arrived. The commission flushed out the sewers and improved ventilation.[^o52] Death rates were sharply reduced, but she never claimed credit for helping to reduce the death rate.[^o53][^o54] Head Nurse Eliza Roberts nursed Nightingale through her critical illness of May 1855.[^o55]

In 2001 and [2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_%282008_film%29), the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC) released documentaries that were critical of Nightingale's performance in the Crimean War, as were some follow-up articles published in *The Guardian* and the *Sunday Times*. Nightingale scholar [Lynn McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_McDonald) has dismissed these criticisms as "often preposterous", arguing they are not supported by primary sources.[^o56][^o57]

Nightingale still believed that the death rates were due to poor nutrition, lack of supplies, stale air, and overworking of the soldiers. After she returned to Britain and began collecting evidence before the Royal Commission on the Health of the Army, she came to believe that most of the soldiers at the hospital were killed by poor living conditions. This experience influenced her later career when she advocated sanitary living conditions as of great importance. Consequently, she reduced peacetime deaths in the army and turned her attention to the sanitary design of hospitals and the introduction of sanitation in working-class homes (see Statistics and Sanitary Reform).[^o58]

*Image: Nightingale receiving the Wounded at Scutari by Jerry BarrettFXD.jpg.* *[The Mission of Mercy: Florence Nightingale receiving the Wounded at Scutari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Receiving_the_Wounded_at_Scutari)* ([Jerry Barrett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Barrett), 1857)

According to some secondary sources, Nightingale had a frosty relationship with her fellow nurse [Mary Seacole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Seacole), who ran a hotel/hospital for officers in the Crimea itself, many miles east of Scutari. Seacole's own memoir, *Wonderful Adventures of Mrs. Seacole in Many Lands*, records only one, friendly, meeting with her, when she asked her for a bed for the night and got it; Seacole was in Scutari en route to the Crimea to join her business partner and start their business. However, Seacole pointed out that when she tried to join Nightingale's group, one of Nightingale's colleagues rebuffed her, and Seacole inferred that racism was at the root of that rebuttal.[^o59] Nightingale told her brother-in-law, in a private letter, that she was worried about contact between her work and Seacole's business, claiming that while "she was very kind to the men and, what is more, to the Officers – and did some good (she) made many drunk".[^o60] Nightingale reportedly wrote, "I had the greatest difficulty in repelling Mrs. Seacole's advances, and in preventing association between her and my nurses (absolutely out of the question!) ... Anyone who employs Mrs. Seacole will introduce much kindness – also much drunkenness and improper conduct".[^o61] On the other hand, Seacole told the French chef [Alexis Soyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Soyer) that "You must know, M Soyer, that Miss Nightingale is very fond of me. When I passed through Scutari, she very kindly gave me board and lodging."

The arrival of two waves of Irish nuns, the [Sisters of Mercy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sisters_of_Mercy), to assist with nursing duties at Scutari met with different responses from Nightingale. [Mary Clare Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Clare_Moore) headed the first wave and placed herself and her Sisters under the authority of Nightingale. The two were to remain friends for the rest of their lives.[^o62] The second wave, headed by [Mary Francis Bridgeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Francis_Bridgeman) met with a cooler reception as Bridgeman refused to give up her authority over her Sisters to Nightingale while at the same time not trusting Nightingale, whom she regarded as ambitious.[^o63][^o64]

### The Lady with the Lamp {#The_Lady_with_the_Lamp}

During the Crimean War, Nightingale gained the nickname "The Lady with the Lamp" from a phrase in a report in *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)*:[^o66]

The phrase was further popularised by [Henry Wadsworth Longfellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wadsworth_Longfellow)'s 1857 poem "Santa Filomena":[^o67]

Lo! in that house of misery A lady with a lamp I see Pass through the glimmering gloom, And flit from room to room.

Nightingale was nicknamed "the lady with the hammer" by the troops after using a hammer to break into locked storage to access medicine to treat the wounded. However, Russell thought the behaviour was unladylike, and invented an alternative, leading to "The Lady with the Lamp".[^o68][^o69]

## Later career {#Later_career}

In the [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimea) on 29 November 1855, the Nightingale Fund was established for the training of nurses during a public meeting to recognise Nightingale for her work in the war. There was an outpouring of generous donations. Sidney Herbert served as honorary secretary of the fund and the [Duke of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_George,_Duke_of_Cambridge) was chairman. In her 1856 letters she described spas in the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire), detailing the health conditions, physical descriptions, dietary information, and other vital details of patients whom she directed there. She noted that the treatment there was significantly less expensive than in Switzerland.[^o71]

Nightingale had 45000 (UK) at her disposal from the Nightingale Fund to set up the first nursing school, the Nightingale Training School, at [St Thomas' Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Thomas'_Hospital) on 9 July 1860.[^o72] The first trained Nightingale nurses began work on 16 May 1865 at the Liverpool Workhouse Infirmary. Now called the [Florence Nightingale Faculty of Nursing and Midwifery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Faculty_of_Nursing_and_Midwifery), the school is part of [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London). In 1866 she said the [Royal Buckinghamshire Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Buckinghamshire_Hospital) in [Aylesbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aylesbury) near her sister's home [Claydon House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claydon_House) would be "the most beautiful hospital in England", and in 1868 called it "an excellent model to follow".[^o73]

Nightingale wrote *[Notes on Nursing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notes_on_Nursing)* (1859). The book served as the cornerstone of the curriculum at the Nightingale School and other nursing schools, though it was written specifically for the education of those nursing at home. Nightingale wrote:

> "Every day sanitary knowledge, or the knowledge of nursing, or in other words, of how to put the constitution in such a state as that it will have no disease, or that it can recover from disease, takes a higher place. It is recognised as the knowledge which every one ought to have – distinct from medical knowledge, which only a profession can have".[^o74]

*Notes on Nursing* also sold well to the general reading public and is considered a classic introduction to nursing. Nightingale spent the rest of her life promoting and organising the nursing profession. In the introduction to the 1974 edition, Joan Quixley of the Nightingale School of Nursing wrote:

> "The book was the first of its kind ever to be written. It appeared at a time when the simple rules of health were only beginning to be known, when its topics were of vital importance not only for the well-being and recovery of patients, when hospitals were riddled with infection, when nurses were still mainly regarded as ignorant, uneducated persons. The book has, inevitably, its place in the history of nursing, for it was written by the founder of modern nursing".[^o75]

As [Mark Bostridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Bostridge) has demonstrated, one of Nightingale's signal achievements was the introduction of trained nurses into the [workhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workhouse) system in Britain from the 1860s onwards.[^o77] This meant that sick paupers were no longer being cared for by other, able-bodied paupers, but by properly trained nursing staff. In the first half of the 19th century, nurses were usually former servants or widows who found no other job and therefore were forced to earn their living by this work. [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens) caricatured the standard of care in his 1843–1844 published novel *[Martin Chuzzlewit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Chuzzlewit)* in the figure of [Sarah Gamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Gamp) as being incompetent, negligent, alcoholic and corrupt. According to Caroline Worthington, director of the [Florence Nightingale Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Museum):

> "When she \[Nightingale\] started out there was no such thing as nursing. The Dickens character Sarah Gamp, who was more interested in drinking gin than looking after her patients, was only a mild exaggeration. Hospitals were places of last resort where the floors were laid with straw to soak up the blood. Florence transformed nursing when she got back \[from Crimea\]. She had access to people in high places and she used it to get things done. Florence was stubborn, opinionated, and forthright but she had to be those things in order to achieve all that she did."[^o156]

*Image: Florence Nightingale salicylic silk letter.jpg.* Letter from Nightingale advocating for the use of salicylic acid, an antiseptic, in dressings for cancer patients, 1886[^o76]

Though Nightingale is sometimes said to have denied the theory of infection for her entire life, a 2008 biography disagrees,[^o77] saying that she was simply opposed to a precursor of germ theory known as [contagionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_contagionism). This theory held that diseases could only be transmitted by touch. Before the experiments of the mid-1860s by [Pasteur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pasteur) and [Lister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Lister,_1st_Baron_Lister), hardly anyone took germ theory seriously; even afterwards, many medical practitioners were unconvinced. Bostridge points out that in the early 1880s Nightingale wrote an article for a textbook in which she advocated strict precautions designed, she said, to kill germs.[^o77] Nightingale's work served as an inspiration for nurses in the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War). The [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_%28American_Civil_War%29) government approached her for advice on organising field medicine. Her ideas inspired the volunteer body of the [United States Sanitary Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Sanitary_Commission).[^o78]

Nightingale advocated autonomous nursing leadership, and that her new style of matrons had full control and discipline over their nursing staff.[^o79] The infamous "[Guy's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy's_Hospital) dispute" in 1879–1880 between matron Margaret Burt and hospital medical staff highlighted how doctors sometimes felt that their authority was being challenged by these new style Nightingale matrons.[^o80] This was not an isolated episode and other matrons experienced similar issues, such as [Eva Luckes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eva_Luckes).[^o81]

In the 1870s, Nightingale mentored [Linda Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linda_Richards), "America's first trained nurse", and enabled her to return to the United States with adequate training and knowledge to establish high-quality nursing schools.[^o82] Richards went on to become a nursing pioneer in the US and Japan.[^o83] In 1873, [Elizabeth Christophers Hobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Christophers_Hobson) co-founded the [Bellevue Training School for Nurses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunter-Bellevue_School_of_Nursing) in New York, setting the trend for nursing training practices in the US according to the Nightingale plan.[^o84]

By 1882, several Nightingale nurses had become matrons at several leading hospitals, including, in London ([St Mary's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary's_Hospital_%28London%29), Westminster Hospital, St Marylebone Workhouse Infirmary and the [Hospital for Incurables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Hospital_for_Neuro-disability) at [Putney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putney)) and throughout Britain ([Royal Victoria Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netley_Hospital), [Netley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netley); [Edinburgh Royal Infirmary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Infirmary_of_Edinburgh); Cumberland Infirmary and Liverpool Royal Infirmary), as well as at [Sydney Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Hospital) in [New South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_South_Wales), Australia.[^o85]

In 1883, Nightingale became the first recipient of the [Royal Red Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Red_Cross). In 1904, she was appointed a [Lady of Grace of the Order of St John (LGStJ)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Saint_John_%28chartered_1888%29). In 1907, she became the first woman to be awarded the [Order of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit). In the following year she was given the [Honorary Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_City) of the [City of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London).[^o89]

From 1857 onwards, Nightingale was intermittently bedridden and suffered from depression. Her birthday is now celebrated as [International May 12th Awareness Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_May_12th_Awareness_Day) for chronic immunological and neurological diseases as Nightingale may have suffered from [myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myalgic_encephalomyelitis/chronic_fatigue_syndrome).[^o90] A recent biography, however, cites chronic [brucellosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brucellosis) and associated [spondylitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spondylitis) as the cause, attributable to an attack of remitting "Crimean fever" she experienced in May 1855 shortly after arriving in Crimea from Scutari that may have represented the acute form of the disease.[^o77] By 1870, she regained capacity to walk and her spinal pain resolved, while the depression, headaches and insomnia dissipated in the early 1880s.[^o91] Despite her symptoms, she remained phenomenally productive in social reform. During her bedridden years, she also did pioneering work in the field of hospital planning, and her work propagated quickly across Britain and the world. From home, she was able to select and purchase [flannel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flannel) via [mail order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mail_order), a then-new method of retail conceived by Welsh entrepreneur [Pryce Pryce-Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pryce_Pryce-Jones), who would use Nightingale's name as a customer in his advertising.[^o92][^o93] Nightingale's output slowed down considerably in her last decade. She wrote very little during that period due to blindness and declining mental abilities, though she still retained an interest in current affairs.[^o56]

## Relationships {#Relationships}

Although much of Nightingale's work improved the lot of women everywhere, Nightingale believed that women craved [sympathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sympathy) and were not as capable as men. She criticised early women's rights activists for decrying an alleged lack of careers for women at the same time that lucrative medical positions, under the supervision of Nightingale and others, went perpetually unfilled. She preferred the friendship of powerful men, insisting they had done more than women to help her attain her goals, writing: "I have never found one woman who has altered her life by one iota for me or my opinions."[^o95][^o96] She often referred to herself as, for example, "a man of action" and "a man of business".[^o97]

However, she did have several important and long-lasting friendships with women. Later in life, she kept up a prolonged correspondence with Irish nun [Mary Clare Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Clare_Moore), with whom she had worked in Crimea.[^o98] Her most beloved confidante was Mary Clarke, an Englishwoman she met in Paris in 1837 and kept in touch with throughout her life.[^o99] She was friends with pioneering computer programmer [Ada Lovelace](wikipedia://en/974), and upon Lovelace's death from cancer at 36 in 1852 after several months of pain, Nightingale wrote, "They said she could not possibly have lived so long, were it not for the tremendous vitality of the brain, that would not die".[^o100]

Some scholars of Nightingale's life believe that she remained chaste for her entire life, perhaps because she felt a religious calling to her career.[^o101]

## Death {#Death}

*Image: Florence Nightingale's Grave - geograph.org.uk - 163967.jpg.* Nightingale's grave in the churchyard of St Margaret's Church, [East Wellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellow,_Hampshire), Hampshire

Florence Nightingale died peacefully in her sleep in her room at 10 [South Street, Mayfair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Street,_Mayfair), London, on 13 August 1910, at the age of 90.[^o103] The offer of burial in [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey) was declined by her relatives and she is buried in the churchyard of St Margaret's Church in [East Wellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellow,_Hampshire), Hampshire, near Embley Park with a memorial with just her initials and dates of birth and death.[^o104][^o105] She left a large body of work, including several hundred notes that were previously unpublished.[^o106] A monument to Nightingale was created in [Carrara marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrara_marble) by Francis William Sargant in 1913 and placed in the cloister of the [Basilica of Santa Croce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Croce,_Florence), in Florence, Italy.[^o107]

## Contributions {#Contributions}

### Statistics and sanitary reform {#Statistics_and_sanitary_reform}

Florence Nightingale exhibited a gift for mathematics from an early age and excelled in the subject under the tutelage of her father. Later, Nightingale became a pioneer in the visual presentation of information and [statistical graphics](wikipedia://en/15934463).[^o110] She used methods such as the [pie chart](wikipedia://en/728487), which had first been developed by [William Playfair](wikipedia://en/1411831) in 1801.[^o111] While common now, it was at the time a relatively novel method of presenting data.[^o112]

Indeed, Nightingale is described as "a true pioneer in the graphical representation of statistics" and is especially well known for her usage of a [polar area diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_area_diagram),[^o112]p. 107 or occasionally the *Nightingale rose diagram*, equivalent to a modern circular [histogram](wikipedia://en/13266), to illustrate seasonal sources of patient mortality in the military field hospital she managed. While frequently credited as the creator of the polar area diagram, it is known to have been used by André-Michel Guerry in 1829[^o113] and Léon Louis Lalanne by 1830.[^o114] Nightingale called a compilation of such diagrams a "[coxcomb](wikipedia://en/728487)", but later that term would frequently be used for the individual diagrams.[^o115]

*Image: Nightingale-mortality.jpg.* "Diagram of the causes of mortality in the army in the East" (1858) by Florence Nightingale, a colored pie chart to illustrate causes of death in the British Army

She made extensive use of coxcombs to present reports on the nature and magnitude of the conditions of medical care in the Crimean War to [Members of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom) and civil servants who would have been unlikely to read or understand traditional statistical reports. In 1859, Nightingale was elected the first female member of the [Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/552664).[^o116] In 1874 she became an honorary member of the [American Statistical Association](wikipedia://en/341988).[^o117]

Her attention turned to the health of the British Army in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India) and she demonstrated that bad drainage, contaminated water, overcrowding, and poor ventilation were causing the high death rate.[^o118] Following the report *The Royal Commission on India* (1858–1863), which included drawings done by her cousin, artist [Hilary Bonham Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonham_Carter_family), with whom Nightingale had lived, Nightingale concluded that the health of the army and the people of India had to go hand in hand and so campaigned to improve the sanitary conditions of the country as a whole.[^n19]

Nightingale made a comprehensive statistical study of [sanitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanitation) in Indian rural life and was the leading figure in the introduction of improved medical care and public health services in India. In 1858 and 1859, she successfully lobbied for the establishment of a Royal Commission into the Indian situation. Two years later, she provided a report to the commission, which completed its own study in 1863. "After 10 years of sanitary reform, in 1873, Nightingale reported that mortality among the soldiers in India had declined from 69 to 18 per 1,000".[^o112]p. 107

The Royal Sanitary Commission of 1868–1869 presented Nightingale with an opportunity to press for compulsory sanitation in private houses. She lobbied the minister responsible, [James Stansfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Stansfeld), to strengthen the proposed Public Health Bill to require owners of existing properties to pay for connection to mains drainage.[^o119] The strengthened legislation was enacted in the Public Health Acts of 1874 and 1875. At the same time, she combined with the retired sanitary reformer [Edwin Chadwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Chadwick) to persuade Stansfeld to devolve powers to enforce the law to local authorities, eliminating central control by medical technocrats.[^o120] Her Crimean War statistics had convinced her that non-medical approaches were more effective given the state of knowledge at the time. Historians now believe that both drainage and devolved enforcement played a crucial role in increasing average national life expectancy by 20 years between 1871 and the mid-1930s during which time medical science made no impact on the most fatal epidemic diseases.[^o53][^o54][^o121]

### Literature and the women's movement {#Literature_and_the_women's_movement}

Historian of science [I. Bernard Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._Bernard_Cohen) argues:

Nightingale's achievements are all the more impressive when they are considered against the background of social restraints on women in Victorian England. Her father, William Edward Nightingale, was an extremely wealthy landowner, and the family moved in the highest circles of English society. In those days, women of Nightingale's class did not attend universities and did not pursue professional careers; their purpose in life was to marry and bear children. Nightingale was fortunate. Her father believed women should be educated, and he personally taught her Italian, Latin, Greek, philosophy, history, and – most unusual of all for women of the time – writing and mathematics.[^o112]p. 98 [Lytton Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lytton_Strachey) was famous for his book debunking 19th-century heroes, *[Eminent Victorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eminent_Victorians)* (1918). Nightingale gets a full chapter, but instead of debunking her, Strachey praised her in a way that raised her national reputation and made her an icon for English feminists of the 1920s and 1930s.[^o123]

While better known for her contributions in the nursing and mathematical fields, Nightingale was also an important link in the study of English [feminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminism). She wrote some 200 books, pamphlets and articles throughout her life.[^o156] During 1850 and 1852, she was struggling with her self-definition and the expectations of an upper-class marriage from her family. As she sorted out her thoughts, she wrote *Suggestions for Thought to Searchers after Religious Truth*. This was an 829-page, three-volume work, which Nightingale had printed privately in 1860, but which until recently was never published in its entirety.[^o124] An effort to correct this was made with a 2008 publication by [Wilfrid Laurier University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_Laurier_University), as volume 11[^o125] of a 16 volume project, the *Collected Works of Florence Nightingale*.[^o126] The best known of these essays, called "Cassandra", was previously published by [Ray Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Strachey) in 1928. Strachey included it in *The Cause*, a history of the women's movement. Apparently, the writing served its original purpose of sorting out thoughts; Nightingale left soon after to train at the Institute for deaconesses at [Kaiserswerth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiserswerth).

"Cassandra" protests against the over-feminisation of women into near helplessness, such as Nightingale saw in her mother's and older sister's lethargic lifestyle, despite their education. She rejected their life of thoughtless comfort for the world of social service. The work also reflects her fear of her ideas being ineffective, as were [Cassandra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassandra)'s. Cassandra was a princess of [Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy) who served as a priestess in the temple of [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo) during the [Trojan War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_War). The god gave her the gift of [prophecy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophecy); when she refused his advances, he cursed her so that her prophetic warnings would go unheeded. [Elaine Showalter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elaine_Showalter) called Nightingale's writing "a major text of English feminism, a link between [Wollstonecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Wollstonecraft) and [Woolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Woolf)".[^o127] Nightingale was initially reluctant to join the Women's [Suffrage Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_suffrage_in_the_United_Kingdom) when asked by [John Stuart Mill](wikipedia://en/15626), but through [Josephine Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephine_Butler) was convinced 'that women's enfranchisement is absolutely essential to a nation if moral and social progress is to be made'.[^o128]

In 1972, the poet [Eleanor Ross Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Ross_Taylor) wrote "Welcome Eumenides", a poem written in Nightingale's voice and quoting frequently from Nightingale's writings.[^o129] [Adrienne Rich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrienne_Rich) wrote that "Eleanor Taylor has brought together the waste of women in society and the waste of men in wars and twisted them inseparably."[^o130]

### Theology {#Theology}

Despite being named as a Unitarian in several older sources, Nightingale's own rare references to conventional Unitarianism are mildly negative. She remained in the [Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England) throughout her life, albeit with unorthodox views. Influenced from an early age by the [Wesleyan tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesleyanism), Nightingale felt that genuine religion should manifest in active care and love for others. She wrote a work of theology: *Suggestions for Thought*, her own [theodicy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodicy), which develops her [heterodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterodoxy) ideas. Nightingale questioned the goodness of a God who would condemn souls to hell and was a believer in [universal reconciliation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_reconciliation) – the concept that even those who die without being saved will eventually make it to heaven. She would sometimes comfort those in her care with this view. For example, a dying young prostitute being tended by Nightingale was concerned she was going to hell and said to her "Pray God, that you may never be in the despair I am in at this time". The nurse replied "Oh, my girl, are you not now more merciful than the God you think you are going to? Yet the real God is far more merciful than any human creature ever was or can ever imagine."[^o132][^o96]

Despite her intense personal devotion to Christ, Nightingale believed for much of her life that the pagan and eastern religions had also contained genuine revelation. She was a strong opponent of discrimination both against Christians of different denominations and against those of non-Christian religions. Nightingale believed religion helped provide people with the fortitude for arduous good work and would ensure the nurses in her care attended religious services. However, she was often critical of organised religion. She disliked the role the 19th century Church of England would sometimes play in worsening the oppression of the poor. Nightingale argued that secular hospitals usually provided better care than their religious counterparts. While she held that the ideal health professional should be inspired by a religious as well as professional motive, she said that in practice many religiously motivated health workers were concerned chiefly in securing their own salvation and that this motivation was inferior to the professional desire to deliver the best possible care.[^o132][^o96]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

### Nursing {#Nursing}

*Image: Nightingaleplaque.JPG.* [Blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque) for Nightingale in [South Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Street,_Mayfair), Mayfair, London

Nightingale's lasting contribution has been her role in founding the modern nursing profession.[^o135] She set an example of compassion, commitment to patient care and diligent and thoughtful hospital administration. The first official nurses' training programme, her [Nightingale School for Nurses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightingale_School_for_Nurses), opened in 1860 and is now called the Florence Nightingale Faculty of Nursing and Midwifery at [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London).[^o136]

She belongs to that select band of historical characters who are instantly recognisable: the Lady with the Lamp, ministering to the wounded and dying.

In 1912, the [International Committee of the Red Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Committee_of_the_Red_Cross) instituted the [Florence Nightingale Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Medal), which is awarded every two years to nurses or nursing aides for outstanding service.[^o137] It is the highest international distinction a nurse can achieve and is awarded to nurses or nursing aides for "exceptional courage and devotion to the wounded, sick or disabled or to civilian victims of a conflict or disaster" or "exemplary services or a creative and pioneering spirit in the areas of public health or nursing education".[^o138] Since 1965, [International Nurses Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Nurses_Day) has been celebrated on her birthday (12 May) each year.[^o139] The [President of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_India) honours nursing professionals with the "National Florence Nightingale Award" every year on International Nurses Day.[^o140] The award, established in 1973, is given in recognition of meritorious services of nursing professionals characterised by devotion, sincerity, dedication and compassion.[^o140]

The [Nightingale Pledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightingale_Pledge) is a modified version of the [Hippocratic Oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocratic_Oath) which nurses in the United States recite at their [pinning ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinning_ceremony_%28nursing%29) at the end of training. Created in 1893 and named after Nightingale as the founder of modern nursing, the pledge is a statement of the ethics and principles of the nursing profession.[^o141]

*Image: Pledge of Florence Nightingale. Wellcome L0008728.jpg.* The [Nightingale Pledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nightingale_Pledge)

The Florence Nightingale Declaration Campaign,[^o142] established by nursing leaders throughout the world through the Nightingale Initiative for Global Health (NIGH), aims to build a global grassroots movement to achieve two [United Nations Resolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Resolutions) for adoption by the UN General Assembly of 2008. They will declare: The International Year of the Nurse–2010 (the centenary of Nightingale's death); The UN Decade for a Healthy World – 2011 to 2020 (the bicentenary of Nightingale's birth). NIGH also works to rekindle awareness about the important issues highlighted by Florence Nightingale, such as preventive medicine and holistic health. As of 2016, the Florence Nightingale Declaration has been signed by over 25,000 signatories from 106 countries.[^o143]

During the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War), Nightingale inspired many [US Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Army) nurses, sparking a renewal of interest in her life and work. Her admirers include [Country Joe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Joe_McDonald) of [Country Joe and the Fish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_Joe_and_the_Fish), who has assembled an extensive website in her honour.[^o144] The Agostino Gemelli Medical School[^o145] in Rome, the first university-based hospital in Italy and one of its most respected medical centres, honoured Nightingale's contribution to the nursing profession by giving the name "Bedside Florence" to a wireless computer system it developed to assist nursing.[^o146]

### Hospitals {#Hospitals}

Four hospitals in Istanbul are named after Nightingale: Florence Nightingale Hospital in [Şişli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Şişli) (the biggest private hospital in Turkey), Metropolitan Florence Nightingale Hospital in Gayrettepe, European Florence Nightingale Hospital in [Mecidiyeköy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecidiyeköy), and Florence Nightingale Hospital in [Kadıköy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadıköy), all belonging to the Turkish Cardiology Foundation.[^o148]

In 2011, an appeal was made for the former [Derbyshire Royal Infirmary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_Royal_Infirmary) hospital in Derby, England to be named after Nightingale. It was suggested the name could be either Nightingale Community Hospital or Florence Nightingale Community Hospital. The area where the hospital is situated is sometimes referred to as the "Nightingale Quarter".[^o149]

During the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic_in_the_United_Kingdom), a number of temporary [NHS Nightingale Hospitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS_Nightingale_Hospitals) were set up in readiness for an expected rise in the number of patients needing critical care. The first was housed in the [ExCeL London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ExCeL_London)[^o150] and several others followed across England.[^o151] Celebrations to mark her bicentenary in 2020, were disrupted by the coronavirus pandemic and Nightingale's contribution to scientific and statistical analysis of infectious disease and nursing practice may have led to the new temporary hospitals being in her name, in Scotland named the [NHS Louisa Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHS_Louisa_Jordan_Hospital) after a nurse who followed in Nightingale's footsteps in battlefield nursing in [World War One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_One).[^o204]

### Museums and monuments {#Museums_and_monuments}

*Image: Florence\_Nightingale\_monument\_London.jpg.* Statue of Nightingale by [Arthur George Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_George_Walker) in Waterloo Place, London

A statue of Florence Nightingale by the 20th-century war memorialist [Arthur George Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_George_Walker) stands in [Waterloo Place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo_Place), [Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster), London, just off [The Mall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mall_%28London%29). There are three statues of Nightingale in Derby – one outside the [Derbyshire Royal Infirmary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derbyshire_Royal_Infirmary) (DRI), one in St Peter's Street, and one above the Nightingale-Macmillan Continuing Care Unit opposite the Derbyshire Royal Infirmary. A [pub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_house) named after her stands close to the DRI.[^o153] The Nightingale-Macmillan continuing care unit is now at the [Royal Derby Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Derby_Hospital), formerly known as The City Hospital, Derby.[^o154]

A [stained glass window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained_glass_window) was commissioned for inclusion in the DRI chapel in the late 1950s. When the chapel was demolished the window was removed and installed in the replacement chapel. At the closure of the DRI, the window was again removed and stored. In October 2010, 6000 (UK) was raised to reposition the window in [St Peter's Church, Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Peter's_Church,_Derby). The work features nine panels, of the original ten, depicting scenes of hospital life, Derby townscapes, and Nightingale herself. Some of the work was damaged and the tenth panel was dismantled for the glass to be used in the repair of the remaining panels. All the figures, who are said to be modelled on prominent Derby town figures of the early sixties, surround and praise a central pane of the triumphant Christ. A nurse who posed for the top right panel in 1959 attended the rededication service in October 2010.[^o155]

The [Florence Nightingale Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Museum) at [St Thomas' Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Thomas'_Hospital) in London reopened in May 2010 in time for the centenary of Nightingale's death.[^o156] Another museum devoted to her is at her sister's family home, [Claydon House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claydon_House), now a property of the [National Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Trust_for_Places_of_Historic_Interest_or_Natural_Beauty).

Upon the centenary of Nightingale's death in 2010, and to commemorate her connection with [Malvern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malvern,_Worcestershire), the [Malvern Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malvern_Museum) held a Florence Nightingale exhibit[^o159] with a school poster competition to promote some events.[^o160]

In Istanbul, the northernmost tower of the Selimiye Barracks building is now the Florence Nightingale Museum.[^o161] and in several of its rooms, relics and reproductions related to Florence Nightingale and her nurses are on exhibition.[^o162]

When Nightingale moved on to the Crimea itself in May 1855, she often travelled on horseback to make hospital inspections. She later transferred to a mule cart and was reported to have escaped serious injury when the cart was toppled in an accident. Following this, she used a solid Russian-built black carriage, with a waterproof hood and curtains. The carriage was returned to England by Alexis Soyer after the war and subsequently given to the Nightingale training school. The carriage was damaged when the hospital was bombed during the Second World War. It was restored and transferred to Claydon House and is now displayed at the [Army Medical Services Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Medical_Services_Museum) in [Mytchett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mytchett), Surrey, near [Aldershot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldershot).[^o163]

*Image: Florence Nightingale Statue, London Road, Derby.jpg.* Florence Nightingale Statue, [London Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Road_%28Sheffield%29), [Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derby)

A bronze plaque, attached to the plinth of the Crimean Memorial in the [Haydarpaşa Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haydarpaşa_Cemetery), Istanbul, Turkey and unveiled on [Empire Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_Day), 1954, to celebrate the 100th anniversary of her nursing service in that region, bears the inscription: "To Florence Nightingale, whose work near this Cemetery a century ago relieved much human suffering and laid the foundations for the nursing profession."[^o164] Other monuments of Nightingale include a statue at [Chiba University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiba_University) in Japan, a bust at [Tarlac State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarlac_State_University) in the Philippines, and a bust at [Gun Hill Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gun_Hill_Park) in Aldershot in the UK. Other nursing schools around the world are named after Nightingale, such as in [Anápolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anápolis) in Brazil.[^o165]

### Audio {#Audio}

Florence Nightingale's voice was saved for posterity in a [phonograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph) recording from 1890 preserved in the [British Library Sound Archive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Library_Sound_Archive). The recording, made in aid of the [Light Brigade Relief Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_of_the_Light_Brigade) and available to hear online, says:

> When I am no longer even a memory, just a name, I hope my voice may perpetuate the great work of my life. God bless my dear old comrades of Balaclava and bring them safe to shore. Florence Nightingale.[^o167]

### Theatre {#Theatre}

The first theatrical representation of Nightingale was [Reginald Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Berkeley)'s *The Lady with the Lamp*, premiering in London in 1929 with [Edith Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Evans) in the title role. It did not portray her as an entirely sympathetic character and draws much characterisation from [Lytton Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lytton_Strachey)'s biography of her in *[Eminent Victorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eminent_Victorians)*.[^o169]

In 2009, a stage musical play representation of Nightingale entitled *The Voyage of the Lass* was produced by the Association of Nursing Service Administrators of the [Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippines). In 2019, *Nightingale*, a stage musical produced by Pamela Gerke and directed by Rachel Rene, was performed in Seattle, Washington.[^o170]

### Film {#Film}

Florence Nightingale first appeared on screen in the silent era. *[The Victoria Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Cross)* (1912) starred [Julia Swayne Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Swayne_Gordon) and was a biographical film; and *[Florence Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_%281915_film%29)* (1915) featured [Elisabeth Risdon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabeth_Risdon). In 1936, *[The White Angel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_White_Angel_%281936_film%29)* starred [Kay Francis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kay_Francis) as Nightingale, depicting Florence Nightingale's pioneering work in nursing during the Crimean War.

Her appearances in theatre returned to British cinema in *[The Lady with a Lamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lady_with_a_Lamp)* (1951), directed by [Herbert Wilcox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Wilcox) and starring [Anna Neagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Neagle), based on a 1929 stage play.[^o172][^o173] In 1993, [Nest Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nest_Family_Entertainment) released an animated *Florence Nightingale* for younger audiences.[^o174]

### Television {#Television}

Portrayals of Nightingale on television, in documentary as in fiction, vary – the BBC's 2008 *[Florence Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_%282008_film%29)*, featuring [Laura Fraser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laura_Fraser),[^o176] emphasised her independence and feeling of religious calling, but in Channel 4's 2006 *Mary Seacole: The Real Angel of the Crimea*, she is portrayed as narrow-minded and opposed to [Mary Seacole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Seacole)'s efforts.[^o177]

Other various notable productions are: [Jaclyn Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaclyn_Smith) in the 1985 television biopic *Florence Nightingale*, [Emma Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emma_Thompson) in an ITV comedy series *[Alfresco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfresco_%28TV_series%29)* episode (1983),[^o178] [Janet Suzman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Suzman) in the 1974 stage-style biographical production *Miss Nightingale*,[^o179] Julie Harris in the *[Hallmark Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallmark_Hall_of_Fame)* episode “[The Holy Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Hallmark_Hall_of_Fame_episodes)” (1965), and [Sarah Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Churchill_%28actress%29) in the episode “Florence Nightingale” (1952).[^o180]

### Banknotes and coins {#Banknotes_and_coins}

*Image: Florence Nightingale coin.jpg.* [£2 coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commemorative_coins_of_the_United_Kingdom#Two_pounds) issued in 2010 celebrating Nightingale and nursing[^o183]

Florence Nightingale's image appeared on the reverse of [£10 banknotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banknotes_of_the_pound_sterling#Historical_figures) issued by the [Bank of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_England_note_issues) from 1975 until 1994. As well as a standing portrait, she was depicted on the notes in a field hospital, holding her lamp.[^o184] Nightingale's note was in circulation alongside the images of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), [William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare), [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens), [Michael Faraday](wikipedia://en/19727), [Sir Christopher Wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Christopher_Wren), the [Duke of Wellington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Wellesley,_1st&nbsp;Duke_of_Wellington) and [George Stephenson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Stephenson), and prior to 2002, other than the female monarchs, she was the only woman whose image had ever adorned British paper currency.[^n19]

The centenary of Nightingale's death in 2010 was commemorated with a [£2 coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commemorative_coins_of_the_United_Kingdom#Two_pounds) issued by the [Royal Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mint) showing her taking a patient's pulse.[^o183]

### Photographs {#Photographs}

Nightingale had a principled objection to having photographs taken or her portrait painted. An extremely rare photograph of her, in May 1858 by William Slater, was discovered in 2006 and is now at the [Florence Nightingale Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale_Museum) in London.[^o186] The photo shows her reading outside her family home in Embley Park, Hampshire.[^o187]

A black-and-white photograph taken in about 1907 by [Lizzie Caswall Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lizzie_Caswall_Smith) at Nightingale's London home in South Street, Mayfair, was auctioned on 19 November 2008 by Dreweatts auction house in [Newbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newbury,_Berkshire), Berkshire, England, for 5500 (UK), which is the last currently-known photo of her.[^o188] The picture shows her in Claydon House, Buckinghamshire.[^o189]

### Biographies {#Biographies}

The first biography of Nightingale was published in England in 1855. In 1911, [Edward Tyas Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Tyas_Cook) was authorised by Nightingale's executors to write the official life, published in two volumes in 1913. Nightingale was also the subject of one of Lytton Strachey's four mercilessly provocative biographical essays, *Eminent Victorians*. Strachey regarded Nightingale as an intense, driven woman who was both personally intolerable and admirable in her achievements.[^o191]

[Cecil Woodham-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_Woodham-Smith), like Strachey, relied heavily on Cook's *Life* in her 1950 biography, though she did have access to new family material preserved at Claydon. In 2008, Mark Bostridge published a major new life of Nightingale, almost exclusively based on unpublished material from the Verney Collections at Claydon and from archival documents from about 200 archives around the world, some of which had been published by Lynn McDonald in her sixteen-volume edition of the *Collected Works of Florence Nightingale* (2001—2012).[^n19]

### Other {#Other}

In 2002, Nightingale was ranked number 52 in the BBC's list of the [100 Greatest Britons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/100_Greatest_Britons) following a UK-wide vote. In 2006, the Japanese public ranked Nightingale number 17 in [The Top 100 Historical Persons in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Top_100_Historical_Persons_in_Japan).[^o193]

Several churches in the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion) commemorate Nightingale with a feast day on their [liturgical calendars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_calendar).[^o194] The [Evangelical Lutheran Church in America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical_Lutheran_Church_in_America) commemorates her as a [Renewer of Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renewer_of_Society) with [Clara Maass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clara_Maass) on 13 August.[^o195] [Florence Li Tim-Oi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Li_Tim-Oi), the first woman ordained priest in the Anglican Communion, in 1944, took Florence as her baptismal name after Florence Nightingale.[^o196]

[Washington National Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_National_Cathedral) celebrates Nightingale's accomplishments with a double-lancet stained glass window featuring six scenes from her life, designed by artist Joseph G. Reynolds and installed in 1983.[^o197]

The [US Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy) ship the Florence Nightingale was commissioned in 1942. Beginning in 1968, the [US Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force) operated a fleet of 20 [C-9A "Nightingale"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_C-9) [aeromedical evacuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_evacuation) aircraft, based on the [McDonnell Douglas DC-9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell_Douglas_DC-9) platform.[^o198] The last of these planes was retired from service in 2005.[^o199]

In 1981, the asteroid [3122 Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3122_Florence) was named after her.[^o200] A Dutch [KLM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KLM) [McDonnell-Douglas MD-11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McDonnell-Douglas_MD-11) (registered PH-KCD) was also named in her honour; it served the airline for 20 years, from 1994 to 2014.[^o201] Nightingale has appeared on international postage stamps, including, the UK, [Alderney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_postage_stamps_of_Alderney), Australia, Belgium, Dominica, Hungary (showing the Florence Nightingale medal awarded by the International Red Cross), and Germany.[^o202]

Florence Nightingale is [remembered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar_of_saints_%28Church_of_England%29) in the [Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England) with a [commemoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commemoration_%28observance%29) on 13 August.[^o203] Celebrations to mark her bicentenary in 2020, were disrupted by the [coronavirus pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic), but the [NHS Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_hospitals_in_the_United_Kingdom) hospitals were named after her.[^o204] Nightingale Road () in [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong), between the [Queen Elizabeth Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_Hospital,_Hong_Kong) and the nursing school, was officially named by the [Lands Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lands_Department) after Florence Nightingale in 2008.[^o205]
