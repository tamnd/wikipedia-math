# Graded ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Graded ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in particular [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **graded ring** is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) such that the underlying [additive group](wikipedia://en/332229) is a [direct sum of abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_abelian_groups) $R_i$ such that $R_i R_j \subseteq R_{i+j}$. The [index set](wikipedia://en/393055) is usually the set of nonnegative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) or the set of integers, but can be any [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652). The direct sum decomposition is usually referred to as **gradation** or **grading**.

:::

A **graded module** is defined similarly (see below for the precise definition). It generalizes [graded vector spaces](wikipedia://en/1155734). A graded module that is also a graded ring is called a **graded algebra**. A graded ring could also be viewed as a graded $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra.

The [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) is not important (in fact not used at all) in the definition of a graded ring; hence, the notion applies to [non-associative algebras](wikipedia://en/6533841) as well; e.g., one can consider a [graded Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/5475402).

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Homogeneous component"}

## First properties {#First_properties}

Generally, the index set of a graded ring is assumed to be the set of nonnegative integers, unless otherwise explicitly specified. This is the case in this article.

A graded ring is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that is decomposed into a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687)

$$R = \bigoplus_{n=0}^\infty R_n = R_0 \oplus R_1 \oplus R_2 \oplus \cdots$$

of [additive groups](wikipedia://en/332229), such that

$$R_mR_n \subseteq R_{m+n}$$

for all nonnegative integers $m$ and $n$.

A nonzero element of $R_n$ is said to be *homogeneous* of *degree* $n$. By definition of a direct sum, every nonzero element $a$ of $R$ can be uniquely written as a sum $a=a_0+a_1+\cdots +a_n$ where each $a_i$ is either 0 or homogeneous of degree $i$. The nonzero $a_i$ are the *homogeneous components* of $a$.

Some basic properties are:

- $R_0$ is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of $R$; in particular, the multiplicative identity $1$ is a homogeneous element of degree zero.
- For any $n$, $R_n$ is a two-sided $R_0$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410), and the direct sum decomposition is a direct sum of $R_0$-modules.
- $R$ is an [associative $R_0$-algebra](wikipedia://en/2112).

### Ideals {#Ideals}

An [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) $I\subseteq R$ is *homogeneous*, if for every $a \in I$, the homogeneous components of $a$ also belong to $I$. (Equivalently, if it is a graded submodule of $R$; see .) The [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of a homogeneous ideal $I$ with $R_n$ is an $R_0$-[submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule) of $R_n$ called the *homogeneous part* of degree $n$ of $I$. A homogeneous ideal is the direct sum of its homogeneous parts.

### Quotients {#Quotients}

If $I$ is a two-sided homogeneous ideal in $R$, then $R/I$ is also a graded ring, decomposed as

$$R/I = \bigoplus_{n=0}^\infty R_n/I_n,$$

where $I_n$ is the homogeneous part of degree $n$ of $I$.

:::

## Basic examples {#Basic_examples}

- Any (non-graded) ring *R* can be given a gradation by letting $R_0=R$, and $R_i=0$ for *i* ≠ 0. This is called the **trivial gradation** on *R*.
- The [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R = k[t_1, \ldots, t_n]$ is graded by [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652): it is a direct sum of $R_i$ consisting of [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) of degree *i*.
- Let *S* be the set of all nonzero homogeneous elements in a graded [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) *R*. Then the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of *R* with respect to *S* is a $\mathbb{Z}$-graded ring.
- If *I* is an ideal in a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R*, then $\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty} I^n/I^{n+1}$ is a graded ring called the [associated graded ring](wikipedia://en/38279783) of *R* along *I*; geometrically, it is the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of the [normal cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_cone) along the [subvariety](wikipedia://en/248808) defined by *I*.
- Let *X* be a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), *H*<sup>*i*</sup>(*X*; *R*) the *i*th [cohomology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group) with coefficients in a ring *R*. Then *H*<sup>\*</sup>(*X*; *R*), the [cohomology ring](wikipedia://en/1069723) of *X* with coefficients in *R*, is a graded ring whose underlying [group](wikipedia://en/2974) is $\bigoplus_{i = 0}^\infty H^i(X; R)$ with the multiplicative structure given by the [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684).

## Graded module {#Graded_module}

The corresponding idea in [module theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_theory) is that of a **graded module**, namely a left [module](wikipedia://en/276410) *M* over a graded ring *R* such that

$$M = \bigoplus_{i\in \mathbb{N}}M_i ,$$

and

$$R_iM_j \subseteq M_{i+j}$$

for every $i$ and $j$.

Examples:

- A [graded vector space](wikipedia://en/1155734) is an example of a graded module over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) (with the field having trivial grading).
- A graded ring is a graded module over itself. An ideal in a graded ring is homogeneous [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is a graded submodule. The [annihilator](wikipedia://en/328684) of a graded module is a homogeneous ideal.
- Given an ideal *I* in a commutative ring *R* and an *R*-module *M*, the direct sum $\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty} I^n M/I^{n+1} M$ is a graded module over the associated graded ring $\bigoplus_0^{\infty} I^n/I^{n+1}$.

A *morphism* $f: N \to M$ of graded modules, called a **graded morphism** or *graded homomorphism* , is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/474118) of the underlying modules that respects grading; i.e., $f(N_i) \subseteq M_i$. A **graded submodule** is a submodule that is a graded module in own right and such that the set-theoretic [inclusion](wikipedia://en/492516) is a morphism of graded modules. Explicitly, a graded module *N* is a graded submodule of *M* if and only if it is a submodule of *M* and satisfies $N_i = N \cap M_i$. The [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) and the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a morphism of graded modules are graded submodules.

Remark: To give a graded morphism from a graded ring to another graded ring with the image lying in the [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) is the same as to give the structure of a graded algebra to the latter ring.

Given a graded module $M$, the $\ell$-twist of $M$ is a graded module defined by $M(\ell)_n = M_{n+\ell}$ (cf. [Serre's twisting sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre's_twisting_sheaf) in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997)).

Let *M* and *N* be graded modules. If $f\colon M \to N$ is a morphism of modules, then *f* is said to have degree *d* if $f(M_n) \subseteq N_{n+d}$. An [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) is an example of such a morphism having degree 1.

:::

## Invariants of graded modules {#Invariants_of_graded_modules}

Given a graded module *M* over a commutative graded ring *R*, one can associate the [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) $P(M, t) \in \mathbb{Z}[\![t]\!]$:

$$P(M, t) = \sum \ell(M_n) t^n$$

(assuming $\ell(M_n)$ are finite.) It is called the [Hilbert–Poincaré series](wikipedia://en/17006296) of *M*.

A graded module is said to be finitely generated if the underlying module is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430). The generators may be taken to be homogeneous (by replacing the generators by their homogeneous parts.)

Suppose *R* is a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $k[x_0, \dots, x_n]$, *k* a field, and *M* a finitely generated graded module over it. Then the function $n \mapsto \dim_k M_n$ is called the Hilbert function of *M*. The function coincides with the [integer-valued polynomial](wikipedia://en/585797) for large *n* called the [Hilbert polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_polynomial) of *M*.

## Graded algebra {#Graded_algebra}

An [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) *A* over a ring *R* is a **graded algebra** if it is graded as a ring.

In the usual case where the ring *R* is not graded (in particular if *R* is a field), it is given the trivial grading (every element of *R* is of degree 0). Thus, $R\subseteq A_0$ and the graded pieces $A_i$ are *R*-modules.

In the case where the ring *R* is also a graded ring, then one requires that

$$R_iA_j \subseteq A_{i+j}$$

In other words, we require *A* to be a graded left module over *R*.

Examples of graded algebras are common in mathematics:

- [Polynomial rings](wikipedia://en/373065). The homogeneous elements of degree *n* are exactly the homogeneous polynomials of degree *n*.
- The [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628) $T^{\bullet} V$ of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V*. The homogeneous elements of degree *n* are the [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965) of order *n*, $T^{n} V$.
- The [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) $\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\bullet} V$ and the [symmetric algebra](wikipedia://en/654098) $S^{\bullet} V$ are also graded algebras.
- The [cohomology ring](wikipedia://en/1069723) $H^{\bullet}$ in any [cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_theory) is also graded, being the direct sum of the cohomology groups $H^n$.

Graded algebras are much used in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), and [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801). One example is the close relationship between [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) and [projective varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_varieties) (cf. [Homogeneous coordinate ring](wikipedia://en/23833909)).

Another example of a graded algebra which is non-commutative is the [Leavitt path algebra](wikipedia://en/64956453) which has a natural $\mathbb{Z}$-grading.

:::

## *G*-graded rings and algebras {#G-graded_rings_and_algebras}

The above definitions have been generalized to rings graded using any [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) *G* as an index set. A ***G*-graded ring** *R* is a ring with a direct sum decomposition

$$R = \bigoplus_{i\in G}R_i$$

such that

$$R_i R_j \subseteq R_{i \cdot j}.$$

Elements of *R* that lie inside $R_i$ for some $i \in G$ are said to be **homogeneous** of **grade** *i*.

The previously defined notion of "graded ring" now becomes the same thing as an $\mathbb{N}$-graded ring, where $\mathbb{N}$ is the monoid of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) under addition. The definitions for graded modules and algebras can also be extended this way replacing the indexing set $\mathbb{N}$ with any monoid *G*.

Remarks:

- If we do not require that the ring have an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962), [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) may replace monoids.

Examples:

- A [group](wikipedia://en/19447) naturally grades the corresponding [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223); similarly, [monoid rings](wikipedia://en/20781) are graded by the corresponding monoid.
- An (associative) [superalgebra](wikipedia://en/469950) is another term for a [$\mathbb{Z}_2$](wikipedia://en/52327)-graded algebra. Examples include [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305). Here the homogeneous elements are either of degree 0 (even) or 1 (odd).

### Anticommutativity {#Anticommutativity}

Some graded rings (or algebras) are endowed with an [anticommutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutative) structure. This notion requires a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/19652#Monoid_homomorphisms) of the monoid of the gradation into the additive monoid of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, the field with two elements. Specifically, a **signed monoid** consists of a pair $(\Gamma, \varepsilon)$ where $\Gamma$ is a monoid and $\varepsilon \colon \Gamma \to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is a homomorphism of additive monoids. An **anticommutative $\Gamma$-graded ring** is a ring *A* graded with respect to $\Gamma$ such that:

$$xy=(-1)^{\varepsilon (\deg x) \varepsilon (\deg y)}yx ,$$

for all homogeneous elements *x* and *y*.

::: {#o15 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- An [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) is an example of an anticommutative algebra, graded with respect to the structure $(\mathbb{Z}, \varepsilon)$ where $\varepsilon \colon \mathbb{Z} \to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ is the quotient map.
- A [supercommutative algebra](wikipedia://en/859686) (sometimes called a **skew-commutative associative ring**) is the same thing as an anticommutative $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}, \varepsilon)$-graded algebra, where $\varepsilon$ is the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) of the additive structure of $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

:::

## Graded monoid {#Graded_monoid}

Intuitively, a graded [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) is the subset of a graded ring, $\bigoplus_{n\in \mathbb N_0}R_n$, generated by the $R_n$'s, without using the additive part. That is, the set of elements of the graded monoid is $\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N_0}R_n$.

Formally, a graded monoid[^o18] is a monoid $(M,\cdot)$, with a gradation function $\phi:M\to\mathbb N_0$ such that $\phi(m\cdot m')=\phi(m)+\phi(m')$. Note that the gradation of $1_M$ is necessarily 0. Some authors request furthermore that $\phi(m)\ne 0$ when *m* is not the identity.

Assuming the gradations of non-identity elements are non-zero, the number of elements of gradation *n* is at most $g^n$ where *g* is the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of a [generating set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generator_%28monoid%29) *G* of the monoid. Therefore, the number of elements of gradation *n* or less is at most $n+1$ (for $g=1$) or $\frac{g^{n+1}-1}{g-1}$ else. Indeed, each such element is the product of at most *n* elements of *G*, and only $\frac{g^{n+1}-1}{g-1}$ such products exist. Similarly, the identity element can not be written as the product of two non-identity elements. That is, there is no unit [divisor](wikipedia://en/32981716#Definition) in such a graded monoid.

### Power series indexed by a graded monoid {#Power_series_indexed_by_a_graded_monoid}

These notions allow us to extend the notion of [power series ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series_ring). Instead of the indexing family being $\mathbb N$, the indexing family could be any graded monoid, assuming that the number of elements of degree *n* is finite, for each integer *n*.

More formally, let $(K,+_K,\times_K)$ be an arbitrary [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468) and $(R,\cdot,\phi)$ a graded monoid. Then $K\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle$ denotes the semiring of power series with coefficients in *K* indexed by *R*. Its elements are functions from *R* to *K*. The sum of two elements $s,s'\in K\langle\langle R\rangle\rangle$ is defined pointwise, it is the function sending $m\in R$ to $s(m)+_Ks'(m)$, and the product is the function sending $m\in R$ to the infinite sum $\sum_{p,q \in R \atop p \cdot q=m}s(p)\times_K s'(q)$. This sum is correctly defined (i.e., finite) because, for each *m*, there are only a finite number of pairs (*p*, *q*) such that *pq* = *m*.

### Free monoid {#Free_monoid}

In [formal language theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_language_theory), given an [alphabet](wikipedia://en/4292269) *A*, the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) of words over *A* can be considered as a graded monoid, where the gradation of a word is its length.
