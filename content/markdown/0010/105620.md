# Orthogonal matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthogonal matrix"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), an **[orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221) matrix** or **[orthonormal](wikipedia://en/194743) matrix $Q$**, is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) whose columns and rows are orthonormal [vectors](wikipedia://en/23218956).

:::

One way to express this is

$$Q^\mathrm{T} Q = Q Q^\mathrm{T} = I,$$

where $Q^{T}$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of $Q$ and $I$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

This leads to the equivalent [characterization](wikipedia://en/780886): a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $Q$ is orthogonal if its transpose is equal to its [inverse](wikipedia://en/217122):

$$Q^\mathrm{T}=Q^{-1},$$

where $Q^{-1}$ is the inverse of $Q$.

An orthogonal matrix $Q$ is necessarily [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) (with inverse $Q^{-1} = Q^{T}$), [unitary](wikipedia://en/129222) ($Q^{-1} = Q^{\ast}$), where $Q^{\ast}$ is the [Hermitian adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) ([conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548)) of $Q$, and therefore [normal](wikipedia://en/170366) ($Q^{\ast}Q = QQ^{\ast}$) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of any orthogonal matrix is either +1 or −1. As a [linear transformation](wikipedia://en/18102), an orthogonal matrix preserves the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) of [vectors](wikipedia://en/23218956), and therefore acts as an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), such as a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009), [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) or [rotoreflection](wikipedia://en/244324). In other words, it is a [unitary transformation](wikipedia://en/236007).

The set of $n \times n$ orthogonal matrices, under [multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280), forms the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $O(n)$, known as the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954). The [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ consisting of orthogonal matrices with determinant +1 is called the [special orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954#Special_orthogonal_group), and each of its elements is a special orthogonal matrix. As a linear transformation, every special orthogonal matrix acts as a rotation.

## Overview {#Overview}

*Image: Matrix multiplication transpose.svg.* Visual understanding of multiplication by the transpose of a matrix. If A is an orthogonal matrix and B is its transpose, the ij-th element of the product AA<sup>T</sup> will vanish if i≠j, because the i-th row of A is orthogonal to the j-th row of A.

An orthogonal matrix is the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) specialization of a [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222), and thus always a [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366). Although we consider only real matrices here, the definition can be used for matrices with entries from any [field](wikipedia://en/10603). However, orthogonal matrices arise naturally from [dot products](wikipedia://en/157093), and for matrices of complex numbers that leads instead to the unitary requirement. Orthogonal matrices preserve the dot product,[^o3] so, for vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in an $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) real [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)

$${\mathbf u} \cdot {\mathbf v} = \left(Q {\mathbf u}\right) \cdot \left(Q {\mathbf v}\right)$$

where $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix. To see the [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856) connection, consider a vector $\mathbf{v}$ in an $n$-dimensional real Euclidean space. Written with respect to an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063), the squared [length](wikipedia://en/53932) of $\mathbf{v}$ is $\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{v}$. If a [linear transformation](wikipedia://en/18102), in matrix form $Q\mathbf{v}$, preserves vector lengths, then

$${\mathbf v}^\mathrm{T}{\mathbf v} = (Q{\mathbf v})^\mathrm{T}(Q{\mathbf v}) = {\mathbf v}^\mathrm{T} Q^\mathrm{T} Q {\mathbf v} .$$

Thus [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) linear [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777)—[rotations](wikipedia://en/39789), [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829), and their combinations—produce orthogonal matrices. The [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) is also true: orthogonal matrices imply [orthogonal transformations](wikipedia://en/1382381). However, linear algebra includes orthogonal transformations between spaces which may be neither finite-dimensional nor of the same dimension, and these have no orthogonal matrix equivalent.

Orthogonal matrices are important for a number of reasons, both theoretical and practical. The $n \times n$ orthogonal matrices form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280), the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) denoted by $O(n)$, which—with its [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011)—is widely used in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and the [physical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_physical_science). For example, the [point group](wikipedia://en/1138942) of a [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) is a subgroup of O(3). Because [floating point](wikipedia://en/11376) versions of orthogonal matrices have advantageous properties, they are key to many algorithms in [numerical linear algebra](wikipedia://en/7330660), such as [$QR$ decomposition](wikipedia://en/305223). As another example, with appropriate normalization the [discrete cosine transform](wikipedia://en/59962) (used in [MP3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MP3) compression) is represented by an orthogonal matrix.

## Examples {#Examples}

Below are a few examples of small orthogonal matrices and possible interpretations.

- $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ ([identity](wikipedia://en/15069) transformation)
- $\begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \\ \end{bmatrix}$ ([rotation](wikipedia://en/856005) about [the origin](wikipedia://en/1313432))
- $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \\ \end{bmatrix}$ ([reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) across *x*-axis)
- $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ ([permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of [coordinate axes](wikipedia://en/81931))

## Elementary constructions {#Elementary_constructions}

### Lower dimensions {#Lower_dimensions}

The simplest orthogonal matrices are the 1 × 1 matrices \[1\] and \[−1\], which we can interpret as the [identity](wikipedia://en/15069) and a [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) of the [real line](wikipedia://en/287188) across [the origin](wikipedia://en/1313432).

The 2 × 2 matrices have the form

$$\begin{bmatrix} p & t\\ q & u \end{bmatrix},$$

which orthogonality demands satisfy the three [equations](wikipedia://en/9284)

$$\begin{aligned} 1 & = p^2+t^2, \\ 1 & = q^2+u^2, \\ 0 & = pq+tu. \end{aligned}$$

In consideration of the first equation, [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) let $p = \cos \theta$, $q = \sin \theta$; then either $t = -q$, $u = p$ or $t = q$, $u = -p$. We can interpret the first case as a [rotation](wikipedia://en/856005) by $\theta$ (where $\theta = 0$ is the identity), and the second as a reflection across a line at an angle of $\tfrac{\theta}{2}$.

$$\begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \cos \theta \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{ (rotation), }\qquad \begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta & \sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & -\cos \theta \\ \end{bmatrix}\text{ (reflection)}$$

The special case of the reflection matrix with $\theta = 90^{\circ}$ generates a reflection about the line at 45° given by $y = x$ and therefore exchanges $x$ and $y$; it is a [permutation matrix](wikipedia://en/238849), with a single 1 in each column and row (and otherwise 0):

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The identity is also a permutation matrix.

A reflection is [its own inverse](wikipedia://en/7917643), which implies that a reflection matrix is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) (equal to its [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844)) as well as orthogonal. The [product](wikipedia://en/125280) of two rotation matrices is a [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005), and the product of two reflection matrices is also a rotation matrix.

### Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

Regardless of the dimension, it is always possible to classify orthogonal matrices as purely rotational or not, but for 3 × 3 matrices and larger the non-rotational matrices can be more complicated than reflections. For example,

$$\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\text{ and } \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0\\ 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

represent an *[inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_point)* through the origin and a *[rotoinversion](wikipedia://en/244324)*, respectively, about the [$z$-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-axis).

Rotations become more complicated in higher dimensions; they can no longer be completely characterized by one [angle](wikipedia://en/1196), and may affect more than one planar [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28mathematics%29). It is common to describe a 3 × 3 rotation matrix in terms of an [axis and angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_and_angle), but this only works in [three dimensions](wikipedia://en/3054853). Above three dimensions two or more angles are needed, each associated with a [plane of rotation](wikipedia://en/25935424).

However, we have elementary building blocks for permutations, reflections, and rotations that apply in general.

### Primitives {#Primitives}

The most elementary permutation is a [transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28mathematics%29), obtained from the identity matrix by exchanging two rows. Any $n \times n$ permutation matrix can be constructed as a [product](wikipedia://en/125280) of no more than $n - 1$ transpositions.

A [Householder reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Householder_reflection) is constructed from a non-[null vector](wikipedia://en/1453977) $\mathbf{v}$ as

$$Q = I - 2 \frac{{\mathbf v}{\mathbf v}^\mathrm{T}}{{\mathbf v}^\mathrm{T}{\mathbf v}} .$$

Here the numerator is a [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) while the denominator is a number, the squared [magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnitude_%28vector%29) of $\mathbf{v}$. This is a reflection in the [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$ (negating any vector component [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to $\mathbf{v}$). If $\mathbf{v}$ is a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053), then $Q = I - 2\mathbf{vv}^{T}$ suffices. A Householder reflection is typically used to simultaneously zero the lower part of a column. Any orthogonal matrix of size *n* × *n* can be constructed as a product of at most $n$ such reflections.

A [Givens rotation](wikipedia://en/680237) acts on a [two-dimensional](wikipedia://en/70691232) (planar) subspace spanned by two coordinate axes, rotating by a chosen angle. It is typically used to zero a single [subdiagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subdiagonal) entry. Any rotation matrix of size $n \times n$ can be constructed as a product of at most $\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}$ such rotations. In the case of 3 × 3 matrices, three such rotations suffice; and by fixing the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) we can thus describe all 3 × 3 rotation matrices (though not [uniquely](wikipedia://en/276894)) in terms of the three angles used, often called [Euler angles](wikipedia://en/411492).

A [Jacobi rotation](wikipedia://en/5795881) has the same form as a Givens rotation, but is used to zero both off-diagonal entries of a 2 × 2 symmetric [submatrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submatrix).

## Properties {#Properties}

### Matrix properties {#Matrix_properties}

A real [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) is orthogonal [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its columns form an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) of the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with the ordinary Euclidean [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093), which is the case if and only if its rows form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. It might be tempting to suppose a matrix with orthogonal (not orthonormal) columns would be called an orthogonal matrix, but such matrices have no special interest and no special name; they only satisfy $M^{T}M = D$, with $D$ a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080).

The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of any orthogonal matrix is +1 or −1. This follows from basic facts about determinants, as follows:

$$1=\det(I)=\det\left(Q^\mathrm{T}Q\right)=\det\left(Q^\mathrm{T}\right)\det(Q)=\bigl(\det(Q)\bigr)^2 .$$

The [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) is not true; having a determinant of ±1 is no guarantee of orthogonality, even with orthogonal columns, as shown by the following counterexample.

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}$$

With permutation matrices the determinant matches the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_permutations), being +1 or −1 as the [parity](wikipedia://en/143135) of the permutation is even or odd, for the determinant is an alternating function of the rows.

Stronger than the determinant restriction is the fact that an orthogonal matrix can always be [diagonalized](wikipedia://en/246325) over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) to exhibit a full set of [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429), all of which must have ([complex](wikipedia://en/5826)) [modulus](wikipedia://en/991) 1.

### Group properties {#Group_properties}

The [inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of every orthogonal matrix is again orthogonal, as is the [matrix product](wikipedia://en/125280) of two orthogonal matrices. In fact, the set of all $n \times n$ orthogonal matrices satisfies all the [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). It is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of dimension $\tfrac{n(n - 1)}{2}$, called the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) and denoted by $O(n)$.

The orthogonal matrices whose [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) is +1 form a [path-connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $O(n)$ of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 2, the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ of [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789). The [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $O(n)/\operatorname{SO}(n)$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to $O(1)$, with the [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) map choosing \[+1\] or \[−1\] according to the determinant. Orthogonal matrices with determinant −1 do not include the [identity](wikipedia://en/59718), and so do not form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) but only a [coset](wikipedia://en/98759); it is also (separately) connected. Thus each orthogonal group falls into two pieces; and because the projection map [splits](wikipedia://en/1531404), $O(n)$ is a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ by $O(1)$. In practical terms, a comparable statement is that any orthogonal matrix can be produced by taking a rotation matrix and possibly [negating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposite_vector) one of its columns, as we saw with 2 × 2 matrices. If $n$ is odd, then the semidirect product is in fact a [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015), and any orthogonal matrix can be produced by taking a rotation matrix and possibly negating all of its columns. This follows from the property of determinants that negating a column [negates](wikipedia://en/228312) the determinant, and thus negating an odd (but not even) number of columns negates the determinant.

Now consider $(n + 1) \times (n + 1)$ orthogonal matrices with bottom right entry equal to 1. The remainder of the last column (and last row) must be zeros, and the product of any two such matrices has the same form. The rest of the matrix is an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix; thus $O(n)$ is a subgroup of $O(n + 1)$ (and of all higher groups).

$$\begin{bmatrix} & & & 0\\ & \mathrm{O}(n) & & \vdots\\ & & & 0\\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since an elementary reflection in the form of a [Householder matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Householder_matrix) can reduce any orthogonal matrix to this constrained form, a series of such reflections can bring any orthogonal matrix to the identity; thus an orthogonal group is a [reflection group](wikipedia://en/2422023). The last column can be fixed to any [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053), and each choice gives a different copy of $O(n)$ in $O(n + 1)$; in this way $O(n + 1)$ is a [bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) over the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) $S^{n}$ with [fiber](wikipedia://en/6603892) $O(n)$.

Similarly, $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{SO}(n + 1)$; and any special orthogonal matrix can be generated by [Givens plane rotations](wikipedia://en/680237) using an analogous procedure. The bundle structure persists: $\mathrm{SO}(n) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{SO}(n+1) \to S^n$. A single rotation can produce a zero in the first row of the last column, and series of $n - 1$ rotations will zero all but the last row of the last column of an $n \times n$ rotation matrix. Since the [planes](wikipedia://en/25935424) are fixed, each rotation has only one [degree of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622), its [angle](wikipedia://en/1196). By induction, $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ therefore has

$$(n-1) + (n-2) + \cdots + 1 = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$

degrees of freedom, and so does $O(n)$.

[Permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849) are simpler still; they form, not a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), but only a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707), the order [$n!$](wikipedia://en/10606) [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$. By the same kind of argument, $S_{n}$ is a subgroup of $S_{n + 1}$. The [even permutations](wikipedia://en/49727) produce the subgroup of permutation matrices of determinant +1, the order $\tfrac{n!}{2}$ [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726).

### Canonical form {#Canonical_form}

More broadly, the effect of any orthogonal matrix separates into independent actions on orthogonal [two-dimensional](wikipedia://en/70691232) subspaces. That is, if $Q$ is special orthogonal then one can always find an orthogonal matrix $P$, a (rotational) [change of basis](wikipedia://en/1179451), that brings $Q$ into [block diagonal](wikipedia://en/457464#Block_diagonal_matrices) form:

$$P^\mathrm{T}QP = \begin{bmatrix} R_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & R_k \end{bmatrix}\ (n\text{ even}), \ P^\mathrm{T}QP = \begin{bmatrix} R_1 & & & \\ & \ddots & & \\ & & R_k & \\ & & & 1 \end{bmatrix}\ (n\text{ odd}).$$

where the matrices $R_{1}, ..., R_{k}$ are 2 × 2 [rotation matrices](wikipedia://en/856005), and with the remaining entries zero. Exceptionally, a rotation block may be [diagonal](wikipedia://en/174080), $\pm I$. Thus, negating one column if necessary, and noting that a 2 × 2 reflection [diagonalizes](wikipedia://en/246325) to a +1 and −1, any orthogonal matrix can be brought to the form [^o15][^o16]

$$P^\mathrm{T}QP = \begin{bmatrix} \begin{matrix}R_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & R_k\end{matrix} & 0 \\ 0 & \begin{matrix}\pm 1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \pm 1\end{matrix} \\ \end{bmatrix},$$

The matrices $R_{1}, ..., R_{k}$ give [conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) pairs of [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) lying on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/5826); so this [decomposition](wikipedia://en/253873) confirms that all eigenvalues have [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) 1. If $n$ is odd, there is at least one [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) eigenvalue, +1 or −1; for a 3 × 3 rotation, the eigenvector associated with +1 is the rotation axis.

### Lie algebra {#Lie_algebra}

Suppose the entries of $Q$ are [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206) of $t$, and that $t = 0$ gives $Q = I$. [Differentiating](wikipedia://en/7921) the orthogonality condition

$$Q^\mathrm{T} Q = I$$

yields

$$\dot{Q}^\mathrm{T} Q + Q^\mathrm{T} \dot{Q} = 0$$

Evaluation at $t = 0$ ($Q = I$) then implies

$$\dot{Q}^\mathrm{T} = -\dot{Q} .$$

In [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) terms, this means that the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of an orthogonal matrix [group](wikipedia://en/19447) consists of [skew-symmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/174055). Going the other direction, the [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731) of any [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055) is an orthogonal matrix (in fact, special orthogonal).

For example, the [three-dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) object physics calls [angular velocity](wikipedia://en/65927) is a [differential rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_rotation_matrix), thus a vector in the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ [tangent](wikipedia://en/30807) to $\operatorname{SO}(3)$. Given $\mathbf{\omega} = (x\theta, y\theta, z\theta)$, with $\mathbf{v} = (x, y, z)$ being a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053), the correct skew-symmetric matrix form of $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is

$$\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -z\theta & y\theta \\ z\theta & 0 & -x\theta \\ -y\theta & x\theta & 0 \end{bmatrix} .$$

The exponential of this is the orthogonal [matrix for rotation](wikipedia://en/856005) around axis $\mathbf{v}$ by angle $\theta$; setting $c = \cos \tfrac{\theta}{2}$, $s = \sin \tfrac{\theta}{2}$,

$$\exp(\Omega) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 2s^2 + 2x^2 s^2 & 2xy s^2 - 2z sc & 2xz s^2 + 2y sc\\ 2xy s^2 + 2z sc & 1 - 2s^2 + 2y^2 s^2 & 2yz s^2 - 2x sc\\ 2xz s^2 - 2y sc & 2yz s^2 + 2x sc & 1 - 2s^2 + 2z^2 s^2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

## Numerical linear algebra {#Numerical_linear_algebra}

### Benefits {#Benefits}

[Numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) takes advantage of many of the properties of orthogonal matrices for [numerical linear algebra](wikipedia://en/7330660), and they arise naturally. For example, it is often desirable to compute an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) for a space, or an orthogonal [change of bases](wikipedia://en/1179451); both take the form of orthogonal matrices. Having [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) ±1 and all [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of [magnitude](wikipedia://en/990534) 1 is of great benefit for [numerical stability](wikipedia://en/233807). One implication is that the [condition number](wikipedia://en/6934) is 1 (which is the minimum), so [errors](wikipedia://en/461509) are not magnified when [multiplying](wikipedia://en/125280) with an orthogonal matrix. Many algorithms use orthogonal matrices like [Householder reflections](wikipedia://en/485424) and [Givens rotations](wikipedia://en/680237) for this reason. It is also helpful that, not only is an orthogonal matrix [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122), but its inverse is available essentially free, by exchanging indices.

[Permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) are essential to the success of many [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775), including the workhorse [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035) with [partial pivoting](wikipedia://en/9811503#Partial,_rook,_and_complete_pivoting) (where permutations do the pivoting). However, they rarely appear explicitly as matrices; their special form allows more efficient [representation](wikipedia://en/6355948), such as a list of $n$ indices.

Likewise, algorithms using Householder and Givens matrices typically use specialized methods of multiplication and storage. For example, a Givens rotation affects only two rows of a matrix it multiplies, changing a full [multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) of order $n^{3}$ to a much more efficient order $n$. When uses of these reflections and rotations introduce zeros in a matrix, the space vacated is enough to store sufficient data to reproduce the transform, and to do so [robustly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robustness). (Following G. W. Stewart of [UMD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Maryland,_College_Park),[^o20] we do *not* store a [rotation angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_displacement), which is both expensive and badly behaved.)

### Decompositions {#Decompositions}

A number of important [matrix decompositions](wikipedia://en/253873) involve orthogonal matrices, including especially:

**[$QR$ decomposition](wikipedia://en/305223)**

$M = QR$, $Q$ orthogonal, $R$ upper [triangular](wikipedia://en/374222)[^o22]

**[Singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207)**

$M = U\Sigma V^{T}$, $U$ and $V$ orthogonal, $\Sigma$ [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080)[^o23]

**[Eigendecomposition of a symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/13576645) (decomposition according to the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495))**

$S = Q\Lambda Q^{T}$, $S$ [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474), $Q$ orthogonal, $\Lambda$ diagonal

**[Polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241)**

$M = QS$, $Q$ orthogonal, $S$ symmetric [positive-semidefinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrices)[^o24]

#### Examples {#Examples}

Consider an [overdetermined system](wikipedia://en/10160606) of [linear equations](wikipedia://en/17570), as might occur with repeated [measurements](wikipedia://en/19022) of a physical [phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) to compensate for [experimental errors](wikipedia://en/22346). Write $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, where $A$ is $m \times n$, $m > n$. A $QR$ decomposition reduces $A$ to upper triangular $R$. For example, if $A$ is 5 × 3 then $R$ has the form

$$R = \begin{bmatrix} \cdot & \cdot & \cdot \\ 0 & \cdot & \cdot \\ 0 & 0 & \cdot \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The [linear least squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_least_squares_%28mathematics%29) problem is to find the $\mathbf{x}$ that [minimizes](wikipedia://en/298420) $\lVert A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\rVert$, which is equivalent to [projecting](wikipedia://en/519182) $\mathbf{b}$ to the subspace [spanned](wikipedia://en/56353) by the columns of $A$. Assuming the columns of $A$ (and hence $R$) are [independent](wikipedia://en/101863), the projection solution is found from $A^{T}A\mathbf{x} = A^{T}\mathbf{b}$. Now $A^{T}A$ is [square](wikipedia://en/166022) ($n \times n$) and [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122), and also equal to $R^{T}R$. But the lower rows of zeros in $R$ are superfluous in the product, which is thus already in lower-triangular upper-triangular [factored](wikipedia://en/82341) form, as in [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035) ([Cholesky decomposition](wikipedia://en/134433)). Here orthogonality is important not only for [reducing](wikipedia://en/1033865) $A^{T}A = (R^{T}Q^{T})QR$ to $R^{T}R$, but also for allowing solution without magnifying [numerical problems](wikipedia://en/12076692).

In the case of a [linear system](wikipedia://en/113087) which is [underdetermined](wikipedia://en/15890311), or an otherwise non-[invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122), singular value decomposition (SVD) is equally useful. With $A$ factored as $U\Sigma V^{T}$, a satisfactory solution uses the [Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse](wikipedia://en/331258), $V\Sigma^{+}U^{T}$, where $\Sigma^{+}$ merely replaces each non-zero diagonal entry with its [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940). Set $\mathbf{x}$ to $V\Sigma^{+}U^{T}\mathbf{b}$.

The case of a square invertible matrix also holds interest. Suppose, for example, that $A$ is a 3 × 3 [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005) which has been computed as the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of numerous twists and turns. [Floating point](wikipedia://en/11376) does not match the mathematical ideal of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), so $A$ has gradually lost its true orthogonality. A [Gram–Schmidt process](wikipedia://en/82361) could [orthogonalize](wikipedia://en/201611) the columns, but it is not the most reliable, nor the most efficient, nor the most invariant method. The [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) factors a matrix into a pair, one of which is the unique *closest* orthogonal matrix to the given matrix, or one of the closest if the given matrix is singular. (Closeness can be measured by any [matrix norm](wikipedia://en/1543735) invariant under an orthogonal change of basis, such as the [spectral norm](wikipedia://en/1543735#Spectral_norm_%28p_=_2%29) or the [Frobenius norm](wikipedia://en/1543735#Frobenius_norm).) For a near-orthogonal matrix, rapid convergence to the orthogonal factor can be achieved by a "[Newton's method](wikipedia://en/22145)" approach due to [Nicholas Higham](wikipedia://en/10011004) of [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester)[^o27][^o28], repeatedly [averaging](wikipedia://en/60167) the matrix with its inverse transpose. Augustin A. Dubrulle of [ORNL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Ridge_National_Laboratory) has published an accelerated method with a convenient [convergence test](wikipedia://en/4576951).[^o29]

For example, consider a non-orthogonal matrix for which the simple averaging [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) takes seven steps

$$\begin{bmatrix}3 & 1\\7 & 5\end{bmatrix} \rightarrow \begin{bmatrix}1.8125 & 0.0625\\3.4375 & 2.6875\end{bmatrix} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \begin{bmatrix}0.8 & -0.6\\0.6 & 0.8\end{bmatrix}$$

and which acceleration trims to two steps (with $\gamma$ = 0.353553, 0.565685).

$$\begin{bmatrix}3 & 1\\7 & 5\end{bmatrix} \rightarrow \begin{bmatrix}1.41421 & -1.06066\\1.06066 & 1.41421\end{bmatrix} \rightarrow \begin{bmatrix}0.8 & -0.6\\0.6 & 0.8\end{bmatrix}$$

Gram-Schmidt yields an inferior solution, shown by a Frobenius distance of 8.28659 instead of the minimum 8.12404.

$$\begin{bmatrix}3 & 1\\7 & 5\end{bmatrix} \rightarrow \begin{bmatrix}0.393919 & -0.919145\\0.919145 & 0.393919\end{bmatrix}$$

:::

### Randomization {#Randomization}

Some numerical applications, such as [Monte Carlo methods](wikipedia://en/56098) and exploration of [high-dimensional data](wikipedia://en/19330658) spaces, require generation of [uniformly distributed](wikipedia://en/1699223) random orthogonal matrices. In this context, "uniform" is defined in terms of [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645), which essentially requires that the distribution not change if multiplied by any freely chosen orthogonal matrix. Orthogonalizing matrices with [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) uniformly distributed random entries does not result in uniformly distributed orthogonal matrices, but the [$QR$ decomposition](wikipedia://en/305223) of independent [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) random entries does, as long as the diagonal of $R$ contains only positive entries.[^o31] G. W. Stewart of [UMD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Maryland,_College_Park)[^o32] replaced this with a more efficient idea that [Persi Diaconis](wikipedia://en/65467) of [Stanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) and Mehrdad Shahshahani of [JPL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory) later generalized as the "[subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) algorithm" (in which form it works just as well for [permutations](wikipedia://en/238849) and [rotations](wikipedia://en/856005)). To generate an $(n + 1) \times (n + 1)$ orthogonal matrix, take an $n \times n$ one and a uniformly distributed [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) of dimension *n* + 1. Construct a [Householder reflection](wikipedia://en/485424) from the vector, then apply it to the smaller matrix (embedded in the larger size with a 1 at the bottom right corner).[^o33]

### Nearest orthogonal matrix {#Nearest_orthogonal_matrix}

The problem of finding the orthogonal matrix $Q$ nearest a given matrix $M$ is related to the [Orthogonal Procrustes problem](wikipedia://en/13548016). There are several different ways to get the [unique](wikipedia://en/276894) solution, the simplest of which is taking the [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207) of $M$ and replacing the [singular values](wikipedia://en/709116) with ones. Another method expresses the $R$ [explicitly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_knowledge) but requires the use of a [matrix square root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_square_root):[^o35]

$$Q = M \left(M^\mathrm{T} M\right)^{-\frac 1 2}$$

This may be combined with the [Babylonian method](wikipedia://en/2698660#Heron's_method) for extracting the square root of a matrix to give a [recurrence](wikipedia://en/146806) which converges to an orthogonal matrix [quadratically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratically_convergent):

$$Q_{n + 1} = 2 M \left(Q_n^{-1} M + M^\mathrm{T} Q_n\right)^{-1}$$

where $Q_{0} = M$.

These [iterations](wikipedia://en/68833) are [stable](wikipedia://en/233807) provided the [condition number](wikipedia://en/6934) of $M$ is less than three.[^o36]

Using a [first-order approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_approximation) of the inverse and the same [initialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initialization_%28programming%29) results in the modified iteration:

$$N_{n} = Q_n^\mathrm{T} Q_n$$

$$P_{n} = \frac 1 2 Q_n N_{n}$$

$$Q_{n + 1} = 2 Q_n + P_n N_n - 3 P_n$$

## Spin and pin {#Spin_and_pin}

A subtle technical problem afflicts some uses of orthogonal matrices. Not only are the group components with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) +1 and −1 not [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) to each other, even the +1 component, $\operatorname{SO}(n)$, is not [simply connected](wikipedia://en/523879) (except for SO(1), which is trivial). Thus it is sometimes advantageous, or even necessary, to work with a [covering group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_map) of SO(*n*), the [spin group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor_group), $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$. Likewise, $O(n)$ has covering groups, the [pin groups](wikipedia://en/1794902), Pin(*n*). For $n > 2$, $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ is simply connected and thus the universal covering group for $\operatorname{SO}(n)$. By far the most famous example of a spin group is $\operatorname{Spin}(3)$, which is nothing but $\operatorname{SU}(2)$, or the group of unit [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440).

The Pin and Spin groups are found within [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305), which themselves can be built from orthogonal matrices.

## Rectangular matrices {#Rectangular_matrices}

If $Q$ is not a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022), then the conditions $Q^{T}Q = I$ and $QQ^{T} = I$ are not equivalent. The condition $Q^{T}Q = I$ says that the columns of $Q$ are [orthonormal](wikipedia://en/194743). This can only happen if $Q$ is an $m \times n$ matrix with $n \le m$ (due to [linear dependence](wikipedia://en/101863)). Similarly, $QQ^{T} = I$ says that the rows of $Q$ are orthonormal, which requires $n \ge m$.

There is no standard [terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminology) for these matrices. They are variously called "[semi-orthogonal matrices](wikipedia://en/34417095)", "orthonormal matrices", "orthogonal matrices", and sometimes simply "matrices with orthonormal rows/columns".

For the case $n \le m$, matrices with orthonormal columns may be referred to as orthogonal [k-frames](wikipedia://en/22256304) and they are elements of the [Stiefel manifold](wikipedia://en/1392495).

A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times3}$ with orthonormal rows represents a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $f\colon\, \mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^2,$ which describes an [orthographic projection](wikipedia://en/203052). Two specific examples can be found in the article [Axonometry](wikipedia://en/53085145) (Examples 3 and 4 in the section [Coordinate calculation](wikipedia://en/53085145#Coordinate_calculation)).
