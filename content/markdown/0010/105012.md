# Solvable group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Solvable group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in the field of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a **solvable group** or **soluble group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) that can be constructed from [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) using [extensions](wikipedia://en/345807). Equivalently, a solvable group is a group whose [derived series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_series) terminates in the [trivial subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_subgroup).

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Historically, the word "solvable" arose from [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) and the proof of the general unsolvability of [quintic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintic) equations. Specifically, a [polynomial equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation) is solvable in [radicals](wikipedia://en/235029) if and only if the corresponding [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) is solvable[^o3] (note this theorem holds only in [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_of_a_field) 0). This means associated to a polynomial $f \in F[x]$ there is a tower of field extensions

> $$F = F_0 \subseteq F_1 \subseteq F_2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq F_m=K$$

such that

1. $F_i = F_{i-1}[\alpha_i]$ where $\alpha_i^{m_i} \in F_{i-1}$, so $\alpha_i$ is a solution to the equation $x^{m_i} - a$ where $a \in F_{i-1}$
1. $F_m$ contains a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) for $f(x)$

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

The smallest Galois field extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing the element

> $$a = \sqrt[5]{\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}}$$

gives a solvable group. The associated field extensions

> $$\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})\left(e^{2i\pi/ 5}\right) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})\left(e^{2i\pi/ 5}, a\right)$$

give a solvable group of Galois extensions containing the following [composition factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_factor) (where $1$ is the identity permutation).

- $\mathrm{Aut}\left(\mathbb{Q(\sqrt{2})}\right/\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ with group action $f\left(\pm\sqrt{2}\right) = \mp\sqrt{2}, \ f^2 = 1$, and [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) $x^2 - 2$
- $\mathrm{Aut}\left(\mathbb{Q(\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3})}\right/\mathbb{Q(\sqrt{2})}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ with group action $g\left(\pm\sqrt{3}\right) = \mp\sqrt{3} ,\ g^2 = 1$, and minimal polynomial $x^2 - 3$
- $\mathrm{Aut}\left( \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})\left(e^{2i\pi/ 5}\right)/ \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}) \right) \cong \mathbb{Z}/4$ with group action $h^n\left(e^{2im\pi/5}\right) = e^{2^{n}im\pi/5} , \ 0 \leq n \leq 3, \ h^4 = 1$, and minimal polynomial $x^4 + x^3+x^2+x+1 = (x^5 - 1)/(x-1)$ containing the 5th roots of unity excluding $1$
- $\mathrm{Aut}\left( \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})\left(e^{2i\pi/ 5}, a\right)/ \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3})\left(e^{2i\pi/ 5}\right) \right) \cong \mathbb{Z}/5$ with group action $j^l(a) = e^{2li\pi/5}a, \ j^5 = 1$, and minimal polynomial $x^5 - \left(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\right)$

Each of the defining group actions (for example, $fgh^3j^4$) changes a single extension while keeping all of the other extensions fixed. The 80 group actions are the set $\{f^ag^bh^nj^l,\ 0 \leq a, b \leq 1,\ 0 \leq n \leq 3,\ 0 \leq l \leq 4 \}$.

This group is not [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974). For example, $hj(a) = h(e^{2i\pi/5}a) = e^{4i\pi/5}a$, whilst $jh(a) = j(a) = e^{2i\pi/5}a$, and in fact, $jh = hj^3$.

It is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{Z}_5 \rtimes_\varphi \mathbb{Z}_4) \times (\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2)$, where $\varphi_h(j) = hjh^{-1} = j^2$, defined using the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) and [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of the [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327). $\mathbb{Z}_4$ is not a normal subgroup.

:::

## Definition {#Definition}

A group *G* is called **solvable** if it has a [subnormal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subnormal_series) whose [factor groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_group) (quotient groups) are all [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974), that is, if there are [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011)

$$1 = G_0\triangleleft G_1 \triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft G_k=G$$

meaning that *G*<sub>*j*−1</sub> is [normal](wikipedia://en/21918) in *G<sub>j</sub>*, such that *G<sub>j</sub>*/*G*<sub>*j*−1</sub> is an abelian group, for *j* = 1, 2, ..., *k*.

Or equivalently, if its [derived series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_series), the descending normal series

$$G\triangleright G^{(1)}\triangleright G^{(2)} \triangleright \cdots,$$

where every subgroup is the [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) of the previous one, eventually reaches the trivial subgroup of *G*. These two definitions are equivalent, since for every group *H* and every [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) *N* of *H*, the quotient *H*/*N* is abelian [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *N* includes the commutator subgroup of *H*. The least *n* such that *G*<sup>(*n*)</sup> = 1 is called the **derived length** of the solvable group *G*.

For finite groups, an equivalent definition is that a solvable group is a group with a [composition series](wikipedia://en/297493) all of whose factors are [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) [order](wikipedia://en/493527). This is equivalent because a finite group has finite composition length, and every [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) abelian group is cyclic of prime order. The [Jordan–Hölder theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Hölder_theorem) guarantees that if one composition series has this property, then all composition series will have this property as well. For the Galois group of a polynomial, these cyclic groups correspond to *n*th roots (radicals) over some [field](wikipedia://en/10603). The equivalence does not necessarily hold for infinite groups: for example, since every nontrivial subgroup of the group **Z** of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) under addition is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to **Z** itself, it has no composition series, but the normal series {0, **Z**}, with its only factor group isomorphic to **Z**, proves that it is in fact solvable.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Abelian groups {#Abelian_groups}

The basic example of solvable groups are abelian groups. They are trivially solvable since a subnormal series is formed by just the group itself and the trivial group. But non-abelian groups may or may not be solvable.

### Nilpotent groups {#Nilpotent_groups}

More generally, all [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569) are solvable. In particular, finite [*p*-groups](wikipedia://en/24664) are solvable, as all finite [*p*-groups](wikipedia://en/24664) are nilpotent.

#### Quaternion groups {#Quaternion_groups}

In particular, the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) is a solvable group given by the group extension

> $$1 \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to Q \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 1$$

where the kernel $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is the subgroup generated by $-1$.

### Group extensions {#Group_extensions}

[Group extensions](wikipedia://en/345807) form the prototypical examples of solvable groups. That is, if $G$ and $G'$ are solvable groups, then any extension

> $$1 \to G \to G'' \to G' \to 1$$

defines a solvable group $G''$. In fact, all solvable groups can be formed from such group extensions.

### Non-abelian group which is non-nilpotent {#Non-abelian_group_which_is_non-nilpotent}

A small example of a solvable, non-nilpotent group is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) *S*<sub>3</sub>. In fact, as the smallest simple non-abelian group is *A*<sub>5</sub>, (the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) of degree 5) it follows that *every* group with order less than 60 is solvable.

### Finite groups of odd order {#Finite_groups_of_odd_order}

The [Feit–Thompson theorem](wikipedia://en/1656473) states that every finite group of odd order is solvable. In particular this implies that if a finite group is simple, it is either a prime cyclic or of even order.

::: {#o17 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-example"}

### Non-example {#Non-example}

The group *S*<sub>5</sub> is not solvable—it has a composition series {E, *A*<sub>5</sub>, *S*<sub>5</sub>} (and the [Jordan–Hölder theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Hölder_theorem) states that every other composition series is equivalent to that one), giving factor groups isomorphic to *A*<sub>5</sub> and *C*<sub>2</sub>; and *A*<sub>5</sub> is not abelian. Generalizing this argument, coupled with the fact that *A*<sub>*n*</sub> is a normal, maximal, non-abelian simple subgroup of *S*<sub>*n*</sub> for *n* > 4, we see that *S*<sub>*n*</sub> is not solvable for *n* > 4. This is a key step in the proof that for every *n* > 4 there are [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) of degree *n* which are not solvable by radicals ([Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518)). This property is also used in complexity theory in the proof of [Barrington's theorem](wikipedia://en/22073).

:::

### Subgroups of GL<sub>2</sub> {#Subgroups_of_GL2}

Consider the subgroups

> $B = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} * & * \\ 0 & * \end{bmatrix} \right\} \text{, } U = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & * \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$ of $GL_2(\mathbb{F})$

for some field $\mathbb{F}$. Then, the group quotient $B/U$ can be found by taking arbitrary elements in $B,U$, multiplying them together, and figuring out what structure this gives. So

> $$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & c \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 & d \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a & ad + b \\ 0 & c \end{bmatrix}$$

Note the determinant condition on $GL_2$ implies $ac \neq 0$, hence $\mathbb{F}^\times \times \mathbb{F}^\times \subset B$ is a subgroup (which are the matrices where $b=0$). For fixed $a,b$, the linear equation $ad + b = 0$ implies $d = -b/a$, which is an arbitrary element in $\mathbb{F}$ since $b \in \mathbb{F}$. Since we can take any matrix in $B$ and multiply it by the matrix

> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & d \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

with $d = -b/a$, we can get a diagonal matrix in $B$. This shows the quotient group $B/U \cong \mathbb{F}^\times \times \mathbb{F}^\times$.

#### Remark {#Remark}

Notice that this description gives the decomposition of $B$ as $\mathbb{F} \rtimes (\mathbb{F}^\times \times \mathbb{F}^\times)$ where $(a,c)$ acts on $b$ by $(a,c)(b) = ab$. This implies $(a,c)(b + b') = (a,c)(b) + (a,c)(b') = ab + ab'$. Also, a matrix of the form

> $$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & c \end{bmatrix}$$

corresponds to the element $(b) \times (a,c)$ in the group.

### Borel subgroups {#Borel_subgroups}

For a [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885) $G$, a [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) is defined as a subgroup which is closed, connected, and solvable in $G$, and is a maximal possible subgroup with these properties (note the first two are topological properties). For example, in $GL_n$ and $SL_n$ the groups of upper-triangular, or lower-triangular matrices are two of the Borel subgroups. The example given above, the subgroup $B$ in $GL_2$, is a Borel subgroup.

#### Borel subgroup in GL<sub>3</sub> {#Borel_subgroup_in_GL3}

In $GL_3$ there are the subgroups

> $$B = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \end{bmatrix} \right\}, \text{ } U_1 = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & * & * \\ 0 & 1 & * \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$

Notice $B/U_1 \cong \mathbb{F}^\times \times \mathbb{F}^\times \times \mathbb{F}^\times$, hence the Borel group has the form

> $$U\rtimes (\mathbb{F}^\times \times \mathbb{F}^\times \times \mathbb{F}^\times)$$

#### Borel subgroup in product of simple linear algebraic groups {#Borel_subgroup_in_product_of_simple_linear_algebraic_groups}

In the product group $GL_n \times GL_m$ the Borel subgroup can be represented by matrices of the form

> $$\begin{bmatrix} T & 0 \\ 0 & S \end{bmatrix}$$

where $T$ is an $n\times n$ upper triangular matrix and $S$ is a $m\times m$ upper triangular matrix.

### Z-groups {#Z-groups}

Any finite group whose [*p*-Sylow subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_group) are cyclic is a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of two cyclic groups, in particular solvable. Such groups are called [Z-groups](wikipedia://en/15757039).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Solvability is closed under a number of operations.

- If *G* is solvable, and *H* is a subgroup of *G*, then *H* is solvable.[^o27]
- If *G* is solvable, and there is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from *G* [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) *H*, then *H* is solvable; equivalently (by the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorem#First_isomorphism_theorem)), if *G* is solvable, and *N* is a normal subgroup of *G*, then *G*/*N* is solvable.[^o28]
- The previous properties can be expanded into the following "three for the price of two" property: *G* is solvable if and only if both *N* and *G*/*N* are solvable.
- In particular, if *G* and *H* are solvable, the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) *G* × *H* is solvable.

Solvability is closed under [group extension](wikipedia://en/345807):

- If *H* and *G*/*H* are solvable, then so is *G*; in particular, if *N* and *H* are solvable, their [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) is also solvable.

It is also closed under wreath product:

- If *G* and *H* are solvable, and *X* is a *G*-set, then the [wreath product](wikipedia://en/146625) of *G* and *H* with respect to *X* is also solvable.

For any positive integer *N*, the solvable groups of [derived length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_length) at most *N* form a [subvariety](wikipedia://en/248808) of the variety of groups, as they are closed under the taking of [homomorphic](wikipedia://en/13564) images, [subalgebras](wikipedia://en/45239), and [(direct) products](wikipedia://en/3706015). The direct product of a sequence of solvable groups with unbounded derived length is not solvable, so the class of all solvable groups is not a variety.

:::

## Burnside's theorem {#Burnside's_theorem}

Burnside's theorem states that if *G* is a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) *p<sup>a</sup>q<sup>b</sup>* where *p* and *q* are [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), and *a* and *b* are [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_and_positive_numbers) [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), then *G* is solvable.

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

### Supersolvable groups {#Supersolvable_groups}

As a strengthening of solvability, a group *G* is called **supersolvable** (or **supersoluble**) if it has an *invariant* normal series whose factors are all cyclic. Since a normal series has finite length by definition, [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) groups are not supersolvable. In fact, all supersolvable groups are [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042), and an abelian group is supersolvable if and only if it is finitely generated. The alternating group *A*<sub>4</sub> is an example of a finite solvable group that is not supersolvable.

If we restrict ourselves to finitely generated groups, we can consider the following arrangement of classes of groups:

[cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) \< [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) \< [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) \< [supersolvable](wikipedia://en/4966866) \< [polycyclic](wikipedia://en/4000598) \< **solvable** \< [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042).

::: {#o34 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Virtually soluble"}

### Virtually solvable groups {#Virtually_solvable_groups}

A group *G* is called **virtually solvable** if it has a solvable subgroup of finite index. This is similar to [virtually abelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtually_abelian). Clearly all solvable groups are virtually solvable, since one can just choose the group itself, which has index 1. Virtually solvable groups are one of the two alternatives in the [Tits alternative](wikipedia://en/12704641) on finitely generated [linear groups](wikipedia://en/3983656).

:::

### Hypoabelian {#Hypoabelian}

A solvable group is one whose derived series reaches the trivial subgroup at a *finite* stage. For an infinite group, the finite derived series may not stabilize, but the transfinite derived series always stabilizes. A group whose transfinite derived series reaches the trivial group is called a **[hypoabelian group](wikipedia://en/4939190)**, and every solvable group is a hypoabelian group. The first ordinal *α* such that *G*<sup>(*α*)</sup> = *G*<sup>(*α*+1)</sup> is called the (transfinite) derived length of the group *G*, and it has been shown that every ordinal is the derived length of some group (Malcev 1949).

### p-solvable {#p-solvable}

A finite group is p-solvable for some prime p if every factor in the composition series is a [p-group](wikipedia://en/24664) or has order prime to p. A finite group is solvable iff it is p-solvable for every p. [^o38]
