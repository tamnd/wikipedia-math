# Degenerate distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Degenerate distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), a **degenerate distribution** on a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $(E, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ is a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) whose [support](wikipedia://en/6591796) is a [null set](wikipedia://en/21520) with respect to $\mu$. For instance, in the $n$-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ endowed with the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), any distribution concentrated on a $d$-dimensional subspace with $d < n$ is a degenerate distribution on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.[^o2] This is essentially the same notion as a [singular probability measure](wikipedia://en/4304500), but the term *degenerate* is typically used when the distribution arises as a [limit](wikipedia://en/50723) of (non-degenerate) distributions.

:::

When the support of a degenerate distribution consists of a single point $a$, this distribution is a **[Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) in $a$**: it is the distribution of a deterministic random variable equal to $a$ with probability 1. This is a special case of a [discrete distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_distribution); its [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) equals 1 in $a$ and 0 everywhere else.

In the case of a real-valued random variable, the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of the degenerate distribution localized in $a$ is

$$F_{a}(x)=\left\{\begin{matrix} 1, & \mbox{if }x\ge a \\ 0, & \mbox{if }x<a \end{matrix}\right.$$

Such degenerate distributions often arise as limits of [continuous distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_distribution) whose [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) goes to 0.

## Constant random variable {#Constant_random_variable}

A **constant random variable** is a [discrete random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_random_variable) that takes a [constant](wikipedia://en/320861) value, regardless of any [event](wikipedia://en/22961) that occurs. This is technically different from an **[almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) constant random variable**, which may take other values, but only on events with probability zero: Let $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued random variable defined on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathbb{P})$. Then $X$ is an *almost surely constant random variable* if there exists $a \in \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$\mathbb{P}(X = a) = 1,$$

and is furthermore a *constant random variable* if

$$X(\omega) = a, \quad \forall\omega \in \Omega.$$

A constant random variable is almost surely constant, but the converse is not true, since if $X$ is almost surely constant then there may still exist $\omega \in \Omega$ such that $X(\omega) \ne a$. For practical purposes, the distinction between $X$ being constant or almost surely constant is unimportant, since these two situation correspond to the same degenerate distribution: the Dirac measure.

Almost surely constant random variables are independent of everything — that is, if $X$ is almost surely constant, then for every event $A$ and every measurable set $B \subset \mathbb{R}$,

$$\mathbb{P}(\{X \in B\} \cap A) = \mathbb{P}(X \in B)\times\mathbb{P}(A).$$

In particular, an almost surely constant random variable is independent of itself. Moreover, this [self-independence](wikipedia://en/27593#Self-independence) characterizes almost surely constant random variables: if a random variable is independent of itself, then it is almost surely constant.

:::

## Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

Degeneracy of a [multivariate distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_distribution) in *n* random variables arises when the support lies in a space of dimension less than *n*.[^o2] This occurs when at least one of the variables is a deterministic function of the others. For example, in the 2-variable case suppose that *Y* = *aX + b* for scalar random variables *X* and *Y* and scalar constants *a* ≠ 0 and *b*; here knowing the value of one of *X* or *Y* gives exact knowledge of the value of the other. All the possible points (*x*, *y*) fall on the one-dimensional line *y = ax + b*.

In general when one or more of *n* random variables are exactly linearly determined by the others, if the [covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/191752) exists its rank is less than *n[^o2]* and its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) is 0, so it is [positive semi-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrix) but not positive definite, and the [joint probability distribution](wikipedia://en/879637) is degenerate.

Degeneracy can also occur even with non-zero covariance. For example, when scalar *X* is [symmetrically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_distribution) about 0 and *Y* is exactly given by *Y* = *X*<sup>2</sup>, all possible points (*x*, *y*) fall on the parabola *y = x*<sup>2</sup>, which is a one-dimensional subset of the two-dimensional space.
