# Vladimir Voevodsky

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vladimir Voevodsky"}

**Vladimir Alexandrovich Voevodsky** (, Влади́мир Алекса́ндрович Воево́дский; 4 June 1966 – 30 September 2017) was a [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia)-[American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). His work in developing a [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) for [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties) and formulating [motivic cohomology](wikipedia://en/809314) led to the award of a [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) in 2002. He is also known for the proof of the [Milnor conjecture](wikipedia://en/2914126) and motivic [Bloch–Kato conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch–Kato_conjecture) and for the [univalent foundations](wikipedia://en/44554214) of mathematics and [homotopy type theory](wikipedia://en/33974223).

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Vladimir Voevodsky's father, Aleksander Voevodsky, was head of the Laboratory of High Energy Leptons in the Institute for Nuclear Research at the [Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences). His mother Tatyana was a chemist.[^o12] Voevodsky attended [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University) for a while, but was expelled without a diploma for refusing to attend classes and failing academically.[^o12] He received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in mathematics from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) in 1992 after being recommended without even applying and without a formal college degree, following several independent publications;[^o12] he was advised there by [David Kazhdan](wikipedia://en/3052833).

While he was a first year undergraduate, he was given a copy of "[Esquisse d'un Programme](wikipedia://en/19575137)" (submitted a few months earlier by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) to [CNRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNRS)) by his advisor George Shabat. He learned the [French language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) "with the sole purpose of being able to read this text" and started his research on some of the themes mentioned there.[^o4]

## Work {#Work}

section Voevodsky's work was in the intersection of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) with [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801). Along with [Fabien Morel](wikipedia://en/28977644), Voevodsky introduced a [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) for [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754). He also formulated what is now believed to be the correct form of [motivic cohomology](wikipedia://en/809314), and used this new tool to prove [Milnor's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor's_conjecture) relating the [Milnor K-theory](wikipedia://en/2914128) of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) to its [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231).[^o6] For the above, he received the [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) at the 24th [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) held in [Beijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing), [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China).[^o7]

In 1998 he gave a plenary lecture (*A<sup>1</sup>-Homotopy Theory*) at the International Congress of Mathematicians in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin).[^o8] He coauthored (with [Andrei Suslin](wikipedia://en/7111820) and [Eric M. Friedlander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_M._Friedlander)) *Cycles, Transfers and Motivic Homology Theories*, which develops the theory of motivic cohomology in some detail.

From 2002, Voevodsky was a professor at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey).

In January 2009, at an anniversary conference in honor of [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042), held at the [Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_des_Hautes_Études_Scientifiques), Voevodsky announced a proof of the full [Bloch–Kato conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch–Kato_conjecture).

In 2009, he constructed the univalent model of [Martin-Löf type theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin-Löf_type_theory) in [simplicial sets](wikipedia://en/1027784). This led to important advances in type theory and in the development of new [univalent foundations](wikipedia://en/44554214) of mathematics that Voevodsky worked on in his final years. He worked on a [Rocq](wikipedia://en/581974) (then: *Coq*) library *UniMath* using univalent ideas.[^o12][^o9]

In April 2016, the [University of Gothenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Gothenburg) awarded an honorary doctorate to Voevodsky.[^o10]

## Death and legacy {#Death_and_legacy}

Voevodsky died on 30 September 2017 at his home in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), aged 51, from an [aneurysm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aneurysm).[^o12][^o13] He was survived by his daughters, Diana Yasmine Voevodsky and Natalia Dalia Shalaby.[^o12]
