# Distributive property

Distributivity

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Distributive property"}

**Distributive property.**

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Distributive property"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **distributive property** of [binary operations](wikipedia://en/3948) is a generalization of the **distributive law**, which asserts that the equality

$$x \cdot (y + z) = x \cdot y + x \cdot z$$

is always true in [elementary algebra](wikipedia://en/9710). For example, in [elementary arithmetic](wikipedia://en/1567386), one has

$$2 \cdot (1 + 3) = (2 \cdot 1) + (2 \cdot 3).$$

Therefore, one would say that [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) *distributes* over [addition](wikipedia://en/61338).

:::

This basic property of numbers is part of the definition of most [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) that have two operations called addition and multiplication, such as [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), and [fields](wikipedia://en/10603). It is also encountered in [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844) and [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), where each of the [logical and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_and) (denoted $\,\land\,$) and the [logical or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_or) (denoted $\,\lor\,$) distributes over the other.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ and two [binary operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_operator) $\,*\,$ and $\,+\,$ on $S,$

- the operation $\,*\,$ is left-distributive over (or with respect to) $\,+\,$ if, [given any](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Given_any) elements $x, y, \text{ and } z$ of $S,$

$$x * (y + z) = (x * y) + (x * z);$$

- the operation $\,*\,$ is right-distributive over $\,+\,$ if, given any elements $x, y, \text{ and } z$ of $S,$

$$(y + z) * x = (y * x) + (z * x);$$

- and the operation $\,*\,$ is distributive over $\,+\,$ if it is left- and right-distributive.[^o5]

When $\,*\,$ is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), the three conditions above are [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324).

:::

## Meaning {#Meaning}

The operators used for examples in this section are those of the usual [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) $\,+\,$ and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) $\,\cdot.\,$

If the operation denoted $\cdot$ is not commutative, there is a distinction between left-distributivity and right-distributivity:

$$a \cdot \left( b \pm c \right) = a \cdot b \pm a \cdot c \qquad \text{ (left-distributive) }$$

$$(a \pm b) \cdot c = a \cdot c \pm b \cdot c \qquad \text{ (right-distributive) }.$$

In either case, the distributive property can be described in words as:

To multiply a [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) (or [difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_%28mathematics%29)) by a factor, each summand (or [minuend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minuend) and [subtrahend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtrahend)) is multiplied by this factor and the resulting products are added (or subtracted).

If the operation outside the parentheses (in this case, the multiplication) is commutative, then left-distributivity implies right-distributivity and vice versa, and one talks simply of distributivity.

One example of an operation that is "only" right-distributive is division, which is not commutative:

$$(a \pm b) \div c = a \div c \pm b \div c.$$

In this case, left-distributivity does not apply:

$$a \div(b \pm c) \neq a \div b \pm a \div c$$

The distributive laws are among the axioms for [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) (like the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563)) and [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) (like the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024)). Here multiplication is distributive over addition, but addition is not distributive over multiplication. Examples of structures with two operations that are each distributive over the other are [Boolean algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebras) such as the [algebra of sets](wikipedia://en/961805) or the [switching algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switching_algebra).

Multiplying sums can be put into words as follows: When a sum is multiplied by a sum, multiply each summand of a sum with each summand of the other sum (keeping track of signs) then add up all of the resulting products.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Real numbers {#Real_numbers}

In the following examples, the use of the distributive law on the set of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ is illustrated. When multiplication is mentioned in elementary mathematics, it usually refers to this kind of multiplication. From the point of view of algebra, the real numbers form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), which ensures the validity of the distributive law.

::: {#o10 .entry cue=term name="First example (mental and written multiplication)"}

**First example (mental and written multiplication)**

During mental arithmetic, distributivity is often used unconsciously:

$$6 \cdot 16 = 6 \cdot (10 + 6) = 6\cdot 10 + 6 \cdot 6 = 60 + 36 = 96$$

Thus, to calculate $6 \cdot 16$ in one's head, one first multiplies $6 \cdot 10$ and $6 \cdot 6$ and add the intermediate results. Written multiplication is also based on the distributive law.

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=term name="Second example (with variables)"}

**Second example (with variables)**

$$3 a^2 b \cdot (4 a - 5 b) = 3 a^2 b \cdot 4a - 3 a^2 b \cdot 5 b = 12 a^3 b - 15 a^2 b^2$$

:::

::: {#o12 .entry cue=term name="Third example (with two sums)"}

**Third example (with two sums)**

$$\begin{aligned} (a + b) \cdot (a - b) & = a \cdot (a - b) + b \cdot (a - b) = a^2 - ab + ba - b^2 = a^2 - b^2 \\ & = (a + b) \cdot a - (a + b) \cdot b = a^2 + ba - ab - b^2 = a^2 - b^2 \\ \end{aligned}$$

Here the distributive law was applied twice, and it does not matter which bracket is first multiplied out.

:::

::: {#o13 .entry cue=term name="Fourth example"}

**Fourth example**

Here the distributive law is applied the other way around compared to the previous examples. Consider

$$12 a^3 b^2 - 30 a^4 b c + 18 a^2 b^3 c^2 \,.$$

Since the factor $6 a^2 b$ occurs in all summands, it can be factored out. That is, due to the distributive law one obtains

$$12 a^3 b^2 - 30 a^4 b c + 18 a^2 b^3 c^2 = 6 a^2 b \left(2 a b - 5 a^2 c + 3 b^2 c^2\right).$$

:::

### Matrices {#Matrices}

The distributive law is valid for [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). More precisely,

$$(A + B) \cdot C = A \cdot C + B \cdot C$$

for all $l \times m$-matrices $A, B$ and $m \times n$-matrices $C,$ as well as

$$A \cdot (B + C) = A \cdot B + A \cdot C$$

for all $l \times m$-matrices $A$ and $m \times n$-matrices $B, C.$ Because the commutative property does not hold for matrix multiplication, the second law does not follow from the first law. In this case, they are two different laws.

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

- [Multiplication](wikipedia://en/2139226#Multiplication) of [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932), in contrast, is only left-distributive, not right-distributive.
- The [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) is left- and right-distributive over [vector addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_addition), though not commutative.
- For [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), [union](wikipedia://en/47949) and [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) distribute over each other:[^o16] $$A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B)\cup(A \cap C)$$ $$A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C).$$
- [Logical disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) ("or") is distributive over [logical conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) ("and"), and vice versa.
- For [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) (and for any [totally ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_set)), the [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum) operation is distributive over the [minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum) operation, and vice versa: $$\max(a, \min(b, c)) = \min(\max(a, b), \max(a, c)) \quad \text{ and } \quad \min(a, \max(b, c)) = \max(\min(a, b), \min(a, c)).$$
- For [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) is distributive over the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961), and vice versa: $$\gcd(a, \operatorname{lcm}(b, c)) = \operatorname{lcm}(\gcd(a, b), \gcd(a, c)) \quad \text{ and } \quad \operatorname{lcm}(a, \gcd(b, c)) = \gcd(\operatorname{lcm}(a, b), \operatorname{lcm}(a, c)).$$
- For real numbers, addition distributes over the maximum operation, and also over the minimum operation: $$a + \max(b, c) = \max(a + b, a + c) \quad \text{ and } \quad a + \min(b, c) = \min(a + b, a + c).$$
- For [binomial](wikipedia://en/542399) multiplication, distribution is sometimes referred to as the [FOIL Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FOIL_Method)[^o17] (First terms $a c,$ Outer $a d,$ Inner $b c,$ and Last $b d$) such as: $(a + b) \cdot (c + d) = a c + a d + b c + b d.$
- In all [semirings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semirings), including the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), and [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), multiplication distributes over addition: $u (v + w) = u v + u w, (u + v)w = u w + v w.$
- In all [algebras over a field](wikipedia://en/191788), including the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) and other [non-associative algebras](wikipedia://en/6533841), multiplication distributes over addition.

:::

## Propositional logic {#Propositional_logic}

### Rule of replacement {#Rule_of_replacement}

In standard truth-functional propositional logic, distribution[^o20][^o21] in logical proofs uses two valid [rules of replacement](wikipedia://en/34770456) to expand individual occurrences of certain [logical connectives](wikipedia://en/18153), within some [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_formula), into separate applications of those connectives across subformulas of the given formula. The rules are

$$(P \land (Q \lor R)) \Leftrightarrow ((P \land Q) \lor (P \land R)) \qquad \text{ and } \qquad (P \lor (Q \land R)) \Leftrightarrow ((P \lor Q) \land (P \lor R))$$

where "$\Leftrightarrow$", also written $\,\equiv,\,$ is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) [symbol](wikipedia://en/24028642) representing "can be replaced in a proof with" or "is [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) to".

### Truth functional connectives {#Truth_functional_connectives}

Distributivity is a property of some logical connectives of truth-functional [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154). The following logical equivalences demonstrate that distributivity is a property of particular connectives. The following are truth-functional [tautologies](wikipedia://en/4495335).

$$\begin{alignedat}{13} &(P &&\;\land &&(Q \lor R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \land Q) &&\;\lor (P \land R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ conjunction } && \text{ over } && \text{ disjunction } \\ &(P &&\;\lor &&(Q \land R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \lor Q) &&\;\land (P \lor R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ disjunction } && \text{ over } && \text{ conjunction } \\ &(P &&\;\land &&(Q \land R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \land Q) &&\;\land (P \land R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ conjunction } && \text{ over } && \text{ conjunction } \\ &(P &&\;\lor &&(Q \lor R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \lor Q) &&\;\lor (P \lor R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ disjunction } && \text{ over } && \text{ disjunction } \\ &(P &&\to &&(Q \to R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \to Q) &&\to (P \to R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ implication } && \text{ } && \text{ } \\ &(P &&\to &&(Q \leftrightarrow R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \to Q) &&\leftrightarrow (P \to R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ implication } && \text{ over } && \text{ equivalence } \\ &(P &&\to &&(Q \land R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \to Q) &&\;\land (P \to R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ implication } && \text{ over } && \text{ conjunction } \\ &(P &&\;\lor &&(Q \leftrightarrow R)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& ((P \lor Q) &&\leftrightarrow (P \lor R)) && \quad\text{ Distribution of } && \text{ disjunction } && \text{ over } && \text{ equivalence } \\ \end{alignedat}$$

**Double distribution**

$$\begin{alignedat}{13} &((P \land Q) &&\;\lor (R \land S)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& (((P \lor R) \land (P \lor S)) &&\;\land ((Q \lor R) \land (Q \lor S))) && \\ &((P \lor Q) &&\;\land (R \lor S)) &&\;\Leftrightarrow\;&& (((P \land R) \lor (P \land S)) &&\;\lor ((Q \land R) \lor (Q \land S))) && \\ \end{alignedat}$$

## Distributivity and rounding {#Distributivity_and_rounding}

In approximate arithmetic, such as [floating-point arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11376), the distributive property of multiplication (and division) over addition may fail because of the limitations of [arithmetic precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_precision). For example, the identity $1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = (1 + 1 + 1) / 3$ fails in [decimal arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_arithmetic), regardless of the number of [significant digits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_digit). Methods such as [banker's rounding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banker's_rounding) may help in some cases, as may increasing the precision used, but ultimately some calculation errors are inevitable.

## In rings and other structures {#In_rings_and_other_structures}

Distributivity is most commonly found in [semirings](wikipedia://en/371468), notably the particular cases of [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29) and [distributive lattices](wikipedia://en/310883).

A semiring has two binary operations, commonly denoted $\,+\,$ and $\,*,$ and requires that $\,*\,$ must distribute over $\,+.$

A ring is a semiring with additive inverses.

A [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) is another kind of [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) with two binary operations, $\,\land \text{ and } \lor.$ If either of these operations distributes over the other (say $\,\land\,$ distributes over $\,\lor$), then the reverse also holds ($\,\lor\,$ distributes over $\,\land\,$), and the lattice is called distributive. See also [Distributivity (order theory)](wikipedia://en/616019).

A [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) can be interpreted either as a special kind of ring (a [Boolean ring](wikipedia://en/54356)) or a special kind of distributive lattice (a [Boolean lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_lattice)). Each interpretation is responsible for different distributive laws in the Boolean algebra.

In any semiring, distributivity can be used to show that any product of sums is a sum of products (though not every sum of products is necessarily a product of sums). The general formula reads:

$$\prod_{i=1}^m \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_i} a_{i,j}\right) = \sum_{j_1=1}^{n_1} \sum_{j_2=1}^{n_2} \cdots \sum_{j_m=1}^{n_m} \;\prod_{i=1}^m a_{i,j_i}$$

Structures without two-sided distributive laws are [near-rings](wikipedia://en/839900) and [near-fields](wikipedia://en/4033255). The operations are usually defined to be distributive on the right but not on the left.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

In several mathematical areas, generalized distributivity laws are considered. This may involve the weakening of the above conditions or the extension to infinitary operations. Especially in [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924) one finds numerous important variants of distributivity, some of which include infinitary operations, such as the [infinite distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_distributive_law); others being defined in the presence of only one binary operation, such as the according definitions and their relations are given in the article [distributivity (order theory)](wikipedia://en/616019). This also includes the notion of a [completely distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/7482029).

In the presence of an ordering relation, one can also weaken the above equalities by replacing $\,=\,$ by either $\,\leq\,$ or $\,\geq.$ Naturally, this will lead to meaningful concepts only in some situations. An application of this principle are the notions of *sub-distributivity*, where equality is replaced by "less than or equal"; and *super-distributivity*, where equality is replaced by "greater than or equal".

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), if $(S, \mu, \nu)$ and $\left(S^{\prime}, \mu^{\prime}, \nu^{\prime}\right)$ are [monads](wikipedia://en/355140) on a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) $C,$ a *[distributive law](wikipedia://en/7820243)* $S . S^{\prime} \to S^{\prime} . S$ is a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) $\lambda : S . S^{\prime} \to S^{\prime} . S$ such that $\left(S^{\prime}, \lambda\right)$ is a [lax map of monads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax_map_of_monads) $S \to S$ and $(S, \lambda)$ is a [colax map of monads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colax_map_of_monads) $S^{\prime} \to S^{\prime}.$ This is exactly the data needed to define a monad structure on $S^{\prime} . S$: the multiplication map is $S^{\prime} \mu . \mu^{\prime} S^2 . S^{\prime} \lambda S$ and the unit map is $\eta^{\prime} S . \eta.$ A [generalized distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_distributive_law) has also been proposed in the area of [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773).

### Antidistributivity {#Antidistributivity}

The ubiquitous [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) that relates inverses to the binary operation in any [group](wikipedia://en/19447), namely $(x y)^{-1} = y^{-1} x^{-1},$ which is taken as an axiom in the more general context of a [semigroup with involution](wikipedia://en/22679231), has sometimes been called an *antidistributive property* (of inversion as a [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006)).[^o28]

In the context of a [near-ring](wikipedia://en/839900), which removes the commutativity of the additively written group and assumes only one-sided distributivity, one can speak of (two-sided) *distributive elements* but also of *antidistributive elements*. The latter reverse the order of (the non-commutative) addition; assuming a left-nearring (i.e. one which all elements distribute when multiplied on the left), then an antidistributive element $a$ reverses the order of addition when multiplied to the right: $(x + y) a = y a + x a.$[^o29]

In the study of [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) and [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844), the term *antidistributive law* is sometimes used to denote the interchange between conjunction and disjunction when implication factors over them:[^o30]

$$(a \lor b) \Rightarrow c \equiv (a \Rightarrow c) \land (b \Rightarrow c)$$

$$(a \land b) \Rightarrow c \equiv (a \Rightarrow c) \lor (b \Rightarrow c).$$

These two [tautologies](wikipedia://en/4495335) are a direct consequence of the duality in [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669).
