# Shortest-path tree

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shortest-path tree"}

*Image: Shortestpathtreeexample.svg.* Example of one of two shortest-path trees where the root vertex is the red square vertex. The edges in the tree are indicated with green lines while the two dashed lines are edges in the full graph but not in the tree. The numbers beside the vertices indicate the distance from the root vertex.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), a **shortest-path tree** rooted at a [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) *v* of a [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph), [undirected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) [graph](wikipedia://en/12401) *G* is a [spanning tree](wikipedia://en/455770) *T* of *G*, such that the path distance from root *v* to any other vertex *u* in *T* is the [shortest path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path) distance from *v* to *u* in *G*.

:::

In connected graphs where shortest paths are well-defined (i.e. where there are no negative-length cycles), we may construct a shortest-path tree using the following algorithm:

1. Compute dist(*u*), the shortest-path distance from root *v* to vertex *u* in *G* using [Dijkstra's algorithm](wikipedia://en/45809) or [Bellman–Ford algorithm](wikipedia://en/221244).
1. For all non-root vertices *u*, we can assign to *u* a parent vertex *p<sub>u</sub>* such that *p*<sub>*u*</sub> is connected to *u*, and that dist(*p*<sub>*u*</sub>) + edge\_dist(*p*<sub>*u*</sub>,*u*) = dist(*u*). In case multiple choices for *p<sub>u</sub>* exist, choose *p*<sub>*u*</sub> for which there exists a shortest path from *v* to *p*<sub>*u*</sub> with as few edges as possible; this tie-breaking rule is needed to prevent loops when there exist zero-length cycles.
1. Construct the shortest-path tree using the edges between each node and its parent.

The above algorithm guarantees the existence of shortest-path trees. Like [minimum spanning trees](wikipedia://en/41795), shortest-path trees in general are not unique.

In graphs for which all edge weights are equal, shortest path trees coincide with [breadth-first search](wikipedia://en/97026) trees.

In graphs that have negative cycles, the set of shortest simple paths from *v* to all other vertices do not necessarily form a tree.

For simple connected graphs, shortest-path trees can be used[^o2] to suggest a non-linear relationship between two network [centrality measures](wikipedia://en/1462712), [closeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closeness_centrality) and [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448). By assuming that the branches of the shortest-path trees are statistically similar for any root node in one network, one may show that the size of the branches depend only on the number of branches connected to the root vertex, i.e. to the degree of the root node. From this one deduces that the inverse of closeness, a length scale associated with each vertex, varies approximately linearly with the logarithm of degree. The relationship is not exact but it captures a correlation between closeness and degree in large number of networks constructed from real data[^o2] and this success suggests that shortest-path trees can be a useful approximation in network analysis.
