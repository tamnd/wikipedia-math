# Generalized Maxwell model

*Image: Weichert.svg.* Schematic of Maxwell–Wiechert model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generalized Maxwell model"}

The **generalized Maxwell model** also known as the **Maxwell–Wiechert model** (after [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) and E Wiechert[^o2][^o3]) is the most general form of the linear model for [viscoelasticity](wikipedia://en/711288). In this model, several [Maxwell elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_material) are assembled in parallel. It takes into account that the [relaxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relaxation_time) does not occur at a single time, but in a set of times. Due to the presence of molecular segments of different lengths, with shorter ones contributing less than longer ones, there is a varying time distribution. The Wiechert model shows this by having as many spring–dashpot Maxwell elements as are necessary to accurately represent the distribution. The figure on the right shows the generalised Wiechert model.[^o4][^o5]

:::

The generalized Maxwell model is widely applied to describe how materials deform under mechanical stress when both elastic and viscous effects are present. It assumes linear viscoelastic behavior and is suitable for cases involving small deformations.[^o6] Because of its ability to represent complex time-dependent responses, the model is commonly used in the study of polymers, soft tissues, and other viscoelastic solids.[^o7] The model can be expressed either in the [time domain](wikipedia://en/173419) using a relaxation function or in the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346) through a complex modulus, making it adaptable for use in experimental and computational analyses. In engineering practice, it is often implemented using a [Prony series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prony's_method) to simulate viscoelastic behavior in finite element analysis.[^o8][^o9]

## General model form {#General_model_form}

A common mathematical formulation of the generalized Maxwell model uses a **discrete relaxation spectrum**, where each Maxwell element contributes a term to the overall [stress relaxation](wikipedia://en/5202789) behavior.[^o7] This leads to a Prony series representation of the relaxation modulus:

$$G(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} G_i \exp\left(-\frac{t}{\tau_i}\right)$$

where G<sub>i</sub> is the modulus and 𝜏<sub>i</sub> is the relaxation time associated with the i<sup>th</sup> Maxwell element. This method works well when the number of relaxation times in the material is already known or can be estimated from experiments. A common [rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thumb) is to include about one relaxation mode for each decade of time or frequency. More advanced statistical tools can also be used to find the smallest number of modes that still give a good fit, while avoiding [overfitting](wikipedia://en/173332) and keeping the model physically realistic.[^o11]

## Comparison of Linear Viscoelastic Models {#Comparison_of_Linear_Viscoelastic_Models}

::: {#o24 .table cue=table}

| Model | Configuration | Best for | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[Maxwell model](wikipedia://en/480282)** | Spring and dashpot in **series** | Describing **stress relaxation** | Fails to model creep accurately; predicts unbounded strain under constant stress |
| **[Kelvin-Voigt model](wikipedia://en/2061865)** | Spring and dashpot in **parallel** | Describing **creep** (delayed strain under constant stress) | Cannot describe stress relaxation behavior |
| **Generalized Maxwell model** | Multiple Maxwell elements in **parallel** | Modeling **realistic** stress relaxation and frequency-dependent behavior[^o11] | Requires fitting many parameters for accurate behavior |

:::
