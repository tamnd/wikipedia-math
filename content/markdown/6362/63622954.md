# Auxiliary normed space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Auxiliary normed space"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a branch of mathematics, two methods of constructing [normed spaces](wikipedia://en/21538) from [disks](wikipedia://en/1734292) were systematically employed by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) to define [nuclear operators](wikipedia://en/63684284) and [nuclear spaces](wikipedia://en/2702957).[^n1] One method is used if the disk $D$ is bounded: in this case, the **auxiliary normed space** is $\operatorname{span} D$ with norm

$$p_D(x) := \inf_{x \in r D, r > 0} r.$$

The other method is used if the disk $D$ is [absorbing](wikipedia://en/1728179): in this case, the auxiliary normed space is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) $X / p_D^{-1}(0).$ If the disk is both bounded and absorbing then the two auxiliary normed spaces are canonically isomorphic (as [topological vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_vector_spaces) and as [normed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space)).

:::

## Induced by a bounded disk – Banach disks {#Induced_by_a_bounded_disk_–_Banach_disks}

Throughout this article, $X$ will be a real or complex vector space (not necessarily a TVS, yet) and $D$ will be a [disk](wikipedia://en/1734292) in $X.$

### Seminormed space induced by a disk {#Seminormed_space_induced_by_a_disk}

Let $X$ will be a real or complex vector space. For any subset $D$ of $X,$ the *[Minkowski functional](wikipedia://en/3510908)* of $D$ defined by:

- If $D = \varnothing$ then define $p_{\varnothing}(x) : \{0\} \to [0, \infty)$ to be the trivial map $p_{\varnothing} = 0$[^n2] and it will be assumed that $\operatorname{span} \varnothing = \{0\}.$[^o6]
- If $D \neq \varnothing$ and if $D$ is [absorbing](wikipedia://en/1728179) in $\operatorname{span} D$ then denote the [Minkowski functional](wikipedia://en/3510908) of $D$ in $\operatorname{span} D$ by $$p_D : \operatorname{span} D \to [0, \infty)$$ where for all $x \in \operatorname{span} D,$ this is defined by $$p_D (x) := \inf_{} \{r : x \in r D, r > 0\}.$$

Let $X$ will be a real or complex vector space. For any subset $D$ of $X$ such that the Minkowski functional $p_D$ is a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067) on $\operatorname{span} D,$ let $X_D$ denote

$$X_D := \left(\operatorname{span} D, p_D\right)$$

which is called the *[seminormed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminormed_space) induced by $D,$* where if $p_D$ is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) then it is called the *[normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) induced by $D.$*

**Assumption** (**Topology**): $X_D = \operatorname{span} D$ is endowed with the seminorm topology induced by $p_D,$ which will be denoted by $\tau_D$ or $\tau_{p_D}$

Importantly, this topology stems *entirely* from the set $D,$ the algebraic structure of $X,$ and the usual topology on $\mathbb{R}$ (since $p_D$ is defined using only the set $D$ and [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373)). This justifies the study of Banach disks and is part of the reason why they play an important role in the theory of [nuclear operators](wikipedia://en/63684284) and [nuclear spaces](wikipedia://en/2702957).

The inclusion map $\operatorname{In}_D : X_D \to X$ is called the *canonical map*.[^n1]

Suppose that $D$ is a disk. Then $\operatorname{span} D = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} n D$ so that $D$ is [absorbing](wikipedia://en/1728179) in $\operatorname{span} D,$ the [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of $D.$ The set $\{r D : r > 0\}$ of all positive scalar multiples of $D$ forms a basis of neighborhoods at the origin for a [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844) topology $\tau_D$ on $\operatorname{span} D.$ The [Minkowski functional](wikipedia://en/3510908) of the disk $D$ in $\operatorname{span} D$ guarantees that $p_D$ is well-defined and forms a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067) on $\operatorname{span} D.$[^n3] The locally convex topology induced by this seminorm is the topology $\tau_D$ that was defined before.

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bounded completant"}

### Banach disk definition {#Banach_disk_definition}

A [bounded](wikipedia://en/1789812) [disk](wikipedia://en/1734292) $D$ in a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ such that $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) is called a **Banach disk**, **infracomplete**, or a **bounded completant** in $X.$

If its shown that $\left(\operatorname{span} D, p_D\right)$ is a Banach space then $D$ will be a Banach disk in any TVS that contains $D$ as a bounded subset.

This is because the Minkowski functional $p_D$ is defined in purely algebraic terms. Consequently, the question of whether or not $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ forms a Banach space is dependent only on the disk $D$ and the Minkowski functional $p_D,$ and not on any particular TVS topology that $X$ may carry. Thus the requirement that a Banach disk in a TVS $X$ be a bounded subset of $X$ is the only property that ties a Banach disk's topology to the topology of its containing TVS $X.$

:::

### Properties of disk induced seminormed spaces {#Properties_of_disk_induced_seminormed_spaces}

**Bounded disks**

The following result explains why Banach disks are required to be bounded.

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n4][^n5][^n1].**

If $D$ is a disk in a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) $X,$ then $D$ is [bounded](wikipedia://en/1789812) in $X$ if and only if the inclusion map $\operatorname{In}_D : X_D \to X$ is continuous.

:::

::: {#o16 .proof cue=math-proof}

If the disk $D$ is bounded in the TVS $X$ then for all neighborhoods $U$ of the origin in $X,$ there exists some $r > 0$ such that $r D \subseteq U \cap X_D.$ It follows that in this case the topology of $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ is finer than the subspace topology that $X_D$ inherits from $X,$ which implies that the inclusion map $\operatorname{In}_D : X_D \to X$ is continuous.

Conversely, if $X$ has a TVS topology such that $\operatorname{In}_D : X_D \to X$ is continuous, then for every neighborhood $U$ of the origin in $X$ there exists some $r > 0$ such that $r D \subseteq U \cap X_D,$ which shows that $D$ is bounded in $X.$

:::

**Hausdorffness**

The space $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) if and only if $p_D$ is a norm, which happens if and only if $D$ does not contain any non-trivial vector subspace.[^n6] In particular, if there exists a Hausdorff TVS topology on $X$ such that $D$ is bounded in $X$ then $p_D$ is a norm. An example where $X_D$ is not Hausdorff is obtained by letting $X = \mathbb{R}^2$ and letting $D$ be the $x$-axis.

**Convergence of nets**

Suppose that $D$ is a disk in $X$ such that $X_D$ is Hausdorff and let $x_\bullet = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}$ be a net in $X_D.$ Then $x_\bullet \to 0$ in $X_D$ if and only if there exists a net $r_\bullet = \left(r_i\right)_{i \in I}$ of real numbers such that $r_\bullet \to 0$ and $x_i \in r_i D$ for all $i$; moreover, in this case it will be assumed [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) that $r_i \geq 0$ for all $i.$

**Relationship between disk-induced spaces**

If $C \subseteq D \subseteq X$ then $\operatorname{span} C \subseteq \operatorname{span} D$ and $p_D \leq p_C$ on $\operatorname{span} C,$ so define the following continuous[^n5] linear map:

If $C$ and $D$ are disks in $X$ with $C \subseteq D$ then call the inclusion map $\operatorname{In}_C^D : X_C \to X_D$ the *canonical inclusion* of $X_C$ into $X_D.$

In particular, the subspace topology that $\operatorname{span} C$ inherits from $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ is weaker than $\left(X_C, p_C\right)$'s seminorm topology.[^n5]

**The disk as the closed unit ball**

The disk $D$ is a closed subset of $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ if and only if $D$ is the closed unit ball of the seminorm $p_D$; that is, $D = \left\{x \in \operatorname{span} D : p_D(x) \leq 1\right\}.$

If $D$ is a disk in a vector space $X$ and if there exists a TVS topology $\tau$ on $\operatorname{span} D$ such that $D$ is a closed and bounded subset of $\left(\operatorname{span} D, \tau\right),$ then $D$ is the closed unit ball of $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ (that is, $D = \left\{x \in \operatorname{span} D : p_D(x) \leq 1\right\}$ ) (see footnote for proof).[^o20]

### Sufficient conditions for a Banach disk {#Sufficient_conditions_for_a_Banach_disk}

The following theorem may be used to establish that $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ is a Banach space. Once this is established, $D$ will be a Banach disk in any TVS in which $D$ is bounded.

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n7].**

Let $D$ be a disk in a vector space $X.$ If there exists a Hausdorff TVS topology $\tau$ on $\operatorname{span} D$ such that $D$ is a bounded [sequentially complete](wikipedia://en/64126151) subset of $(\operatorname{span} D, \tau),$ then $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ is a Banach space.

:::

::: {#o24 .proof cue=math-proof}

Assume without loss of generality that $X = \operatorname{span} D$ and let $p := p_D$ be the [Minkowski functional](wikipedia://en/3510908) of $D.$ Since $D$ is a bounded subset of a Hausdorff TVS, $D$ do not contain any non-trivial vector subspace, which implies that $p$ is a norm. Let $\tau_D$ denote the norm topology on $X$ induced by $p$ where since $D$ is a bounded subset of $(X, \tau),$ $\tau_D$ is finer than $\tau.$

Because $D$ is convex and balanced, for any $0 < m < n$

$$2^{-(n+1)} D + \cdots + 2^{-(m+2)} D = 2^{-(m+1)} \left(1 - 2^{m-n}\right) D \subseteq 2^{-(m+2)} D.$$

Let $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ be a Cauchy sequence in $\left(X_D, p\right).$ By replacing $x_{\bullet}$ with a subsequence, we may assume without loss of generality<sup>†</sup> that for all $i,$

$$x_{i+1} - x_i \in \frac{1}{2^{i+2}} D.$$

This implies that for any $0 < m < n,$

$$x_n - x_m = \left(x_n - x_{n-1}\right) + \left(x_{m+1} - x_m\right) \in 2^{-(n+1)} D + \cdots + 2^{-(m+2)} D \subseteq 2^{-(m+2)} D$$

so that in particular, by taking $m = 1$ it follows that $x_{\bullet}$ is contained in $x_1 + 2^{-3} D.$ Since $\tau_D$ is finer than $\tau,$ $x_{\bullet}$ is a Cauchy sequence in $(X, \tau).$ For all $m > 0,$ $2^{-(m+2)} D$ is a Hausdorff sequentially complete subset of $(X, \tau).$ In particular, this is true for $x_1 + 2^{-3} D$ so there exists some $x \in x_1 + 2^{-3} D$ such that $x_{\bullet} \to x$ in $(X, \tau).$

Since $x_n - x_m \in 2^{-(m+2)} D$ for all $0 < m < n,$ by fixing $m$ and taking the limit (in $(X, \tau)$) as $n \to \infty,$ it follows that $x - x_m \in 2^{-(m+2)} D$ for each $m > 0.$ This implies that $p\left(x - x_m\right) \to 0$ as $m \to \infty,$ which says exactly that $x_{\bullet} \to x$ in $\left(X_D, p\right).$ This shows that $\left(X_D, p\right)$ is complete.

<sup>†</sup>This assumption is allowed because $x_{\bullet}$ is a Cauchy sequence in a metric space (so the limits of all subsequences are equal) and a sequence in a metric space converges if and only if every subsequence has a sub-subsequence that converges.

:::

Note that even if $D$ is not a bounded and sequentially complete subset of any Hausdorff TVS, one might still be able to conclude that $\left(X_D, p_D\right)$ is a Banach space by applying this theorem to some disk $K$ satisfying

$$\left\{x \in \operatorname{span} D : p_D(x) < 1\right\} \subseteq K \subseteq \left\{x \in \operatorname{span} D : p_D(x) \leq 1\right\}$$

because $p_D = p_K.$

The following are consequences of the above theorem:

- A sequentially complete bounded disk in a Hausdorff TVS is a Banach disk.[^n5]
- Any disk in a Hausdorff TVS that is complete and bounded (e.g. compact) is a Banach disk.[^n8]
- The closed unit ball in a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) is sequentially complete and thus a Banach disk.[^n5]

Suppose that $D$ is a bounded disk in a TVS $X.$

- If $L : X \to Y$ is a continuous linear map and $B \subseteq X$ is a Banach disk, then $L(B)$ is a Banach disk and $L\big\vert_{X_B} : X_B \to L\left(X_B\right)$ induces an isometric TVS-isomorphism $Y_{L(B)} \cong X_B / \left(X_B \cap \operatorname{ker} L\right).$

### Properties of Banach disks {#Properties_of_Banach_disks}

Let $X$ be a TVS and let $D$ be a bounded disk in $X.$

If $D$ is a bounded Banach disk in a Hausdorff locally convex space $X$ and if $T$ is a barrel in $X$ then $T$ [absorbs](wikipedia://en/1728179) $D$ (that is, there is a number $r > 0$ such that $D \subseteq r T.$[^n4]

If $U$ is a convex balanced closed neighborhood of the origin in $X$ then the collection of all neighborhoods $r U,$ where $r > 0$ ranges over the positive real numbers, induces a topological vector space topology on $X.$ When $X$ has this topology, it is denoted by $X_U.$ Since this topology is not necessarily Hausdorff nor complete, the completion of the Hausdorff space $X / p_U^{-1}(0)$ is denoted by $\overline{X_U}$ so that $\overline{X_U}$ is a complete Hausdorff space and $p_U(x) := \inf_{x \in r U, r > 0} r$ is a norm on this space making $\overline{X_U}$ into a Banach space. The polar of $U,$ $U^{\circ},$ is a weakly compact bounded equicontinuous disk in $X^{\prime}$ and so is infracomplete.

If $X$ is a [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) TVS then for every [bounded](wikipedia://en/1789812) subset $B$ of $X,$ there exists a bounded [disk](wikipedia://en/1734292) $D$ in $X$ such that $B \subseteq X_D,$ and both $X$ and $X_D$ induce the same [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) on $B.$[^n5]

## Induced by a radial disk – quotient {#Induced_by_a_radial_disk_–_quotient}

Suppose that $X$ is a topological vector space and $V$ is a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [balanced](wikipedia://en/1734238) and [radial](wikipedia://en/33297737) set. Then $\left\{\tfrac{1}{n} V : n = 1, 2, \ldots\right\}$ is a neighborhood basis at the origin for some locally convex topology $\tau_V$ on $X.$ This TVS topology $\tau_V$ is given by the [Minkowski functional](wikipedia://en/3510908) formed by $V,$ $p_V : X \to \mathbb{R},$ which is a seminorm on $X$ defined by $p_V(x) := \inf_{x \in r V, r > 0} r.$ The topology $\tau_V$ is Hausdorff if and only if $p_V$ is a norm, or equivalently, if and only if $X / p_V^{-1}(0) = \{0\}$ or equivalently, for which it suffices that $V$ be [bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_bounded) in $X.$ The topology $\tau_V$ need not be Hausdorff but $X / p_V^{-1}(0)$ is Hausdorff. A norm on $X / p_V^{-1}(0)$ is given by $\left\|x + X / p_V^{-1}(0)\right\| := p_V(x),$ where this value is in fact independent of the representative of the equivalence class $x + X / p_V^{-1}(0)$ chosen. The normed space $\left(X / p_V^{-1}(0), \| \cdot \|\right)$ is denoted by $X_V$ and its completion is denoted by $\overline{X_V}.$

If in addition $V$ is bounded in $X$ then the seminorm $p_V : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is a norm so in particular, $p_V^{-1}(0) = \{0\}.$ In this case, we take $X_V$ to be the vector space $X$ instead of $X / \{0\}$ so that the notation $X_V$ is unambiguous (whether $X_V$ denotes the space induced by a radial disk or the space induced by a bounded disk).[^n1]

The [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) $\tau_Q$ on $X / p_V^{-1}(0)$ (inherited from $X$'s original topology) is finer (in general, strictly finer) than the norm topology.

### Canonical maps {#Canonical_maps}

The *canonical map* is the [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map) $q_V : X \to X_V = X / p_V^{-1}(0),$ which is continuous when $X_V$ has either the norm topology or the quotient topology.[^n1]

If $U$ and $V$ are radial disks such that $U \subseteq V$ then $p_U^{-1}(0) \subseteq p_V^{-1}(0)$ so there is a continuous linear surjective *canonical map* $q_{V,U} : X / p_U^{-1}(0) \to X / p_V^{-1}(0) = X_V$ defined by sending $x + p_U^{-1}(0) \in X_U = X / p_U^{-1}(0)$ to the equivalence class $x + p_V^{-1}(0),$ where one may verify that the definition does not depend on the representative of the equivalence class $x + p_U^{-1}(0)$ that is chosen.[^n1] This canonical map has norm $\,\leq 1$[^n1] and it has a unique continuous linear canonical extension to $\overline{X_U}$ that is denoted by $\overline{g_{V,U}} : \overline{X_U} \to \overline{X_V}.$

Suppose that in addition $B \neq \varnothing$ and $C$ are bounded disks in $X$ with $B \subseteq C$ so that $X_B \subseteq X_C$ and the inclusion $\operatorname{In}_B^C : X_B \to X_C$ is a continuous linear map. Let $\operatorname{In}_B : X_B \to X,$ $\operatorname{In}_C : X_C \to X,$ and $\operatorname{In}_B^C : X_B \to X_C$ be the canonical maps. Then $\operatorname{In}_C = \operatorname{In}_B^C \circ \operatorname{In}_C : X_B \to X_C$ and $q_V = q_{V,U} \circ q_U.$[^n1]

## Induced by a bounded radial disk {#Induced_by_a_bounded_radial_disk}

Suppose that $S$ is a bounded radial disk. Since $S$ is a bounded disk, if $D := S$ then we may create the auxiliary normed space $X_D = \operatorname{span} D$ with norm $p_D(x) := \inf_{x \in r D, r > 0} r$; since $S$ is radial, $X_S = X.$ Since $S$ is a radial disk, if $V := S$ then we may create the auxiliary seminormed space $X / p_V^{-1}(0)$ with the seminorm $p_V(x) := \inf_{x \in r V, r > 0} r$; because $S$ is bounded, this seminorm is a norm and $p_V^{-1}(0) = \{0\}$ so $X / p_V^{-1}(0) = X / \{0\} = X.$ Thus, in this case the two auxiliary normed spaces produced by these two different methods result in the same normed space.

## Duality {#Duality}

Suppose that $H$ is a weakly closed equicontinuous disk in $X^{\prime}$ (this implies that $H$ is weakly compact) and let

$$U := H^{\circ} = \{x \in X : |h(x)| \leq 1 \text{ for all } h \in H\}$$

be the [polar](wikipedia://en/1764022) of $H.$ Because $U^\circ = H^{\circ\circ} = H$ by the [bipolar theorem](wikipedia://en/35128576), it follows that a continuous linear functional $f$ belongs to $X^{\prime}_H = \operatorname{span} H$ if and only if $f$ belongs to the continuous dual space of $\left(X, p_U\right),$ where $p_U$ is the [Minkowski functional](wikipedia://en/3510908) of $U$ defined by $p_U(x) := \inf_{x \in r U, r > 0} r.$[^n9]

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

A disk in a TVS is called *[infrabornivorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrabornivorous)*[^n5] if it [absorbs](wikipedia://en/1728179) all Banach disks.

A linear map between two TVSs is called *[infrabounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrabounded_map)*[^n5] if it maps Banach disks to bounded disks.

::: {#o45 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Fast convergence"}

### Fast convergence {#Fast_convergence}

A sequence $x_\bullet = \left(x_i\right)_{i=1}^\infty$ in a TVS $X$ is said to be *fast convergent*[^n5] to a point $x \in X$ if there exists a Banach disk $D$ such that both $x$ and the sequence is (eventually) contained in $\operatorname{span} D$ and $x_\bullet \to x$ in $\left(X_D, p_D\right).$

Every fast convergent sequence is [Mackey convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mackey_convergent_sequence).[^n5]

:::
