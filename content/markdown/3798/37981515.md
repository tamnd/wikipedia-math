# Geometric mean theorem

*Image: Hoehensatz2.svg.* area of grey square = area of grey rectangle: $h^{2} = pq \iff h = \sqrt{pq}$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geometric mean theorem"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), the **right triangle altitude theorem** or **geometric mean theorem** is a relation between the [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241) on the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) in a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) and the two line segments it creates on the hypotenuse. It states that the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of those two segments equals the altitude.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem and its converse"}

## Theorem and its converse {#Theorem_and_its_converse}

*Image: Sehnensatz hoehensatz.svg.* geometric mean theorem as a special case of the [intersecting chords theorem](wikipedia://en/19359050): $\lvert CD\rvert\lvert DE\rvert = \lvert AD\rvert\lvert DB\rvert$ $\iff h^{2} = pq$

If $h$ denotes the altitude in a right triangle and $p$ and $q$ the segments on the hypotenuse then the theorem can be stated as:[^o6]

$$h=\sqrt{pq}$$

or in term of areas:

$$h^2=pq.$$

The converse statement is true as well. Any triangle, in which the altitude equals the geometric mean of the two line segments created by it, is a right triangle.

The theorem can also be thought of as a special case of the [intersecting chords theorem](wikipedia://en/19359050) for a circle, since the converse of [Thales' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales'_theorem) ensures that the hypotenuse of the right angled triangle is the diameter of its [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432).[^o6]

:::

::: {#o5 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Root\_construction\_geometric\_mean5.svg.* Construction of $\sqrt{p}$ by setting $q$ to 1

*Image: Am gm half circle3.svg.* Demonstration of [AM–GM inequality](wikipedia://en/605011)

The formulation in terms of areas yields a method to square a rectangle with [ruler and compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions), that is to construct a square of equal area to a given rectangle. For such a rectangle with sides $p$ and $q$ we denote its top left [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) with $D$ #Proof based on similarity, below, for a graphic of the construction. Now we extend the segment $q$ to its left by $p$ (using arc $\overline{AE}$ centered on $D$) and draw a half circle with endpoints $A$ and $B$ with the new segment $p + q$ as its diameter. Then we erect a perpendicular line to the diameter in $D$ that intersects the half circle in $C$. Due to [Thales' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales'_theorem) $C$ and the diameter form a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) with the line segment $\overline{DC}$ as its altitude, hence $\overline{DC}$ is the side of a square with the area of the rectangle. The method also allows for the construction of square roots (see [constructible number](wikipedia://en/7439)), since starting with a rectangle that has a width of 1 the constructed square will have a side length that equals the square root of the rectangle's length.[^o6]

Another application of this theorem provides a geometrical proof of the [AM–GM inequality](wikipedia://en/605011) in the case of two numbers, since the half-circle's [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) is the arithmetic mean of $p$ and $q$. This radius can be drawn parallel to the geometric mean constructed as above, which shows that geometric mean is always smaller or equal to the radius, and yields the inequality.[^o7]

:::

## History {#History}

The theorem is usually attributed to [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) (ca. 360–280 BC), who stated it as a corollary to proposition 8 in book VI of his [Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_elements). In proposition 14 of book II Euclid gives a method for squaring a rectangle, which essentially matches the method given here. Euclid however provides a different slightly more complicated proof for the correctness of the construction rather than relying on the geometric mean theorem.[^o6][^o9]

## Proofs {#Proofs}

::: {#o13 .proof cue=heading name="Proof based on similarity"}

### Proof based on similarity {#Proof_based_on_similarity}

*Image: Hoehensatz.svg.* $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle ADC \sim \triangle DBC$

::: {#o15 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of theorem"}

#### Proof of theorem {#Proof_of_theorem}

The triangles $\triangle ADC , \triangle BCD$ are [similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_triangle#Similar_triangles), since:

- consider triangles $\triangle ABC, \triangle ACD$; here we have $$\angle ACB=\angle ADC=90^\circ, \quad \angle BAC=\angle CAD;$$ therefore by the [AA postulate](wikipedia://en/1406077) $$\triangle ABC \sim \triangle ACD .$$
- further, consider triangles $\triangle ABC, \triangle BCD$; here we have $$\angle ACB=\angle BDC= 90^\circ, \quad \angle ABC=\angle CBD;$$ therefore by the AA postulate $$\triangle ABC \sim \triangle BCD.$$

Therefore, both triangles $\triangle ACD, \triangle BCD$ are similar to $\triangle ABC$ and themselves, i.e.

$$\triangle ACD \sim \triangle ABC \sim \triangle BCD.$$

Because of the similarity we get the following equality of ratios and its algebraic rearrangement yields the theorem:[^o6]

$$\frac{h}{p}=\frac{q}{h}\,\iff\,h^2=pq\,\iff\,h=\sqrt{pq}\qquad (h,p,q> 0)$$

:::

::: {#o17 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of converse"}

#### Proof of converse {#Proof_of_converse}

For the converse we have a triangle $\triangle ABC$ in which $h^{2} = pq$ holds and need to show that the angle at $C$ is a right angle. Now because of $h^{2} = pq$ we also have $\tfrac{h}{p}=\tfrac{q}{h}.$ Together with $\angle ADC=\angle CDB$ the triangles $\triangle ADC, \triangle BDC$ have an angle of equal size and have corresponding pairs of legs with the same ratio. This means the triangles are similar, which yields:

$$\begin{aligned} \angle ACB &= \angle ACD +\angle DCB \\ &= \angle ACD+(90^\circ-\angle DBC) \\ &= \angle ACD+(90^\circ-\angle ACD) \\ &= 90^\circ \end{aligned}$$

:::

:::

::: {#o19 .proof cue=heading name="Proof based on the Pythagorean theorem"}

### Proof based on the Pythagorean theorem {#Proof_based_on_the_Pythagorean_theorem}

*Image: Hoehensatz beweis pythagoras.svg.* Proof with the Pythagorean theorem

In the setting of the geometric mean theorem there are three right triangles $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle ADC$ and $\triangle DBC$ in which the Pythagorean theorem yields:

$$\begin{aligned} h^2 &= a^2-q^2 \\ h^2 &= b^2-p^2 \\ c^2 &= a^2+b^2 \end{aligned}$$

Adding the first 2 two equations and then using the third then leads to:

$$\begin{aligned} 2h^2 &= a^2+b^2-p^2-q^2 \\ &= c^2-p^2-q^2 \\ &= (p+q)^2-p^2-q^2 \\ &= 2pq \\ \therefore \ h^2 &= pq, \end{aligned}$$

which finally yields the formula of the geometric mean theorem.[^o20]

:::

::: {#o22 .proof cue=heading name="Proof based on dissection and rearrangement"}

### Proof based on dissection and rearrangement {#Proof_based_on_dissection_and_rearrangement}

*Image: Geometrischer Höhensatzbeweis.svg.*

Dissecting the right triangle along its altitude $h$ yields two similar triangles, which can be augmented and arranged in two alternative ways into a larger right triangle with perpendicular sides of lengths $p + h$ and $q + h$. One such arrangement requires a square of area $h^{2}$ to complete it, the other a rectangle of area $pq$. Since both arrangements yield the same triangle, the areas of the square and the rectangle must be identical.

:::
