# Convenient number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Convenient number"}

A **convenient number** is a number which in several situations can prove convenient for use by humans for counting and measuring, and is related to [preferred numbers](wikipedia://en/1469133) (which are [standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_standard) recommendations used for choosing [product dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preferred_metric_sizes)).

:::

The convenient numbers in this article were developed in the US in connection with the attempted introduction of the [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) in the United States in the 1970s. Hence, they may be viewed as a recommendation for choosing product dimensions when switching to the metric system.[^o7]

## History {#History}

In the 1970s, the American [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) (NBS), which was later renamed to the [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) (NIST), defined a set of convenient numbers when it was developing procedures for [metrication in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrication_in_the_United_States).

An NBS technical note describes that system of convenient metric values as the [1-2-5 series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-2-5_series) in reverse, with assigned preferences for those numbers which are multiples of 5, 2, and 1 (plus their powers of 10). Linear dimensions above 100 mm were excluded (because such measurements are defined by another set of rules). A table of this 5, 2, 1 series can be seen below in the section "Schedule of convenient numbers between 10 and 100".[^o7]

The NBS technical note also states that "Integers are more convenient than expressions which include decimal parts \[[decimal fractions](wikipedia://en/1704824)\]. Furthermore, where measuring devices are used, values which represent numbered subdivisions on such instruments are more useful than values which have to be interpolated. For example, where a tape or a scale is graduated in intervals of 5, any value that represents a multiple of 5 is more "convenient" to measure or verify than one which is not. In addition, where operations involve the subdivision of quantities into two or more equal parts, any number that is highly divisible has an explicit advantage."
