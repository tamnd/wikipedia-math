# William Brereton, 3rd Baron Brereton

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="William Brereton, 3rd Baron Brereton"}

*Image: British School - William Brereton (1631–1679), 3rd Lord of Leighlin - 1885P3185 - Birmingham Museums Trust.jpg.* Image of William Brereton

**William Brereton, 3rd Baron Brereton** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) (4 May 1631 – 17 March 1680) was an English mathematician and politician who sat in the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_England) in 1659 and became Baron Brereton in the Irish peerage in 1664. He was chairman of the Committee of Accounts, better known as the Brooke House Committee, in 1667–1670. In that capacity he clashed repeatedly with [Samuel Pepys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Pepys), whose description of Brereton in his Second Diary, or Brooke House Journal, although no doubt biased, is the best portrait we have of the man.[^o2]

:::

## Early career {#Early_career}

*Image: Brereton Hall.jpg.* Brereton Hall, the seat of the Brereton family.

Brereton was the eldest son of [William Brereton, 2nd Baron Brereton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brereton,_2nd_Baron_Brereton) of Brereton Hall, near [Chester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester)[^o4] and his wife Lady Elizabeth Goring, daughter of [George Goring, 1st Earl of Norwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Goring,_1st_Earl_of_Norwich) and Mary Neville. He studied mathematics and Greek at the [Orange College of Breda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_College_of_Breda),[^o5] where he was tutored by [John Pell](wikipedia://en/491570).[^o6]

In 1659, Brereton was elected [Member of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_Parliament) for [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_%28UK_Parliament_constituency%29) in the [Third Protectorate Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Protectorate_Parliament)[^o7] and for [Bossiney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bossiney_%28UK_Parliament_constituency%29) in 1660.[^o8] By his own account he was a man utterly without influence at Court.[^o9]

Brereton became an original [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_A,_B,_C) on 22 April 1663.[^o10] He inherited the Irish peerage [Baron Brereton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Brereton) on the death of his father in 1664. In 1668 he gave the rectory of [Tilston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilston), near [Malpas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malpas,_Cheshire) to the mathematician [Thomas Branker](wikipedia://en/23533501).

## Brooke House Committee {#Brooke_House_Committee}

Brereton briefly became a figure of national importance as Chairman of the Brooke House Committee, properly called the Committee of Accounts, set up by the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_England) to inquire into the failures of the Navy during the [Second Anglo-Dutch War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Anglo-Dutch_War).[^o13] By his own admission he was chosen because his lack of influence at Court, which in the climate of the times was seen as a sign of his independence. He faced a formidable opponent in Samuel Pepys, who defended the record of the Navy Office with great eloquence. Pepys was noted for the accuracy of his reporting, so his description of the frequent hot-tempered exchanges between them is probably accurate enough. When Brereton angrily denounced Pepys for questioning the Committee's reading of the Statute empowering them to act, Pepys replied that any Englishman may read and form his own interpretation of a public statute.[^o14] When Brereton sarcastically asked if Pepys would defy the whole world, Pepys replied that he would "defy the whole world and Lord Brereton in particular".[^o15] Brereton was hampered by the fact that King [Charles II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_II_of_England) and his brother [James, Duke of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_II_of_England), were present for most of the hearings and made their support for Pepys evident. James on one occasion rebuked Brereton for improper language, and when Brereton produced what he described as written proof that Pepys had taken a bribe, the King asked drily if anyone could believe that Pepys would stoop to take such a paltry sum.[^o16] When the committee was dissolved, having produced no lasting results, Brereton effectively retired from public life.[^o17]

## Family {#Family}

Brereton married Frances, daughter and co-heiress of [Francis Willoughby, 5th Baron Willoughby of Parham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Willoughby,_5th_Baron_Willoughby_of_Parham) and his wife Elizabeth Cecil (who has great-granddaughter of [William Cecil, 1st Baron Burghley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Cecil,_1st_Baron_Burghley). He died in 1680, aged only 48, and was succeeded by his sons [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Brereton,_4th_Baron_Brereton) and [Francis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Brereton,_5th_Baron_Brereton); on the death of Francis in 1722 the title became extinct.
