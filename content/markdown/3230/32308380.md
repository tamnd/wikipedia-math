# Lee Sallows

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Lee Sallows"}

**Lee Cecil Fletcher Sallows** (born April 30, 1944) is a British electronics engineer known for his contributions to [recreational mathematics](wikipedia://en/26446). He is particularly noted as the inventor of [golygons](wikipedia://en/4303848), [self-enumerating sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-enumerating_sentence), and [geomagic squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagic_square).

:::

## Recreational mathematics {#Recreational_mathematics}

Sallows is an expert on the theory of [magic squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_squares)[^o4] and has invented several variations on them, including [alphamagic squares](wikipedia://en/36544208)[^o5][^o6] and [geomagic squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomagic_square).[^o7] The latter invention caught the attention of mathematician [Peter Cameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Cameron_%28mathematician%29), who has said that he believes that "an even deeper structure may lie hidden beyond geomagic squares".[^o8]

In "The lost theorem" published in 1997 he showed that every 3 × 3 magic square is associated with a unique parallelogram on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), a discovery that had escaped all previous researchers from ancient times down to the present day.[^o9]

A [golygon](wikipedia://en/4303848) is a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) containing only [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956), such that adjacent sides exhibit consecutive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) lengths. Golygons were invented and named by Sallows[^o10] and introduced by [A.K. Dewdney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.K._Dewdney) in the *Computer Recreations* column of the July 1990 issue of *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*.[^o11]

In 2012 Sallows invented and named [self-tiling tile sets](wikipedia://en/42595150)—a new generalization of [rep-tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rep-tiles).[^o12]

## Triangle theorem {#Triangle_theorem}

*Image: sallows\_triangle\_theorem.svg.* Visual proof of Lee Sallows's triangle theorem

In 2014, Sallows discovered a previously unnoticed result, a way of using the [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006) to divide any triangle into three smaller triangles, all congruent with one another. Repeating the process on each triangle yields triangles [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) to the original but a ninth the area.[^o15]

:::

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Lee Sallows is the only son of Florence Eliza Fletcher and Leonard Gandy Sallows. He was born on 30 April 1944 at [Brocket Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brocket_Hall) in [Hertfordshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertfordshire), England, and grew up in the district of [Upper Clapton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Clapton) in northeast London. Sallows attended [Dame Alice Owen's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dame_Alice_Owen's_School), then located at [The Angel, Islington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_of_Islington), but failed to settle in and was without diplomas when he left at age 17. Knowledge gained via interest in [short-wave radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-wave_radio) enabled him to find work as a technician within the electronics industry. In 1970, he moved to [Nijmegen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nijmegen) in the Netherlands, where, until 2009, he worked as an electronic engineer at [Radboud University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radboud_University). In 1975, Sallows met his partner Evert Lamfers, a Dutch [cardiologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiologist),[^o17] with whom he has lived ever since.
