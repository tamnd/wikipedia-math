# Plane quartic curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Plane quartic curve"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **plane quartic curve** is a [plane algebraic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_algebraic_curve) of the fourth [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652). It can be defined by a bivariate [quartic equation](wikipedia://en/251591):

:::

$$Ax^4+By^4+Cx^3y+Dx^2y^2+Exy^3+Fx^3+Gy^3+Hx^2y+Ixy^2+Jx^2+Ky^2+Lxy+Mx+Ny+P=0,$$

with at least one of $A, B, C, D, E$ not equal to zero. This equation has 15 constants. However, it can be multiplied by any non-zero constant without changing the curve; thus by the choice of an appropriate constant of multiplication, any one of the coefficients can be set to 1, leaving only 14 constants. Therefore, the space of quartic curves can be identified with the [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) $\mathbb{RP}^{14}.$ It also follows, from [Cramer's theorem on algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cramer's_theorem_%28algebraic_curves%29), that there is exactly one quartic curve that passes through a set of 14 distinct points in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736), since a quartic has 14 [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29).

A quartic curve can have a maximum of:

- Four connected components
- Twenty-eight [bi-tangents](wikipedia://en/2605212)
- Three ordinary [double points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_point).

One may also consider quartic curves over other [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) (or even [rings](wikipedia://en/48404)), for instance the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers). In this way, one gets [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), which are one-dimensional objects over $\mathbb{C},$ but are two-dimensional over $\mathbb{R}.$ An example is the [Klein quartic](wikipedia://en/390976). Additionally, one can look at curves in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350), given by homogeneous polynomials.

### Ampersand curve {#Ampersand_curve}

The [ampersand curve](wikipedia://en/79847126) is a quartic plane curve given by the equation:

$$\ (y^2-x^2)(x-1)(2x-3)=4(x^2+y^2-2x)^2.$$

It has [genus](wikipedia://en/61891#Graph_theory) zero, with three ordinary double points, all in the real plane.

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bean curve"}

### Bean curve {#Bean_curve}

The **bean curve** is a quartic plane curve with the equation:

$$x^4+x^2y^2+y^4=x(x^2+y^2). \,$$

The bean curve has genus zero. It has one [singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) at the origin, an ordinary triple point. [^o8]

:::

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bicuspid curve"}

### Bicuspid curve {#Bicuspid_curve}

The **bicuspid** is a quartic plane curve with the equation

$$(x^2-a^2)(x-a)^2+(y^2-a^2)^2=0 \,$$

where *a* determines the size of the curve. The bicuspid has only the two cusps as singularities, and hence is a curve of genus one.

:::

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bow curve"}

### Bow curve {#Bow_curve}

The **bow curve** is a quartic plane curve with the equation:

$$x^4=x^2y-y^3. \,$$

The bow curve has a single triple point at *x*=0, *y*=0, and consequently is a rational curve, with genus zero.

:::

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cruciform curve"}

### Cruciform curve {#Cruciform_curve}

The **cruciform curve**, or **cross curve** is a quartic plane curve given by the equation

$$x^2y^2-b^2x^2-a^2y^2=0 \,$$

where *a* and *b* are two [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) determining the shape of the curve. The cruciform curve is related by a standard quadratic transformation, *x* ↦ 1/*x*, *y* ↦ 1/*y* to the ellipse *a*<sup>2</sup>*x*<sup>2</sup> + *b*<sup>2</sup>*y*<sup>2</sup> = 1, and is therefore a [rational plane algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260#Rational_curves) of genus zero. The cruciform curve has three double points in the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325), at *x*=0 and *y*=0, *x*=0 and *z*=0, and *y*=0 and *z*=0.

Because the curve is rational, it can be parametrized by rational functions. For instance, if *a*=1 and *b*=2, then

$$x = -\frac{t^2-2t+5}{t^2-2t-3},\quad y = \frac{t^2-2t+5}{2t-2}$$

parametrizes the points on the curve outside of the exceptional cases where a denominator is zero.

*Image: inverse\_pythagorean\_theorem.svg.* Illustration of the inverse Pythagorean and regular Pythagorean theorems

The [inverse Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/62694924) is obtained from the above equation by substituting *x* with *AC*, *y* with *BC*, and each *a* and *b* with *CD*, where *A*, *B* are the endpoints of the hypotenuse of a right triangle *ABC*, and *D* is the foot of a perpendicular dropped from *C*, the vertex of the right angle, to the hypotenuse:

$$\begin{aligned} AC^2 BC^2 - CD^2 AC^2 - CD^2 BC^2 &= 0 \\ AC^2 BC^2 &= CD^2 BC^2 + CD^2 AC^2 \\ \frac{1}{CD^2} &= \frac{BC^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} + \frac{AC^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} \\ \therefore \;\; \frac{1}{CD^2} &= \frac{ 1 }{AC^2 } + \frac{ 1 }{ BC^2} \end{aligned}$$

:::

### Spiric section {#Spiric_section}

Spiric sections can be defined as [bicircular](wikipedia://en/24215692) quartic curves that are symmetric with respect to the *x* and *y* axes. Spiric sections are included in the family of [toric sections](wikipedia://en/5350369) and include the family of [hippopedes](wikipedia://en/2737959) and the family of [Cassini ovals](wikipedia://en/2040906). The name is from σπειρα meaning torus in ancient Greek.

The Cartesian equation can be written as

$$(x^2+y^2)^2=dx^2+ey^2+f ,$$

and the equation in polar coordinates as

$$r^4=dr^2\cos^2\theta+er^2\sin^2\theta+f. \,$$

::: {#o21 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Three-leaved clover"}

### Three-leaved clover (trifolium) {#Three-leaved_clover_(trifolium)}

The **three-leaved clover** or **trifolium** is the quartic plane curve

$$x^4+2x^2y^2+y^4-x^3+3xy^2=0. \,$$

By solving for *y*, the curve can be described by the following function:

$$y=\pm\sqrt{\frac{-2x^2-3x\pm\sqrt{16x^3+9x^2}}{2}},$$

where the two appearances of ± are independent of each other, giving up to four distinct values of *y* for each *x*.

The parametric equation of curve is

$x = \cos(3t) \cos t,\quad y = \cos(3t) \sin t. \,$[^o23]

In polar coordinates (*x* = *r* cos φ, *y* = *r* sin φ) the equation is

$$r = \cos(3\varphi). \,$$

It is a special case of [rose curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_curve) with *k* = 3. This curve has a triple point at the origin (0, 0) and has three double tangents.
