# Egyptian numerals

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Egyptian numerals"}

The system of ancient **Egyptian numerals** was used in [Ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) from around 3000 BC[^o2] until the early first millennium AD. It was a system of numeration based on multiples of ten, often rounded off to the higher power, written in [hieroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_hieroglyph).

:::

The Egyptians had no concept of a [positional notation](wikipedia://en/437052) such as the [decimal system](wikipedia://en/8214).[^o3] The [hieratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieratic) form of numerals stressed an exact finite series notation, ciphered one-to-one onto the Egyptian alphabet.

## Zero {#Zero}

There was no symbol or concept of [zero](wikipedia://en/34513) as a placeholder in Egyptian numeration and zero was not used in calculations.[^n1] The symbol [nfr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nefer):meaning "good", "complete", "beautiful" served two similar, albeit non-mathematical, purposes:[^n2]

- in a papyrus listing the court expenses, c. 1740 [BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anno_Domini), it indicated a fulfilled balance;[^o9]
- in setting marks for the [Nyuserre pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_of_Nyuserre) and [Mastabat al-Fir'aun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastabat_al-Fir'aun), *nfr* is used to indicate the architectural baseline: height and depths are measured "above *nfr*" or "below *nfr*" respectively.[^o10]

Mathematician and historian [Carl Boyer](wikipedia://en/6694636), has claimed that a deed from [Edfu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edfu) contained a "zero concept" replacing the magnitude in geometry.[^n3] Friedhelm Hoffmann however clarifies that in the Egyptian method of calculating area it was only in this later, Ptolemaic era document that it contained an explicit placeholder for no length on one side. He emphasizes that it was not merely the negation particle *jw* "𓂜" but *jwty*, meaning "missing":[^o9]

## Fractions {#Fractions}

[Rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) could also be expressed, but only as sums of [unit fractions](wikipedia://en/491316), *i.e.*, sums of [reciprocals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29) of positive integers, except for $\tfrac{2}{3}$ and $\tfrac{3}{4}$. The hieroglyph indicating a fraction looked like a mouth, which meant "part":

Fractions were written with this fractional [solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_%28punctuation%29), *i.e.*, the [numerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerator) 1, and the positive [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) below. Thus, $\tfrac{1}{3}$ was written as:

|  | $= \frac{1}{3}$ |
|---|---|

Special symbols were used for $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and for the non-unit fractions $\tfrac{2}{3}$ and, less frequently, $\tfrac{3}{4}$:

|  | $= \frac{1}{2}$ |  |  | $= \frac{2}{3}$ |  |  | $= \frac{3}{4}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

If the denominator became too large, the "mouth" was just placed over the beginning of the "denominator":

|  | $= \frac{1}{100}$ |
|---|---|

## Hieratic numerals {#Hieratic_numerals}

As administrative and accounting texts were written on [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus) or [ostraca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostracon), rather than being carved into hard stone (as were hieroglyphic texts), the vast majority of texts employing the Egyptian numeral system utilize the [hieratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieratic) script. Instances of numerals written in hieratic can be found as far back as the [Early Dynastic Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Dynastic_Period_of_Egypt). The Old Kingdom [Abusir Papyri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abusir_Papyri) are a particularly important corpus of texts that utilize hieratic numerals.

*Image: A History Of Mathematical Notations Vol I - Egyptian numerals. Hieroglyphic, hieratic, and demotic numeral symbols. (This table, was compiled by Kurt Sethe.).png.* A comparative chart of Egyptian numerals, including hieratic and demotic

Boyer proved 50 years ago that [hieratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieratic) script used a different numeral system, using individual signs for the numbers 1 to 9, multiples of 10 from 10 to 90, the hundreds from 100 to 900, and the thousands from 1000 to 9000. A large number like 9999 could thus be written with only four signs—combining the signs for 9000, 900, 90, and 9—as opposed to 36 hieroglyphs. Boyer saw the new hieratic numerals as ciphered, mapping one number onto one Egyptian letter for the first time in human history. Greeks adopted the new system, mapping their counting numbers onto two of their alphabets, the Doric and Ionian.

In the oldest hieratic texts the individual numerals were clearly written in a ciphered relationship to the Egyptian alphabet. But during the Old Kingdom a series of standardized writings had developed for sign-groups containing more than one numeral, repeated as [Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657) practiced. However, repetition of the same numeral for each place-value was not allowed in the hieratic script. As the hieratic writing system developed over time, these sign-groups were further simplified for quick writing; this process continued into [Demotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demotic_Egyptian), as well.

Two famous mathematical papyri using hieratic script are the [Moscow Mathematical Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Mathematical_Papyrus) and the [Rhind Mathematical Papyrus](wikipedia://en/9210114).
