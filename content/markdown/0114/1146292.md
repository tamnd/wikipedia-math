# Kac–Moody algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kac–Moody algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Kac–Moody algebra** (named for [Victor Kac](wikipedia://en/4697697) and [Robert Moody](wikipedia://en/4311313), who independently and simultaneously discovered them in 1968[^n1]) is a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944), usually infinite-dimensional, that can be defined by generators and relations through a [generalized Cartan matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Cartan_matrix). These algebras form a generalization of finite-dimensional [semisimple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/1337587), and many properties related to the structure of a Lie algebra such as its [root system](wikipedia://en/277087), [irreducible representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_a_Lie_algebra), and connection to [flag manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_manifold) have natural analogues in the Kac–Moody setting.

:::

A class of Kac–Moody algebras called **[affine Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/1350865)** is of particular importance in mathematics and [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), especially [two-dimensional conformal field theory](wikipedia://en/52183913) and the theory of [exactly solvable models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exactly_solvable_model). Kac discovered an elegant proof of certain combinatorial identities, the [Macdonald identities](wikipedia://en/5027268), which is based on the representation theory of affine Kac–Moody algebras. Howard Garland and [James Lepowsky](wikipedia://en/5033025) demonstrated that [Rogers–Ramanujan identities](wikipedia://en/2324711) can be derived in a similar fashion.[^o3]

## History of Kac–Moody algebras {#History_of_Kac–Moody_algebras}

The initial construction by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) and [Wilhelm Killing](wikipedia://en/1531824) of finite dimensional [simple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/59500649) from the [Cartan integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartan_integer) was type dependent. In 1966 [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) showed that relations of [Claude Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040) and [Harish-Chandra](wikipedia://en/373190),[^o5] with simplifications by [Nathan Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Jacobson),[^o6] give a defining presentation for the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944).[^o7] One could thus describe a simple Lie algebra in terms of generators and relations using data from the matrix of Cartan integers, which is naturally [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix).

"Almost simultaneously in 1967, [Victor Kac](wikipedia://en/4697697) in the USSR and [Robert Moody](wikipedia://en/4311313) in Canada developed what was to become Kac–Moody algebra. Kac and Moody noticed that if [Wilhelm Killing](wikipedia://en/1531824)'s conditions were relaxed, it was still possible to associate to the [Cartan matrix](wikipedia://en/1146433) a Lie algebra which, necessarily, would be infinite dimensional." – A. J. Coleman[^o8]

In his 1967 thesis, [Robert Moody](wikipedia://en/4311313) considered Lie algebras whose [Cartan matrix](wikipedia://en/1146433) is no longer positive definite.[^o9][^o10] This still gave rise to a Lie algebra, but one which is now infinite dimensional. Simultaneously, **Z**-[graded Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/5475402) were being studied in Moscow where [I. L. Kantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._L._Kantor) introduced and studied a general class of Lie algebras including what eventually became known as Kac–Moody algebras.[^o11] [Victor Kac](wikipedia://en/4697697) was also studying simple or nearly simple Lie algebras with polynomial growth. A rich mathematical theory of infinite dimensional Lie algebras evolved. An account of the subject, which also includes works of many others is given in (Kac 1990).[^o12] See also (Seligman 1987).[^o13]

## Introduction {#Introduction}

Given an *n*×*n* [generalized Cartan matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Cartan_matrix) $C = \begin{pmatrix} c_{ij}\end{pmatrix}$, one can construct a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}'(C)$ defined by [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set) $e_i$, $h_i$, and $f_i \left(i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}\right)$ and relations given by:

- $\left[h_i, h_j\right] = 0\$ for all $i, j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}$;
- $$\left[h_i, e_j\right] = c_{ij}e_j;$$
- $$\left[h_i, f_j\right] = -c_{ij}f_j;$$
- $\left[e_i, f_j\right] = \delta_{ij}h_i$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta;
- If $i \neq j$ (so $c_{ij} \leq 0$) then $\textrm{ad}(e_i)^{1-c_{ij}}(e_j) = 0$ and $\operatorname{ad}(f_i)^{1-c_{ij}}(f_j) = 0$, where $\operatorname{ad}: \mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g}),\operatorname{ad}(x)(y) = [x, y],$ is the [adjoint representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) of $\mathfrak{g}$.

Under a "symmetrizability" assumption, $\mathfrak{g}'(C)$ identifies with the derived subalgebra $\mathfrak{g}'(C) = [\mathfrak{g}(C), \mathfrak{g}(C)]$ of the affine Kac-Moody algebra $\mathfrak{g}(C)$ defined below.[^o15]

## Definition {#Definition}

Assume we are given an $n \times n$ [generalized Cartan matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Cartan_matrix) *C* = (*c<sub>ij</sub>*) of [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) *r*. For every such $C$, there exists a unique up to isomorphism *realization* of $C$, i.e. a triple $(\mathfrak{h}, \{\alpha_i\}_{i = 1}^n, \{\alpha_i^\vee\}_{i = 1}^n,$) where $\mathfrak{h}$ is a complex vector space, $\{\alpha_i^\vee\}_{i = 1}^n$ is a subset of elements of $\mathfrak{h}$, and $\{\alpha_i\}_{i = 1}^n$ is a subset of the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) $\mathfrak{h}^*$ satisfying the following three conditions:[^o18]

1. The vector space $\mathfrak{h}$ has dimension 2*n* − *r*
1. The sets $\{\alpha_i\}_{i = 1}^n$ and $\{\alpha_i^\vee\}_{i = 1}^n$ are linearly independent and
1. For every $1 \leq i, j \leq n, \alpha_i\left(\alpha_j^\vee\right) = c_{ji}$.

The $\alpha_i$ are analogue to the [simple roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_system_of_a_semi-simple_Lie_algebra) of a semi-simple Lie algebra, and the $\alpha_i^\vee$ to the simple coroots.

Then we define the *Kac-Moody algebra* associated to $C$ as the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} := \mathfrak{g}(C)$ defined by [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set) $e_i$ and $f_i \left(i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}\right)$ and the elements of $\mathfrak{h}$ and relations

- $\left[h, h'\right] = 0\$ for $h,h' \in \mathfrak{h}$;
- $\left[h, e_i\right] = \alpha_i(h)e_i$, for $h \in \mathfrak{h}$;
- $\left[h, f_i\right] = -\alpha_i(h)f_i$, for $h \in \mathfrak{h}$;
- $\left[e_i, f_j\right] = \delta_{ij}\alpha_i^\vee$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta;
- If $i \neq j$ (so $c_{ij} \leq 0$) then $\textrm{ad}(e_i)^{1-c_{ij}}(e_j) = 0$ and $\operatorname{ad}(f_i)^{1-c_{ij}}(f_j) = 0$, where $\operatorname{ad}: \mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g}),\operatorname{ad}(x)(y) = [x, y],$ is the [adjoint representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) of $\mathfrak{g}$.

A [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) (possibly infinite-dimensional) [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) is also considered a Kac–Moody algebra if its [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) is a Kac–Moody algebra.

:::

## Root-space decomposition of a Kac–Moody algebra {#Root-space_decomposition_of_a_Kac–Moody_algebra}

$\mathfrak{h}$ is the analogue of a [Cartan subalgebra](wikipedia://en/1336000) for the Kac–Moody algebra $\mathfrak{g}$.

If $x\neq 0$ is an element of $\mathfrak{g}$ such that

$$\forall h\in\mathfrak{h}, [h, x] = \lambda(h)x$$

for some $\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^*\backslash\{0\}$, then $x$ is called a **root vector** and $\lambda$ is a **root** of $\mathfrak{g}$. (The zero functional is not considered a root by convention.) The set of all roots of $\mathfrak{g}$ is often denoted by $\Delta$ and sometimes by $R$. For a given root $\lambda$, one denotes by $\mathfrak{g}_\lambda$ the **root space** of $\lambda$; that is,

$$\mathfrak{g}_\lambda = \{x\in\mathfrak{g}:\forall h\in\mathfrak{h}, [h,x] = \lambda(h)x\}.$$

It follows from the defining relations of $\mathfrak{g}$ that $e_i\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_i}$ and $f_i\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha_i}$. Also, if $x_1\in\mathfrak{g}_{\lambda_1}$ and $x_2\in\mathfrak{g}_{\lambda_2}$, then $\left[x_1, x_2\right]\in\mathfrak{g}_{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}$ by the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370).

A fundamental result of the theory is that any Kac–Moody algebra can be decomposed into the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of $\mathfrak{h}$ and its root spaces, that is

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Delta} \mathfrak{g}_\lambda,$$

and that every root $\lambda$ can be written as $\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^n z_i\alpha_i$ with all the $z_i$ being [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) of the same [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270).

## Types of Kac–Moody algebras {#Types_of_Kac–Moody_algebras}

Properties of a Kac–Moody algebra are controlled by the algebraic properties of its generalized Cartan matrix *C*. In order to classify Kac–Moody algebras, it is enough to consider the case of an *indecomposable* matrix *C*, that is, assume that there is no decomposition of the set of indices *I* into a disjoint union of non-empty subsets *I*<sub>1</sub> and *I*<sub>2</sub> such that *C*<sub>*ij*</sub> = 0 for all *i* in *I*<sub>1</sub> and *j* in *I*<sub>2</sub>. Any decomposition of the generalized Cartan matrix leads to the direct sum decomposition of the corresponding Kac–Moody algebra:

$$\mathfrak{g}(C) \simeq \mathfrak{g}\left(C_1\right) \oplus \mathfrak{g}\left(C_2\right),$$

where the two Kac–Moody algebras in the right hand side are associated with the submatrices of *C* corresponding to the index sets *I*<sub>1</sub> and *I*<sub>2</sub>.

An important subclass of Kac–Moody algebras corresponds to *[symmetrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetrizable_matrix)* generalized Cartan matrices *C*, which can be decomposed as *DS*, where *D* is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) with positive integer entries and *S* is a [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474). Under the assumptions that *C* is symmetrizable and indecomposable, the Kac–Moody algebras are divided into three classes:

- A [positive definite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_matrix) *S* gives rise to a finite-dimensional [simple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/59500649).
- A [positive semidefinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrix) *S* gives rise to an infinite-dimensional Kac–Moody algebra of **affine type**, or an [affine Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1350865).
- An [indefinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_matrix) *S* gives rise to a Kac–Moody algebra of **indefinite type**.
- Since the diagonal entries of *C* and *D* are positive, *S* cannot be [negative definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_definite_matrix) or negative semidefinite.

Symmetrizable indecomposable generalized Cartan matrices of finite and affine type have been completely classified. They correspond to [Dynkin diagrams](wikipedia://en/277109) and [affine Dynkin diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_Dynkin_diagram). Little is known about the Kac–Moody algebras of indefinite type, although the groups corresponding to these Kac–Moody algebras were constructed over arbitrary fields by [Jacques Tits](wikipedia://en/219570).[^o21]

Among the Kac–Moody algebras of indefinite type, most work has focused on those **hyperbolic type**, for which the matrix *S* is indefinite, but for each proper subset of *I*, the corresponding submatrix is positive definite or positive semidefinite. Hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebras have rank at most 10, and they have been completely classified.[^o22] There are infinitely many of rank 2, and [238 of ranks between 3 and 10](wikipedia://en/277109#238_Hyperbolic_groups_%28compact_and_noncompact%29).
