# LOBPCG

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="LOBPCG"}

**Locally Optimal Block Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient** (**LOBPCG**) is a [matrix-free method](wikipedia://en/8515187) for finding the largest (or smallest) [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) and the corresponding [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvectors) of a symmetric [generalized eigenvalue problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenvalue_problem)

:::

$$A x = \lambda B x,$$

for a given pair $(A, B)$ of complex [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) or real [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474) matrices, where the matrix $B$ is also assumed [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix).

## Background {#Background}

[Kantorovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantorovich) in 1948 proposed calculating the smallest [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) $\lambda_1$ of a symmetric matrix $A$ by [steepest descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steepest_descent) using a direction $r = Ax-\lambda (x) x$ of a scaled [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of a [Rayleigh quotient](wikipedia://en/306343) $\lambda(x) = \langle x, Ax \rangle/\langle x, x\rangle$ in a [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product) $\langle x, y \rangle = x^\mathsf{T} y$, with the step size computed by minimizing the Rayleigh quotient in the [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of the vectors $x$ and $r$, i.e. in a locally optimal manner. Samokish[^o3] proposed applying a [preconditioner](wikipedia://en/5165246) $T$ to the residual vector $r$ to generate the preconditioned direction $w = T r$ and derived asymptotic, as $x$ approaches the [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector), convergence rate bounds. [D'yakonov](wikipedia://en/26950041) suggested[^o4] spectrally equivalent [preconditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preconditioning) and derived non-asymptotic convergence rate bounds. Block locally optimal multi-step steepest descent for eigenvalue problems was described in.[^o5] Local minimization of the Rayleigh quotient on the subspace spanned by the current approximation, the current residual and the previous approximation, as well as its block version, appeared in.[^o6] The preconditioned version was analyzed in [^o7] and.[^o8]

## Main features {#Main_features}

Source:[^o10]

- [Matrix-free](wikipedia://en/8515187), i.e. does not require storing the coefficient matrix explicitly, but can access the matrix by evaluating matrix-vector products.
- [Factorization](wikipedia://en/82341)-free, i.e. does not require any [matrix decomposition](wikipedia://en/253873) even for a [generalized eigenvalue problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenvalue_problem).
- The costs per iteration and the memory use are competitive with those of the [Lanczos method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanczos_method), computing a single extreme eigenpair of a symmetric matrix.
- Linear convergence is theoretically guaranteed and practically observed.
- Accelerated convergence due to direct [preconditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preconditioning), in contrast to the [Lanczos method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanczos_method), including variable and non-symmetric as well as fixed and positive definite [preconditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preconditioning).
- Allows trivial incorporation of efficient [domain decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_decomposition) and [multigrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multigrid) techniques via preconditioning.
- Warm starts and computes an approximation to the eigenvector on every iteration.
- More numerically stable compared to the [Lanczos method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanczos_method), and can operate in low-precision computer arithmetic.
- Easy to implement, with many versions already appeared.
- Blocking allows utilizing highly efficient matrix-matrix operations, e.g., [BLAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLAS) 3.
- The block size can be tuned to balance convergence speed vs. computer costs of orthogonalizations and the [Rayleigh-Ritz method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh-Ritz_method) on every iteration.

## Algorithm {#Algorithm}

### Single-vector version {#Single-vector_version}

#### Preliminaries: [Gradient descent](wikipedia://en/201489) for eigenvalue problems {#Preliminaries:_Gradient_descent_for_eigenvalue_problems}

The method performs an [iterative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterative) maximization (or minimization) of the generalized [Rayleigh quotient](wikipedia://en/306343)

$$\rho(x) := \rho(A,B; x) :=\frac{x^\mathsf{T} A x}{x^\mathsf{T} B x},$$

which results in finding largest (or smallest) eigenpairs of $A x = \lambda B x.$

The direction of the steepest ascent, which is the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461), of the generalized [Rayleigh quotient](wikipedia://en/306343) is positively proportional to the vector

$$r := Ax - \rho(x) Bx,$$

called the eigenvector [residual](wikipedia://en/6773580). If a [preconditioner](wikipedia://en/5165246) $T$ is available, it is applied to the residual and gives the vector

$$w := Tr,$$

called the preconditioned residual. Without preconditioning, we set $T := I$ and so $w := r$. An iterative method

$$x^{i+1} := x^i + \alpha^i T \left(Ax^i - \rho(x^i) Bx^i\right),$$

or, in short,

$$\begin{aligned} x^{i+1} &:= x^i + \alpha^i w^i,\, \\ w^i &:= Tr^i,\, \\ r^i &:= Ax^i - \rho(x^i) Bx^i, \end{aligned}$$

is known as preconditioned [steepest ascent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steepest_ascent) (or descent), where the scalar $\alpha^i$ is called the step size. The optimal step size can be determined by maximizing the Rayleigh quotient, i.e.,

$$x^{i+1} := \arg\max_{y\in \operatorname{span}\{x^i,w^i\}} \rho(y)$$

(or $\arg\min$ in case of minimizing), in which case the method is called locally optimal.

#### Three-term recurrence {#Three-term_recurrence}

To dramatically accelerate the convergence of the locally optimal preconditioned steepest ascent (or descent), one extra vector can be added to the two-term [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) to make it three-term:

$$x^{i+1} := \arg\max_{y\in \operatorname{span}\{x^i,w^i,x^{i-1}\}} \rho(y)$$

(use $\arg\min$ in case of minimizing). The maximization/minimization of the Rayleigh quotient in a 3-dimensional subspace can be performed numerically by the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](wikipedia://en/2111994). Adding more vectors, see, e.g., [Richardson extrapolation](wikipedia://en/650022), does not result in significant acceleration[^o16] but increases computation costs, so is not generally recommended.

#### Numerical stability improvements {#Numerical_stability_improvements}

As the iterations converge, the vectors $x^i$ and $x^{i-1}$ become nearly [linearly dependent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_dependent), resulting in a precision loss and making the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](wikipedia://en/2111994) numerically unstable in the presence of round-off errors. The loss of precision may be avoided by substituting the vector $x^{i-1}$ with a vector $p^i$, that may be further away from $x^{i}$, in the basis of the three-dimensional subspace $\operatorname{span}\{x^i, w^i, x^{i-1}\}$, while keeping the subspace unchanged and avoiding [orthogonalization](wikipedia://en/201611) or any other extra operations.[^o16] Furthermore, orthogonalizing the basis of the three-dimensional subspace may be needed for [ill-conditioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ill-conditioned) eigenvalue problems to improve stability and attainable accuracy.

#### Krylov subspace analogs {#Krylov_subspace_analogs}

This is a single-vector version of the LOBPCG method—one of possible generalization of the [preconditioned](wikipedia://en/5165246) [conjugate gradient](wikipedia://en/1448821) linear solvers to the case of symmetric [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) problems.[^o16] Even in the trivial case $T=I$ and $B=I$ the resulting approximation with $i>3$ will be different from that obtained by the [Lanczos algorithm](wikipedia://en/2593852), although both approximations will belong to the same [Krylov subspace](wikipedia://en/2542615).

#### Practical use scenarios {#Practical_use_scenarios}

Extreme simplicity and high efficiency of the single-vector version of LOBPCG make it attractive for eigenvalue-related applications under severe hardware limitations, ranging from [spectral clustering](wikipedia://en/13651683) based real-time [anomaly detection](wikipedia://en/8190902) via [graph partitioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_partitioning) on embedded [ASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASIC) or [FPGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FPGA) to modelling physical phenomena of record computing complexity on exascale [TOP500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TOP500) supercomputers.

### Block version {#Block_version}

#### Summary {#Summary}

Subsequent eigenpairs can be computed one-by-one via single-vector LOBPCG supplemented with an orthogonal deflation or simultaneously as a block. In the former approach, imprecisions in already computed approximate eigenvectors additively affect the accuracy of the subsequently computed eigenvectors, thus increasing the error with every new computation. Iterating several approximate [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvectors) together in a block in a locally optimal fashion in the block version of the LOBPCG[^o16] allows fast, accurate, and robust computation of eigenvectors, including those corresponding to nearly-multiple eigenvalues where the single-vector LOBPCG suffers from slow convergence. The block size can be tuned to balance numerical stability vs. convergence speed vs. computer costs of orthogonalizations and the Rayleigh-Ritz method on every iteration.

#### Core design {#Core_design}

The block approach in LOBPCG replaces single-vectors $x^{i},\, w^{i},$ and $p^{i}$ with block-vectors, i.e. matrices $X^{i},\, W^{i},$ and $P^{i}$, where, e.g., every column of $X^{i}$ approximates one of the eigenvectors. All columns are iterated simultaneously, and the next matrix of approximate eigenvectors $X^{i+1}$ is determined by the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](wikipedia://en/2111994) on the subspace spanned by all columns of matrices $X^{i},\, W^{i},$ and $P^{i}$. Each column of $W^{i}$ is computed simply as the preconditioned residual for every column of $X^{i}.$ The matrix $P^{i}$ is determined such that the subspaces spanned by the columns of $[X^{i},\, P^{i}]$ and of $[X^{i},\, X^{i-1}]$ are the same.

#### Numerical stability vs. efficiency {#Numerical_stability_vs._efficiency}

The outcome of the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](wikipedia://en/2111994) is determined by the subspace spanned by all columns of matrices $X^{i},\, W^{i},$ and $P^{i}$, where a basis of the subspace can theoretically be arbitrary. However, in inexact computer arithmetic the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](wikipedia://en/2111994) becomes numerically unstable if some of the basis vectors are approximately linearly dependent. Numerical instabilities typically occur, e.g., if some of the eigenvectors in the iterative block already reach attainable accuracy for a given computer precision and are especially prominent in low precision, e.g., [single precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_precision).

The art of multiple different implementation of LOBPCG is to ensure numerical stability of the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](wikipedia://en/2111994) at minimal computing costs by choosing a good basis of the subspace. The arguably most stable approach of making the basis vectors orthogonal, e.g., by the [Gram–Schmidt process](wikipedia://en/82361), is also the most computational expensive. For example, LOBPCG implementations,[^o24][^o25] utilize unstable but efficient [Cholesky decomposition](wikipedia://en/134433) of the [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/1651906#Normal_matrix), which is performed only on individual matrices $W^{i}$ and $P^{i}$, rather than on the whole subspace. The constantly increasing amount of computer memory allows typical block sizes nowadays in the $10^3-10^4$ range, where the percentage of compute time spend on orthogonalizations and the Rayleigh-Ritz method starts dominating.

#### Locking of previously converged eigenvectors {#Locking_of_previously_converged_eigenvectors}

Block methods for eigenvalue problems that iterate subspaces commonly have some of the iterative eigenvectors converged faster than others that motivates locking the already converged eigenvectors, i.e., removing them from the iterative loop, in order to eliminate unnecessary computations and improve numerical stability. A simple removal of an eigenvector may likely result in forming its duplicate in still iterating vectors. The fact that the eigenvectors of symmetric eigenvalue problems are pair-wise orthogonal suggest keeping all iterative vectors orthogonal to the locked vectors.

Locking can be implemented differently maintaining numerical accuracy and stability while minimizing the compute costs. For example, LOBPCG implementations,[^o24][^o25] follow,[^o16][^o27] separating hard locking, i.e. a deflation by restriction, where the locked eigenvectors serve as a code input and do not change, from soft locking, where the locked vectors do not participate in the typically most expensive iterative step of computing the residuals, however, fully participate in the Rayleigh—Ritz method and thus are allowed to be changed by the Rayleigh—Ritz method.

#### Modifications, LOBPCG II {#Modifications,_LOBPCG_II}

LOBPCG includes all columns of matrices $X^{i},\, W^{i},$ and $P^{i}$ into the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](wikipedia://en/2111994) resulting in an up to $3k$-by-$3k$ eigenvalue problem needed to solve and up to $9k^2$ [dot products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_products) to compute at every iteration, where $k$ denotes the block size — the number of columns. For large block sizes $k$ this starts dominating compute and I/O costs and limiting parallelization, where multiple compute devices are running simultaneously.

The original LOBPCG paper[^o16] describes a modification, called LOBPCG II, to address such a problem running the single-vector version of the LOBPCG method for each desired eigenpair with the Rayleigh-Ritz procedure solving $k$ of 3-by-3 projected eigenvalue problems. The global Rayleigh-Ritz procedure for all $k$ eigenpairs is on every iteration but only on the columns of the matrix $X^{i}$, thus reducing the number of the necessary [dot products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dot_products) to $k^2+3k$ from $9k^2$ and the size of the global projected eigenvalue problem to $k$-by-$k$ from $3k$-by-$3k$ on every iteration. Reference [^o29] goes further applying the LOBPCG algorithm to each approximate eigenvector separately, i.e., running the unblocked version of the LOBPCG method for each desired eigenpair for a fixed number of iterations. The Rayleigh-Ritz procedures in these runs only need to solve a set of 3 × 3 projected eigenvalue problems. The global Rayleigh-Ritz procedure for all desired eigenpairs is only applied periodically at the end of a fixed number of unblocked LOBPCG iterations.

Such modifications may be less robust compared to the original LOBPCG. Individually running branches of the single-vector LOBPCG may not follow continuous iterative paths flipping instead and creating duplicated approximations to the same eigenvector. The single-vector LOBPCG may be unsuitable for clustered eigenvalues, but separate small-block LOBPCG runs require determining their block sizes automatically during the process of iterations since the number of the clusters of eigenvalues and their sizes may be unknown a priori.

## Convergence theory and practice {#Convergence_theory_and_practice}

LOBPCG by construction is guaranteed[^o16] to minimize the [Rayleigh quotient](wikipedia://en/306343) not slower than the block steepest [gradient descent](wikipedia://en/201489), which has a comprehensive convergence theory. Every [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) is a stationary point of the [Rayleigh quotient](wikipedia://en/306343), where the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) vanishes. Thus, the [gradient descent](wikipedia://en/201489) may slow down in a vicinity of any [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector), however, it is guaranteed to either converge to the eigenvector with a linear convergence rate or, if this eigenvector is a [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249), the iterative [Rayleigh quotient](wikipedia://en/306343) is more likely to drop down below the corresponding eigenvalue and start converging linearly to the next eigenvalue below. The worst value of the linear convergence rate has been determined[^o16] and depends on the relative gap between the eigenvalue and the rest of the matrix [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum) and the quality of the [preconditioner](wikipedia://en/5165246), if present.

For a general matrix, there is evidently no way to predict the eigenvectors and thus generate the initial approximations that always work well. The iterative solution by LOBPCG may be sensitive to the initial eigenvectors approximations, e.g., taking longer to converge slowing down as passing intermediate eigenpairs. Moreover, in theory, one cannot guarantee convergence necessarily to the smallest eigenpair, although the probability of the miss is zero. A good quality [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) [Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian) function with the zero [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) is commonly the default in LOBPCG to generate the initial approximations. To fix the initial approximations, one can select a fixed seed for the [random number generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_number_generator).

In contrast to the [Lanczos method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanczos_method), LOBPCG rarely exhibits asymptotic [superlinear convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superlinear_convergence) in practice.

## Partial [Principal component analysis](wikipedia://en/76340) (PCA) and [Singular Value Decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_Value_Decomposition) (SVD) {#Partial_Principal_component_analysis_(PCA)_and_Singular_Value_Decomposition_(SVD)}

LOBPCG can be trivially adapted for computing several largest [singular values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_values) and the corresponding singular vectors (partial SVD), e.g., for [iterative computation of PCA](wikipedia://en/76340#Iterative_computation), for a data matrix $D$ with zero mean, without explicitly computing the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) matrix $D^{T}D$, i.e. in [matrix-free fashion](wikipedia://en/8515187). The main calculation is evaluation of a function of the product $D^{T}(D X)$ of the covariance matrix $D^{T}D$ and the block-vector $X$ that iteratively approximates the desired singular vectors. PCA needs the largest eigenvalues of the covariance matrix, while LOBPCG is typically implemented to calculate the smallest ones. A simple work-around is to negate the function, substituting $-D^{T}(D X)$ for $D^{T}(D X)$ and thus reversing the order of the eigenvalues, since LOBPCG does not care if the matrix of the eigenvalue problem is positive definite or not.[^o24]

LOBPCG for PCA and SVD is implemented in SciPy since revision 1.4.0[^o32]

## General software implementations {#General_software_implementations}

LOBPCG's inventor, [Andrew Knyazev](wikipedia://en/26948388), published a reference implementation called Block Locally Optimal Preconditioned Eigenvalue Xolvers (BLOPEX)[^o34][^o35] with interfaces to [PETSc](wikipedia://en/5161497), [hypre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypre), and Parallel Hierarchical Adaptive MultiLevel method (PHAML).[^o36] Other implementations are available in, e.g., [GNU Octave](wikipedia://en/48707),[^o37] [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412) (including for distributed or tiling arrays),[^o24] [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29),[^o38] Anasazi ([Trilinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trilinos)),[^o39] [SLEPc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLEPc),[^o40][^o41] [SciPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciPy),[^o25] [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29),[^o42] MAGMA,[^o43] [Pytorch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pytorch),[^o44] [Rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_%28programming_language%29),[^o45] [OpenMP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenMP) and [OpenACC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenACC),[^o46] [CuPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CuPy) (A [NumPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NumPy)-compatible array library accelerated by [CUDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CUDA)),[^o47] [Google JAX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_JAX),[^o48] and [NVIDIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NVIDIA) AMGX.[^o49] LOBPCG is implemented,[^o50] but not included, in [TensorFlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TensorFlow).

## Applications {#Applications}

### [Data mining](wikipedia://en/42253) {#Data_mining}

Software packages [scikit-learn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scikit-learn) and Megaman[^o54] use LOBPCG to scale [spectral clustering](wikipedia://en/13651683)[^o55] and [manifold learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_learning)[^o56] via [Laplacian eigenmaps](wikipedia://en/309261#Laplacian_eigenmaps) to large data sets. [NVIDIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NVIDIA) has implemented[^o57] LOBPCG in its nvGRAPH library introduced in [CUDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CUDA) 8. Sphynx,[^o58] a hybrid distributed- and shared-memory-enabled parallel graph partitioner - the first graph partitioning tool that works on GPUs on distributed-memory settings - uses [spectral clustering](wikipedia://en/13651683) for [graph partitioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_partitioning), computing eigenvectors on the [Laplacian matrix](wikipedia://en/1448472) of the graph using LOBPCG from the [Anasazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anasazi) package.

### [Material sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_sciences) {#Material_sciences}

LOBPCG is implemented in [ABINIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABINIT)[^o60] (including [CUDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CUDA) version) and [Octopus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octopus_%28software%29).[^o61] It has been used for multi-billion size matrices by [Gordon Bell Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Bell_Prize) finalists, on the [Earth Simulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_Simulator) [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer) in Japan.[^o62][^o63] [Hubbard model](wikipedia://en/1702398) for strongly-correlated electron systems to understand the mechanism behind the [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity) uses LOBPCG to calculate the [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state) of the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) on the [K computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K_computer)[^o64] and multi-GPU systems.[^o65]

There are [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412)[^o66] and [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29)[^o67][^o68] versions of LOBPCG for [Kohn-Sham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohn-Sham) equations and [density functional theory](wikipedia://en/209874) (DFT) using the plane wave basis. Recent implementations include TTPY,[^o69] Platypus‐QM,[^o70] MFDn,[^o71] ACE-Molecule,[^o72] LACONIC.[^o73]

### [Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) and [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) {#Mechanics_and_fluids}

LOBPCG from BLOPEX is used for [preconditioner](wikipedia://en/5165246) setup in Multilevel [Balancing Domain Decomposition by Constraints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balancing_Domain_Decomposition_by_Constraints) (BDDC) solver library BDDCML, which is incorporated into OpenFTL (Open [Finite element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element) Template Library) and Flow123d simulator of underground water flow, solute and [heat transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_transport) in fractured [porous media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porous_media). LOBPCG has been implemented[^o75] in [LS-DYNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LS-DYNA) and indirectly in [ANSYS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ANSYS).[^o76]

### [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) {#Maxwell's_equations}

LOBPCG is one of core eigenvalue solvers in PYFEMax and high performance multiphysics [finite element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element) software Netgen/NGSolve. LOBPCG from [hypre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypre) is incorporated into [open source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_source) lightweight scalable [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++) library for [finite element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element) methods [MFEM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MFEM), which is used in many projects, including [BLAST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLAST_%28biotechnology%29), XBraid, [VisIt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VisIt), xSDK, the FASTMath institute in [SciDAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciDAC), and the co-design Center for Efficient Exascale Discretizations (CEED) in the [Exascale computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exascale_computing) Project.

### [Denoising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denoising) {#Denoising}

Iterative LOBPCG-based approximate [low-pass filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pass_filter) can be used for [denoising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denoising); see,[^o79] e.g., to accelerate [total variation denoising](wikipedia://en/26792343).

### [Image segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_segmentation) {#Image_segmentation}

[Image segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_segmentation) via [spectral clustering](wikipedia://en/13651683) performs a low-dimension [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) using an [affinity](wikipedia://en/38449) matrix between pixels, followed by clustering of the components of the eigenvectors in the low dimensional space, e.g., using the [graph Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_Laplacian) for the [bilateral filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_filter). [Image segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_segmentation) via spectral [graph partitioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_partitioning) by LOBPCG with [multigrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multigrid) [preconditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preconditioning) has been first proposed in [^o81] and actually tested in [^o82] and.[^o83] The latter approach has been later implemented in Python [scikit-learn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scikit-learn)[^o84] that uses LOBPCG from [SciPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciPy) with [algebraic multigrid preconditioning](wikipedia://en/1747763#Algebraic_multigrid_%28AMG%29) for solving the eigenvalue problem for the graph Laplacian.
