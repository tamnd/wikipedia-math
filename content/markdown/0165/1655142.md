# Graph property

*Image: 6n-graf.svg.* An example graph, with the properties of being [planar](wikipedia://en/24314) and being [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492), and with order 6, size 7, [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) 3, [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) 3, [vertex connectivity](wikipedia://en/1614492) 1, and [degree sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_sequence) $<3, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1>$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Graph property"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **graph property** or **graph invariant** is a property of [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) that depends only on the abstract structure, not on graph representations such as particular [labellings](wikipedia://en/1609861) or [drawings](wikipedia://en/325813) of the graph.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

While graph drawing and graph representation are valid topics in graph theory, in order to focus only on the abstract structure of graphs, a **graph property** is defined to be a property preserved under all possible [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/247577) of a graph. In other words, it is a property of the graph itself, not of a specific drawing or representation of the graph. Informally, the term "graph invariant" is used for properties expressed quantitatively, while "property" usually refers to descriptive characterizations of graphs. For example, the statement "graph does not have vertices of degree 1" is a "property" while "the number of vertices of degree 1 in a graph" is an "invariant".

More formally, a graph property is a class of graphs with the property that any two [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) graphs either both belong to the class, or both do not belong to it.[^o2] Equivalently, a graph property may be formalized using the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the class, a function from graphs to Boolean values that is true for graphs in the class and false otherwise; again, any two isomorphic graphs must have the same function value as each other. A graph invariant or graph parameter may similarly be formalized as a function from graphs to a broader class of values, such as integers, [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), sequences of numbers, or [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), that again has the same value for any two isomorphic graphs.[^o5]

:::

## Properties of properties {#Properties_of_properties}

Many graph properties are well-behaved with respect to certain natural [partial orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) or [preorders](wikipedia://en/23582) defined on graphs:

- A graph property *P* is *[hereditary](wikipedia://en/13552465)* if every [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) of a graph with property *P* also has property *P*. For instance, being a [perfect graph](wikipedia://en/670531) or being a [chordal graph](wikipedia://en/744165) are hereditary properties.[^o2]
- A graph property is *[monotone](wikipedia://en/13552465)* if every [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraphs) of a graph with property *P* also has property *P*. For instance, being a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) or being a [triangle-free graph](wikipedia://en/7667996) is monotone. Every monotone property is hereditary, but not necessarily vice versa; for instance, subgraphs of chordal graphs are not necessarily chordal, so being a chordal graph is not monotone.[^o2]
- A graph property is *[minor-closed](wikipedia://en/13552465)* if every [graph minor](wikipedia://en/353042) of a graph with property *P* also has property *P*. For instance, being a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is minor-closed. Every minor-closed property is monotone, but not necessarily vice versa; for instance, minors of triangle-free graphs are not necessarily themselves triangle-free.[^o2]

These definitions may be extended from properties to numerical invariants of graphs: a graph invariant is hereditary, monotone, or minor-closed if the function formalizing the invariant forms a [monotonic function](wikipedia://en/48260) from the corresponding partial order on graphs to the real numbers.

Additionally, graph invariants have been studied with respect to their behavior with regard to [disjoint unions](wikipedia://en/188946) of graphs:

- A graph invariant is *additive* if, for all two graphs *G* and *H*, the value of the invariant on the disjoint union of *G* and *H* is the sum of the values on *G* and on *H*. For instance, the number of vertices is additive.[^o2]
- A graph invariant is *multiplicative* if, for all two graphs *G* and *H*, the value of the invariant on the disjoint union of *G* and *H* is the product of the values on *G* and on *H*. For instance, the [Hosoya index](wikipedia://en/14221581) (number of matchings) is multiplicative.[^o2]
- A graph invariant is *maxing* if, for all two graphs *G* and *H*, the value of the invariant on the disjoint union of *G* and *H* is the maximum of the values on *G* and on *H*. For instance, the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) is maxing.[^o2]

In addition, graph properties can be classified according to the type of graph they describe: whether the graph is [undirected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) or [directed](wikipedia://en/19721986), whether the property applies to [multigraphs](wikipedia://en/1774970), etc.[^o2]

## Values of invariants {#Values_of_invariants}

The [target set](wikipedia://en/50264) of a function that defines a graph invariant may be one of:

- A truth-value, true or false, for the indicator function of a graph property.
- An integer, such as the number of vertices or chromatic number of a graph.
- A [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), such as the [fractional chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_chromatic_number) of a graph.
- A sequence of integers, such as the [degree sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_sequence) of a graph.
- A [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000), such as the [Tutte polynomial](wikipedia://en/3892303) of a graph.

## Graph invariants and graph isomorphism {#Graph_invariants_and_graph_isomorphism}

Easily computable graph invariants are instrumental for fast recognition of [graph isomorphism](wikipedia://en/247577), or rather non-isomorphism, since for any invariant at all, two graphs with different values cannot (by definition) be isomorphic. Two graphs with the same invariants may or may not be isomorphic, however.

A graph invariant *I*(*G*) is called **complete** if the identity of the invariants *I*(*G*) and *I*(*H*) implies the isomorphism of the graphs *G* and *H*. Finding an efficiently computable such invariant (the problem of [graph canonization](wikipedia://en/20199287)) would imply an easy solution to the challenging [graph isomorphism problem](wikipedia://en/1950766). However, even polynomial-valued invariants such as the [chromatic polynomial](wikipedia://en/1460126) are not usually complete. The [claw graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claw_%28graph_theory%29) and the [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190) on 4 vertices both have the same chromatic polynomial, for example.

### Integer invariants {#Integer_invariants}

- [Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_%28graph_theory%29), the number of vertices
- [Size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Size_%28graph_theory%29), the number of edges
- Number of [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29)
- [Circuit rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_rank), a linear combination of the numbers of edges, vertices, and components
- [diameter](wikipedia://en/1020021), the longest of the shortest [path](wikipedia://en/638889) lengths between pairs of vertices
- [girth](wikipedia://en/12013), the length of the shortest cycle
- [Vertex connectivity](wikipedia://en/1614492), the smallest number of vertices whose removal disconnects the graph
- [Edge connectivity](wikipedia://en/4593774), the smallest number of edges whose removal disconnects the graph
- [Chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number), the smallest number of colors for the vertices in a proper coloring
- [Chromatic index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_index), the smallest number of colors for the edges in a proper edge coloring
- [Choosability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choosability) (or **list chromatic number**), the least number k such that G is [k-choosable](wikipedia://en/690669)
- [Independence number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_number), the largest size of an independent set of vertices
- [Clique number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique_number), the largest order of a complete subgraph
- [Arboricity](wikipedia://en/2476311)
- [Graph genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_genus)
- [Pagenumber](wikipedia://en/3987703)
- [Hosoya index](wikipedia://en/14221581)
- [Wiener index](wikipedia://en/14223173)
- [Colin de Verdière graph invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_de_Verdière_graph_invariant)
- [Boxicity](wikipedia://en/11167471)
