# Wheel graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wheel graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **wheel graph** is a graph formed by connecting a single [universal vertex](wikipedia://en/51352780) to all vertices of a [cycle](wikipedia://en/878962). A wheel graph with $n$ vertices can also be defined as the 1-[skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton_%28topology%29) of an ($n - 1$)-gonal [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045).

:::

Some authors write $W_{n}$ to denote a wheel graph with $n$ vertices ($n \ge 4$); other authors[^o4] instead use $W_{n}$ to denote a wheel graph with $n + 1$ vertices ($n \ge 3$), which is formed by connecting a single vertex to all vertices of a cycle of length $n$. The former notation is used in the rest of this article and in the table on the right.

## Edge Set {#Edge_Set}

{{1, 2}, {1, 3}, …, {1, v}, {2, 3}, {3, 4}, …, {v − 1, v}, {v, 2}}[^o6] would be the edge set of a wheel graph with vertex set {1, 2, …, v} in which the vertex 1 is a [universal vertex](wikipedia://en/51352780).

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Wheel graphs are [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), and have a unique planar embedding. More specifically, every wheel graph is a [Halin graph](wikipedia://en/19658548). They are self-dual: the [planar dual](wikipedia://en/2536864) of any wheel graph is an [isomorphic graph](wikipedia://en/247577). Every maximal planar graph, other than *K*<sub>4</sub> = *W*<sub>4</sub>, contains as a subgraph either *W*<sub>5</sub> or *W*<sub>6</sub>.

There is always a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) in the wheel graph and there are $n^2-3n+3$ cycles in *W*<sub>*n*</sub> .

*Image: CyclesW4.svg.* The 7 cycles of the wheel graph *W*<sub>4</sub>.

For odd values of *n*, *W*<sub>*n*</sub> is a [perfect graph](wikipedia://en/670531) with [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) 3: the vertices of the cycle can be given two colors, and the center vertex given a third color. For even *n*, *W*<sub>*n*</sub> has [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) 4, and (when *n* ≥ 6) is not perfect. *W*<sub>7</sub> is the only wheel graph that is a [unit distance graph](wikipedia://en/7158665) in the Euclidean plane.[^o9]

The [chromatic polynomial](wikipedia://en/1460126) of the wheel graph *W<sub>n</sub>* is :

$$P_{W_n}(x) = x((x - 2)^{(n - 1)} - (-1)^n(x - 2)).$$

In [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) theory, two particularly important special classes of matroids are the *wheel matroids* and the *whirl matroids*, both derived from wheel graphs. The *k*-wheel matroid is the [graphic matroid](wikipedia://en/745714) of a wheel *W*<sub>*k+1*</sub>, while the *k*-whirl matroid is derived from the *k*-wheel by considering the outer cycle of the wheel, as well as all of its [spanning trees](wikipedia://en/455770), to be independent.

The wheel *W*<sub>6</sub> supplied a counterexample to a conjecture of [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) on [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175): he had conjectured that the complete graph has the smallest Ramsey number among all graphs with the same chromatic number, but Faudree and McKay (1993) showed *W*<sub>6</sub> has Ramsey number 17 while the complete graph with the same chromatic number, *K*<sub>4</sub>, has Ramsey number 18.[^o10] That is, for every 17-vertex graph *G*, either *G* or its complement contains *W*<sub>6</sub> as a subgraph, while neither the 17-vertex [Paley graph](wikipedia://en/5719307) nor its complement contains a copy of *K*<sub>4</sub>.
