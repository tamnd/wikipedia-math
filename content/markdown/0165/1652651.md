# Michael Aschbacher

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Michael Aschbacher"}

**Michael George Aschbacher** (born April 8, 1944) is an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) best known for his work on [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707). He was a leading figure in the completion of the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445) in the 1970s and 1980s. It later turned out that the classification was incomplete, because the case of [quasithin groups](wikipedia://en/12939181) had not been finished. This gap was fixed by Aschbacher and Stephen D. Smith in 2004, in a pair of books comprising about 1300 pages. Aschbacher is currently the Shaler Arthur Hanisch Professor of Mathematics at the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology).

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Aschbacher received his B.S. at the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology) in 1966 and his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D.) at the [University of Wisconsin–Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison) in 1969. He joined the faculty of the California Institute of Technology in 1970 and became a full professor in 1976. He was a visiting scholar at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in 1978–79.[^o5] He was awarded the [Cole Prize](wikipedia://en/1372177) in 1980, and was elected to the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1990. In 1992, Aschbacher was elected a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences).[^o6] He was awarded the [Rolf Schock Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Schock_Prize) for Mathematics by the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences) in 2011.[^o7] In 2012 he received the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) for Mathematical Exposition and the [Wolf Prize in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/714278), and became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o8]

## Classification of finite simple groups {#Classification_of_finite_simple_groups}

In 1973, Aschbacher became a leading figure in the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). Aschbacher considered himself somewhat of an outsider in the world of conventional group theory, claiming that he was not "plugged into the system at that point in time".[^o10] Although he had access to several preprints that were shared among the practitioners of the field, he reproduced many proofs that had already been discovered by other researchers and published them in his early papers. Aschbacher only became interested in [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_groups) as a postdoctorate. He wrote his dissertation in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and was able to utilize many techniques developed in this area to make early contributions to the study of [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_groups), which surprised the community of researchers. In particular, [Daniel Gorenstein](wikipedia://en/1569478), another leader of the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445), said that Aschbacher's entrance was "dramatic".[^o11]

While Aschbacher's ideas were held in esteem by the group theory community, his writing style drew complaints. Some commented that his proofs lacked explanations of very sophisticated counting arguments. The difficulty in reading his papers became more pronounced as the papers became longer, with even some of his coauthors finding their joint papers hard to read. The challenge in understanding Aschbacher's proofs was attributed not to a lack of ability, but rather to the complexity of the ideas he was able to produce. This was part of a general trend where researchers working on the Classification of Finite Simple Groups began to view papers as being reliable because of the reputation of their authors and their previous work, rather than as self-contained documents. As a result, responsibility of finding errors in the classification problem was up to the entire community of researchers rather than just peer-reviewers alone.[^o12]
