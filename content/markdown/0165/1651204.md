# Locally compact group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Locally compact group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **locally compact group** is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) *G* for which the underlying topology is [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) and [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637). Locally compact groups are important because many examples of groups that arise throughout mathematics are locally compact and such groups have a natural [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) called the [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645). This allows one to define [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) of [Borel measurable](wikipedia://en/4322) functions on *G* so that standard analysis notions such as the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) and [$L^p$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) can be generalized.

:::

Many of the results of [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) [representation theory](wikipedia://en/12695) are proved by averaging over the group. For compact groups, modifications of these proofs yields similar results by averaging with respect to the normalized [Haar integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haar_integral). In the general locally compact setting, such techniques need not hold. The resulting theory is a central part of [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147). The representation theory for [locally compact abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454325) is described by [Pontryagin duality](wikipedia://en/366136).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples and counterexamples"}

## Examples and counterexamples {#Examples_and_counterexamples}

- Any [compact group](wikipedia://en/750326) is locally compact.
  - In particular the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) *S*<sup>1</sup> of complex numbers of unit modulus under multiplication is compact, and therefore locally compact. The circle group historically served as the first topologically nontrivial group to also have the property of local compactness, and as such motivated the search for the more general theory, presented here.
- Any [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) is locally compact. The theory of locally compact groups therefore encompasses the theory of ordinary groups since any group becomes a topological group when given the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology).
- The additive groups of the real numbers **R** and of the complex numbers **C**, if given their standard topologies, are locally compact, as are the multiplicative groups of non-zero numbers **R**<sup>x</sup> and **C**<sup>x</sup>.
- [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), which are locally Euclidean, are locally compact. Here we find many examples of non-abelian locally compact groups.
- A normable [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) over a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) is locally compact if and only if it is [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-dimensional).
- The additive group of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q** is not locally compact if given its standard topology, the [relative topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_topology) as a subset of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). It is locally compact if given the discrete topology.
- The additive group of [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) **Q**<sub>*p*</sub> with its standard topology is locally compact for any [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

By homogeneity, local compactness of the underlying space for a topological group need only be checked at the identity. That is, a group *G* is a locally compact space if and only if the identity element has a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29). It follows that there is a [local base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_base) of compact neighborhoods at every point.

Every [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of a locally compact group is locally compact. (The closure condition is necessary as the group of rationals demonstrates.) Conversely, every locally compact subgroup of a Hausdorff group is closed. Every [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) of a locally compact group is locally compact. The [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_product_%28group_theory%29) of a family of locally compact groups is locally compact if and only if all but a finite number of factors are actually compact.

Topological groups are always [completely regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_regular) as topological spaces. Locally compact groups have the stronger property of being [normal](wikipedia://en/48629).

Every locally compact group which is [*T*<sub>*0*</sub>](wikipedia://en/40117302) and [first-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_countable_space) is [metrisable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrisable) as a topological group (i.e. can be given a left-invariant metric compatible with the topology) and [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space). If furthermore the space is [second-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countable_space), the metric can be chosen to be proper. (See the article on [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315#Metrisability).)

In a [Polish group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_group) *G*, the σ-algebra of [Haar null sets](wikipedia://en/21520#Haar_null) satisfies the [countable chain condition](wikipedia://en/1550771) if and only if *G* is locally compact.[^o6]

:::

## Locally compact abelian groups {#Locally_compact_abelian_groups}

For any locally compact abelian (LCA) group *A*, the group of continuous homomorphisms

Hom(*A*, *S*<sup>1</sup>)

from *A* to the circle group is again locally compact. [Pontryagin duality](wikipedia://en/366136) asserts that this [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) induces an [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051)

LCA<sup>op</sup> → LCA.

This functor exchanges several properties of topological groups. For example, finite groups correspond to finite groups, compact groups correspond to discrete groups, and [metrisable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrisable_space) groups correspond to countable unions of compact groups (and vice versa in all statements).

LCA groups form an [exact category](wikipedia://en/10987985), with admissible monomorphisms being closed subgroups and admissible epimorphisms being topological quotient maps. It is therefore possible to consider the [K-theory](wikipedia://en/246748) [spectrum](wikipedia://en/1033697) of this category. (Clausen 2017) has shown that it measures the difference between the [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) of **Z** and **R**, the integers and the reals, respectively, in the sense that there is a [homotopy fiber sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_pullback)

K(**Z**) → K(**R**) → K(LCA).
