# Symbolic integration

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symbolic integration"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), **symbolic integration** is the problem of finding a [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula_%28mathematics%29) for the [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823), or *indefinite integral*, of a given [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f*(*x*), i.e. to find a formula for a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) *F*(*x*) such that

:::

$$\frac{dF}{dx} = f(x).$$

The family of all functions that satisfy this property can be denoted

$$\int f(x) \, dx.$$

## Discussion {#Discussion}

The term *symbolic* is used to distinguish this problem from that of [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089), where the value of *F* is sought at a particular input or set of inputs, rather than a general formula for *F*.

Both problems were held to be of practical and theoretical importance long before the time of digital computers, but they are now generally considered the domain of [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), as computers are most often used currently to tackle individual instances.

Finding the derivative of an expression is a straightforward process for which it is easy to construct an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775). The reverse question of finding the integral is much more difficult. Many expressions that are relatively simple do not have integrals that can be expressed in [closed form](wikipedia://en/585143). See [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) and [nonelementary integral](wikipedia://en/1520379) for more details.

A procedure called the [Risch algorithm](wikipedia://en/289450) exists that is capable of determining whether the integral of an [elementary function](wikipedia://en/10412) (function built from a finite number of [exponentials](wikipedia://en/9678), [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860), [constants](wikipedia://en/51203), and [*n*th roots](wikipedia://en/235029) through [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) and combinations using the four [elementary operations](wikipedia://en/3118)) is elementary and returning it if it is. In its original form, the Risch algorithm was not suitable for a direct implementation, and its complete implementation took a long time. It was first implemented in [Reduce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduce_%28computer_algebra_system%29) in the case of purely [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021); the case of purely [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169) was solved and implemented in Reduce by [James H. Davenport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_H._Davenport); the general case was solved by Manuel Bronstein, who implemented almost all of it in [Axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_%28computer_algebra_system%29), though to date there is no implementation of the Risch algorithm that can deal with all of the special cases and branches in it.[^o3][^o4]

However, the Risch algorithm applies only to *indefinite* integrals, while most of the integrals of interest to physicists, theoretical chemists, and engineers are *definite* integrals often related to [Laplace transforms](wikipedia://en/18610), [Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/52247), and [Mellin transforms](wikipedia://en/387297). Lacking a general algorithm, the developers of [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) have implemented [heuristics](wikipedia://en/14220429) based on pattern-matching and the exploitation of special functions, in particular the [incomplete gamma function](wikipedia://en/456050).[^o5] Although this approach is heuristic rather than algorithmic, it is nonetheless an effective method for solving many definite integrals encountered by practical engineering applications. Earlier systems such as [Macsyma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macsyma) had a few definite integrals related to special functions within a look-up table. However this particular method, involving differentiation of special functions with respect to its parameters, variable transformation, [pattern matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_matching) and other manipulations, was pioneered by developers of the [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29)[^o6] system and then later emulated by [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica), [Axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_%28computer_algebra_system%29), [MuPAD](wikipedia://en/30874575) and other systems.

## Recent advances {#Recent_advances}

The main problem in the classical approach of symbolic integration is that, if a function is represented in [closed form](wikipedia://en/585143), then, in general, its [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) has not a similar representation. In other words, the class of functions that can be represented in closed form is not [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under antiderivation.

[Holonomic functions](wikipedia://en/5817043) are a large class of functions, which is closed under antiderivation and allows algorithmic implementation in computers of integration and many other operations of calculus.

More precisely, a holonomic function is a solution of a homogeneous [linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868) with polynomial coefficients. Holonomic functions are closed under addition and multiplication, derivation, and antiderivation. They include [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169), [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678), [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860), [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine), [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine), [inverse trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/374220), [inverse hyperbolic functions](wikipedia://en/3804557).

They include also most common special functions such as [Airy function](wikipedia://en/406896), [error function](wikipedia://en/177111), [Bessel functions](wikipedia://en/4700), and all [hypergeometric functions](wikipedia://en/2055223).

A fundamental property of holonomic functions is that the coefficients of their [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) at any point satisfy a linear [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) with polynomial coefficients, and that this recurrence relation may be computed from the differential equation defining the function. Conversely given such a recurrence relation between the coefficients of a [power series](wikipedia://en/59958), this power series defines a holonomic function whose differential equation may be computed algorithmically. This recurrence relation allows a fast computation of the Taylor series, and thus of the value of the function at any point, with an arbitrary small certified error.

This makes algorithmic most operations of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), when restricted to holonomic functions, represented by their differential equation and initial conditions. This includes the computation of antiderivatives and [definite integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integral) (this amounts to evaluating the antiderivative at the endpoints of the interval of integration). This includes also the computation of the [asymptotic behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_behavior) of the function at infinity, and thus the definite integrals on unbounded intervals.

All these operations are implemented in the *algolib* library for [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29).[^o8]

See also the Dynamic Dictionary of Mathematical Functions.[^o9]

## Example {#Example}

For example:

$$\int x^2\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3} + C$$

is a symbolic result for an indefinite integral (here *C* is a [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939)),

$$\int_{-1}^1 x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^1= \frac{1^3}{3} - \frac{(-1)^3}{3}=\frac{2}{3}$$

is a symbolic result for a definite integral, and

$$\int_{-1}^1 x^2\,dx \approx 0.6667$$

is a numerical result for the same definite integral.
