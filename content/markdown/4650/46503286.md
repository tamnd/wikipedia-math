# Von Staudt conic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Von Staudt conic"}

In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), a **von Staudt conic** is the point set defined by all the absolute points of a polarity that has absolute points. In the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325) a von Staudt conic is a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) in the usual sense. In more general [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350) this is not always the case. [Karl Georg Christian von Staudt](wikipedia://en/2186050) introduced this definition in *Geometrie der Lage* (1847) as part of his attempt to remove all metrical concepts from projective geometry.

:::

## Polarities {#Polarities}

A **polarity**, $\pi$, of a projective plane, $P$, is an involutory (i.e., of order two) [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the points and the lines of $P$ that preserves the [incidence relation](wikipedia://en/387224). Thus, a polarity relates a point $Q$ with a line $q$ and, following [Gergonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gergonne), $q$ is called the **polar** of $Q$ and $Q$ the **pole** of $q$.[^o3] An **absolute point** (**line**) of a polarity is one which is incident with its polar (pole).[^o4][^o5]

A polarity may or may not have absolute points. A polarity with absolute points is called a **hyperbolic polarity** and one without absolute points is called an **elliptic polarity**.[^o6] In the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155) all polarities are hyperbolic but in the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325) only some are.[^o6]

A classification of polarities over arbitrary fields follows from the classification of sesquilinear forms given by [Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916) and [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942).[^o7] Orthogonal polarities, corresponding to symmetric bilinear forms, are also called *ordinary polarities* and the locus of absolute points forms a non-degenerate conic (set of points whose coordinates satisfy an irreducible homogeneous quadratic equation) if the field does not have [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28field%29) two. In characteristic two the orthogonal polarities are called *pseudopolarities* and in a plane the absolute points form a line.[^o8]

## Finite projective planes {#Finite_projective_planes}

If $\pi$ is a polarity of a finite projective plane (which need not be desarguesian), $P$, of order $n$ then the number of its absolute points (or absolute lines), $a(\pi)$ is given by:

$$a(\pi) = n + 2r\sqrt{n} + 1,$$

where $r$ is a non-negative integer.[^o10] Since $a(\pi)$ is an integer, $a(\pi) = n + 1$ if $n$ is not a square, and in this case, $\pi$ is called an *orthogonal polarity*.

[R. Baer](wikipedia://en/1612114) has shown that if $n$ is odd, the absolute points of an orthogonal polarity form an [oval](wikipedia://en/4255174) (that is, $n + 1$ points, no three [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear)), while if $n$ is even, the absolute points lie on a non-absolute line.[^o11]

In summary, von Staudt conics are not ovals in finite projective planes (desarguesian or not) of even order.[^o12][^o13]

## Relation to other types of conics {#Relation_to_other_types_of_conics}

In a [pappian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappian_plane) (i.e., a projective plane coordinatized by a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29)), if the field does not have [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28field%29) two, a von Staudt conic is equivalent to a [Steiner conic](wikipedia://en/46462753).[^o15] However, R. Artzy has shown that these two definitions of conics can produce non-isomorphic objects in (infinite) [Moufang planes](wikipedia://en/14860374).[^o16]
