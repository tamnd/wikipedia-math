# Philo of Byzantium

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Philo of Byzantium"}

**Philo of Byzantium** (Φίλων ὁ Βυζάντιος, c. 280 BC), also known as **Philo Mechanicus** in Latin, was a Greek engineer, physicist and writer on [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), who lived during the latter half of the 3rd century BC. Although he was from [Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantium) he lived most of his life in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria), Egypt. He was probably younger than [Ctesibius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesibius), though some place him a century earlier.

:::

## Works {#Works}

Philo was the author of a large work, the *Syntaxis* (Μηχανική Σύνταξη), which contained the following sections:[^o4]

- Isagoge (Εἰσαγωγή; general mathematics)
- Mochlica (Μοχλικά; mechanics)
- Limenopoeica (Λιμενοποιικά)
- Belopoeica Βελοποιικά)
- Pneumatica (Πνευματικά)
- Automatopoeica (Αὐτοματοποιητικά; mechanical toys and diversions)
- Parasceuastica (Παρασκευαστικά; for [sieges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege))
- Poliorcetica (Πολιορκητικά)
- Peri Epistolon (Περὶ Ἐπιστολῶν; on coding and hidden letters for military use)

The military sections *Belopoeica* and *Poliorcetica* are extant in Greek, detailing missiles, the construction of fortresses, provisioning, attack and defence, as are fragments of *Isagoge* and *Automatopoeica* (ed. R. Schone, 1893, with German translation in Hermann August Theodor Köchly's *Griechische Kriegsschriftsteller*, vol. i. 1853; E. A. Rochas d'Aiglun,*Poliorcetique des Grecs*, 1872).

Another portion of the work, on pneumatic engines, has been preserved in the form of a Latin translation (De Ingeniis Spiritualibus) of an Arabic translation (ed. W. Schmidt, with German translation, in the works of [Heron of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron_of_Alexandria), vol. i., in the [Teubner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teubner) series, 1899; with French translation by Rochas, *La Science des philosophes... dans l'antiquité*, 1882). Further portions probably survive in a derivative form, incorporated into the works of [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius) and of [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) authors.

The [Philo line](wikipedia://en/9350828), a geometric construction that can be used to [double the cube](wikipedia://en/91110), is attributed to Philo.

### Devices {#Devices}

According to recent research, a section of Philo's *Pneumatics* which so far has been regarded as a later Arabic interpolation, includes the first description of a [water mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_mill) in history,[^o6] placing the invention of the water mill in the mid-third century BC by the Greeks.[^o7]

Philo's works also contain the oldest known application of a chain drive in a [repeating crossbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repeating_crossbow). Two flat-linked chains were connected to a [windlass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windlass), which by winding back and forth would automatically fire the machine's arrows until its magazine was empty.[^o8]

Philo also was the first to describe a [gimbal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimbal): an eight-sided [ink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ink) pot that could be turned any way without spilling and expose the ink on top. This was done by the suspension of the inkwell at the centre, which was mounted on a series of concentric metal rings which remained stationary no matter which way the pot turns.[^o9]

In his *Pneumatics* (chapter 31) Philo describes an [escapement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escapement) mechanism, the earliest known, as part of a [washstand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washstand).[^o10] A counterweighted spoon, supplied by a water tank, tips over in a basin when full releasing a pumice in the process. Once the spoon has emptied, it is pulled up again by the counterweight, closing the door on the pumice by the tightening string. Remarkably, Philo's comment that "its construction is similar to that of clocks" indicates that such escapements mechanism were already integrated in ancient water clocks.[^o10]

He is also credited with the construction of the first [thermoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoscope) (or Philo thermometer), an early version of the thermometer.[^o11]

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

In mathematics, Philo tackled the problem of [doubling the cube](wikipedia://en/91110). The doubling of the cube was necessitated by the following problem: given a catapult, construct a second catapult that is capable of firing a projectile twice as heavy as the projectile of the first catapult. His solution was to find the point of intersection of a rectangular [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) and a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), a solution that is similar to the solution given by [Hero of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/68274) several centuries later.

### Apocrypha {#Apocrypha}

A treatise titled "[Seven Wonders of the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Wonders_of_the_Ancient_World)" (Περὶ τῶν Ἑπτὰ Θεαμάτων) is sometimes attributed to this Philo but more probably belongs to a different Philo of Byzantium, distinguished as Philo the Paradoxographer, who lived in a much later date, probably the 4th–5th century AD.[^o14][^o15] It is printed in [R. Hercher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._Hercher)'s edition of Aelian (Paris: Firmin Didot, 1858); an English translation by Jean Blackwood is included as an appendix in *The Seven Wonders of the World* by Michael Ashley (Glasgow: Fontana Paperbacks, 1980).
