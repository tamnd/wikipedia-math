# Path (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Path"}

*Image: Path.svg.* The points traced by a path from $A$ to $B$ in $\mathbb{R}^2.$ However, different paths can trace the same set of points.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **path** in a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is a [continuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) from a [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) into $X.$

:::

Paths play an important role in the fields of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). For example, a topological space for which there exists a path connecting any two points is said to be [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected_space). Any space may be broken up into [path-connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected_component). The set of path-connected components of a space $X$ is often denoted $\pi_0(X).$

One can also define paths and loops in [pointed spaces](wikipedia://en/1236666), which are important in [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831). If $X$ is a topological space with basepoint $x_0,$ then a path in $X$ is one whose initial point is $x_0$. Likewise, a loop in $X$ is one that is based at $x_0$.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A *[curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_curve)* in a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is a [continuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) $f : J \to X$ from a non-empty and [non-degenerate interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate_interval) $J \subseteq \mathbb{R}.$ A **path** in $X$ is a curve $f : [a, b] \to X$ whose domain $[a, b]$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) non-degenerate interval (meaning $a < b$ are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)), where $f(a)$ is called the **initial point** of the path and $f(b)$ is called its **terminal point**. A **path from $x$ to $y$** is a path whose initial point is $x$ and whose terminal point is $y.$ Every non-degenerate compact interval $[a, b]$ is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to $[0, 1],$ which is why a **path** is sometimes, especially in homotopy theory, defined to be a [continuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) $f : [0, 1] \to X$ from the closed [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) $I := [0, 1]$ into $X.$

An **arc** or **$C$<sup>0</sup>-arc** in $X$ is a path in $X$ that is also a [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding).

Importantly, a path is not just a subset of $X$ that "looks like" a [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_curve), it also includes a [parameterization](wikipedia://en/453198). For example, the maps $f(x) = x$ and $g(x) = x^2$ represent two different paths from 0 to 1 on the real line.

A **[loop](wikipedia://en/1189560)** in a space $X$ based at $x \in X$ is a path from $x$ to $x.$ A loop may be equally well regarded as a map $f : [0, 1] \to X$ with $f(0) = f(1)$ or as a continuous map from the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) $S^1$ to $X$

$$f : S^1 \to X.$$

This is because $S^1$ is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of $I = [0, 1]$ when $0$ is identified with $1.$ The set of all loops in $X$ forms a space called the [loop space](wikipedia://en/2174332) of $X.$

:::

## Homotopy of paths {#Homotopy_of_paths}

*Image: Homotopy between two paths.svg.* A homotopy between two paths.

Paths and loops are central subjects of study in the branch of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) called [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831). A [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) of paths makes precise the notion of continuously deforming a path while keeping its endpoints fixed.

Specifically, a homotopy of paths, or **path-homotopy**, in $X$ is a family of paths $f_t : [0, 1] \to X$ indexed by $I = [0, 1]$ such that

- $f_t(0) = x_0$ and $f_t(1) = x_1$ are fixed.
- the map $F : [0, 1] \times [0, 1] \to X$ given by $F(s, t) = f_t(s)$ is continuous.

The paths $f_0$ and $f_1$ connected by a homotopy are said to be **homotopic** (or more precisely **path-homotopic**, to distinguish between the relation defined on all continuous functions between fixed spaces). One can likewise define a homotopy of loops keeping the base point fixed.

The relation of being homotopic is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on paths in a topological space. The [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of a path $f$ under this relation is called the **homotopy class** of $f,$ often denoted $[f].$

## Path composition {#Path_composition}

One can compose paths in a topological space in the following manner. Suppose $f$ is a path from $x$ to $y$ and $g$ is a path from $y$ to $z$. The path $fg$ is defined as the path obtained by first traversing $f$ and then traversing $g$:

$$fg(s) = \begin{cases}f(2s) & 0 \leq s \leq \frac{1}{2} \\ g(2s-1) & \frac{1}{2} \leq s \leq 1.\end{cases}$$

Clearly path composition is only defined when the terminal point of $f$ coincides with the initial point of $g.$ If one considers all loops based at a point $x_0,$ then path composition is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948).

Path composition, whenever defined, is not [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) due to the difference in parametrization. However it is associative up to path-homotopy. That is, $[(fg)h] = [f(gh)].$ Path composition defines a [group structure](wikipedia://en/19447) on the set of homotopy classes of loops based at a point $x_0$ in $X.$ The resultant group is called the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of $X$ based at $x_0,$ usually denoted $\pi_1\left(X, x_0\right).$

In situations calling for associativity of path composition "on the nose," a path in $X$ may instead be defined as a continuous map from an interval $[0, a]$ to $X$ for any real $a \geq 0.$ (Such a path is called a [Moore path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore_path).) A path $f$ of this kind has a length $|f|$ defined as $a.$ Path composition is then defined as before with the following modification:

$$fg(s) = \begin{cases}f(s) & 0 \leq s \leq |f| \\ g(s-|f|) & |f| \leq s \leq |f| + |g|\end{cases}$$

Whereas with the previous definition, $f,$ $g$, and $fg$ all have length $1$ (the length of the domain of the map), this definition makes $|fg| = |f| + |g|.$ What made associativity fail for the previous definition is that although $(fg)h$ and $f(gh)$have the same length, namely $1,$ the midpoint of $(fg)h$ occurred between $g$ and $h,$ whereas the midpoint of $f(gh)$ occurred between $f$ and $g$. With this modified definition $(fg)h$ and $f(gh)$ have the same length, namely $|f| + |g| + |h|,$ and the same midpoint, found at $\left(|f| + |g| + |h|\right)/2$ in both $(fg)h$ and $f(gh)$; more generally they have the same parametrization throughout.

:::

## Fundamental groupoid {#Fundamental_groupoid}

There is a [categorical](wikipedia://en/5869) picture of paths which is sometimes useful. Any topological space $X$ gives rise to a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) where the objects are the points of $X$ and the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) are the homotopy classes of paths. Since any morphism in this category is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), this category is a [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) called the [fundamental groupoid](wikipedia://en/55264190) of $X.$ Loops in this category are the [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) (all of which are actually [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160)). The [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of a point $x_0$ in $X$ is just the fundamental group based at $x_0$. More generally, one can define the fundamental groupoid on any subset $A$ of $X,$ using homotopy classes of paths joining points of $A.$ This is convenient for [Van Kampen's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Kampen's_Theorem).
