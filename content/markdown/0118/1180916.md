# Garrett Birkhoff

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Garrett Birkhoff"}

**Garrett Birkhoff** (January 19, 1911 – November 22, 1996) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). He is best known for his work in [lattice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory) and [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200).

:::

## Life {#Life}

The son of the mathematician [George David Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/30871906), Garrett was born in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey).[^o4] He began the [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University)'s BA course in 1928 after less than seven years of prior formal education. Upon completing his Harvard BA in 1932, he went to [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University) to study [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) but switched to studying [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) under [Philip Hall](wikipedia://en/1655033). While visiting the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München), he met [Constantin Carathéodory](wikipedia://en/470625) who pointed him towards two important texts, [Van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390) on [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) and [Speiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Speiser) on [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).

Birkhoff held no Ph.D., a qualification British higher education did not emphasize at that time, and did not obtain an M.A. Nevertheless, after being a member of Harvard's [Society of Fellows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Fellows), 1933–36, he spent the rest of his career teaching at Harvard.

During the 1930s, Birkhoff, along with his Harvard colleagues [Marshall Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Stone) and [Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574), substantially advanced American teaching and research in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). In 1941, he and Mac Lane published *A Survey of Modern Algebra*, the second undergraduate textbook in English on the subject ([Cyrus Colton MacDuffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrus_Colton_MacDuffee)'s *An Introduction to Abstract Algebra* was published in 1940). Mac Lane and Birkhoff's *Algebra* (1967) is a more advanced text on [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). A number of papers he wrote in the 1930s, culminating in his monograph, *Lattice Theory* (1940; the third edition remains in print), turned [lattice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory) into a major branch of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). His 1935 paper, "On the Structure of Abstract Algebras" founded a new branch of mathematics, [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200). Birkhoff's approach to this development of universal [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and lattice theory acknowledged prior ideas of [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117), [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015), and [Alfred North Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395); in fact, Whitehead had written an 1898 monograph entitled *Universal Algebra*.

During and after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), Birkhoff's interests gravitated towards what he called "engineering" mathematics. During the war, he worked on radar aiming and ballistics, including the [bazooka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazooka). In the development of weapons, mathematical questions arose, some of which had not yet been addressed by the literature on [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034). Birkhoff's research was presented in his texts on fluid dynamics, *Hydrodynamics* (1950) and *Jets, Wakes and Cavities* (1957).

Birkhoff, a friend of [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), took a close interest in the rise of the electronic computer. Birkhoff supervised the Ph.D. thesis of [David M. Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_M._Young,_Jr.) on the numerical solution of [the partial differential equation of Poisson](wikipedia://en/193735), in which Young proposed the [successive over-relaxation](wikipedia://en/4068447) (SOR) method. Birkhoff then worked with [Richard S. Varga](wikipedia://en/25492358), a former student, who was employed at [Bettis Atomic Power Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bettis_Atomic_Power_Laboratory) of the [Westinghouse Electronic Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westinghouse_Electric_%281886%29) in Pittsburgh and was helping to design nuclear reactors. Extending the results of Young, the Birkhoff–Varga collaboration led to many publications on [positive operators](wikipedia://en/454936) and [iterative methods](wikipedia://en/15237) for *p*-cyclic matrices.

Birkhoff's research and consulting work (notably for [General Motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors_Corporation)) developed computational methods besides [numerical linear algebra](wikipedia://en/7330660), notably the representation of smooth curves via [cubic splines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_spline).

Birkhoff published more than 200 papers and supervised more than 50 Ph.D.s. He was a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences),[^o5] the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society),[^o6] and the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences).[^o7] He was a [Guggenheim Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellow) for the academic year 1948–1949 and the president of the [Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1883376) for 1966–1968. He won a [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award) in 1974.[^o8]
