# Rational agent

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rational agent"}

A **rational agent** or **rational being** is a person or entity that always aims to perform [optimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_%28mathematics%29) actions based on given premises and information. A rational agent can be anything that makes decisions, typically a [person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Person), [firm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firm), [machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine), or [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software).

:::

The concept of rational agents can be found in various disciplines such as [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence), [cognitive science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science), [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics), [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924), and the study of [practical reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Practical_reason).

## Economics {#Economics}

In reference to economics, rational agent refers to hypothetical consumers and how they make decisions in a [free market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_market). This concept is one of the assumptions made in [neoclassical economic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_economics). The concept of economic [rationality](wikipedia://en/61032) arises from a tradition of [marginal analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_analysis) used in neoclassical economics. The idea of a rational agent is important to the philosophy of [utilitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism), as detailed by philosopher [Jeremy Bentham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham)'s theory of the [felicific calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felicific_calculus), also known as the hedonistic calculus.

The action a rational agent takes depends on:

- the preferences of the agent
- the agent's information of its environment, which may come from past experiences
- the actions, duties and obligations available to the agent
- the estimated or actual benefits and the chances of success of the actions.

In [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924) and classical [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), it is often assumed that the [actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor), people, and firms are rational. However, the extent to which people and firms behave rationally is subject to debate. Economists often assume the models of [rational choice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_choice_theory) and [bounded rationality](wikipedia://en/70400) to formalize and predict the behavior of individuals and firms. Rational agents sometimes behave in manners that are counter-intuitive to many people, as in the [traveler's dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traveler's_dilemma).

### Alternate theories {#Alternate_theories}

[Neuroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroeconomics) is a concept that uses [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience), social [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) and other fields of science to better understand how people make decisions. Unlike rational agent theory, [neuroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroeconomics) does not attempt to predict large-scale human behavior but rather how individuals make decisions in case-by-case scenarios.

## Artificial intelligence {#Artificial_intelligence}

[Artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) has borrowed the term "rational agents" from economics to describe autonomous programs that are capable of goal directed behavior. Today there is a considerable overlap between AI research, game theory and decision theory. Rational agents in AI are closely related to *[intelligent agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_agent)*, autonomous software programs that display intelligence.[^n1]
