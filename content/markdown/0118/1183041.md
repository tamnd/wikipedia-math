# Quadratic residuosity problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quadratic residuosity problem"}

The **quadratic residuosity problem** (**QRP**[^o2]) in [computational number theory](wikipedia://en/511466) is to decide, given [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $a$ and $N$, whether $a$ is a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091) [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $N$ or not. Here $N = p_1 p_2$ for two unknown [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) $p_1$ and $p_2$, and $a$ is among the numbers which are not obviously quadratic non-residues (see below).

:::

The problem was first described by [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss) in his *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)* in 1801. This problem is believed to be [computationally difficult](wikipedia://en/7543). Several cryptographic methods [rely on its hardness](wikipedia://en/6158383), see .

An efficient [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for the quadratic residuosity problem immediately implies efficient algorithms for other [number theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theoretic) problems, such as deciding whether a [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) $N$ of unknown factorization is the product of 2 or 3 primes.[^o3]

## Precise formulation {#Precise_formulation}

Given integers $a$ and $T$, $a$ is said to be a *quadratic residue modulo $T$* if there exists an integer $b$ such that

$$a \equiv b^2 \pmod T.$$

Otherwise we say it is a quadratic non-residue. When $T = p$ is a prime, it is customary to use the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567):

$$\left( \frac{a}{p} \right) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{ if } a \text{ is a quadratic residue modulo } p \text{ and } a \not\equiv 0\pmod{p}, \\ -1 & \text{ if } a \text{ is a quadratic non-residue modulo } p, \\ 0 & \text{ if } a \equiv 0 \pmod{p}. \end{cases}$$

This is a [multiplicative character](wikipedia://en/293418) which means $\big(\tfrac{a}{p}\big) = 1$ for exactly $(p-1)/2$ of the values $1,\ldots,p-1$, and it is $-1$ for the remaining.

It is easy to compute using the [law of quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_quadratic_reciprocity) in a manner akin to the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377); see [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567).

Consider now some given $N = p_1 p_2$ where $p_1$ and $p_2$ are two different unknown primes. A given $a$ is a quadratic residue modulo $N$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $a$ is a quadratic residue modulo both $p_1$ and $p_2$ and $\gcd(a, N) = 1$.

Since we don't know $p_1$ or $p_2$, we cannot compute $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_1}\big)$ and $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_2}\big)$. However, it is easy to compute their product. This is known as the [Jacobi symbol](wikipedia://en/97313):

$$\left(\frac{a}{N}\right) = \left(\frac{a}{p_1}\right)\left(\frac{a}{p_2}\right)$$

This also [can be efficiently computed](wikipedia://en/97313#Calculating_the_Jacobi_symbol) using the [law of quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_quadratic_reciprocity) for Jacobi symbols.

However, $\big(\tfrac{a}{N}\big)$ cannot in all cases tell us whether $a$ is a quadratic residue modulo $N$ or not! More precisely, if $\big(\tfrac{a}{N}\big) = -1$ then $a$ is necessarily a quadratic non-residue modulo either $p_1$ or $p_2$, in which case we are done. But if $\big(\tfrac{a}{N}\big) = 1$ then it is either the case that $a$ is a quadratic residue modulo both $p_1$ and $p_2$, or a quadratic non-residue modulo both $p_1$ and $p_2$. We cannot distinguish these cases from knowing just that $\big(\tfrac{a}{N}\big) = 1$.

This leads to the precise formulation of the quadratic residue problem:

::: {#o5 .problem cue=inline-head name="Problem"}

**Problem:** Given integers $a$ and $N = p_1 p_2$, where $p_1$ and $p_2$ are distinct unknown primes, and where $\big(\tfrac{a}{N}\big) = 1$, determine whether $a$ is a quadratic residue modulo $N$ or not.

:::

## Distribution of residues {#Distribution_of_residues}

If $a$ is drawn [uniformly at random](wikipedia://en/1699214) from integers $0,\ldots,N-1$ such that $\big(\tfrac{a}{N}\big) = 1$, is $a$ more often a quadratic residue or a quadratic non-residue modulo $N$?

As mentioned earlier, for exactly half of the choices of $a \in \{1,\ldots,p_1-1\}$, then $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_1}\big) = 1$, and for the rest we have $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_1}\big) = -1$. By extension, this also holds for half the choices of $a \in \{1,\ldots,N-1\} \setminus p_1\mathbb{Z}$. Similarly for $p_2$. From basic algebra, it follows that this partitions $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ into 4 parts of equal size, depending on the sign of $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_1}\big)$ and $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_2}\big)$.

The allowed $a$ in the quadratic residue problem given as above constitute exactly those two parts corresponding to the cases $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_1}\big) = \big(\tfrac{a}{p_2}\big) = 1$ and $\big(\tfrac{a}{p_1}\big) = \big(\tfrac{a}{p_2}\big) = -1$. Consequently, exactly half of the possible $a$ are quadratic residues and the remaining are not.

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The intractability of the quadratic residuosity problem is the basis for the security of the [Blum Blum Shub](wikipedia://en/39599) [pseudorandom number generator](wikipedia://en/45524). It also yields the [public key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key_encryption) [Goldwasser–Micali cryptosystem](wikipedia://en/2461162),[^o9][^o10] as well as the [identity based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_based_encryption) [Cocks scheme](wikipedia://en/16254122).
