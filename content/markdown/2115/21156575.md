# Complete field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complete field"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **complete field** is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) equipped with a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) and [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) with respect to that metric. A field supports the elementary operations of [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), and [division](wikipedia://en/53696), while a metric represents the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) between two points in the set. Basic examples include the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), and [complete valued fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_valued_field) (such as the [*p*-adic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_numbers)).

:::

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Field {#Field}

A [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $F$ with [binary operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_operations) $+$ and $\cdot$ (called *addition* and *multiplication*, respectively), along with [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) $0$ and $1$ such that for all $a,b,c \in F$, the following relations hold:[^o5]

1. $$a+(b+c)=(a+b)+c$$
1. $$a+b=b+a$$
1. $$a+0=a=0+a$$
1. $a+x=0$ has a solution
1. $$a(bc)=(ab)c$$
1. $$ab=ba$$
1. $a(b+c)=ab+ac$ and $(a+b)c=ac+bc$
1. $$a1=a=1a$$
1. $ax=1$ has a solution for $a \neq 0$

### Complete metric {#Complete_metric}

A [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) on a set $F$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $d: F^2 \to [0, \infty)$, that is, it takes two points in $F$ and sends them to a [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), such that the following relations hold for all $x,y,z \in F$:[^o7]

1. $d(x,y) = 0$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $x=y$
1. $$d(x,y)=d(y,x)$$
1. $$d(x,y) \leq d(x,z)+d(z,y)$$

A [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $x_n$ in the space is [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/6085) with respect to this metric if for all $\epsilon > 0$ there exists an $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n,m \geq N$ we have $d(x_n,x_m) < \epsilon$, and a metric is then [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) if every Cauchy sequence in the metric space [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_sequence), that is, there is some $x \in F$ where for all $\epsilon > 0$ there exists an $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \geq N$ we have $d(x_n,x) < \epsilon$. Every convergent sequence is Cauchy, however the converse does not hold in general.[^o7][^o8]

## Constructions {#Constructions}

### Real and complex numbers {#Real_and_complex_numbers}

The real numbers are the field with the standard Euclidean metric $|x-y|$, and this measure is complete.[^o7] Extending the reals by adding the [imaginary number](wikipedia://en/14554) $i$ satisfying $i^2=-1$ gives the field $\mathbb{C}$, which is also a complete field.[^o8]

### p-adic {#p-adic}

The p-adic numbers are constructed from $\mathbb{Q}$ by using the p-adic absolute value

> $$v_p(a/b) = v_p(a) - v_p(b)$$

where $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}.$ Then using the factorization $a = p^nc$ where $p$ does not divide $c,$ its valuation is the integer $n$. The completion of $\mathbb{Q}$ by $v_p$ is the complete field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ called the p-adic numbers. This is a case where the field is not algebraically closed. Typically, the process is to take the separable closure and then complete it again. This field is usually denoted $\mathbb{C}_p.$[^o13]
