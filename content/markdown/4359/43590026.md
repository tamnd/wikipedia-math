# Weighted automaton

*Image: Quantitative automata.svg.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of some classes of quantitative automata, ordered by expressiveness.[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Weighted automaton"}

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392) and [formal language theory](wikipedia://en/10939), a **weighted automaton** or **weighted finite-state machine** is a generalization of a [finite-state machine](wikipedia://en/10931) in which the edges have [weights](wikipedia://en/325802#weight), for example [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). Finite-state machines are only capable of answering [decision problems](wikipedia://en/8336); they take as input a [string](wikipedia://en/27701) and produce a [Boolean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_data_type) output, i.e. either "accept" or "reject". In contrast, weighted automata produce a [quantitative](wikipedia://en/43507282) output, for example a count of *how many* answers are possible on a given input string, or a [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) of *how likely* the input string is according to a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543).[^o3] They are one of the simplest studied models of **quantitative automata.**[^o1]

:::

The definition of a weighted automaton is generally given over an arbitrary [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468) $R$, an [abstract](wikipedia://en/19616384) set with an addition operation $\oplus$ and a multiplication operation $\otimes$. The automaton consists of a finite set of states, a finite input alphabet of [characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_%28computing%29) $\Sigma$ and edges which are labeled with both a character in $\Sigma$ and a weight in $R$. The weight of any [path](wikipedia://en/638889) in the automaton is defined to be the product of weights along the path, and the weight of a string is the sum of the weights of all paths which are labeled with that string. The weighted automaton thus defines a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) from $\Sigma^*$ to $R$.[^o3]

Weighted automata [generalize](wikipedia://en/12746) [deterministic finite automata](wikipedia://en/653404) (DFAs) and [nondeterministic finite automata](wikipedia://en/653406) (NFAs), which correspond to weighted automata over the [Boolean semiring](wikipedia://en/371468#Boolean_semiring), where addition is [logical disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) and multiplication is [logical conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152). In the DFA case, there is only one accepting path for any input string, so disjunction is not applied. When the weights are real numbers and the outgoing weights for each state add to one, weighted automata can be considered a [probabilistic model](wikipedia://en/27576) and are also known as [probabilistic automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_automaton). These machines define a probability distribution over all strings, and are related to other probabilistic models such as [Markov decision processes](wikipedia://en/1125883) and [Markov chains](wikipedia://en/60876).

Weighted automata have applications in [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing) where they are used to assign weights to words and sentences,[^o4][^o3]p. 571–596 as well as in [image compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_compression).[^o3]p. 453–480 They were first introduced by [Marcel-Paul Schützenberger](wikipedia://en/2817337) in his 1961 paper *On the definition of a family of automata.*[^o5] Since their introduction, many extensions have been proposed, for example nested weighted automata,[^o6] cost register automata,[^o7] and [weighted finite-state transducers](wikipedia://en/1071289#Weighted_automata).[^o8] Researchers have studied weighted automata from the perspective of [learning](wikipedia://en/233488) a machine from its input-output behavior[^o9] (see [computational learning theory](wikipedia://en/387537)) and studying [decidability](wikipedia://en/8336) questions.[^o10]

## Definition {#Definition}

A [commutative semiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_semiring) (also known as a **rig**) is a set *R* equipped with two distinguished elements $0$ and $1$ and addition and multiplication operations $\oplus$ and $\otimes$ such that $\oplus$ is [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390) and [associative](wikipedia://en/1335) with [identity](wikipedia://en/14962) $0$, $\otimes$ is commutative and associative with identity $1$, $\otimes$ [distributes](wikipedia://en/103118) over $\oplus$, and 0 is an [absorbing element](wikipedia://en/14206224) for $\otimes$.

A **weighted automaton over $R$** is a tuple $\mathcal{A} = (Q, \Sigma, \Delta, I, F)$ where:

- $Q$ is a finite set of states.
- $\Sigma$ is a finite alphabet.
- $\Delta \subseteq Q \times \Sigma \times R \times Q$ is a finite set of transitions $(q, \sigma, w, q')$, where $\sigma$ is called a **character** and $w$ is called a **weight**.
- $I: Q \to R$ is an initial weight function.
- $F: Q \to R$ is a final weight function.

A **path** on input $w \in \Sigma^*$ is a finite path in the graph, where the concatenation of the character labels equals $w$. The **weight of the path** $q_0, q_1, \ldots, q_n$ is the product ($\otimes$) of the weights along the path, additionally multiplied by the initial and final weights $I(q_0) \otimes F(q_n)$. The **weight of the word $w$** is the sum ($\oplus$) of the weights of all paths on input $w$ (or 0 if there are no accepting paths). In this way the machine defines a function $[\![ \mathcal{A} ]\!]: \Sigma^* \to R$.

:::

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

The definition is often considered over specific semirings over $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, or $\mathbb{R}$.[^o1][^o10][^o5] These can be either defined with the standard addition and multiplication, with $\oplus = +$ and $\otimes = \times$; or over the [tropical semiring](wikipedia://en/3892336), with $\otimes = +$ and $\oplus = \text{max or min},$ in which case the domain $R$ is extended to include either negative infinity $-\infty$ (for max) or positive $+\infty$ (for min).

:::

## Underlying NFA, unambiguity, and determinism {#Underlying_NFA,_unambiguity,_and_determinism}

Since $\Delta$ is a *set* of transitions, weighted automata allow multiple transitions (or paths) on a single input string, similar to a [nondeterministic finite automaton](wikipedia://en/653406) (NFA). The precise connection is suggested by the following definition: the **underlying NFA** of $\mathcal{A}$ is an NFA formed by removing all transitions with weight $0$ and then erasing all of the weights on the transitions $\Delta$, so that the new transition set lies in $Q \times \Sigma \times Q$. The initial states and final states are the set of states $q$ such that $I(q) \ne 0$ and $F(q) \ne 0$, respectively.

As is the case with NFAs, restrictions of weighted automata are considered that correspond to the concepts of [deterministic finite automaton](wikipedia://en/653404) and [unambiguous finite automaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unambiguous_finite_automaton) (deterministic weighted automata and unambiguous weighted automata, respectively). A weighted automaton is **deterministic** if the underlying NFA is deterministic and **unambiguous** if the underlying NFA is unambiguous. Every deterministic weighted automaton is unambiguous.

In both the deterministic and unambiguous cases, there is always at most one accepting path, so the $\oplus$ operation is never applied and can be omitted from the definition.

## Variations {#Variations}

- The requirement that there is a zero element for $\oplus$ is sometimes omitted; in this case the machine defines a [partial function](wikipedia://en/23577) from $\Sigma^*$ to $R$ rather than a total function.
- It is possible to extend the definition to allow [epsilon transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsilon_transition_%28disambiguation%29) $(q, \epsilon, w, q')$, where $\epsilon$ is the empty string. In this case, one must then require that there are no [cycles](wikipedia://en/168609) of epsilon transitions. This does not increase the expressiveness of weighted automata.[^o3][^o18] If epsilon transitions are allowed, the initial weights and final weights can be replaced by initial and final sets of states without loss of expressiveness.
- Some authors omit the initial and final weight functions $I$ and $F$.[^o1] Instead, $I$ and $F$ are replaced by a set of initial and final states. If epsilon transitions are not present, this technically decreases expressiveness as it forces $[\![ \mathcal{A} ]\!](\varepsilon)$ to depend only on the number of states that are both initial and final.
- The transition function can be given as a [matrix](wikipedia://en/487541) $\Delta_\sigma \in R^{Q \times Q}$ with entries in $R$ for each $\sigma$, rather than a set of transitions. The entry of the matrix at $(q, q')$ is the sum of all transitions labeled $(q, \sigma, q')$.[^o3][^o19]
- Some authors restrict to specific semirings, such as $\mathbb{N}$ or $\mathbb{Z}$, particularly when studying decidability results.[^o1][^o10][^o5]
