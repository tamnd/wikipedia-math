# List of regions by past GDP (PPP)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of regions by past GDP"}

These are **lists of regions and countries by their estimated [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_versus_nominal_value_%28economics%29) [gross domestic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP)** in terms of [purchasing power parity](wikipedia://en/64489) (PPP), the value of all final goods and services produced within a country/region in a given year. GDP dollar ([international dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_dollar)) estimates here are derived from PPP estimates. These datasets are widely used in economic history and have also been used in various secondary analyses based on Maddison's estimates.[^o2][^o3][^o4]

:::

## Methodology {#Methodology}

In the absence of sufficient data for nearly all economies until well into the 19th century, past GDP cannot be calculated, but at best only roughly estimated. In a first step, economic historians try to reconstruct the [GDP per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDP_per_capita) for a given political or geographical entity from the meagre evidence. This value is then multiplied by estimated population size, another determinant for which as a rule only little ancient data is available.

A key notion in the whole process is that of [subsistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsistence), the [income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income) level which is necessary for sustaining one's life. Since [pre-modern societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-modern_societies), by modern standards, were characterized by a very low degree of [urbanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbanization) and a large majority of people working in the [agricultural sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_sector), economic historians prefer to express income in [cereal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cereal) units. To achieve comparability over space and time, these numbers are then converted into monetary units such as International Dollars, a third step which leaves a relatively wide margin of interpretation.

The formula thus is: GDP (PPP) = GDP per capita (PPP) x population size

It should be stressed that, historically speaking, population size is the far more important multiplier in the equation. This is because, in contrast to [industrial economies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_age), the average income ceiling of premodern [agrarian societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrarian_societies) was quite low everywhere, possibly not higher than twice the subsistence level.[^o6] Therefore, the total GDP as given below primarily reflects the respective historical population size, and is much less indicative of contemporary [living standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_standard) than, for example, [estimations of past GDP per capita](wikipedia://en/28026009) are.

According to the 20th-century macroeconomist [Paul Bairoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Bairoch), a pioneer in historical economic analysis, it is obvious that by itself the volume of total [GNP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNP) has no important significance, and that the volume of GNP is not by itself the expression of the economic strength of a nation.

Rather, Bairoch advocates a formula combining GNP per capita and total GNP to give a better measure of the economic performance of national economies.[^o7]

## Empires {#Empires}

### Indian empires (AD 1–1947) {#Indian_empires_(AD_1–1947)}

At year 1, year 1000, year 1500 and till the start of British colonisation in India in 17th century, India's GDP always varied between ~22 - 33% world's total GDP and was the largest economy in the world from year 1 until year 1500,[^o13] which dropped to 2% by [Independence of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_India) in 1947.[^o58] At the same time, the Britain's share of the world economy rose from 2.9% in 1700 up to 9% in 1870 alone.[^o59][^o60][^o61]

### British Empire and India {#British_Empire_and_India}

Goedele De Keersmaeker estimated the GDP of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) using Angus Maddison's data. Keersmaeker estimated that the British Empire's share of world GDP was 24.28% in 1870 and 19.7% in 1913. The empire's largest economy in 1870 was [British India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_India) with a 12.15% share of world GDP, followed by the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) with a 9.03% share. The empire's largest economy in 1913 was the United Kingdom with an 8.22% share of world GDP, followed by British India with a 7.47% share.[^o65]

### Roman Empire {#Roman_Empire}

Much work in estimating past GDP has been done in the study of the [Roman economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_economy), following the pioneering studies by [Keith Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Hopkins) (1980) and [Raymond Goldsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_W._Goldsmith) (1984).[^o67] The estimates by [Peter Temin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Temin), [Angus Maddison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_Maddison), [Branko Milanović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branko_Milanovic) and [Peter Fibiger Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Fibiger_Bang) follow the basic method established by Goldsmith, varying mainly only in their set of initial numbers; these are then stepped up to estimations of the expenditure checked by those on the income side. [Walter Scheidel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scheidel)/Steven Friesen determine GDP on the relationship between certain significant economic indicators which were historically found to be plausible; two independent control assumptions provide the upper and lower limit of the probable size of the Roman GDP.[^o68]

The GDP per capita of the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire), the continuation of the Roman Empire in the east, has been estimated by the [World Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank) economist Branko Milanović to range between \$680 and 770 (in 1990 International Dollars) at its peak around 1000 AD, that is the reign of [Basil II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_II).[^o69] The Byzantine population size at the time is estimated to have been 12 to 18 million.[^o70] This would yield a total GDP somewhere between 8.16 and 12.24 billion dollars.
