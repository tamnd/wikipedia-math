# Rationalisation (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rationalisation"}

In [elementary algebra](wikipedia://en/9710), **root rationalisation** (or **rationalization**) is a process by which [radicals](wikipedia://en/235029) in the [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) of an [algebraic fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29#Algebraic_fractions) are eliminated.

:::

If the denominator is a [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) in some radical, say $a{\sqrt[n]{x}}^k,$ with $k < n$, rationalisation consists of multiplying the numerator and the denominator by $\sqrt[n]{x}^{n - k}$, and replacing ${\sqrt[n]{x}}^n$ by $x$ (this is allowed, as, by definition, a [$n$th root](wikipedia://en/235029) of $x$ is a number that has $x$ as its $n$th power). If $k \ge n$, one writes $k = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$ ([Euclidean division](wikipedia://en/679987)), and ${\sqrt[n]{x}}^k = x^q\sqrt[n]x^r;$ then one proceeds as above by multiplying by $\sqrt[n]{x}^{n - r}.$

If the denominator is [linear](wikipedia://en/152111) in some square root, say $a+b\sqrt{x},$ rationalisation consists of multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the [conjugate](wikipedia://en/7523925) $a-b\sqrt{x},$ and expanding the product in the denominator.

This technique may be extended to any algebraic denominator, by multiplying the numerator and the denominator by all [algebraic conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_conjugate) of the denominator, and expanding the new denominator into the [norm](wikipedia://en/450555) of the old denominator. However, except in special cases, the resulting fractions may have huge numerators and denominators, and, therefore, the technique is generally used only in the above elementary cases.

## Rationalisation of a monomial square root and cube root {#Rationalisation_of_a_monomial_square_root_and_cube_root}

For the fundamental technique, the numerator and denominator must be multiplied by the same factor.

Example 1:

$$\frac{10}{\sqrt{5}}$$

To rationalise this kind of [expression](wikipedia://en/609125), bring in the factor $\sqrt{5}$:

$$\frac{10}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{5}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{{10\sqrt{5}}}{\left(\sqrt{5}\right)^2}$$

The [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) disappears from the denominator, because $\left(\sqrt 5\right)^2= 5$ by definition of a square root:

$$\frac{{10\sqrt{5}}}{\left(\sqrt{5}\right)^2} = \frac{10\sqrt{5}}{5} = 2\sqrt{5},$$

which is the result of the rationalisation.

Example 2:

$$\frac{10}{\sqrt[3]{a}}$$

To rationalise this radical, bring in the factor $\sqrt[3]{a}^2$:

$$\frac{10}{\sqrt[3]{a}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt[3]{a}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt[3]{a}^2}{\sqrt[3]{a}^2} = \frac{{10\sqrt[3]{a}^2}}{\sqrt[3]{a}^3}$$

The cube root disappears from the denominator, because it is cubed; so

$$\frac{{10\sqrt[3]{a}^2}}{\sqrt[3]{a}^3} = \frac{10\sqrt[3]{a}^2}{a},$$

which is the result of the rationalisation.

## Dealing with more square roots {#Dealing_with_more_square_roots}

For a [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) that is:

$$\sqrt{2}\pm \sqrt{3}\,$$

Rationalisation can be achieved by multiplying by the [conjugate](wikipedia://en/7523925):

$$\sqrt{2} \mp \sqrt{3}\,$$

and applying the [difference of two squares](wikipedia://en/1102297) identity, which here will yield −1. To get this result, the entire fraction should be multiplied by

$$\frac{ \sqrt{2}-\sqrt{3} }{\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{3}} = 1.$$

This technique works much more generally. It can easily be adapted to remove one square root at a time, i.e. to rationalise

$$x \pm \sqrt{y}\,$$

by multiplication by

$$x \mp \sqrt{y}$$

Example:

$$\frac{3}{\sqrt{3}\pm \sqrt{5}}$$

The fraction must be multiplied by a quotient containing ${\sqrt{3}\mp \sqrt{5}}$.

$$\frac{3}{\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{5}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{5}} = \frac{3(\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{5})}{\sqrt{3}^2 - \sqrt{5}^2}$$

Now, we can proceed to remove the square roots in the denominator:

$$\frac{{3(\sqrt{3}-\sqrt{5}) }}{\sqrt{3}^2 - \sqrt{5}^2} = \frac{ 3 (\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{5} ) }{ 3 - 5 } = \frac{ 3( \sqrt{3}-\sqrt{5} ) }{-2}$$

Example 2:

This process also works with [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) with $i=\sqrt{-1}$

$$\frac{7}{1\pm \sqrt{-5}}$$

The fraction must be multiplied by a quotient containing ${1\mp \sqrt{-5}}$.

$$\frac{7}{1+\sqrt{-5}} \cdot \frac{1-\sqrt{-5}}{1-\sqrt{-5}} = \frac{7(1-\sqrt{-5})}{1^2 - \sqrt{-5}^2} = \frac{ 7 (1 - \sqrt{-5} ) }{ 1 - (-5) } = \frac{ 7 -7\sqrt{5} i }{6}$$

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Rationalisation can be extended to all [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158) and [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169) (as an application of [norm forms](wikipedia://en/7204666)). For example, to rationalise a [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506), two linear factors involving [cube roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_roots_of_unity) should be used, or equivalently a quadratic factor.

:::
