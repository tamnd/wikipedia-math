# Unilateral shift operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unilateral shift operator"}

In [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675), the **unilateral shift** is a one-sided [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384), that is, a shift operator acting on one-sided [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) or [shift spaces](wikipedia://en/15470598). The term "[operator](wikipedia://en/22213)" is used to draw contrast to finite-dimensional [shift matrices](wikipedia://en/7864525). The term "unilateral" draws a distinction to the [bilateral shift operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_shift_operator), of which the [Baker's map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker's_map) is an example.

:::

Shift operators are commonly studied in the context of [measure-preserving dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/398931). In such general settings, the unilateral shift operator is usually called the [transfer operator](wikipedia://en/1346096) or the Frobenius-Peron operator; its inverse is the [Koopman operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koopman_operator). The properties of shift operators depend very strongly on the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of the spaces on which they act; for example, the [Bernoulli shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_shift) famously has a [discrete spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_spectrum) given by the [Bernoulli polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_polynomial) when acting on the space of bounded [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) on the unit interval, but has a continuous spectrum (on the unit disk), when acting on the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions. When acting on a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270), the eigenfunctions of shift operators are characteristically fractal in shape, often differentiable-nowhere or even continuous-nowhere. Eigenvalues on the unit circle are associated with [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999) time evolution, while those inside the unit disk are conventionally identified with [decaying modes](wikipedia://en/237587) in statistical systems. In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the prototypical unilateral shift operator is the [annihilation operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annihilation_operator) of the [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719); it's eigenfunctions correspond to [coherent states](wikipedia://en/277213).

This article deals primarily with unilateral shifts acting on [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), specifically in two representations: as an operator on the [sequence space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ℓp_space) $\ell^2$, or as a multiplication operator on a [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780). Its properties, particularly its [invariant subspaces](wikipedia://en/585826), are well-understood and serve as a model for more general theories.[^o2][^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\ell^2$ be the Hilbert space of square-summable sequences of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), i.e.,

$$\ell^2 = \left\{ (a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots) : a_n \in \mathbb{C} \text{ and } \sum_{n=0}^\infty |a_n|^2 < \infty \right\}$$

The **unilateral shift** is the [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $S: \ell^2 \to \ell^2$ defined by:

$$S(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots) = (0, a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots)$$

This operator is also called the **forward shift**.

With respect to the standard [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) $(e_n)_{n=0}^\infty$ for $\ell^2$, where $e_n$ is the sequence with a 1 in the *n*-th position and 0 elsewhere, the action of $S$ is $Se_n = e_{n+1}$. Its [matrix representation](wikipedia://en/1523927) is:

$$S = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}$$

This is a [Toeplitz operator](wikipedia://en/151569) whose symbol is the function $f(z) = z$. It can be regarded as an infinite-dimensional [lower shift matrix](wikipedia://en/7864525).

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

### Adjoint operator {#Adjoint_operator}

The [adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_operator) of the unilateral shift, denoted $S^*$, is the **backward shift**. It acts on $\ell^2$ as:

$$S^*(b_0, b_1, b_2, b_3, \dots) = (b_1, b_2, b_3, \dots)$$

The matrix representation of $S^*$ is the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of the matrix for $S$:

$$S^* = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}$$

It can be regarded as an infinite-dimensional upper shift matrix.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

### Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

- $S, S^*$ are both continuous but not [compact](wikipedia://en/577441).
- $$S^*S = I.$$
- $S, S^*$ make up a pair of [unitary equivalence](wikipedia://en/225999) between $\ell^2$ and the set of $\ell^2$-sequences whose first element is zero.

The [resolvent operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolvent_operator) has matrix representation

$$(zI - S)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} z^{-1} & 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ z^{-2} & z^{-1} & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ z^{-3} & z^{-2} & z^{-1} & 0 & \cdots \\ z^{-4} & z^{-3} & z^{-2} & z^{-1} & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix}$$

which is bounded iff $|z| > 1$. Similarly, $(zI - S^*)^{-1} = ((z^*I - S)^{-1})^*$.

For any $z \in \mathbb{C}, a \in \ell^2$ with $\|a \| = 1$,

$$\|(zI - S) a\|^2 = 1 + |z|^2 - 2 \Re (\langle Sa, a \rangle z), \quad \|(zI - S^*) a\|^2 = 1-|a_0|^2 + |z|^2 - 2 \Re (\langle Sa, a \rangle z^*)$$

where $\Re$ is the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part).

:::

### Spectral theory {#Spectral_theory}

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Spectrum of the forward shift"}

**Spectrum of the forward shift.**

Let $\mathbb{D}$ be the open unit disk, $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$ the closed unit disk, and $\mathbb{T}$ the unit circle.

The [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_an_operator) of $S$ is $\sigma(S) = \overline{\mathbb{D}}$.

The [point spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_spectrum) of $S$ is empty: $\sigma_p(S) = \emptyset$.

The [approximate point spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximate_point_spectrum) of $S$ is the unit circle: $\sigma_{ap}(S) = \mathbb{T}$.

:::

::: {#o13 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof.**

To show $\sigma(S) = \overline{\mathbb{D}}$, use the matrix representation of $(zI-S)^{-1}$, and note that it is bounded iff $|z| > 1$. To show $\sigma_p(S) = \emptyset$, directly show that $S a = \lambda a$ implies $a = 0$.

To show $\sigma_{ap}(S) = \mathbb T$, note that $\|(zI - S) a\|^2 \geq 1 + |z|^2 - 2|z| = (1-|z|)^2$ for any $z \in \mathbb{C}, a \in \ell^2$ with $\|a \| = 1$, so $\sigma_{ap}(S) \subset \overline{\mathbb{D}} \setminus \mathbb D = \mathbb T$. Conversely, for any $z \in \mathbb T$, construct the following unit vector

$$a = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}(1, z^{-1}, z^{-2}, \dots, z^{-(N-1)}, 0, 0, \dots)$$

then $\|(zI - S) a\|^2 = 2/N$, which converges to 0 at $N \to \infty$.

:::

The spectral properties of $S^*$ differ significantly from those of $S$:[^o2]

- $\sigma(S^*) = \overline{\mathbb{D}}$ (since $\sigma(A^*) = \overline{\sigma(A)}$).
- The point spectrum $\sigma_p(S^*)$ is the entire open unit disk $\mathbb{D}$. For any $\lambda \in \mathbb{D}$, the corresponding eigenvector is the geometric sequence $(1, \lambda, \lambda^2, \lambda^3, \dots)$.
- The approximate point spectrum $\sigma_{ap}(S^*)$ is the entire closed unit disk $\overline{\mathbb{D}}$. To show this, it remains to show $\mathbb T \subset \sigma_{ap}(S^*)$, which can be proven by a similar construction as before, using $a = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}(1, z^{1}, z^{2}, \dots, z^{(N-1)}, 0, 0, \dots)$.

:::

## Hardy space model {#Hardy_space_model}

The unilateral shift can be studied using [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759).

Define the [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/20041584) $H^2$ as the Hilbert space of [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) $f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n z^n$ on the open unit disk $\mathbb{D}$ for which the sequence of coefficients $(a_n)$ is in $\ell^2$.

Define the [multiplication operator](wikipedia://en/976642) $M_z$ on $H^2$:

$$(M_z f)(z) = zf(z)$$

then $S$ and $M_z$ are unitarily equivalent via the [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999) map $U: \ell^2 \to H^2$ defined by[^o2]

$$U(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n z^n$$

which gives $U^* M_z U = S$. Using this unitary equivalence, it is common in the literature to use $S$ to denote $M_z$ and to treat $H^2$ as the primary setting for the unilateral shift.[^o2]

### Commutant {#Commutant}

The [commutant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutant) of an operator $A$, denoted $\{A\}'$, is the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of all bounded operators that [commute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuting#In_operator_theory_and_quantum_mechanics) with $A$. The commutant of the unilateral shift is the algebra of multiplication operators on $H^2$ by bounded analytic functions.[^o2]

$$\{S\}' = \{M_\varphi : \varphi \in H^\infty\}$$

Here, $H^\infty$ is the space of bounded analytic functions on $\mathbb{D}$, and $(M_\varphi f)(z) = \varphi(z)f(z)$.

### Cyclic vectors {#Cyclic_vectors}

A vector $x$ is a [cyclic vector](wikipedia://en/58713633) for an operator $A$ if the linear span of its orbit $\{A^n x : n \ge 0\}$ is dense in the space. We have:[^o2]

- For the unilateral shift $S$ on $H^2$, the cyclic vectors are the [outer functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_function).
- A function $f \in H^2$ that has a zero in the open unit disk $\mathbb{D}$ is not a cyclic vector. This is because every function in the span of its orbit will also be zero at that point, so the subspace cannot be dense.
- A function $f \in H^2$ that is bounded away from zero (i.e., $\inf_{z \in \mathbb{D}} |f(z)| > 0$) is a cyclic vector.
- A function $f \in H^2$, that is in the open unit disk $\mathbb{D}$ is nonzero but $\inf_{z \in \mathbb{D}} |f(z)| = 0$, may or may not be cyclic. For example, $f(z) = 1-z$ is a cyclic vector.

The cyclic vectors are precisely the [outer functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_function).

### Lattice of invariant subspaces {#Lattice_of_invariant_subspaces}

The $S$-invariant subspaces of $H^2$ are completely characterized analytically. Specifically, they are precisely $M_u(H^2)$ where $u$ is an [inner function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_function).

The $S$-invariant subspaces make up a [lattice of subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357#Lattice_of_subspaces). The two lattice operators, [join and meet](wikipedia://en/3956618), correspond to operations on inner functions.

Given two invariant subspaces $M_u(H^2), M_v(H^2)$, we have $M_u(H^2) \subset M_v(H^2)$ iff $u/v \in H^2$.[^o2]
