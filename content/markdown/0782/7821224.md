# Consequentia mirabilis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Consequentia mirabilis"}

***Consequentia mirabilis*** ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for "admirable consequence"), also known as **[Clavius](wikipedia://en/633645)'s Law**, is used in [traditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_logic) and [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) to establish the truth of a proposition from the [inconsistency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistency_proof) of its negation.[^o2] It is thus related to *[reductio ad absurdum](wikipedia://en/22456146)*, but it can prove a proposition using just its own negation and the concept of consistency. For a more concrete formulation, it states that if a proposition is a consequence of its negation, then it is true, for consistency. In formal notation:

:::

$$(\neg A \to A) \to A.$$

Weaker variants of the principle are provable in [minimal logic](wikipedia://en/26267678), but the full principle itself is not provable even in [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262).

## History {#History}

*Consequentia mirabilis* was a pattern of argument popular in 17th-century Europe that first appeared in a fragment of Aristotle's *[Protrepticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protrepticus_%28Aristotle%29):* "If we ought to philosophise, then we ought to philosophise; and if we ought not to philosophise, then we ought to philosophise (i.e. in order to justify this view); in any case, therefore, we ought to philosophise."[^o4]

Barnes claims in passing that the term *consequentia mirabilis* refers only to the inference of the proposition from the inconsistency of its negation, and that the term *Lex Clavia* (or Clavius' Law) refers to the inference of the proposition's negation from the inconsistency of the proposition.[^o5]

## Derivations {#Derivations}

The first demonstration purposely makes use of only rather conservative logical assumptions, up to the last step:

Firstly, that implications can be chained together to obtain yet another one is a consequence of [Frege's theorem](wikipedia://en/4725226). This states

$$\big(B \rightarrow (C\to D)\big) \to \Big((B \rightarrow C) \to (B\to D)\Big)$$

For $D=\bot$ reduces to a form of [negation introduction](wikipedia://en/42211842), and indeed for further $B=C$ and using the [law of identity](wikipedia://en/1403190), one obtains

$$\big(B \rightarrow \neg B\big) \rightarrow \neg B$$

Now for $B=\neg A$, it follows that $(\neg A \to \neg \neg A) \to \neg \neg A$. By [implication introduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implication_introduction), this is indeed an *equivalence*,

$$(\neg A \to \neg \neg A) \leftrightarrow \neg \neg A.$$

Minimal logic lacks [excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excluded_middle) and the [principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394). But already here, the first double-negation in the original implication can optionally also be removed, *weakening* the forward direction statement to

$$(\neg A \to A) \to \neg \neg A.$$

Hence, consequentia mirabilis thus holds whenever $\neg \neg A \leftrightarrow A$.

### Minimal logic {#Minimal_logic}

The variant with the equivalence above shows that minimal logic validates that a proposition $A$ cannot be rejected ($\neg\neg A$) exactly if this is implied by its negation ($\neg A \to \neg \neg A$). Further, that $A$ holds exactly when it is implied by both $\neg A$ and $\neg \neg A$.

The weak form $(\neg A\to A) \to \neg (\neg A)$ can also be seen to be equivalent to the [principle of non-contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_non-contradiction) $\neg\big(A\land\neg A\big)$. To this end, first note that using [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900) and implication introduction, the latter principle is equivalent to $\neg\big((\neg A\to A)\land(\neg A)\big)$. The claim now follows from $(B\to\neg C)\leftrightarrow\neg(B\land C)$, i.e. the fact that there are equivalent characterizations of two propositions being mutually exclusive.

#### Equivalence to excluded middle {#Equivalence_to_excluded_middle}

The negation of any excluded middle disjunction $B\lor\neg B$ in particular implies $\neg B$, and thus the weaker disjunction $B\lor\neg B$ itself. From the above weak form, it thus follows that any double-negated excluded middle statement is valid, in minimal logic. Likewise, this argument shows how the full consequentia mirabilis implies excluded middle.

The following argument shows that the converse also holds. A principle related to case analysis may be formulated as such: If both $B$ and $C$ each imply $A$, and either of them must hold, then $A$ follows. Formally,

$$\big((B\to A)\land (C\to A)\land (B\lor C)\big)\to A$$

For $B=A$ and $C=\neg A$, the [principle of identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_identity) now entails

$$\big((\neg A\to A)\land (A\lor\neg A)\big)\to A$$

Whenever the second conjunct of the antecedent, $A\lor\neg A$, is true, then so is the instance of the principle.

### Intuitionistic logic {#Intuitionistic_logic}

One has that $(A\to B)\to A$ implies $(A\to B)\to(A\land B)$. By [conjunction elimination](wikipedia://en/788704), this is in fact an equivalence. In particular, one has

$$\big(\neg A\to A\big) \leftrightarrow \big(\neg A\to(A \land \bot)\big)$$

The right hand of this also implies $\neg\neg A$, which gives another demonstration of how double-negation elimination implies consequentia mirabilis, in minimal logic.

The principle of explosion itself may be formulated as $\bot\to (A\land \bot)$, and therefore $\bot\leftrightarrow (A\land \bot)$. As explosion is valid in intuitionistic logic, here the above reduces to an equivalence,

$$(\neg A\to A) \leftrightarrow \neg \neg A$$

For what it's worth, this also gives a derivation of double-negation elimination for statements that have already been rejected. In other words, in intuitionistic logic $\neg A\vdash \neg \neg A\to A$. Compare this with explosion in general.

### Further classical derivations {#Further_classical_derivations}

Above it was established how consequentia mirabilis is equivalent to excluded middle over minimal logic.

A quick derivation from double-negation elimination was demonstrated as well. What follows is two more lines or reasoning:

- When excluded middle holds for $A$, one [contraposition](wikipedia://en/8556497) gives $(B\to A)\to (A\lor \neg B)$, the propositional form of the reverse [disjunctive syllogism](wikipedia://en/7963). The law is now obtained with $B=\neg A$ and, again, double-negation elimination.
- Related to the last intuitionistic derivation given above, consequentia mirabilis also follows as the special case of [Peirce's law](wikipedia://en/47261948) $\big((A\to B)\to A\big) \to A$ for $B=\bot$. That article can be consulted for more, related equivalences.
