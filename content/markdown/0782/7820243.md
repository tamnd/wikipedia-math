# Distributive law between monads

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Distributive law between monads"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), an abstract branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **distributive laws** between [monads](wikipedia://en/355140) are a way to express abstractly that two [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) [distribute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_law) one over the other.

:::

Suppose that $(S, \mu^S, \eta^S)$ and $(T, \mu^T, \eta^T)$ are two monads on a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) **C**. In general, there is no natural monad structure on the composite [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) *ST*. However, there is a natural monad structure on the functor *ST* if there is a distributive law of the monad *S* over the monad *T*.

Formally, a **distributive law** of the monad *S* over the monad *T* is a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022)

$$l:TS\to ST$$

such that the diagrams

*Image: Distributive law monads mult1.svg.* Distributive law monads mult1

*Image: Distributive law monads unit1.svg.* Distributive law monads unit1

*Image: Distributive law monads mult2.svg.* Distributive law monads mult2

*Image: Distributive law monads unit2.svg.* Distributive law monads unit2

[commute](wikipedia://en/138484).

This law induces a composite monad *ST* with

- as multiplication: $STST\xrightarrow{SlT}SSTT\xrightarrow{\mu^S\mu^T}ST$,
- as unit: $1\xrightarrow{\eta^S\eta^T}ST$.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Monoids {#Monoids}

Informally, one might say that the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) on a set is given by "the [free semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_semigroup), plus an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962)". We can formalise this intuition, using the fact that both the free semigroup and the "free element" functors are monads.[^o5] Explicitly, consider the monads

$$T(X) := \sum_{n \ge 1} X^{n}$$

$$S(X) := X+1,$$

(here addition denotes [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946)), with unit maps given by:

$$\eta^{T}_{X} : X \to T(X)$$

$$\eta^T_X(x) := x \in X^1$$

$$\eta^S_X : X \to S(X)$$

$$\eta^S_X(x) := x \in X.$$

The multiplication map $\mu^S_X : X+2 \to X+1$ sends both elements of 2 to that of 1, but the multiplication on $T$ is a bit more tricky to describe. First, note that an arbitrary element of $T(X)$ is just a finite (nonempty) string of elements of $X$. The multiplication map $T^2(X) \to T(X)$ should therefore take a string of words on $X$ to one word. We choose $\mu^T_X(list)$ to be the concatenation of all (finitely many) words in $list$.

What does a distributive law $l : TS \to ST$ mean here? It takes a word on the set $X+1$ to either a word on $X$, or the disjoint point in $1$. We choose the map that interprets the unit element $e \in 1$ as the empty word; explicitly:

$$l(w) = w-e,$$

where $w-e$ is the word derived from $w$ by removing all instances of $e$, unless $w$ is all $e$'s, in which case it's defined to be $e \in 1$. The idea, in short, is this: if we view words on $X$ as formal products of elements (i.e., the free semigroup operation), then $l$ encodes that the freely added element $e$ can be incorporated into the semigroup as a unit element (thereby making the structure into a monoid). Let us check that all the diagrams in the definition commute.

- $$\mu^S_{T(X)} \circ S(l_X) \circ l_{S(X)} = l_X \circ T(\mu^S_X)$$

$$LHS: w \mapsto (w-e') \mapsto (w-e'-e) \mapsto (w-e'-e)$$

$$RHS: w \mapsto w[e/e'] \mapsto (w[e/e']-e),$$

$e,e'$ being the elements of each copy of $1$ in $X+1+1$, and where $w[e/e']$ denotes the word $w$ with every instance of $e'$ replaced by $e$.

- $$l_X \circ T(\eta^S_X) = \eta^S_{T(X)}$$

$$LHS: w \mapsto w \mapsto w-e$$

$$RHS: w \mapsto w,$$

and note that for $w \in T(X)$ we have $w-e = w$, as required.

- $$S(\mu^T_X) \circ l_{T(X)} \circ T(l_X) = l_X \circ \mu^T_{S(X)}$$

$$LHS: w \mapsto w[(v-e)/v] \mapsto w[(v-e)/v]-e \mapsto concat(w[(v-e)/v]-e)$$

$$RHS: w \mapsto concat(w) \mapsto concat(w)-e,$$

where: $concat(w)$ is the concatenation of all words in $w$, i.e. the multiplication on $T$; and $w[(v-e)/v]$ is the result of replacing every word $v$ in $w$ with $(v-e)$.

- $$l_X \circ \eta^T_{S(X)} = S(\eta^T_X)$$

$$LHS: (x \in X \ or \ e \in 1) \mapsto (x \ or \ e) \mapsto (x \ or \ e) - e$$

$$RHS: (x \ or \ e) \mapsto (x \ or \ e).$$

In summary, the four commutation requirements encode the following ideas:

1. "Deleting" elements $w-e'$ behaves like replacing them with the identity, $w[e/e']$.
1. Any word $w$ on $X$ doesn't contain the element $e$, so $w-e = w$. In other words, $X+1$ truly is a disjoint union.
1. In a list of words $w = (v_1,...,v_n)$, removing any instance of $e$ in $v_i$, removing any remaining "empty word" $v_i = e$, and concatenating the resulting list, yields the same result as first concatenating $v_1v_2...v_n$ then removing all instances of $e$.
1. $x-e = x$, as already covered in 2; and $e-e = e$, meaning we should interpret $e$ as the empty word.

### Rings {#Rings}

This is the original example given by Beck,[^o9] and is the motivation for the term "distributive law". Consider the two monads $G,M:\mathbf{Set} \to \mathbf{Set}$ which send a set $X$ to (the underlying set of) the [free commutative group](wikipedia://en/59735#Free_abelian_group) and the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) on it, respectively. We shall regard the [formal group](wikipedia://en/480335) operation as addition, and that of the monoid as multiplication. In this case, both composites $GM$ and $MG$ give the [free ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_ring) functor. We can now use the fact that the free ring admits a distributive law $MG \to GM$ to show that it's also a monad.

The distributive law of G over M means exactly that a product of sums (i.e. an element of $M(G(X))$) can be identified with a sum of products (an element of $G(M(X))$). Note that we say $G(X)$, the additive group, distributes over $M(X)$, the multiplicative monoid - contrary to the usual convention; see the next section.

:::

::: {#o8 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

The original description by Beck calls $l: TS \to ST$ a distributive law *of S over T*.[^o9] However, some authors prefer the reverse terminology, where $l$ is instead said to be a distributive law *of T over S*; this is because, arguably, the latter convention corresponds more closely to the usual distributive law between multiplication and addition - multiplication is typically said to distribute *over addition*, by which one means that a *product of sums* can be rewritten as a *sum of products* $\prod \sum \to \sum \prod$ (see [Examples](wikipedia://en/7820243#Examples)). Nevertheless, both terminologies are in use in different sources, and the original is what this article uses.

:::

## Related notions {#Related_notions}

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="BD-law"}

### BD-law {#BD-law}

In the case where $T=S$, the distributive law map reduces to $K:T^2 \Rightarrow T^2$, and one may impose some additional conditions. $K$ is called a **BD-law** on $T$ if:

***(D)*** $K$ is a distributive law, and

***(B)*** it satisfies the [Yang-Baxter equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang-Baxter_equation):

$$K_{T(X)} \circ T(K_X) \circ K_{T(X)} = T(K_X) \circ K_{T(X)} \circ T(K_X)$$

(as maps $T^3(X) \to T^3(X)$).

A *BD-law* is a **BCD-law** if the multiplication on $T$ is "$K$-commutative", meaning that

***(C)*** $\mu^T_X \circ K_X = \mu^T_X$

(as maps $T^2(X) \to T(X)$).

Condition ***(B)*** is called a Yang-Baxter equation because it literally is the Yang-Baxter equation defined on [monoid objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_object), when $T:\mathbf{C} \to \mathbf{C}$ is viewed as a monoid in the category of [endofunctors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endofunctor) on $\mathbf{C}$.[^o14]

:::

### Entwining {#Entwining}

For $P$ an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) and $C$ a [coalgebra](wikipedia://en/310886) over a shared [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $k$, an **entwining** between them is a linear map $\psi:C \otimes P \to P \otimes C$ satisfying analogous conditions to those that would be required, were $\psi$ a distributive law. Specifically, the commutation diagram $CCP \to PC$ that would be induced by a hypothetical multiplication $CC \to C$ is replaced with one for $C \otimes P \to P \otimes C \otimes C$ induced by the comultiplication $C \to C \otimes C$; likewise, the triangle diagram $P \to PC$ is replaced with one of type $C \otimes P \to P \otimes k$, induced by the counit.[^o16]
