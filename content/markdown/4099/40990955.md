# Factorial moment measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Factorial moment measure"}

In [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a **factorial moment measure** is a [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) quantity, [function](wikipedia://en/185427) or, more precisely, [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) that is defined in relation to [mathematical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_objects) known as [point processes](wikipedia://en/2147685), which are types of [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) often used as [mathematical models](wikipedia://en/20590) of physical phenomena representable as [randomly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) positioned [points](wikipedia://en/593693) in [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time), [space](wikipedia://en/27667) or both. Moment measures generalize the idea of [factorial moments](wikipedia://en/790823), which are useful for studying [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563)-valued [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables).[^o2]

:::

The first factorial moment measure of a point process coincides with its [first moment measure](wikipedia://en/40969212#First_moment_measure) or *intensity measure*,[^o11] which gives the [expected](wikipedia://en/9653) or [average](wikipedia://en/60167) number of points of the point process located in some region of space. In general, if the number of points in some region is considered as a random variable, then the moment factorial measure of this region is the factorial moment of this random variable.[^o3] Factorial moment measures completely characterize a wide class of point processes, which means they can be used to uniquely identify a point process.

If a factorial moment measure is [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous), then with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) it is said to have a *density* (which is a generalized form of a [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921)), and this density is known by a number of names such as **factorial moment density** and **product density**, as well as *coincidence density*,[^o2] *joint intensity*[^o4] , *correlation function* or *multivariate frequency spectrum*[^o5] The first and second factorial moment densities of a point process are used in the definition of the *pair correlation function*, which gives a way to statistically quantify the strength of interaction or [correlation](wikipedia://en/157057) between points of a point process.[^o6]

Factorial moment measures serve as useful tools in the study of point processes[^o2][^o6][^o7] as well as the related fields of [stochastic geometry](wikipedia://en/22037813)[^o3] and [spatial statistics](wikipedia://en/9467349),[^o6][^o8] which are applied in various [scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) disciplines such as [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), and [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications).[^o2][^o3][^o10]

## Point process notation {#Point_process_notation}

Point processes are mathematical objects that are defined on some underlying [mathematical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_space). Since these processes are often used to represent collections of points randomly scattered in space, time or both, the underlying space is usually *d*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) denoted here by **R**<sup>d</sup>, but they can be defined on more [abstract](wikipedia://en/454446) mathematical spaces.[^o7]

Point processes have a number of interpretations, which is reflected by the various types of [point process notation](wikipedia://en/40765261).[^o3][^o10] For example, if a point $\textstyle x$ belongs to or is a member of a point process, denoted by *N*, then this can be written as:[^o3]

$$\textstyle x\in {N},$$

and represents the point process being interpreted as a random [set](wikipedia://en/26691). Alternatively, the number of points of *N* located in some [Borel set](wikipedia://en/43327) *B* is often written as:[^o11][^o3][^o8]

$$\textstyle {N}(B),$$

which reflects a [random measure](wikipedia://en/10040846) interpretation for point processes. These two notations are often used in parallel or interchangeably.[^o3][^o8][^o11]

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### *n*-th factorial power of a point process {#n-th_factorial_power_of_a_point_process}

For some positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $\textstyle n=1,2,\ldots$, the $\textstyle n$-th factorial power of a point process $\textstyle {N}$ on $\textstyle \textbf{R}^d$ is defined as:[^o11]

$${N}^{(n)}(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)= \sum_{(x_1\neq\dots\neq x_n)\in {N} } \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}_{B_i}(x_i)$$

where $\textstyle B_1,...,B_n$ is a collection of not necessarily [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) Borel sets in $\textstyle \textbf{R}^d$, which form an $\textstyle n$-fold [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of sets denoted by:

$$B_1\times\cdots\times B_n.$$

The symbol $\textstyle \mathbf{1}$ denotes an [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) such that $\textstyle \mathbf{1}_{B_1}$ is a [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) for the set $\textstyle B_n$. The [summation](wikipedia://en/246160) in the above expression is performed over all $\textstyle n$-[tuples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuples) of distinct points, including [permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutations), which can be contrasted with the definition of the [*n*-th power of a point process](wikipedia://en/40969212#n-th_power_of_a_point_process). The symbol $\textstyle \Pi$ denotes [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845#Capital_Pi_notation) while the existence of various [point process notation](wikipedia://en/40765261) means that the *n*-th factorial power of a point process is sometimes defined using other notation.[^o11]

### *n*-th factorial moment measure {#n-th_factorial_moment_measure}

The *n*-th factorial moment measure or *n*-th order factorial moment measure is defined as:

$$M^{(n)}(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)=E [{N}^{(n)}(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)],$$

where the *E* denotes the [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653) ([operator](wikipedia://en/22213#Probability_theory)) of the point process *N*. In other words, the *n*-th factorial moment measure is the expectation of the *n*-th factorial power of some point process.

The *n*-th factorial moment measure of a point process *N* is equivalently defined[^o3] by:

$$\int_{\textbf{R}^{n d}}f(x_1,\ldots,x_n) M^{(n)}(dx_1,\ldots,dx_n)=E \left[ \sum_{(x_1\neq\cdots\neq x_n)\in {N} } f(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \right],$$

where $f$ is any non-negative [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) on $\textbf{R}^{n d}$, and the above summation is performed over all $n$ tuples of distinct points, including permutations. Consequently, the factorial moment measure is defined such that there are no points repeating in the product set, as opposed to the moment measure.[^o7]

### First factorial moment measure {#First_factorial_moment_measure}

The first factorial moment measure $\textstyle M^1$ coincides with the [first moment measure](wikipedia://en/40969212#First_moment_measure):[^o11]

$$M^{(1)}(B)=M^1(B)=E [{N}(B)],$$

where $\textstyle M^1$ is known, among other terms, as the *intensity measure*[^o3] or *mean measure*,[^o17] and is interpreted as the expected number of points of $\textstyle {N}$ found or located in the set $\textstyle B$

### Second factorial moment measure {#Second_factorial_moment_measure}

The second factorial moment measure for two Borel sets $\textstyle A$ and $\textstyle B$ is:

$$M^{(2)}(A\times B)=M^2(A\times B)-M^1(A\cap B).$$

:::

## Name explanation {#Name_explanation}

For some Borel set $\textstyle B$, the namesake of this measure is revealed when the $\textstyle n\,$th factorial moment measure reduces to:

$$M^{(n)}(B\times\cdots\times B)=E [{N}(B)({N}(B)-1)\cdots ({N}(B)-n+1)],$$

which is the $\textstyle n\,$-th [factorial moment](wikipedia://en/790823) of the random variable $\textstyle {N}(B)$.[^o3]

## Factorial moment density {#Factorial_moment_density}

If a factorial moment measure is [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous), then it has a density (or more precisely, a [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) or density) with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) and this density is known as the *factorial moment density* or *product density*, *joint intensity*, *correlation function*, or *multivariate frequency spectrum*. Denoting the $\textstyle n$-th factorial moment density by $\textstyle \mu^{(n)}(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$, it is defined in respect to the equation:[^o3]

$$M^{(n)}(B_1\times\ldots\times B_n)=\int_{B_1}\cdots\int_{B_n}\mu^{(n)}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) dx_1\cdots dx_n.$$

Furthermore, this means the following expression

$$E \left[ \sum_{(x_1\neq\cdots\neq x_n)\in {N} } f(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \right]= \int_{\textbf{R}^{n d}} f(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \mu^{(n)}(x_1,\ldots,x_n) dx_1\cdots dx_n,$$

where $\textstyle f$ is any non-negative [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) measurable function defined on $\textstyle \textbf{R}^{ n}$.

## Pair correlation function {#Pair_correlation_function}

In spatial statistics and stochastic geometry, to measure the statistical [correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_and_dependence) relationship between points of a point process, the *pair correlation function* of a point process ${N}$ is defined as:[^o3][^o6]

$$\rho(x_1,x_2)=\frac{\mu^{(2)}(x_1,x_2)}{\mu^{(1)}(x_1) \mu^{(1)}(x_2) },$$

where the points $x_1,x_2\in R^d$. In general, $\rho(x_1,x_2)\geq 0$ whereas $\rho(x_1,x_2)=1$ corresponds to no correlation (between points) in the typical statistical sense.[^o6]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Poisson point process {#Poisson_point_process}

For a [general Poisson point process](wikipedia://en/41597450#General_Poisson_point_process) with intensity measure $\textstyle \Lambda$ the $\textstyle n$-th factorial moment measure is given by the expression:[^o3]

$$M^{(n)}(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)=\prod_{i=1}^n[\Lambda(B_i)],$$

where $\textstyle \Lambda$ is the intensity measure or first moment measure of $\textstyle {N}$, which for some Borel set $\textstyle B$ is given by:

$$\Lambda(B)=M^1(B)=E[{N}(B)].$$

For a [homogeneous Poisson point process](wikipedia://en/41597450#Homogeneous_Poisson_point_process) the $\textstyle n$-th factorial moment measure is simply:[^o11]

$$M^{(n)}(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)=\lambda^n \prod_{i=1}^n |B_i|,$$

where $\textstyle |B_i|$ is the length, area, or volume (or more generally, the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)) of $\textstyle B_i$. Furthermore, the $\textstyle n$-th factorial moment density is:[^o3]

$$\mu^{(n)}(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=\lambda^n.$$

The pair-correlation function of the homogeneous Poisson point process is simply

$$\rho(x_1,x_2)=1,$$

which reflects the lack of interaction between points of this point process.

:::

## Factorial moment expansion {#Factorial_moment_expansion}

The expectations of general [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) of simple point processes, provided some certain mathematical conditions, have (possibly infinite) expansions or [series](wikipedia://en/15287) consisting of the corresponding factorial moment measures.[^o26][^o27] In comparison to the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448), which consists of a series of [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of some function, the *n*th factorial moment measure plays the roll as that of the *n* th derivative the Taylor series. In other words, given a general functional *f* of some simple point process, then this [Taylor-like theorem](wikipedia://en/51714) for non-Poisson point processes means an expansion exists for the expectation of the function *E*, provided some mathematical condition is satisfied, which ensures convergence of the expansion.
