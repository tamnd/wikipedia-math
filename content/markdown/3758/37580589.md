# Locally finite operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Locally finite operator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $f: V\to V$ is called **locally finite** if the [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_space) $V$ is the union of a family of finite-dimensional $f$-[invariant subspaces](wikipedia://en/585826).[^o2][^o3]

:::

In other words, there exists a family $\{ V_i\vert i\in I\}$ of linear subspaces of $V$, such that we have the following:

- $$\bigcup_{i\in I} V_i=V$$
- $$(\forall i\in I) f[V_i]\subseteq V_i$$
- Each $V_i$ is finite-dimensional.

An equivalent condition only requires $V$ to be spanned by finite-dimensional $f$-invariant subspaces.[^o4][^o5] If $V$ is also a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), sometimes an operator is called locally finite when the sum of the $\{ V_i\vert i\in I\}$ is only [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $V$.[^o3]

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Every linear operator on a finite-dimensional space is trivially locally finite.
- Every [diagonalizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonalizable) (i.e. there exists a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of $V$ whose elements are all [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of $f$) linear operator is locally finite, because it is the union of subspaces spanned by finitely many eigenvectors of $f$.
- The operator on $\mathbb{C}[x]$, the space of polynomials with complex coefficients, defined by $T(f(x))=xf(x)$, is *not* locally finite; any $T$-invariant subspace is of the form $\mathbb{C}[x]f_0(x)$ for some $f_0(x)\in\mathbb{C}[x]$, and so has infinite (or zero) dimension.
- The operator on $\mathbb{C}[x]$ defined by $T(f(x))=\frac{f(x)-f(0)}{x}$ is locally finite; for any $n$, the polynomials of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_%28polynomials%29) at most $n$ form a $T$-invariant subspace.[^o8]

:::
