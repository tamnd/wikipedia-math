# Necklace polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Necklace polynomial"}

In [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) mathematics, the **necklace polynomial**, or **Moreau's necklace-counting function,** introduced by (txt), counts the number of distinct [necklaces](wikipedia://en/6226587) of $n$ colored beads chosen out of $k$ available colors, arranged in a cycle. Unlike the usual problem of [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/1460126#Examples), the necklaces are assumed to be aperiodic (not composed from a repeated subsequence), and counted up to rotation (rotating the beads around the necklace counts as the same necklace), but without flipping over (reversing the order of the beads counts as a different necklace). This counting function also describes the dimensions in a free Lie algebra and the number of irreducible polynomials over a finite field.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The necklace polynomials are a family of polynomials $M_n(k)$ in the variable $k$ such that

$$k^n \ =\ \sum_{d\,|\,n} d \, M_d(k).$$

By [Möbius inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_inversion) they are given by

$$M_n(k) \ =\ {1\over n}\sum_{d\,|\,n}\mu\!\left({n \over d}\right)k^d,$$

where $\mu$ is the classic [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961).

A closely related family, called the **general necklace polynomial** or **general necklace-counting function**, is:

$$N_n(k)\ =\ \sum_{d\,|\,n} M_d(k)\ =\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\,|\,n}\varphi\!\left({n \over d}\right)k^d,$$

where $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452).

:::

::: {#o6 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The necklace polynomials $M_n(k)$ appear as:

- The number of [aperiodic necklaces](wikipedia://en/6226587#aperiodic_necklaces) (or equivalently [Lyndon words](wikipedia://en/3919355)), which are cyclic arrangements of $n$ colored beads having $k$ available colors. Two such necklaces are considered equal if they are related by a rotation (not considering reflections). *Aperiodic* refers to necklaces without [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096), having *$n$* distinct rotations. Correspondingly, $N_n(k)$ gives the number of [necklaces](wikipedia://en/6226587) including the periodic ones: this is easily computed using [Pólya theory](wikipedia://en/4396962).
- The dimension of the degree *$n$* component of the [free Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/3174646) on *$k$* generators ("Witt's formula"[^o7]), or equivalently the number of [Hall words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_set) of length *$n$*. Correspondingly, $N_n(k)$ should be the dimension of the degree *$n$* component of a free [Jordan algebra](wikipedia://en/936371).
- The number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree *$n$* over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with *$k$* elements (when $k=p^d$ is a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483)). Correspondingly, $N_n(k)$ is the number of polynomials which are [primary](wikipedia://en/3021435) (a power of an irreducible).
- The exponent in the [cyclotomic identity](wikipedia://en/429966): $\textstyle {1 \over 1-k z}\ =\ \prod_{j=1}^\infty\left({1 \over 1-z^j}\right)^{\! M_j(k)}$.

Although these various types of objects are all counted by the same polynomial, their precise relationships remain unclear. For example, there is no canonical [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the irreducible polynomials and the Lyndon words.[^o8] However, there is a non-canonical bijection as follows. For any degree *$n$* monic [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) over a field *$F$* with $k=p^d$ elements, its roots lie in a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) field *$L$* with $k^n$ elements. One may choose an element $x\in L$ such that $\{x,\sigma x, ...,\sigma^{n-1} x\}$ is an *$F$*-basis for *$L$* (a [normal basis](wikipedia://en/1028589)), where *σ* is the [Frobenius automorphism](wikipedia://en/1088270) $\sigma y = y^k$. Then the bijection can be defined by taking a necklace, viewed as an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of functions $f:\{1,...,n\}\rightarrow F$, to the irreducible polynomial

> $\phi(T)=(T-y)(T-\sigma y)\cdots (T-\sigma^{n-1}y) \in F[T]$ for $y=f(1)x+f(2)\sigma x+\cdots+f(n)\sigma^{n-1} x$.

Different cyclic rearrangements of $f$, i.e. different representatives of the same necklace equivalence class, yield cyclic rearrangements of the factors of $\phi(T)$, so this correspondence is well-defined.[^o9]

:::

## Relations between *M* and *N* {#Relations_between_M_and_N}

The polynomials for $M_n$ and $N_n$ are easily related in terms of [Dirichlet convolution](wikipedia://en/179017) of arithmetic functions $\textstyle f(n)*g(n) = \sum_{d|n}f(n/d)\,g(d)$, regarding $k$ as a constant.

- The formula for *$M_n$* gives $n\,M_n \,=\, \mu(n)*k^n$,
- The formula for *$N_n$* gives $n\,N_n \,=\, \varphi(n)*k^n \,=\, n*\mu(n)*k^n$.
- Their relation gives $N_n\,=\,1*M_n$ or equivalently $n\,N_n \,=\, n*(n\,M_n)$, since the function $f(n)=n$ is [completely multiplicative](wikipedia://en/1055940#Properties).

Any two of these imply the third, for example:

$$n*\mu(n)*k^n \,=\, n\,N_n \,=\, n*(n\,M_n) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu(n)*k^n = n\,M_n$$

by cancellation in the [Dirichlet algebra](wikipedia://en/8984724).

## Examples {#Examples}

$$\begin{aligned} M_n(1) & = 0 \text{ if }n>1 \\[6pt] M_1(k) & = k \\[6pt] M_2(k) & = \tfrac12 (k^2-k) \\[6pt] M_3(k) & = \tfrac13 (k^3-k) \\[6pt] M_4(k) & = \tfrac14 (k^4-k^2) \\[6pt] M_5(k) & = \tfrac15(k^5-k) \\[6pt] M_6(k) & = \tfrac16(k^6-k^3-k^2+k) \\[6pt] M_p(k) & = \tfrac1p (k^{p}-k) & \text{ if }p\text{ is prime} \\[6pt] M_{p^N}(k) & = \tfrac1{p^N}(k^{p^N}-k^{p^{N-1}}) & \text{ if }p\text{ is prime} \end{aligned}$$

For $k=2$, starting with length zero, these form the [integer sequence](wikipedia://en/51421)

1, 2, 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18, 30, 56, 99, 186, 335, ...

:::

## Identities {#Identities}

The polynomials obey various combinatorial identities, given by Metropolis & Rota:

$$M_n(k\ell, n) =\sum_{\operatorname{lcm}(i,j)=n} \gcd(i,j)M_i(k)M_j(\ell),$$

where "gcd" is [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) and "lcm" is [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961). More generally,

$$M_n(k\ell\cdots m) =\sum_{\operatorname{lcm}(i,j,\ldots,r)=n} \gcd(i,j,\cdots,r)M_i(k)M_j(\ell)\cdots M_r(m),$$

which also implies:

$$M_n(k^m) =\sum_{\operatorname{lcm}(j,m)=nm} \frac{j}{n} M_j(k).$$
