# Moving frame

*Image: Frenet-Serret moving frame1.png.* The [Frenet–Serret frame](wikipedia://en/666987) on a curve is the simplest example of a moving frame.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Moving frame"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **moving frame** is a flexible generalization of the notion of a [coordinate frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_frame) (an [ordered basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_basis) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), in conjunction with an [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432)) often used to study the [extrinsic differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) of [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) embedded in a [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325).

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

In lay terms, a *[frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference)* is a system of [measuring rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measuring_rod) used by an [observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation) to measure the surrounding space by providing [coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706). A **moving frame** is then a frame of reference which moves with the observer along a [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) (a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246)). The method of the moving frame, in this simple example, seeks to produce a "preferred" moving frame out of the [kinematic](wikipedia://en/65914) properties of the observer. In a geometrical setting, this problem was solved in the mid 19th century by [Jean Frédéric Frenet](wikipedia://en/1047369) and [Joseph Alfred Serret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Alfred_Serret).[^o3] The [Frenet–Serret frame](wikipedia://en/666987) is a moving frame defined on a curve which can be constructed purely from the [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) and [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) of the curve.[^o4]

The Frenet–Serret frame plays a key role in the [differential geometry of curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry_of_curves), ultimately leading to a more or less complete classification of smooth curves in Euclidean space up to [congruence](wikipedia://en/39330).[^o5] The [Frenet–Serret formulas](wikipedia://en/666987) show that there is a pair of functions defined on the curve, the [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039281) and [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770), which are obtained by [differentiating](wikipedia://en/7921) the frame, and which describe completely how the frame evolves in time along the curve. A key feature of the general method is that a preferred moving frame, provided it can be found, gives a complete kinematic description of the curve.

*Image: Darboux trihedron.svg.* Darboux trihedron, consisting of a point *P*, and a triple of [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221) [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) **e**<sub>1</sub>, **e**<sub>2</sub>, and **e**<sub>3</sub> which is *adapted to a surface* in the sense that *P* lies on the surface, and **e**<sub>3</sub> is perpendicular to the surface.

In the late 19th century, [Gaston Darboux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaston_Darboux) studied the problem of constructing a preferred moving frame on a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) in Euclidean space instead of a curve, the [Darboux frame](wikipedia://en/11067444) (or the *trièdre mobile* as it was then called). It turned out to be impossible in general to construct such a frame, and that there were [integrability conditions](wikipedia://en/968734) which needed to be satisfied first.[^o3]

Later, moving frames were developed extensively by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) and others in the study of submanifolds of more general [homogeneous spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_spaces) (such as [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135)). In this setting, a **frame** carries the geometric idea of a basis of a vector space over to other sorts of geometrical spaces ([Klein geometries](wikipedia://en/2908224)). Some examples of frames are:[^o5]

- A **[linear frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_frame)** is an [ordered basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_basis) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).
- An **[orthonormal frame](wikipedia://en/350995)** of a vector space is an ordered basis consisting of [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) (an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063)).
- An **[affine frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_frame)** of an affine space consists of a choice of [origin](wikipedia://en/298834) along with an ordered basis of vectors in the associated [difference space](wikipedia://en/298834).[^o6]
- A **[Cartesian frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_frame)** of an affine space is a choice of origin along with an orthonormal basis of the difference space.
- A **[projective frame](wikipedia://en/763902)** on *n*-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) is an ordered collection of *n*+2 points such that any subset of *n*+1 points is [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent).
- [Frame fields in general relativity](wikipedia://en/2336109) are four-dimensional frames, or [vierbeins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vierbein), in German.

In each of these examples, the collection of all frames is [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325) in a certain sense. In the case of linear frames, for instance, any two frames are related by an element of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564). Projective frames are related by the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667). This homogeneity, or symmetry, of the class of frames captures the geometrical features of the linear, affine, Euclidean, or projective landscape. A moving frame, in these circumstances, is just that: a frame which varies from point to point.

Formally, a frame on a [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325) *G*/*H* consists of a point in the tautological bundle *G* → *G*/*H*. A ***moving frame*** is a section of this bundle. It is *moving* in the sense that as the point of the base varies, the frame in the fibre changes by an element of the symmetry group *G*. A moving frame on a submanifold *M* of *G*/*H* is a section of the [pullback](wikipedia://en/1665392) of the tautological bundle to *M*. Intrinsically[^o7] a moving frame can be defined on a [principal bundle](wikipedia://en/310950) *P* over a manifold. In this case, a moving frame is given by a *G*-equivariant mapping φ : *P* → *G*, thus *framing* the manifold by elements of the Lie group *G*.

One can extend the notion of frames to a more general case: one can "[solder](wikipedia://en/9792297)" a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) to a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), in such a way that the fibers behave as if they were tangent. When the fiber bundle is a homogenous space, this reduces to the above-described frame-field. When the homogenous space is a quotient of [special orthogonal groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group), this reduces to the standard conception of a [vierbein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vierbein).

Although there is a substantial formal difference between extrinsic and intrinsic moving frames, they are both alike in the sense that a moving frame is always given by a mapping into *G*. The strategy in Cartan's **method of moving frames**, as outlined briefly in [Cartan's equivalence method](wikipedia://en/1841654), is to find a *natural moving frame* on the manifold and then to take its [Darboux derivative](wikipedia://en/5194894), in other words [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) the [Maurer-Cartan form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurer-Cartan_form) of *G* to *M* (or *P*), and thus obtain a complete set of structural invariants for the manifold.[^o5]

## Method of the moving frame {#Method_of_the_moving_frame}

(Cartan 1937) formulated the general definition of a moving frame and the method of the moving frame, as elaborated by (Weyl 1938). The elements of the theory are

- A [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) *G*.
- A [Klein space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein_space) *X* whose group of geometric automorphisms is *G*.
- A [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) Σ which serves as a space of (generalized) coordinates for *X*.
- A collection of *frames* ƒ each of which determines a coordinate function from *X* to Σ (the precise nature of the frame is left vague in the general axiomatization).

The following axioms are then assumed to hold between these elements:

- There is a free and transitive [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of *G* on the collection of frames: it is a [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441) for *G*. In particular, for any pair of frames ƒ and ƒ′, there is a unique transition of frame (ƒ→ƒ′) in *G* determined by the requirement (ƒ→ƒ′)ƒ = ƒ′.
- Given a frame ƒ and a point *A* ∈ *X*, there is associated a point *x* = (*A*,ƒ) belonging to Σ. This mapping determined by the frame ƒ is a bijection from the points of *X* to those of Σ. This bijection is compatible with the law of composition of frames in the sense that the coordinate *x*′ of the point *A* in a different frame ƒ′ arises from (*A*,ƒ) by application of the transformation (ƒ→ƒ′). That is, $$(A,f') = (f\to f')\circ(A,f).$$

Of interest to the method are parameterized submanifolds of *X*. The considerations are largely local, so the parameter domain is taken to be an open subset of **R**<sup>λ</sup>. Slightly different techniques apply depending on whether one is interested in the submanifold along with its parameterization, or the submanifold up to reparameterization.

:::

## Moving tangent frames {#Moving_tangent_frames}

The most commonly encountered case of a moving frame is for the bundle of tangent frames (also called the *[frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550)*) of a manifold. In this case, a moving tangent frame on a manifold *M* consists of a collection of vector fields *e*<sub>1</sub>, *e*<sub>2</sub>, …, *e*<sub>*n*</sub> forming a basis of the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at each point of an open set *U* ⊂ *M*.

If $(x^1,x^2,\dots,x^n)$ is a coordinate system on *U*, then each vector field *e<sub>j</sub>* can be expressed as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of the coordinate vector fields $\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}$:

$$e_j = \sum_{i=1}^n A^i_j \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i},$$

where each $A^i_j$ is a function on *U*. These can be seen as the components of a matrix $A$. This matrix is useful for finding the coordinate expression of the dual coframe, as explained in the next section.

### Coframes {#Coframes}

A moving frame determines a **dual frame** or **[coframe](wikipedia://en/3131766)** of the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) over *U*, which is sometimes also called a moving frame. This is a *n*-tuple of smooth *1*-forms

*θ*<sup>1</sup>, *θ*<sup>2</sup>, …, *θ*<sup>*n*</sup>

which are linearly independent at each point *q* in *U*. Conversely, given such a coframe, there is a unique moving frame *e*<sub>1</sub>, *e*<sub>2</sub>, …, *e*<sub>*n*</sub> which is dual to it, i.e., satisfies the duality relation *θ*<sup>*i*</sup>(*e*<sub>*j*</sub>) = *δ*<sup>*i*</sup><sub>*j*</sub>, where *δ*<sup>*i*</sup><sub>*j*</sub> is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) function on *U*.

If $(x^1,x^2,\dots,x^n)$ is a coordinate system on *U*, as in the preceding section, then each covector field *θ*<sup>i</sup> can be expressed as a linear combination of the coordinate covector fields $dx^i$:

$$\theta^i = \sum_{j=1}^n B^i_j dx^j,$$

where each $B^i_j$ is a function on *U.* Since $dx^i \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}\right) = \delta^i_j$, the two coordinate expressions above combine to yield $\sum_{k=1}^n B^i_k A^k_j = \delta^i_j$; in terms of matrices, this just says that $A$ and $B$ are [inverses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inverse) of each other.

In the setting of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), when working with [canonical coordinates](wikipedia://en/706311), the canonical coframe is given by the [tautological one-form](wikipedia://en/1855974). Intuitively, it relates the velocities of a mechanical system (given by vector fields on the tangent bundle of the coordinates) to the corresponding momenta of the system (given by vector fields in the cotangent bundle; i.e. given by forms). The tautological one-form is a special case of the more general [solder form](wikipedia://en/9792297), which provides a (co-)frame field on a general [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526).

::: {#o15 .application cue=heading name="Uses"}

### Uses {#Uses}

Moving frames are important in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), where there is no privileged way of extending a choice of frame at an event *p* (a point in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), which is a manifold of dimension four) to nearby points, and so a choice must be made. In contrast in [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), *M* is taken to be a vector space *V* (of dimension four). In that case a frame at a point *p* can be translated from *p* to any other point *q* in a well-defined way. Broadly speaking, a moving frame corresponds to an observer, and the distinguished frames in special relativity represent [inertial observers](wikipedia://en/14838).

In relativity and in [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), the most useful kind of moving frames are the **orthogonal** and **[orthonormal frames](wikipedia://en/350995)**, that is, frames consisting of orthogonal (unit) vectors at each point. At a given point *p* a general frame may be made orthonormal by [orthonormalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormalization); in fact this can be done smoothly, so that the existence of a moving frame implies the existence of a moving orthonormal frame.

:::

### Further details {#Further_details}

A moving frame always exists *locally*, i.e., in some neighbourhood *U* of any point *p* in *M*; however, the existence of a moving frame globally on *M* requires [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) conditions. For example when *M* is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), or more generally a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), such frames exist; but not when *M* is a 2-[sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). A manifold that does have a global moving frame is called *[parallelizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelizable)*. Note for example how the unit directions of [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) and [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) on the Earth's surface break down as a moving frame at the north and south poles.

The **method of moving frames** of [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) is based on taking a moving frame that is adapted to the particular problem being studied. For example, given a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) in space, the first three derivative vectors of the curve can in general define a frame at a point of it (cf. [torsion tensor](wikipedia://en/4342484) for a quantitative description – it is assumed here that the torsion is not zero). In fact, in the method of moving frames, one more often works with coframes rather than frames. More generally, moving frames may be viewed as sections of [principal bundles](wikipedia://en/310950) over open sets *U*. The general Cartan method exploits this abstraction using the notion of a [Cartan connection](wikipedia://en/412272).

## Atlases {#Atlases}

In many cases, it is impossible to define a single frame of reference that is valid globally. To overcome this, frames are commonly pieced together to form an [atlas](wikipedia://en/47436), thus arriving at the notion of a **[local frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_frame)**. In addition, it is often desirable to endow these atlases with a [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238), so that the resulting frame fields are differentiable.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Although this article constructs the frame fields as a coordinate system on the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), the general ideas move over easily to the concept of a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524), which is a manifold endowed with a vector space at each point, that vector space being arbitrary, and not in general related to the tangent bundle.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Flight dynamics with text.svg.* The principal axes of rotation in space.

[Body-fixed frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body-fixed_frame) are widely used in [rigid body mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_body_mechanics). [Aircraft maneuvers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobatic_maneuver) can be expressed in terms of the moving frame ([aircraft principal axes](wikipedia://en/9023486)) when described by the pilot.
