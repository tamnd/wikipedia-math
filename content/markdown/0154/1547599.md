# Joseph L. Doob

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Joseph L. Doob"}

**Joseph Leo Doob** (February 27, 1910 – June 7, 2004) was an American mathematician and one of the principal architects of modern [measure-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) probability. Building on [Kolmogorov's](wikipedia://en/91161) axiomatization of probability, he established a rigorous framework for continuous-parameter [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895), developed [martingale theory](wikipedia://en/274536) into a central method of probability, and pioneered [probabilistic potential theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_potential_theory).[^o3] His 1953 monograph *[Stochastic Processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_Processes_%28book%29)* became a foundational work in the development of modern probability theory.[^o4]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Doob was born in [Cincinnati, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cincinnati,_Ohio), February 27, 1910, the son of a Jewish couple, Leo Doob and Mollie Doerfler Doob. The family moved to [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) before he was three years old. The parents felt that he was underachieving in grade school and placed him in the [Ethical Culture School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethical_Culture_School), from which he graduated in 1926. He then went on to [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) where he received a BA in 1930, an MA in 1931, and a PhD (*Boundary Values of Analytic Functions*, advisor [Joseph L. Walsh](wikipedia://en/14002828)) in 1932. After postdoctoral research at [Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) and [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University), he joined the department of mathematics of the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana-Champaign) in 1935 and served until his retirement in 1978. He was a member of the Urbana campus's Center for Advanced Study from its beginning in 1959. During the Second World War, he worked in Washington, D.C., and Guam as a civilian consultant to the Navy from 1942 to 1945; he was at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) for the academic year 1941–1942[^o6] when [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen) approached him to work on mine warfare for the Navy.

## Work {#Work}

Doob's thesis was on boundary values of analytic functions. He published two papers based on this thesis, which appeared in 1932 and 1933 in the Transactions of the American Mathematical Society. Doob returned to this subject many years later when he proved a probabilistic version of [Fatou](wikipedia://en/629100)'s boundary limit theorem for harmonic functions.

The [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression) of 1929 was still going strong in the thirties and Doob could not find a job. [B.O. Koopman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.O._Koopman) at Columbia University suggested that statistician [Harold Hotelling](wikipedia://en/935621) might have a grant that would permit Doob to work with him. Hotelling did, so the Depression led Doob to probability.

In 1933 [Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) provided the first axiomatic foundation for the theory of probability. Thus a subject that had originated from intuitive ideas suggested by real life experiences and studied informally, suddenly became mathematics. Probability theory became [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) with its own problems and terminology. Doob recognized that this would make it possible to give rigorous proofs for existing probability results, and he felt that the tools of measure theory would lead to new probability results.

Doob's approach to probability was evident in his first probability paper,[^o8] in which he proved theorems related to the [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055), using a probabilistic interpretation of [Birkhoff's ergodic theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkhoff's_ergodic_theorem). Then he used these theorems to give rigorous proofs of theorems proven by [Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.A._Fisher) and Hotelling related to Fisher's [maximum likelihood estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood_estimator) for estimating a parameter of a distribution.

After writing a series of papers on the foundations of probability and stochastic processes including [martingales](wikipedia://en/274536), [Markov processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process), and [stationary processes](wikipedia://en/329898), Doob realized that there was a real need for a book showing what is known about the various types of [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895), so he wrote the book *Stochastic Processes*.[^o9] It was published in 1953 and soon became one of the most influential books in the development of modern probability theory.

Beyond this book, Doob is best known for his work on [martingales](wikipedia://en/274536) and probabilistic [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342). After he retired, Doob wrote a book of over 800 pages: *Classical Potential Theory and Its Probabilistic Counterpart*.[^o10] The first half of this book deals with classical potential theory and the second half with [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), especially martingale theory. In writing this book, Doob shows that his two favorite subjects, martingales and potential theory, can be studied by the same mathematical tools.

The [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822)'s **Joseph L. Doob Prize**, endowed in 2005 and awarded every three years for an outstanding mathematical book, is named in Doob's honor.[^o11] The postdoctoral members of the department of mathematics of the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana-Champaign) are named J L Doob Research Assistant Professors.

## Honors {#Honors}

- President of the [Institute of Mathematical Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Mathematical_Statistics) in 1950.
- Elected to [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) 1957.
- President of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) 1963–1964.
- Elected to [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) 1965.
- Associate of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) 1975.
- Awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science) by the [President of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) [Jimmy Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Carter) 1979.[^o13]
- Awarded the [Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steele_Prize) by the American Mathematical Society. 1984.
