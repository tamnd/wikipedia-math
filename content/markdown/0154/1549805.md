# Linear complex structure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear complex structure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [complex structure](wikipedia://en/6871218) on a [real vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) $V$ is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of $V$ that squares to the minus [identity](wikipedia://en/15069), $- \text{id}_V$. Such a structure on $V$ allows one to define multiplication by [complex scalars](wikipedia://en/5826) in a canonical fashion so as to regard $V$ as a [complex vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space).

:::

Every complex vector space can be equipped with a compatible complex structure in a canonical way; however, there is in general no canonical complex structure. Complex structures have applications in [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) as well as in [complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101) where they play an essential role in the definition of [almost complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/514258), by contrast to [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742). The term "complex structure" often refers to this structure on manifolds; when it refers instead to a structure on vector spaces, it may be called a **linear complex structure**.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and properties"}

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

A **complex structure** on a [real vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) $V$ is a real [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation)

$$J :V \to V$$

such that

$$J^2 = -\text{id}_V.$$

Here $J^2$ means $J$ [composed](wikipedia://en/195947) with itself and $\text{id}_V$ is the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069) on $V$. That is, the effect of applying $J$ twice is the same as multiplication by $-1$. This is reminiscent of multiplication by the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358), $i$. A complex structure allows one to endow $V$ with the structure of a [complex vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space). Complex [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) can be defined by

$$(x + iy)\vec{v} = x\vec{v} + yJ(\vec{v})$$

for all real numbers $x,y$ and all vectors $\vec{v}$ in $V$. One can check that this does, in fact, give $V$ the structure of a complex vector space which we denote $V_J$.

Going in the other direction, if one starts with a complex vector space $W$ then one can define a complex structure on the underlying real space by defining $Jw = iw~~\forall w\in W$.

More formally, a linear complex structure on a real vector space is an [algebra representation](wikipedia://en/854978) of the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$, thought of as an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). This algebra is realized concretely as

$$\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1),$$

which corresponds to $i^2=-1$. Then a representation of $\mathbb{C}$ is a real vector space $V$, together with an action of $\mathbb{C}$ on $V$ (a map $\mathbb{C}\rightarrow \text{End}(V)$). Concretely, this is just an action of $i$, as this generates the algebra, and the operator representing $i$ (the image of $i$ in $\text{End}(V)$) is exactly $J$.

If $V_J$ has complex [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) $n$, then $V$ must have real dimension $2n$. That is, a finite-dimensional space $V$ admits a complex structure only if it is even-dimensional. It is not hard to see that every even-dimensional vector space admits a complex structure. One can define $J$ on pairs $e,f$ of [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) vectors by $Je=f$ and $Jf=-e$ and then [extend by linearity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extend_by_linearity) to all of $V$. If $(v_1, \dots,v_n)$ is a basis for the complex vector space $V_J$ then $(v_1,Jv_1,\dots ,v_n ,Jv_n)$ is a basis for the underlying real space $V$.

A real linear transformation $A:V \rightarrow V$ is a ***complex*** linear transformation of the corresponding complex space $V_J$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $A$ commutes with $J$, i.e. if and only if

$$AJ = JA.$$

Likewise, a real [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) $U$ of $V$ is a complex subspace of $V_J$ if and only if $J$ preserves $U$, i.e. if and only if

$$JU = U.$$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Elementary example {#Elementary_example}

The collection of $2\times2$ real matrices $\mathbb{M}(2,\mathbb{R})$ over the real field is 4-dimensional. Any matrix

$$J = \begin{pmatrix}a & c \\ b & -a \end{pmatrix},~~a^2+bc=-1$$

has square equal to the negative of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). A complex structure may be formed in $\mathbb{M}(2,\mathbb{R})$: with identity matrix $I$, elements $xI+yJ$, with [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) form complex numbers.

### Complex *n*-dimensional space **C**<sup>*n*</sup> {#Complex_n-dimensional_space_Cn}

The fundamental example of a linear complex structure is the structure on **R**<sup>2*n*</sup> coming from the complex structure on **C**<sup>*n*</sup>. That is, the complex *n*-dimensional space **C**<sup>*n*</sup> is also a real 2*n*-dimensional space – using the same vector addition and real scalar multiplication – while multiplication by the complex number *i* is not only a *complex* linear transform of the space, thought of as a complex vector space, but also a *real* linear transform of the space, thought of as a real vector space. Concretely, this is because scalar multiplication by *i* commutes with scalar multiplication by real numbers $i (\lambda v) = (i \lambda) v = (\lambda i) v = \lambda (i v)$ – and distributes across vector addition. As a complex *n*×*n* matrix, this is simply the [scalar matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_matrix) with *i* on the diagonal. The corresponding real 2*n*×2*n* matrix is denoted *J*.

Given a basis $\left\{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n \right\}$ for the complex space, this set, together with these vectors multiplied by *i,* namely $\left\{ie_1, ie_2, \dots, ie_n\right\},$ form a basis for the real space. There are two natural ways to order this basis, corresponding abstractly to whether one writes the tensor product as $\mathbb{C}^n = \mathbb{R}^n \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$ or instead as $\mathbb{C}^n = \mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{R}^n.$

If one orders the basis as $\left\{e_1, ie_1, e_2, ie_2, \dots, e_n, ie_n\right\},$ then the matrix for *J* takes the [block diagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_diagonal) form (subscripts added to indicate dimension):

$$J_{2n} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ & & 0 & -1 \\ & & 1 & 0 \\ & & & & \ddots \\ & & & & & \ddots \\ & & & & & & 0 & -1 \\ & & & & & & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} J_2 \\ & J_2 \\ & & \ddots \\ & & & J_2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

This ordering has the advantage that it respects direct sums of complex vector spaces, meaning here that the basis for $\mathbb{C}^m \oplus \mathbb{C}^n$ is the same as that for $\mathbb{C}^{m+n}.$

On the other hand, if one orders the basis as $\left\{e_1,e_2,\dots,e_n, ie_1, ie_2, \dots, ie_n\right\}$, then the matrix for *J* is block-antidiagonal:

$$J_{2n} = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -I_n \\ I_n & 0\end{bmatrix}.$$

This ordering is more natural if one thinks of the complex space as a direct sum of real spaces, as discussed below.

The data of the real vector space and the *J* matrix is exactly the same as the data of the complex vector space, as the *J* matrix allows one to define [complex multiplication](wikipedia://en/537183). At the level of [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) and [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), this corresponds to the inclusion of gl(*n*,**C**) in gl(2*n*,**R**) (Lie algebras – matrices, not necessarily invertible) and [GL(*n*,**C**)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GL%28n,C%29) in GL(2*n*,**R**):

The inclusion corresponds to forgetting the complex structure (and keeping only the real), while the subgroup GL(*n*,**C**) can be characterized (given in equations) as the matrices that *commute* with *J:*

$$\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C}) = \left\{ A \in \mathrm{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R}) \mid AJ = JA \right\}.$$

The corresponding statement about Lie algebras is that the subalgebra gl(*n*,**C**) of complex matrices are those whose [Lie bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_bracket) with *J* vanishes, meaning $[J,A] = 0;$ in other words, as the kernel of the map of bracketing with *J,* $[J,-].$

Note that the defining equations for these statements are the same, as $AJ = JA$ is the same as $AJ - JA = 0,$ which is the same as $[A,J] = 0,$ though the meaning of the Lie bracket vanishing is less immediate geometrically than the meaning of commuting.

### Direct sum {#Direct_sum}

If *V* is any real vector space there is a canonical complex structure on the [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_vector_spaces) *V* ⊕ *V* given by

$$J(v,w) = (-w,v).$$

The [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464) form of *J* is

$$J = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -I_V \\ I_V & 0\end{bmatrix}$$

where $I_V$ is the identity map on *V*. This corresponds to the complex structure on the tensor product $\mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} V.$

:::

## Compatibility with other structures {#Compatibility_with_other_structures}

If $B$ is a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) on $V$ then we say that $J$ **preserves** $B$ if

$$B(Ju, Jv) = B(u, v)$$

for all $u, v \in V$. An equivalent characterization is that $J$ is [skew-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-adjoint) with respect to $B$:

$$B(Ju,v) = -B(u,Jv).$$

If $g$ is an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) on $V$ then $J$ preserves $g$ if and only if $J$ is an [orthogonal transformation](wikipedia://en/1382381). Likewise, $J$ preserves a [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate), [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055) form $\omega$ if and only if $J$ is a [symplectic transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_transformation) (that is, if $\omega(Ju,Jv) = \omega(u,v)$). For symplectic forms $\omega$ an interesting compatibility condition between $J$ and $\omega$ is that

$$\omega(u, Ju) > 0$$

holds for all non-zero $u$ in $V$. If this condition is satisfied, then we say that $J$ **tames** $\omega$ (synonymously: that $\omega$ is **tame** with respect to $J$; that $J$ is **tame** with respect to $\omega$; or that the pair $(\omega,J)$ is tame).

Given a symplectic form $\omega$ and a linear complex structure $J$ on $V$, one may define an associated bilinear form $g_{J}$ on $V$ by

$$g_J(u, v) = \omega(u, Jv).$$

Because a [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/292852) is nondegenerate, so is the associated bilinear form. The associated form is preserved by $J$ if and only if the symplectic form is. Moreover, if the symplectic form is preserved by $J$, then the associated form is symmetric. If in addition $\omega$ is tamed by $J$, then the associated form is [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_bilinear_form). Thus in this case $V$ is an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) with respect to $g_{J}$.

If the symplectic form $\omega$ is preserved (but not necessarily tamed) by $J$, then $g_{J}$ is the [real part](wikipedia://en/5826) of the [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) (by convention antilinear in the first argument) $h_J\colon V_J\times V_J\to\mathbb{C}$ defined by

$$h_J(u,v) = g_J(u,v) + ig_J(Ju,v) = \omega(u,Jv) +i\omega(u,v).$$

## Relation to complexifications {#Relation_to_complexifications}

Given any real vector space *V* we may define its [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) by [extension of scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_of_scalars):

$$V^{\mathbb C}=V\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}.$$

This is a complex vector space whose complex dimension is equal to the real dimension of *V*. It has a canonical [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) defined by

$$\overline{v\otimes z} = v\otimes\bar z$$

If *J* is a complex structure on *V*, we may extend *J* by linearity to *V*<sup>**C**</sup>:

$$J(v\otimes z) = J(v)\otimes z.$$

Since **C** is [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed), *J* is guaranteed to have [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) which satisfy λ<sup>2</sup> = −1, namely λ = ±*i*. Thus we may write

$$V^{\mathbb C}= V^{+}\oplus V^{-}$$

where *V*<sup>\+</sup> and *V*<sup>−</sup> are the [eigenspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) of +*i* and −*i*, respectively. Complex conjugation interchanges *V*<sup>\+</sup> and *V*<sup>−</sup>. The projection maps onto the *V*<sup>±</sup> eigenspaces are given by

$$\mathcal P^{\pm} = {1\over 2}(1\mp iJ).$$

So that

$$V^{\pm} = \{v\otimes 1 \mp Jv\otimes i: v \in V\}.$$

There is a natural complex linear isomorphism between *V*<sub>*J*</sub> and *V*<sup>\+</sup>, so these vector spaces can be considered the same, while *V*<sup>−</sup> may be regarded as the [complex conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugate_vector_space) of *V*<sub>*J*</sub>.

Note that if *V*<sub>*J*</sub> has complex dimension *n* then both *V*<sup>\+</sup> and *V*<sup>−</sup> have complex dimension *n* while *V*<sup>**C**</sup> has complex dimension 2*n*.

Abstractly, if one starts with a complex vector space *W* and takes the complexification of the underlying real space, one obtains a space isomorphic to the direct sum of *W* and its conjugate:

$$W^{\mathbb C} \cong W\oplus \overline{W}.$$

## Extension to related vector spaces {#Extension_to_related_vector_spaces}

Let *V* be a real vector space with a complex structure *J*. The [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) *V*\* has a natural complex structure *J*\* given by the dual (or [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844)) of *J*. The complexification of the dual space (*V*\*)<sup>**C**</sup> therefore has a natural decomposition

$$(V^*)^\mathbb{C} = (V^*)^{+}\oplus (V^*)^-$$

into the ±*i* eigenspaces of *J*\*. Under the natural identification of (*V*\*)<sup>**C**</sup> with (*V*<sup>**C**</sup>)\* one can characterize (*V*\*)<sup>\+</sup> as those complex linear functionals which vanish on *V*<sup>−</sup>. Likewise (*V*\*)<sup>−</sup> consists of those complex linear functionals which vanish on *V*<sup>\+</sup>.

The (complex) [tensor](wikipedia://en/363628), [symmetric](wikipedia://en/654098), and [exterior algebras](wikipedia://en/221537) over *V*<sup>**C**</sup> also admit decompositions. The exterior algebra is perhaps the most important application of this decomposition. In general, if a vector space *U* admits a decomposition *U* = *S* ⊕ *T*, then the exterior powers of *U* can be decomposed as follows:

$$\Lambda^r U = \bigoplus_{p+q=r}(\Lambda^p S)\otimes(\Lambda^q T).$$

A complex structure *J* on *V* therefore induces a decomposition

$$\Lambda^r\,V^\mathbb{C} = \bigoplus_{p+q=r} \Lambda^{p,q}\,V_J$$

where

$$\Lambda^{p,q}\,V_J\;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\, (\Lambda^p\,V^+)\otimes(\Lambda^q\,V^-).$$

All exterior powers are taken over the complex numbers. So if *V*<sub>*J*</sub> has complex dimension *n* (real dimension 2*n*) then

$$\dim_{\mathbb C}\Lambda^{r}\,V^{\mathbb C} = {2n\choose r}\qquad \dim_{\mathbb C}\Lambda^{p,q}\,V_J = {n \choose p}{n \choose q}.$$

The dimensions add up correctly as a consequence of [Vandermonde's identity](wikipedia://en/916157).

The space of (*p*,*q*)-forms Λ<sup>*p*,*q*</sup> *V*<sub>*J*</sub>\* is the space of (complex) [multilinear forms](wikipedia://en/832212) on *V*<sup>**C**</sup> which vanish on homogeneous elements unless *p* are from *V*<sup>\+</sup> and *q* are from *V*<sup>−</sup>. It is also possible to regard Λ<sup>*p*,*q*</sup> *V*<sub>*J*</sub>\* as the space of real [multilinear maps](wikipedia://en/221525) from *V*<sub>*J*</sub> to **C** which are complex linear in *p* terms and [conjugate-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate-linear) in *q* terms.

See [complex differential form](wikipedia://en/2497815) and [almost complex manifold](wikipedia://en/514258) for applications of these ideas.
