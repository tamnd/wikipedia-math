# Subgroup series

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subgroup series"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a **subgroup series** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ is a [chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_%28order_theory%29) of [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011):

:::

$$1 = A_0 \leq A_1 \leq \cdots \leq A_n = G$$

where $1$ is the [trivial subgroup](wikipedia://en/193059). Subgroup series can simplify the study of a group to the study of simpler subgroups and their relations, and several subgroup series can be invariantly defined and are important invariants of groups. A subgroup series is used in the [subgroup method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgroup_method).

Subgroup series are a special example of the use of [filtrations](wikipedia://en/726014) in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Normal series, subnormal series {#Normal_series,_subnormal_series}

A **subnormal series** (also **normal series**, **normal tower**, **subinvariant series**, or just **series**) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is a sequence of [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011), each a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of the next one. In a standard notation

$$1 = A_0\triangleleft A_1\triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft A_n = G.$$

There is no requirement made that *A*<sub>*i*</sub> be a normal subgroup of *G*, only a normal subgroup of *A*<sub>*i* +1</sub>. The [quotient groups](wikipedia://en/11526) *A*<sub>*i* +1</sub>/*A*<sub>*i*</sub> are called the **factor groups** of the series.

If in addition each *A*<sub>*i*</sub> is normal in *G*, then the series is called a **normal series**, when this term is not used for the weaker sense, or an **invariant series**.

### Length {#Length}

A series with the additional property that *A*<sub>*i*</sub> ≠ *A*<sub>*i* +1</sub> for all *i* is called a series *without repetition*; equivalently, each *A*<sub>*i*</sub> is a proper subgroup of *A*<sub>*i* +1</sub>. The *length* of a series is the number of strict inclusions *A*<sub>*i*</sub> \< *A*<sub>*i* +1</sub>. If the series has no repetition then the length is *n*.

For a subnormal series, the length is the number of [non-trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) factor groups. Every nontrivial group has a normal series of length 1, namely $1 \triangleleft G$, and any nontrivial proper normal subgroup gives a normal series of length 2. For [simple groups](wikipedia://en/47600), the trivial series of length 1 is the longest subnormal series possible.

### Ascending series, descending series {#Ascending_series,_descending_series}

Series can be notated in either ascending order:

$$1 = A_0\leq A_1\leq \cdots \leq A_n = G$$

or descending order:

$$G = B_0\geq B_1\geq \cdots \geq B_n = 1.$$

For a given finite series, there is no distinction between an "ascending series" or "descending series" beyond notation. For *infinite* series however, there is a distinction: the ascending series

$$1 = A_0\leq A_1\leq \cdots \leq G$$

has a smallest term, a second smallest term, and so forth, but no largest proper term, no second largest term, and so forth, while conversely the descending series

$$G = B_0\geq B_1\geq \cdots \geq 1$$

has a largest term, but no smallest proper term.

Further, given a recursive formula for producing a series, the terms produced are either ascending or descending, and one calls the resulting series an ascending or descending series, respectively. For instance the [derived series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_series) and [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) are descending series, while the [upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series) is an ascending series.

### Noetherian groups, Artinian groups {#Noetherian_groups,_Artinian_groups}

A group that satisfies the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189) (ACC) on subgroups is called a **Noetherian group**, and a group that satisfies the [descending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_chain_condition) (DCC) is called an **Artinian group** (not to be confused with [Artin groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_group)), by analogy with [Noetherian rings](wikipedia://en/56129) and [Artinian rings](wikipedia://en/489273). The ACC is equivalent to the **maximal condition**: every [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) collection of subgroups has a maximal member, and the DCC is equivalent to the analogous **minimal condition**.

A group can be Noetherian but not Artinian, such as the [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group), and unlike for [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), a group can be Artinian but not Noetherian, such as the [Prüfer group](wikipedia://en/4885031). Every finite group is clearly Noetherian and Artinian.

[Homomorphic](wikipedia://en/12396) [images](wikipedia://en/579311) and subgroups of Noetherian groups are Noetherian, and an [extension](wikipedia://en/345807) of a Noetherian group by a Noetherian group is Noetherian. Analogous results hold for Artinian groups.

Noetherian groups are equivalently those such that every subgroup is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042), which is stronger than the group itself being finitely generated: the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) on 2 or finitely more generators is finitely generated, but contains free groups of infinite rank.

Noetherian groups need not be finite extensions of [polycyclic groups](wikipedia://en/4000598).[^o8]

### Infinite and transfinite series {#Infinite_and_transfinite_series}

Infinite subgroup series can also be defined and arise naturally, in which case the specific ([totally ordered](wikipedia://en/30330)) indexing set becomes important, and there is a distinction between ascending and descending series. An ascending series $1 = A_0\leq A_1\leq \cdots \leq G$ where the $A_i$ are indexed by the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) may simply be called an **infinite ascending series**, and conversely for an **infinite descending series**. If the subgroups are more generally [indexed by ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932#Indexing_classes_of_ordinals), one obtains a **transfinite series**,[^o10] such as this ascending series:

$$1 = A_0\leq A_1\leq \cdots \leq A_\omega \leq A_{\omega+1} = G$$

Given a recursive formula for producing a series, one can define a transfinite series by [transfinite recursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfinite_recursion) by defining the series at [limit ordinals](wikipedia://en/373137) by $A_\lambda := \bigcup_{\alpha < \lambda} A_\alpha$ (for ascending series) or $A_\lambda := \bigcap_{\alpha < \lambda} A_\alpha$ (for descending series). Fundamental examples of this construction are the transfinite [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) and [upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series).

Other totally ordered sets arise rarely, if ever, as indexing sets of subgroup series. For instance, one can define but rarely sees naturally occurring bi-infinite subgroup series (series indexed by the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563)):

$$1 \leq \cdots \leq A_{-1} \leq A_0\leq A_1 \leq \cdots \leq G$$

:::

## Comparison of series {#Comparison_of_series}

A *refinement* of a series is another series containing each of the terms of the original series. Two subnormal series are said to be *equivalent* or *isomorphic* if there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the sets of their factor groups such that the corresponding factor groups are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397). Refinement gives a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) on series, up to equivalence, and they form a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304), while subnormal series and normal series form sublattices. The existence of the supremum of two subnormal series is the [Schreier refinement theorem](wikipedia://en/947167). Of particular interest are *maximal* series without repetition.

## Examples {#Examples}

Subgroup series

### Maximal series {#Maximal_series}

- A **[composition series](wikipedia://en/297493)** is a maximal *subnormal* series.

Equivalently, a subnormal series for which each of the *A*<sub>*i*</sub> is a [maximal](wikipedia://en/1472649) normal subgroup of *A*<sub>*i* +1</sub>. Equivalently, a composition series is a subnormal series for which each of the factor groups are [simple](wikipedia://en/47600).

- A **[chief series](wikipedia://en/12505519)** is a maximal *normal* series.

### Solvable and nilpotent {#Solvable_and_nilpotent}

- A **[solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012)**, or soluble group, is one with a subnormal series whose factor groups are all [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974).
- A **[nilpotent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_series)** is a subnormal series such that successive quotients are [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569).

A nilpotent series exists if and only if the group is [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012).

- A **[central series](wikipedia://en/15202655)** is a subnormal series such that successive quotients are [central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28group%29), i.e. given the above series, $A_{i+1}/A_i \subseteq Z(G/A_i)$ for $i=0, 1, \ldots, n-2$.

A central series exists if and only if the group is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569).

### Functional series {#Functional_series}

Some subgroup series are defined , in terms of subgroups such as the center and operations such as the commutator. These include:

## Subgroup method {#Subgroup_method}

The **subgroup method** is an algorithm used in the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) field of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). It is used to find the [word](wikipedia://en/12792326) of an element. It doesn't always return the minimal word, but it can return optimal words based on the series of subgroups that is used. The code looks like this:

**function** operate(element, generator)

\<returns generator operated on element>

**function** subgroup(g) sequence := (set of subgroups that will be used, depending on the method.) word := \[\] **for** subgroup in sequence coset\_representatives := \[\]

\<fill coset\_representatives with coset representatives of (next subgroup)/subgroup>

**for** operation in coset\_representatives **if** **operate**(g, operation) is in the next subgroup **then** append operation onto word g = **operate**(g, operation) **break** **return** word

:::
