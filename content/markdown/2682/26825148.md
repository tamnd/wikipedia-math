# De Bruijn–Erdős theorem (incidence geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="De Bruijn–Erdős theorem"}

In [incidence geometry](wikipedia://en/1576323), the **De Bruijn–Erdős theorem**, originally published by [Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn](wikipedia://en/3166399) and [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) in 1948,[^o2] states a [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) on the number of lines determined by *n* points in a [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). By [duality](wikipedia://en/539925), this is also a bound on the number of intersection points determined by a configuration of lines.[^o3]

:::

Although the proof given by De Bruijn and Erdős is [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/741875), De Bruijn and Erdős noted in their paper that the analogous ([Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417)) result is a consequence of the [Sylvester–Gallai theorem](wikipedia://en/1052632), by an [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) on the number of points.[^o2]

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

*Image: Nearpencil.svg.* A near-pencil on seven points

Let *P* be a configuration of *n* points in a projective plane, not all on a line. Let *t* be the number of lines determined by *P*. Then,

- *t* ≥ *n*, and
- if *t* = *n*, any two lines have exactly one point of *P* in common. In this case, *P* is either a projective plane or *P* is a *near pencil*, meaning that exactly *n* - 1 of the points are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear).[^o3]

:::

## Euclidean proof {#Euclidean_proof}

The theorem is clearly true for three non-collinear points. We proceed by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881).

Assume *n* > 3 and the theorem is true for *n* − 1. Let *P* be a set of *n* points not all collinear. The [Sylvester–Gallai theorem](wikipedia://en/1052632) states that there is a line containing exactly two points of *P*. Such two point lines are called *ordinary lines*. Let *a* and *b* be the two points of *P* on an ordinary line.

If the removal of point *a* produces a set of collinear points then *P* generates a near pencil of *n* lines (the *n* - 1 ordinary lines through *a* plus the one line containing the other *n* - 1 points).

Otherwise, the removal of *a* produces a set, *P'*, of *n* − 1 points that are not all collinear. By the induction hypothesis, *P'* determines at least *n* − 1 lines. The ordinary line determined by *a* and *b* is not among these, so *P* determines at least *n* lines.

## J. H. Conway's proof {#J._H._Conway's_proof}

[John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) has a purely combinatorial proof which consequently also holds for points and lines over the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers), [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions) and [octonions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octonions).[^o8]
