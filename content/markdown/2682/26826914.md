# Pauli–Lubanski pseudovector

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pauli–Lubanski pseudovector"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the **Pauli–Lubański pseudovector** is an [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29) defined from the momentum and [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839), used in the [quantum-relativistic](wikipedia://en/19389837) description of angular momentum. It is named after [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli) and [Józef Lubański](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Józef_Lubański).[^o2]

:::

It describes the spin states of moving particles.[^o3] It is the generator of the [little group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_group) of the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487), that is the maximal subgroup (with four generators) leaving the eigenvalues of the [four-momentum](wikipedia://en/204680) vector $P_{\mu}$ invariant.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

It is usually denoted by $W$ (or less often by $S$) and defined by:[^o7][^o8][^o9]

where

- $\varepsilon_{\mu \nu \rho \sigma}$ is the [four-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-dimensional) totally [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/696955) [Levi-Civita symbol](wikipedia://en/195982);
- $J^{\nu \rho}$ is the [relativistic angular momentum tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_angular_momentum_tensor) operator ($M^{\nu \rho}$);
- $P^{\sigma}$ is the [four-momentum](wikipedia://en/204680) operator.

In the language of [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537), it can be written as the [Hodge dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_dual) of a [trivector](wikipedia://en/2727288),[^o11]

$$\mathbf{W} = \star(\mathbf{J} \wedge \mathbf{p}).$$

Note $W_0 = \vec{J} \cdot \vec{P}$, and $\vec{W} = E \vec{J}- \vec{P} \times \vec{K}$ where $\vec{J}$ is the generator of rotations and $\vec{K}$ is the generator of boosts.

$W_{\mu}$ evidently satisfies

$$P^{\mu}W_{\mu}=0,$$

as well as the following [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) relations,

$$\begin{aligned} \left[P^\mu, W^\nu\right] &= 0, \\ \left[J^{\mu \nu}, W^\rho\right] &= i \left( g^{\rho \nu} W^\mu - g^{\rho \mu} W^\nu\right), \end{aligned}$$

Consequently,

$$\left[W_{\mu}, W_{\nu}\right] = -i \epsilon_{\mu \nu \rho \sigma} W^{\rho} P^{\sigma}.$$

The scalar $W_{\mu}W^{\mu}$ is a Lorentz-invariant operator, and commutes with the four-momentum, and can thus serve as a label for [irreducible unitary representations of the Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/863813). That is, it can serve as the label for the [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), a feature of the spacetime structure of the representation, over and above the relativistically invariant label $P_{\mu}P^{\mu}$ for the mass of all states in a representation.

:::

## Little group {#Little_group}

On an eigenspace $S$ of the [4-momentum operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-momentum_operator) $P$ with 4-momentum eigenvalue $k$ of the Hilbert space of a [quantum system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_system) (or for that matter the *standard representation* with $\mathbb{R}^4$ interpreted as [momentum space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum_space) acted on by 5×5 matrices with the upper left 4×4 block an ordinary Lorentz transformation, the last column reserved for translations and the action effected on elements $p$ (column vectors) of momentum space with $1$ appended as a *fifth* row, see standard texts[^o13][^o14]) the following holds:[^o15]

- The components of $W$ with $P^\mu$ replaced by $k^\mu$ form a Lie algebra. It is the Lie algebra of the Little group $L_k$of $k$, i.e. the subgroup of the homogeneous Lorentz group that leaves $k$ invariant.
- For every irreducible unitary representation of $L_k$ there is an irreducible unitary representation of the full Poincaré group called an [induced representation](wikipedia://en/357452).
- A representation space of the induced representation can be obtained by successive application of elements of the full Poincaré group to a non-zero element of $S$ and extending by linearity.

The irreducible unitary representation of the Poincaré group are characterized by the eigenvalues of the two Casimir operators $P^2$ and $W^2$. The best way to see that an irreducible unitary representation actually is obtained is to exhibit its action on an element with arbitrary 4-momentum eigenvalue $p$ in the representation space thus obtained.[^o16] Irreducibility follows from the construction of the representation space.

## Massive fields {#Massive_fields}

In [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), in the case of a massive field, the [Casimir invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimir_invariant) $W_{\mu}W^{\mu}$ describes the total [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_quantum_number) of the particle, with [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues)

$$W^2 = W_\mu W^\mu = -m^2 s(s + 1),$$

where $s$ is the [spin quantum number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_quantum_number) of the particle and $m$ is its [rest mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_mass).

It is straightforward to see this in the [rest frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_frame) of the particle, the above commutator acting on the particle's state amounts to $[W_{j} , W_{k}] = i \varepsilon_{jkl} W_{l} m$; hence $\vec{W} = m\vec{J}$ and $W^{0} = 0$, so that the little group amounts to the rotation group,

$$W_\mu W^\mu = -m^2 \vec{J}\cdot\vec{J}.$$

Since this is a [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariant) quantity, it will be the same in all other [reference frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference).

It is also customary to take $W^{3}$ to describe the spin projection along the third direction in the rest frame.

In moving frames, decomposing $W = (W_{0}, \vec{W})$ into components $(W_{1}, W_{2}, W_{3})$, with $W_{1}$ and $W_{2}$ orthogonal to $\vec{P}$, and $W_{3}$ parallel to $\vec{P}$, the Pauli–Lubanski vector may be expressed in terms of the spin vector $\vec{S}$ = $(S_{1}, S_{2}, S_{3})$ (similarly decomposed) as

$$\begin{aligned} W_0 &= \frac{P}{c} S_3, & W_1 &= m S_1, & W_2 &= m S_2, & W_3 &= \frac{E}{c^2} S_3, \end{aligned}$$

where

$$E^2 = P^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4$$

is the [energy–momentum relation](wikipedia://en/2875647).

The transverse components $W_{1}, W_{2}$, along with $S_{3}$, satisfy the following commutator relations (which apply generally, not just to non-zero mass representations),

$$\begin{aligned}[] [W_1, W_2] &= \frac{ih}{2\pi} \left(\left(\frac{E}{c^2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{P}{c}\right)^2\right) S_3, & [W_2, S_3] &= \frac{ih}{2\pi} W_1, & [S_3, W_1] &= \frac{ih}{2\pi} W_2. \end{aligned}$$

For particles with non-zero mass, and the fields associated with such particles,

$$[W_1, W_2] = \frac{ih}{2\pi} m^2 S_3.$$

## Massless fields {#Massless_fields}

In general, in the case of non-massive representations, two cases may be distinguished. For massless particles,[^o16]

$$W^2 = W_\mu W^\mu = -E^{2}\left((K_2 - J_1)^2 + (K_1 + J_2)^2\right) \mathrel\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} -E^2\left(A^2 + B^2\right) ,$$

where $\vec{K}$ is the [dynamic mass moment vector](wikipedia://en/39139834). So, mathematically, $P$<sup>2</sup> = 0 does not imply $W$<sup>2</sup> = 0.

### Continuous spin representations {#Continuous_spin_representations}

In the more general case, the components of $\vec{W}$ transverse to $\vec{P}$ may be non-zero, thus yielding the family of representations referred to as the *cylindrical* luxons ("luxon" is another term for "massless particle"), their identifying property being that the components of $\vec{W}$ form a Lie subalgebra isomorphic to the 2-dimensional Euclidean group $\operatorname{ISO}(2)$, with the longitudinal component of $\vec{W}$ playing the role of the rotation generator, and the transverse components the role of translation generators. This amounts to a [group contraction](wikipedia://en/34976899) of $\operatorname{SO}(3)$, and leads to what are known as the *continuous spin* representations. However, there are no known physical cases of fundamental particles or fields in this family. It can be argued that continuous spin states possess an internal degree of freedom not seen in observed massless particles.[^o16]

### Helicity representations {#Helicity_representations}

In a special case, $\vec{W}$ is parallel to $\vec{P} ;$ or equivalently $\vec{W} \times \vec{P} = \vec{\boldsymbol{0}} .$ For non-zero $\vec{W}$ this constraint can only be consistently imposed for [luxons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle) ([massless particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle)), since the commutator of the two transverse components of $\vec{W}$ is proportional to $m^2 \vec{J} \cdot \vec{P} \, .$ For this family, $W^2 = 0$ and $W^\mu = \lambda \, P^\mu$ the invariant is, instead given by

$$\left(W^0\right)^2 = \left(W^3\right)^2 ,$$

where

$$W^0 = -\vec{J} \cdot \vec{P} ,$$

so the invariant is represented by the [helicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicity_%28particle_physics%29) operator

$$W^0 / P .$$

All particles that interact with the [weak nuclear force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_nuclear_force), for instance, fall into this family, since the definition of weak nuclear charge ([weak isospin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_isospin)) involves helicity, which, by above, must be an invariant. The appearance of non-zero mass in such cases must then be explained by other means, such as the [Higgs mechanism](wikipedia://en/644550). Even after accounting for such mass-generating mechanisms, however, the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) (and therefore the electromagnetic field) continues to fall into this class, although the other mass eigenstates of the carriers of the [electroweak force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_force) (the $W \operatorname{boson}+-$ [boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) and anti-[boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) and $Z \operatorname{boson}0$ [boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson)) acquire non-zero mass.

[Neutrinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino) were formerly considered to fall into this class as well. However, because [neutrinos have been observed to oscillate in flavour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation), it is now known that at least two of the three mass eigenstates of the left-helicity neutrinos and right-helicity anti-neutrinos each must have non-zero mass.
