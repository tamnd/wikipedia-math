# Niven's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Niven's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Niven's theorem**, named after [Ivan Niven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Niven), states that the only [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) values of $\theta$ in the interval $0^{\circ} \le \theta \le 90^{\circ}$ for which the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) of *$\theta$* degrees is also a rational number are:[^o2]

:::

$$\begin{aligned} \sin 0^\circ & = 0, \\[10pt] \sin 30^\circ & = \frac 12, \\[10pt] \sin 90^\circ & = 1. \end{aligned}$$

In [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), one would require that $0 \le x \le \pi/2$, that $x/\pi$ be rational, and that $\sin(x)$ be rational. The conclusion is then that the only such values are $\sin(0) = 0$, $\sin(\pi/6) = 1/2$, and $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$.

The theorem appears as Corollary 3.12 in Niven's book on [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689).[^o3]

The theorem extends to the other [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) as well.[^o3] For rational values of $\theta$, the only rational values of the sine or cosine (i.e., the only rational [trigonometric numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_number)) are $0$, $\pm1/2$, and $\pm1$; the only rational values of the secant or cosecant are $\pm1$ and $\pm2$; and the only rational values of the tangent or cotangent are $0$ and $\pm1$.[^o4]

## History {#History}

Niven's proof of his theorem appears in his book *Irrational Numbers*. Earlier, the theorem had been proven by [D. H. Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._H._Lehmer) and J. M. H. Olmstead.[^o3] In his 1933 paper, Lehmer proved the theorem for the cosine by proving a more general result. Namely, Lehmer showed that for relatively prime integers $k$ and $n$ with $n > 2$, the number $2 \cos(2\pi k/n)$ is an [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) of degree $\varphi(n)/2$, where $\varphi$ denotes [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452). Because rational numbers have degree 1, we must have $n \le 2$ or $\varphi(n) = 2$ and therefore the only possibilities are $n = 1,2,3,4,6$. Next, he proved a corresponding result for the sine using the trigonometric identity $\sin(\theta) = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$.[^o6] In 1956, Niven extended Lehmer's result to the other trigonometric functions.[^o3] Other mathematicians have given new proofs in subsequent years.[^o4]
