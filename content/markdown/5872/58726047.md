# Partial inverse of a matrix

::: {#o2 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **partial inverse** of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is an operation related to [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035) which has applications in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939). It is also known by various authors as the **principal pivot transform**, or as the **sweep**, **gyration**, or **exchange** operator, represented by $\mathrm{inv}_k$ if restricted to blocks along the [main diagonal](wikipedia://en/194990), or by $\hat{Y}_{k\ell}$ if considering the general case of any arbitrary block from the matrix.[^o3]

Given an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ over a vector space $V$ partitioned into blocks:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{pmatrix}$$

If $A_{11}$ is invertible, then the partial inverse of $A$ around the **pivot block** $A_{11}$ is created by inverting $A_{11}$, putting the [Schur complement](wikipedia://en/372620) $A / A_{11}$ in place of $A_{22}$, and adjusting the off-diagonal elements accordingly:[^o5]

$$\operatorname{inv}_1 A = \begin{pmatrix} (A_{11})^{-1} & - (A_{11})^{-1} A_{12} \\ A_{21} (A_{11})^{-1} & A_{22} - A_{21} (A_{11})^{-1}A_{12} \end{pmatrix}$$

Conceptually, partial inversion corresponds to a rotation[^o4] of the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the matrix $(X, AX) \in V \times V$, such that, for conformally-partitioned column matrices $(x_1, x_2)^T$ and $(y_1, y_2)^T$:[^o5]

$$A \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix} \Leftrightarrow \operatorname{inv}_1(A) \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ y_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

As defined this way, this operator is its own inverse: $\operatorname{inv}_k(\operatorname{inv}_k(A)) = A$, and if the pivot block $A_{11}$ is chosen to be the entire matrix, then the transform simply gives the matrix inverse $A^{-1}$. Note that some authors define a related operation (under one of the other names) which is not an inverse per se; particularly, one common definition instead has $(\operatorname{inv}_k)^2 (A) = -A$.

The transform is often presented as a pivot around a single non-zero element $a_{kk}$, in which case one has

$$\left[ \operatorname{inv}_k (A) \right]_{ij} = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{a_{kk}} & i = j = k \\ -\frac{a_{kj}}{a_{kk}} & i = k, j \neq k \\ \frac{a_{ik}}{a_{kk}} & i \neq k, j = k \\ a_{ij} - \frac{a_{ik} a_{kj}}{a_{kk}} & i \neq k, j \neq k \end{cases}$$

## Properties {#Properties}

### Restricted Partial Inversion {#Restricted_Partial_Inversion}

Restricted partial inverses obey a number of nice properties:[^o9]

- inversions around different blocks commute, so larger pivots may be built up from sequences of smaller ones.
- partial inversion preserves the space of symmetric matrices.
- partial inversion over a block of size 1 along the main diagonal of a matrix can be expressed in terms of a composition of an identity operator, a [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) operator and a special commutator that resembles the [Yang-Baxter equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang-Baxter_equation).[^o3]

### General Partial Inversion {#General_Partial_Inversion}

The general partial inversion group $Z(n)$ of generators $\hat{Y}_{k\ell}$ that act on objects of size $n$ is a [non-Abelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Abelian_group), since it is a composition of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S(n)$, which is non-Abelian, with the restricted partial inversion group $V(n)$ (which is Abelian) .[^o3] $Z(n)$ admits representations of the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) and the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993).

$$Z(n) = V(n) \times S(n)$$

#### Commutators {#Commutators}

It was suggested in [^o3] that a composition $\hat{Y}_{ik}\hat{Y}_{j\ell}$ only commutes if their row indices are different and their column indices are also different:

$$\text{[} \hat{Y}_{ik}, \hat{Y}_{j\ell} \text{]} =0 \quad \text{iff} \quad i \neq j \quad \mathrm{and}\quad k \neq \ell.$$

The [Yang-Baxter equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang-Baxter_equation) $ABA = BAB$ occurs as a constraint[^o3] of generators of $Z(n)$, by introducing the substitutions

$$\begin{aligned} A =\hat{Y}_{i-1,i}, \\B= \hat{Y}_{i+1,i}. \end{aligned}$$

#### Dihedral Group Representation {#Dihedral_Group_Representation}

Compositions of matrix inverse along the main diagonal, $\hat{y}_1$ and a generalized inverse along the secondary diagonal $\hat{y}_2$ form a representation of the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267), $D(n)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{y}_1 &= \prod_{k=1}^{n} \mathrm{inv}_k, \\ \hat{y}_2 &= \prod_{k=1}^{n}\hat{Y}_{k,n-k+1}, \\ \hat{y}_3 &:= \hat{y}_1\hat{y}_2 = \hat{J}, \\ \text{[}\hat{y}_k,\, \hat{y}_\ell\text{]} &= 0, \quad \mathrm{with} \quad k,\ell \in \{1,2,3\}, \end{aligned}$$

where $\hat{J}$ is a similarity transformation made of the reverse [permutation matrix](wikipedia://en/238849).

In three dimensions, the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) $D(3)$ over a matrix $M$ is represented in terms of the identity $\hat{I}$ operator and the generators $\hat{y}_1,\hat{y}_2,\hat{y}_3$ as

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{I}M &= M, \\ \hat{y}_1 M &= \mathrm{inv}_3\,\mathrm{inv}_2\, \mathrm{inv}_1M = \hat{Y}_{33}\hat{Y}_{22}\hat{Y}_{11}M, \\ \hat{y}_2 M &= \hat{Y}_{31}\hat{Y}_{22}\hat{Y}_{13}M, \\ \hat{y}_3 M &= \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&1 \\0&1&0 \\1&0&0 \end{pmatrix}M \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&1 \\0&1&0 \\1&0&0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

#### Additional Properties {#Additional_Properties}

- A sequence of partial inversions and matrix powers defines the graph operation of Renormalized Growth,[^o3] where the dimension of the matrix is kept constant, while the graph that represents it is interpreted to grow.

## Applications {#Applications}

### Physics {#Physics}

In [S-matrix theory](wikipedia://en/14026380) and pseudo-unitary [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), partial inversion is used to map S-matrices to T-matrices, and, consequently, unitary matrices to Z-pseudo-unitary matrices.[^o3]

### Numerical Analysis {#Numerical_Analysis}

Use of the partial inverse in numerical analysis is due to the fact that there is some flexibility in the choices of pivots, allowing for non-invertible elements to be avoided, and because the operation of *rotation* (of the graph of the pivoted matrix) has better [numerical stability](wikipedia://en/233807) than the *shearing* operation which is implicitly performed by Gaussian elimination.[^o4] Use in statistics is due to the fact that the resulting matrix nicely decomposes into blocks which have useful meanings in the context of linear regression.[^o9]
