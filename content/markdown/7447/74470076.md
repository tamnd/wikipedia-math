# Coulomb gas

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coulomb gas"}

In [statistical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics), a **Coulomb gas** is a [many-body system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-body_system) of [charged particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_particles) interacting under the [electrostatic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_force). It is named after [Charles-Augustin de Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Augustin_de_Coulomb), as the force by which the particles interact is also known as the Coulomb force.

:::

The system can be defined in any number of dimensions. While the three-dimensional Coulomb gas is the most experimentally realistic, the best understood is the two-dimensional Coulomb gas. The two-dimensional Coulomb gas is known to be equivalent to the continuum [XY model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XY_model) of magnets and the [sine-Gordon model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine-Gordon_model) (upon taking certain limits) in a physical sense, in that physical observables ([correlation functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_functions)) calculated in one model can be used to calculate physical observables in another model. This aided the understanding of the [BKT transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BKT_transition), and the discoverers earned a [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) for their work on this [phase transition](wikipedia://en/54423).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Define the function (**Coulomb kernel**, or **Riesz kernel**)

$$\begin{aligned} g_s(x) = \begin{cases}-\log|x| & \text{ if } s = 0, \\ \frac{1}{s|x|^{s}} & \text{ if } s \neq 0 \end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

The setup starts with considering $N$ charged particles in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with positions $\mathbf{r}_i$ and charges $q_i$. From [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics), the pairwise potential energy between particles labelled by indices $i,j$ is (up to scale factor)

$$V_{ij} = q_iq_jg_{d-2}(|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|),$$

where $g_{d-2}(x)$ is the **Coulomb kernel** or [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) of the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation) in $d$ dimensions.[^o5] The [free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy) due to these interactions is then (proportional to) $F = \sum_{i \neq j} V_{ij}$, and the [partition function](wikipedia://en/312252) is given by integrating over different configurations, that is, the positions of the charged particles.

More generally, any choice of $s \in [d-2, d)$ makes sense. This general case is called **Riesz gas**, of which the Coulomb gas is a special case. The naming comes from the fact that the Riesz kernel is the Green's function of the [fractional Laplacian](wikipedia://en/54352245), which can be defined using the [Riesz potential](wikipedia://en/6779393). Specifically,[^o6]

$$(-\Delta)^{\frac{d-s}2} g_s = c_{d, s} \delta_0$$

where

$$c_{d, s} = \begin{cases}\frac{2^{d-s} \pi^{d / 2} \Gamma\left(\frac{d-s}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)} & \text { for } s>\max (0, d-2) \\ \frac{2 \pi^{d / 2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}=\left|\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right| & \text { if } s=d-2>0 \\ 2 \pi & \text { if } s=0, d=1 \text { or } d=2\end{cases}$$

:::

## Names {#Names}

When there is only one type of charge (conventionally assumed positive), it is called a **one-component [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_%28physics%29)**. Sometimes there is an additional background charge distribution that cancels out the charge on average. For example, in the case of the [Ginibre ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginibre_ensemble), the background charge would be the uniform distribution on the unit disk. With such a neutralizing background, it is called a **[jellium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jellium)**.[^o6]

When $d = 2, s = 0$, i is called a log gas, two-dimensional one-component plasma (2DOCP), two-dimensional jellium, or Dyson gas.[^o6]

## Coulomb gas in conformal field theory {#Coulomb_gas_in_conformal_field_theory}

The two-dimensional Coulomb gas can be used as a framework for describing fields in [minimal models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_model_%28physics%29). This comes from the similarity of the two-point [correlation function](wikipedia://en/59500109) of the free boson $\varphi$,

$$\langle \varphi(z, \bar z) \varphi(w, \bar w) \rangle = - \log|z - w|^2$$

to the electric potential energy between two unit charges in two dimensions.[^o9]
