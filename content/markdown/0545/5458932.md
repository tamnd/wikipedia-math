# Geometric function theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geometric function theory"}

**Geometric function theory** is the study of [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) properties of [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478). A fundamental result in the theory is the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215).

:::

## Topics in geometric function theory {#Topics_in_geometric_function_theory}

The following are some of the most important topics in geometric function theory:[^o3][^o4]

### Conformal maps {#Conformal_maps}

*Image: Conformal map.svg.* A rectangular grid (top) and its image under a conformal map *f* (bottom). It is seen that *f* maps pairs of lines intersecting at 90° to pairs of curves still intersecting at 90°.

A **conformal map** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) which preserves [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) locally. In the most common case the function has a [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and [range](wikipedia://en/275991) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).

More formally, a map,

$f: U \rightarrow V\qquad$ with $U,V \subset \mathbb{C}^n$

is called **conformal** (or **angle-preserving**) at a point $u_0$ if it preserves oriented angles between [curves](wikipedia://en/89246) through $u_0$ with respect to their [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) (i.e., not just the magnitude of the angle). Conformal maps preserve both angles and the shapes of infinitesimally small figures, but not necessarily their size or [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770).

### Quasiconformal maps {#Quasiconformal_maps}

In mathematical [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a **quasiconformal mapping**, introduced by (Grötzsch 1928) and named by (Ahlfors 1935), is a homeomorphism between plane domains which to first order takes small circles to small ellipses of bounded [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/9277#Eccentricity).

Intuitively, let *f* : *D* → *D*′ be an [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29)-preserving [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) between [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in the plane. If *f* is [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable), then it is *K*-quasiconformal if the derivative of *f* at every point maps circles to ellipses with eccentricity bounded by *K*.

If *K* is 0, then the function is [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627).

### Analytic continuation {#Analytic_continuation}

*Image: Imaginary log analytic continuation.png.* Analytic continuation of natural logarithm (imaginary part)

**Analytic continuation** is a technique to extend the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of a given [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478). Analytic continuation often succeeds in defining further values of a function, for example in a new region where an [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) representation in terms of which it is initially defined becomes divergent.

The step-wise continuation technique may, however, come up against difficulties. These may have an essentially topological nature, leading to inconsistencies (defining more than one value). They may alternatively have to do with the presence of [mathematical singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularities). The case of [several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables) is rather different, since singularities then cannot be isolated points, and its investigation was a major reason for the development of [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357).

### Geometric properties of polynomials and algebraic functions {#Geometric_properties_of_polynomials_and_algebraic_functions}

Topics in this area include Riemann surfaces for algebraic functions and zeros for algebraic functions.

#### Riemann surface {#Riemann_surface}

A **Riemann surface**, first studied by and named after [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980), is a one-dimensional [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742). Riemann surfaces can be thought of as deformed versions of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628): locally near every point they look like patches of the complex plane, but the global [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) can be quite different. For example, they can look like a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) or a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) or several sheets glued together.

The main point of Riemann surfaces is that [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) may be defined between them. Riemann surfaces are nowadays considered the natural setting for studying the global behavior of these functions, especially [multi-valued functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-valued_function) such as the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) and other [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169), or the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476).

### Extremal problems {#Extremal_problems}

Topics in this area include "Maximum principle; Schwarz's lemma, Lindelöf principle, analogues and generalizations".[^o13]

### Univalent and multivalent functions {#Univalent_and_multivalent_functions}

A [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) on an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) is called **univalent** if it is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196).

One can prove that if $G$ and $\Omega$ are two open [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) sets in the complex plane, and

$$f: G \to \Omega$$

is a univalent function such that $f(G) = \Omega$ (that is, $f$ is [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873)), then the derivative of $f$ is never zero, $f$ is [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible), and its inverse $f^{-1}$ is also holomorphic. More, one has by the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113)

Alternate terms in common use are *schlicht*( this is German for plain, simple) and *simple*. It is a remarkable fact, fundamental to the theory of univalent functions, that univalence is essentially preserved under uniform convergence.

## Important theorems {#Important_theorems}

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Riemann mapping theorem"}

### Riemann mapping theorem {#Riemann_mapping_theorem}

Let $z_0$ be a point in a simply-connected region $D_1 (D_1 \neq \mathbb{C})$ and $D_1$ having at least two boundary points. Then there exists a unique analytic function $w=f(z)$ mapping $D_1$ bijectively into the open unit disk $|w| < 1$ such that $f(z_0)=0$ and $f'(z_0) > 0$.

Although [Riemann's mapping theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann's_mapping_theorem) demonstrates the existence of a mapping function, it does not actually *exhibit* this function. An example is given below.

*Image: Illustration of Riemann Mapping Theorem.JPG.* Illustration of Riemann Mapping Theorem

In the above figure, consider $D_1$ and $D_2$ as two simply connected regions different from $\mathbb C$. The [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215) provides the existence of $w=f(z)$ mapping $D_1$ onto the unit disk and existence of $w=g(z)$ mapping $D_2$ onto the unit disk. Thus $g^{-1}f$ is a one-to-one mapping of $D_1$ onto $D_2$. If we can show that $g^{-1}$, and consequently the composition, is analytic, we then have a conformal mapping of $D_1$ onto $D_2$, proving "any two simply connected regions different from the whole plane $\mathbb C$ can be mapped conformally onto each other."

:::

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Schwarz's Lemma"}

### Schwarz's Lemma {#Schwarz's_Lemma}

The **Schwarz lemma**, named after [Hermann Amandus Schwarz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Amandus_Schwarz), is a result in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) about [holomorphic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_functions) from the [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) to itself. The lemma is less celebrated than stronger theorems, such as the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215), which it helps to prove. It is however one of the simplest results capturing the rigidity of holomorphic functions.

::: {#o22 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

#### Statement {#Statement}

> **Schwarz Lemma.** Let **D** = {*z* : |*z*| \< 1} be the open [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/5826) **C** centered at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) and let *f* : **D** → **D** be a [holomorphic map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_map) such that *f*(0) = 0.
>
> Then, |*f*(*z*)| ≤ |*z*| for all *z* in **D** and |*f′*(0)| ≤ 1.
>
> Moreover, if |*f*(*z*)| = |*z*| for some non-zero *z* or if |*f′*(0)| = 1, then *f*(*z*) = *az* for some *a* in **C** with |*a*| (necessarily) equal to 1.

:::

::: {#o24 .statement cue=named-heading name="Maximum principle"}

### Maximum principle {#Maximum_principle}

The [maximum principle](wikipedia://en/1436104) is a property of solutions to certain [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations), of the [elliptic](wikipedia://en/662787) and [parabolic](wikipedia://en/7466971) types. Roughly speaking, it says that the [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum) of a function in a [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643) is to be found on the boundary of that domain. Specifically, the *strong* maximum principle says that if a function achieves its maximum in the interior of the domain, the function is uniformly a constant. The *weak* maximum principle says that the maximum of the function is to be found on the boundary, but may re-occur in the interior as well. Other, even weaker maximum principles exist which merely bound a function in terms of its maximum on the boundary.

:::

::: {#o26 .statement cue=named-heading name="Riemann-Hurwitz formula"}

### Riemann-Hurwitz formula {#Riemann-Hurwitz_formula}

the **Riemann–Hurwitz formula**, named after [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) and [Adolf Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690), describes the relationship of the [Euler characteristics](wikipedia://en/148420) of two [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) when one is a *ramified covering* of the other. It therefore connects [ramification](wikipedia://en/384644) with [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), in this case. It is a prototype result for many others, and is often applied in the theory of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181) (which is its origin) and [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260).

::: {#o28 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

#### Statement {#Statement}

For an [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable) surface *S* the Euler characteristic χ(*S*) is

$$2-2g \,$$

where *g* is the [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) (the *number of handles*), since the [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262) are 1, 2*g*, 1, 0, 0, ... . In the case of an (*unramified*) [covering map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_map) of surfaces

$$\pi:S' \to S \,$$

that is surjective and of degree *N*, we should have the formula

$$\chi(S') = N\cdot\chi(S). \,$$

That is because each simplex of *S* should be covered by exactly *N* in *S*′ — at least if we use a fine enough [triangulation](wikipedia://en/1440970) of *S*, as we are entitled to do since the Euler characteristic is a [topological invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant). What the Riemann–Hurwitz formula does is to add in a correction to allow for ramification (*sheets coming together*).

Now assume that *S* and *S′* are [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), and that the map π is [complex analytic](wikipedia://en/61478). The map π is said to be *ramified* at a point *P* in *S*′ if there exist analytic coordinates near *P* and π(*P*) such that π takes the form π(*z*) = *z*<sup>*n*</sup>, and *n* > 1. An equivalent way of thinking about this is that there exists a small neighborhood *U* of *P* such that π(*P*) has exactly one preimage in *U*, but the image of any other point in *U* has exactly *n* preimages in *U*. The number *n* is called the *[ramification index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramification_index) at P* and also denoted by *e*<sub>*P*</sub>. In calculating the Euler characteristic of *S*′ we notice the loss of *e<sub>P</sub>* − 1 copies of *P* above π(*P*) (that is, in the inverse image of π(*P*)). Now let us choose triangulations of *S* and *S′* with vertices at the branch and ramification points, respectively, and use these to compute the Euler characteristics. Then *S′* will have the same number of *d*-dimensional faces for *d* different from zero, but fewer than expected vertices. Therefore, we find a "corrected" formula

$$\chi(S') = N\cdot\chi(S) - \sum_{P\in S'} (e_P -1)$$

(all but finitely many *P* have *e<sub>P</sub>* = 1, so this is quite safe). This formula is known as the *Riemann–Hurwitz formula* and also as **Hurwitz's theorem**.
