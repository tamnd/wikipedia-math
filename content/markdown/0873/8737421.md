# Series acceleration

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Series acceleration"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **series acceleration** method is any one of a collection of [sequence transformations](wikipedia://en/2293999) for improving the [rate of convergence](wikipedia://en/999701) of a [series](wikipedia://en/15287). Techniques for series acceleration are often applied in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), where they are used to improve the speed of [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089). Series acceleration techniques may also be used, for example, to obtain a variety of identities on [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110). Thus, the [Euler transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_transform) applied to the [hypergeometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_series) gives some of the classic, well-known hypergeometric series identities.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given an infinite [series](wikipedia://en/15287) with a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of partial sums

$$( S_n )_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$$

having a [limit](wikipedia://en/285773)

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} S_n = S,$$

an accelerated series is an infinite series with a second sequence of partial sums

$$( S'_n )_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$$

which [asymptotically](wikipedia://en/641995) [converges faster](wikipedia://en/999701) to $S$ than the original sequence of partial sums would:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{S'_n-S}{S_n-S} = 0.$$

A series acceleration method is a [sequence transformation](wikipedia://en/2293999) that transforms the convergent sequences of partial sums of a series into more quickly convergent sequences of partial sums of an accelerated series with the same limit. If a series acceleration method is applied to a [divergent series](wikipedia://en/876428) then the proper limit of the series is undefined, but the sequence transformation can still act usefully as an [extrapolation method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrapolation_method) to an [antilimit](wikipedia://en/72396541) of the series.

The mappings from the original to the transformed series may be linear sequence transformations or non-linear sequence transformations. In general, the non-linear sequence transformations tend to be more powerful.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Two classical techniques for series acceleration are [Euler's transformation of series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_transformation_of_series)[^o5] and [Kummer's transformation of series](wikipedia://en/61690057).[^o6] A variety of much more rapidly convergent and special-case tools have been developed in the 20th century, including [Richardson extrapolation](wikipedia://en/650022), introduced by [Lewis Fry Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Fry_Richardson) in the early 20th century but also known and used by [Katahiro Takebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takebe_Kenko) in 1722; the [Aitken delta-squared process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aitken_delta-squared_process), introduced by [Alexander Aitken](wikipedia://en/3348690) in 1926 but also known and used by [Takakazu Seki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takakazu_Seki) in the 18th century; the [epsilon method](http://mathworld.wolfram.com/WynnsEpsilonMethod.html) given by [Peter Wynn](wikipedia://en/27024678) in 1956; the Levin u-transform; and the Wilf-Zeilberger-Ekhad method or [WZ method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WZ_theory).

For [alternating series](wikipedia://en/494308), several powerful techniques, offering convergence rates from $5.828^{-n}$ all the way to $17.93^{-n}$ for a summation of $n$ terms, are described by Cohen *et al*.[^o7]

## Euler's transform {#Euler's_transform}

A basic example of a [linear sequence transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_sequence_transformation), offering improved convergence, is Euler's transform. It is intended to be applied to an alternating series; it is given by

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n a_n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n \frac{(\Delta^n a)_0}{2^{n+1}}$$

where $\Delta$ is the [forward difference operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference_operator), for which one has the formula

$$(\Delta^n a)_0 = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k {n \choose k} a_{n-k}.$$

If the original series, on the left hand side, is only slowly converging, the forward differences will tend to become small quite rapidly; the additional power of two further improves the rate at which the right hand side converges.

A particularly efficient numerical implementation of the Euler transform is the [van Wijngaarden transformation](wikipedia://en/12480332).[^o9]

## Conformal mappings {#Conformal_mappings}

A series

$$S = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n$$

can be written as $f(1)$, where the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f* is defined as

$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n z^n.$$

The function $f(z)$ can have [singularities](wikipedia://en/54240#Complex_analysis) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) ([branch point](wikipedia://en/483173) singularities, [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) or [essential singularities](wikipedia://en/80862)), which limit the [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476) of the series. If the point $z = 1$ is close to or on the boundary of the disk of convergence, the series for $S$ will converge very slowly. One can then improve the convergence of the series by means of a [conformal mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_mapping) that moves the singularities such that the point that is mapped to $z = 1$ ends up deeper in the new disk of convergence.

The conformal transform $z = \Phi(w)$ needs to be chosen such that $\Phi(0) = 0$, and one usually chooses a function that has a finite [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) at *w* = 0. One can assume that $\Phi(1) = 1$ without loss of generality, as one can always rescale *w* to redefine $\Phi$. We then consider the function

$$g(w) = f(\Phi(w)).$$

Since $\Phi(1) = 1$, we have $f(1) = g(1)$. We can obtain the series expansion of $g(w)$ by putting $z = \Phi(w)$ in the series expansion of $f(z)$ because $\Phi(0)=0$; the first $n$ terms of the series expansion for $f(z)$ will yield the first $n$ terms of the series expansion for $g(w)$ if $\Phi'(0) \neq 0$. Putting $w = 1$ in that series expansion will thus yield a series such that if it converges, it will converge to the same value as the original series.

## Non-linear sequence transformations {#Non-linear_sequence_transformations}

Examples of such nonlinear sequence transformations are [Padé approximants](wikipedia://en/3090820), the [Shanks transformation](wikipedia://en/21581860), and [Levin-type sequence transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levin-type_sequence_transformations).

Especially nonlinear sequence transformations often provide powerful numerical methods for the [summation](wikipedia://en/246160) of [divergent series](wikipedia://en/876428) or [asymptotic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_series) that arise for instance in [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/102140), and therefore may be used as effective [extrapolation methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrapolation_method).

### Aitken method {#Aitken_method}

A simple nonlinear sequence transformation is the Aitken extrapolation or delta-squared method,

$$\mathbb{A} : S \to S'=\mathbb{A}(S) = {(s'_n)}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$$

defined by

$$s'_n = s_{n+2} - \frac{(s_{n+2}-s_{n+1})^2}{s_{n+2}-2s_{n+1}+s_n}.$$

This transformation is commonly used to improve the [rate of convergence](wikipedia://en/999701) of a slowly converging sequence; heuristically, it eliminates the largest part of the [absolute error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_error).
