# Proportionality (mathematics)

*Image: Proportional variables.svg.* The variable $y$ is directly proportional to the variable $x$ with proportionality constant ~0.6.

*Image: Inverse proportionality function plot.gif.* The variable $y$ is inversely proportional to the variable $x$ with proportionality constant 1.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proportionality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), two [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) of numbers, often [experimental data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_data), are **proportional** or **directly proportional** if their corresponding elements have a constant [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837). The ratio is called ***coefficient of proportionality*** (or ***proportionality constant***) and its reciprocal is known as ***constant of normalization*** (or ***normalizing constant***). Two sequences are **inversely proportional** if corresponding elements have a constant [product](wikipedia://en/24977).

:::

Two [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are *proportional* if their ratio $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ is a [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861).

If several pairs of variables share the same direct proportionality constant, the [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) expressing the equality of these ratios is called a **proportion**, e.g., $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{x}{y} = \cdots = k$ (for details see [Ratio](wikipedia://en/87837)). Proportionality is closely related to *[linearity](wikipedia://en/91591)*.

## Direct proportionality {#Direct_proportionality}

::: {#o3 .definition cue=dfn name="directly proportional"}

Given an [independent variable](wikipedia://en/3728109#Dependent_and_independent_variables) $x$ and a dependent variable $y$, $y$ is ****directly proportional**** to $x$[^o4] if there is a positive constant $k$ such that:

$$y = kx.$$

:::

The relation is often denoted using the symbols ∝ (not to be confused with the Greek letter [alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha)) or ~, with exception of Japanese texts, where ~ is reserved for intervals:

$$y \propto x \quad\text{or}\quad y \sim x.$$

For $x \ne 0$ the **proportionality constant** can be expressed as the ratio:

$$k = \frac{y}{x}.$$

It is also called the *constant of variation* or *constant of proportionality*. Given such a constant $k$, the proportionality [relation](wikipedia://en/3931) ∝ with proportionality constant $k$ between two sets $A$ and $B$ is the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) defined by

$$\{(a, b) \in A \times B : a = k b\}.$$

A direct proportionality can also be viewed as a [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570) in two variables with a [$y$-intercept](wikipedia://en/464331) of $0$ and a [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of $k > 0$, which corresponds to [linear growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_growth).

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- If an object travels at a constant [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed), then the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) traveled is directly proportional to the [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) spent traveling, with the speed being the constant of proportionality.
- The [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is directly proportional to its [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007), with the constant of proportionality equal to [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601).
- On a [map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map) of a sufficiently small geographical area, drawn to [scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_%28map%29) distances, the distance between any two points on the map is directly proportional to the beeline distance between the two locations represented by those points; the constant of proportionality is the scale of the map.
- The [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_%28physics%29), acting on a small object with small [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) by a nearby large extended mass due to [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), is directly proportional to the object's mass; the constant of proportionality between the force and the mass is known as [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration).
- The net force acting on an object is proportional to the acceleration of that object with respect to an inertial frame of reference. The constant of proportionality in this, [Newton's second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_second_law), is the classical mass of the object.

:::

## Inverse proportionality {#Inverse_proportionality}

*Image: Inverse proportionality function plot.gif.* Inverse proportionality with product $xy = 1 .$

Two variables are **inversely proportional** (also called **varying inversely**, in **inverse variation**, in **inverse proportion**)[^o9] if each of the variables is directly proportional to the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) (reciprocal) of the other, or equivalently if their [product](wikipedia://en/24977) is a constant.[^o10]

The relation is often denoted using the symbols ∝ (not to be confused with the Greek letter [alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha)) or ~, with exception of Japanese texts, where ~ is reserved for intervals:

$$y \propto 1/x \quad\text{or}\quad y \sim 1/x.$$

It follows that the variable $y$ is inversely proportional to the variable $x$ if there exists a non-zero constant $k$ such that

$$y = \frac{k}{x} \quad \iff \quad xy = k.$$

Hence the constant $k$ is the product of $x$ and $y$.

The graph of two variables varying inversely on the [Cartesian coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate) plane is a [rectangular hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola). The product of the $x$ and $y$ values of each point on the curve equals the constant of proportionality $k$. Since neither $x$ nor $y$ can equal zero (because $k$ is non-zero), the graph never crosses either axis.

Direct and inverse proportion contrast as follows: in direct proportion the variables increase or decrease together. With inverse proportion, an increase in one variable is associated with a decrease in the other. For instance, in travel, a constant speed dictates a direct proportion between distance and time travelled; in contrast, for a given distance (the constant), the time of travel is inversely proportional to speed: $s \times t = d$.

:::

## Hyperbolic coordinates {#Hyperbolic_coordinates}

The concepts of *direct* and *inverse* proportion lead to the location of points in the Cartesian plane by [hyperbolic coordinates](wikipedia://en/1264398); the two coordinates correspond to the constant of direct proportionality that specifies a point as being on a particular [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29#Ray) and the [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132) of inverse proportionality that specifies a point as being on a particular [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052).
