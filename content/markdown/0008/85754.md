# Phonon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Phonon"}

A **phonon** is a [quasiparticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiparticle), [collective excitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_excitation) in a periodic, [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29) arrangement of [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) or [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) in [condensed matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics), specifically in [solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) and some [liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid). In the context of optically trapped objects, the quantized vibration mode can be defined as phonons as long as the modal wavelength of the oscillation is smaller than the size of the object. A type of [quasiparticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiparticle) in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939),[^o2] a phonon is an [excited state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state) in the [quantum mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanical) [quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_%28physics%29) of the [modes of vibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_of_vibration) for elastic structures of interacting particles. Phonons can be thought of as quantized [sound waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_waves), similar to [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photons) as quantized [light waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_waves).[^o3]

:::

The study of phonons is an important part of condensed matter physics. They play a major role in many of the physical properties of condensed matter systems, such as [thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity) and [electrical conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity), as well as in models of [neutron scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_scattering) and related effects.

The concept of phonons was introduced in 1930 by [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) physicist [Igor Tamm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Tamm). The name *phonon* was suggested by [Yakov Frenkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakov_Frenkel).[^o4] It comes from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language) word φωνή (grc), which translates to *sound* or *voice*, because long-wavelength phonons give rise to [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound). The name emphasizes the analogy to the word *photon*, in that phonons represent [wave-particle duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave–particle_duality) for [sound waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) in the same way that photons represent wave-particle duality for [light waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light). Solids with more than one atom in the smallest [unit cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_cell) exhibit both acoustic and optical phonons.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A phonon is the [quantum mechanical](wikipedia://en/25202) description of an elementary [vibrational](wikipedia://en/20646772) motion in which a [lattice](wikipedia://en/291472) of atoms or molecules uniformly oscillates at a single [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779).[^o7] In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) this designates a [normal mode](wikipedia://en/478195) of vibration. Normal modes are important because any arbitrary lattice vibration can be considered to be a [superposition](wikipedia://en/1201321) of these *elementary* vibration modes (cf. [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659)). While normal modes are [wave-like](wikipedia://en/33516) phenomena in classical mechanics, phonons have [particle-like](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) properties too, in a way related to the [wave–particle duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave–particle_duality) of quantum mechanics.

:::

## Lattice dynamics {#Lattice_dynamics}

The equations in this section do not use [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) of quantum mechanics but instead use relations for which there exists a direct [correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_principle) in classical mechanics.

For example: a rigid regular, [crystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystalline) (not [amorphous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorphous_solid)) lattice is composed of *N* particles. These particles may be atoms or molecules. *N* is a large number, say of the order of 10<sup>23</sup>, or on the order of the [Avogadro number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_number) for a typical sample of a solid. Since the lattice is rigid, the atoms must be exerting [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) on one another to keep each atom near its equilibrium position. These forces may be [Van der Waals forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waals_force), [covalent bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond), [electrostatic attractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_attraction), and others, all of which are ultimately due to the [electric](wikipedia://en/41092) force. [Magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism) and [gravitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) forces are generally negligible. The forces between each pair of atoms may be characterized by a [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) function *V* that depends on the distance of separation of the atoms. The potential energy of the entire lattice is the sum of all pairwise potential energies multiplied by a factor of 1/2 to compensate for double counting:[^o9]

$$\frac12\sum_{i \neq j} V\left(r_i - r_j\right)$$

where *r<sub>i</sub>* is the [position](wikipedia://en/27667) of the *i*th atom, and *V* is the potential energy between two atoms.

It is difficult to solve this [many-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-body_problem) explicitly in either classical or quantum mechanics. In order to simplify the task, two important [approximations](wikipedia://en/336271) are usually imposed. First, the sum is only performed over neighboring atoms. Although the electric forces in real solids extend to infinity, this approximation is still valid because the fields produced by distant atoms are effectively [screened](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field_screening). Secondly, the potentials *V* are treated as [harmonic potentials](wikipedia://en/13899). This is permissible as long as the atoms remain close to their equilibrium positions. Formally, this is accomplished by [Taylor expanding](wikipedia://en/30448) *V* about its equilibrium value to quadratic order, giving *V* proportional to the displacement *x*<sup>2</sup> and the elastic force simply proportional to *x*. The error in ignoring higher order terms remains small if *x* remains close to the equilibrium position.

The resulting lattice may be visualized as a system of balls connected by springs. The following figure shows a cubic lattice, which is a good model for many types of crystalline solid. Other lattices include a linear chain, which is a very simple lattice which we will shortly use for modeling phonons. (For other common lattices, see [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure).)

*Image: Cubic.svg.* A cubic crystal structure

The potential energy of the lattice may now be written as

$$\sum_{\{ij\} (\mathrm{nn})} \tfrac12 m \omega^2 \left(R_i - R_j\right)^2.$$

Here, *ω* is the [natural frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_frequency) of the harmonic potentials, which are assumed to be the same since the lattice is regular. *R<sub>i</sub>* is the position coordinate of the *i*th atom, which we now measure from its equilibrium position. The sum over nearest neighbors is denoted (nn).

It is important to mention that the mathematical treatment given here is highly simplified in order to make it accessible to non-experts. The simplification has been achieved by making two basic assumptions in the expression for the total potential energy of the crystal. These assumptions are that (i) the total potential energy can be written as a sum of pairwise interactions, and (ii) each atom interacts with only its nearest neighbors. These are used only sparingly in modern lattice dynamics.[^o10] A more general approach is to express the potential energy in terms of force constants.[^o10] See, for example, the Wiki article on [multiscale Green's functions.](wikipedia://en/52430241)

### Lattice waves {#Lattice_waves}

*Image: Lattice wave.svg.* Phonon propagating through a square lattice (atom displacements greatly exaggerated)

Due to the connections between atoms, the displacement of one or more atoms from their equilibrium positions gives rise to a set of vibration [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) propagating through the lattice. One such wave is shown in the figure to the right. The [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) of the wave is given by the displacements of the atoms from their equilibrium positions. The [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) *λ* is marked.

There is a minimum possible wavelength, given by twice the equilibrium separation *a* between atoms. Any wavelength shorter than this can be mapped onto a wavelength longer than 2*a*, due to the periodicity of the lattice. This can be thought of as a consequence of the [Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem](wikipedia://en/37864), the lattice points being viewed as the "sampling points" of a continuous wave.

Not every possible lattice vibration has a well-defined wavelength and frequency. However, the [normal modes](wikipedia://en/478195) do possess well-defined wavelengths and [frequencies](wikipedia://en/10779).

### One-dimensional lattice {#One-dimensional_lattice}

*Image: 1D normal modes (280 kB).gif.* Animation showing 6 normal modes of a one-dimensional lattice: a linear chain of particles. The shortest wavelength is at top, with progressively longer wavelengths below. In the lowest lines the motion of the waves to the right can be seen.

In order to simplify the analysis needed for a 3-dimensional lattice of atoms, it is convenient to model a 1-dimensional lattice or linear chain. This model is complex enough to display the salient features of phonons.

#### Classical treatment {#Classical_treatment}

The forces between the atoms are assumed to be linear and nearest-neighbour, and they are represented by an elastic spring. Each atom is assumed to be a point particle and the nucleus and electrons move in step ([adiabatic theorem](wikipedia://en/848629)):

*n* − 1 1em *n* 2em *n* + 1 5em ← 1em *a* 1em →

···o++++++o++++++o++++++o++++++o++++++o++++++o++++++o++++++o++++++o···

→→2em→2em→→→

*u*<sub>*n* − 1</sub>2em*u<sub>n</sub>*2em*u*<sub>*n* + 1</sub>

where $n$ labels the $n$th atom out of a total of $N$, $a$ is the distance between atoms when the chain is in equilibrium, and $u_{n}$ the displacement of the $n$th atom from its equilibrium position.

If *C* is the elastic constant of the spring and $m$ the mass of the atom, then the equation of motion of the $n$th atom is

$$-2Cu_n + C\left(u_{n+1} + u_{n-1}\right) = m\frac{d^2u_n}{dt^2} .$$

This is a set of coupled equations.

Since the solutions are expected to be oscillatory, new coordinates are defined by a [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811), in order to decouple them.[^o14]

Put

$$u_n = \sum_{Nak/2\pi=1}^N Q_k e^{ikna}.$$

Here, $na$ corresponds and devolves to the continuous variable $x$ of scalar field theory. The $Q_{k}$ are known as the *normal coordinates* for continuum field modes $\phi_k = e^{ikna}$ with $k = 2\pi j/(Na)$ for $j=1\dots N$.

Substitution into the equation of motion produces the following *decoupled equations* (this requires a significant manipulation using the orthonormality and completeness relations of the discrete Fourier transform),[^o15]

$$2C(\cos {ka-1})Q_k = m\frac{d^2Q_k}{dt^2}.$$

These are the equations for decoupled [harmonic oscillators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_oscillators) which have the solution

$$Q_k=A_ke^{i\omega_kt};\qquad \omega_k=\sqrt{ \frac{2C}{m}(1-\cos{ka})}.$$

Each normal coordinate *Q<sub>k</sub>* represents an independent vibrational mode of the lattice with wavenumber $k$, which is known as a [normal mode](wikipedia://en/478195).

The second equation, for $\omega_{k}$, is known as the [dispersion relation](wikipedia://en/853778) between the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) and the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber).

In the [continuum limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_limit), $a$→0, $N$→∞, with $Na$ held fixed, $u_{n}$ → $\varphi(x)$, a scalar field, and $\omega(k) \propto k a$. This amounts to classical free [scalar field theory](wikipedia://en/5173456), an assembly of independent oscillators.

#### Quantum treatment {#Quantum_treatment}

A one-dimensional quantum mechanical harmonic chain consists of *N* identical atoms. This is the simplest quantum mechanical model of a lattice that allows phonons to arise from it. The formalism for this model is readily generalizable to two and three dimensions.

In contrast to the previous section, the positions of the masses are not denoted by $u_i$, but instead by $x_1,x_2,\dots$ as measured from their equilibrium positions. (I.e. $x_i=0$ if particle $i$ is at its equilibrium position.) In two or more dimensions, the $x_i$ are vector quantities. The [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) for this system is

$$\mathcal{H} = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{p_i^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2} m\omega^2 \sum_{\{ij\} (\mathrm{nn})} \left(x_i - x_j\right)^2$$

where *m* is the mass of each atom (assuming it is equal for all), and *x<sub>i</sub>* and *p<sub>i</sub>* are the position and [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) operators, respectively, for the *i*th atom and the sum is made over the nearest neighbors (nn). However one expects that in a lattice there could also appear waves that behave like particles. It is customary to deal with [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) in [Fourier space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_space) which uses [normal modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_modes) of the [wavevector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavevector) as variables instead of coordinates of particles. The number of normal modes is the same as the number of particles. Still, the Fourier space is very useful given the [periodicity](wikipedia://en/59038) of the system.

A set of *N* "normal coordinates" *Q<sub>k</sub>* may be introduced, defined as the [discrete Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/8811) of the *x<sub>k</sub>* and *N* "conjugate momenta" *Π<sub>k</sub>* defined as the Fourier transforms of the *p<sub>k</sub>*:

$$\begin{aligned} Q_k &= \frac{1}\sqrt{N} \sum_{l} e^{ikal} x_l \\ \Pi_{k} &= \frac{1}\sqrt{N} \sum_{l} e^{-ikal} p_l. \end{aligned}$$

The quantity *k* turns out to be the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber) of the phonon, i.e. 2$\pi$ divided by the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125).

This choice retains the desired commutation relations in either real space or wavevector space

$$\begin{aligned} \left[x_l , p_m \right]&=i\hbar\delta_{l,m} \\ \left[ Q_k , \Pi_{k'} \right] &=\frac{1}N \sum_{l,m} e^{ikal} e^{-ik'am} \left[x_l , p_m \right] \\ &= \frac{i \hbar}N \sum_{l} e^{ial\left(k-k'\right)} = i\hbar\delta_{k,k'} \\ \left[ Q_k , Q_{k'} \right] &= \left[ \Pi_k , \Pi_{k'} \right] = 0 \end{aligned}$$

From the general result

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{l}x_l x_{l+m}&=\frac{1}N\sum_{kk'}Q_k Q_{k'}\sum_{l} e^{ial\left(k+k'\right)}e^{iamk}= \sum_{k}Q_k Q_{-k}e^{iamk} \\ \sum_{l}{p_l}^2 &= \sum_{k}\Pi_k \Pi_{-k} \end{aligned}$$

The potential energy term is

$$\tfrac12 m \omega^2 \sum_{j} \left(x_j - x_{j+1}\right)^2= \tfrac12 m\omega^2\sum_{k}Q_k Q_{-k}(2-e^{ika}-e^{-ika})= \tfrac12 \sum_{k}m{\omega_k}^2Q_k Q_{-k}$$

where

$$\omega_k = \sqrt{2 \omega^2 \left( 1 - \cos{ka} \right)} = 2\omega\left|\sin\frac{ka}2\right|$$

The Hamiltonian may be written in wavevector space as

$$\mathcal{H} = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_k \left( \Pi_k\Pi_{-k} + m^2 \omega_k^2 Q_k Q_{-k} \right)$$

The couplings between the position variables have been transformed away; if the *Q* and *Π* were [Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_operator) (which they are not), the transformed Hamiltonian would describe *N* uncoupled harmonic oscillators.

The form of the quantization depends on the choice of boundary conditions; for simplicity, *periodic* boundary conditions are imposed, defining the (*N* + 1)th atom as equivalent to the first atom. Physically, this corresponds to joining the chain at its ends. The resulting quantization is

$$k=k_n = \frac{2\pi n}{Na} \quad \mbox{for } n = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \ldots \pm \frac{N}2 .\$$

The upper bound to *n* comes from the minimum wavelength, which is twice the lattice spacing *a*, as discussed above.

The harmonic oscillator eigenvalues or energy levels for the mode *ω<sub>k</sub>* are:

$$E_n = \left(\tfrac12+n\right)\hbar\omega_k \qquad n=0,1,2,3 \ldots$$

The levels are evenly spaced at:

$$\tfrac12\hbar\omega , \ \tfrac32\hbar\omega ,\ \tfrac52\hbar\omega \ \cdots$$

where $\tfrac{1}{2}$*ħω* is the [zero-point energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-point_energy) of a [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719).

An **exact** amount of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) *ħω* must be supplied to the harmonic oscillator lattice to push it to the next energy level. By analogy to the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) case when the [electromagnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) is quantized, the quantum of vibrational energy is called a phonon.

All quantum systems show wavelike and particlelike properties simultaneously. The particle-like properties of the phonon are best understood using the methods of [second quantization](wikipedia://en/418156) and operator techniques described later.[^o17]

### Three-dimensional lattice {#Three-dimensional_lattice}

This may be generalized to a three-dimensional lattice. The wavenumber *k* is replaced by a three-dimensional [wavevector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavevector) **k**. Furthermore, each **k** is now associated with three normal coordinates.

The new indices *s* = 1, 2, 3 label the [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) of the phonons. In the one-dimensional model, the atoms were restricted to moving along the line, so the phonons corresponded to [longitudinal waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave). In three dimensions, vibration is not restricted to the direction of propagation, and can also occur in the perpendicular planes, like [transverse waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_wave). This gives rise to the additional normal coordinates, which, as the form of the Hamiltonian indicates, we may view as independent species of phonons.

### Dispersion relation {#Dispersion_relation}

*Image: Diatomic phonons.png.* Dispersion curves in linear diatomic chain

*Image: Optical & acoustic vibrations-en.svg.* Optical and acoustic vibrations in a linear diatomic chain.

*Image: Diatomic chain.gif.* Vibrations of the diatomic chain at different frequencies.

*Image: Phonon dispersion relations in GaAs.png.* Dispersion relation *ω* = *ω*(**k**) for some waves corresponding to lattice vibrations in GaAs.[^o21]

For a one-dimensional alternating array of two types of ion or atom of mass *m*<sub>1</sub>, *m*<sub>2</sub> repeated periodically at a distance *a*, connected by springs of spring constant *K*, two modes of vibration result:[^o20]

$$\omega_\pm^2 = K\left(\frac{1}{m_1} +\frac{1}{m_2}\right) \pm K \sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{m_1} +\frac{1}{m_2}\right)^2-\frac{4\sin^2\frac{ka}{2}}{m_1 m_2}} ,$$

where *k* is the wavevector of the vibration related to its wavelength by $k = \tfrac{2 \pi}{\lambda}$.

The connection between frequency and wavevector, *ω* = *ω*(*k*), is known as a [dispersion relation](wikipedia://en/853778). The plus sign results in the so-called *optical* mode, and the minus sign to the *acoustic* mode. In the optical mode two adjacent different atoms move against each other, while in the acoustic mode they move together.

The speed of propagation of an acoustic phonon, which is also the [speed of sound](wikipedia://en/147853) in the lattice, is given by the slope of the acoustic dispersion relation, $\tfrac{\partial\omega_{k}}{\partial k}$ (see [group velocity](wikipedia://en/12778).) At low values of *k* (i.e. long wavelengths), the dispersion relation is almost linear, and the speed of sound is approximately *ωa*, independent of the phonon frequency. As a result, packets of phonons with different (but long) wavelengths can propagate for large distances across the lattice without breaking apart. This is the reason that sound propagates through solids without significant distortion. This behavior fails at large values of *k*, i.e. short wavelengths, due to the microscopic details of the lattice.

For a crystal that has at least two atoms in its [primitive cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigner-Seitz_cell#Primitive_cell), the dispersion relations exhibit two types of phonons, namely, optical and acoustic modes corresponding to the upper blue and lower red curve in the diagram, respectively. The vertical axis is the energy or frequency of phonon, while the horizontal axis is the [wavevector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavevector). The boundaries at −$\tfrac{\pi}{a}$ and $\tfrac{\pi}{a}$ are those of the first [Brillouin zone](wikipedia://en/562067).[^o20] A crystal with *N* ≥ 2 different atoms in the [primitive cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_cell) exhibits three acoustic modes: one [longitudinal acoustic mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave) and two [transverse acoustic modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_wave). The number of optical modes is 3*N* – 3. The lower figure shows the dispersion relations for several phonon modes in [GaAs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GaAs) as a function of wavevector **k** in the [principal directions](wikipedia://en/562067#Critical_points) of its Brillouin zone.[^o21]

The modes are also referred to as the branches of phonon dispersion. In general, if there are p atoms (denoted by N earlier) in the primitive unit cell, there will be 3p branches of phonon dispersion in a 3-dimensional crystal. Out of these, 3 branches correspond to acoustic modes and the remaining 3p-3 branches will correspond to optical modes. In some special directions, some branches coincide due to symmetry. These branches are called degenerate. In acoustic modes, all the p atoms vibrate in phase. So there is no change in the relative displacements of these atoms during the wave propagation.

Study of phonon dispersion is useful for modeling propagation of sound waves in solids, which is characterized by phonons. The energy of each phonon, as given earlier, is *ħω.* The velocity of the wave also is given in terms of *ω* and k *.* The direction of the wave vector is the direction of the wave propagation and the phonon polarization vector gives the direction in which the atoms vibrate. Actually, in general, the wave velocity in a crystal is different for different directions of k. In other words, most crystals are anisotropic for phonon propagation.

A wave is longitudinal if the atoms vibrate in the same direction as the wave propagation. In a transverse wave, the atoms vibrate perpendicular to the wave propagation. However, except for isotropic crystals, waves in a crystal are not exactly longitudinal or transverse. For general anisotropic crystals, the phonon waves are longitudinal or transverse only in certain special symmetry directions. In other directions, they can be nearly longitudinal or nearly transverse. It is only for labeling convenience, that they are often called longitudinal or transverse but are actually quasi-longitudinal or quasi-transverse. Note that in the three-dimensional case, there are two directions perpendicular to a straight line at each point on the line. Hence, there are always two (quasi) transverse waves for each (quasi) longitudinal wave.

Many phonon dispersion curves have been measured by [inelastic neutron scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inelastic_neutron_scattering).

The physics of sound in [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) differs from the physics of sound in solids, although both are density waves: sound waves in fluids only have longitudinal components, whereas sound waves in solids have longitudinal and transverse components. This is because fluids cannot support [shear stresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) (but see [viscoelastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscoelastic) fluids, which only apply to high frequencies).

### Interpretation of phonons using second quantization techniques {#Interpretation_of_phonons_using_second_quantization_techniques}

The above-derived Hamiltonian may look like a classical Hamiltonian function, but if it is interpreted as an [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29), then it describes a [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) of non-interacting [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson).[^o3] The [second quantization](wikipedia://en/418156) technique, similar to the [ladder operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladder_operator) method used for [quantum harmonic oscillators](wikipedia://en/50719#Ladder_operator_method), is a means of extracting energy [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) without directly solving the differential equations. Given the Hamiltonian, $\mathcal{H}$, as well as the conjugate position, $Q_k$, and conjugate momentum $\Pi_{k}$ defined in the quantum treatment section above, we can define [creation and annihilation operators](wikipedia://en/701991):[^o23]

$b_k=\sqrt\frac{m\omega_k}{2\hbar}\left(Q_k+\frac{i}{m\omega_k}\Pi_{-k}\right)$ and ${b_k}^\dagger=\sqrt\frac{m\omega_k}{2\hbar}\left(Q_{-k}-\frac{i}{m\omega_k}\Pi_{k}\right)$

The following commutators can be easily obtained by substituting in the [canonical commutation relation](wikipedia://en/706295):

$$\left[b_k , {b_{k'}}^\dagger \right] = \delta_{k,k'} ,\quad \Big[b_k , b_{k'} \Big] = \left[{b_k}^\dagger , {b_{k'}}^\dagger \right] = 0$$

Using this, the operators *b<sub>k</sub>*<sup>†</sup> and *b<sub>k</sub>* can be inverted to redefine the conjugate position and momentum as:

$Q_k=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_k}}\left({b_k}^\dagger+b_{-k}\right)$ and $\Pi_k=i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m\omega_k}{2}}\left({b_k}^\dagger-b_{-k}\right)$

Directly substituting these definitions for $Q_k$ and $\Pi_k$ into the wavevector space Hamiltonian, as it is defined above, and simplifying then results in the Hamiltonian taking the form:[^o3]

$$\mathcal{H} =\sum_k \hbar\omega_k \left({b_k}^\dagger b_k+\tfrac12\right)$$

This is known as the second quantization technique, also known as the occupation number formulation, where *n<sub>k</sub>* = *b<sub>k</sub>*<sup>†</sup>*b<sub>k</sub>* is the occupation number. This can be seen to be a sum of N independent oscillator Hamiltonians, each with a unique wave vector, and compatible with the methods used for the quantum harmonic oscillator (note that *n<sub>k</sub>* is [hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682)).[^o23] When a Hamiltonian can be written as a sum of commuting sub-Hamiltonians, the energy eigenstates will be given by the products of eigenstates of each of the separate sub-Hamiltonians. The corresponding [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_an_operator) is then given by the sum of the individual eigenvalues of the sub-Hamiltonians.[^o23]

As with the quantum harmonic oscillator, one can show that *b<sub>k</sub>*<sup>†</sup> and *b<sub>k</sub>* respectively create and destroy a single field excitation, a phonon, with an energy of *ħω<sub>k</sub>*.[^o23][^o3]

Three important properties of phonons may be deduced from this technique. First, phonons are [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson), since any number of identical excitations can be created by repeated application of the creation operator *b<sub>k</sub>*<sup>†</sup>. Second, each phonon is a "collective mode" caused by the motion of every atom in the lattice. This may be seen from the fact that the creation and annihilation operators, defined here in momentum space, contain sums over the position and momentum operators of every atom when written in position space. (See [position and momentum space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_and_momentum_space).)[^o23] Finally, using the *position–position [correlation function](wikipedia://en/59500109)*, it can be shown that phonons act as waves of lattice displacement.

This technique is readily generalized to three dimensions, where the Hamiltonian takes the form:[^o23][^o3]

$$\mathcal{H} = \sum_k \sum_{s = 1}^3 \hbar \, \omega_{k,s} \left( {b_{k,s}}^\dagger b_{k,s} + \tfrac12 \right).$$

This can be interpreted as the sum of 3N independent oscillator Hamiltonians, one for each wave vector and polarization.[^o23]

## Acoustic and optical phonons {#Acoustic_and_optical_phonons}

Solids with more than one atom in the smallest [unit cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_cell) exhibit two types of phonons: acoustic phonons and optical phonons.

**Acoustic phonons** are coherent movements of atoms of the lattice out of their equilibrium positions. If the displacement is in the direction of propagation, then in some areas the atoms will be closer, in others farther apart, as in a sound wave in air (hence the name acoustic). Displacement perpendicular to the propagation direction is comparable to waves on a string. If the wavelength of acoustic phonons goes to infinity, this corresponds to a simple displacement of the whole crystal, and this costs zero deformation energy. Acoustic phonons exhibit a linear relationship between frequency and phonon wave-vector for long wavelengths. The frequencies of acoustic phonons tend to zero with longer wavelength. Longitudinal and transverse acoustic phonons are often abbreviated as LA and TA phonons, respectively.

**Optical phonons** are out-of-phase movements of the atoms in the lattice, one atom moving to the left, and its neighbor to the right. This occurs if the lattice basis consists of two or more atoms. They are called *optical* because in ionic crystals, such as [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride), fluctuations in displacement create an electrical polarization that couples to the electromagnetic field.[^o3] Hence, they can be excited by [infrared radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_radiation): the electric field of the light will move every positive sodium ion in the direction of the field, and every negative chloride ion in the other direction, causing the crystal to vibrate.

Optical phonons have a non-zero frequency at the [Brillouin zone](wikipedia://en/562067) center and show no dispersion near that long wavelength limit. This is because they correspond to a mode of vibration where positive and negative ions at adjacent lattice sites swing against each other, creating a time-varying [electrical dipole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_dipole_moment). Optical phonons that interact in this way with light are called *infrared active*. Optical phonons that are *Raman active* can also interact indirectly with light, through [Raman scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raman_scattering). Optical phonons are often abbreviated as LO and TO phonons, for the longitudinal and transverse modes respectively; the splitting between LO and TO frequencies is often described accurately by the [Lyddane–Sachs–Teller relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyddane–Sachs–Teller_relation).

When measuring optical phonon energy experimentally, optical phonon frequencies are sometimes given in spectroscopic [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber) notation, where the symbol *ω* represents ordinary frequency (not angular frequency), and is expressed in units of [cm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre)<sup>−1</sup>. The value is obtained by dividing the frequency by the [speed of light in vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light_in_vacuum). In other words, the wave-number in cm<sup>−1</sup> units corresponds to the inverse of the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) of a [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) in vacuum that has the same frequency as the measured phonon.[^o26]

## Crystal momentum {#Crystal_momentum}

*Image: phonon k 3k.gif.* k-vectors exceeding the first [Brillouin zone](wikipedia://en/562067) (red) do not carry any more information than their counterparts (black) in the first Brillouin zone.

By analogy to [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) and [matter waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie_wavelength), phonons have been treated with [wavevector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavevector) *k* as though it has a [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) *ħk*;[^o28] however, this is not strictly correct, because *ħk* is not actually a physical momentum; it is called the *crystal momentum* or *pseudomomentum*. This is because *k* is only determined up to addition of constant vectors (the [reciprocal lattice vectors](wikipedia://en/664488) and integer multiples thereof). For example, in the one-dimensional model, the normal coordinates *Q* and *Π* are defined so that

$$Q_k \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} Q_{k+K} ;\quad \Pi_k \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \Pi_{k + K}$$

where

$$K = \frac{2n\pi}{a}$$

for any integer *n*. A phonon with wavenumber *k* is thus equivalent to an infinite family of phonons with wavenumbers *k* ± $\tfrac{2\pi}{a}$, *k* ± $\tfrac{4\pi}{a}$, and so forth. Physically, the reciprocal lattice vectors act as additional chunks of momentum which the lattice can impart to the phonon. [Bloch electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch_electron) obey a similar set of restrictions.

*Image: Brillouin zone.svg.* Brillouin zones, (a) in a square lattice, and (b) in a hexagonal lattice

It is usually convenient to consider phonon wavevectors *k* which have the smallest magnitude |*k*| in their "family". The set of all such wavevectors defines the *first [Brillouin zone](wikipedia://en/562067)*. Additional Brillouin zones may be defined as copies of the first zone, shifted by some reciprocal lattice vector.

## Thermodynamics {#Thermodynamics}

The [thermodynamic](wikipedia://en/29952) properties of a solid are directly related to its phonon structure. The entire set of all possible phonons that are described by the phonon dispersion relations combine in what is known as the phonon [density of states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_states) which determines the [heat capacity](wikipedia://en/194227) of a crystal. By the nature of this distribution, the heat capacity is dominated by the high-frequency part of the distribution, while thermal conductivity is primarily the result of the low-frequency region.

At [absolute zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_zero) temperature, a crystal lattice lies in its [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state), and contains no phonons. A lattice at a nonzero [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) has an energy that is not constant, but fluctuates [randomly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) about some [mean value](wikipedia://en/612). These energy fluctuations are caused by random lattice vibrations, which can be viewed as a gas of phonons. Because these phonons are generated by the temperature of the lattice, they are sometimes designated thermal phonons.[^o30]

Thermal phonons can be created and destroyed by random energy fluctuations. In the language of statistical mechanics this means that the chemical potential for adding a phonon is zero.[^o30] This behavior is an extension of the harmonic potential into the anharmonic regime. The behavior of thermal phonons is similar to the [photon gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_gas) produced by an [electromagnetic cavity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_cavity), wherein photons may be emitted or absorbed by the cavity walls. This similarity is not coincidental, for it turns out that the electromagnetic field behaves like a set of harmonic oscillators, giving rise to [black-body radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-body_radiation). Both gases obey the [Bose–Einstein statistics](wikipedia://en/188935): in thermal equilibrium and within the harmonic regime, the probability of finding phonons or photons in a given state with a given angular frequency is:[^o31]

$$n\left(\omega_{k,s}\right) = \frac{1}{\exp\left(\dfrac{\hbar\omega_{k,s}}{k_\mathrm{B}T}\right) - 1}$$

where *ω*<sub>*k*,*s*</sub> is the frequency of the phonons (or photons) in the state, *k*<sub>B</sub> is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant), and *T* is the temperature.

::: {#o33 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Phonon tunneling"}

### Phonon tunneling {#Phonon_tunneling}

Phonons have been shown to exhibit [quantum tunneling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunneling) behavior (or *phonon tunneling*) where, across gaps up to a nanometer wide, heat can flow via phonons that "tunnel" between two materials.[^o34] This type of heat transfer works between distances too large for [conduction](wikipedia://en/72536) to occur but too small for [radiation](wikipedia://en/185239) to occur and therefore cannot be explained by classical [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726) models.[^o34]

## Operator formalism {#Operator_formalism}

The phonon Hamiltonian is given by

$$\mathcal{H} = \tfrac12 \sum_\alpha\left(p_\alpha^2 + \omega^2_\alpha q_\alpha^2 - \hbar\omega_\alpha\right)$$

In terms of the [creation and annihilation operators](wikipedia://en/701991), these are given by

$$\mathcal{H} = \sum_\alpha\hbar\omega_\alpha {a_\alpha}^\dagger a_\alpha$$

Here, in expressing the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) in operator formalism, we have not taken into account the $\tfrac{1}{2}$*ħω<sub>q</sub>* term as, given a [continuum](wikipedia://en/17368856) or [infinite lattice](wikipedia://en/661808), the $\tfrac{1}{2}$*ħω<sub>q</sub>* terms will add up yielding an [infinite term](wikipedia://en/54240). Because the difference in energy is what we measure and not the absolute value of it, the constant term $\tfrac{1}{2}$*ħω<sub>q</sub>* can be ignored without changing the equations of motion. Hence, the $\tfrac{1}{2}$*ħω<sub>q</sub>* factor is absent in the operator formalized expression for the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381).

The ground state, also called the "[vacuum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_state)", is the state composed of no phonons. Hence, the energy of the ground state is 0. When a system is in the state *n*<sub>1</sub>*n*<sub>2</sub>*n*<sub>3</sub>…, we say there are *n<sub>α</sub>* phonons of type *α*, where *n<sub>α</sub>* is the occupation number of the phonons. The energy of a single phonon of type *α* is given by *ħω<sub>q</sub>* and the total energy of a general phonon system is given by *n*<sub>1</sub>*ħω*<sub>1</sub> + *n*<sub>2</sub>*ħω*<sub>2</sub> +.... As there are no cross terms (e.g. *n*<sub>1</sub>*ħω*<sub>2</sub>), the phonons are said to be non-interacting. The action of the creation and annihilation operators is given by:

$${a_\alpha}^\dagger\Big|n_1\ldots n_{\alpha -1}n_\alpha n_{\alpha +1}\ldots\Big\rangle = \sqrt{n_\alpha +1}\Big|n_1\ldots,n_{\alpha -1}, (n_\alpha+1), n_{\alpha+1}\ldots\Big\rangle$$

and,

$$a_\alpha\Big|n_1\ldots n_{\alpha -1}n_\alpha n_{\alpha +1}\ldots\Big\rangle = \sqrt{n_\alpha}\Big|n_1\ldots,n_{\alpha -1},(n_\alpha-1),n_{\alpha+1},\ldots\Big\rangle$$

The creation operator, *a<sub>α</sub>*<sup>†</sup> creates a phonon of type *α* while *a<sub>α</sub>* annihilates one. Hence, they are respectively the creation and annihilation operators for phonons. Analogous to the [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719) case, we can define [particle number operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_number_operator) as

$$N = \sum_\alpha {a_\alpha}^\dagger a_\alpha.$$

The number operator commutes with a string of products of the creation and annihilation operators if and only if the number of creation operators is equal to number of annihilation operators.

It can be shown that phonons are symmetric under exchange (i.e. *α*,*β* = *β*,*α*), so therefore they are considered [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosons).[^o36]

## Nonlinearity {#Nonlinearity}

As well as [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photons), phonons can interact via [parametric down conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_down_conversion)[^o38] and form [squeezed coherent states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squeezed_coherent_state).[^o39]

## Predicted properties {#Predicted_properties}

Recent research has shown that phonons and [rotons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roton) may have a non-negligible mass and be affected by gravity just as standard particles are.[^o41] In particular, phonons are predicted to have a kind of [negative mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_mass) and negative gravity.[^o42] This can be explained by how phonons are known to travel faster in denser materials. Because the part of a material pointing towards a gravitational source is closer to the object, it becomes denser on that end. From this, it is predicted that phonons would deflect away as it detects the difference in densities, exhibiting the qualities of a negative gravitational field.[^o43] Although the effect would be too small to measure, it is possible that future equipment could lead to successful results.

## Superconductivity {#Superconductivity}

[Superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity) is a state of electronic matter in which [electrical resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance_and_conductance) vanishes and [magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/36563) are expelled from the material. In a superconductor, electrons are bound together into [Cooper pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooper_pair) by a weak attractive force. In a conventional superconductor, this attraction is known as [Bardeen–Pines interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardeen–Pines_interaction) and it is caused by an [exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_carrier) of phonons between the electrons.[^o45] The evidence that phonons, the vibrations of the ionic lattice, are relevant for superconductivity is provided by the [isotope effect](wikipedia://en/4403#Underlying_evidence), the dependence of the superconducting critical temperature on the mass of the ions.

## Other research {#Other_research}

In 2019, researchers were able to isolate individual phonons without destroying them for the first time.[^o47]

It was also shown that phonon flow is responsible for nanoscale electrical current generation when a liquid is flowing above a graphene surface.[^o48]
