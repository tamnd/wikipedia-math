# Spheroid

*Image: Spheroids.svg.* These spheroids are obtained by revolving an ellipse about a vertical axis. The one on the left is **oblate** and the one on the right is **prolate**.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spheroid"}

A **spheroid**, also known as an **ellipsoid of revolution** or **rotational ellipsoid**, is a [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570) [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) obtained by [rotating](wikipedia://en/329549) an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) about one of its principal axes; in other words, an [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381) with two equal [semi-diameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-diameter). A spheroid has [circular symmetry](wikipedia://en/2217599).

:::

If the ellipse is rotated about its [major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_axis), the result is a **prolate spheroid**, elongated like a [rugby ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_ball). The [American football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_%28gridiron_football%29) is similar but has a pointier end than a spheroid could. If the ellipse is rotated about its [minor axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_axis), the result is an **oblate spheroid**, flattened like a [lentil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentil) or a plain [M&M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M&M's). If the generating ellipse is a circle, the result is a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859).

Due to the combined effects of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) and [rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_of_the_Earth), the [figure of the Earth](wikipedia://en/233668) (and of all [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet)) is not quite a sphere, but instead is slightly [flattened](wikipedia://en/2043875) in the direction of its axis of rotation. For that reason, in [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography) and [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) the Earth is often approximated by an oblate spheroid, known as the [reference ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid), instead of a sphere. The current [World Geodetic System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System) model uses a spheroid whose radius is 6,378.137 at the [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) and 6,356.752 at the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_pole).

The word *spheroid* originally meant "an approximately spherical body", admitting irregularities even beyond the bi- or tri-axial ellipsoidal shape; that is how the term is used in some older papers on geodesy (for example, referring to truncated spherical harmonic expansions of the [Earth's gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity) [geopotential model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geopotential_model)).[^o2]

## Equation {#Equation}

*Image: ellipsoid-rot-ax.svg.* The assignment of semi-axes on a spheroid. It is oblate if $c < a$ (left) and prolate if $c > a$ (right).

The equation of a tri-axial ellipsoid centred at the origin with semi-axes $a$, $b$ and $c$ aligned along the coordinate axes is

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2} = 1.$$

The equation of a spheroid with $z$ as the [symmetry axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_axis) is given by setting $a = b$:

$$\frac{x^2+y^2}{a^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2}=1.$$

The semi-axis $a$ is the equatorial radius of the spheroid, and $c$ is the distance from centre to pole along the symmetry axis. There are two possible cases:

- $c < a$: oblate spheroid
- $c > a$: prolate spheroid

The case of $a = c$ reduces to a sphere.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

### Circumference {#Circumference}

The equatorial circumference of a spheroid is measured around its [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) and is given as:

$$C_\text{e} = 2\pi a$$

The meridional or polar circumference of a spheroid is measured through its [poles](wikipedia://en/23409699) and is given as:

$$C_\text{p} \,=\, 4a\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt {1 - e^2 \sin^2\theta}\ d\theta$$

The volumetric circumference of a spheroid is the circumference of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) of equal volume as the spheroid and is given as:

$$C_\text{v} = 2\sqrt[3]{a^2c}$$

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Spheroidal area"}

### Area {#Area}

An oblate spheroid with $c < a$ has [surface area](wikipedia://en/27725)

$$S_\text{o} = 2\pi a^2\left(1+\frac{1-e_o^2}{e_o}\operatorname{arctanh}e_o\right)=2\pi a^2+\pi \frac{c^2}{e_o}\ln \left( \frac{1+e_o}{1-e_o}\right)$$

where $e_o^2=1-\frac{c^2}{a^2}$. A prolate spheroid with $c > a$ has surface area

$$S_\text{p} = 2\pi a^2\left(1+\frac{c}{a e_p}\arcsin \, e_p\right)$$

where $e_p^2=1-\frac{a^2}{c^2}.$ In both cases, $e_{o}$ and $e_{p}$ may be identified as the [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472) (see [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277)).[^o9][^o10]

These formulas are identical in the sense that the formula for $S_{o}$ can be used to calculate the surface area of a prolate spheroid and vice versa. However, $e_{o}$ then becomes [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) and can no longer directly be identified with the eccentricity. Both of these results may be cast into many other forms using standard mathematical identities and relations between parameters of the ellipse.

:::

### Volume {#Volume}

The volume inside a spheroid (of any kind) is

$$V=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi a^2c\approx4.19a^2c.$$

If $A = 2a$ is the equatorial diameter, and $C = 2c$ is the polar diameter, the volume is

$$V=\tfrac{\pi}{6}A^2C\approx0.523A^2C.$$

### Curvature {#Curvature}

Let a spheroid be parameterized as

$$\boldsymbol\sigma (\beta,\lambda) = (a \cos \beta \cos \lambda, a \cos \beta \sin \lambda, c \sin \beta),$$

where $\beta$ is the *reduced latitude* or *[parametric latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_latitude)* and $\lambda$ is the [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude), with domain $-\tfrac{\pi}{2} < \beta < +\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ and $-\pi < \lambda < +\pi$, respectively. Then, the spheroid's [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is:

$$K(\beta) = \frac{c^2}{\left(a^2 + \left(c^2 - a^2\right) \cos^2 \beta\right)^2},$$

and its [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) is

$$H(\beta) = \frac{c \left(2a^2 + \left(c^2 - a^2\right) \cos^2 \beta\right)}{2a \left(a^2 + \left(c^2 - a^2\right) \cos^2\beta\right)^\frac32}.$$

Both of these curvatures are a function of latitude only and are always positive, so that every point on a spheroid is elliptic.

### Aspect ratio {#Aspect_ratio}

The *[aspect ratio](wikipedia://en/1021)* of an oblate spheroid/ellipse, $c : a$, is the ratio of the polar to equatorial lengths, while the *[flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875)* (also called *oblateness*) $f$, is the ratio of the equatorial-polar length difference to the equatorial length:

$$f = \frac{a - c}{a} = 1 - \frac{c}{a} .$$

The first [*eccentricity*](wikipedia://en/1239472#Ellipses) (usually simply eccentricity, as above) is often used instead of flattening.[^o14] It is defined by:

$$e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{c^2}{a^2}}$$

The relations between eccentricity and flattening are:

$$\begin{aligned} e &= \sqrt{2f - f^2} \\ f &= 1 - \sqrt{1 - e^2} \end{aligned}$$

All modern geodetic ellipsoids are defined by the semi-major axis plus either the semi-minor axis (giving the aspect ratio), the flattening, or the first eccentricity. While these definitions are mathematically interchangeable, real-world calculations must lose some precision. To avoid confusion, an ellipsoidal definition considers its own values to be exact in the form it gives.

:::

## Occurrence and applications {#Occurrence_and_applications}

The most common shapes for the density distribution of protons and neutrons in an [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) are [spherical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical), prolate, and oblate spheroidal, where the polar axis is assumed to be the spin axis (or direction of the spin [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) vector). Deformed nuclear shapes occur as a result of the competition between [electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_force) repulsion between protons, [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) and [quantum](wikipedia://en/25202) [shell effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_shell_model).

Spheroids are common in [3D cell cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_cell_culture). Rotating equilibrium spheroids include the [Maclaurin spheroid](wikipedia://en/3826733) and the [Jacobi ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/34439226). [Spheroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spheroid_%28lithic%29) is also a shape of archaeological artifacts.

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Oblate sphere"}

### Oblate spheroids {#Oblate_spheroids}

*Image: Jupiter\_OPAL\_2024.png.* The planet [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter), a slight oblate spheroid with a [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875) of 0.06487

The oblate spheroid is the approximate shape of rotating [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) and other [celestial bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object), including Earth, [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn), [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter), and the quickly spinning star [Altair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altair). Saturn is the most oblate planet in the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), with a [flattening](wikipedia://en/2043875) of 0.09796.[^o18] See [planetary flattening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_flattening) and [equatorial bulge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_bulge) for details.

[Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment) scientist [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), working from [Jean Richer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Richer)'s pendulum experiments and [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127)'s theories for their interpretation, reasoned that Jupiter and [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228) are oblate spheroids owing to their [centrifugal force](wikipedia://en/19265670).[^o19][^o20][^o21] Earth's diverse cartographic and geodetic systems are based on [reference ellipsoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid), all of which are oblate.

:::

### Prolate spheroids {#Prolate_spheroids}

*Image: Gilbert\_rugby\_ball\_on\_grass.jpg.* A [rugby ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_ball)

The prolate spheroid is the approximate shape of the ball used in [American football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_%28gridiron_football%29) and in [rugby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_ball).

Several [moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moons) of the Solar System approximate prolate spheroids in shape, though they are closer to [triaxial ellipsoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triaxial_ellipsoid). Examples are [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn)'s satellites [Mimas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimas_%28moon%29), [Enceladus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enceladus_%28moon%29), and [Tethys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tethys_%28moon%29) and [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus)'s satellite [Miranda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miranda_%28moon%29).

In contrast to being distorted into oblate spheroids via rapid rotation, celestial objects distort slightly into prolate spheroids via [tidal forces](wikipedia://en/30718) when they orbit a massive body in a close orbit. The most extreme example is Jupiter's moon [Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_%28moon%29), which becomes slightly more or less prolate in its orbit due to a slight eccentricity, causing intense [volcanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism). The major axis of the prolate spheroid does not run through the satellite's poles in this case, but through the two points on its equator directly facing toward and away from the primary. This combines with the smaller oblate distortion from the synchronous rotation to cause the body to become triaxial.

The term is also used to describe the shape of some [nebulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula) such as the [Crab Nebula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crab_Nebula).[^o24] [Fresnel zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresnel_zone), used to analyze wave propagation and interference in space, are a series of concentric prolate spheroids with principal axes aligned along the direct line-of-sight between a transmitter and a receiver.

The [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) of the [actinide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinide) and [lanthanide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanthanide) elements are shaped like prolate spheroids.[^o25] In anatomy, near-spheroid organs such as [testis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testicle) may be measured by their [long and short axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomical_terms_of_location#Axes).[^o26]

Many submarines have a shape which can be described as prolate spheroid.[^o27]

:::

### Dynamical properties {#Dynamical_properties}

For a spheroid having uniform density, the [moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700) is that of an ellipsoid with an additional axis of symmetry. Given a description of a spheroid as having a [major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_axis) $c$, and minor axes $a = b$, the moments of inertia along these principal axes are $C$, $A$, and $B$. However, in a spheroid the minor axes are symmetrical. Therefore, our inertial terms along the major axes are:[^o29]

$$\begin{aligned} A = B &= \tfrac15 M\left(a^2+c^2\right), \\ C &= \tfrac15 M\left(a^2+b^2\right) =\tfrac25 M\left(a^2\right), \end{aligned}$$

where $M$ is the mass of the body defined as

$$M = \tfrac43 \pi a^2 c\rho.$$
