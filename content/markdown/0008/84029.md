# Euclidean planes in three-dimensional space

*Image: Plane equation qtl3.svg.* Plane equation in normal form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euclidean planes in three-dimensional space"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), a **plane** is a [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_space) two-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_%28geometry%29) that extends indefinitely. [Euclidean planes](wikipedia://en/15575410) often arise as [subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_subspace) of [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) $\mathbb{R}^3$. A prototypical example is one of a room's walls, infinitely extended and assumed infinitesimally thin. While a pair of real numbers $\mathbb{R}^2$ suffices to describe points on a plane, the relationship with out-of-plane points requires special consideration for their [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) in the [ambient space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_space) $\mathbb{R}^3$.

:::

## Derived concepts {#Derived_concepts}

A **plane segment** or **planar region** (or simply "plane", in lay use) is a planar surface [region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_%28mathematics%29); it is analogous to a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860). A *[bivector](wikipedia://en/884040)* is an [oriented](wikipedia://en/2544098) plane segment, analogous to [directed line segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_line_segment). A *[face](wikipedia://en/149926)* is a plane segment bounding a [solid object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_object).[^o3] A *[slab](wikipedia://en/70309041)* is a region bounded by two parallel planes. A *[parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975)* is a region bounded by three pairs of parallel planes.

## Background {#Background}

[Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) set forth the first great landmark of mathematical thought, an axiomatic treatment of geometry.[^o5] He selected a small core of undefined terms (called *common notions*) and postulates (or [axioms](wikipedia://en/928)) which he then used to prove various geometrical statements. Although the plane in its modern sense is not directly given a definition anywhere in the *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, it may be thought of as part of the common notions.[^o6] Euclid never used numbers to measure length, angle, or area. The Euclidean plane equipped with a chosen [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) is called a *Cartesian plane*; a non-Cartesian Euclidean plane equipped with a [polar coordinate system](wikipedia://en/25120) would be called a *polar plane*.

A plane is a [ruled surface](wikipedia://en/705158).

## Representation {#Representation}

This section is solely concerned with planes embedded in three dimensions: specifically, in [$\mathbf{R}^{3}$](wikipedia://en/24104095).

### Determination by contained points and lines {#Determination_by_contained_points_and_lines}

In a Euclidean space of any number of dimensions, a plane is uniquely determined by any of the following:

- Three non-[collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear) points (points not on a single line).
- A line and a point not on that line.
- Two distinct but intersecting lines.
- Two distinct but [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) lines.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The following statements hold in three-dimensional Euclidean space but not in higher dimensions, though they have higher-dimensional analogues:

- Two distinct planes are either parallel or they intersect in a [line](wikipedia://en/946975).
- A line is either parallel to a plane, intersects it at a single point, or is contained in the plane.
- Two distinct lines [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the same plane must be parallel to each other.
- Two distinct planes perpendicular to the same line must be parallel to each other.

:::

### Point–normal form and general form of the equation of a plane {#Point–normal_form_and_general_form_of_the_equation_of_a_plane}

In a manner analogous to the way lines in a two-dimensional space are described using a point-slope form for their equations, planes in a three dimensional space have a natural description using a point in the plane and a vector orthogonal to it (the [normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector)) to indicate its "inclination".

Specifically, let $\boldsymbol{r}_{0}$ be the position vector of some point $P_{0} = (x_{0}, y_{0}, z_{0})$, and let $\boldsymbol{n} = (a, b, c)$ be a nonzero vector. The plane determined by the point $P_{0}$ and the vector $\boldsymbol{n}$ consists of those points $P$, with position vector $\boldsymbol{r}$, such that the vector drawn from $P_{0}$ to $P$ is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol{n}$. Recalling that two vectors are perpendicular if and only if their dot product is zero, it follows that the desired plane can be described as the set of all points $\boldsymbol{r}$ such that

$$\boldsymbol{n} \cdot (\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_0)=0.$$

The dot here means a [dot (scalar) product](wikipedia://en/157093). Expanded this becomes

$$a (x-x_0) + b(y-y_0) + c(z-z_0) = 0,$$

which is the *point–normal* form of the equation of a plane.[^o15] This is just a [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570)

$$ax + by + cz + d = 0,$$

where

$$d = -(ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0),$$

which is the expanded form of $- \boldsymbol{n} \cdot \boldsymbol{r}_0.$

In mathematics it is a common convention to express the normal as a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053), but the above argument holds for a normal vector of any non-zero length.

Conversely, it is easily shown that if $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$ are constants and $a$, $b$, and $c$ are not all zero, then the graph of the equation

$$ax + by + cz + d = 0,$$

is a plane having the vector $\boldsymbol{n} = (a, b, c)$ as a normal.[^o16] This familiar equation for a plane is called the *general form* of the equation of the plane or just the *plane equation*.[^o17]

Thus for example a [regression equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_equation) of the form $y = d + ax + cz$ (with $b = -1$) establishes a best-fit plane in three-dimensional space when there are two explanatory variables.

:::

### Describing a plane with a point and two vectors lying on it {#Describing_a_plane_with_a_point_and_two_vectors_lying_on_it}

Alternatively, a plane may be described parametrically as the set of all points of the form

$$\boldsymbol{r} = \boldsymbol{r}_0 + s \boldsymbol{v} + t \boldsymbol{w},$$

*Image: PlaneR.jpg.* Vector description of a plane

where $s$ and $t$ range over all real numbers, $\boldsymbol{v}$ and $\boldsymbol{w}$ are given [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863) [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) defining the plane, and $\boldsymbol{r}_{0}$ is the vector representing the position of an arbitrary (but fixed) point on the plane. The vectors $\boldsymbol{v}$ and $\boldsymbol{w}$ can be visualized as vectors starting at $\boldsymbol{r}_{0}$ and pointing in different [directions](wikipedia://en/54516626) along the plane. The vectors $\boldsymbol{v}$ and $\boldsymbol{w}$ can be [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944), but cannot be parallel.

### Describing a plane through three points {#Describing_a_plane_through_three_points}

Let $\boldsymbol{p}_{1} = (x_{1}, y_{1}, z_{1})$, $\boldsymbol{p}_{2} = (x_{2}, y_{2}, z_{2})$, and $\boldsymbol{p}_{3} = (x_{3}, y_{3}, z_{3})$ be non-collinear points.

#### Method 1 {#Method_1}

The plane passing through $\boldsymbol{p}_{1}$, $\boldsymbol{p}_{2}$, and $\boldsymbol{p}_{3}$ can be described as the set of all points (*x*,*y*,*z*) that satisfy the following [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) equations:

$$\begin{vmatrix} x - x_1 & y - y_1 & z - z_1 \\ x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ x_3 - x_1 & y_3 - y_1 & z_3 - z_1 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} x - x_1 & y - y_1 & z - z_1 \\ x - x_2 & y - y_2 & z - z_2 \\ x - x_3 & y - y_3 & z - z_3 \end{vmatrix} = 0.$$

#### Method 2 {#Method_2}

To describe the plane by an equation of the form $ax + by + cz + d = 0$, solve the following system of equations:

$$ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 + d = 0$$

$$ax_2 + by_2 + cz_2 + d = 0$$

$$ax_3 + by_3 + cz_3 + d = 0.$$

This system can be solved using [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008) and basic matrix manipulations. Let

$$D = \begin{vmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & z_1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & z_2 \\ x_3 & y_3 & z_3 \end{vmatrix}.$$

If $D$ is non-zero (so for planes not through the origin) the values for $a$, $b$ and $c$ can be calculated as follows:

$$a = \frac{-d}{D} \begin{vmatrix} 1 & y_1 & z_1 \\ 1 & y_2 & z_2 \\ 1 & y_3 & z_3 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$b = \frac{-d}{D} \begin{vmatrix} x_1 & 1 & z_1 \\ x_2 & 1 & z_2 \\ x_3 & 1 & z_3 \end{vmatrix}$$

$$c = \frac{-d}{D} \begin{vmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{vmatrix}.$$

These equations are parametric in *d*. Setting *d* equal to any non-zero number and substituting it into these equations will yield one solution set.

#### Method 3 {#Method_3}

This plane can also be described by the Euclidean planes in three-dimensional space prescription above. A suitable normal vector is given by the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092)

$$\boldsymbol n = ( \boldsymbol p_2 - \boldsymbol p_1 ) \times ( \boldsymbol p_3 - \boldsymbol p_1 ),$$

and the point $\boldsymbol{r}_{0}$ can be taken to be any of the given points $\boldsymbol{p}_{1}$, $\boldsymbol{p}_{2}$ or $\boldsymbol{p}_{3}$[^o23] (or any other point in the plane).

:::

## Occurrence in nature {#Occurrence_in_nature}

*Image: Plane wave wavefronts 3D.svg.* The [wavefronts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefront) of a plane wave traveling in [3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-space)

A plane serves as a mathematical model for many physical phenomena, such as [specular reflection](wikipedia://en/679297) in a [plane mirror](wikipedia://en/11439770) or [wavefronts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefront) in a [traveling plane wave](wikipedia://en/60466580). The [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) of undisturbed liquids tends to be nearly flat (see [flatness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatness_%28liquids%29)). The flattest surface ever manufactured is a quantum-stabilized atom mirror.[^o31] In astronomy, various *[reference planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_plane)* are used to define positions in orbit. *[Anatomical planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomical_plane)* may be lateral ("sagittal"), frontal ("coronal") or transversal. In geology, *[beds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bed_%28geology%29)* (layers of sediments) often are planar. Planes are involved in different forms of [imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaging), such as the *[focal plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focal_plane)*, *[picture plane](wikipedia://en/937408)*, and *[image plane](wikipedia://en/1669473)*.

*Image: Webysther 20211009102044 - Parque Geológico do Varvito.jpg.* Beds of sedimentary rock at Parque Geológico do Varvito, [Itu, São Paulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itu,_São_Paulo), Brazil

### Miller indices {#Miller_indices}

*Image: Miller Indices Cubes2.svg.* Planes with different Miller indices in cubic crystals

The attitude of a [lattice plane](wikipedia://en/15798971) is the orientation of the line normal to the plane,[^o33] and is described by the plane's [Miller indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_indices). In three-space a family of planes (a series of parallel planes) can be denoted by its [Miller indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller_indices) (*hkl*),[^o34][^o35] so the family of planes has an attitude common to all its constituent planes.

### Strike and dip {#Strike_and_dip}

*Image: StrikeLine&Dip.JPG.* Strike line and dip of a plane describing attitude relative to a horizontal plane and a vertical plane perpendicular to the strike line

Many features observed in geology are planes or lines, and their orientation is commonly referred to as their *attitude*. These attitudes are specified with two angles.

For a line, these angles are called the *trend* and the *plunge*. The trend is the compass direction of the line, and the plunge is the downward angle it makes with a horizontal plane.[^o37]

For a plane, the two angles are called its *strike (angle)* and its *dip (angle)*. A *strike line* is the intersection of a horizontal plane with the observed planar feature (and therefore a horizontal line), and the strike angle is the *bearing* of this line (that is, relative to [geographic north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_north) or from [magnetic north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_declination)). The dip is the angle between a horizontal plane and the observed planar feature as observed in a third vertical plane perpendicular to the strike line.
