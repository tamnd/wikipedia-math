# Safe and Sophie Germain primes

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Safe and Sophie Germain primes"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* is a **Sophie Germain prime** if 2*p* + 1 is also prime. The number 2*p* + 1 associated with a Sophie Germain prime is called a **safe prime**. For example, 11 is a Sophie Germain prime and 2 × 11 + 1 = 23 is its associated safe prime. Sophie Germain primes and safe primes have applications in [public key cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key_cryptography) and [primality testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality_testing). It has been [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that there are [infinitely](wikipedia://en/142679) many Sophie Germain primes, but this remains unproven.

:::

Sophie Germain primes are named after the French mathematician [Sophie Germain](wikipedia://en/27791), who used them in her investigations of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953).[^o2] One attempt by Germain to prove Fermat's Last Theorem was to let *p* be a prime number of the form 8*k* + 7 and to let *n* = *p* – 1. In this case, $x^n + y^n = z^n$ is unsolvable. Germain's proof, however, remained unfinished.[^o3][^o4] Through her attempts to solve Fermat's Last Theorem, she proved a result now known as [Sophie Germain's theorem](wikipedia://en/22788074), which states that if *p* is an odd prime and 2*p* + 1 is also prime, then *p* must divide *x*, *y*, or *z*. Otherwise, $x^n + y^n \neq z^n$. This case where *p* does not divide *x*, *y*, or *z* is called the first case. Sophie Germain's work was the most progress achieved on Fermat's last theorem at that time.[^o3] Later work by [Kummer](wikipedia://en/319117) and others always divided the problem into first and second cases.

## Properties {#Properties}

There is no special primality test for safe primes, the way there is for [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime) and [Mersenne primes](wikipedia://en/18908). However, [Pocklington's criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocklington_primality_test) can be used to prove the primality of 2*p* + 1 once one has proven the primality of *p*.

Just as every term except the last one of a [Cunningham chain](wikipedia://en/321790) of the first kind is a Sophie Germain prime, so every term except the first of such a chain is a safe prime. Safe primes ending in 7, that is, of the form 10*n* + 7, are the last terms in such chains when they occur, since 2(10*n* + 7) + 1 = 20*n* + 15 is [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisibility) by 5.

For a safe prime, every [quadratic nonresidue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_nonresidue), except −1 (if nonresidue), is a [primitive root](wikipedia://en/186864). It follows that for a safe prime, the least positive primitive root is a prime number.[^o20]

### Modular restrictions {#Modular_restrictions}

With the exception of 7, a safe prime *q* is of the form 6*k* − 1 or, equivalently, *q* ≡ 5 ([mod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_operation) 6) – as is *p* > 3. Similarly, with the exception of 5, a safe prime *q* is of the form 4*k* − 1 or, equivalently, *q* ≡ 3 (mod 4) — trivially true since (*q* − 1) / 2 must evaluate to an [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474). Combining both forms using [lcm](wikipedia://en/17961)(6, 4) we determine that a safe prime *q* > 7 also must be of the form 12*k* − 1 or, equivalently, *q* ≡ 11 (mod 12).

It follows that, for any safe prime *q* > 7:

- both 3 and 12 are [quadratic residues](wikipedia://en/200091) mod *q* (per [law of quadratic reciprocity](wikipedia://en/200091#Law_of_quadratic_reciprocity))
  - neither 3 nor 12 is a [primitive root](wikipedia://en/186864) of *q*
  - the only safe primes that are also [full reptend primes](wikipedia://en/2673943) in [base 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_12) are 5 and 7
  - *q* divides 3<sup>(*q*−1)/2</sup> − 1 and 12<sup>(*q*−1)/2</sup> − 1, same as 3<sup>(*q*−1)/2</sup> ≡ 1 mod *q* and 12<sup>(*q*−1)/2</sup> ≡ 1 mod *q* (per [Euler's criterion](wikipedia://en/105622))
- *q* − 3, *q* − 4, *q* − 9, *q* − 12 are quadratic nonresidues
  - *q* − 3, *q* − 4, *q* − 9, and, for *q* > 11, *q* − 12 are primitive roots

If *p* is a Sophie Germain prime greater than 3, then *p* must be congruent to 2 mod 3. For, if not, it would be congruent to 1 mod 3 and 2*p* + 1 would be congruent to 3 mod 3, impossible for a prime number.[^o22] Similar restrictions hold for larger prime moduli, and are the basis for the choice of the "correction factor" 2*C* in the Hardy–Littlewood estimate on the density of the Sophie Germain primes.[^o27]

If a Sophie Germain prime *p* is [congruent](wikipedia://en/48167) to 3 (mod 4) (, *Lucasian primes*), then its matching safe prime 2*p* + 1 ([congruent](wikipedia://en/20087) to 7 modulo 8) will be a divisor of the [Mersenne number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_number) 2<sup>*p*</sup> − 1. Historically, this result of [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) was the first known criterion for a Mersenne number with a prime index to be [composite](wikipedia://en/82289).[^o23] It can be used to generate the largest Mersenne numbers (with prime indices) that are known to be composite.[^o24]

## Infinitude and density {#Infinitude_and_density}

::: {#o26 .problem cue=unsolved}

Are there infinitely many Sophie Germain primes?

:::

It is [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that there are infinitely many Sophie Germain primes, but this has not been [proven](wikipedia://en/82285).[^o27] Several other famous conjectures in number theory generalize this and the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture); they include [Dickson's conjecture](wikipedia://en/31611712), [Schinzel's hypothesis H](wikipedia://en/604052), and the [Bateman–Horn conjecture](wikipedia://en/1085053).

A [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) estimate for the [number](wikipedia://en/21690) of Sophie Germain primes less than *n* is[^o27]

$$2C \frac{n}{(\ln n)^2} \approx 1.32032\frac{n}{(\ln n)^2}$$

where

$$C=\prod_{p>2} \frac{p(p-2)}{(p-1)^2}\approx 0.660161$$

is [Hardy–Littlewood's twin prime constant](wikipedia://en/41997#First_Hardy–Littlewood_conjecture). For *n* = 10<sup>4</sup>, this estimate predicts 156 Sophie Germain primes, which has a 20% error compared to the exact value of 190. For *n* = 10<sup>7</sup>, the estimate predicts 50822, which is still 10% off from the exact value of 56032. The form of this estimate is due to [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) and [J. E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._E._Littlewood), who applied a similar estimate to [twin primes](wikipedia://en/41997).[^o28]

A [sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) (*p*, 2*p* + 1, 2(2*p* + 1) + 1, ...) in which all of the numbers are prime is called a [Cunningham chain](wikipedia://en/321790) of the first kind. Every term of such a sequence except the last is a Sophie Germain prime, and every term except the first is a safe prime. Extending the conjecture that there exist infinitely many Sophie Germain primes, it has also been conjectured that arbitrarily long Cunningham chains exist,[^o29] although infinite chains are known to be impossible.[^o30]

## Strong primes {#Strong_primes}

A prime number *q* is a [strong prime](wikipedia://en/3843453) if *q* + 1 and *q* − 1 both have some large (around 500 digits) prime factors. For a safe prime *q* = 2*p* + 1, the number *q* − 1 naturally has a large prime factor, namely *p*, and so a safe prime *q* meets part of the criteria for being a strong prime. The running times of some methods of [factoring](wikipedia://en/15491) a number with *q* as a prime factor depend partly on the size of the prime factors of *q* − 1. This is true, for instance, of the [*p* − 1 method](wikipedia://en/578753).

## Applications {#Applications}

### Cryptography {#Cryptography}

Safe primes are also important in cryptography because of their use in [discrete logarithm](wikipedia://en/181334)-based techniques like [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903). If 2*p* + 1 is a safe prime, the multiplicative [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 2*p* + 1 has a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of large prime [order](wikipedia://en/493527). It is usually this prime-order subgroup that is desirable, and the reason for using safe primes is so that the modulus is as small as possible relative to *p*.

A prime number *p* = 2*q* + 1 is called a *safe prime* if *q* is prime. Thus, *p* = 2*q* + 1 is a safe prime if and only if *q* is a Sophie Germain prime, so finding safe primes and finding Sophie Germain primes are equivalent in computational difficulty. The notion of a safe prime can be strengthened to a strong prime, for which both *p* − 1 and *p* + 1 have large prime factors. Safe and strong primes were useful as the factors of secret keys in the [RSA cryptosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29), because they prevent the system being broken by some [factorization](wikipedia://en/15491) algorithms such as [Pollard's *p* − 1 algorithm](wikipedia://en/578753). However, with the current factorization technology, the advantage of using safe and strong primes appears to be negligible.[^o35]

Similar issues apply in other cryptosystems as well, including [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903) and similar systems that depend on the security of the [discrete logarithm problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_logarithm_problem) rather than on integer factorization.[^o36] For this reason, key generation protocols for these methods often rely on efficient algorithms for generating strong primes, which in turn rely on the conjecture that these primes have a sufficiently high density.[^o37]

In [Sophie Germain Counter Mode](wikipedia://en/33163568), it was proposed to use the arithmetic in the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) of order equal to the safe prime 2<sup>128</sup> + 12451, to counter weaknesses in [Galois/Counter Mode](wikipedia://en/5897139) using the binary finite field GF(2<sup>128</sup>). However, SGCM has been shown to be vulnerable to many of the same cryptographic attacks as GCM.[^o38]

### Primality testing {#Primality_testing}

In the first version of the [AKS primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AKS_primality_test) paper, a conjecture about Sophie Germain primes is used to lower the worst-case complexity from $O(\log^{12}n)$ to $O(\log^{6}n)$. A later version of the paper is shown to have time complexity $O(\log^{7.5}n)$ which can also be lowered to $O(\log^{6}n)$ using the conjecture.[^o40] Later variants of AKS have been proven to have complexity of $O(\log^{6}n)$ without any conjectures or use of Sophie Germain primes.

### Pseudorandom number generation {#Pseudorandom_number_generation}

Safe primes obeying certain congruences can be used to generate [pseudo-random numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-random_numbers) of use in [Monte Carlo simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_simulation).

Similarly, Sophie Germain primes may be used in the generation of [pseudo-random numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-random_numbers). The decimal expansion of 1/*q* will produce a [stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurring_decimal#Fractions_with_prime_denominators) of *q* − 1 pseudo-random digits, if *q* is the safe prime of a Sophie Germain prime *p*, with *p* [congruent](wikipedia://en/20087#Congruence) to 3, 9, or 11 modulo 20.[^o42] Thus "suitable" prime numbers *q* are 7, 23, 47, 59, 167, 179, etc. () (corresponding to *p* = 3, 11, 23, 29, 83, 89, etc.) (). The result is a stream of [length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_length) *q* − 1 digits (including leading zeros). So, for example, using *q* = 23 generates the pseudo-random digits 0, 4, 3, 4, 7, 8, 2, 6, 0, 8, 6, 9, 5, 6, 5, 2, 1, 7, 3, 9, 1, 3. Note that these digits are not appropriate for cryptographic purposes, as the value of each can be derived from its predecessor in the digit-stream.
