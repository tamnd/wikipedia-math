# Mathematical proof

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematical proof"}

*Image: P. Oxy. I 29.jpg.* [P. Oxy. 29](wikipedia://en/33044326), one of the oldest surviving fragments of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, a textbook used for millennia to teach proof-writing techniques. The diagram accompanies Book II, Proposition 5.[^o2]

A **mathematical proof** is a [deductive](wikipedia://en/61093) [argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument-deduction-proof_distinctions) for a [mathematical statement](wikipedia://en/81094), showing that the stated assumptions [logically](wikipedia://en/46426065) guarantee the conclusion. The argument may use other previously established statements, such as [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977); but every proof can, in principle, be constructed using only certain basic or original assumptions known as [axioms](wikipedia://en/928),[^o3][^o4][^o5] along with the accepted rules of [inference](wikipedia://en/317465). Proofs are examples of exhaustive [deductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/61093) that establish logical certainty, to be distinguished from [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence) arguments or non-exhaustive [inductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/393736) that establish "reasonable expectation". Presenting many cases in which the statement holds is not enough for a proof, which must demonstrate that the statement is true in *all* possible cases. A proposition that has not been proved but is believed to be true is known as a [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138), or a hypothesis if frequently used as an assumption for further mathematical work.[^o6]

:::

Proofs employ [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) expressed in mathematical symbols, along with [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173) that usually admits some ambiguity. In most mathematical literature, proofs are written in terms of [rigorous](wikipedia://en/655974#Mathematics) [informal logic](wikipedia://en/14122398). Purely [formal proofs](wikipedia://en/1197184), written fully in [symbolic language](wikipedia://en/60311953) without the involvement of natural language, are considered in [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478). The distinction between [formal and informal proofs](wikipedia://en/183478#Formal_and_informal_proof) has led to much examination of current and historical [mathematical practice](wikipedia://en/159730), [quasi-empiricism in mathematics](wikipedia://en/159731), and so-called [folk mathematics](wikipedia://en/168905), oral traditions in the mainstream mathematical community or in other cultures. The [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439) is concerned with the role of language and logic in proofs, and [mathematics as a language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_as_a_language).

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="History of mathematical proof"}

## History and etymology {#History_and_etymology}

The word *proof* derives from the Latin probare 'to test'; related words include English *probe*, *probation*, and *probability*, as well as Spanish probar 'to taste' (sometimes 'to touch' or 'to test'),[^o9] Italian it 'to try', and German probieren 'to try'. The legal term *probity* means authority or credibility, the power of testimony to prove facts when given by persons of reputation or status.[^o10]

Plausibility arguments using heuristic devices such as pictures and analogies preceded strict mathematical proof.[^o50] It is likely that the idea of demonstrating a conclusion first arose in connection with [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), which originated in practical problems of land measurement.[^o11] The development of mathematical proof is primarily the product of [ancient Greek mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics).[^o12] [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales) (624–546 BCE) and [Hippocrates of Chios](wikipedia://en/3632522) (c. 470–410 BCE) gave some of the first known proofs of theorems in geometry. [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388) (408–355 BCE) and [Theaetetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theaetetus_%28mathematician%29) (417–369 BCE) formulated theorems but did not prove them. [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) (384–322 BCE) said definitions should describe the concept being defined in terms of other concepts already known.

Mathematical proof was revolutionized by [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) (300 BCE), who introduced the [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method) still in use today. It starts with [undefined terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undefined_term) and [axioms](wikipedia://en/928), propositions concerning the undefined terms which are assumed to be self-evidently true (from Greek grc 'something worthy'). From this basis, the method proves theorems using [deductive logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_logic). *[Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)* was read by anyone who was considered educated in the West until the middle of the 20th century.[^o13] In addition to theorems of geometry, such as the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), the *Elements* also covers [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), including a proof that the [square root of two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root_of_two) is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689) and a proof that there are infinitely many [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666).

Further advances also took place in [medieval Islamic mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam). In the 10th century, the Iraqi mathematician [Al-Hashimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Ali_Muhammad_ibn_Abd_al-Aziz_al-Hashimi) worked with numbers as such, called "lines" but not necessarily considered as measurements of geometric objects, to prove algebraic propositions concerning multiplication, division, etc., including the existence of [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689).[^o14] An [inductive proof](wikipedia://en/18881) for [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389) was introduced in the *Al-Fakhri* (1000) by [Al-Karaji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Karaji), who used it to prove the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) and properties of [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497).

Modern [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478) treats proofs as inductively defined [data structures](wikipedia://en/8519), not requiring an assumption that axioms are "true" in any sense. This allows parallel mathematical theories as formal models of a given intuitive concept, based on alternate sets of axioms, for example [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory) and [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610).

:::

## Nature and purpose {#Nature_and_purpose}

As practiced, a proof is expressed in natural language and is a rigorous [argument](wikipedia://en/13018310) intended to convince the audience of the truth of a statement. The standard of rigor is not absolute and has varied throughout history. A proof can be presented differently depending on the intended audience. To gain acceptance, a proof has to meet communal standards of rigor; an argument considered vague or incomplete may be rejected.

The concept of proof is formalized in the field of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636).[^o16] A [formal proof](wikipedia://en/1197184) is written in a [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939) instead of natural language. A formal proof is a sequence of [formulas](wikipedia://en/404582) in a formal language, starting with an assumption, and with each subsequent formula a logical consequence of the preceding ones. This definition makes the concept of proof amenable to study. Indeed, the field of [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478) studies formal proofs and their properties, the most famous and surprising being that almost all axiomatic systems can generate certain [undecidable statements](wikipedia://en/1141208) not provable within the system.

The definition of a formal proof is intended to capture the concept of proofs as written in the practice of mathematics. The soundness of this definition amounts to the belief that a published proof can, in principle, be converted into a formal proof. However, outside the field of automated [proof assistants](wikipedia://en/1258607), this is rarely done in practice. A classic question in philosophy asks whether mathematical proofs are [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_proposition) or [synthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_proposition). [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant), who introduced the [analytic–synthetic distinction](wikipedia://en/6395956), believed mathematical proofs are synthetic, whereas [Quine](wikipedia://en/33833) argued in his 1951 "[Two Dogmas of Empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Dogmas_of_Empiricism)" that such a distinction is untenable.[^o17]

Proofs may be admired for their [mathematical beauty](wikipedia://en/18974136). The mathematician [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) was known for describing proofs which he found to be particularly elegant as coming from "The Book", a hypothetical tome containing the most beautiful method(s) of proving each theorem. The book *[Proofs from THE BOOK](wikipedia://en/4500115)*, published in 2003, is devoted to presenting 32 proofs its editors find particularly pleasing.

## Methods of proof {#Methods_of_proof}

### Direct proof {#Direct_proof}

In direct proof, the conclusion is established by logically combining the axioms, definitions, and earlier theorems.[^o20] For example, direct proof can be used to prove that the sum of two [even](wikipedia://en/143135) [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) is always even:

Consider two even integers *x* and *y*. Since they are even, they can be written as *x* = 2*a* and *y* = 2*b*, respectively, for some integers *a* and *b*. Then the sum is *x* + *y* = 2*a* + 2*b* = 2(*a* + *b*). Therefore *x* + *y* has 2 as a [factor](wikipedia://en/49492) and, by definition, is even. Hence, the sum of any two even integers is even.

This proof uses the definition of even integers, the integer properties of [closure](wikipedia://en/293802) under addition and multiplication, and the [distributive property](wikipedia://en/103118).

::: {#o22 .proof cue=heading name="Proof by mathematical induction"}

### Proof by mathematical induction {#Proof_by_mathematical_induction}

Despite its name, mathematical induction is a method of [deduction](wikipedia://en/61093), not a form of [inductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/393736). In proof by mathematical induction, a single "base case" is proved, and an "induction rule" is proved that establishes that any arbitrary case [implies](wikipedia://en/658808) the next case. Since in principle the induction rule can be applied repeatedly (starting from the proven base case), it follows that all (usually [infinitely](wikipedia://en/142679) many) cases are provable.[^o23] This avoids having to prove each case individually. A variant of mathematical induction is [proof by infinite descent](wikipedia://en/358069), which can be used, for example, to prove the [irrationality of the square root of two](wikipedia://en/341682#Proofs_of_irrationality).

A common application of proof by mathematical induction is to prove that a property known to hold for one number holds for all [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474):[^o24] Let $\mathbf{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, ...$} be the set of natural numbers, and let $P(n)$ be a mathematical statement involving the natural number $n$ belonging to $\mathbf{N}$ such that

- **(i)** $P(1)$ is true, i.e., $P(n)$ is true for $n = 1$.
- **(ii)** $P(n+1)$ is true whenever $P(n)$ is true, i.e., $P(n)$ is true implies that $P(n+1)$ is true.
- **Then $P(n)$ is true for all natural numbers $n$.**

For example, we can prove by induction that all positive integers of the form $2n - 1$ are [odd](wikipedia://en/143135). Let $P(n)$ represent "$2n - 1$ is odd":

**(i)** For $n = 1$, $2n - 1 = 2(1) - 1 = 1$, and $1$ is odd, since it leaves a remainder of $1$ when divided by $2$. Thus $P(1)$ is true.

**(ii)** For any $n$, if $2n - 1$ is odd ($P(n)$), then $(2n - 1) + 2$ must also be odd, because adding $2$ to an odd number results in an odd number. But $(2n - 1) + 2 = 2n + 1 = 2(n+1) - 1$, so $2(n+1) - 1$ is odd ($P(n+1)$). So $P(n)$ implies $P(n+1)$.

**Thus** $2n - 1$ is odd, for all positive integers $n$.

The shorter phrase "proof by induction" is often used instead of "proof by mathematical induction".[^o25]

:::

### Proof by contraposition {#Proof_by_contraposition}

[Proof by contraposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contrapositive) [infers](wikipedia://en/252311) the statement "if *p* then *q*" by establishing the [logically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logically_equivalent) [contrapositive statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrapositive): "if *not q* then *not p*".

For example, contraposition can be used to establish that, given an integer $x$, if $x^2$ is even, then $x$ is even:

Suppose $x$ is not even. Then $x$ is odd. The product of two odd numbers is odd, hence $x^2 = x\cdot x$ is odd. Thus $x^2$ is not even. Thus, if $x^2$ *is* even, the supposition must be false, so $x$ has to be even.

:::

::: {#o29 .proof cue=heading name="Proof by contradiction"}

### Proof by contradiction {#Proof_by_contradiction}

In proof by contradiction, also known by the Latin phrase *[reductio ad absurdum](wikipedia://en/22456146)* (by reduction to the absurd), it is shown that if some statement is assumed true, a [logical contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807) occurs, hence the statement must be false. A famous example involves the proof that $\sqrt{2}$ is an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689):

Suppose that $\sqrt{2}$ were a rational number. Then it could be written in lowest terms as $\sqrt{2} = {a\over b}$ where *a* and *b* are non-zero integers with [no common factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime). Thus, $b\sqrt{2} = a$. Squaring both sides yields 2*b*<sup>2</sup> = *a*<sup>2</sup>. Since the expression on the left is an integer multiple of 2, the right expression is by definition divisible by 2. That is, *a*<sup>2</sup> is even, which implies that *a* must also be even, as seen in the proposition above (in #Proof by contraposition). So we can write *a* = 2*c*, where *c* is also an integer. Substitution into the original equation yields 2*b*<sup>2</sup> = (2*c*)<sup>2</sup> = 4*c*<sup>2</sup>. Dividing both sides by 2 yields *b*<sup>2</sup> = 2*c*<sup>2</sup>. But then, by the same argument as before, 2 divides *b*<sup>2</sup>, so *b* must be even. However, if *a* and *b* are both even, they have 2 as a common factor. This contradicts our previous statement that *a* and *b* have no common factor, so we must conclude that $\sqrt{2}$ is an irrational number.

To paraphrase: if one could write $\sqrt{2}$ as a [fraction](wikipedia://en/1704824), this fraction could never be written in lowest terms, since 2 could always be factored from numerator and denominator.

:::

::: {#o31 .proof cue=heading name="Proof by construction"}

### Proof by construction {#Proof_by_construction}

Proof by construction, or proof by example, is the construction of a concrete example with a property to show that something having that property exists. [Joseph Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810), for instance, proved the existence of [transcendental numbers](wikipedia://en/30325) by constructing an [explicit example](wikipedia://en/50964). It can also be used to construct a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) to disprove a proposition that all elements have a certain property.

:::

::: {#o33 .proof cue=heading name="Proof by exhaustion"}

### Proof by exhaustion {#Proof_by_exhaustion}

In proof by exhaustion, the conclusion is established by dividing it into a finite number of cases and proving each one separately. The number of cases sometimes can become very large. For example, the first proof of the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949) was a proof by exhaustion with 1,936 cases. This proof was controversial because the majority of the cases were checked by a computer program, not by hand.[^o34]

:::

### Closed chain inference {#Closed_chain_inference}

A closed chain inference shows that a collection of statements are pairwise equivalent.

In order to prove that the statements $\varphi_1,\ldots,\varphi_n$ are each pairwise equivalent, proofs are given for the implications $\varphi_1\Rightarrow\varphi_2$, $\varphi_2\Rightarrow\varphi_3$, $\dots$, $\varphi_{n-1}\Rightarrow\varphi_n$ and $\varphi_{n}\Rightarrow\varphi_1$.[^o36][^o37]

The pairwise equivalence of the statements then results from the [transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463) of the [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808).

### Probabilistic proof {#Probabilistic_proof}

A probabilistic proof is one in which an example is shown to exist, with certainty, by using methods of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). Probabilistic proof, like proof by construction, is one of many ways to prove [existence theorems](wikipedia://en/251177).

In the probabilistic method, one seeks an object having a given property, starting with a large set of candidates. One assigns a certain probability for each candidate to be chosen, and then proves that there is a non-zero probability that a chosen candidate will have the desired property. This does not specify which candidates have the property, but the probability could not be positive without at least one.

A probabilistic proof is not to be confused with an argument that a theorem is 'probably' true, a 'plausibility argument'. The work toward the [Collatz conjecture](wikipedia://en/37895) shows how far plausibility is from genuine proof, as does the disproof of the [Mertens conjecture](wikipedia://en/379847). While most mathematicians do not think that probabilistic evidence for the properties of a given object counts as a genuine mathematical proof, a few mathematicians and philosophers have argued that at least some types of probabilistic evidence (such as Rabin's [probabilistic algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_algorithm) for [testing primality](wikipedia://en/183751)) are as good as genuine mathematical proofs.[^o39][^o40]

### Combinatorial proof {#Combinatorial_proof}

A combinatorial proof establishes the equivalence of different expressions by showing that they count the same object in different ways.[^o42] Often a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) is used to show that the expressions for their two sizes are equal. Alternatively, a [double counting argument](wikipedia://en/296942) provides two different expressions for the size of a single set, again showing that the two expressions are equal.

### Nonconstructive proof {#Nonconstructive_proof}

A nonconstructive proof establishes that a [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961) with a certain property exists—without explaining how such an object can be found.[^o44] Often, this takes the form of a proof by contradiction in which the nonexistence of the object is proved to be impossible. In contrast, a constructive proof establishes that a particular object exists by providing a method of finding it. The following famous example of a nonconstructive proof shows that there exist two [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689) *a* and *b* such that $a^b$ is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024). This proof uses that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational (an easy proof is known since [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)), but not that $\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}$ is irrational (this is true, but the proof is not elementary).

Either $\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}$ is a rational number and we are done (take $a=b=\sqrt{2}$), or $\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}$ is irrational so we can write $a=\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}$ and $b=\sqrt{2}$. This then gives $\left (\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}\right )^{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{2}^{2}=2$, which is thus a rational number of the form $a^b.$

### Statistical proofs in pure mathematics {#Statistical_proofs_in_pure_mathematics}

The expression "statistical proof" may be used technically or colloquially in areas of [pure mathematics](wikipedia://en/457210), such as involving [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), [chaotic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaotic_series), and [probabilistic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic) or [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513).[^o46][^o47][^o48] It is less commonly used to refer to a mathematical proof in the branch of mathematics known as [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711). See also the "Statistical proof using data" section below.

### Computer-assisted proofs {#Computer-assisted_proofs}

Until the twentieth century it was assumed that any proof could, in principle, be checked by a competent mathematician to confirm its validity.[^o50] However, [automated theorem provers](wikipedia://en/2546) and [proof assistants](wikipedia://en/1258607) are now used to prove theorems and carry out calculations that are too long for any human or team of humans to check; the first proof of the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949) is an example of a computer-assisted proof. Some mathematicians are concerned that the possibility of an error in a computer program or a run-time error in its calculations calls the validity of such computer-assisted proofs into question. In practice, the chances of an error invalidating a computer-assisted proof can be reduced by incorporating redundancy and self-checks into calculations, and by developing multiple independent approaches and programs. Errors can never be completely ruled out in case of verification of a proof by humans either, especially if the proof contains natural language and requires deep mathematical insight to uncover the potential hidden assumptions and fallacies involved.

## Undecidable statements {#Undecidable_statements}

A statement that is neither provable nor disprovable from a set of [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) is called undecidable (from those axioms). One example is the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493), which is neither provable nor refutable from the remaining axioms of [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417).

Mathematicians have shown there are many statements that are neither provable nor disprovable in [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel_set_theory_with_the_axiom_of_choice) (ZFC), the standard system of set theory in mathematics (assuming that ZFC is consistent); see [List of statements undecidable in ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_statements_undecidable_in_ZFC).

[Gödel's (first) incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_incompleteness_theorem) shows that many axiom systems of mathematical interest will have undecidable statements.

## Heuristic mathematics and experimental mathematics {#Heuristic_mathematics_and_experimental_mathematics}

While early mathematicians such as [Eudoxus of Cnidus](wikipedia://en/48388) did not use proofs, from [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) to the [foundational mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_mathematics) developments of the late 19th and 20th centuries, proofs were an essential part of mathematics.[^o53] With the increase in computing power in the 1960s, significant work began to be done investigating [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) beyond the proof-theorem framework,[^o54] in [experimental mathematics](wikipedia://en/256363). Early pioneers of these methods intended the work ultimately to be resolved into a classical proof-theorem framework, e.g. the early development of [fractal geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_geometry),[^o55] which was ultimately so resolved.

### Two-column proof {#Two-column_proof}

*Image: twocolumnproof.png.* A two-column proof published in 1913

A particular way of organizing a proof using two parallel columns is often used as a [mathematical exercise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_exercise) in elementary geometry classes in the United States.[^o62] The proof is written as a series of lines in two columns. In each line, the left-hand column contains a proposition, while the right-hand column contains a brief explanation of how the corresponding proposition in the left-hand column is either an axiom, a hypothesis, or can be logically derived from previous propositions. The left-hand column is typically headed "Statements" and the right-hand column is typically headed "Reasons".[^o63]

## Ending a proof {#Ending_a_proof}

Sometimes, the abbreviation *"Q.E.D."* is written to indicate the end of a proof. This abbreviation stands for *"quod erat demonstrandum"*, which is [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for *"that which was to be demonstrated"*. A more common alternative is to use a square or a rectangle, such as □ or ∎, known as a "[tombstone](wikipedia://en/819251)" or "Halmos" after its [eponym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eponym) [Paul Halmos](wikipedia://en/318598). Often, "which was to be shown" is verbally stated when writing "QED", "□", or "∎" during an oral presentation. Unicode explicitly provides the "end of proof" character, U+220E (∎) (220E(hex) = 8718(dec)).
