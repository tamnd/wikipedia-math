# Snake lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Snake lemma"}

The **snake lemma** is a tool used in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), to construct [long exact sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence). The snake lemma is valid in every [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) and is a crucial tool in homological algebra and its applications, for instance in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801). Homomorphisms constructed with its help are generally called *connecting homomorphisms*.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

In an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) (such as the category of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) or the category of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a given [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29)), consider a [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484):

*Image: Snake lemma origin.svg.*

where the rows are [exact sequences](wikipedia://en/138215) and 0 is the [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object).

Then there is an exact sequence relating the [kernels](wikipedia://en/142616) and [cokernels](wikipedia://en/279715) of *a*, *b*, and *c*:[^o4]*$\ker(f) ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \ker a ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \ker b ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \ker c ~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~ \operatorname{coker}a ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \operatorname{coker}b ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \operatorname{coker}c ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \operatorname{coker}g'$*where *d* is a homomorphism, known as the *connecting homomorphism*.

Furthermore, if the morphism *f* is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538), then so is the morphism $\ker a ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \ker b$, and if *g**is an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539), then so is $\operatorname{coker} b ~{\color{Gray}\longrightarrow}~ \operatorname{coker} c$.***

The cokernels here are: $\operatorname{coker}a = A'/\operatorname{im}a$, $\operatorname{coker}b = B'/\operatorname{im}b$, $\operatorname{coker}c = C'/\operatorname{im}c$.

:::

## Explanation of the name {#Explanation_of_the_name}

To see where the snake lemma gets its name, expand the diagram above as follows:

and then the exact sequence that is the conclusion of the lemma can be drawn on this expanded diagram in the reversed "S" shape of a slithering [snake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snake).

## Construction of the maps {#Construction_of_the_maps}

*Image: Snake lemma map construction.gif.* An animation of the construction of the map d

The maps between the kernels and the maps between the cokernels are induced in a natural manner by the given (horizontal) maps because of the diagram's commutativity. The exactness of the two induced sequences follows in a straightforward way from the exactness of the rows of the original diagram. The important statement of the lemma is that a *connecting homomorphism* $d$ exists which completes the exact sequence.

In the case of abelian groups or [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over some [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), the map $d$ can be constructed as follows:

Pick an element $x$ in $\operatorname{ker} c$ and view it as an element of $C$. Since $g$ is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), there exists $y$ in $B$ with $g(y)=x$. By commutativity of the diagram, we have $g'(b(y)) = c(g(y)) = c(x) = 0$ (since $x$ is in the kernel of $c$), and therefore $b(y)$ is in the kernel of $g'$. Since the bottom row is exact, we find an element $z$ in $A'$ with $f'(z)=b(y)$. By injectivity of $f'$, $z$ is unique. We then define $d(x)=z+\operatorname{im}(a)$. Now one has to check that $d$ is well-defined (i.e., $d(x)$ only depends on $x$ and not on the choice of $y$), that it is a homomorphism, and that the resulting long sequence is indeed exact. One may routinely verify the exactness by [diagram chasing](wikipedia://en/138484#Diagram_chasing) (see the proof of Lemma 9.1 in [^o7]).

Once that is done, the theorem is proven for abelian groups or modules over a ring. For the general case, the argument may be rephrased in terms of properties of arrows and cancellation instead of elements. Alternatively, one may invoke [Mitchell's embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/453755).

## Example {#Example}

Let $k$ be field, $V$ be a $k$-vector space. $V$ is $k[t]$-module by $t:V \to V$ being a $k$-linear transformation, so we can tensor $V$ and $k$ over $k[t]$.

$$V \otimes_{k[t]} k = V \otimes_{k[t]} (k[t]/(t)) = V/tV = \operatorname{coker}(t) .$$

Given a short exact sequence of $k$-vector spaces $0 \to M \to N \to P \to 0$, we can induce an exact sequence $M \otimes_{k[t]} k \to N \otimes_{k[t]} k \to P \otimes_{k[t]} k \to 0$ by right exactness of tensor product. But the sequence $0 \to M \otimes_{k[t]} k \to N \otimes_{k[t]} k \to P \otimes_{k[t]} k \to 0$ is not exact in general. Hence, a natural question arises. Why is this sequence not exact?

*Image: Snklem.png.*

According to the diagram above, we can induce an exact sequence $\ker(t_M) \to \ker(t_N) \to \ker(t_P) \to M \otimes_{k[t]} k \to N \otimes_{k[t]} k \to P \otimes_{k[t]} k \to 0$ by applying the snake lemma. Thus, the snake lemma reflects the tensor product's failure to be exact.

:::

## In the category of groups {#In_the_category_of_groups}

Whether the snake lemma holds in the category of groups depends on the definition of cokernel. If $f: A \to B$ is a homomorphism of groups, the universal property of the cokernel is satisfied by the natural map $B \to B / N(\operatorname{im} f)$, where $N(\operatorname{im} f)$ is the normalization of the image of $f$. The snake lemma fails with this definition of cokernel: The connecting homomorphism can still be defined, and one can write down a sequence as in the statement of the snake lemma. This will always be a chain complex, but it may fail to be exact.

If one simply replaces the cokernels in the statement of the snake lemma with the (right) cosets $A' / \operatorname{im} a, B' / \operatorname{im} b, C' / \operatorname{im} c'$, the lemma is still valid. The quotients however are not groups, but pointed sets (a short sequence $(X, x) \to (Y, y) \to (Z, z)$ of pointed sets with maps $f: X \to Y$ and $g: Y \to Z$ is called exact if $f(X) = g^{-1}(z)$).

::: {#o13 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=named-heading name="Counterexample to snake lemma with categorical cokernel"}

### Counterexample to snake lemma with categorical cokernel {#Counterexample_to_snake_lemma_with_categorical_cokernel}

Consider the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_5$: this contains a subgroup isomorphic to the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_3$, which in turn can be written as a semidirect product of [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327): $S_3\simeq C_3\rtimes C_2$.[^o14] This gives rise to the following diagram with exact rows:

$$\begin{matrix} & 1 & \to & C_3 & \to & C_3 & \to 1\\ & \downarrow && \downarrow && \downarrow \\ 1 \to & 1 & \to & S_3 & \to & A_5 \end{matrix}$$

Note that the middle column is not exact: $C_2$ is not a normal subgroup in the semidirect product.

Since $A_5$ is [simple](wikipedia://en/47600), the right vertical arrow has trivial cokernel. Meanwhile the quotient group $S_3/C_3$ is isomorphic to $C_2$. The sequence in the statement of the snake lemma is therefore

$$1 \longrightarrow 1 \longrightarrow 1 \longrightarrow 1 \longrightarrow C_2 \longrightarrow 1,$$

which indeed fails to be exact.

:::
