# Essential singularity

*Image: Essential singularity.png.* Plot of the function $\exp(1/z)$, centered on the essential singularity at $z = 0$. The hue represents the [complex argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arg_%28mathematics%29), the luminance represents the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991). This plot shows how approaching the essential singularity from different directions yields different behaviors (as opposed to a pole, which, approached from any direction, would be uniformly white).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Essential singularity"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), an **essential singularity** of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) is a "severe" [singularity](wikipedia://en/54240) near which the function exhibits striking behavior.

:::

The category *essential singularity* is a "left-over" or default group of [isolated singularities](wikipedia://en/334325) that are especially unmanageable: by definition they fit into neither of the other two categories of singularity that may be dealt with in some manner – [removable singularities](wikipedia://en/81644) and [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29). In practice some include non-isolated singularities too; those do not have a [residue](wikipedia://en/73102).

## Formal description {#Formal_description}

Consider an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) $U$ of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) $\mathbb{C}$. Let $a$ be an element of $U$, and $f: U\smallsetminus\{a\}\to \mathbb{C}$ a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110). The point $a$ is called an *essential singularity* of the function $f$ if the singularity is neither a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) nor a [removable singularity](wikipedia://en/81644).

For example, the function $f(z)=e^{1/z}$ has an essential singularity at $z=0$.

## Alternative descriptions {#Alternative_descriptions}

Let $a$ be a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826), and assume that $f(z)$ is not defined at $a$ but is [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) in some region $U$ of the complex plane, and that every [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $a$ has non-empty intersection with $U$.

- If both $\lim_{z \to a}f(z)$ and $\lim_{z \to a} {1}/{f(z)}$ exist, then $a$ is a *[removable singularity](wikipedia://en/81644)* of both $f$ and ${1}/{f}$.
- If $\lim_{z \to a}f(z)$ exists but $\lim_{z \to a} {1}/{f(z)}$ does not exist ($\lim_{z\to a} \left\vert {1}/{f(z)} \right\vert = \infty$), then $a$ is a [*zero*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29) of $f$ and a [*pole*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of ${1}/{f}$.
- If $\lim_{z \to a}f(z)$ does not exist (in fact $\lim_{z\to a} \vert f(z) \vert = \infty$) but $\lim_{z \to a} {1}/{f(z)}$ exists, then $a$ is a *pole* of $f$ and a *zero* of ${1}/{f}$.
- If neither $\lim_{z \to a} f(z)$ nor $\lim_{z \to a} {1}/{f(z)}$ exists, then $a$ is an **essential singularity** of both $f$ and ${1}/{f}$.

Another way to characterize an essential singularity is that the [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213) of $f$ at the point $a$ has infinitely many negative degree terms (i.e., the [principal part](wikipedia://en/1225337) of the Laurent series is an infinite sum). A related definition is that if there is a point $a$ for which $f(z)(z-a)^n$ is not differentiable for any integer $n > 0$, then $a$ is an essential singularity of $f$.[^o4]

On a [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) with a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139), $\infty_\mathbb{C}$, the function ${f(z)}$ has an essential singularity at that point if and only if the ${f(1/z)}$ has an essential singularity at $0$: i.e. neither $\lim_{z \to 0}{f(1/z)}$ nor $\lim_{z \to 0} {1}/{f(1/z)}$ exists.[^o5] The [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) on the Riemann sphere has only one essential singularity, which is at $\infty_\mathbb{C}$.[^o6] Indeed, every [meromorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic) function aside that is not a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) has a unique essential singularity at $\infty_\mathbb{C}$.

The behavior of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) near their essential singularities is described by the [Casorati–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/33868) and by the considerably stronger [Picard's great theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard's_great_theorem). The latter says that in every neighborhood of an essential singularity $a$, the function $f$ takes on *every* complex value, except possibly one, infinitely many times. (The exception is necessary; for example, the function $\exp(1/z)$ never takes on the value $0$.)
