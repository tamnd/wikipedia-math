# Identity (philosophy)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Identity"}

Identity

In [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), **identity** (from identitas, "**sameness**") is the [relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relations_%28philosophy%29) each thing bears only to itself.[^o2][^o3] The notion of identity gives rise to [many philosophical problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_philosophy), including the [identity of indiscernibles](wikipedia://en/630398) (if *x* and *y* share all their properties, are they one and the same thing?), and questions about change and [personal identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identity) over time (what has to be the case for a person *x* at one time and a person *y* at a later time to be one and the same person?). It is important to distinguish between *qualitative identity* and *numerical identity*. For example, consider two children with identical bicycles engaged in a race while their mother is watching. The two children have the *same* bicycle in one sense (*qualitative identity*) and the *same* mother in another sense (*numerical identity*).[^o4] This article is mainly concerned with *numerical identity*, which is the stricter notion.

:::

The philosophical concept of identity is distinct from the better-known notion of identity in use in [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) and the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science). The philosophical concept concerns a [*relation*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_%28logic%29), specifically, a relation that *x* and *y* stand in [if, and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are one and the same thing, or *identical to* each other (i.e. if, and only if *x* = *y*). The [sociological notion of identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_%28social_science%29), by contrast, has to do with a person's self-conception, social presentation, and more generally, the aspects of a person that make them unique, or qualitatively different from others (e.g. [cultural identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_identity), [gender identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_identity), [national identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_identity), [online identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_identity), and processes of [identity formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_formation)). Recently, identity has been conceptualized in relation to humans’ position within the ecological web of life; this combination of sociocultural and ecological identification is known as ecocultural identity.[^o5]

## Metaphysics of identity {#Metaphysics_of_identity}

Metaphysicians and philosophers of language and mind ask other questions:

- What does it mean for an object to be the same as itself?
- If x and y are identical (are the same thing), must they always be identical? Are they [*necessarily* identical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessity_of_identity)?
- What does it mean for an object to be the same, if it changes over time? (Is apple<sub>*t*</sub> the same as apple<sub>*t*+1</sub>?)
- If an object's parts are entirely replaced over time, as in the [Ship of Theseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_Theseus) example, in what way is it the same?

The [law of identity](wikipedia://en/1403190) used to be referred to as a "[law of thought](wikipedia://en/1252308)". The modern formulation of identity is that of [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz), who held that *x* is the same as *y* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) every [predicate](wikipedia://en/285109) true of *x* is true of *y* as well.

Leibniz's ideas have taken root in the [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439), where they have influenced the development of the [predicate calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_calculus) as [Leibniz's law](wikipedia://en/630398). Mathematicians sometimes distinguish identity from [equality](wikipedia://en/90446). More mundanely, an *identity* in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) may be an *[equation](wikipedia://en/9284)* that holds true for all values of a [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109). [Hegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegel) argued that things are inherently self-contradictory[^o8][^o9] and that the notion of something being self-identical only made sense if it were not also not-identical or different from itself and did not also imply the latter. In [Hegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegel)'s words, "Identity is the identity of identity and non-identity." More recent metaphysicians have discussed [trans-world identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-world_identity)—the notion that there can be the same object in different [possible worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_worlds).[^o10] An alternative to trans-world identity is the counterpart relation in [counterpart theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterpart_theory). It is a similarity relation that rejects trans-world individuals and instead defends an object's counterpart—the most similar object.

Some philosophers have denied that there is such a relation as identity. Thus [Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittgenstein) writes (*[Tractatus](wikipedia://en/30062)* 5.5301): "That identity is not a relation between objects is obvious." At 5.5303 he elaborates: "Roughly speaking: to say of two things that they are identical is nonsense, and to say of one thing that it is identical with itself is to say nothing." [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) had earlier voiced a worry that seems to be motivating Wittgenstein's point (*[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics)* §64): "\[I\]dentity, an objector may urge, cannot be anything at all: two terms plainly are not identical, and one term cannot be, for what is it identical with?" Even before Russell, [Gottlob Frege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frege), at the beginning of "[On Sense and Reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Sense_and_Reference)," expressed a worry with regard to identity as a relation: "Equality gives rise to challenging questions which are not altogether easy to answer. Is it a relation?" More recently, [C. J. F. Williams](wikipedia://en/31437132)[^o11] has suggested that identity should be viewed as a second-order relation, rather than a relation between objects, and [Kai Wehmeier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kai_Wehmeier)[^o12] has argued that appealing to a binary relation that every object bears to itself, and to no others, is both logically unnecessary and metaphysically suspect.
