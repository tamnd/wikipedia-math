# Heaviside step function

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Heaviside step function"}

The **Heaviside step function**, or the **unit step function**, usually denoted by $H$ or $\theta$ (but sometimes $u$, $\mathbf{1}$ or $\text{𝟙}$), is a [step function](wikipedia://en/170939) named after [Oliver Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831), the value of which is [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) for negative arguments and [one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_%28number%29) for positive arguments. Different conventions concerning the value $H(0)$ are in use. It is an example of the general class of step functions, all of which can be represented as [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) of translations of this one.

:::

The function was originally developed in [operational calculus](wikipedia://en/8950551) for the solution of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309), where it represents a signal that switches on at a specified time and stays switched on indefinitely. Heaviside developed the operational calculus as a tool in the analysis of telegraphic communications and represented the function as $\mathbf{1}$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Taking the convention that $H(0) = 1$, the Heaviside function may be defined as:

- A [piecewise function](wikipedia://en/404130): $$H(x) := \begin{cases} 1, & x \geq 0 \\ 0, & x < 0 \end{cases}$$
- Using the [Iverson bracket](wikipedia://en/1721092) notation: $$H(x) := [x \geq 0]$$
- An [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790): $$H(x) := \mathbf{1}_{x \geq 0}=\mathbf 1_{\mathbb R_+}(x)$$

For the alternative convention that $H(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, it may be expressed as:

- A [piecewise function](wikipedia://en/404130): $$H(x) := \begin{cases} 1, & x > 0 \\ \frac{1}{2}, & x = 0 \\ 0, & x < 0 \end{cases}$$
- A [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449): $$H(x) := \frac{1}{2} \left(\mbox{sgn}\, x + 1\right)$$
- The [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of two [Iverson brackets](wikipedia://en/1721092): $$H(x) := \frac{[x\geq 0] + [x>0]}{2}$$
- A [one-sided limit](wikipedia://en/379840) of the [two-argument arctangent](wikipedia://en/3856703): $$H(x) =: \lim_{\epsilon \to 0^{+}} \frac{\mbox{atan2}(\epsilon,-x)}{\pi}$$
- A [hyperfunction](wikipedia://en/1678626): $$H(x) =: \left(1-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\log z,\ -\frac{1}{2\pi i}\log z\right)$$ Or equivalently: $$H(x) =: \left( -\frac{\log -z}{2\pi i}, -\frac{\log -z}{2\pi i}\right),$$ where $\log z$ is the [principal value of the complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290#Principal_value) of $z$.

Other definitions that are undefined at $H(0)$ include:

- A [piecewise function](wikipedia://en/404130): $$H(x) := \begin{cases} 1, & x > 0 \\ 0, & x < 0 \end{cases}$$
- The derivative of the [ramp function](wikipedia://en/5092636): $$H(x) := \frac{d}{dx} \max \{ x, 0 \}\quad \mbox{for } x \ne 0$$
- Expressed in terms of the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) function, such as: $$H(x) = \frac{1}{2}+\frac{|x|}{2x}$$

:::

## Relationship with Dirac delta function {#Relationship_with_Dirac_delta_function}

The [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021) is the [weak derivative](wikipedia://en/1411087) of the Heaviside function:

$$\delta(x)= \frac{d}{dx} \ H(x),$$

Hence the Heaviside function can be considered to be the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the Dirac delta function. This is sometimes written as:

$$H(x) := \int_{-\infty}^x \delta(s)\,ds,$$

although this expansion may not hold (or even make sense) for $x = 0$, depending on which formalism one uses to give meaning to integrals involving $\delta$. In this context, the Heaviside function is the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) that is [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) 0. (See *[Constant random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_random_variable)*.)

## Analytic approximations {#Analytic_approximations}

Approximations to the Heaviside step function are of use in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry) and [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience), where [logistic](wikipedia://en/84563) approximations of step functions (such as the [Hill](wikipedia://en/2125927) and the [Michaelis–Menten equations](wikipedia://en/253272)) may be used to approximate binary cellular switches in response to chemical signals.

For a [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) approximation to the step function, one can use the [logistic function](wikipedia://en/84563):

$$H(x) \approx \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\tanh kx = \frac{1}{1+e^{-2kx}},$$

where a larger $k$ corresponds to a sharper transition at $x = 0$.

If we take $H(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, equality holds in the limit:

$$H(x)=\lim_{k \to \infty}\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\tanh kx)=\lim_{k \to \infty}\frac{1}{1+e^{-2kx}}.$$

*Image: Step function approximation.png.* $\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} \tanh(kx) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-2kx}}$approaches the step function as $k \to \infty$.

There are [many other smooth, analytic approximations](wikipedia://en/87210#Examples) to the step function. Among the possibilities are:

$$\begin{aligned} H(x) &= \lim_{k \to \infty} \left(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{\pi}\arctan kx\right)\\ H(x) &= \lim_{k \to \infty}\left(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac12\operatorname{erf} kx\right) \end{aligned}$$

These limits hold [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) and in the sense of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29). In general, however, [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198) need not imply distributional convergence, and vice versa distributional convergence need not imply pointwise convergence. (However, if all members of a pointwise convergent sequence of functions are uniformly bounded by some "nice" function, then [convergence holds in the sense of distributions too](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_dominated_convergence_theorem).)

One could also use a scaled and shifted [Sigmoid function](wikipedia://en/87210).

In general, any [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_distribution) [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) that is peaked around zero and has a parameter that controls for [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) can serve as an approximation, in the limit as the variance approaches zero. For example, all three of the above approximations are [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) of common probability distributions: the [logistic](wikipedia://en/542666), [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/7003) and [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) distributions, respectively.

:::

## Non-analytic approximations {#Non-analytic_approximations}

Approximations to the Heaviside step function could be made through [Smooth transition function](wikipedia://en/421463#Smooth_transition_functions) like $1 \leq m \to \infty$:

$$\begin{aligned}f(x) &= \begin{cases} {\displaystyle \frac{1}{2}\left(1+\tanh\left(m\frac{2x}{1-x^2}\right)\right)}, & |x| < 1 \\ \\ 1, & x \geq 1 \\ 0, & x \leq -1 \end{cases}\end{aligned}$$

## Integral representations {#Integral_representations}

Often an [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_%28mathematics%29) representation of the Heaviside step function is useful:

$$\begin{aligned} H(x)&=\lim_{ \varepsilon \to 0^+} -\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\tau+i\varepsilon} e^{-i x \tau} d\tau \\ &=\lim_{ \varepsilon \to 0^+} \ \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{1}{\tau-i\varepsilon} e^{i x \tau} d\tau, \end{aligned}$$

where the second representation is easy to deduce from the first, given that the step function is real and thus is its own [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918).

## Zero argument {#Zero_argument}

Since $H$ is usually used in [integration](wikipedia://en/15532), and the value of a function at a single point does not affect its integral, it rarely matters what particular value is chosen of $H(0)$. Indeed when $H$ is considered as a [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) or an element of $L\infty$ (see [$Lp$ space](wikipedia://en/45194)) it does not even make sense to talk of a value at zero, since such objects are only defined [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688). If using some analytic approximation (as in the examples above) then often whatever happens to be the relevant limit at zero is used.

There exist various reasons for choosing a particular value.

- $H(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ is often used since the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) then has [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096); put another way, $H - \tfrac{1}{2}$ is then an [odd function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_function). In this case the following relation with the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449) holds for all $x$: $$H(x) = \tfrac12(1 + \operatorname{sgn} x).$$ Also, $\forall x, \ H(x) + H(-x) = 1$.
- $H(0) = 1$ is used when $H$ needs to be [right-continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-continuous). For instance [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) are usually taken to be right continuous, as are functions integrated against in [Lebesgue–Stieltjes integration](wikipedia://en/368525). In this case $H$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) semi-infinite interval: $$H(x) = \mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x).$$ The corresponding probability distribution is the [degenerate distribution](wikipedia://en/101843).
- $H(0) = 0$ is used when $H$ needs to be [left-continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-continuous). In this case $H$ is an indicator function of an [open](wikipedia://en/39358) semi-infinite interval: $$H(x) = \mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x).$$
- In functional-analysis contexts from [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization) and [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924), it is often useful to define the Heaviside function as a [set-valued function](wikipedia://en/222367) to preserve the continuity of the limiting functions and ensure the existence of certain solutions. In these cases, the Heaviside function returns a whole interval of possible solutions, $H(0) = [0,1]$.

## Discrete form {#Discrete_form}

An alternative form of the unit step, defined instead as a function $H : \mathbb{Z} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ (that is, taking in a discrete variable $n$), is:

$$H[n]=\begin{cases} 0, & n < 0, \\ 1, & n \ge 0, \end{cases}$$

Or using the half-maximum convention:[^o13]

$$H[n]=\begin{cases} 0, & n < 0, \\ \tfrac12, & n = 0,\\ 1, & n > 0, \end{cases}$$

where $n$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). If $n$ is an integer, then $n < 0$ must imply that $n \le -1$, while $n > 0$ must imply that the function attains unity at $n = 1$. Therefore the "step function" exhibits ramp-like behavior over the domain of −1, 1, and cannot authentically be a step function, using the half-maximum convention.

Unlike the continuous case, the definition of $H[0]$ is significant.

The discrete-time unit impulse is the first difference of the discrete-time step:

$$\delta[n] = H[n] - H[n-1].$$

This function is the cumulative summation of the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890):

$$H[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} \delta[k],$$

where $\delta[k] = \delta_{k,0}$ is the [discrete unit impulse function](wikipedia://en/101843).

## Antiderivative and derivative {#Antiderivative_and_derivative}

The [ramp function](wikipedia://en/5092636) is an [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) of the Heaviside step function:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{x} H(\xi)\,d\xi = x H(x) = \max\{0,x\} \,.$$

The [distributional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative) of the Heaviside step function is the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021):

$$\frac{d H(x)}{dx} = \delta(x) \,.$$

## Fourier transform {#Fourier_transform}

The [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of the Heaviside step function is a distribution. Using one choice of constants for the definition of the Fourier transform we have

$$\hat{H}(s) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\int^N_{-N} e^{-2\pi i x s} H(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2} \left( \delta(s) - \frac{i}{\pi} \operatorname{p.v.}\frac{1}{s} \right).$$

Here $p.v.\tfrac{1}{s}$ is the [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) that takes a test function $\varphi$ to the [Cauchy principal value](wikipedia://en/408195) of $\textstyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\varphi(s)}{s} \, ds$. The limit appearing in the integral is also taken in the sense of (tempered) distributions.

## Unilateral Laplace transform {#Unilateral_Laplace_transform}

The [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) of the Heaviside step function is a [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857). Using the unilateral Laplace transform we have:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat{H}(s) &= \lim_{N\to\infty}\int^N_{0} e^{-sx} H(x)\,dx\\ &= \lim_{N\to\infty}\int^N_{0} e^{-sx} \,dx\\ &= \frac{1}{s} \end{aligned}$$

When the [bilateral transform](wikipedia://en/18610#Bilateral_Laplace_transform) is used, the integral can be split in two parts and the result will be the same.
