# Decimal separator

*Image: Decimal separators.svg.* Both a [comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma) and a [full stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_stop) (or period) are generally accepted decimal separators for international use. The [apostrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostrophe) and Arabic decimal separator are also used in certain contexts.

*Image: Thousands separators.gif.* Three ways to group the number ten thousand with digit group separators: [Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_%28punctuation%29), the internationally recommended thousands separator.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Decimal separator"}

A **decimal separator** is a symbol that separates the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) part from the [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532) of a [number](wikipedia://en/21690) written in [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) form. Different countries officially designate different symbols for use as the separator. The choice of symbol can also affect the choice of symbol for the thousands separator used in digit grouping.

:::

Any such symbol can be called a **decimal mark**, **decimal marker**, or **decimal sign**. Symbol-specific names are also used; **decimal point** and **decimal comma** refer to a dot (either at the [baseline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseline_dot) or the [vertically middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpunct) of the written characters) and [comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma), respectively, when it is used as a decimal separator; these are the usual terms used in English,[^o2][^o3][^o4] with the aforementioned generic terms reserved for abstract usage.[^o5][^o6]

In many contexts, when a number is spoken, the function of the separator is assumed by the spoken name of the symbol: *comma* or *point* in most cases.[^o7][^o3][^o8] In some specialized contexts, the word *decimal* is instead used for this purpose (such as in [International Civil Aviation Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Civil_Aviation_Organization)-regulated [air traffic control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_traffic_control) communications). In mathematics, the decimal separator is a type of radix point, a term that also applies to number systems with bases other than ten.

## Current international standards {#Current_international_standards}

The 22nd [General Conference on Weights and Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Conference_on_Weights_and_Measures)[^o10] declared in 2003, "The symbol for the decimal marker shall be either the point on the line or the comma on the line." It further reaffirmed,[^o10]

That is, "1 000 000 000" is preferred over "1,000,000,000" or "1.000.000.000". This use has therefore been recommended by technical organizations, such as the United States's [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology).[^o11]

[ISO 80000-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_80000-1) stipulates, "The decimal sign is either a comma or a point on the line." The standard does not stipulate any preference, observing that usage will depend on customary usage in the language concerned, but adds a note that as per ISO/IEC directives, all ISO standards should use the comma as the decimal marker.

## History {#History}

### Hellenistic–Renaissance eras {#Hellenistic–Renaissance_eras}

In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), before printing, a [bar](wikipedia://en/4780165) ( ¯ ) over the [units digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_digit) was used to separate the integral part of a number from its [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532), as in 9$\overline{9}$95 (meaning 99.95 in [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) point format). A similar notation remains in common use as an underbar to superscript digits, especially for monetary values without a decimal separator, as in 99<sup>95</sup>. Later, a "separatrix" (i.e., a short, roughly vertical ink stroke) between the units and tenths position became the norm among [Arab mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam) (e.g. 99**ˌ**95), while an L-shaped or [vertical bar](wikipedia://en/641073) (|) served as the separatrix in England. When this character was [typeset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeset), it was convenient to use the existing [comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma_%28punctuation%29) (99**,**95) or [full stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_stop) (99**.**95) instead.

Positional [decimal fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_fraction) appear for the first time in a book by the Arab mathematician [Abu'l-Hasan al-Uqlidisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu'l-Hasan_al-Uqlidisi) written in the 10th century.[^o14] He used a short vertical bar above the digit. The practice is ultimately derived from the decimal [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371) used in [Indian mathematics](wikipedia://en/1848052),[^o15] and popularized by the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people) mathematician [Al-Khwarizmi](wikipedia://en/649861),[^o16] when [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) translation of his work on the [Indian numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numerals) introduced the decimal [positional number system](wikipedia://en/437052) to the Western world. His *[Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compendious_Book_on_Calculation_by_Completion_and_Balancing)* presented the first systematic solution of [linear](wikipedia://en/17570) and [quadratic equations](wikipedia://en/25175) in Arabic.

[Gerbert of Aurillac](wikipedia://en/24614) marked triples of columns with an arc (called a "Pythagorean arc"), when using his Hindu–Arabic numeral-based abacus in the 10th century. [Fibonacci](wikipedia://en/17949) followed this convention when writing numbers, such as in his influential work [Liber Abaci](wikipedia://en/167699) in the 13th century.[^o17]

The earliest known record of using the decimal point is in the astronomical tables compiled by the Italian merchant and mathematician [Giovanni Bianchini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Bianchini) in the 1440s.[^o18]History of mathematical notation#Indian and Arabic numerals and notation

Tables of [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860) prepared by [John Napier](wikipedia://en/15993) in 1614 and 1619 used the period (full stop) as the decimal separator, which was then adopted by [Henry Briggs](wikipedia://en/210399) in his influential 17th century work.

In [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France), the full stop was already in use in printing to make [Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657) more readable, so the comma was chosen.[^o19]

Many other countries, such as Italy, also chose to use the comma to mark the decimal units position.[^o19] It has been [made standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_80000-2) by the [ISO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Organization_for_Standardization) for international blueprints.[^o20] However, English-speaking countries took the comma to separate sequences of three digits. In some countries, a raised dot or dash (*upper comma*) may be used for grouping or decimal separator; this is particularly common in handwriting.

### English-speaking countries {#English-speaking_countries}

In the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), the full stop or period (.) is used as the standard decimal separator.

*Image: Interpunct as decimal point in Henderson 1839.png.* The [interpunct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpunct) (·) used as a decimal separator in a British print from 1839[^o22]

In the nations of the [British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Empire) (and, later, the [Commonwealth of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_of_Nations)), the full stop could be used in typewritten material and its use was not banned, although the [interpunct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpunct) (a.k.a. decimal point, point or mid dot) was preferred as a decimal separator, in printing technologies that could accommodate it, e.g. 99·95 .[^o23] However, as the mid dot was already in common use in the mathematics world to indicate multiplication, the [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) rejected its use as the decimal separator.

During the beginning of British [metrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrication) in the late 1960s and with impending currency [decimalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimalisation), there was some debate in the United Kingdom as to whether the decimal comma or decimal point should be preferred: the [British Standards Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Standards_Institution) and some sectors of industry advocated the comma and the [Decimal Currency Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_Day) advocated for the point. In the event, the point was chosen by the [Ministry of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Technology) in 1968.[^o24]

*Image: MUTCD-CA G11-6.svg.* California milepost marker at mile 144.44

When South Africa [adopted the metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrication), it adopted the comma as its decimal separator,[^o25] although a number of house styles, including some English-language newspapers such as *[The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_Times_%28South_Africa%29)*, continue to use the full stop.

Previously, signs along [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California) roads expressed distances in decimal numbers with the decimal part in superscript, as in 3<sup>7</sup>, meaning 3.7.[^o26] Though California has since transitioned to [mixed numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_number) with [common fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_fraction), the older style remains on [postmile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_postmile) markers and bridge inventory markers.

### Constructed languages {#Constructed_languages}

The three most spoken [international auxiliary languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language), [Ido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ido), [Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto), and [Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua), all use the comma as the decimal separator.

Interlingua has used the comma as its decimal separator since the publication of the [Interlingua Grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua:_A_Grammar_of_the_International_Language) in 1951.[^o28]

Esperanto also uses the comma as its official decimal separator, whilst thousands are usually separated by [non-breaking spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-breaking_space) (e.g. 12 345 678,9). It is possible to separate thousands by a [full stop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_stop) (e.g. 12.345.678,9), though this is not as common.[^o29]

Ido's *Kompleta Gramatiko Detaloza di la Linguo Internaciona Ido* (Complete Detailed Grammar of the International Language Ido) officially states that commas are used for the decimal separator whilst full stops are used to separate thousands, millions, etc. So the number 12,345,678.90123 (in American notation), for instance, would be written *\12.345.678,90123* in Ido.

The 1931 grammar of [Volapük](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volapük) uses the comma as its decimal separator but, somewhat unusually, the middle dot as its thousands separator (12·345·678,90123).[^o30]

In 1958, disputes between European and American delegates over the correct representation of the decimal separator nearly stalled the development of the [ALGOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL) computer programming language.[^o31] ALGOL ended up allowing different decimal separators, but most computer languages and standard data formats (e.g., [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29), [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29), [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran), [Cascading Style Sheets (CSS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS)) specify a dot.

Most programming languages, that allow digit grouping, use the underscore (\_). For example [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29),[^o32] [D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_%28programming_language%29),[^o33] [Rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_%28programming_language%29),[^o34] [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29),[^o35] [Ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_%28programming_language%29)[^o36] use underscore. [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++14#Digit_separators) uses the apostrophe ('). Digit separators are ignored by the computer and can be used flexibly. For example, a programmer could write `1_00_00_000` to match the Indian numbering system's format for ten million, `1,00,00,000`.)

### Radix point {#Radix_point}

In mathematics and [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing), a **radix point**, or **radix character**, is a symbol used in the display of numbers to separate the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) part of the value from its [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532). In English and many other languages (including many that are written right-to-left), the integer part is at the left of the radix point, and the fraction part at the right of it.[^o39]

A radix point is most often used in [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) (base 10) notation, when it is more commonly called the *decimal point* (with deci- indicating [base&nbsp;10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base&nbsp;10)). In [English-speaking countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_where_English_is_an_official_language), the decimal point is usually a small dot (.)[^o40] placed either on the baseline, or halfway between the baseline and the top of the [digits](wikipedia://en/280582) (**·**)[^o40] In many other countries, the radix point is a comma (,) placed on the baseline.[^o40]

These conventions are generally used both in machine displays ([printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing), [computer monitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_monitor)) and in [handwriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwriting). It is important to know which notation is being used when working in different software programs. The respective [ISO 31-0 standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_31-0#Numbers) defines both the comma and the small dot as decimal markers, but does not explicitly define universal radix marks for bases other than 10.

Fractional numbers are rarely displayed in other [number bases](wikipedia://en/701207), but, when they are, a radix character may be used for the same purpose. When used with the [binary](wikipedia://en/238686) (base 2) representation, it may be called "binary point".

## Digit grouping {#Digit_grouping}

For ease of reading, numbers with many digits (e.g. numbers over 999) may be divided into groups using a [delimiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delimiter),[^o43] such as comma (,), dot (.), [Narrow No-Break Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrow_no-break_space) (" "), [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_%28punctuation%29) (" "), underscore (\_) in [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015), or apostrophe (') in [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland). In some countries, these "digit group separators" are only employed to the left of the decimal separator; in others, they are also used to separate numbers with a long [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532). An important reason for grouping is that it allows rapid judgement of the number of digits, via telling at a glance ("[subitizing](wikipedia://en/734854)") rather than counting (contrast, for example, 100 000 000 with 100000000 for one hundred million).

The use of thin spaces as separatorsp. 133 instead of dots or commas (for example: 20 000 and 1 000 000 for "twenty thousand" and "one million"), has been official policy of the [International Bureau of Weights and Measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Bureau_of_Weights_and_Measures) (BIPM) since 1948 (and reaffirmed in 2003),[^o10] as well as of the [International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Pure_and_Applied_Chemistry) (IUPAC),[^o45][^o46] the [American Medical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Medical_Association)'s widely followed *[AMA Manual of Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMA_Manual_of_Style)*, and the UK [Metrication Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrication_Board), among others.

According to the BIPM neither dots or commas are allowed to be used for grouping and when there are only four digits, grouping is regarded as superfluous.[^o44]

The correct [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) delimiter is Narrow No-Break Space " " (U+202F) which is thinner than a normal space and prevents the number from being broken up at the end of a line.

The groups created by the delimiters tend to follow the usages of local languages, which vary. In European languages, large numbers are read in groups of thousands, and the delimiter (occurring every three digits when used) may be called a "thousands separator". In [East Asian cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_culture), particularly [China](wikipedia://en/5781), [Japan](wikipedia://en/146615), and [Korea](wikipedia://en/994834), large numbers are read in groups of [myriads](wikipedia://en/20088) (10 000s), but the delimiter often separates the digits into groups of three.[^o47]

The [Indian numbering system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numbering_system) is more complex: It groups the rightmost three digits together (until the hundreds place) and then groups digits in sets of two. For example, one trillion would be written "10,00,00,00,00,000" or "10 [hi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numbering_system)".[^o48]

The convention for digit group separators historically varied among countries, but usually sought to distinguish the delimiter from the decimal separator. Traditionally, [English-speaking countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-speaking_countries) (except South Africa)[^o49] employed commas as the delimiter—10,000—and other European countries employed periods (full stops) or spaces: 10.000 or 10 000. Because of the confusion that could result in international documents, in recent years, the use of spaces as separators has been advocated by the superseded [SI/ISO 31-0 standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_31-0#Numbers),[^o50] as well as by the BIPM and IUPAC. These groups have also begun advocating the use of a "thin space" in "groups of three".[^o45][^o46]

Within the United States, the American Medical Association's widely followed *AMA Manual of Style* also calls for a thin space.[^o43] In programming languages and online [encoding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_encoding) environments (for example, [ASCII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCII)-only languages and environments) a thin space is not practical or available. Often, either underscores[^o51] and regular word spaces, or no delimiters at all are used instead.

### Data vis-à-vis mask {#Data_vis-à-vis_mask}

Digit group separators can occur either as part of the data or as a mask through which the data is displayed. This is an example of the [separation of presentation and content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_presentation_and_content), making it possible to display numbers in spaced groups while not inserting any [whitespace characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitespace_character) into the string of digits that make up those numbers. In many computing contexts, it is preferred to omit the digit group separators from the data and instead overlay them as a mask (an [input mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input_mask) or an output mask).

Common examples include [spreadsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet) and [databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database), in which currency values are entered without such marks but are displayed with them inserted. Similarly, phone numbers can have hyphens, spaces or parentheses as a mask rather than as data. In [web content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_content), digit grouping can be done with [CSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS). This is useful because the number can be copied and pasted elsewhere (such as into a calculator) and parsed by the computer as-is (i.e., without the user manually purging the extraneous characters). For example:

In many [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015), it is possible to group the digits in the program's [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) to make it easier to read (see: Integer literal#Digit separators).

[Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29), Free-form [Fortran 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran_90), [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl), [D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_%28programming_language%29), [OCaml](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OCaml), [Ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_%28programming_language%29), [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29), [Rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_%28programming_language%29), [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29), [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) (since version 3.6), [Kotlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kotlin_%28programming_language%29),[^o53] [C#](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_Sharp_%28programming_language%29) (since version 7.0),[^o54] [Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swift_%28programming_language%29), [Haskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskell_%28programming_language%29) (from GHC version 8.6.1), [Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_%28programming_language%29) (since version 1.13), [PHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHP) (since version 7.4),[^o55] [Zig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zig_%28programming_language%29),[^o56], [JavaScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JavaScript) (since ES2021) and [Scala](wikipedia://en/3254510) (since version 2.13) have adopted the [underscore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underscore) (\_) character for this purpose. As such, these languages would allow the number seven hundred million to be entered as "700\_000\_000". On the other hand, fixed-form Fortran ignores whitespace in all contexts, so "700 000 000" would be allowed. In [C++14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++14), [Rebol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebol) and [Red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_%28programming_language%29), the use of an [apostrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostrophe) for digit grouping is allowed. Thus, "700'000'000" would be allowed in those languages.

### Exceptions to digit grouping {#Exceptions_to_digit_grouping}

The International Bureau of Weights and Measures states that "when there are only four digits before or after the decimal marker, it is customary not to use a space to isolate a single digit."[^o45] Likewise, some [manuals of style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_guide) state that thousands separators should not be used in normal text for numbers from 1000 to 9999 where no decimal fractional part is shown (or, in other words, for four-digit whole numbers), whereas others use thousands separators and others use both. For example, [APA style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APA_style) stipulates a thousands separator for "most figures of 1000 or more" except for page numbers, binary digits, temperatures, etc.

There are always "common-sense" country-specific exceptions to digit grouping, such as year numbers, [postal codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postal_code), and ID numbers of predefined nongrouped format, which style guides usually point out.

### In non-base-10 numbering systems {#In_non-base-10_numbering_systems}

In binary (base-2), a full space can be used between groups of four digits, corresponding to a [nibble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nibble), or equivalently to a [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) digit. For integer numbers, dots are used as well to separate groups of four bits. Alternatively, binary digits may be grouped by threes, corresponding to an [octal](wikipedia://en/22330) digit. Similarly, in hexadecimal (base-16), full spaces are usually used to group digits into twos, making each group correspond to a [byte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte). Additionally, groups of eight bytes are often separated by a hyphen.

## Influence of calculators and computers {#Influence_of_calculators_and_computers}

In countries with a decimal comma, the decimal point is also common as the "international" notation because of the influence of devices, such as [electronic calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_calculator), which use the decimal point. Most computer [operating systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_systems) allow selection of the decimal separator; programs that have been carefully [internationalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I18n) will follow this, but some programs ignore it and a few may even fail to operate if the setting has been changed.

Computer interfaces may be set to the Unicode international "Common locale" using LC\_NUMERIC=C as defined at "Unicode CLDR project". *Unicode Consortium*. <http://cldr.unicode.org/index> Details of the current (2020) definitions may be found at "01102-POSIX15897". *Unicode Consortium*. <http://www.unicode.org/L2/L2001/01102-POSIX15897.htm>

### Other numeral systems {#Other_numeral_systems}

Unicode defines a *decimal separator key symbol* (⎖ in hex U+2396, decimal 9110) which looks similar to the [apostrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostrophe). This symbol is from [ISO/IEC 9995](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO/IEC_9995) and is intended for use on a keyboard to indicate a key that performs decimal separation.

In the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world), where [Eastern Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/3655571) are used for writing numbers, a different character is used to separate the integer and fractional parts of numbers. It is referred to as an [Arabic decimal separator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_%28Unicode_block%29) (U+066B, rendered: Arab) in [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode). An Arabic thousands separator (U+066C, rendered: Arab) also exists. Example: Arab (9,999.99)

In [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language), the decimal separator is called fa. The Unicode Consortium's investigation concluded that "computer programs should render U+066B as a shortened, lowered, and possibly more slanted [slash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_%28punctuation%29) (fa-Arab); this should be distinguishable from the slash at the first sight." To separate [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) of three digits, an Arabic thousands separator (rendered as: fa-Arab), a Latin comma, or a [blank space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_%28punctuation%29) may be used; however this is not a standard.[^o72][^o73][^o74] Example: fa-Arab (9,999.99)

In [English Braille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Braille), the decimal point, 46, is distinct from both the comma, 2, and the full stop, 256.

:::

- In Belgium (Dutch), Brazil, Denmark, Germany, Greece, Indonesia, Italy, Netherlands, Portugal, Romania, Russia, Slovenia, Sweden and much of Europe, "1" or "1.234.567,89" are seen. In [handwriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handwriting), "1˙234˙567,89" is also seen, but never in Belgium, Brazil, Denmark, Estonia, Germany, the Netherlands, Portugal, Romania, Russia, Slovenia or Sweden. In Italy, a straight apostrophe (') is also used in handwriting: "1'234'567,89". In the Netherlands and [Dutch-speaking Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch-speaking_Belgium), points are used as thousands separators, and is preferred for currency values, but the space is recommended by some style guides, mostly in technical writing.[^o86]
- In Estonia, currency numbers often use a dot (.) as the decimal separator, and a space as a thousands separator. This is most visible on shopping receipts and in documents that also use other numbers with decimals, such as measurements. This practice is used to better distinguish between prices and other values with decimals. An older convention uses dots to separate thousands (with commas for decimals) – this older practice makes it easier to avoid word breaks with larger numbers.
- Historically, in Germany and Austria, thousands separators were occasionally denoted by alternating uses of commas and points, e.g. "1.234,567.890,12" (or "1.234,567.890·12" in [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary) and Austria prior to 1938)[^o87][^o88] for "eine Milliarde 234 Millionen ...", but this is not seen today and contemporary German readers would require an explanation to understand it.
- In Switzerland, there are two styles. Currency values use an apostrophe (') as a thousands separator along with a dot (.) as the decimal separator, like "1'234'567.89". For other values, the SI-style "1" is used, with a comma (,) as the decimal separator. The apostrophe is also the most common thousands separator for non-currency values, like "1'234'567,89".
- In Ireland, Israel, Japan, [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea) (both), Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore, Taiwan, Thailand, the United Kingdom, and the United States, "1,234,567.89" or "1,234,567·89" are seen. However, the latter is generally only found in older, [handwritten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript) documents.
- For English-speaking regions of Canada, there are two styles. The preferred style for currency values is "\$10,000.00", while the preferred style for numeric values is "1". However, commas are also sometimes used, despite no longer being taught in school or used in official publications.
- The [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI) uses the following two styles: "1" and "1". In their own publications, a dot (.) is used in the English version, while a comma (,) is used in the official French version.
- In [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden), a colon (:) is sometimes used as the decimal separator for currency values, like "1 234 567:89".
- In China, commas and spaces are used to mark digit groups, since dots are used as decimal separators. There is no universal convention on digit grouping, however, so both thousands grouping and no digit grouping can be found. Japan and Taiwan are similar, although, when grouping by myriads, [ja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji) or [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters) are frequently used as separators, like the following: "1億2345万6789" or "1億2345萬6789" (see [Japanese numerals](wikipedia://en/146615) and [Chinese numerals](wikipedia://en/5781)). Commas are used when grouping by thousands.
- In [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India), which uses the [Indian numbering system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numbering_system), commas (,) are used at the thousands, [hi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakh), and [hi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crore) levels. For example, 10 million (1 hi) would be written "1,00,00,000", while 100 thousand (1 hi) would be written "1,00,000". In [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan), there is a greater tendency to use the standard western system, but the Indian numbering system is used when conducting business in [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu).
