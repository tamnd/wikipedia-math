# Circumcircle

*Image: Circumcentre.svg.* The perpendicular bisectors of the three sides of a triangle pass through the triangle's circumcenter.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Circumcircle"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **circumscribed circle** or **circumcircle** of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) that passes through all three [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417). The center of this circle is called the **circumcenter** of the triangle, and its radius is called the **circumradius**. The circumcenter is equidistant from all vertices, and can thus be constructed as the point of [intersection](wikipedia://en/41604289) between any two [perpendicular bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of the triangle's sides. It is a [triangle center](wikipedia://en/12401488).

:::

More generally, an $n$-sided [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) with all its vertices on the same circle, also called the circumscribed circle, is called a [cyclic polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon), or in the special case $n = 4$, a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131). All [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048), [isosceles trapezoids](wikipedia://en/511691), [right kites](wikipedia://en/37263250), and [regular polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polygons) are cyclic, but not every polygon is.

## Straightedge and compass construction {#Straightedge_and_compass_construction}

The circumcircle of a triangle can be [constructed using straightedge and compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass-and-straightedge_construction) by first constructing any two of the three [perpendicular bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Perpendicular_bisectors) of the sides; their point of intersection is the *circumcenter*. The *circumcircle* can immediately be drawn as the circle centered there and passing through one of the triangle's vertices; its radius is the *circumradius*.

Any point on a perpendicular bisector of one side is equidistant from the two adjacent vertices of the triangle. Therefore any point which is simultaneously on two of the perpendicular bisectors must be equidistant from all three vertices.

### Alternative construction {#Alternative_construction}

*Image: Triangle circumcenter alternate construction.svg.* Alternative construction of the circumcenter (intersection of broken lines)

An alternative method to determine the circumcenter is to draw any two lines each one departing from one of the vertices at an angle with the common side, the common angle of departure being 90° minus the angle of the opposite vertex. (In the case of the opposite angle being obtuse, drawing a line at a negative angle means going outside the triangle.)

In [coastal navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilotage), a triangle's circumcircle is sometimes used as a way of obtaining a [position line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_line) using a [sextant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextant) when no [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass) is available. The horizontal angle between two landmarks defines the circumcircle upon which the observer lies.

## Location relative to the triangle {#Location_relative_to_the_triangle}

The circumcenter's position depends on the type of triangle:

- For an acute triangle (all angles smaller than a right angle), the circumcenter always lies inside the triangle.
- For a right triangle, the circumcenter always lies at the midpoint of the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366). This is one form of [Thales' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales'_theorem).
- For an obtuse triangle (a triangle with one angle bigger than a right angle), the circumcenter always lies outside the triangle.

These locational features can be seen by considering the trilinear or barycentric coordinates given below for the circumcenter: all three coordinates are positive for any interior point, at least one coordinate is negative for any exterior point, and one coordinate is zero and two are positive for a non-vertex point on a side of the triangle.

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Alternate segment theorem"}

## Angles {#Angles}

The angles which the circumscribed circle forms with the sides of the triangle coincide with angles at which sides meet each other. The side opposite angle $\alpha$ meets the circle twice: once at each end; in each case at angle $\alpha$ (similarly for the other two angles). This is due to the **alternate segment theorem**, which states that the angle between the tangent and chord equals the angle in the alternate segment.

:::

## Circumcircle equations {#Circumcircle_equations}

### Cartesian coordinates {#Cartesian_coordinates}

In the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), it is possible to give explicitly an equation of the circumcircle in terms of the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of the vertices of the inscribed triangle. Suppose that

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{A} &= (A_x, A_y) \\ \mathbf{B} &= (B_x, B_y) \\ \mathbf{C} &= (C_x, C_y) \end{aligned}$$

are the coordinates of points $A, B, C$. The circumcircle is then the locus of points $\mathbf v = (v_x,v_y)$ in the Cartesian plane satisfying the equations

$$\begin{aligned} |\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u}|^2 &= r^2 \\ |\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{u}|^2 &= r^2 \\ |\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{u}|^2 &= r^2 \\ |\mathbf{C} - \mathbf{u}|^2 &= r^2 \end{aligned}$$

guaranteeing that the points $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{v}$ are all the same distance $r$ from the common center $\mathbf u$ of the circle. Using the [polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965), these equations reduce to the condition that the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859)

$$\begin{bmatrix} |\mathbf{v}|^2 & -2v_x & -2v_y & -1 \\ |\mathbf{A}|^2 & -2A_x & -2A_y & -1 \\ |\mathbf{B}|^2 & -2B_x & -2B_y & -1 \\ |\mathbf{C}|^2 & -2C_x & -2C_y & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

has a nonzero [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915). Thus the circumcircle may alternatively be described as the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of zeros of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of this matrix:

$$\det\begin{bmatrix} |\mathbf{v}|^2 & v_x & v_y & 1 \\ |\mathbf{A}|^2 & A_x & A_y & 1 \\ |\mathbf{B}|^2 & B_x & B_y & 1 \\ |\mathbf{C}|^2 & C_x & C_y & 1 \end{bmatrix}=0.$$

Using [cofactor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_expansion), let

$$\begin{aligned} S_x &= \frac{1}{2}\det\begin{bmatrix} |\mathbf{A}|^2 & A_y & 1 \\ |\mathbf{B}|^2 & B_y & 1 \\ |\mathbf{C}|^2 & C_y & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \\[5pt] S_y &= \frac{1}{2}\det\begin{bmatrix} A_x & |\mathbf{A}|^2 & 1 \\ B_x & |\mathbf{B}|^2 & 1 \\ C_x & |\mathbf{C}|^2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \\[5pt] a &= \det\begin{bmatrix} A_x & A_y & 1 \\ B_x & B_y & 1 \\ C_x & C_y & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \\[5pt] b &= \det\begin{bmatrix} A_x & A_y & |\mathbf{A}|^2 \\ B_x & B_y & |\mathbf{B}|^2 \\ C_x & C_y & |\mathbf{C}|^2 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

we then have $a|\mathbf v|^2 - 2\mathbf{Sv} - b = 0$ where $\mathbf S = (S_x, S_y),$ and – assuming the three points were not in a line (otherwise the circumcircle is that line that can also be seen as a generalized circle with $\mathbf{S}$ at infinity) – $\left|\mathbf v - \tfrac{\mathbf S}{a}\right|^2 = \tfrac{b}{a} + \tfrac{|\mathbf S|^2}{a^2},$ giving the circumcenter $\tfrac{\mathbf S}{a}$ and the circumradius $\sqrt{\tfrac{b}{a} + \tfrac{|\mathbf S|^2}{a^2}}.$ A similar approach allows one to deduce the equation of the [circumsphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumsphere) of a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606).

### Parametric equation {#Parametric_equation}

A [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the plane containing the circle is given by

$$\widehat{n} = \frac{(P_2 - P_1) \times (P_3 - P_1)}{| (P_2 - P_1) \times (P_3 - P_1)|}.$$

Hence, given the radius, $r$, center, $P_{c}$, a point on the circle, $P_{0}$ and a unit normal of the plane containing the circle, $\widehat n,$ one [parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108) of the circle starting from the point $P_{0}$ and proceeding in a positively oriented (i.e., [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule)) sense about $\widehat n$ is the following:

$$\mathrm{R} (s) = \mathrm{P_c} + \cos\left(\frac{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{r}}\right) (P_0 - P_c) + \sin\left(\frac{\mathrm{s}}{\mathrm{r}}\right) \left[\widehat{n} \times(P_0 - P_c)\right].$$

### Trilinear and barycentric coordinates {#Trilinear_and_barycentric_coordinates}

An equation for the circumcircle in [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) $x : y : z$ is[^o11] $\tfrac{a}{x} + \tfrac{b}{y} + \tfrac{c}{z} =0.$ An equation for the circumcircle in [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29) $x : y : z$ is $\tfrac{a^2}{x} + \tfrac{b^2}{y} + \tfrac{c^2}{z} =0.$

The [isogonal conjugate](wikipedia://en/2083100) of the circumcircle is the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142), given in [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) by $ax+by+cz=0$ and in barycentric coordinates by $x+y+z=0.$

### Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

Additionally, the circumcircle of a triangle embedded in three dimensions can be found using a generalized method. Let $\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}$ be three-dimensional points, which form the vertices of a triangle. We start by transposing the system to place $\mathbf{C}$ at the origin:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{a} &= \mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}, \\ \mathbf{b} &= \mathbf{B}-\mathbf{C}. \end{aligned}$$

The circumradius $r$ is then

$$r = \frac {\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\right\|} {2 \left\|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\right\|} = \frac{\left\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\right\|}{2 \sin\theta} = \frac{\left\|\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{B}\right\|}{2 \sin\theta},$$

where $\theta$ is the interior angle between $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. The circumcenter, $p_{0}$, is given by

$$p_0 = \frac {\left(\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^2\mathbf{b} - \left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^2\mathbf{a}\right) \times (\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b})} {2 \left\|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\right\|^2} + \mathbf{C}.$$

This formula only works in three dimensions as the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) is not defined in other dimensions, but it can be generalized to the other dimensions by replacing the cross products with following identities:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u} \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{w}) &= (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w})\mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{w}, \\ \left\|\mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{v}\right\|^2 &= \left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|^2 \left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|^2 - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2. \end{aligned}$$

This gives us the following equation for the circumradius $r$:

$$r = \frac {\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\right\|} {2 \sqrt{\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^{2}\left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^2 - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})^2}}$$

and the following equation for the circumcenter $p_{0}$:

$$p_0 = \frac {\left(\left(\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^2\mathbf{b} - \left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^2\mathbf{a}\right) \cdot \mathbf{b}\right) \mathbf{a} - \left(\left(\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^2\mathbf{b} - \left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^2\mathbf{a}\right) \cdot \mathbf{a}\right) \mathbf{b}} {2 \left(\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^{2}\left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^2 - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})^2\right)} + \mathbf{C}$$

which can be simplified to:

$$p_0 = \frac {\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^{2}\left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2}(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}) - (\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}) \left(\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^{2}\mathbf{b} + \left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^{2}\mathbf{a}\right)} {2 \left(\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|^{2}\left\|\mathbf{b}\right\|^2 - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})^2\right)} + \mathbf{C}$$

## Circumcenter coordinates {#Circumcenter_coordinates}

### Cartesian coordinates {#Cartesian_coordinates}

The [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of the circumcenter $U = \left(U_x, U_y\right)$ are

$$\begin{aligned} U_x &= \frac{1}{D}\left[\left(A_x^2 + A_y^2\right)(B_y - C_y) + \left(B_x^2 + B_y^2\right)(C_y - A_y) + \left(C_x^2 + C_y^2\right)(A_y - B_y)\right] \\[5pt] U_y &= \frac{1}{D}\left[\left(A_x^2 + A_y^2\right)(C_x - B_x) + \left(B_x^2 + B_y^2\right)(A_x - C_x) + \left(C_x^2 + C_y^2\right)(B_x - A_x)\right] \end{aligned}$$

with

$$D = 2\left[A_x(B_y - C_y) + B_x(C_y - A_y) + C_x(A_y - B_y)\right].\,$$

[Without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) this can be expressed in a simplified form after translation of the vertex $A$ to the origin of the Cartesian coordinate systems, i.e., when $A' = A-A = (A'_x,A'_y) = (0,0).$ In this case, the coordinates of the vertices $B'=B-A$ and $C'=C-A$ represent the vectors from vertex $A'$ to these vertices. Observe that this trivial translation is possible for all triangles, and the coordinates of the circumcenter $U' = (U'_x, U'_y)$ of the triangle $\triangle A'B'C'$ follow as

$$\begin{aligned} U'_x &= \frac{1}{D'}\left[C'_y({B'_x}^2 + {B'_y}^2) - B'_y({C'_x}^2 + {C'_y}^2)\right], \\[5pt] U'_y &= \frac{1}{D'}\left[B'_x({C'_x}^2 + {C'_y}^2) - C'_x({B'_x}^2 + {B'_y}^2)\right] \end{aligned}$$

with

$$D' = 2(B'_x C'_y - B'_y C'_x). \,$$

Due to the translation of vertex $A$ to the origin, the circumradius $r$ can be computed as

$$r = \|U'\| = \sqrt{{U'_x}^2 + {U'_y}^2}$$

and the actual circumcenter of $\triangle ABC$ follows as

$$U = U' + A$$

### Trilinear coordinates {#Trilinear_coordinates}

The circumcenter has [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790)[^n1]

$$\cos \alpha : \cos \beta : \cos \gamma$$

where $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are the angles of the triangle.

In terms of the side lengths $a, b, c$, the trilinears are[^o19]

$$a\left(b^2 + c^2 - a^2\right) : b\left(c^2 + a^2 - b^2\right) : c\left(a^2 + b^2 - c^2\right).$$

### Barycentric coordinates {#Barycentric_coordinates}

The circumcenter has [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29)

$$a^2\left(b^2 + c^2 - a^2\right):\; b^2\left(c^2 + a^2 - b^2\right):\; c^2\left(a^2 + b^2 - c^2\right),\,$$

where $a, b, c$ are edge lengths $\overline{BC}, \overline{CA}, \overline{AB}$ respectively) of the triangle.

In terms of the triangle's angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$, the barycentric coordinates of the circumcenter are[^o19]

$$\sin 2\alpha :\sin 2\beta :\sin 2\gamma .$$

### Circumcenter vector {#Circumcenter_vector}

Since the Cartesian coordinates of any point are a weighted average of those of the vertices, with the weights being the point's barycentric coordinates normalized to sum to unity, the circumcenter vector can be written as

$$U = \frac {a^2\left(b^2 + c^2 - a^2\right)A + b^2\left(c^2 + a^2 - b^2\right)B + c^2\left(a^2 + b^2 - c^2\right)C} {a^2\left(b^2 + c^2 - a^2\right) + b^2\left(c^2 + a^2 - b^2\right) + c^2\left(a^2 + b^2 - c^2\right)}.$$

Here $U$ is the vector of the circumcenter and $A, B, C$ are the vertex vectors. The divisor here equals $16S^{2}$ where $S$ is the area of the triangle.

### Cartesian coordinates from cross- and dot-products {#Cartesian_coordinates_from_cross-_and_dot-products}

In [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), there is a unique circle passing through any given three non-collinear points $P_{1}, P_{2}, P_{3}$. Using [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) to represent these points as [spatial vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_vector), it is possible to use the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) and [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) to calculate the radius and center of the circle. Let

$$\mathrm{P_1} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ y_1 \\ z_1 \end{bmatrix}, \mathrm{P_2} = \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ y_2 \\ z_2 \end{bmatrix}, \mathrm{P_3} = \begin{bmatrix} x_3 \\ y_3 \\ z_3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Then the radius of the circle is given by

$$\mathrm{r} = \frac {\left|P_1 - P_2\right| \left|P_2 - P_3\right|\left|P_3 - P_1\right|} {2 \left|\left(P_1 - P_2\right) \times \left(P_2 - P_3\right)\right|}$$

The center of the circle is given by the [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632)

$$\mathrm{P_c} = \alpha \, P_1 + \beta \, P_2 + \gamma \, P_3$$

where

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha = \frac {\left|P_2 - P_3\right|^2 \left(P_1 - P_2\right) \cdot \left(P_1 - P_3\right)} {2 \left|\left(P_1 - P_2\right) \times \left(P_2 - P_3\right)\right|^2} \\ \beta = \frac {\left|P_1 - P_3\right|^2 \left(P_2 - P_1\right) \cdot \left(P_2 - P_3\right)} {2 \left|\left(P_1 - P_2\right) \times \left(P_2 - P_3\right)\right|^2} \\ \gamma = \frac {\left|P_1 - P_2\right|^2 \left(P_3 - P_1\right) \cdot \left(P_3 - P_2\right)} {2 \left|\left(P_1 - P_2\right) \times \left(P_2 - P_3\right)\right|^2} \end{aligned}$$

## Triangle centers on the circumcircle {#Triangle_centers_on_the_circumcircle}

In this section, the vertex angles are labeled $A, B, C$ and all coordinates are [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790):

- [Steiner point](wikipedia://en/35857329): the non-vertex point of intersection of the circumcircle with the Steiner ellipse. $$\frac{bc}{b^2 - c^2} : \frac{ca}{c^2 - a^2} : \frac{ab}{a^2 - b^2}$$ (The [Steiner ellipse](wikipedia://en/19106257), with center = centroid ($ABC$), is the ellipse of least area that passes through $A, B, C$. An equation for this ellipse is $\tfrac{1}{ax} + \tfrac{1}{by} + \tfrac{1}{cz} = 0$.)
- [Tarry point](wikipedia://en/14662924): antipode of the Steiner point $$\sec(A + \omega) : \sec(B + \omega) : \sec(C + \omega)$$
- Focus of the [Kiepert parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiepert_parabola): $$\csc(B-C) : \csc(C-A) : \csc(A-B).$$

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

The [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of the circumcircle, called the **circumdiameter** and equal to twice the **circumradius**, can be computed as the length of any side of the triangle divided by the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) of the opposite [angle](wikipedia://en/1196):

$$\text{diameter} = \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}.$$

As a consequence of the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222), it does not matter which side and opposite angle are taken: the result will be the same.

The diameter of the circumcircle can also be expressed as

$$\begin{aligned} \text{diameter} & {}= \frac{abc}{2\cdot\text{area}} = \frac{|AB| |BC| |CA|}{2|\Delta ABC|} \\[5pt] & {}= \frac{abc}{2\sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}}\\[5pt] & {}= \frac{2abc}{\sqrt{(a + b + c)(-a + b + c)(a - b + c)(a + b - c)}} \end{aligned}$$

where $a, b, c$ are the lengths of the sides of the triangle and $s=\tfrac{a+b+c}{2}$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617). The expression $\scriptstyle \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$ above is the area of the triangle, by [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123).[^o25] Trigonometric expressions for the diameter of the circumcircle include[^o26]

$$\text{diameter} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \cdot \text{area}}{\sin A \sin B \sin C}}.$$

The triangle's [nine-point circle](wikipedia://en/161243) has half the diameter of the circumcircle.

In any given triangle, the circumcenter is always collinear with the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) and [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244). The line that passes through all of them is known as the [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622).

The [isogonal conjugate](wikipedia://en/2083100) of the circumcenter is the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244).

The useful [minimum bounding circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallest_circle_problem) of three points is defined either by the circumcircle (where three points are on the minimum bounding circle) or by the two points of the longest side of the triangle (where the two points define a diameter of the circle). It is common to confuse the minimum bounding circle with the circumcircle.

The circumcircle of three [collinear points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear_points) is the line on which the three points lie, often referred to as a *circle of infinite radius*. Nearly collinear points often lead to [numerical instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_instability) in computation of the circumcircle.

Circumcircles of triangles have an intimate relationship with the [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of points.

By [Euler's theorem in geometry](wikipedia://en/3338987), the distance between the circumcenter $O$ and the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) $I$ is

$$\overline{OI} = \sqrt{R(R - 2r)},$$

where $r$ is the incircle radius and $R$ is the circumcircle radius; hence the circumradius is at least twice the inradius ([Euler's triangle inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_inequality)), with equality only in the [equilateral](wikipedia://en/173285) case.[^o27][^o28]

The distance between $O$ and the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) $H$ is[^o29][^o30]

$$\overline{OH} = \sqrt{R^2 - 8R^2\cos A \cos B \cos C} = \sqrt{9R^2 - \left(a^2 + b^2 + c^2\right)}.$$

For [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) $G$ and [nine-point center](wikipedia://en/4517570) $N$ we have

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{IG} &< \overline{IO}, \\ 2\overline{IN} &< \overline{IO}, \\ \overline{OI}^2 &= 2R\cdot \overline{IN}. \end{aligned}$$

The product of the incircle radius and the circumcircle radius of a triangle with sides $a, b, c$ is[^o31]

$$rR = \frac{abc}{2(a + b + c)}.$$

With circumradius $R$, sides $a, b, c$, and [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006) $m_{a}, m_{b}, m_{c}$, we have[^o32]

$$\begin{aligned} 3\sqrt{3}R &\geq a + b + c \\[5pt] 9R^2 &\geq a^2 + b^2 + c^2 \\[5pt] \frac{27}{4}R^2 &\geq m_a^2 + m_b^2 + m_c^2. \end{aligned}$$

If median $m$, altitude $h$, and internal bisector $t$ all emanate from the same vertex of a triangle with circumradius $R$, then[^o33]

$$4R^2 h^2\left(t^2 - h^2\right) = t^4\left(m^2 - h^2\right).$$

[Carnot's theorem](wikipedia://en/309936) states that the sum of the distances from the circumcenter to the three sides equals the sum of the circumradius and the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius).[^n2] Here a segment's length is considered to be negative [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the segment lies entirely outside the triangle.

If a triangle has two particular circles as its circumcircle and [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle), there exist an infinite number of other triangles with the same circumcircle and incircle, with any point on the circumcircle as a vertex. (This is the $n = 3$ case of [Poncelet's porism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poncelet's_porism)). A necessary and sufficient condition for such triangles to exist is the above equality $\overline{OI}=\sqrt{R(R-2r)}.$[^n3]

## Cyclic polygons {#Cyclic_polygons}

*Image: Cyclic quadrilateral.svg.* [Cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131)

A set of points lying on the same circle are called *[concyclic](wikipedia://en/504404)*, and a polygon whose vertices are concyclic is called a *[cyclic polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon)*. Every triangle is concyclic, but polygons with more than three sides are not in general.

Cyclic polygons, especially four-sided [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131), have various special properties. In particular, the opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are [supplementary angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_angles) (adding up to 180° or $\pi$ radians).
