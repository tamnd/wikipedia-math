# Volume element

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Volume element"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **volume element** provides a means for [integrating](wikipedia://en/15532) a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) with respect to [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) in various coordinate systems such as [spherical coordinates](wikipedia://en/29181) and [cylindrical coordinates](wikipedia://en/176733). Thus a volume element is an expression of the form

$$\mathrm{d}V = \rho(u_1,u_2,u_3)\,\mathrm{d}u_1\,\mathrm{d}u_2\,\mathrm{d}u_3$$

where the $u_i$ are the coordinates, so that the volume of any set $B$ can be computed by

$$\operatorname{Volume}(B) = \int_B \rho(u_1,u_2,u_3)\,\mathrm{d}u_1\,\mathrm{d}u_2\,\mathrm{d}u_3.$$

For example, in spherical coordinates $\mathrm{d}V = u_1^2\sin u_2\,\mathrm{d}u_1\,\mathrm{d}u_2\,\mathrm{d}u_3$, and so $\rho = u_1^2\sin u_2$.

:::

The notion of a volume element is not limited to three dimensions: in two dimensions it is often known as the **area element**, and in this setting it is useful for doing [surface integrals](wikipedia://en/528867). Under changes of coordinates, the volume element changes by the absolute value of the [Jacobian determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_determinant) of the coordinate transformation (by the [change of variables formula](wikipedia://en/193748#Substitution_for_multiple_variables)). This fact allows volume elements to be defined as a kind of [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). On an [orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219), a volume element typically arises from a [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811): a top degree [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519). On a non-orientable manifold, the volume element is typically the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of a (locally defined) volume form: it defines a [1-density](wikipedia://en/3428935).

## Volume element in Euclidean space {#Volume_element_in_Euclidean_space}

In [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the volume element is given by the product of the differentials of the [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706)

$$\mathrm{d}V = \mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z.$$

In different coordinate systems of the form $x=x(u_1,u_2,u_3)$, $y=y(u_1,u_2,u_3)$, $z=z(u_1,u_2,u_3)$, the volume element changes by the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the [Jacobian determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_determinant) of the coordinate change:

$$\mathrm{d}V = \left|\frac{\partial (x,y,z)}{\partial (u_1,u_2,u_3)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}u_1\,\mathrm{d}u_2\,\mathrm{d}u_3.$$

For example, in [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) (mathematical convention)

$$\begin{aligned} x &= \rho \cos\theta \sin\phi\\ y &= \rho \sin\theta \sin\phi\\ z &= \rho \cos\phi \end{aligned}$$

the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant is

$$\left |\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial (\rho,\phi,\theta)}\right| = \rho^2|\sin\phi|$$

so that

$$\mathrm{d}V = \rho^2|\sin\phi|\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi.$$

This can be seen as a special case of the fact that differential forms transform through a pullback $F^*$ as

$$F^*(u \; dy^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dy^n) = (u \circ F) \det \left(\frac{\partial F^j}{\partial x^i}\right) \mathrm{d}x^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \mathrm{d}x^n$$

## Volume element of a linear subspace {#Volume_element_of_a_linear_subspace}

Consider the [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of the *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>*n*</sup> that is spanned by a collection of [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) vectors

$$X_1,\dots,X_k.$$

To find the volume element of the subspace, it is useful to know the fact from [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) that the volume of the [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975) spanned by the $X_i$ is the square root of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [Gramian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gramian_matrix) of the $X_i$:

$$\sqrt{\det(X_i\cdot X_j)_{i,j=1\dots k}}.$$

Any point *p* in the subspace can be given coordinates $(u_1,u_2,\dots,u_k)$ such that

$$p = u_1X_1 + \cdots + u_kX_k.$$

At a point *p*, if we form a small parallelepiped with sides $\mathrm{d}u_i$, then the volume of that parallelepiped is the square root of the determinant of the Grammian matrix

$$\sqrt{\det\left((du_i X_i)\cdot (du_j X_j)\right)_{i,j=1\dots k}} = \sqrt{\det(X_i\cdot X_j)_{i,j=1\dots k}}\; \mathrm{d}u_1\,\mathrm{d}u_2\,\cdots\,\mathrm{d}u_k.$$

This therefore defines the volume form in the linear subspace.

## Volume element of manifolds {#Volume_element_of_manifolds}

On an *oriented* [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) of dimension *n*, the volume element is a volume form equal to the [Hodge dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_dual) of the unit [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861), $f(x) = 1$:

$$\omega = \star 1 .$$

Equivalently, the volume element is precisely the [Levi-Civita tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi-Civita_tensor) $\epsilon$.[^o5] In coordinates,

$$\omega = \epsilon =\sqrt{\left|\det g\right|}\, \mathrm{d}x^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \mathrm{d}x^n$$

where $\det g$ is the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) *g* written in the coordinate system.

### Area element of a surface {#Area_element_of_a_surface}

A simple example of a volume element can be explored by considering a two-dimensional surface embedded in *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). Such a volume element is sometimes called an *area element*. Consider a subset $U \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ and a mapping function

$$\varphi:U\to \mathbb{R}^n$$

thus defining a surface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^n$. In two dimensions, volume is just area, and a volume element gives a way to determine the area of parts of the surface. Thus a volume element is an expression of the form

$$f(u_1,u_2)\,\mathrm{d}u_1\,\mathrm{d}u_2$$

that allows one to compute the area of a set *B* lying on the surface by computing the integral

$$\operatorname{Area}(B) = \int_B f(u_1,u_2)\,\mathrm{d}u_1\,\mathrm{d}u_2.$$

Here we will find the volume element on the surface that defines area in the usual sense. The [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) of the mapping is

$$J_{ij} = \frac{\partial \varphi_i} {\partial u_j}$$

with index *i* running from 1 to *n*, and *j* running from 1 to 2. The Euclidean [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) in the *n*-dimensional space induces a metric $g = J^T J$ on the set *U*, with matrix elements

$$g_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n J_{ki} J_{kj} = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{\partial \varphi_k} {\partial u_i} \frac{\partial \varphi_k} {\partial u_j}.$$

The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the metric is given by

$$\det g = \left| \frac{\partial \varphi} {\partial u_1} \wedge \frac{\partial \varphi} {\partial u_2} \right|^2 = \det (J^T J)$$

For a regular surface, this determinant is non-vanishing; equivalently, the Jacobian matrix has rank 2.

Now consider a change of coordinates on *U*, given by a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564)

$$f \colon U\to U ,$$

so that the coordinates $(u_1, u_2)$ are given in terms of $(v_1,v_2)$ by $(u_1,u_2) = f(v_1,v_2)$. The Jacobian matrix of this transformation is given by

$$F_{ij}= \frac{\partial f_i} {\partial v_j}.$$

In the new coordinates, we have

$$\frac{\partial \varphi_i} {\partial v_j} = \sum_{k=1}^2 \frac{\partial \varphi_i} {\partial u_k} \frac{\partial f_k} {\partial v_j}$$

and so the metric transforms as

$$\tilde{g} = F^T g F$$

where $\tilde{g}$ is the pullback metric in the *v* coordinate system. The determinant is

$$\det \tilde{g} = \det g \left( \det F \right)^2.$$

Given the above construction, it should now be straightforward to understand how the volume element is invariant under an orientation-preserving change of coordinates.

In two dimensions, the volume is just the area. The area of a subset $B\subset U$ is given by the integral

$$\begin{aligned} \mbox{Area}(B) &= \iint_B \sqrt{\det g}\; \mathrm{d}u_1\; \mathrm{d}u_2 \\[1.6ex] &= \iint_B \sqrt{\det g} \left|\det F\right| \;\mathrm{d}v_1 \;\mathrm{d}v_2 \\[1.6ex] &= \iint_B \sqrt{\det \tilde{g}} \;\mathrm{d}v_1 \;\mathrm{d}v_2. \end{aligned}$$

Thus, in either coordinate system, the volume element takes the same expression: the expression of the volume element is invariant under a change of coordinates.

Note that there was nothing particular to two dimensions in the above presentation; the above trivially generalizes to arbitrary dimensions.

:::

### Example: Sphere {#Example:_Sphere}

For example, consider the sphere with radius *r* centered at the origin in **R**<sup>3</sup>. This can be parametrized using [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) with the map

$$\phi(u_1,u_2) = (r \cos u_1 \sin u_2, r \sin u_1 \sin u_2, r \cos u_2).$$

Then

$$g = \begin{pmatrix} r^2\sin^2u_2 & 0 \\ 0 & r^2 \end{pmatrix},$$

and the area element is

$$\omega = \sqrt{\det g}\; \mathrm{d}u_1 \mathrm{d}u_2 = r^2\sin u_2\, \mathrm{d}u_1 \mathrm{d}u_2.$$
