# Fano factor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fano factor"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Fano factor**,[^o2] like the [coefficient of variation](wikipedia://en/1012687), is a measure of the [dispersion](wikipedia://en/23773247) of a [counting process](wikipedia://en/20814853). It was originally used to measure the [Fano noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fano_noise) in ion detectors. It is named after [Ugo Fano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugo_Fano), an Italian-American physicist.

:::

The Fano factor after a time $t$ is defined as

$$F(t)=\frac{\sigma_t^2}{\mu_t},$$

where $\sigma_t$ is the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) and $\mu_t$ is the mean number of events of a [counting process](wikipedia://en/20814853) after some time $t$. The Fano factor can be viewed as a kind of noise-to-signal ratio; it is a measure of the reliability with which the waiting time [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) can be estimated after several [random events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_event).

For a [Poisson counting process](wikipedia://en/41597450), the variance in the count equals the mean count, so $F=1$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For a counting process $N_t$, the Fano factor after a time $t>0$ is defined as,

$$F(t)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(N_t)}{\operatorname{E}[N_t]}.$$

Sometimes, the long-term limit is also termed the Fano factor,

$$F=\lim_{t\to\infty}F(t).$$

For a renewal process with holding times distributed similar to a random variable $S$, we have that,

$F = \lim_{t\to\infty} F(t) = \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{\operatorname{Var}(N_t)}{\operatorname{E}[N_t]} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(S)}{\operatorname{E}[S]^2}.$[^o5]

Since we have that the right-hand side is equal to the square of the coefficient of variation $c_v^2=\operatorname{Var}(S)/\operatorname{E}[S]^2$, the right-hand side of this equation is sometimes referred to as the Fano factor as well.[^o6]

:::

## Interpretation {#Interpretation}

When considered as the dispersion of the number, the Fano factor $F$ roughly corresponds to the width of the peak of $N_t$. As such, the Fano factor is often interpreted as the unpredictability of the underlying process.

### Example: Constant Random Variable {#Example:_Constant_Random_Variable}

When the holding times are constant, then $F=0$. As such, if $F\approx0$ then we interpret the renewal process as being very predictable.

### Example: Poisson Counting Process {#Example:_Poisson_Counting_Process}

When the likelihood of an event occurring in any time interval is equal for all time, then the holding times must be exponentially distributed, giving a [Poisson counting process](wikipedia://en/41597450), for which $F=1$.

## Use in particle detection {#Use_in_particle_detection}

In [particle detectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_detector), the Fano factor results from the energy loss in a collision not being purely statistical. The process giving rise to each individual [charge carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_carrier) is not independent as the number of ways an atom may be ionized is limited by the discrete [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) shells. The net result is a better energy resolution than predicted by purely statistical considerations. For example, if *w* is the average energy for a particle to produce a charge carrier in a detector, then the relative [FWHM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_width_at_half_maximum) resolution for measuring the particle energy *E* is:[^o11]

$$R = \frac{\mathrm{FWHM}}{\mu} = 2.35 \sqrt{\frac{F w}{E}},$$

where the factor of 2.35 relates the standard deviation to the FWHM.

The Fano factor is material-specific. Its value shows an empirical correlation with $W/I$ for gases, $F=0.188 W/I - 0.15$, where W is the mean energy required to form an ionisation pair and I is the [ionisation potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionisation_potential).[^o12] Some theoretical F values are:[^o13]

\|- | Si: || 0.115 *(note discrepancy to experimental value)* |- | Ge: || 0.13 [^o14] |- |GaAs: || 0.12 [^o15] |- |Diamond: || 0.08 |}

Measuring the Fano factor is difficult because many factors contribute to the resolution, but some experimental values are:

## Use in neuroscience {#Use_in_neuroscience}

The Fano factor is used in [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience) to describe variability in neural spiking. [^o21] In this context, the events are the neural spiking events and the holding times are the Inter-Spike Intervals (ISI). Often, the limit definition of the Fano factor is used, for which,

$$F = \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{\operatorname{Var}(N_t)}{\operatorname{E}[N_t]} = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(ISI)}{(\operatorname{E}[ISI])^2} = CV^2,$$

where $CV$ is the coefficient of variation of ISI.

Some neurons are found to have varying ISI distributions, meaning that the counting process is no longer a renewal process. Rather, a [Markov renewal process](wikipedia://en/5446890) is used. In the case that we have only two Markov states with equal transition probabilities $p$, we have that the limit above again converges,[^o22] $F= 2\frac{CV_2^2\mu_1^2 + CV_1^2\mu_2^2}{(\mu_1 + \mu_2)^2} + \left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)\frac{(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{(\mu_1+\mu_2)^2},$ where $\mu$ represents the mean for the ISI of the corresponding state.

While most work assumes a constant Fano factor, recent work has considered neurons with non-constant Fano factors.[^o23] In this case, it is found that non-constant Fano factors can be achieved by introducing both noise and non-linearity to the rate of the underlying Poisson process.
