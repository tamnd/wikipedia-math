# Domino (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Domino"}

*Image: Domino green.svg.* The single free domino

In mathematics, a **domino** is a [polyomino](wikipedia://en/72634) of order 2, that is, a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) in the [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) made of two equal-sized [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) connected edge-to-edge,[^o2] and a 1x2 rectangle. [^o3] When [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) and [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829) are not considered to be distinct shapes, there is only one [*free*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_polyomino) domino.

:::

Since it has [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873), it is also the only *one-sided* domino (with reflections considered distinct). When rotations are also considered distinct, there are two *fixed* dominoes: The second one can be created by rotating the one above by 90°.[^o4][^o5]

In a wider sense, the term *domino* is sometimes understood to mean a [tile](wikipedia://en/321671) of any shape.[^o6]

## Packing and tiling {#Packing_and_tiling}

Dominos can tile the plane in a countably infinite number of ways. The number of tilings of a 2×*n* rectangle with dominoes is $F_n$, the *n*th [Fibonacci number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number).[^o8]

Domino tilings figure in several celebrated problems, including the [Aztec diamond](wikipedia://en/20282540) problem in which large diamond-shaped regions have a number of tilings equal to a [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948),[^o9] with most tilings appearing random within a central circular region and having a more regular structure outside of this "arctic circle", and the [mutilated chessboard problem](wikipedia://en/9308202), in which removing two opposite corners from a [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) makes it impossible to tile with dominoes.[^o10]
