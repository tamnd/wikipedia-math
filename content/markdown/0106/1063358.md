# SEED

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="SEED"}

**SEED** is a [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594) developed by the [Korea Information Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea_Internet_&_Security_Agency) (KISA). It is used broadly throughout [South Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korea) industry, but seldom found elsewhere. It gained popularity in Korea because [40-bit encryption](wikipedia://en/487403) was not considered strong enough, so the Korea Information Security Agency developed its own standard. However, this decision has historically limited the competition of [web browsers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Web_browser) in Korea, as no major SSL libraries or web browsers supported the SEED algorithm, requiring users to use an [ActiveX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ActiveX) control in [Internet Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Explorer) for secure web sites.[^o4]

:::

On April 1, 2015 the [Ministry of Science, ICT and Future Planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Science,_ICT_and_Future_Planning) (MSIP) announced its plan to remove the ActiveX dependency from at least 90 percent of the country's top 100 websites by 2017. Instead, [HTML5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTML5)-based technologies will be employed as they operate on many platforms, including mobile devices. Starting with the private sector, the ministry plans to expand this further to ultimately remove this dependency from public websites as well.[^o5]

## Design {#Design}

SEED is a 16-round [Feistel network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feistel_network) with [128-bit blocks](wikipedia://en/294065) and a [128-bit key](wikipedia://en/5749). It uses two 8 × 8 [S-boxes](wikipedia://en/240991) which, like those of [SAFER](wikipedia://en/630497), are derived from discrete [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) (in this case, x<sup>247</sup> and x<sup>251</sup> – plus some "incompatible operations"). It also has some resemblance to [MISTY1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MISTY1) in the recursiveness of its structure: the 128-bit full cipher is a Feistel network with an F-function operating on 64-bit halves, while the F-function itself is a Feistel network composed of a G-function operating on 32-bit halves. However the recursion does not extend further because the G-function is not a Feistel network. In the G-function, the 32-bit word is considered as four 8-bit bytes, each of which is passed through one or the other of the S-boxes, then combined in a moderately complex set of [boolean functions](wikipedia://en/753349) such that each output bit depends on 3 of the 4 input bytes.

SEED has a fairly complex [key schedule](wikipedia://en/609603), generating its thirty-two 32-bit subkeys through application of its G-function on a series of rotations of the raw key, combined with round constants derived (as in [TEA](wikipedia://en/528720)) from the [Golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).

## Use and adoption {#Use_and_adoption}

SEED has been adopted by several standard protocols: [S/MIME](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S/MIME) (RFC 4010), [TLS/SSL](wikipedia://en/187813) (RFC 4162), [IPSec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPSec) (RFC 4196), and ISO/IEC 18033-3:2010.

[NSS](wikipedia://en/5137674) software security library in Mozilla's Gecko platform has implemented support for SEED,[^o8] and [Mozilla Firefox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozilla_Firefox) as of 3.5.4 supports SEED as a TLS cipher;[^o9] however, Mozilla decided to drop the support of SEED by default in Firefox 27 and above because support for SEED has not had any practical positive effect in terms of helping South Korea migrate away from ActiveX-based e-commerce, and other browsers are not offering any SEED-based cipher suites.[^o10][^o11] NSS still supports SEED-based cipher suites.

The [Linux kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux_kernel) has supported SEED since 2007.[^o12]

Bloombase supports SEED in their full suite of data cryptography solutions.[^o13]
