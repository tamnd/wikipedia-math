# Characteristic (algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Characteristic"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **characteristic** of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$, often denoted $\operatorname{char}(R)$, is defined to be the smallest positive number of copies of the ring's [multiplicative identity](wikipedia://en/14962) ($1$) that will sum to the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225) ($0$). If no such number exists, the ring is said to have characteristic zero.

:::

That is, $\operatorname{char}(R)$ is the smallest positive number $n$ such that[^o3]p. 198, Thm. 23.14

$$\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{n \text{ summands}} = 0$$

if such a number $n$ exists, and $0$ otherwise.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

The special definition of the characteristic zero is motivated by the equivalent definitions characterized in the next section, where the characteristic zero is not required to be considered separately.

The characteristic may also be taken to be the [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent_%28group_theory%29) of the ring's [additive group](wikipedia://en/332229), that is, the smallest positive integer $n$ such that:[^o3]p. 198, Def. 23.12

$$\underbrace{a+\cdots+a}_{n \text{ summands}} = 0$$

for every element $a$ of the ring (again, if $n$ exists; otherwise zero). This definition is equivalent for a ring, because of [distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity). For [rngs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rng_%28mathematics%29) (rings without identity), the former definition is nonsensical, and the latter definition is generally used.

[Integers](wikipedia://en/14563), [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) and [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) have characteristic 0.

The [integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/20087#integers_modulo_m) have characteristic $n$.

Every [Boolean ring](wikipedia://en/54356) has characteristic 2.

The characteristic of a field is either 0 or a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666).

## Equivalent characterizations {#Equivalent_characterizations}

- The characteristic of a ring $R$ is the [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$ such that $n\mathbb{Z}$ is the [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28ring_theory%29) of the unique [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $R$.
- The characteristic is the [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$ such that $R$ contains a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/26411) to the [factor ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_ring) $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, which is the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of the above homomorphism.
- When the non-negative integers $\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$ are [partially ordered](wikipedia://en/23572) by divisibility, then $1$ is the smallest and $0$ is the largest. Then the characteristic of a ring is the smallest value of $n$ for which $n\cdot 1 = 0$. If nothing "smaller" (in this ordering) than $0$ will suffice, then the characteristic is $0$. This is the appropriate partial ordering because of such facts as that $\operatorname{char}(A\times B)$ is the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) of $\operatorname{char}(A)$ and $\operatorname{char}(B)$, and that no ring homomorphism $f:A\to B$ exists unless $\operatorname{char}(B)$ divides $\operatorname{char}(A)$.
- The characteristic of a ring $R$ is $n$ precisely if the statement $ka=0$ for all $a\in R$ holds if an only if $k$ is a multiple of $n$.

## Case of rings {#Case_of_rings}

If $R$ and $S$ are [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) and there exists a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) $R \to S$, then the characteristic of $S$ divides the characteristic of $R$. This can sometimes be used to exclude the possibility of certain ring homomorphisms. The only ring with characteristic $1$ is the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874), which has only a single element $0$. If a nontrivial ring $R$ does not have any nontrivial [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441), then its characteristic is either $0$ or [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). In particular, this applies to all [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), to all [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462), and to all [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067). Any ring of characteristic zero is infinite.

The ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ of integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $n$ has characteristic $n$. If $R$ is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of $S$, then $R$ and $S$ have the same characteristic. For example, if $p$ is prime and $q(X)$ is an [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) with coefficients in the field $\mathbb F_p$ with $p$ elements, then the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $\mathbb F_p[X]/(q(X))$ is a field of characteristic $p$. Another example: The field $\mathbb{C}$ of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) contains $\mathbb{Z}$, so the characteristic of $\mathbb{C}$ is $0$.

A $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$-algebra is equivalently a ring whose characteristic divides $n$. This is because for every ring $R$ there is a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to R$, and this map factors through $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ if and only if the characteristic of $R$ divides $n$. In this case for any $r$ in the ring, then adding $r$ to itself $n$ times gives $nr = 0$.

If a commutative ring $R$ has *prime characteristic* $p$, then we have $(x + y)^{p} = x^{p} + y^{p}$ for all elements $x$ and $y$ in $R$ – the normally incorrect "[freshman's dream](wikipedia://en/17373539)" holds for power $p$. The map $x \mapsto x^{p}$ then defines a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) $R \to R$, which is called the *[Frobenius homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_homomorphism)*. If $R$ is also an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), the homomorphism is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective).

## Case of fields {#Case_of_fields}

As mentioned above, the characteristic of any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is either $0$ or a prime number. A field of non-zero characteristic is called a field of *finite characteristic* or *positive characteristic* or *prime characteristic*. The *characteristic exponent* is defined similarly, except that it is equal to $1$ when the characteristic is $0$; otherwise it has the same value as the characteristic.[^o8]

Any field $F$ has a unique minimal [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634), also called its [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field). This subfield is isomorphic to either the [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) field $\mathbb{Q}$ or a finite field $\mathbb F_p$ of prime order. Two prime fields of the same characteristic are isomorphic, and this isomorphism is unique. In other words, there is essentially a unique prime field in each characteristic.

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Field of characteristic zero"}

### Fields of characteristic zero {#Fields_of_characteristic_zero}

The fields of *characteristic zero* are those that have a subfield isomorphic to the field $\mathbb{Q}$ of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). The most common of such fields are the subfields of the field $\mathbb{C}$ of the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826); this includes the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$ and all [algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822).

Other fields of characteristic zero are the [p-adic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_field) that are widely used in number theory.

Fields of [rational fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction) over the integers or a field of characteristic zero are other common examples.

[Ordered fields](wikipedia://en/22430) always have characteristic zero; they include $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}.$

:::

### Fields of prime characteristic {#Fields_of_prime_characteristic}

The [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ has characteristic $p$.

There exist infinite fields of prime characteristic. For example, the field of all [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) over $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ or the field of [formal Laurent series](wikipedia://en/60012) $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}((T))$.

The size of any [finite ring](wikipedia://en/23706953) of prime characteristic $p$ is a power of $p$. Since in that case it contains $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ it is also a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over that field, and from [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) we know that the sizes of finite vector spaces over finite fields are a power of the size of the field. This also shows that the size of any finite vector space is a prime power.

:::
