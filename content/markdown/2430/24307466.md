# Harmonic progression (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Harmonic progression"}

*Image: First members of harmonic sequence.svg.* The first ten members of the harmonic sequence $a_n = \tfrac{1}{n}$.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **harmonic progression** (or **harmonic sequence**) is a [progression](wikipedia://en/27838) formed by taking the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of an [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389), which is also known as an arithmetic sequence.

:::

Equivalently, a **sequence** is a **harmonic progression** when each term is the [harmonic mean](wikipedia://en/14463) of the neighboring terms.

As a third equivalent characterization, it is an infinite sequence of the form

$$\frac{1}{a},\ \frac{1}{a+d},\ \frac{1}{a+2d},\ \frac{1}{a+3d}, \cdots,$$

where *a* is not zero and −*a*/*d* is not a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474), or a finite sequence of the form

$$\frac{1}{a},\ \frac{1}{a+d},\ \frac{1}{a+2d},\ \frac{1}{a+3d}, \cdots,\ \frac{1}{a+kd},$$

where *a* is not zero, *k* is a natural number and −*a*/*d* is not a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) or is greater than *k*.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In the following $n$ is a natural number, in sequence: $\ n = 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ \ldots\$

- $1, \tfrac{\ 1\ }{2},\ \tfrac{\ 1\ }{3},\ \tfrac{\ 1\ }{4},\ \tfrac{\ 1\ }{5},\ \tfrac{\ 1\ }{6},\ \ldots \ , \ \tfrac{\ 1\ }{n},\ \ldots \$ is called the *harmonic sequence*
- 12, 6, 4, 3, $\ \tfrac{12}{\ 5\ },\ 2,\ \ldots\ ,\ \tfrac{12}{\ n\ },\ \ldots\$
- 30, −30, −10, −6, $\ -\tfrac{30}{\ 7\ },\ \ldots\ ,\ \tfrac{30}{\ \left( 3\ -\ 2 n \right)\ },\ \ldots\$
- 10, 30, −30, −10, −6, $\ \ldots\ ,\ \tfrac{30}{\ \left( 5\ -\ 2 n \right)\ },\ \ldots\$

:::

## Sums of harmonic progressions {#Sums_of_harmonic_progressions}

Infinite harmonic progressions are not [summable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summable) (sum to infinity).

It is not possible for a harmonic progression of distinct [unit fractions](wikipedia://en/491316) (other than the trivial case where *a* = 1 and *k* = 0) to sum to an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). The reason is that, necessarily, at least one denominator of the progression will be [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisibility) by a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) that does not divide any other denominator.[^o5]

## Use in geometry {#Use_in_geometry}

If [collinear points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear_points) A, B, C, and D are such that D is the [harmonic conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_harmonic_conjugates) of C with respect to A and B, then the distances from any one of these points to the three remaining points form harmonic progression.[^o7][^o8] Specifically, each of the sequences AC, AB, AD; BC, BA, BD; CA, CD, CB; and DA, DC, DB are harmonic progressions, where each of the distances is signed according to a fixed orientation of the line.

In a triangle, if the [altitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_%28geometry%29) are in [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389), then the sides are in harmonic progression.

## Leaning Tower of Lire {#Leaning_Tower_of_Lire}

An excellent example of Harmonic Progression is the [Leaning Tower of Lire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaning_Tower_of_Lire). In it, uniform blocks are stacked on top of each other to achieve the maximum sideways or lateral distance covered. The blocks are stacked 1/2, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8, 1/10, … distance sideways below the original block. This ensures that the center of gravity is just at the center of the structure so that it does not collapse. A slight increase in weight on the structure causes it to become unstable and fall.
