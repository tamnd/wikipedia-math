# Zero divisor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zero divisor"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) $a$ of a [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29) $R$ is called a **left zero divisor** if there exists a nonzero $x$ in $R$ such that $ax = 0$,[^o2] or equivalently if the [map](wikipedia://en/185427) from $R$ to $R$ that sends $x$ to $ax$ is not [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective). Similarly, an element $a$ of a ring is called a **right zero divisor** if there exists a nonzero $y$ in $R$ such that $ya = 0$. This is a partial case of [divisibility in rings](wikipedia://en/32981716). An element that is a left or a right zero divisor is simply called a **zero divisor**.[^o3] An element $a$ that is both a left and a right zero divisor is called a **two-sided zero divisor** (the nonzero $x$ such that $ax = 0$ may be different from the nonzero $y$ such that $ya = 0$). If the ring is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346), then the left and right zero divisors are the same.

:::

An element of a ring that is not a left zero divisor (respectively, not a right zero divisor) is called **left regular** or **left cancellable** (respectively, **right regular** or **right cancellable**). An element of a ring that is left and right cancellable, and is hence not a zero divisor, is called **regular** or **cancellable**, or a **non-zero-divisor**. (*N.B.:* In "non-zero-divisor", the prefix "non-" is understood to modify "zero-divisor" as a whole rather than just the word "zero". In some texts, "zero divisor" is written as "zerodivisor" and "non-zero-divisor" as "nonzerodivisor"[^o4] or "non-zerodivisor"[^o5] for clarity). A zero divisor that is nonzero is called a **nonzero zero divisor** or a **nontrivial zero divisor**. A non-[zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) with no nontrivial zero divisors is called a [domain](wikipedia://en/487627).

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) [$\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$](wikipedia://en/20087), the residue class $\overline{2}$ is a zero divisor since $\overline{2} \times \overline{2}=\overline{4}=\overline{0}$.
- The only zero divisor of the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties) is $0$.
- A [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) element of a nonzero ring is always a two-sided zero divisor.
- An [idempotent element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent_element_%28ring_theory%29) $e\ne 1$ of a ring is always a two-sided zero divisor, since $e(1-e)=0=(1-e)e$.
- The [ring of *n* × *n* matrices](wikipedia://en/487541) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) has nonzero zero divisors if *n* ≥ 2. Examples of zero divisors in the ring of 2 × 2 [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) (over any nonzero ring) are shown here:

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} ,$$

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

- A [direct product](wikipedia://en/25063) of two or more nonzero rings always has nonzero zero divisors. For example, in $R_1 \times R_2$ with each $R_i$ nonzero, $(1,0)(0,1) = (0,0)$, so $(1,0)$ is a zero divisor.
- Let $K$ be a field and $G$ be a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). Suppose that $G$ has an element $g$ of finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527) $n > 1$. Then in the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) $K[G]$ one has $(1-g)(1+g+ \cdots +g^{n-1})=1-g^{n}=0$, with neither factor being zero, so $1-g$ is a nonzero zero divisor in $K[G]$.

### One-sided zero-divisor {#One-sided_zero-divisor}

- Consider the ring of (formal) matrices $\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\0&z\end{pmatrix}$ with $x,z\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $y\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Then $\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\0&z\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&c\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}xa&xb+yc\\0&zc\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\0&z\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}xa&ya+zb\\0&zc\end{pmatrix}$. If $x\ne0\ne z$, then $\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\0&z\end{pmatrix}$ is a left zero divisor [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $x$ is [even](wikipedia://en/143135), since $\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\0&z\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&x\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, and it is a right zero divisor if and only if $z$ is even for similar reasons. If either of $x,z$ is $0$, then it is a two-sided zero-divisor.
- Here is another example of a ring with an element that is a zero divisor on one side only. Let $S$ be the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) of integers $(a_1,a_2,a_3,...)$. Take for the ring all [additive maps](wikipedia://en/22473529) from $S$ to $S$, with [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) addition and [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) as the ring operations. (That is, our ring is $\mathrm{End}(S)$, the *[endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623)* of the additive group $S$.) Three examples of elements of this ring are the **right shift** $R(a_1,a_2,a_3,...)=(0,a_1,a_2,...)$, the **left shift** $L(a_1,a_2,a_3,...)=(a_2,a_3,a_4,...)$, and the **projection map** onto the first factor $P(a_1,a_2,a_3,...)=(a_1,0,0,...)$. All three of these additive maps are not zero, and the composites $LP$ and $PR$ are both zero, so $L$ is a left zero divisor and $R$ is a right zero divisor in the ring of additive maps from $S$ to $S$. However, $L$ is not a right zero divisor and $R$ is not a left zero divisor: the composite $LR$ is the identity. $RL$ is a two-sided zero-divisor since $RLP=0=PRL$, while $LR=1$ is not in any direction.

:::

## Non-examples {#Non-examples}

- The ring of integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) has no nonzero zero divisors. Since every nonzero element is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666), this ring is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).
- More generally, a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) has no nonzero zero divisors.
- A nonzero commutative ring whose only zero divisor is 0 is called an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- In the ring of $n$ × $n$ matrices over a field, the left and right zero divisors coincide; they are precisely the [singular matrices](wikipedia://en/245528). In the ring of $n$ × $n$ matrices over an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), the zero divisors are precisely the matrices with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) zero.
- Left or right zero divisors can never be [units](wikipedia://en/289666), because if $a$ is invertible and $ax = 0$ for some nonzero $x$, then $0 = a^{-1}0 = a^{-1}ax = x$, a contradiction.
- An element is [cancellable](wikipedia://en/490646) on the side on which it is regular. That is, if $a$ is a left regular, $ax = ay$ implies that $x = y$, and similarly for right regular.

:::

## Zero as a zero divisor {#Zero_as_a_zero_divisor}

There is no need for a separate convention for the case $a = 0$, because the definition applies also in this case:

- If $R$ is a ring other than the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874), then $0$ is a (two-sided) zero divisor, because any nonzero element $x$ satisfies $0x = 0 = x\,0$.
- If $R$ is the zero ring, in which $0 = 1$, then $0$ is not a zero divisor, because there is no *nonzero* element that when multiplied by $0$ yields $0$.

Some references include or exclude $0$ as a zero divisor in *all* rings by convention, but they then suffer from having to introduce exceptions in statements such as the following:

- In a commutative ring $R$, the set of non-zero-divisors is a [multiplicative set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_set) in $R$. (This, in turn, is important for the definition of the [total quotient ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_quotient_ring).) The same is true of the set of non-left-zero-divisors and the set of non-right-zero-divisors in an arbitrary ring, commutative or not.
- In a commutative [noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) $R$, the set of zero divisors is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the [associated prime ideals](wikipedia://en/17286442) of $R$.

## Zero divisor on a module {#Zero_divisor_on_a_module}

Let $R$ be a commutative ring, let $M$ be an $R$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410), and let $a$ be an element of $R$. One says that $a$ is **$M$-regular** if the "multiplication by $a$" map $M \,\stackrel{a}\to\, M$ is injective, and that $a$ is a **zero divisor on $M$** otherwise.[^o15] The set of $M$-regular elements is a [multiplicative set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_set) in $R$.[^o15]

Specializing the definitions of "$M$-regular" and "zero divisor on $M$" to the case $M = R$ recovers the definitions of "regular" and "zero divisor" given earlier in this article.
