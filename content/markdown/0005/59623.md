# Endomorphism ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Endomorphism ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) *X* form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). This ring is called the **endomorphism ring** of *X*, denoted by End(*X*); the set of all [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) of *X* into itself. Addition of endomorphisms arises naturally in a [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797#Pointwise_operations) manner and multiplication via [endomorphism composition](wikipedia://en/195947). Using these operations, the set of endomorphisms of an abelian group forms a (unital) ring, with the [zero map](wikipedia://en/320638) $0: x \mapsto 0$ as [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225) and the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) $1: x \mapsto x$ as [multiplicative identity](wikipedia://en/14962).[^n1][^n2][^n3]

:::

The functions involved are restricted to what is defined as a homomorphism in the context, which depends upon the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of the object under consideration. The endomorphism ring consequently encodes several internal properties of the object. As the endomorphism ring is often an [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_%28ring_theory%29) over some ring *R,* this may also be called the **endomorphism algebra**.

An abelian group is the same thing as a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), which is the [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object) in the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181). In a similar fashion, if *R* is any [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the endomorphisms of an *R*-module form an [algebra over *R*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring) by the same axioms and derivation. In particular, if *R* is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), its modules *M* are [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) and the endomorphism ring of each is an [algebra over the field](wikipedia://en/191788) *R*.

## Description {#Description}

Let (*A*, +) be an abelian group and we consider the group homomorphisms from *A* into *A*. Then addition of two such homomorphisms may be defined pointwise to produce another group homomorphism. Explicitly, given two such homomorphisms *f* and *g*, the sum of *f* and *g* is the homomorphism *f* + *g* : *x* ↦ *f*(*x*) + *g*(*x*). Under this operation End(*A*) is an abelian group. With the additional operation of composition of homomorphisms, End(*A*) is a ring with multiplicative identity. This composition is explicitly *fg* : *x* ↦ *f*(*g*(*x*)). The multiplicative identity is the identity homomorphism on *A*. The additive inverses are the pointwise inverses.

If the set *A* does not form an *abelian* group, then the above construction is not necessarily well-defined, as then the sum of two homomorphisms need not be a homomorphism.[^n4] However, the closure of the set of endomorphisms under the above operations is a canonical example of a [near-ring](wikipedia://en/839900) that is not a ring.

## Properties {#Properties}

- Endomorphism rings always have additive and multiplicative [identities](wikipedia://en/14962), respectively the [zero map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_map) and [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069).
- Endomorphism rings are [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), but typically [non-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring).
- If a module is [simple](wikipedia://en/29430), then its endomorphism ring is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) (this is sometimes called [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696)).[^n5]
- A module is [indecomposable](wikipedia://en/810549) if and only if its endomorphism ring does not contain any non-trivial [idempotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent_element_%28ring_theory%29).[^n6] If the module is an [injective module](wikipedia://en/473775), then indecomposability is equivalent to the endomorphism ring being a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391).[^n7]
- For a [semisimple module](wikipedia://en/1001361), the endomorphism ring is a [von Neumann regular ring](wikipedia://en/907524).
- The endomorphism ring of a nonzero right [uniserial module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniserial_module) has either one or two maximal right ideals. If the module is Artinian, Noetherian, projective or injective, then the endomorphism ring has a unique maximal ideal, so that it is a local ring.
- The endomorphism ring of an Artinian [uniform module](wikipedia://en/31553078) is a local ring.[^n8]
- The endomorphism ring of a module with finite [composition length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_length) is a [semiprimary ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiprimary_ring).
- The endomorphism ring of a [continuous module](wikipedia://en/50319816) or [discrete module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_module) is a [clean ring](wikipedia://en/50769242).[^n9]
- If an *R* module is finitely generated and projective (that is, a [progenerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progenerator)), then the endomorphism ring of the module and *R* share all Morita invariant properties. A fundamental result of Morita theory is that all rings equivalent to *R* arise as endomorphism rings of progenerators.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- In the category of *R*-[modules](wikipedia://en/276410), the endomorphism ring of an *R*-module *M* will only use the *R*-[module homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/474118), which are typically a proper subset of the abelian group homomorphisms. When *M* is a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) [projective module](wikipedia://en/364488), the endomorphism ring is central to [Morita equivalence](wikipedia://en/3296134) of module categories.
- For any abelian group $A$, $\mathrm{M}_n(\operatorname{End}(A))\cong \operatorname{End}(A^n)$, since any matrix in $\mathrm{M}_n(\operatorname{End}(A))$ carries a natural homomorphism structure of $A^n$ as follows:

$$\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{11}&\cdots &\varphi_{1n}\\ \vdots& &\vdots \\ \varphi_{n1}&\cdots& \varphi_{nn} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\\vdots\\a_n\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^n\varphi_{1i}(a_i)\\\vdots\\\sum_{i=1}^n\varphi_{ni}(a_i) \end{pmatrix}.$$

One can use this isomorphism to construct many non-commutative endomorphism rings. For example: $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{Z}\times \mathbb{Z})\cong \mathrm{M}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, since $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{Z})\cong \mathbb{Z}$.

Also, when $R=K$ is a field, there is a canonical isomorphism $\operatorname{End}(K)\cong K$, so $\operatorname{End}(K^n)\cong \mathrm{M}_n(K)$, that is, the endomorphism ring of a $K$-vector space is identified with the [ring of *n*-by-*n* matrices](wikipedia://en/487541) with entries in $K$.[^n10] More generally, the endomorphism algebra of the [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) $M = R^n$ is naturally $n$-by-$n$ matrices with entries in the ring $R$.

- As a particular example of the last point, for any ring *R* with unity, End(*R*<sub>*R*</sub>) = *R*, where the elements of *R* act on *R* by *left* multiplication.
- In general, endomorphism rings can be defined for the objects of any [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521).

:::
