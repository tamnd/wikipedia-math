# Impossible object

John Frankenheimer's film

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Impossible object"}

An **impossible object** (also known as an **impossible figure** or an **undecidable figure**) is a type of [optical illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_illusion) that consists of a two-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional) figure which is instantly and naturally understood as representing a [projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_projection) of a [three-dimensional object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_object) but cannot exist as a solid object. Impossible objects are of interest to [psychologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychologist), mathematicians and artists without falling entirely into any one discipline.

:::

## Explanations {#Explanations}

*Image: Necker cube.svg.* A Necker cube

Impossible objects can be unsettling because of a human tendency in many cultures to interpret 2D drawings as three-dimensional objects. This is why a drawing of a [Necker cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necker_cube) would most likely be seen as a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), rather than "two squares connected with diagonal lines", "a square surrounded by irregular planar figures" or any other planar figure. Looking at different parts of an impossible object makes one reassess the 3D nature of the object, which confuses the mind.[^o8]

In most cases the impossibility becomes apparent after viewing the figure for a few seconds. However, the initial impression of a 3D object remains even after it has been contradicted. There are also more subtle examples of impossible objects where the impossibility does not become apparent spontaneously and it is necessary to consciously examine the [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) of the implied object to determine that it is impossible.

Roger Penrose wrote about describing and defining impossible objects mathematically using the [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) concept of [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060).[^o9][^o10]

## History {#History}

An early example of an impossible object comes from *[Apolinère Enameled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apolinère_Enameled)*, a 1916 advertisement painted by [Marcel Duchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Duchamp). It depicts a girl painting a bed-frame with white enamelled paint, and deliberately includes conflicting perspective lines, to produce an impossible object. To emphasise the deliberate impossibility of the shape, a piece of the frame is missing.

*Image: Penrosetrianglemodel.jpg.* A 3D-printed version of the Reutersvärd Triangle illusion, its appearance created by a [forced perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_perspective)

[Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden) artist [Oscar Reutersvärd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Reutersvärd) was one of the first to deliberately design many impossible objects. He has been called "the father of impossible figures".[^o12] In 1934, he drew the Penrose triangle, some years before the Penroses. In Reutersvärd's version, the sides of the triangle are broken up into cubes.

In 1956, British psychiatrist [Lionel Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Penrose) and his son, mathematician [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193), submitted a short article to the *[British Journal of Psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Journal_of_Psychology)* titled "Impossible Objects: A Special Type of Visual Illusion". This was illustrated with the Penrose triangle and Penrose stairs. The article referred to Escher, whose work had sparked their interest in the subject, but not Reutersvärd, of whom they were unaware. The article was published in 1958.[^o13]

From the 1930s onwards, Dutch artist [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) produced many drawings featuring paradoxes of perspective gradually working towards impossible objects.[^o12] In 1957, he produced his first drawing containing a true impossible object: *[Cube with Magic Ribbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_with_Magic_Ribbons)*. He produced many further drawings featuring impossible objects, sometimes with the entire drawing being an impossible object. *[Waterfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall_%28M._C._Escher%29)* and *[Belvedere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belvedere_%28M._C._Escher%29)* are good examples of impossible constructions. His work did much to draw the attention of the public to impossible objects.

Some contemporary artists are also experimenting with impossible figures, for example, [Jos de Mey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos_de_Mey), [Shigeo Fukuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeo_Fukuda), [Sandro del Prete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandro_del_Prete), [István Orosz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/István_Orosz) ([Utisz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utisz)), [Guido Moretti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_Moretti), [Tamás F. Farkas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamás_F._Farkas), [Mathieu Hamaekers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathieu_Hamaekers), and [Kokichi Sugihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kokichi_Sugihara).

## Constructed impossible objects {#Constructed_impossible_objects}

Although possible to represent in two dimensions, it is not geometrically possible for such an object to exist in the physical world. However, some models of impossible objects have been constructed, such that when they are viewed from a very specific point, the illusion is maintained. Rotating the object or changing the viewpoint breaks the illusion, and therefore many of these models rely on [forced perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_perspective) or having parts of the model appearing to be further or closer than they actually are.

The notion of an "interactive impossible object" is an impossible object that can be viewed from any angle without breaking the illusion.[^o15]
