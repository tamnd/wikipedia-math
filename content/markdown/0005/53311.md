# Hermann von Helmholtz

*Image: Helmholtz's polyphonic siren, Hunterian Museum, Glasgow.jpg.* Helmholtz's polyphonic siren, Hunterian Museum, Glasgow

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Hermann von Helmholtz"}

**Hermann Ludwig Ferdinand von Helmholtz** (31 August 1821 – 8 September 1894; "von" since 1883) was a German [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) and [physician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician) who made significant contributions in several scientific fields, particularly [hydrodynamic stability](wikipedia://en/21757046).[^o4] The [Helmholtz Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_Association_of_German_Research_Centres), the largest German association of [research institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_institution), was named in his honour.[^o5] Helmholtz is remembered for at the time groundbreaking work in [natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science) linking theory and practical research work. He was given the *von* in his surname by [Emperor William II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_William_II).[^o6]

:::

In the fields of [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology) and [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), Helmholtz is known for his mathematics concerning the [eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_eye), [theories of vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_vision), ideas on the [visual perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_perception) of space, [colour vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_vision) research, the sensation of tone, perceptions of sound, and [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism) in the physiology of perception. In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), he is known for his theories on the conservation of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) and on the electrical [double layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_layer_%28surface_science%29), work in [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics), [chemical thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_thermodynamics), and on a [mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) foundation of [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952). Although credit is shared with [Julius von Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_von_Mayer), [James Joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joule), and [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784)—among others—for the energy conservation principles that eventually led to the [first law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/166404), he is credited with the first formulation of the energy conservation principle in its maximally general form.[^o15]

As a [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher), he is known for his [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science), ideas on the relation between the laws of perception and the [laws of nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law), the science of [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics), and ideas on the civilizing power of science. By the late nineteenth century, Helmholtz's development of a broadly Kantian methodology, including the *a priori* determination of the manifold of possible orientations in perceptual space, had inspired new readings of Kant[^o15] and contributed to the late modern [neo-Kantianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Kantianism) movement in philosophy.[^o7]

## Career {#Career}

### Early years {#Early_years}

Helmholtz was born in [Potsdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potsdam), the son of the local [gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28school%29) headmaster, Ferdinand Helmholtz, who had studied [classical philology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_philology) and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), and who was a close friend of the publisher and philosopher [Immanuel Hermann Fichte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Hermann_Fichte). Helmholtz's work was influenced by the philosophy of [Johann Gottlieb Fichte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Gottlieb_Fichte) and [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant). He tried to trace their theories in empirical matters like [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology).

As a young man, Helmholtz was interested in natural science, but his father wanted him to study medicine. Helmholtz earned a [medical doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_doctorate) at Medizinisch-chirurgisches Friedrich-Wilhelm-Institute in 1842 and served a one-year internship at the [Charité](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charité) hospital[^o10] (because there was financial support for medical students).

Trained primarily in physiology, Helmholtz wrote on many other topics, ranging from theoretical physics to the [age of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_Earth), and to the origin of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System).

### University posts {#University_posts}

Helmholtz's first academic position was as a teacher of anatomy at the Academy of Arts in Berlin in 1848.[^o12] He then moved to take a post of associate professor of physiology at the Prussian [University of Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Königsberg), where he was appointed in 1849. In 1855 he accepted a full professorship of anatomy and physiology at the [University of Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bonn). He was not particularly happy in Bonn, however, and three years later he transferred to the [University of Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Heidelberg), in [Baden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baden), where he served as professor of physiology. In 1871 he accepted his final university position, as professor of physics at the [Friedrich Wilhelm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_University_of_Berlin) in Berlin.

## Research {#Research}

*Image: Helmholtz 1848.jpg.* Helmholtz in 1848

### Mechanics {#Mechanics}

His first important scientific achievement, an 1847 treatise on the [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088), was written in the context of his medical studies and philosophical background. His work on energy conservation came about while studying [muscle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle) [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism). He tried to demonstrate that no energy is lost in muscle movement, motivated by the implication that there were no *vital forces* necessary to move a muscle. This was a rejection of the speculative tradition of *[Naturphilosophie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturphilosophie)* and [vitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitalism) which was at that time a dominant philosophical paradigm in German physiology. He was working against the argument, promoted by some vitalists, that "living force" can power a machine indefinitely.[^o15]

Drawing on the earlier work of [Sadi Carnot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Léonard_Sadi_Carnot), [Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoît_Paul_Émile_Clapeyron) and [James Prescott Joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Prescott_Joule), he postulated a relationship between [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), [heat](wikipedia://en/19593167), [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light), [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity) and [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism) by treating them all as manifestations of a single *force*, or [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) in today's terminology. He published his theories in his book *Über die Erhaltung der Kraft* (*On the Conservation of Force*, 1847).[^o16]

In the 1850s and 60s, building on the publications of [William Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin), Helmholtz and [William Rankine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rankine) helped popularize the idea of the [heat death of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_death_of_the_universe).

In fluid dynamics, Helmholtz made several contributions, including [Helmholtz's theorems](wikipedia://en/477513) for vortex dynamics in inviscid fluids.

### Sensory physiology {#Sensory_physiology}

Helmholtz was a pioneer in the scientific study of human vision and audition. Inspired by [psychophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychophysics), he was interested in the relationships between measurable physical stimuli and their correspondent human perceptions. For example, the amplitude of a sound wave can be varied, causing the sound to appear louder or softer, but a linear step in sound pressure amplitude does not result in a linear step in perceived loudness. The physical sound needs to be increased exponentially in order for equal steps to seem linear, a fact that is used in current electronic devices to control volume. Helmholtz paved the way in experimental studies on the relationship between the physical energy (physics) and its appreciation (psychology), with the goal in mind to develop "psychophysical laws".

The sensory physiology of Helmholtz was the basis of the work of [Wilhelm Wundt](wikipedia://en/34128), Helmholtz's student, who is considered one of the founders of experimental [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology). More explicitly than Helmholtz, Wundt described his research as a form of empirical philosophy and as a study of the mind as something separate. Helmholtz had, in his early repudiation of [Naturphilosophie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturphilosophie), stressed the importance of [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism), and was focusing more on the unity of "mind" and body.[^o18]

### Ophthalmic optics {#Ophthalmic_optics}

In 1851, Helmholtz revolutionized the field of [ophthalmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophthalmology) with the invention of the [ophthalmoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ophthalmoscope); an instrument used to examine the inside of the [human eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_eye). This made him world-famous overnight. Helmholtz's interests at that time were increasingly focused on the physiology of the senses. His main publication, titled *Handbuch der Physiologischen Optik* (*Handbook of Physiological Optics* or *Treatise on Physiological Optics*; English translation of the 3rd volume [here](https://web.archive.org/web/20180320133752/http://poseidon.sunyopt.edu/BackusLab/Helmholtz/)), provided empirical theories on [depth perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_perception), [colour vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_vision), and [motion perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_perception), and became the fundamental reference work in his field during the second half of the nineteenth century. In the third and final volume, published in 1867, Helmholtz described the importance of [unconscious inferences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconscious_inference) for perception. The *Handbuch* was first translated into English under the editorship of [James P. C. Southall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_P._C._Southall) on behalf of the [Optical Society of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_Society_of_America) in 1924–5. His theory of [accommodation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accommodation_reflex) went unchallenged until the final decade of the 20th century.

Helmholtz continued to work for several decades on several editions of the handbook, frequently updating his work because of his dispute with [Ewald Hering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ewald_Hering) who held opposite views on spatial and colour vision. This dispute divided the discipline of physiology during the second half of the 1800s.

### Nerve physiology {#Nerve_physiology}

In 1849, while at Königsberg, Helmholtz measured the speed at which the signal is carried along a nerve fibre. At that time most people believed that nerve signals passed along nerves immeasurably fast.[^o21] He used a recently dissected sciatic nerve of a frog and the calf muscle to which it attached. He used a [galvanometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanometer) as a sensitive timing device, attaching a mirror to the needle to reflect a light beam across the room to a scale which gave much greater sensitivity.[^o21] Helmholtz reported[^o22][^o23] transmission speeds in the range of 24.6 – 38.4 meters per second.[^o21]

### Acoustics and aesthetics {#Acoustics_and_aesthetics}

*Image: Hermann von Helmholtz-2.jpg.* Last photograph of von Helmholtz, taken three days before his final illness

*Image: Helmholtz resonator 2.jpg.* The Helmholtz resonator (*i*) and instrumentation

In 1863, Helmholtz published *[Sensations of Tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensations_of_Tone)*, once again demonstrating his interest in the physics of perception. This book influenced musicologists into the twentieth century. Helmholtz invented the [Helmholtz resonator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_resonance) to identify the various [frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_frequency) or [pitches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_%28music%29) of the pure [sine wave](wikipedia://en/324749) components of [complex sounds containing multiple tones](wikipedia://en/11659#Applications_in_signal_processing).[^o25]

Helmholtz showed that different combinations of resonators could mimic [vowel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vowel) sounds: [Alexander Graham Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Graham_Bell) in particular was interested in this but, not being able to read German, misconstrued Helmholtz's diagrams as meaning that Helmholtz had transmitted multiple frequencies by wire—which would allow multiplexing of telegraph signals—whereas, in reality, electrical power was used only to keep the resonators in motion. Bell failed to reproduce what he thought Helmholtz had done but later said that, had he been able to read German, he would not have gone on to invent the telephone on the [harmonic telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_telegraph) principle.[^o26][^o27][^o28][^o29]

The translation by [Alexander J. Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_J._Ellis) was first published in 1875 (the first English edition was from the 1870 third German edition; Ellis's second English edition from the 1877 fourth German edition was published in 1885; the 1895 and 1912 third and fourth English editions were reprints of the second).[^o30]

### Electromagnetism {#Electromagnetism}

Helmholtz studied electrical oscillations from 1869 to 1871, and in a lecture delivered to the Naturhistorisch-medizinischen Verein zu Heidelberg (Natural History and Medical Association of Heidelberg) on 30 April 1869, titled *On Electrical Oscillations*, he indicated that the perceptible damped electrical oscillations in a coil connected to a [Leyden jar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyden_jar) were about $\tfrac{1}{50}$ second in duration.[^o32]

In 1871, Helmholtz moved from Heidelberg to Berlin to become a professor of physics. He became interested in [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), and the [Helmholtz equation](wikipedia://en/1156215) is named for him. Although he made no major contributions to this field, his student [Heinrich Rudolf Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Rudolf_Hertz) became famous as the first to demonstrate [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426). [Oliver Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831) criticised Helmholtz's electromagnetic theory because it allowed the existence of [longitudinal waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave). Based on work on [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737), Heaviside pronounced that longitudinal waves could not exist in a vacuum or a homogeneous medium. Heaviside did not note, however, that longitudinal electromagnetic waves can exist at a boundary or in an enclosed space.[^o33]

## Philosophy {#Philosophy}

Helmholtz's scientific work in physiology and mechanics occasioned much that he is known for in [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science), including ideas on the relation between the laws of perception and the [laws of nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law) and his rejection of the exclusive use of [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417).[^o35]

His philosophy of science wavered between some version of [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism) and [transcendental idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_idealism).[^o36] Despite the speculative associations of the latter, his philosophy of science is thoroughly indebted to his use of mathematical physics to supplant vitalism and articulate the general conservation of energy principle.[^o15]

His rejection of Euclidean geometry as the only possible science of space is central to understanding his appropriation of Kant's philosophy of space, which ostensibly requires Euclidean geometry to be that exclusive *a priori* science of [physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_space). Helmholtz introduced a new conception of the *a priori* in space: that of the determination of the manifold of possible orientations in perceptual space. These developments inspired new readings of Kant[^o15] and contributed to the rise of late modern [neo-Kantianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Kantianism) movement in philosophy.

## Students and associates {#Students_and_associates}

Other students and research associates of Helmholtz at Berlin included [Max Planck](wikipedia://en/19848), [Heinrich Kayser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Kayser), [Eugen Goldstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugen_Goldstein), [Wilhelm Wien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Wien), [Arthur König](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_König), [Henry Augustus Rowland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Augustus_Rowland), [Albert A. Michelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_A._Michelson), [Wilhelm Wundt](wikipedia://en/34128), [Fernando Sanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fernando_Sanford), [Arthur Gordon Webster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Gordon_Webster) and [Michael I. Pupin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_I._Pupin). [Leo Koenigsberger](wikipedia://en/3346000), who was his colleague from 1869 to 1871 in Heidelberg, wrote the definitive biography of him in 1902.

## Honours and legacy {#Honours_and_legacy}

*Image: Hermann von Helmholtz-Statue vor der Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin.jpg.* Helmholtz's statue in front of Humboldt University in Berlin

- In 1873, Helmholtz was elected as a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society).[^o39]
- In 1881, Helmholtz was elected [Honorary Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellowship_of_the_Royal_College_of_Surgeons) of the [Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_College_of_Surgeons_in_Ireland).[^o40]
- On 10 November 1881, he was awarded the [Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour): au grade de Commandeur, or Level 3 – a senior grade. (No. 2173).
- In 1883, Professor Helmholtz was honoured by the Emperor, being raised to the nobility, or *Adel*. The *Adelung* meant that he and his family were now styled: **von** Helmholtz. The distinction was not a peerage or title, but it was hereditary and conferred a certain social cachet.
- Helmholtz was conferred the Honorary Membership of the [Institution of Engineers and Shipbuilders in Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution_of_Engineers_and_Shipbuilders_in_Scotland) in 1884.[^o41]
- The largest German association of [research institutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_institution), the [Helmholtz Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_Association_of_German_Research_Centres), is named after him.[^o5][^o42]
- The asteroid [11573 Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11573_Helmholtz) and the lunar crater *[Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_%28lunar_crater%29)* as well as the crater *[Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_%28Martian_crater%29)* on Mars were named in his honour.[^o62][^o43][^o44]
- In [Charlottenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottenburg), [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin), the street *Helmholtzstraße* is named after von Helmholtz.[^o45]
- In [Prenzlauer Berg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prenzlauer_Berg), [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin), the plaza Helmholtzplatz and the surrounding neighborhood, Helmholtzkiez, are named after von Helmholtz.
