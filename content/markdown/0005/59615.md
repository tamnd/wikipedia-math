# Electric potential

Electrostatics

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Electric potential"}

**Electric potential,** also known as the **electrostatic potential** or **electric field potential**, is a field of [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) quantities through space, often denoted with the letter $\varphi$ or $V$, that gives, for each point in space, the [electric potential energy](wikipedia://en/1975956) per unit of [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) that a [point charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_particle) would have if it was located at that point: at every point, a particle of charge $q_{\text{particle}}$ would have the electric potential energy $U_{\text{particle when at the point}}=q_{\text{particle}}\,V_{\text{point}}$. The difference in [electric potential energy](wikipedia://en/1975956) U, $U = U_B - U_A$, that a charged particle of charge $q$ would have between two points $A$ and $B$ in a [static electric field](wikipedia://en/41092#Electrostatics), equals $q$ times the difference $V_B - V_A$ of the electric potentials at A and B: $U_B - U_A = q \times (V_B - V_A)$. More precisely, electric potential is the amount of [work](wikipedia://en/149861) needed to move a [test charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_charge) from a reference point to a specific point in a static electric field, normalized to a unit of charge. The test charge used is small enough that disturbance to the field-producing charges is unnoticeable, and its motion across the field is supposed to proceed with negligible acceleration, so as to avoid the test charge acquiring kinetic energy or producing radiation. By definition, the electric potential at the reference point is zero units. Typically, the reference point is [earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_%28electricity%29) or a point at [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), although any point can be used.

:::

In classical [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics), the electrostatic field is a vector quantity expressed as the gradient of the electrostatic potential, which is a [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_%28physics%29) quantity denoted by $V$ or occasionally $\varphi$,[^o3] equal to the [electric potential energy](wikipedia://en/1975956) of any [charged particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_particle) at any location (measured in [joules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule)) divided by the [charge](wikipedia://en/9804) of that particle (measured in [coulombs](wikipedia://en/47719)). By dividing out the charge on the particle a quotient is obtained that is a property of the electric field itself. In short, an electric potential is the electric potential energy per unit charge.

This value can be calculated in either a static (time-invariant) or a dynamic (time-varying) [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) at a specific time with the unit joules per coulomb (J⋅C<sup>−1</sup>) or [volt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt) (V). The electric potential at infinity is assumed to be zero.

In [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics), when time-varying fields are present, the electric field cannot be expressed only as a [scalar potential](wikipedia://en/461654). Instead, the electric field can be expressed as both the scalar electric potential and the [magnetic vector potential](wikipedia://en/1191067).[^o4] The electric potential and the magnetic vector potential together form a [four-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_vector), so that the two kinds of potential are mixed under [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404).

Practically, the electric potential is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) in all space, because a spatial derivative of a discontinuous electric potential yields an electric field of impossibly infinite magnitude. Notably, the electric potential due to an idealized [point charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) (proportional to $1 \text{⁄} r$, with $r$ the distance from the point charge) is continuous in all space except at the location of the point charge. Though electric field is not continuous across an idealized [surface charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_charge), it is not infinite at any point. Therefore, the electric potential is continuous across an idealized surface charge. Additionally, an idealized line of charge has electric potential (proportional to $\ln(r)$, with $r$ the radial distance from the line of charge) is continuous everywhere except on the line of charge.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

[Classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) explores concepts such as [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_%28physics%29), [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy), and [potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential).[^o6] Force and potential energy are directly related. A net force acting on any object will cause it to [accelerate](wikipedia://en/2443). As an object moves in the direction of a force acting on it, its potential energy decreases. For example, the [gravitational potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential_energy) of a cannonball at the top of a hill is greater than at the base of the hill. As it rolls downhill, its potential energy decreases and is being translated to motion – [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327).

It is possible to define the potential of certain force fields so that the potential energy of an object in that field depends only on the position of the object with respect to the field. Two such force fields are a [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) and an electric field (in the absence of time-varying magnetic fields). Such fields affect objects because of the intrinsic properties (e.g., [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) or charge) and positions of the objects.

An object may possess a property known as [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804). Since an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) exerts force on a charged object, if the object has a positive charge, the force will be in the direction of the [electric field vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field_vector) at the location of the charge; if the charge is negative, the force will be in the opposite direction.

The magnitude of force is given by the quantity of the charge multiplied by the magnitude of the electric field vector,

$$|\mathbf{F}| = q |\mathbf{E}|.$$

### Electric potential due to a point charge {#Electric_potential_due_to_a_point_charge}

*Image: Electric potential varying charge.gif.* The electric potential created by a charge, *Q*, is *V* = *Q*/(4πε<sub>0</sub>*r*). Different values of *Q* yield different values of electric potential, *V*, (shown in the image).

The electric potential arising from a point charge, $Q$, at a distance, $r$, from the location of $Q$ is observed to be

$$V_\mathbf{E} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r},$$

where $\varepsilon_{0}$ is the [permittivity of vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permittivity_of_vacuum)eps0, $V_{\mathbf{E}}$ is known as the **Coulomb potential**. Note that, in contrast to the magnitude of an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) due to a point charge, the electric potential scales respective to the reciprocal of the radius, rather than the radius squared.

The electric potential at any location, $\mathbf{r}$, in a system of point charges is equal to the sum of the individual electric potentials due to every point charge in the system. This fact simplifies calculations significantly, because addition of potential (scalar) fields is much easier than addition of the electric (vector) fields. Specifically, the potential of a set of discrete point charges $q_{i}$ at points $\mathbf{r}_{i}$ becomes

$$V_\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{q_i}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_i|}\,$$

where

- $\mathbf{r}$ is a point at which the potential is evaluated;
- $\mathbf{r}_{i}$ is a point at which there is a nonzero charge; and
- $q_{i}$ is the charge at the point $\mathbf{r}_{i}$.

And the potential of a continuous charge distribution $\rho(\mathbf{r})$ becomes

$$V_\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int_R \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|} \mathrm{d}^3 r'\,,$$

where

- $\mathbf{r}$ is a point at which the potential is evaluated;
- $R$ is a region containing all the points at which the charge density is nonzero;
- $\mathbf{r}$ is a point inside $R$; and
- $\rho(\mathbf{r})$ is the charge density at the point $\mathbf{r}$.

The equations given above for the electric potential (and all the equations used here) are in the forms required by [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_units). In some other (less common) systems of units, such as [CGS-Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units), many of these equations would be altered.

## Generalization to electrodynamics {#Generalization_to_electrodynamics}

When time-varying magnetic fields are present (which is true whenever there are time-varying electric fields and vice versa), it is not possible to describe the electric field simply as a scalar potential $V$ because the electric field is no longer [conservative](wikipedia://en/44158): $\textstyle\int_C \mathbf{E}\cdot \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell}$ is path-dependent because $\mathbf{\nabla} \times \mathbf{E} \neq \mathbf{0}$ (due to the [Maxwell-Faraday equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell-Faraday_equation)).

Instead, one can still define a scalar potential by also including the [magnetic vector potential](wikipedia://en/1191067) $\mathbf{A}$. In particular, $\mathbf{A}$ is defined to satisfy:

$$\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{\nabla} \times \mathbf{A}$$

where $\mathbf{B}$ is the [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563). By the [fundamental theorem of vector calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_vector_calculus), such an $\mathbf{A}$ can always be found, since [the divergence of the magnetic field is always zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law_for_magnetism) due to the absence of [magnetic monopoles](wikipedia://en/175470). Now, the quantity

$$\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{E} + \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}$$

*is* a conservative field, since the curl of $\mathbf{E}$ is canceled by the curl of $\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}$ according to the [Maxwell–Faraday equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell–Faraday_equation). One can therefore write

$$\mathbf{E} = -\mathbf{\nabla}V - \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t} ,$$

where $V$ is the scalar potential defined by the conservative field $\mathbf{F}$.

The electrostatic potential is simply the special case of this definition where $\mathbf{A}$ is time-invariant. On the other hand, for time-varying fields,

$$-\int_a^b \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\ell} \neq V_{(b)} - V_{(a)}$$

unlike electrostatics.

### Gauge freedom {#Gauge_freedom}

Gauge fixing The electrostatic potential could have any constant added to it without affecting the electric field. In electrodynamics, the electric potential has infinitely many degrees of freedom. For any (possibly time-varying or space-varying) scalar field, $\text{𝜓}$, we can perform the following [gauge transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_Transformation) to find a new set of potentials that produce exactly the same electric and magnetic fields:[^o14]

$$\begin{aligned} V^\prime &= V - \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} \\ \mathbf{A}^\prime &= \mathbf{A} + \nabla\psi \end{aligned}$$

Given different choices of gauge, the electric potential could have quite different properties. In the [Coulomb gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_Gauge), the electric potential is given by [Poisson's equation](wikipedia://en/193735)

$$\nabla^2 V=-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$

just like in electrostatics. However, in the [Lorenz gauge](wikipedia://en/1718317), the electric potential is a [retarded potential](wikipedia://en/11230759) that propagates at the speed of light and is the solution to an [inhomogeneous wave equation](wikipedia://en/4603613#A_and_%CF%86_potential_fields):

$$\nabla^2 V - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial t^2} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$

## Units {#Units}

The [SI derived unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_derived_unit) of electric potential is the [volt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt) (in honor of [Alessandro Volta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Volta)), denoted as V, which is why the electric potential difference between two points in space is known as a [voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage). Older units are rarely used today. Variants of the [centimetre–gram–second system of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre–gram–second_system_of_units) included a number of different units for electric potential, including the [abvolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_of_units#Electromotive_force,_electric_potential_difference) and the [statvolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statvolt).

## Galvani potential versus electrochemical potential {#Galvani_potential_versus_electrochemical_potential}

Inside metals (and other solids and liquids), the energy of an electron is affected not only by the electric potential, but also by the specific atomic environment that it is in. When a [voltmeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltmeter) is connected between two different types of metal, it measures the **potential difference** corrected for the different atomic environments.[^o17] The quantity measured by a voltmeter is called [electrochemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_potential) or [fermi level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_level), while the pure unadjusted electric potential, $V$, is sometimes called the [Galvani potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvani_potential), $\phi$. The terms "voltage" and "electric potential" are a bit ambiguous but one may refer to either of these in different contexts.
