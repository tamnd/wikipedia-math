# Noetherian ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Noetherian ring"}

*Image: Noether.jpg.* Emmy Noether, around 1900

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Noetherian ring** is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that satisfies the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189) on left and right [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977). If the chain condition is satisfied only for left ideals or for right ideals, then the ring is said **left-Noetherian** or **right-Noetherian** respectively. Formally, every increasing sequence $I_1\subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ of left (or right) ideals has a largest element; that is, there exists an $n$ such that $I_{n}=I_{n+1}=\cdots.$

:::

Equivalently, a ring is left-Noetherian (respectively right-Noetherian) if every left ideal (respectively right-ideal) is [finitely generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_generated_ideal). A ring is Noetherian if it is both left- and right-Noetherian.

Noetherian rings are fundamental in both [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) and [noncommutative](wikipedia://en/24062516) ring theory since many rings that are encountered in mathematics are Noetherian (in particular the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613), [polynomial rings](wikipedia://en/373065), and [rings of algebraic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_algebraic_integers) in [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field)), and many general theorems on rings rely heavily on the Noetherian property (for example, the [Lasker–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–Noether_theorem) and the [Krull intersection theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_intersection_theorem)).

Noetherian rings are named after [Emmy Noether](wikipedia://en/149896), but the importance of the concept was recognized earlier by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), with the proof of [Hilbert's basis theorem](wikipedia://en/13733) (which asserts that polynomial rings are Noetherian) and [Hilbert's syzygy theorem](wikipedia://en/1280843).

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

For [noncommutative rings](wikipedia://en/24062516), it is necessary to distinguish between three very similar concepts:

- A ring is **left-Noetherian** if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on left ideals.
- A ring is **right-Noetherian** if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on right ideals.
- A ring is **Noetherian** if it is both left- and right-Noetherian.

For [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346), all three concepts coincide, but in general they are different. There are rings that are left-Noetherian and not right-Noetherian, and vice versa.

There are other, equivalent, definitions for a ring *R* to be left-Noetherian:

- Every left ideal *I* in *R* is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/25977#Types_of_ideals), i.e. there exist elements $a_1, \ldots , a_n$ in *I* such that $I=Ra_1 + \cdots + Ra_n$.[^o4]
- Every [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) set of left ideals of *R*, [partially ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partially_ordered) by inclusion, has a [maximal element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_element).[^o4]

Similar results hold for right-Noetherian rings.

The following condition is also an equivalent condition for a ring *R* to be left-Noetherian and it is [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s original formulation:[^o5]

- Given a sequence $f_1, f_2, \dots$ of elements in *R*, there exists an integer $n$ such that each $f_i$ is a finite [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) $f_i = \sum_{j=1}^n r_j f_j$ with coefficients $r_j$ in *R*.

For a commutative ring to be Noetherian it suffices that every [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) of the ring is finitely generated.[^o6] However, it is not enough to ask that all the [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) are finitely generated, as there is a non-Noetherian [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) whose maximal ideal is [principal](wikipedia://en/145126) (see a counterexample to Krull's intersection theorem at [Local ring#Commutative case](wikipedia://en/153391#Commutative_case).)

## Properties {#Properties}

- If *R* is a Noetherian ring, then the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[X]$ is Noetherian by the [Hilbert's basis theorem](wikipedia://en/13733). By [induction](wikipedia://en/18881), $R[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ is a Noetherian ring. Also, $R[[X]]$, the [power series ring](wikipedia://en/60012), is a Noetherian ring.
- If $R$ is a Noetherian ring and $I$ is a two-sided ideal, then the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $R/I$ is also Noetherian. Stated differently, the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of any [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian.
- Every finitely-generated [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29) over a commutative Noetherian ring is Noetherian. (This follows from the two previous properties.)
- A ring *R* is left-Noetherian [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) every finitely generated left [*R*-module](wikipedia://en/276410) is a [Noetherian module](wikipedia://en/542431).
- If a commutative ring admits a [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_module) Noetherian module over it, then the ring is a Noetherian ring.[^o9]
- ([Eakin–Nagata](wikipedia://en/62674651)) If a ring *A* is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of a commutative Noetherian ring *B* such that *B* is a [finitely generated module](wikipedia://en/326430) over *A*, then *A* is a Noetherian ring.[^o10]
- Similarly, if a ring *A* is a subring of a commutative Noetherian ring *B* such that *B* is [faithfully flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithfully_flat_ring_homomorphism) over *A* (or more generally exhibits *A* as a [pure subring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_subring)), then *A* is a Noetherian ring (see the "faithfully flat" article for the reasoning).
- Every [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of a commutative Noetherian ring is Noetherian.
- A consequence of the [Akizuki–Hopkins–Levitzki theorem](wikipedia://en/29435296) is that every left [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273) is left Noetherian. Another consequence is that a left Artinian ring is right Noetherian if and only if it is right Artinian. The analogous statements with "right" and "left" interchanged are also true.
- A left Noetherian ring is left [coherent](wikipedia://en/14777654) and a left Noetherian [domain](wikipedia://en/487627) is a left [Ore domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore_domain).
- (Bass) A ring is (left/right) Noetherian if and only if every [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of [injective](wikipedia://en/473775) (left/right) modules is injective. Every left injective module over a left Noetherian module can be decomposed as a direct sum of [indecomposable](wikipedia://en/810549) injective modules.[^o11] See also #Implication on injective modules below.
- In a commutative Noetherian ring, there are only finitely many [minimal prime ideals](wikipedia://en/16143832). Also, the [descending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_chain_condition) holds on prime ideals.
- In a commutative Noetherian domain *R*, every element can be factorized into [irreducible elements](wikipedia://en/60693) (in short, *R* is a [factorization domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorization_domain)). Thus, if, in addition, the factorization is unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) multiplication of the factors by [units](wikipedia://en/289666), then *R* is a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546).

## Examples {#Examples}

- Any field, including the fields of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), and [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), is Noetherian. (A field only has two ideals — itself and (0).)
- Any [principal ideal ring](wikipedia://en/6961771), such as the integers, is Noetherian since every ideal is generated by a single element. This includes [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039) and [Euclidean domains](wikipedia://en/10376).
- A [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) (e.g., [rings of integers](wikipedia://en/460613)) is a Noetherian domain in which every ideal is generated by at most two elements.
- The [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of an [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468) is a Noetherian ring, as a consequence of the Hilbert basis theorem.
- The enveloping algebra *U* of a finite-dimensional [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{g}$ is a both left and right Noetherian ring; this follows from the fact that the [associated graded ring](wikipedia://en/38279783) of *U* is a quotient of $\operatorname{Sym}(\mathfrak{g})$, which is a polynomial ring over a field (the [PBW theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBW_theorem)); thus, Noetherian.[^o14] For the same reason, the [Weyl algebra](wikipedia://en/491095), and more general rings of [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886), are Noetherian.[^o15]
- The ring of polynomials in finitely-many variables over the integers or a field is Noetherian.

Rings that are not Noetherian tend to be (in some sense) very large. Here are some examples of non-Noetherian rings:

- The ring of polynomials in infinitely-many variables, *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, *X*<sub>3</sub>, etc. The sequence of ideals (*X*<sub>1</sub>), (*X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>), (*X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, *X*<sub>3</sub>), etc. is ascending, and does not terminate.
- The ring of all [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) is not Noetherian. For example, it contains the infinite ascending chain of principal ideals: (2), (2<sup>1/2</sup>), (2<sup>1/4</sup>), (2<sup>1/8</sup>), ...
- The ring of [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) from the real numbers to the real numbers is not Noetherian: Let *I<sub>n</sub>* be the ideal of all continuous functions *f* such that *f*(*x*) = 0 for all *x* ≥ *n*. The sequence of ideals *I*<sub>0</sub>, *I*<sub>1</sub>, *I*<sub>2</sub>, etc., is an ascending chain that does not terminate.
- The ring of [stable homotopy groups of spheres](wikipedia://en/1104697#Ring_structure) is not Noetherian.[^o16]

However, a non-Noetherian ring can be a subring of a Noetherian ring. Since any [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) is a subring of a field, any integral domain that is not Noetherian provides an example. To give a less trivial example,

- The ring of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) generated by *x* and *y*/*x*<sup>*n*</sup> over a field *k* is a subring of the field *k*(*x*,*y*) in only two variables.

Indeed, there are rings that are right Noetherian, but not left Noetherian, so that one must be careful in measuring the "size" of a ring this way. For example, if *L* is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of **Q**<sup>2</sup> [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to **Z**, let *R* be the ring of homomorphisms *f* from **Q**<sup>2</sup> to itself satisfying *f*(*L*) ⊂ *L*. Choosing a basis, we can describe the same ring *R* as

$$R=\left\{\left.\begin{bmatrix}a & \beta \\0 & \gamma \end{bmatrix} \, \right\vert\, a\in \mathbf{Z}, \beta\in \mathbf{Q},\gamma\in \mathbf{Q}\right\}.$$

This ring is right Noetherian, but not left Noetherian; the subset *I* ⊂ *R* consisting of elements with *a* = 0 and *γ* = 0 is a left ideal that is not finitely generated as a left *R*-module.

If *R* is a commutative subring of a left Noetherian ring *S*, and *S* is finitely generated as a left *R*-module, then *R* is Noetherian.[^o17] (In the special case when *S* is commutative, this is known as [Eakin's theorem](wikipedia://en/62674651).) However, this is not true if *R* is not commutative: the ring *R* of the previous paragraph is a subring of the left Noetherian ring *S* = Hom(**Q**<sup>2</sup>, **Q**<sup>2</sup>), and *S* is finitely generated as a left *R*-module, but *R* is not left Noetherian.

A [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) is not necessarily a Noetherian ring. It does satisfy a weaker condition: the [ascending chain condition on principal ideals](wikipedia://en/22243102). A ring of polynomials in infinitely-many variables is an example of a non-Noetherian unique factorization domain.

A [valuation ring](wikipedia://en/966856) is not Noetherian unless it is a principal ideal domain. It gives an example of a ring that arises naturally in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) but is not Noetherian.

### Noetherian group rings {#Noetherian_group_rings}

Consider the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) $R[G]$ of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$. It is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), and an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over $R$ if $R$ is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346). For a group $G$ and a commutative ring $R$, the following two conditions are equivalent.

- The ring $R[G]$ is left-Noetherian.
- The ring $R[G]$ is right-Noetherian.

This is because there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the left and right ideals of the group ring in this case, via the $R$-[associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564)

$$R[G]\to R[G]^{\operatorname{op}},$$

$$g\mapsto g^{-1}\qquad(\forall g\in G).$$

Let $G$ be a group and $R$ a ring. If $R[G]$ is left/right/two-sided Noetherian, then $R$ is left/right/two-sided Noetherian and $G$ is a [Noetherian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_group). Conversely, if $R$ is a Noetherian commutative ring and $G$ is an [extension](wikipedia://en/345807) of a [Noetherian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_group) [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012) (i.e. a [polycyclic group](wikipedia://en/4000598)) by a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707), then $R[G]$ is two-sided Noetherian. On the other hand, however, there is a [Noetherian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_group) $G$ whose group ring over any Noetherian commutative ring is not two-sided Noetherian.[^o19]

## Key theorems {#Key_theorems}

Many important theorems in ring theory (especially the theory of [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346)) rely on the assumptions that the rings are Noetherian.

### Commutative case {#Commutative_case}

- Over a commutative Noetherian ring, each ideal has a [primary decomposition](wikipedia://en/1208391), meaning that it can be written as an [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of finitely many [primary ideals](wikipedia://en/3021435) (whose [radicals](wikipedia://en/252009) are all distinct) where an ideal *Q* is called primary if it is [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_ideal) and whenever *xy* ∈ *Q*, either *x* ∈ *Q* or *y*<sup>*n*</sup> ∈ *Q* for some positive integer *n*. For example, if an element $f = p_1^{n_1} \cdots p_r^{n_r}$ is a product of powers of distinct prime elements, then $(f) = (p_1^{n_1}) \cap \cdots \cap (p_r^{n_r})$ and thus the primary decomposition is a direct generalization of [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) of integers and polynomials.[^o23]
- A Noetherian ring is defined in terms of ascending chains of ideals. The [Artin–Rees lemma](wikipedia://en/9553854), on the other hand, gives some information about a descending chain of ideals given by powers of ideals $I \supseteq I^2 \supseteq I^3 \supseteq \cdots$. It is a technical tool that is used to [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) other key theorems such as the [Krull intersection theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_intersection_theorem).
- The [dimension theory](wikipedia://en/20519569) of commutative rings behaves poorly over non-Noetherian rings; the very fundamental theorem, [Krull's principal ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/542421), already relies on the "Noetherian" assumption. Here, in fact, the "Noetherian" assumption is often not enough and (Noetherian) [universally catenary rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_catenary_ring), those satisfying a certain dimension-theoretic assumption, are often used instead. Noetherian rings appearing in applications are mostly universally catenary.

## Implication on injective modules {#Implication_on_injective_modules}

Given a ring, there is a close connection between the behaviors of [injective modules](wikipedia://en/473775) over the ring and whether the ring is a Noetherian ring or not. Namely, given a ring *R*, the following are equivalent:

- *R* is a left Noetherian ring.
- (Bass) Each direct sum of injective left *R*-modules is injective.[^o11]
- Each injective left *R*-module is a direct sum of [indecomposable](wikipedia://en/810549) injective modules.[^o26]
- (Faith–Walker) There exists a [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173) $\mathfrak{c}$ such that each injective left module over *R* is a direct sum of $\mathfrak{c}$-generated modules (a module is $\mathfrak{c}$-generated if it has a [generating set](wikipedia://en/50718738) of [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) at most $\mathfrak{c}$).[^o27]
- There exists a left *R*-module *H* such that every left *R*-module [embeds](wikipedia://en/52119) into a direct sum of copies of *H*.[^o28]

The [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) of an indecomposable injective module is [local](wikipedia://en/153391)[^o29] and thus [Azumaya's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azumaya's_theorem) says that, over a left Noetherian ring, each indecomposable decomposition of an injective module is equivalent to one another (a variant of the [Krull–Schmidt theorem](wikipedia://en/21100715)).
