# Group object

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Group object"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **group objects** are certain generalizations of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) that are built on more complicated structures than [sets](wikipedia://en/26691). A typical example of a group object is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315), a group whose underlying set is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) such that the group operations are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Formally, we start with a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) *C* with finite products (i.e. *C* has a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) 1 and any two objects of *C* have a [product](wikipedia://en/237868)). A **group object** in *C* is an object *G* of *C* together with [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891)

- *m* : *G* × *G* → *G* (thought of as the "group multiplication")
- *e* : 1 → *G* (thought of as the "inclusion of the identity element")
- *inv* : *G* → *G* (thought of as the "inversion operation")

such that the following properties (modeled on the group axioms – more precisely, on the [definition of a group](wikipedia://en/45200#Groups) used in [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200)) are satisfied

- *m* is associative, i.e. *m* (*m* × id<sub>*G*</sub>) = *m* (id<sub>*G*</sub> × *m*) as morphisms *G* × *G* × *G* → *G*, and where e.g. *m* × id<sub>*G*</sub> : *G* × *G* × *G* → *G* × *G*; here we identify *G* × (*G* × *G*) in a canonical manner with (*G* × *G*) × *G*.
- *e* is a two-sided unit of *m*, i.e. *m* (id<sub>*G*</sub> × *e*) = *p*<sub>1</sub>, where *p*<sub>1</sub> : *G* × 1 → *G* is the canonical projection, and *m* (*e* × id<sub>*G*</sub>) = *p*<sub>2</sub>, where *p*<sub>2</sub> : 1 × *G* → *G* is the canonical projection
- *inv* is a two-sided inverse for *m*, i.e. if *d* : *G* → *G* × *G* is the diagonal map, and *e*<sub>*G*</sub> : *G* → *G* is the composition of the unique morphism *G* → 1 (also called the counit) with *e*, then *m* (id<sub>*G*</sub> × *inv*) *d* = *e*<sub>*G*</sub> and *m* (*inv* × id<sub>*G*</sub>) *d* = *e*<sub>*G*</sub>.

Note that this is stated in terms of maps – product and inverse must be maps in the category – and without any reference to underlying "elements" of the group object – categories in general do not have elements of their objects.

Another way to state the above is to say *G* is a group object in a category *C* if for every object *X* in *C*, there is a group structure on the morphisms Hom(*X*, *G*) from *X* to *G* such that the association of *X* to Hom(*X*, *G*) is a (contravariant) [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from *C* to the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092).

Yet another way to state the above is to define a group object as a [monoid object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_object) in the [cartesian monoidal category](wikipedia://en/43676277) (that is, the [monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604) where the product is × and the unit is the terminal object 1), together with an inverse morphism satisfying the above conditions.

## Examples {#Examples}

- Each set *G* for which a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) structure (*G*, *m*, *u*, <sup>−1</sup>) can be defined can be considered a group object in the category of [sets](wikipedia://en/27553). The map *m* is the group operation, the map *e* (whose domain is a [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798)) picks out the identity element *u* of *G*, and the map *inv* assigns to every group element its inverse. *e*<sub>*G*</sub> : *G* → *G* is the map that sends every element of *G* to the identity element.
- A [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) is a group object in the category of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/29954) with [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29).
- A [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) is a group object in the category of [smooth manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) with [smooth maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map).
- A [Lie supergroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_supergroup) is a group object in the category of [supermanifolds](wikipedia://en/891255).
- An [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) is a group object in the category of [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808). In modern [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), one considers the more general [group schemes](wikipedia://en/510523), group objects in the category of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754).
- A localic group is a group object in the category of [locales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locale_%28mathematics%29).
- The group objects in the category of groups (or [monoids](wikipedia://en/19652)) are the [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974). The reason for this is that, if *inv* is assumed to be a homomorphism, then *G* must be abelian. More precisely: if *A* is an abelian group and we denote by *m* the group multiplication of *A*, by *e* the inclusion of the identity element, and by *inv* the inversion operation on *A*, then (*A*, *m*, *e*, *inv*) is a group object in the category of groups (or monoids). Conversely, if (*A*, *m*, *e*, *inv*) is a group object in one of those categories, then *m* necessarily coincides with the given operation on *A*, *e* is the inclusion of the given identity element on *A*, *inv* is the inversion operation and *A* with the given operation is an abelian group. See also [Eckmann–Hilton argument](wikipedia://en/488809).
- The strict [2-group](wikipedia://en/21368493) is the group object in the [category of small categories](wikipedia://en/2072741).
- Given a category *C* with finite [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889), a **cogroup object** is an object *G* of *C* together with a "comultiplication" *m*: *G* → *G* $\oplus$ *G,* a "coidentity" *e*: *G* → 0, and a "coinversion" *inv*: *G* → *G* that satisfy the [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) versions of the axioms for group objects. Here 0 is the [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object) of *C*. Cogroup objects occur naturally in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).
