# Imaginary unit

*Image: ImaginaryUnit5.svg.* The imaginary unit $i$ in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628): Real numbers are conventionally drawn on the horizontal axis and imaginary numbers on the vertical axis.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Imaginary unit"}

The **imaginary unit**, usually denoted by $i$, is a [mathematical constant](wikipedia://en/48779551) that is a solution to the [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) $x2 = -1$, which is not solved by any [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438). Any real-number multiple of the imaginary unit is called an *[imaginary number](wikipedia://en/14554)*.

:::

By combining the real numbers with the imaginary unit using [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), a new number system known as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) is formed; it consists of all numbers of the form $a + bi$ with real numbers $a$ and $b$.

There are two complex [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208) of $-1$: the imaginary unit $i$ and its [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) $-i$. More generally, every nonzero complex number has two distinct complex-valued square roots, which are additive inverses of each other, while [zero](wikipedia://en/34513) has only zero as its ([double](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_root)) square root.

Historically, the imaginary unit was denoted by $\sqrt{-1}$, though this is now rare. In contexts in which use of the letter $i$ is ambiguous or problematic, the letter $j$ is sometimes used instead. For example, in [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) the imaginary unit is normally denoted by $j$ instead of $i$, because $i$ is commonly used to denote [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

Square roots of negative numbers are called *imaginary* because in [early-modern mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220#Renaissance), only what are now called [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), obtainable by physical measurements or basic arithmetic, were considered to be numbers at all—even [negative numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_numbers) were treated with skepticism—so the square root of a negative number was previously considered undefined or nonsensical. The name *imaginary* is generally credited to [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), and [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) used the term as early as 1670.[^o5] The $i$ notation was introduced by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902).[^o7]

The imaginary unit $i$ is defined solely by the property that its square is −1:

$$i^2 = -1.$$

With $i$ defined this way, it follows directly from [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) that $i$ and $-i$ are both square roots of −1.

Although the construction is called *imaginary*, and although the concept of an imaginary number may be intuitively more difficult to grasp than that of a real number, the construction is valid from a mathematical standpoint. Real number operations can be extended to imaginary and complex numbers, by treating $i$ as an unknown quantity while manipulating an expression (and using the definition to replace any occurrence of $i2$ with $-1$). Higher integral powers of $i$ are thus

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} i^3 &= i^2 i &&= (-1) i &&= -i, \\[3mu] i^4 &= i^3 i &&= \;\!(-i) i &&= \ \,1, \\[3mu] i^5 &= i^4 i &&= \ \, (1) i &&= \ \ i, \end{alignedat}$$

and so on, cycling through the four values $1$, $i$, $-1$, and $-i$. As with any non-zero real number, $i0 = 1.$

As a complex number, $i$ can be represented in [rectangular form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_coordinate_system) as $0 + 1i$, with a zero real component and a unit imaginary component. In [polar form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_form), $i$ can be represented as $1 \times e\pi i /2$ (or just $e\pi i /2$), with an [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) (or magnitude) of 1 and an [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) (or angle) of $\tfrac\pi2$ [radians](wikipedia://en/26003). (Adding any integer multiple of $2\pi$ to this angle works as well.) In the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), which is a special interpretation of a [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane), $i$ is the point located one unit from the origin along the [imaginary axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_axis) (which is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the [real axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_axis)).

### $i$ vs. $-i$ {#vs.}

Being a [quadratic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) with no [multiple root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_root), the defining equation $x2 = -1$ has two distinct solutions, which are equally valid and which happen to be [additive](wikipedia://en/228312) and [multiplicative inverses](wikipedia://en/229940) of each other. Although the two solutions are distinct numbers, their properties are indistinguishable; there is no property that one has that the other does not. One of these two solutions is labelled $+i$ (or simply $i$) and the other is labelled $-i$, though it is inherently ambiguous which is which.

The only differences between $+i$ and $-i$ arise from this labelling. For example, by convention $+i$ is said to have an [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) of $+\tfrac\pi2$ and $-i$ is said to have an argument of $-\tfrac\pi2,$ related to the convention of labelling orientations in the [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane) relative to the positive $x$-axis with positive angles turning [anticlockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticlockwise) in the direction of the positive $y$-axis. Also, despite the signs written with them, neither $+i$ nor $-i$ is inherently positive or negative in the sense that real numbers are.[^o12]

A more formal expression of this indistinguishability of $+i$ and $-i$ is that, although the complex [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29) is [unique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_%28mathematics%29) (as an extension of the real numbers) [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), it is not unique up to a unique isomorphism. That is, there are two [field automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ that keep each real number fixed, namely the identity and [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation). For more on this general phenomenon, see [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673).

### Matrices {#Matrices}

Using the concepts of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) and [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280), complex numbers can be represented in linear algebra. The real unit $1$ and imaginary unit $i$ can be represented by any pair of matrices $I$ and $J$ satisfying $I2 = I,$ $IJ = JI = J,$ and $J2 = -I.$ Then a complex number $a + bi$ can be represented by the matrix $aI + bJ,$ and all of the ordinary rules of complex arithmetic can be derived from the rules of matrix arithmetic.

The most common choice is to represent $1$ and $i$ by the $2 \times 2$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) $I$ and the matrix $J$,

$$I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then an arbitrary complex number $a + bi$ can be represented by:

$$aI + bJ = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}.$$

More generally, any real-valued $2 \times 2$ matrix with a [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of zero and a [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of one squares to $-I$, so could be chosen for $J$. Larger matrices could also be used; for example, $1$ could be represented by the $4 \times 4$ identity matrix and $i$ could be represented by any of the [Dirac matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_matrices) for spatial dimensions.

### Root of $x^{2} + 1$ {#Root_of}

[Polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) (weighted sums of the powers of a variable) are a basic tool in algebra. Polynomials whose [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) are real numbers form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), denoted $\mathbb{R}[x],$ an algebraic structure with addition and multiplication and sharing many properties with the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563).

The polynomial $x^2 + 1$ has no real-number [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial), but the set of all real-coefficient polynomials divisible by $x^2 + 1$ forms an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977), and so there is a [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/373065#Quotient_ring) $\mathbb{R}[x] / \langle x^2 + 1\rangle.$ This quotient ring is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the field of complex numbers, and the variable $x$ expresses the imaginary unit.

### Graphic representation {#Graphic_representation}

The complex numbers can be represented graphically by drawing the real [number line](wikipedia://en/287188) as the horizontal axis and the imaginary numbers as the vertical axis of a [Cartesian plane](wikipedia://en/7706) called the *[complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628)*. In this representation, the numbers $1$ and $i$ are at the same distance from $0$, with a right angle between them. Addition by a complex number corresponds to [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) in the plane, while multiplication by a unit-magnitude complex number corresponds to rotation about the origin. Every [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377) transformation of the plane can be represented by a complex-linear function $z \mapsto az + b.$

### Geometric algebra {#Geometric_algebra}

In the [geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/12939) of the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), the geometric product or quotient of two arbitrary [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) is a sum of a scalar (real number) part and a [bivector](wikipedia://en/884040) part. (A scalar is a quantity with no orientation, a vector is a quantity oriented like a line, and a bivector is a quantity oriented like a plane.) The square of any vector is a positive scalar, representing its length squared, while the square of any bivector is a negative scalar.

The quotient of a vector with itself is the scalar $1 = u/u$, and when multiplied by any vector leaves it unchanged (the [identity transformation](wikipedia://en/15069)). The quotient of any two perpendicular vectors of the same magnitude, $J = u/v$, which when multiplied rotates the divisor a quarter turn into the dividend, $Jv = u$, is a unit bivector which squares to $-1$, and can thus be taken as a representative of the imaginary unit. Any sum of a scalar and bivector can be multiplied by a vector to scale and rotate it, and the algebra of such sums is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the algebra of complex numbers. In this interpretation points, vectors, and sums of scalars and bivectors are all distinct types of geometric objects.[^o17]

More generally, in the geometric algebra of any higher-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), a unit bivector of any arbitrary planar orientation squares to $-1$, so can be taken to represent the imaginary unit $i$.

## Proper use {#Proper_use}

The imaginary unit was historically written $\sqrt{-1},$ and still is in some modern works. However, great care needs to be taken when manipulating formulas involving [radicals](wikipedia://en/235029). The radical sign notation $\sqrt{x}$ is reserved either for the principal (positive) square root of a positive real number or for the [principal square root of a complex number](wikipedia://en/29208#Principal_square_root_of_a_complex_number). Attempting to apply the calculation rules of square roots of positive real numbers to manipulate square roots of complex numbers can produce false results:[^o19]

$$-1 = i \cdot i = \sqrt{-1} \cdot \sqrt{-1} \mathrel{\stackrel{\mathrm{fallacy}}{=}} {\textstyle \sqrt{(-1) \cdot (-1)}} = \sqrt{1} = 1 \qquad \text{(incorrect).}$$

Generally, the calculation rules $\sqrt{x\vphantom{ty}} \cdot\! \sqrt{y\vphantom{ty}} = \sqrt{x \cdot y\vphantom{ty}}$ and $\sqrt{x\vphantom{ty}}\big/\!\sqrt{y\vphantom{ty}} = \sqrt{x/y}$ are guaranteed to be valid only when $x$ and $y$ are both positive real numbers.[^o20][^o21][^o22]

When $x$ or $y$ is real but negative, these problems can be avoided by writing and manipulating expressions like $i \sqrt{7}$, rather than $\sqrt{-7}$. For a more thorough discussion, see the articles [Square root](wikipedia://en/29208) and [Branch point](wikipedia://en/483173).

## Properties {#Properties}

As a complex number, the imaginary unit follows all of the rules of [complex arithmetic](wikipedia://en/5826#Relations_and_operations).

### Imaginary integers and imaginary numbers {#Imaginary_integers_and_imaginary_numbers}

When the imaginary unit is repeatedly added or subtracted, the result is some [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) times the imaginary unit, an *imaginary integer*; any such numbers can be added and the result is also an imaginary integer:

$$ai + bi = (a + b)i.$$

Thus, the imaginary unit is the generator of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under addition, specifically an infinite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327).

The imaginary unit can also be multiplied by any arbitrary [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) to form an [imaginary number](wikipedia://en/14554). These numbers can be pictured on a [number line](wikipedia://en/287188), the *imaginary axis*, which as part of the complex plane is typically drawn with a vertical orientation, perpendicular to the real axis which is drawn horizontally.

### Gaussian integers {#Gaussian_integers}

Integer sums of the real unit $1$ and the imaginary unit $i$ form a [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853) in the complex plane called the [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers). The sum, difference, or product of Gaussian integers is also a Gaussian integer:

$$\begin{aligned} (a + bi) + (c + di) &= (a + c) + (b + d)i, \\[5mu] (a + bi)(c + di) &= (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i. \end{aligned}$$

### Quarter-turn rotation {#Quarter-turn_rotation}

When multiplied by the imaginary unit $i$, any arbitrary complex number in the complex plane is rotated by a quarter turn ($\tfrac12\pi$ radians or $90^{\circ}$) [anticlockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticlockwise). When multiplied by $-i$, any arbitrary complex number is rotated by a quarter turn clockwise. In polar form:

$$i \, re^{\varphi i} = re^{(\varphi + \pi/2)i}, \quad -i \, re^{\varphi i} = re^{(\varphi - \pi/2)i}.$$

In rectangular form,

$$i(a + bi) = -b + ai, \quad -i(a + bi) = b - ai.$$

### Integer powers {#Integer_powers}

The powers of $i$ repeat in a cycle expressible with the following pattern, where $n$ is any integer:

$$i^{4n} = 1, \quad i^{4n+1} = i, \quad i^{4n+2} = -1, \quad i^{4n+3} = -i.$$

Thus, under multiplication, $i$ is a generator of a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 4, a discrete subgroup of the continuous [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) of the unit complex numbers under multiplication.

Written as a special case of [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613) for an integer $n$,

$$i^n = {\exp}\bigl(\tfrac12\pi i\bigr)^n = {\exp}\bigl(\tfrac12 n \pi i\bigr) = {\cos}\bigl(\tfrac12 n\pi \bigr) + {i \sin}\bigl(\tfrac12 n\pi \bigr).$$

With a careful choice of [branch cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branch_cut) and [principal values](wikipedia://en/408225), this last equation can also apply to arbitrary complex values of $n$, including cases like $n = i$.

### Roots {#Roots}

*Image: Imaginary2Root.svg.* The two square roots of $i$ in the complex plane

Just like all nonzero complex numbers, $i = e^{\pi i/ 2}$ has two distinct [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208) which are [additive inverses](wikipedia://en/228312). In polar form, they are

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} \sqrt{i} &= {\exp}\bigl(\tfrac12{\pi i}\bigr)^{1/2} &&{}= {\exp}\bigl(\tfrac14\pi i\bigr), \\ -\sqrt{i} &= {\exp}\bigl(\tfrac14{\pi i}-\pi i\bigr) &&{}= {\exp}\bigl({-\tfrac34\pi i}\bigr). \end{alignedat}$$

In rectangular form, they are

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} \sqrt{i} &= \frac{1 + i}{ \sqrt{2}} &&{}= \phantom{-}\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}i, \\[5mu] -\sqrt{i} &= - \frac{1 + i}{ \sqrt{2}} &&{}= - \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}i. \end{alignedat}$$

Squaring either expression yields

$$\left( \pm \frac{1 + i}{\sqrt2} \right)^2 = \frac{1 + 2i - 1}{2} = \frac{2i}{2} = i.$$

*Image: Imaginary3Root.svg.* The three cube roots of $i$ in the complex plane

The three [cube roots](wikipedia://en/292506) of $i$ are[^o30]

$$\sqrt[3]i = {\exp}\bigl(\tfrac16 \pi i\bigr) = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \tfrac12i, \quad {\exp}\bigl(\tfrac56 \pi i\bigr) = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \tfrac12i, \quad {\exp}\bigl({-\tfrac12 \pi i}\bigr) = -i.$$

For a general positive integer $n$, the [$n$-th roots](wikipedia://en/235029) of $i$ are, for $k = 0, 1, ..., n - 1,$

$$\exp \left(2 \pi i \frac{k+\frac14}{n} \right) = \cos \left(\frac{4k+1}{2n}\pi \right) + i \sin \left(\frac{4k+1}{2n}\pi \right).$$

The value associated with $k = 0$ is the [principal](wikipedia://en/408225) $n$-th root of $i$. The set of roots equals the corresponding set of [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) rotated by the principal $n$-th root of $i$. These are the vertices of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) inscribed within the complex [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).

### Exponential and logarithm {#Exponential_and_logarithm}

The [complex exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_exponential) function relates complex addition in the domain to [complex multiplication](wikipedia://en/537183) in the codomain. Real values in the domain represent scaling in the codomain (multiplication by a real scalar) with $1$ representing multiplication by $e$, while imaginary values in the domain represent rotation in the codomain (multiplication by a unit complex number) with $i$ representing a rotation by $1$ radian. The complex exponential is thus a [periodic function](wikipedia://en/76848) in the imaginary direction, with period $2\pi i$ and image $1$ at points $2k\pi i$ for all integers $k$, a real multiple of the lattice of imaginary integers.

The complex exponential can be broken into [even and odd](wikipedia://en/592151) components, the [hyperbolic functions](wikipedia://en/56567) $\cosh$ and $\sinh$ or the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) $\cos$ and $\sin$:

$$\exp z = \cosh z + \sinh z = \cos(-iz) + i\sin(-iz)$$

[Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613) decomposes the exponential of an imaginary number representing a rotation:

$$\exp i\varphi = \cos \varphi + i\sin \varphi.$$

This fact can be used to demonstrate, among other things, the apparently counterintuitive result that $i^i$ is a real number.[^o32]

The quotient $\coth z = \cosh z / \sinh z,$ with appropriate scaling, can be represented as an infinite [partial fraction decomposition](wikipedia://en/247288) as the sum of [reciprocal functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_function) translated by imaginary integers:[^o33]

$$\pi \coth \pi z = \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=-n}^n \frac{1}{z + ki}.$$

Other functions based on the complex exponential are well-defined with imaginary inputs. For example, a number raised to the $ni$ power is:

$$x^{n i} = \cos(n\ln x) + i \sin(n\ln x ).$$

Because the exponential is periodic, its inverse the [complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290) is a [multi-valued function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-valued_function), with each complex number in the domain corresponding to multiple values in the codomain, separated from each-other by any integer multiple of $2\pi i.$ One way of obtaining a single-valued function is to treat the codomain as a [cylinder](wikipedia://en/859283), with complex values separated by any integer multiple of $2\pi i$ treated as the same value; another is to take the domain to be a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) consisting of multiple copies of the complex plane stitched together along the negative real axis as a [branch cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branch_cut), with each branch in the domain corresponding to one infinite strip in the codomain.[^o34] Functions depending on the complex logarithm therefore depend on careful choice of branch to define and evaluate clearly.

For example, if one chooses any branch where $\ln i = \tfrac12 \pi i$ then when $x$ is a positive real number,

$$\log_i x = -\frac{2i \ln x }{\pi}.$$

### Factorial {#Factorial}

The [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) of the imaginary unit $i$ is most often given in terms of the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) evaluated at $1 + i$:[^o36]

$$i! = \Gamma(1+i) = i\Gamma(i) = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-t} \cos(\ln t) \, dt + i \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-t} \sin(\ln t) \, dt \approx 0.4980 - 0.1549\,i.$$

The magnitude and argument of this number are:[^o37]

$$|\Gamma(1+i)| = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{ \sinh \pi}} \approx 0.5216, \quad \arg{\Gamma(1+i)} \approx -0.3016.$$
