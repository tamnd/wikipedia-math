# Initial and terminal objects

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Initial and terminal objects"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **initial object** of a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) $C$ is an object $I$ in $C$ such that for every object $X$ in $C$, there exists precisely one [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) $I \to X$.

:::

The [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) notion is that of a **terminal object** (also called **terminal element**): $T$ is terminal if for every object $X$ in $C$ there exists exactly one morphism $X \to T$. Initial objects are also called **coterminal** or **universal**, and terminal objects are also called **final**.

If an object is both initial and terminal, it is called a **zero object** or **null object**. A **pointed category** is one with a zero object.

A **[strict initial object](wikipedia://en/46597851)** $I$ is one for which every morphism into $I$ is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) (**strict terminal objects** are defined analogously).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is the unique initial object in **Set**, the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829). Every one-element set ([singleton](wikipedia://en/381798)) is a terminal object in this category; there are no zero objects. Similarly, the empty space is the unique initial object in **Top**, the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) and every one-point space is a terminal object in this category.
- In the category **[Rel](wikipedia://en/5930730)** of sets and relations, the empty set is the unique initial object, the unique terminal object, and hence the unique zero object.

*Image: Terminal and initial object.svg.* Morphisms of pointed sets. The image also applies to algebraic zero objects

- In the category of [pointed sets](wikipedia://en/1242444) (whose objects are non-empty sets together with a distinguished element; a morphism from $(A,\,a)$ to $(B,\,b)$ being a function $f : A \to B$ with $f(a) = b$), every singleton is a zero object. Similarly, in the category of [pointed topological spaces](wikipedia://en/1236666), every singleton is a zero object.
- In **Grp**, the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), any [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) is a zero object. The trivial object is also a zero object in **Ab**, the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748), **Rng** the [category of pseudo-rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_pseudo-rings), ***R*-Mod**, the [category of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812) over a ring, and ***K*-Vect**, the [category of vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_spaces) over a field. See *[Zero object (algebra)](wikipedia://en/7455889)* for details. This is the origin of the term "zero object".
- In **Ring**, the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181) with unity and unity-preserving morphisms, the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) **Z** is an initial object. The [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) consisting only of a single element $0 = 1$ is a terminal object.
- In **Rig**, the category of [rigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rig_%28mathematics%29) with unity and unity-preserving morphisms, the rig of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) **N** is an initial object. The zero rig, which is the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874), consisting only of a single element $0 = 1$ is a terminal object.
- In **Field**, the [category of fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_fields), there are no initial or terminal objects. However, in the subcategory of fields of fixed characteristic, the [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field) is an initial object.
- Any [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) $(P,\,\le)$ can be interpreted as a category: the objects are the elements of $P$, and there is a single morphism from $x$ to $y$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $x \le y$. This category has an initial object if and only if $P$ has a [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element); it has a terminal object if and only if $P$ has a [greatest element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_element).
- **Cat**, the [category of small categories](wikipedia://en/2072741) with [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) as morphisms has the empty category, **0** (with no objects and no morphisms), as initial object and the terminal category, **1** (with a single object with a single identity morphism), as terminal object.
- In the category of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754), Spec(**Z**), the [prime spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of the ring of integers, is a terminal object. The empty scheme (equal to the prime spectrum of the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874)) is an initial object.
- A [limit](wikipedia://en/39248) of a [diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625) *F* may be characterised as a terminal object in the [category of cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_cones) to *F*. Likewise, a colimit of *F* may be characterised as an initial object in the category of co-cones from *F*.
- In the category **Ch<sub>*R*</sub>** of chain complexes over a commutative ring *R*, the zero complex is a zero object.
- In a [short exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) of the form 0 → *a* → *b* → *c* → 0, the initial and terminal objects are the anonymous zero object. This is used frequently in [cohomology theories.](wikipedia://en/194060)
- In the category of types the [empty type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_type) is the initial object and the [unit type](wikipedia://en/3646268) is the terminal object.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Existence and uniqueness"}

### Existence and uniqueness {#Existence_and_uniqueness}

Initial and terminal objects are not required to exist in a given category. However, if they do exist, they are essentially unique. Specifically, if $I_{1}$ and $I_{2}$ are two different initial objects, then there is a unique [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) between them. Moreover, if $I$ is an initial object then any object isomorphic to $I$ is also an initial object. The same is true for terminal objects.

For [complete categories](wikipedia://en/62781) there is an existence theorem for initial objects. Specifically, a ([locally small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_small_category)) complete category $C$ has an initial object if and only if there exist a set $I$ (not a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class)) and an $I$-[indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) $(K_{i})$ of objects of $C$ such that for any object $X$ of $C$, there is at least one morphism $K_{i} \to X$ for some $i \in I$.

:::

### Equivalent formulations {#Equivalent_formulations}

Terminal objects in a category $C$ may also be defined as [limits](wikipedia://en/39248) of the unique empty [diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625) $\mathbf{0} \to C$. Since the empty category is vacuously a [discrete category](wikipedia://en/808519), a terminal object can be thought of as an [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289) (a product is indeed the limit of the discrete diagram $\{X_{i}\}$, in general). Dually, an initial object is a [colimit](wikipedia://en/39248) of the empty diagram $\mathbf{0} \to C$ and can be thought of as an [empty](wikipedia://en/209748) [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) or categorical sum.

It follows that any [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) which preserves limits will take terminal objects to terminal objects, and any functor which preserves colimits will take initial objects to initial objects. For example, the initial object in any [concrete category](wikipedia://en/167001) with [free objects](wikipedia://en/333835) will be the free object generated by the empty set (since the [free functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_functor), being [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) to the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) to **Set**, preserves colimits).

Initial and terminal objects may also be characterized in terms of [universal properties](wikipedia://en/32245) and [adjoint functors](wikipedia://en/53991). Let **1** be the discrete category with a single object (denoted by •), and let $U : C \to \mathbf{1}$ be the unique (constant) functor to **1**. Then

- An initial object $I$ in $C$ is a [universal morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_morphism) from • to $U$. The functor which sends • to $I$ is left adjoint to *U*.
- A terminal object $T$ in $C$ is a universal morphism from $U$ to •. The functor which sends • to $T$ is right adjoint to $U$.

### Relation to other categorical constructions {#Relation_to_other_categorical_constructions}

Many natural constructions in category theory can be formulated in terms of finding an initial or terminal object in a suitable category.

- A [universal morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_morphism) from an object $X$ to a functor $U$ can be defined as an initial object in the [comma category](wikipedia://en/840950) $(X \downarrow U)$. Dually, a universal morphism from $U$ to $X$ is a terminal object in $(U \downarrow X)$.
- The limit of a diagram $F$ is a terminal object in $\operatorname{Cone}(F)$, the [category of cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_cones) to $F$. Dually, a colimit of $F$ is an initial object in the category of cones from $F$.
- A [representation of a functor](wikipedia://en/844461) $F$ to **Set** is an initial object in the [category of elements](wikipedia://en/4877413) of $F$.
- The notion of [final functor](wikipedia://en/42681604) (respectively, initial functor) is a generalization of the notion of final object (respectively, initial object).

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

- The [endomorphism monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endomorphism_monoid) of an initial or terminal object $I$ is trivial: $\operatorname{End}(I) = \operatorname{Hom}(I, I) = \{\operatorname{id}_{I}\}$.
- If a category $C$ has a zero object $0$, then for any pair of objects $X$ and $Y$ in $C$, the unique composition $X \to 0 \to Y$ is a [zero morphism](wikipedia://en/320638) from $X$ to $Y$.
