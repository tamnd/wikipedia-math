# Interior (topology)

*Image: Interior illustration.svg.* The point $x$ is an interior point of $S$. The point $y$ is on the boundary of $S$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Interior"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [topology](wikipedia://en/178649), the **interior** of a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of all subsets of $S$ that are [open](wikipedia://en/39358) in $X$. A point that is in the interior of $S$ is an **interior point** of $S$. The interior of $S$ is the [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_complement) of the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of the complement of $S$. In this sense interior and closure are [dual](wikipedia://en/609737#Duality_in_logic_and_set_theory) notions.

:::

The **exterior** of a set $S$ is the complement of the closure of $S$; it consists of the points that are in neither the set nor its [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824). The interior, boundary, and exterior of a subset together [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) the whole space into three blocks (or fewer when one or more of these is [empty](wikipedia://en/9566)).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Interior point {#Interior_point}

If $S$ is a subset of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), then $x$ is an interior point of $S$ if there exists an [open ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) centered at $x$ which is completely contained in $S.$ (This is illustrated in the introductory section to this article.)

This definition generalizes to any subset $S$ of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) $X$ with metric $d$: $x$ is an interior point of $S$ if there exists a real number $r > 0,$ such that $y$ is in $S$ whenever the distance $d(x, y) < r.$

This definition generalizes to [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) by replacing "open ball" with "[open set](wikipedia://en/39358)". If $S$ is a subset of a topological space $X$ then $x$ is an interior point of $S$ in $X$ if $x$ is contained in an open subset of $X$ that is completely contained in $S.$ (Equivalently, $x$ is an interior point of $S$ if $S$ is a [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $x.$)

### Interior of a set {#Interior_of_a_set}

The **interior** of a subset $S$ of a topological space $X,$ denoted by $\operatorname{int}_X S$ or $\operatorname{int} S$ or $S^\circ,$ can be defined in any of the following equivalent ways:

1. $\operatorname{int} S$ is the largest open subset of $X$ contained in $S.$
1. $\operatorname{int} S$ is the union of all open sets of $X$ contained in $S.$
1. $\operatorname{int} S$ is the set of all interior points of $S.$

If the space $X$ is understood from context then the shorter notation $\operatorname{int} S$ is usually preferred to $\operatorname{int}_X S.$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Set of real numbers with epsilon-neighbourhood.svg.* $a$ is an interior point of $M$ because there is an ε-neighbourhood of $a$ which is a subset of $M.$

- In any space, the interior of the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is the empty set.
- In any space $X,$ if $S \subseteq X,$ then $\operatorname{int} S \subseteq S.$
- If $X$ is the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ (with the standard topology), then $\operatorname{int} ([0, 1]) = (0, 1)$ whereas the interior of the set $\mathbb{Q}$ of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) is empty: $\operatorname{int} \mathbb{Q} = \varnothing.$
- If $X$ is the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C},$ then $\operatorname{int} (\{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| \leq 1\}) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| < 1\}.$
- In any [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the interior of any [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) is the empty set.

On the set of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), one can put other topologies rather than the standard one:

- If $X$ is the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ with the [lower limit topology](wikipedia://en/310517), then $\operatorname{int} ([0, 1]) = [0, 1).$
- If one considers on $\mathbb{R}$ the topology in which [every set is open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology), then $\operatorname{int} ([0, 1]) = [0, 1].$
- If one considers on $\mathbb{R}$ the topology in which the only open sets are the empty set and $\mathbb{R}$ itself, then $\operatorname{int} ([0, 1])$ is the empty set.

These examples show that the interior of a set depends upon the topology of the underlying space. The last two examples are special cases of the following.

- In any [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061), since every set is open, every set is equal to its interior.
- In any [indiscrete space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_space) $X,$ since the only open sets are the empty set and $X$ itself, $\operatorname{int} X = X$ and for every [proper subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of $X,$ $\operatorname{int} S$ is the empty set.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Let $X$ be a topological space and let $S$ and $T$ be subsets of $X.$

- $\operatorname{int} S$ is [open](wikipedia://en/39358) in $X.$
- If $T$ is open in $X$ then $T \subseteq S$ if and only if $T \subseteq \operatorname{int} S.$
- $\operatorname{int} S$ is an open subset of $S$ when $S$ is given the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926).
- $S$ is an open subset of $X$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\operatorname{int} S = S.$
- Intensive: $\operatorname{int} S \subseteq S.$
- [Idempotence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent): $\operatorname{int} (\operatorname{int} S) = \operatorname{int} S.$
- Preserves/[distributes over](wikipedia://en/103118) binary intersection: $\operatorname{int} (S \cap T) = (\operatorname{int} S) \cap (\operatorname{int} T).$
  - However, the interior operator does not distribute over unions since only $\operatorname{int} (S \cup T) ~\supseteq~ (\operatorname{int} S) \cup (\operatorname{int} T)$ is guaranteed in general and equality might not hold.[^o20] For example, if $X = \mathbb{R}, S = (-\infty, 0],$ and $T = (0, \infty)$ then $(\operatorname{int} S) \cup (\operatorname{int} T) = (-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty) = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ is a proper subset of $\operatorname{int} (S \cup T) = \operatorname{int} \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}.$
- Monotone/nondecreasing with respect to $\subseteq$: If $S \subseteq T$ then $\operatorname{int} S \subseteq \operatorname{int} T.$

Other properties include:

- If $S$ is closed in $X$ and $\operatorname{int} T = \varnothing$ then $\operatorname{int} (S \cup T) = \operatorname{int} S.$

**Relationship with closure**

The above statements will remain true if all instances of the symbols/words

"interior", "int", "open", "subset", and "largest"

are respectively replaced by

"[closure](wikipedia://en/42309)", "cl", "closed", "superset", and "smallest"

and the following symbols are swapped:

1. "$\subseteq$" swapped with "$\supseteq$"
1. "$\cup$" swapped with "$\cap$"

For more details on this matter, see [interior operator](wikipedia://en/55610#Interior_operator) below or the article [Kuratowski closure axioms](wikipedia://en/331597).

:::

## Interior operator {#Interior_operator}

The **interior operator** $\operatorname{int}_X$ is dual to the [closure operator](wikipedia://en/42309#Closure_operator), which is denoted by $\operatorname{cl}_X$ or by an overline <sup>—</sup>, in the sense that

$$\operatorname{int}_X S = X \setminus \overline{(X \setminus S)}$$

and also

$$\overline{S} = X \setminus \operatorname{int}_X (X \setminus S),$$

where $X$ is the [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) containing $S,$ and the backslash $\,\setminus\,$ denotes [set-theoretic difference](wikipedia://en/54347). Therefore, the abstract theory of closure operators and the [Kuratowski closure axioms](wikipedia://en/331597) can be readily translated into the language of interior operators, by replacing sets with their complements in $X.$

In general, the interior operator does not commute with unions. However, in a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832) the following result does hold:

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (C. Ursescu)"}

**Theorem[^o11] (C. Ursescu).**

Let $S_1, S_2, \ldots$ be a sequence of subsets of a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832) $X.$

If each $S_i$ is closed in $X$ then

$${\operatorname{cl}_X} \biggl( \bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \operatorname{int}_X S_i \biggr) = {\operatorname{cl}_X \operatorname{int}_X} \biggl( \bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}} S_i \biggr).$$

If each $S_i$ is open in $X$ then

$${\operatorname{int}_X} \biggl( \bigcap_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \operatorname{cl}_X S_i \biggr) = {\operatorname{int}_X \operatorname{cl}_X} \biggl( \bigcap_{i \in \mathbb{N}} S_i \biggr).$$

:::

The result above implies that every complete metric space is a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550).

## Exterior of a set {#Exterior_of_a_set}

The **exterior** of a subset $S$ of a topological space $X,$ denoted by $\operatorname{ext}_X S$ or simply $\operatorname{ext} S,$ is the largest open set [disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoint_%28sets%29) from $S,$ namely, it is the union of all open sets in $X$ that are disjoint from $S.$ The exterior is the interior of the complement, which is the same as the complement of the closure;[^n1] in formulas,

$$\operatorname{ext} S = \operatorname{int}(X \setminus S) = X \setminus \overline{S}.$$

Similarly, the interior is the exterior of the complement:

$$\operatorname{int} S = \operatorname{ext}(X \setminus S).$$

The interior, [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824), and exterior of a set $S$ together [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) the whole space into three blocks (or fewer when one or more of these is empty):

$$X = \operatorname{int} S \cup \partial S \cup \operatorname{ext} S,$$

where $\partial S$ denotes the boundary of $S.$[^n2] The interior and exterior are always [open](wikipedia://en/39358), while the boundary is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279).

Some of the properties of the exterior operator are unlike those of the interior operator:

- The exterior operator reverses inclusions; if $S \subseteq T,$ then $\operatorname{ext} T \subseteq \operatorname{ext} S.$
- The exterior operator is not [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent). It does have the property that $\operatorname{int} S \subseteq \operatorname{ext}\left(\operatorname{ext} S\right).$

:::

## Interior-disjoint shapes {#Interior-disjoint_shapes}

*Image: Interior-disjoint.svg.* The red shapes are not interior-disjoint with the blue Triangle. The green and the yellow shapes are interior-disjoint with the blue Triangle, but only the yellow shape is entirely disjoint from the blue Triangle.

Two shapes $a$ and $b$ are called *interior-disjoint* if the intersection of their interiors is empty. Interior-disjoint shapes may or may not intersect in their boundary.
