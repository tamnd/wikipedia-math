# Demographics of the Netherlands

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Demographics of the Netherlands"}

[Demographic](wikipedia://en/45642) features of the population of the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands) include [population density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density), [ethnicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group), education level, health of the population, economic status, [religious affiliations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Netherlands) and other aspects of the [population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_%28human_biology%29).

## Population {#Population}

The [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands) is the [69th most populous](wikipedia://en/69058) country in the world. As of January 2023, the Netherlands has a population of 17,821,419.[^o10]

Between 1900 and 1950 the population almost doubled from 5.14 to 10.11 million people. From 1950 to 2000 the population increased from 10.11 to 15.92 million people, increasing by a smaller proportion but, still, at an impressive pace for a European country, recording a growth of 57.45% over a 50-year time span.[^o11]

Of countries with at least 7.5 million people, The Netherlands is the [4th most densely populated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population_density#Most_populous_countries_by_density), and is the [26th most densely populated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_and_dependencies_by_population_density) in the world overall. It is the [5th most densely populated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_and_population_of_European_countries) country in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe); the first four are [microstates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstate). The 17,821,419 million Dutch inhabitants are concentrated on an area (land surface) of 33,895 km2. This means that the country has a population density of 526 /km2. The density of 500 inhabitants/km<sup>2</sup> was reached in the first half of 2014.

As a result of these demographic characteristics, the Netherlands has had to plan its [land use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_management) strictly. Since 1946 the [Ministry of Housing, Spatial Planning and the Environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Housing,_Spatial_Planning_and_the_Environment_%28Netherlands%29) has been occupied with the national coordination of land use. Because of its high population density the Netherlands has also [reclaimed land from the sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation) by [poldering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polder). Between 1927 and 1968 an entire province – [Flevoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flevoland) – was created. As of May 2023 it houses 447,193 people.[^o12] Because of these policies, the Dutch have been able to combine high levels of population density with extremely high levels of [agricultural production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture).

Even though the Netherlands is so densely populated, it has no municipalities with a population over one million. Nevertheless, the two largest municipalities of the country do score well over a million if the complete city region is counted, thus including the neighbouring satellite towns that often are physically connected to the main municipality. Moreover, the "four big cities" ([Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam), [Rotterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotterdam), [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague) and [Utrecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utrecht)) can in many ways be regarded as a single metropolitan area, the [Randstad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randstad) (*"rim city"* or *"edge city"*) with over 7.5 million inhabitants around an agricultural "green heart" (*[Groene Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groene_Hart)*).

### Fertility {#Fertility}

1000

The Dutch population is [ageing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ageing_of_the_Netherlands). Furthermore, [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669) has increased because of developments in [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine), and in addition to this, the Netherlands has seen increasing immigration. Despite these developments combined with the [population boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_boom) after the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), the low birth rate has caused extremely low population growth: 2005 saw the lowest absolute population growth since 1900.

This demographic development has consequences for [health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care) and [social security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_security) policy. As the Dutch population ages, the proportion of people of working age, as a percentage of the entire population, decreases. Important policy advisors like the [CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centraal_Bureau_voor_de_Statistiek) (Statistical Office) and the [CPB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centraal_Planbureau) (Planning Office) have pointed out that this will cause problems with the current system of old age pensions: fewer people will work to pay for old age pensions, while there will be more people receiving those pensions. Furthermore, the costs of health care are also projected to increase. These developments have caused several cabinets, notably the [second Balkenende cabinet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_cabinet_Balkenende) to reform the system of health care and social security to increase participation in the labour market and make people more conscious of the money they spend on health care.

In 2003, the annual birth rate per thousand was highest in the province of [Flevoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flevoland) (15.9). The overall lifelong [Total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) (TFR), was highest in the province of Flevoland (2.0) and lowest in the province of [Limburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburg_%28Netherlands%29) (1.6). The municipality with the highest TFR was [Urk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urk) (3.23) followed by [Valkenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valkenburg,_South_Holland) (2.83), [Graafstroom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graafstroom_%28former_municipality%29) (2.79) and [Staphorst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staphorst) (2.76). The lowest TFRs were recorded in [Vaals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaals) (1.11) and [Thorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorn,_Netherlands) (1.21).[^o18]

The total population at 31 December 2006 was 16,356,914. The population loss due to net emigration was 35,502 (an estimated 40–50% of emigrants were ethnic non-Dutch).

In 2007, there were 117,000 immigrants (including 7000 Germans, 6000 Poles, 5000 Bulgarians, 3000 Turks and 2000 Moroccans) and 123,000 emigrants. Nearly half the emigrants were native Dutch, followed at a distance by nearly 5000 Poles and more than 3000 Germans. There was an observable increase in net immigration from the former USSR, [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria) and [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania).[^o19]

The annual death rate was lowest in the municipalities of Valkenburg (2.9 per 1000), Zeewolde (3.2), Renswoude (3.4), Westervoort and Zeevang (both 3.9). The highest annual death rates were recorded in Warmond (22.3 per 1000), Laren (19.9) and Doorn (18.8).[^o20]

\16.4% of the total births in 2003 were to parents of non-European origin, although they account for only 12.4% of the population in the 25–34 age group. For example, 3.8% of the births were ethnic Moroccan, although they were only 2.26% of the 25–34 age group. Respective figures were 3.27% and 3.0% for Turks. The TFR for Moroccans in 2003 was 3.3 while the general TFR was 1.73. TFR was 2.3 for [Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_people), 1.7 for [Surinamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surinamese_people), 1.8 for [Arubans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aruba), 3.0 for [Africans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_diaspora) and 1.8 for [Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans).[^o21] (These figures compare with a figure of around 2.1 required to maintain a stable overall population figure.)

According to Statistics Netherlands, for the year 2007, the TFR for those born in Netherlands was 1.72[^o22] (1.65 in 2000). TFR of Moroccan immigrants was 2.87 (3.22 in 2000) and that of Turkish immigrants was 1.88 (2.18 in 2000).[^o23]

The [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) is the annual average number of children born per woman over her lifespan. It is based on fairly good data for the entire period. ## Migration and origin groups {#Migration_and_origin_groups}

Between 1590 and 1800 the estimated share of foreign-born population was consistently above 5%.[^o79]

According to [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat), in 2010 there were 1,800,000 foreign-born residents in the Netherlands, corresponding to 11.1% of the total population. Of these, 1,400,000 (8.5%) were born outside the EU (including those from Dutch colonies) and 428,000 (2.6%) were born in another EU member state. The most common countries of birth being: [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany), [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) and the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom).[^o76] On 1 January 2016, 26.2% of persons aged 0–50 had at least one parent born in a foreign country. 11.4% of persons aged 0–50 of 'Dutch background' belonged to the 'third generation', the majority descending from Western immigrants. The third generation constitutes persons born from two second generation immigrants or one second generation immigrant and one person with a Dutch background. First and second generation immigrants and the third generation were 34.5% of the population aged 0–50.[^o77]

As the result of [immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration) from overseas, the Netherlands have a sizeable minority of non-indigenous peoples. There is also a considerable level of emigration, in majority consisting of former immigrants. In 2005, some 121,000 people left the country, while 94,000 entered it. Out of a total of 101,150 people immigrating into the Netherlands in 2006, 66,658 were from Europe, Oceania, the Americas or Japan, and 34,492 were from other (mostly developing) countries. Out of a total of 132,470 emigrants, 94,834 were going to Europe, Oceania, the Americas or Japan and 37,636 to other countries.[^o78]

A large number[^o79] of immigrants come from countries in Western Europe, mostly from the neighbouring countries of [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) and [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium). There were five subsequent waves of immigration to the Netherlands in recent history.

1. In the late-1940s and into the 1950s, following the end of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), people from the newly independent [Republic of Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia) repatriated or emigrated to the Netherlands – mainly [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo_people) (people of mixed European and Indonesian ancestry of Dutch nationality) and supporters of the [Republic of South Maluku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_South_Maluku).
1. Between 1960 and 1974, migrants from [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece), [Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portugal), [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain), [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey) and [Morocco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco) came to work in the Netherlands as [guest workers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guest_worker). They were expected to return to their own country and many did, but others remained and in the 1970s and 1980s were joined by their families. Until 2004, when marriage immigration was restricted, their children usually married others from their home country.
1. After 1974, people emigrated from the newly independent [Suriname](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suriname) and from the [Netherlands Antilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_Antilles), which remained part of the [Kingdom of the Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_Netherlands). In 1974, about forty thousand Surinamese migrated while still retaining Dutch citizenship; between 1975 and 1980 there was a transitional arrangement allowing migration. Antilleans have the Dutch nationality and behave like typical labour migrants, travelling to and from the country in response to the employment available.
1. During the 1970s and 1980s, the number of [asylum seekers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asylum_seeker) was low, consisting e.g. of [Chileans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chileans) fleeing from political oppression and/or persecution. In the 1990s, asylum migration sharply increased,[^o80] largely consisting of [Yugoslavs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslavs), [Somalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somalis), [Iraqis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqis), [Iranians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_peoples), [Ethiopians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People_of_Ethiopia), [Eritreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritreans), [Afghans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghans) and [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_people), fleeing war or famine.[^o81] Between 2000 and 2014 asylum migration strongly decreased due to the strict "[Cohen Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_law_for_immigrants_to_the_Netherlands)". However, the [Syrian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrian_Civil_War) from 2011 resulted in a large influx of Syrian asylum seekers in 2015 and 2016; about ninety thousand Syrians had been granted asylum by 2018.
1. Since the 2000s, migrant workers and their families from the newly joined EU member states in Central and [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe), including: [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland), and [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria), and non-EU states, [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine) and the former [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia). In addition, a number of Spaniards, Greeks, Italians, and Portuguese also migrated due to the impact of the Great Recession in those countries. In 2005, non-Western ethnic population comprised 1.7 million individuals, about 10% of the population in the country.[^o82]

Illegal immigration to the Netherlands results in automatic deportation[^o83] but this is often not enforced for various reasons such as unknown country of origin, etc. Many Dutch provinces in 2012 had quotas for deporting illegal immigrants.[^o84][^o85][^o86][^o87]

By 2017, persons with a [migration background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Migration_background), both western and non-western, formed a majority in Amsterdam (2011), Rotterdam (2013) and The Hague, the three largest cities of the Netherlands.[^o88]

In 2005, the governmental *Sociaal en Cultureel Planbureau* in its annual report, noted recurring integration problems for ethnic minorities. While during the economic boom of the 1990s their unemployment and dependence on welfare had strongly decreased, the economic downturn between 2001 and 2004 disproportionally affected immigrant groups. This would be explained by their functioning as a labour reserve, by their lower educational levels and by lower language skills. The report also noted that per capita [social interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_interaction) between native Dutch and immigrant populations tended to decline over time, partly explainable by the size growth of immigrant groups. Integration levels strongly varied between groups. Surinamese and Antilleans were well economically integrated, but the latter less so culturally. Cultural integration was limited for Turks, but better for Moroccans. Of the asylum seekers, Somalis were among the least integrated into Dutch society, while Iranians were the best integrated with the highest education levels and modern lifestyles. Though the educational levels of the second generation were a significant improvement over those of the first generation, they still lagged behind the native Dutch who themselves on average had been attending ever higher school types. While half of all native Dutch pupils in 2005 proceeded to higher secondary education (HAVO and VWO), for Turks and Moroccans the share was a fifth and for Somali pupils even lower.[^o82]

In 2007, non-Western groups were generally socio-economically disadvantaged. Of the four largest non-Western groups, Turks and Moroccans were the most disadvantaged. In 2006, only 38.7% of Moroccans and 43.9% of Turks aged 15–64 were employed, meaning their unemployment rate was about four times that of the native Dutch (17.2% for Moroccans and 15.1% for Turks), and 30% received some type of [social welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_spending).[^o79]

The [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis) and the [Euro area crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_area_crisis) affected immigrant groups particularly hard. In 2012, at 12% non-Western migrants were six times more likely than native Dutch to receive social welfare benefits, with 2% for the Dutch.[^o89]

*Image: 2017 Immigrants to Netherlands low-income households.png.* Western and non-Western fraction of low-income households and source of income. Data sourced from [Statistics Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics_Netherlands).[^o90]

According to a 2010 [Statistics Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics_Netherlands) report, the third generation of non-Western immigrants was predominantly young with 80% being younger than 15, while growing with 10% each year. The third generation have a similar employment rate as the native Dutch and receive a similar amount of social benefits. The third non-Western generation follow adult education more frequently than both the native Dutch and non-Western immigrants. The non-Western immigrant population as a whole has 1.5 to 2 times the benefit dependence compared to the natives. The non-Western third generation had a rate slightly higher than the native Dutch [as crime suspects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_and_crime_in_the_Netherlands), but lower than for non-Western immigrants as a whole.[^o91][^o92]

According to Statistics Netherlands, nearly 53% of refugee households have a low income, six times the Netherlands average (8.2%).[^o90] For Syrian and Eritrean households the share is about 80%. At 33% the poverty risk is lowest among Iranian refugee households. The group of Syrian households at risk of poverty grew from ten thousand (76% of all Syrian households in 2016) to eighteen thousand (79% of all Syrian households) in 2018. Households of Polish, Romanian or Bulgarian origin have a greater than average risk of poverty even though households from these [Eastern European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe) countries generally depend on work for their income. Migrant workers from Eastern Europe generally perform low-skilled work while migrants from [Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe) are often highly educated.[^o90]

Immigrants from foreign countries are divided into several ethnic groups. For example, there are both Russians and Chechens from [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia), [Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_people) and [Kurds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_people) from [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey), [Serbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbs) and [Albanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanians) from [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbia) and immigrants from [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran) are divided into Persians, Azeris and Kurds.[^o93]

### National origins {#National_origins}

*Image: Population of the Netherlands by Country of Birth.png.* Population of the Netherlands by country of birth

, 5,271,067 citizens are of non-Dutch/[Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frisians) origin. A majority of these (51,6%) are from eight backgrounds alone: Turks (inc. Kurds), Moroccans (inc. Berbers), Surinamese, Indonesians (inc. Moluccans), Germans, Former [Soviets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_people), Poles and Syrians.[^o98]

With the huge expansion of the European Union during the 2000s, the Netherlands has seen a rise in the number of immigrants coming from new member states. Migrant workers from these countries are estimated to be about 100,000 as of 2007.[^o99] Legal migrants from new EU-member states doubled between 2007–11 to 200,000,[^o100] with estimates totaling up to 300,000. Of the [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people) who initially moved to the Netherlands in 2004, about a quarter had returned to [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) by 2006.[^o101] In addition, a large number of [Syrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syrians) moved to the Netherlands in the 2010s mostly as refugees, with the population increasing seven-fold between 2014 and 2019. Both Poles and Syrians have overtaken the population of [Antilleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_Antilles) during the decade.[^o106] More than 36,000 [Roma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people) live in the Netherlands.[^o102] Dutch Roma, [Sinti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinti) and Dutch [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews) were decimated by [the Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust).[^o103][^o104] However, the Jewish population has seen growth in the Netherlands in recent years.[^o105]

### Emigration {#Emigration}

The Netherlands have seen considerable emigration. In the 1950s and early 1960s, 560,000 people migrated to [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada), [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia), the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa) and [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand), in response to low wage levels and a government programme aiming to reduce the perceived overpopulation. At least 60,000 of these migrants were [Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo_people) (mixed Dutch–Indonesian) repatriates that moved on, mostly to the United States, or returned to Indonesia after being repatriated to the Netherlands from the former [Dutch East Indies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_East_Indies) during and after the [Indonesian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_Revolution). The official emigration policy combined with a strong economic growth already in the early 1950s led to serious manpower shortages.

In 2005, some 121,000 people migrated from the Netherlands. There is considerable migration towards neighbouring states, [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) and the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) and to the [Netherlands Antilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_Antilles). Furthermore, almost half of the current emigration consists of people returning to their country of birth, including rejected [asylum seekers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asylum_seekers), after the more stringent migration laws were implemented.

### Education by background {#Education_by_background}

According to a 2016 study by Statistics Netherlands, students with a non-western background generally study at a lower level of secondary education. 48 percent of those with a Dutch background were at [HAVO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HAVO) or [VWO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voorbereidend_wetenschappelijk_onderwijs) level at third year, compared to 23 percent for [Dutch Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turks_in_the_Netherlands), 28 percent for [Dutch Moroccans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moroccans_in_the_Netherlands), 35 percent for [Dutch Surinamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surinamese_people_in_the_Netherlands) and 31 percent for [Dutch Antilleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_the_Netherlands_Antilles). Some backgrounds fared better: over 40 percent of [Dutch Afghans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghans_in_the_Netherlands) studied at HAVO/VWO level, and for [Dutch Iranians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranians_in_the_Netherlands) the rate was 50 percent, which was above native Dutch students.[^o177]

## Religion {#Religion}

In 2025, [Statistics Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics_Netherlands) found that 16% of the population identified as Roman Catholic, 12% as Protestant, 6% as Muslim, and 7% as "other" (the last includes other Christian denominations, Hindus, Jews, and Buddhists).[^o180] In 2017, the Central Bureau of Statistics reported that for the first time non-religious people were in the majority in the Netherlands, with only 49% of people older than 15 years reported to be religious. The largest religion was Catholicism (24%), while 5% identified with Islam. In 2012 54% reported to be religious.[^o181][^o182]

## Language {#Language}

The main language is [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language), while [Frisian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Frisian_language) (known as West Frisian outside of the Netherlands) is also a recognized language in the province of Friesland and is used by the government and schools there. Several dialects of [Low Saxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Low_Saxon) (Nedersaksisch in Dutch) are spoken in much of the north and east and are recognized by the Netherlands as regional languages according to the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages. Another group of dialects granted the status of regional language is [Limburgish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburgish), which is spoken in the south-eastern province of [Limburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limburg_%28Netherlands%29). The vast majority of the population also speaks [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language_in_the_Netherlands). Major immigrant languages are [Indonesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_language), [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language), [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic), [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages), [Papiamento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papiamento), [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) and [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language).
