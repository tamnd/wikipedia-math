# Uniform convergence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Uniform convergence"}

*Image: Uniform convergence.svg.* A sequence of functions $(f_n)$ converges uniformly to $f$ when for arbitrary small $\varepsilon$ there is an index $N$ such that the graph of $f_n$ is in the $\varepsilon$-tube around $f$ whenever $n\ge N.$

*Image: Drini-nonuniformconvergence.png.* The limit of a sequence of continuous functions does not have to be continuous: the sequence of functions $f_n(x)=\sin^n(x)$ (marked in green and blue) converges pointwise over the entire domain, but the limit function is discontinuous (marked in red).

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), **uniform convergence** is a [mode of convergence](wikipedia://en/9966817) of functions stronger than [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198). A [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) $(f_n)$ **converges uniformly** to a limiting function $f$ if, roughly speaking, they **uniformly approximate** the function $f$ over the whole domain, meaning that all but finitely many of the functions of the sequence lie in a uniform [error bar](wikipedia://en/4963820) of the original function. Graphically this means that, given any thin band around the graph of $f$, the graphs of all but finitely many of the functions $f_n$ lie within that thin band. This is in contrast to pointwise convergence, in which all but finitely many of the functions lie in a thin band at each point, but the finite set of functions which must be excluded in order for that to be the case varies from point to point.

:::

The strength of uniform convergence makes it ideal in many applications, where pointwise convergence is not sufficient. For example, the uniform limit of a sequence of continuous functions is automatically continuous; the uniform limit of [Riemann integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integrable) functions is automatically Riemann integrable. With additional hypotheses, [differentiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiability) can be transferred to the limit function as well. The difference between uniform convergence and pointwise convergence was not fully appreciated early in the history of calculus, leading to instances of faulty reasoning. The concept was first formalized by [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459).

## History {#History}

In 1821 [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) published a proof that a convergent sum of continuous functions is always continuous, to which [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573) in 1826 found purported counterexamples in the context of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038), arguing that Cauchy's proof had to be incorrect. Completely standard notions of convergence did not exist at the time, and Cauchy handled convergence using infinitesimal methods. When put into the modern language, what Cauchy proved is that a uniformly convergent sequence of continuous functions has a continuous limit. The failure of a merely pointwise-convergent limit of continuous functions to converge to a continuous function illustrates the importance of distinguishing between different types of convergence when handling sequences of functions.[^o3]

The term uniform convergence was probably first used by [Christoph Gudermann](wikipedia://en/1503772), in an 1838 paper on [elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_functions), where he employed the phrase "convergence in a uniform way" when the "mode of convergence" of a series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n(x,\phi,\psi)$ is independent of the variables $\phi$ and $\psi.$ While he thought it a "remarkable fact" when a series converged in this way, he did not give a formal definition, nor use the property in any of his proofs.[^o4]

Later Gudermann's pupil, [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459), who attended his course on elliptic functions in 1839–1840, coined the term *gleichmäßig konvergent* (uniformly convergent) which he used in his 1841 paper *Zur Theorie der Potenzreihen*, published in 1894. Independently, similar concepts were articulated by [Philipp Ludwig von Seidel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Ludwig_von_Seidel)[^o5] and [George Gabriel Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gabriel_Stokes). [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) compares the three definitions in his paper "Sir George Stokes and the concept of uniform convergence" and remarks: "Weierstrass's discovery was the earliest, and he alone fully realized its far-reaching importance as one of the fundamental ideas of analysis."

Under the influence of Weierstrass and [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) this concept and related questions were intensely studied at the end of the 19th century by [Hermann Hankel](wikipedia://en/932515), [Paul du Bois-Reymond](wikipedia://en/7457861), [Ulisse Dini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulisse_Dini), [Cesare Arzelà](wikipedia://en/1683941) and others.

## Definition {#Definition}

We first define uniform convergence for [real-valued functions](wikipedia://en/22284647), although the concept is readily generalized to functions mapping to [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) and, more generally, [uniform spaces](wikipedia://en/32339) (see [below](wikipedia://en/50652#Generalizations)).[^o23]

Suppose $E$ is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) and $(f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence of real-valued functions defined on it. We say the sequence $(f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is **uniformly convergent** on $E$ with limit $f: E \to \mathbb{R}$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0,$ there exists a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $N$ such that for all $n \geq N$ and for all $x \in E$

$$\bigl|f_n(x)-f(x)\bigr|<\varepsilon.$$

The notation for uniform convergence of $f_n$ to $f$ is not quite standardized and different authors have used a variety of symbols, including (in roughly decreasing order of popularity):

$$f_n\rightrightarrows f, \quad \underset{n\to\infty}{\mathrm{unif\ lim}}f_n = f, \quad f_n \overset{\mathrm{unif.}}{\longrightarrow} f, \quad f=\mathrm{u}\!\!-\!\!\!\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n .$$

Frequently, no special symbol is used, and authors simply write

$$f_n\to f \quad \mathrm{uniformly}$$

to indicate that convergence is uniform. (In contrast, the expression $f_n\to f$ on $E$ without an adverb is taken to mean [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198) on $E$: for all $x \in E$, $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ as $n\to\infty$.)

Since $\mathbb{R}$ is a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832), the [Cauchy criterion](wikipedia://en/6085) can be used to give an equivalent alternative formulation for uniform convergence: $(f_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ converges uniformly on $E$ (in the previous sense) if and only if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a natural number $N$ such that

$$x\in E, m,n\geq N \implies \bigl|f_m(x)-f_n(x)\bigr|<\varepsilon.$$

In yet another equivalent formulation (derived by using the definition of [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602#Real_numbers) and [supremum](wikipedia://en/39382)), if we define

$$d_n = \sup_{x\in E} \bigl|f_n(x) - f(x)\bigr|,$$

then $f_n$ converges to $f$ uniformly if and only if $d_n\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$. Thus, we can characterize uniform convergence of $\textstyle (f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ on $E$ as (simple) convergence of $(f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in the [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) $\mathbb{R}^E$ with respect to the *[uniform metric](wikipedia://en/349755)* (also called the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) metric), defined by

$$d(f,g)=\sup_{x\in E} \bigl|f(x)-g(x)\bigr|.$$

Symbolically,

$$f_n\rightrightarrows f\iff d(f_n,f) \to 0.$$

The sequence $(f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is said to be **locally uniformly convergent** with limit $f$ if $E$ is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) and for every $x\in E$, there exists an $r > 0$ such that $(f_n)$ converges uniformly on $B(x,r)\cap E$ where $B(x,r)$ is a [ball](wikipedia://en/160556#In_general_metric_spaces) centered at *x* with the radius *r*. It is clear that uniform convergence implies local uniform convergence, which implies pointwise convergence.

### Notes {#Notes}

Intuitively, a sequence of functions $f_n$ converges uniformly to $f$ if, given an arbitrarily small $\varepsilon>0$, we can find an $N\in\mathbb{N}$ so that the functions $f_n$ with $n>N$ all fall within a "tube" of width $2\varepsilon$ centered around $f$ (i.e., between $f(x)-\varepsilon$ and $f(x)+\varepsilon$) for the *entire domain* of the function.

Note that interchanging the order of quantifiers in the definition of uniform convergence by moving "for all $x\in E$" in front of "there exists a natural number $N$" results in a definition of [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198) of the sequence. To make this difference explicit, in the case of uniform convergence, $N=N(\varepsilon)$ can only depend on $\varepsilon$, and the choice of $N$ has to work for all $x\in E$, for a specific value of $\varepsilon$ that is given. In contrast, in the case of pointwise convergence, $N=N(\varepsilon,x)$ may depend on both $\varepsilon$ and $x$, and the choice of $N$ only has to work for the specific values of $\varepsilon$ and $x$ that are given. Thus uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence, however the converse is not true, as the example in the section below illustrates.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

One may straightforwardly extend the concept to functions *E* → *M*, where (*M*, *d*) is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), by replacing $|f_n(x)-f(x)|$ with $d(f_n(x),f(x))$.

The most general setting is the uniform convergence of [nets](wikipedia://en/22170) of functions *E* → *X*, where *X* is a [uniform space](wikipedia://en/32339). We say that the net $(f_\alpha)$ *converges uniformly* with limit *f* : *E* → *X* if and only if for every [entourage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entourage_%28topology%29) *V* in *X*, there exists an $\alpha_0$, such that for every *x* in *E* and every $\alpha\geq \alpha_0$, $(f_\alpha(x),f(x))$ is in *V*. In this situation, uniform limit of continuous functions remains continuous.

:::

### Definition in a hyperreal setting {#Definition_in_a_hyperreal_setting}

Uniform convergence admits a simplified definition in a [hyperreal](wikipedia://en/51429) setting. Thus, a sequence $f_n$ converges to *f* uniformly if for all hyperreal *x* in the domain of ${f}^*$ and all infinite *n*, ${f}_n^*(x)$ is infinitely close to ${f}^*(x)$ (see [microcontinuity](wikipedia://en/33952467) for a similar definition of uniform continuity). In contrast, pointwise continuity requires this only for real *x*.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

For $x \in [0,1)$, a basic example of uniform convergence can be illustrated as follows: the sequence $\textstyle \bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)\vphantom)^{x+n}$ converges uniformly, while $x^n$ does not. Specifically, assume $\varepsilon=\tfrac14$. Each function $\textstyle \bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)\vphantom)^{x+n}$ is less than or equal to $\tfrac14$ when $n \geq 2$, regardless of the value of $x$. On the other hand, $x^n$ is only less than or equal to $\tfrac14$ at ever increasing values of $n$ when values of $x$ are selected closer and closer to 1 (explained more in depth further below).

Given a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X*, we can equip the space of [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued functions over *X* with the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755) topology, with the uniform metric defined by

$$d(f,g)={\|f-g\|}_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in X} \bigl|f(x)-g(x)\bigr|.$$

Then uniform convergence simply means [convergence](wikipedia://en/285773) in the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755) topology:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}{\|f_n-f\|}_{\infty}=0.$$

The sequence of functions $(f_n)$

$$\begin{cases} f_n:[0,1]\to [0,1] \\[3mu] f_n(x)=x^n \end{cases}$$

is a classic example of a sequence of functions that converges to a function $f$ pointwise but not uniformly. To show this, we first observe that the pointwise limit of $(f_n)$ as $n\to\infty$ is the function $f$, given by

$$f(x) = \lim_{n\to \infty} f_n(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \in [0,1); \\[3mu] 1, & x=1. \end{cases}$$

*Pointwise convergence:* Convergence is trivial for $x=0$ and $x=1$, since $f_n(0)=f(0)=0$ and $f_n(1)=f(1)=1$, for all $n$. For $x \in (0,1)$ and given $\varepsilon>0$, we can ensure that $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $n\geq N$ by choosing $N = \lceil\log\varepsilon/\log x\rceil$, which is the minimum integer exponent of $x$ that allows it to reach or dip below $\varepsilon$ (here the upper square brackets indicate rounding up, see [ceiling function](wikipedia://en/54267)). Hence, $f_n\to f$ pointwise for all $x\in[0,1]$. Note that the choice of $N$ depends on the value of $\varepsilon$ and $x$. Moreover, for a fixed choice of $\varepsilon$, $N$ (which cannot be defined to be smaller) grows without bound as $x$ approaches 1. These observations preclude the possibility of uniform convergence.

*Non-uniformity of convergence:* The convergence is not uniform, because we can find an $\varepsilon>0$ so that no matter how large we choose $N,$ there will be values of $x \in [0,1]$ and $n \geq N$ such that $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\geq\varepsilon.$ To see this, first observe that regardless of how large $n$ becomes, there is always an $x_0 \in [0,1)$ such that $f_n(x_0)=\tfrac12.$ Thus, if we choose $\varepsilon = \tfrac14,$ we can never find an $N$ such that $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $x\in[0,1]$ and $n\geq N$. Explicitly, whatever candidate we choose for $N$, consider the value of $f_N$ at $\textstyle x_0 = \bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)\vphantom)^{1/N}$. Since

$$\bigl|f_N(x_0) - f(x_0)\bigr| = \left| {\Bigl( \bigl(\tfrac{1}{2} \bigr)^{1/N} \Bigr)\vphantom\big|^N\! - 0}~\! \right| = \tfrac{1}{2} > \tfrac{1}{4} = \varepsilon,$$

the candidate fails because we have found an example of an $x\in[0,1]$ that "escaped" our attempt to "confine" each $f_n\ (n\geq N)$ to within $\varepsilon$ of $f$ for all $x\in[0,1]$. In fact, it is easy to see that

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_n-f\|_{\infty}=1,$$

contrary to the requirement that $\|f_n-f\|_{\infty}\to 0$ if $f_n \rightrightarrows f$.

In this example one can easily see that pointwise convergence does not preserve differentiability or continuity. While each function of the sequence is smooth, that is to say that for all *n*, $f_n\in C^{\infty}([0,1])$, the limit $\lim_{n\to \infty}f_n$ is not even continuous.

### Exponential function {#Exponential_function}

The series expansion of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) can be shown to be uniformly convergent on any bounded subset $S \subset \mathbb{C}$ using the [Weierstrass M-test](wikipedia://en/444456).

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem (Weierstrass M-test)"}

**Theorem (Weierstrass M-test).** *Let $(f_n)$ be a sequence of functions $f_n:E\to \mathbb{C}$ and let $M_n$ be a sequence of positive real numbers such that $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ for all $x\in E$ and $n=1,2, 3, \ldots$ If $\sum_n M_n$ converges, then $\sum_n f_n$ converges absolutely and uniformly on $E$.*

The complex exponential function can be expressed as the series:

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n!}.$$

Any bounded subset is a subset of some disc $D_R$ of radius $R,$ centered on the origin in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). The Weierstrass M-test requires us to find an upper bound $M_n$ on the terms of the series, with $M_n$ independent of the position in the disc:

$$\left| \frac{z^n}{n!} \right|\le M_n, \forall z\in D_R.$$

To do this, we notice

$$\left| \frac{z^n}{n!}\right| \le \frac{|z|^n}{n!} \le \frac{R^n}{n!}$$

and take $M_n=\tfrac{R^n}{n!}.$

If $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_n$ is convergent, then the M-test asserts that the original series is uniformly convergent.

The [ratio test](wikipedia://en/421341) can be used here:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{M_{n+1}}{M_n}=\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{R^{n+1}}{R^n}\frac{n!}{(n+1)!}=\lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{R}{n+1}=0$$

which means the series over $M_n$ is convergent. Thus the original series converges uniformly for all $z\in D_R,$ and since $S\subset D_R$, the series is also uniformly convergent on $S.$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- Every uniformly convergent sequence is locally uniformly convergent.
- Every locally uniformly convergent sequence is [compactly convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactly_convergent).
- For [locally compact spaces](wikipedia://en/48632) local uniform convergence and compact convergence coincide.
- A sequence of continuous functions on metric spaces, with the image metric space being complete, is uniformly convergent if and only if it is [uniformly Cauchy](wikipedia://en/7185671).
- If $S$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) interval (or in general a compact topological space), and $(f_n)$ is a [monotone increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonic) sequence (meaning $f_n(x) \leq f_{n+1}(x)$ for all *n* and *x*) of *continuous* functions with a pointwise limit $f$ which is also continuous, then the convergence is necessarily uniform ([Dini's theorem](wikipedia://en/9177825)). Uniform convergence is also guaranteed if $S$ is a compact interval and $(f_n)$ is an [equicontinuous](wikipedia://en/460642) sequence that converges pointwise.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### To continuity {#To_continuity}

*Image: Drini nonuniformconvergence SVG.svg.* Counterexample to a strengthening of the uniform convergence theorem, in which pointwise convergence, rather than uniform convergence, is assumed. The continuous green functions $\sin^n(x)$ converge to the non-continuous red function. This can happen only if convergence is not uniform.

If $E$ and $M$ are [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), then it makes sense to talk about the [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) of the functions $f_n,f:E\to M$. If we further assume that $M$ is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), then (uniform) convergence of the $f_n$ to $f$ is also well defined. The following result states that continuity is preserved by uniform convergence:

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Uniform limit theorem"}

**Uniform limit theorem.**

Suppose $E$ is a topological space, $M$ is a metric space, and $(f_n)$ is a sequence of continuous functions $f_n:E\to M$. If $f_n \rightrightarrows f$ on $E$, then $f$ is also continuous.

:::

This theorem is proved by the "$\varepsilon/3$ trick", and is the archetypal example of this trick: to prove a given inequality (that a desired quantity is less than $\varepsilon$), one uses the definitions of continuity and uniform convergence to produce 3 inequalities (demonstrating three separate quantities are each less than $\varepsilon/3$), and then combines them via the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) to produce the desired inequality.

Let $x_0\in E$ be an arbitrary point. We will prove that $f$ is continuous at $x_0$. Let $\varepsilon >0$. By uniform convergence, there exists a natural number $N$ such that

$$\forall x \in E\quad d\bigl(f_N(x),f(x)\bigr)\leq \tfrac13\varepsilon$$

(uniform convergence shows that the above statement is true for all $n\geq N$, but we will only use it for one function of the sequence, namely $f_N$).

It follows from the continuity of $f_N$ at $x_0\in E$ that there exists an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $U$ containing $x_0$ such that

$$\forall x\in U\quad d\bigl(f_N(x),f_N(x_0)\bigr)\leq\tfrac13\varepsilon.$$

Hence, using the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941),

$$\forall x \in U\quad d\bigl(f(x), f(x_0)\bigr) \leq d\bigl(f(x),f_N(x)\bigr) + d\bigl(f_N(x),f_N(x_0)\bigr) + d\bigl(f_N(x_0),f(x_0)\bigr) \leq \varepsilon,$$

which gives us the continuity of $f$ at $x_0$.$\quad\square$

This theorem is an important one in the history of real and Fourier analysis, since many 18th century mathematicians had the intuitive understanding that a sequence of continuous functions always converges to a continuous function. The image above shows a counterexample, and many discontinuous functions could, in fact, be written as a [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of continuous functions. The erroneous claim that the pointwise limit of a sequence of continuous functions is continuous (originally stated in terms of convergent series of continuous functions) is infamously known as "Cauchy's wrong theorem". The uniform limit theorem shows that a stronger form of convergence, uniform convergence, is needed to ensure the preservation of continuity in the limit function.

More precisely, this theorem states that the uniform limit of *[uniformly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_continuous)* functions is uniformly continuous; for a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) space, continuity is equivalent to local uniform continuity, and thus the uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous.

### To differentiability {#To_differentiability}

If $S$ is an interval and all the functions $f_n$ are [differentiable](wikipedia://en/7921) and converge to a limit $f$, it is often desirable to determine the derivative function $f'$ by taking the limit of the sequence $f'_n$. This is however in general not possible: even if the convergence is uniform, the limit function need not be differentiable (not even if the sequence consists of everywhere-[analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) functions, see [Weierstrass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_function)), and even if it is differentiable, the derivative of the limit function need not be equal to the limit of the derivatives. Consider for instance $f_n(x) = n^{-1/2}{\sin(nx)}$ with uniform limit $f_n\rightrightarrows f\equiv 0$. Clearly, $f'$ is also identically zero. However, the derivatives of the sequence of functions are given by $f'_n(x)=n^{1/2}\cos nx,$ and the sequence $f'_n$ does not converge to $f',$ or even to any function at all. In order to ensure a connection between the limit of a sequence of differentiable functions and the limit of the sequence of derivatives, the uniform convergence of the sequence of derivatives plus the convergence of the sequence of functions at at least one point is required:[^o23]

*If $(f_n)$ is a sequence of differentiable functions on $[a,b]$ such that $\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x_0)$ exists (and is finite) for some $x_0\in[a,b]$ and the sequence $(f'_n)$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$, then $f_n$ converges uniformly to a function $f$ on $[a,b]$, and $f'(x) = \lim_{n\to \infty} f'_n(x)$ for $x \in [a, b]$.*

### To integrability {#To_integrability}

Similarly, one often wants to exchange integrals and limit processes. For the [Riemann integral](wikipedia://en/26390), this can be done if uniform convergence is assumed:

*If ${(f_n)}_{n=1}^\infty$ is a sequence of Riemann integrable functions defined on a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) interval $I$ which uniformly converge with limit $f$, then $f$ is Riemann integrable and its integral can be computed as the limit of the integrals of the $f_n$:*

$$\int_I f = \lim_{n\to\infty}\int_I f_n.$$

In fact, for a uniformly convergent family of bounded functions on an interval, the upper and lower Riemann integrals converge to the upper and lower Riemann integrals of the limit function. This follows because, for *n* sufficiently large, the graph of $f_n$ is within $\varepsilon$ of the graph of *f*, and so the upper sum and lower sum of $f_n$ are each within $\varepsilon |I|$ of the value of the upper and lower sums of $f$, respectively.

Much stronger theorems in this respect, which require not much more than pointwise convergence, can be obtained if one abandons the Riemann integral and uses the [Lebesgue integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) instead.

### To analyticity {#To_analyticity}

Using [Morera's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morera's_Theorem), one can show that if a sequence of [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) functions converges uniformly in a region S of the complex plane, then the limit is analytic in S. This example demonstrates that complex functions are more well-behaved than real functions, since the uniform limit of analytic functions on a real interval need not even be differentiable (see [Weierstrass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_function)).

### To series {#To_series}

We say that $\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n$ converges:

pointwise on *E* if and only if the sequence of partial sums $\textstyle s_n(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n} f_j(x)$ converges for every $x\in E$.; uniformly on *E* if and only if *s*<sub>*n*</sub> converges uniformly as $n\to\infty$.; absolutely on *E* if and only if $\sum_{n=1}^\infty |f_n|$ converges for every $x \in E$.

With this definition comes the following result:

> Let *x*<sub>0</sub> be contained in the set *E* and each *f*<sub>*n*</sub> be continuous at *x*<sub>0</sub>. If $f = \sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n$ converges uniformly on *E* then *f* is continuous at *x*<sub>0</sub> in *E*. Suppose that $E = [a, b]$ and each *f*<sub>*n*</sub> is integrable on *E*. If $\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n$ converges uniformly on *E* then *f* is integrable on *E* and the series of integrals of *f*<sub>*n*</sub> is equal to integral of the series of f<sub>n</sub>.

:::

## Function spaces {#Function_spaces}

Uniform convergence is the norm topology on several important [function spaces](wikipedia://en/340630). For example, let $C([0,1])$ denote the space of real-valued or complex-valued continuous functions on the unit interval. With the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm)

$$\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in[0,1]} |f(x)|,$$

this is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989). A sequence $f_n$ in $C([0,1])$ converges to $f$ in this norm precisely when

$$\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to 0,$$

which is exactly uniform convergence on $[0,1]$.

The completeness of $C([0,1])$ follows from the completeness of the real or complex numbers. If $(f_n)$ is Cauchy in the supremum norm, then for each $x\in[0,1]$ the sequence $(f_n(x))$ is Cauchy, since

$$|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\leq \|f_n-f_m\|_\infty.$$

Thus $f_n(x)$ converges pointwise to a function $f(x)$. Moreover, the convergence is uniform: for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is an $N$ such that $\|f_n-f_m\|_\infty<\varepsilon$ whenever $n,m\geq N$; letting $m\to\infty$ gives

$$|f_n(x)-f(x)|\leq\varepsilon$$

for all $x\in[0,1]$ and all $n\geq N$. Since a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, $f\in C([0,1])$.

More generally, if $X$ is a [compact Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Hausdorff_space), then $C(X)$, with the supremum norm, is a Banach space. If $X$ is not compact, the supremum norm need not be finite on all of $C(X)$. In that case one often uses the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets, also called the [compact-open topology](wikipedia://en/726335) in this setting. For example, if $X$ is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact), [σ-compact Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_compact_space), this topology is generated by the [seminorms](wikipedia://en/292067)

$$p_K(f)=\sup_{x\in K}|f(x)|,$$

where $K$ ranges over compact subsets of $X$, making $C(X)$ into a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801).

For any set $X$, the space $\ell^\infty(X)$ of all bounded real-valued or complex-valued functions on $X$, equipped with the supremum norm

$$\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|,$$

is also a Banach space. Convergence in this norm is exactly uniform convergence on $X$. The sup norm on $C(X)$ for a compact space $X$ is inherited from the norm of $\ell^\infty$, so $C(X)\subset\ell^\infty(X)$ is a closed subspace.

If $(X,\mu)$ is a measure space, the space $L^\infty(X,\mu)$ of [essentially bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentially_bounded) [measurable functions](wikipedia://en/44055) on $X$, together with the [essential supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_supremum) norm $\|f\|=\operatorname{ess sup}_{x\in X}|f(x)|$. For many spaces of practical interest, such as compact subsets of Euclidean space and compact topological, the natural Borel measure embeds $C(X)$ into a closed subspace of $L^\infty(X,\mu)$. In these cases the norm giving uniform convergence is the same as the $L^\infty$ norm, and therefore called the $L^\infty$ norm.

## Almost uniform convergence {#Almost_uniform_convergence}

If the domain of the functions is a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) *E* then the related notion of **almost uniform convergence** can be defined. We say a sequence of functions $(f_n)$ converges almost uniformly on *E* if for every $\delta > 0$ there exists a measurable set $E_\delta$ with measure less than $\delta$ such that the sequence of functions $(f_n)$ converges uniformly on $E \setminus E_\delta$. In other words, almost uniform convergence means there are sets of arbitrarily small measure for which the sequence of functions converges uniformly on their complement.

Note that almost uniform convergence of a sequence does not mean that the sequence converges uniformly [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) as might be inferred from the name. However, [Egorov's theorem](wikipedia://en/1767634) does guarantee that on a finite measure space, a sequence of functions that converges [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/340198#Almost_everywhere_convergence) also converges almost uniformly on the same set.

Almost uniform convergence implies [almost everywhere convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_everywhere_convergence) and [convergence in measure](wikipedia://en/7996261).
