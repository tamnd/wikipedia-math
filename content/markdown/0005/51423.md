# P-adic number

*Image: 3-adic integers with labels.svg.* The 3-adic integers, depicted as a slice of a [solenoid](wikipedia://en/2217712)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="P-adic number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), given a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$, the **$p$-adic numbers** form an extension of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) that is distinct from the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), though with some similar properties; $p$-adic numbers can be written in a form similar to (possibly [infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity_%28mathematics%29)) [decimals](wikipedia://en/3293594), but with digits based on a prime number $p$ rather than ten, and extending to the left rather than to the right.

:::

For example, comparing the expansion of the rational number $\tfrac15$ in [base $3$](wikipedia://en/62950) versus the $3$-adic expansion,

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} \tfrac15 &{}= 0.\overline{0121}\ (\text{base } 3) &&{}= 0\cdot 3^0 + 0\cdot 3^{-1} + 1\cdot 3^{-2} + 2\cdot 3^{-3} + \cdots \\[5mu] \tfrac15 &{}= \overline{1210}2 \ \ (\text{3-adic}) &&{}= \cdots + 2\cdot 3^3 + 1 \cdot 3^2 + 0\cdot3^1 + 2 \cdot 3^0. \end{alignedat}$$

Formally, given a prime number $p$, a $p$-adic number can be defined as a [series](wikipedia://en/15287)

$$s=\sum_{i=k}^\infty a_i p^i = a_k p^k + a_{k+1} p^{k+1} + a_{k+2} p^{k+2} + \cdots$$

where $k$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) (possibly negative), and each $a_i$ is an integer such that $0\le a_i < p.$ A **$p$-adic integer** is a $p$-adic number such that $k\ge 0.$

In general, the series that represents a $p$-adic number is not [convergent](wikipedia://en/1743431) in the usual sense, but it is convergent for the [$p$-adic absolute value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_absolute_value) $|s|_p=p^{-k},$ where $k$ is the least integer $i$ such that $a_i\ne 0$ (if all $a_i$ are zero, one has the zero $p$-adic number, which has $0$ as its $p$-adic absolute value).

Every rational number can be uniquely expressed as the sum of a series as above, with respect to the $p$-adic absolute value. This allows considering rational numbers as special $p$-adic numbers, and alternatively defining the $p$-adic numbers as the [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28metric_space%29) of the rational numbers for the $p$-adic absolute value, exactly as the real numbers are the completion of the rational numbers for the usual absolute value.

$p$-adic numbers were first described by [Kurt Hensel](wikipedia://en/2242565) in 1897,[^o2] though, with hindsight, some of [Ernst Kummer](wikipedia://en/319117)'s earlier work can be interpreted as implicitly using $p$-adic numbers.

For every prime number $p$, the $p$-adic numbers form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). The definition of $p$-adic numbers may be generalized to the case where $p$ is not prime. However, when $p$ is a [power of a prime](wikipedia://en/1630483) $q$, these generalized $p$-adic numbers can be identified with the $q$-adic numbers. Otherwise, the generalized $p$-adic numbers do not form a field. Therefore, these generalized $p$-adic numbers are rarely encountered in the literature.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Roughly speaking, [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) modulo a positive integer $n$ consists of "approximating" every integer by the remainder of its [division](wikipedia://en/679987) by $n$, called its *residue modulo* $n$. The main property of modular arithmetic is that the residue modulo $n$ of the result of a succession of operations on integers is the same as the result of the same succession of operations on residues modulo $n$.

When studying [Diophantine equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_equations), it is sometimes useful to reduce the equation modulo a prime $p$, since this usually provides more insight about the equation itself. Unfortunately, doing this loses some information because the reduction $\mathbb Z\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb Z/p$ is not [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective).

One way to preserve more information is to use larger moduli, such as higher prime powers, $p^{2}$, $p^{3}, ...$. However, this has the disadvantage of $\mathbb Z/p^e$ not being a field, which loses a lot of the algebraic properties that $\mathbb Z/p$ has.[^o4]

[Kurt Hensel](wikipedia://en/2242565) discovered a method which consists of using a prime modulus $p$, and applying [Hensel's lemma](wikipedia://en/1633368) to lift solutions modulo $p$ to modulo $p^{2}$, $p^{3}, ...$. This process creates an infinite sequence of residues, and a $p$-adic number is defined as the "limit" of such a sequence.

Essentially, $p$-adic numbers allows "taking modulo $p^{e}$ for all $e$ at once". A distinguishing feature of $p$-adic numbers from ordinary modulo arithmetic is that the set of $p$-adic numbers $\mathbb Q_p$ forms a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), making division by $p$ possible (unlike when working modulo $p^{e}$). Furthermore, the mapping $\mathbb Z\hookrightarrow\mathbb Z_p$ is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196), so not much information is lost when reducing to $p$-adic numbers.[^o4]

## Informal description {#Informal_description}

There are multiple ways to understand $p$-adic numbers.

### As a base-*p* expansion {#As_a_base-p_expansion}

One way to think about $p$-adic integers is using "base $p$". For example, every integer can be written in base $p$,

$$50_{10}=1212_3=1\cdot3^3+2\cdot3^2+1\cdot3^1+2\cdot3^0$$

Informally, **$p$-adic integers** can be thought of as integers in base-$p$, but the digits extend *infinitely to the left*.[^o4]

$$\ldots121012102_3=\cdots+2\cdot3^3+1\cdot3^2+0\cdot3^1+2\cdot3^0$$

Addition and multiplication on $p$-adic integers can be carried in a way somewhat similar to integers in base-$p$.[^o7]

When adding together two $p$-adic integers (for example, $\ldots012102_3+\ldots101211_3$), their digits are added with [carries](wikipedia://en/1629687) being propagated from right to left.

$$\begin{array}{cccccccc} & & &_1 &_1 & &_1 & \\ & \cdots & 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 & 2 \,_3 \\ + & \cdots & 1 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 \,_3 \\\hline & \cdots & 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 & 2 & 0 \,_3 \end{array}$$

Multiplication of $p$-adic integers works similarly via [long multiplication](wikipedia://en/57411). Since addition and multiplication can be performed with $p$-adic integers, they form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), denoted $\mathbb Z_p$ or $\mathbf Z_p$.

Note that some rational numbers can also be $p$-adic integers, even if they aren't integers in a real sense. For example, the rational number $\tfrac{1}{5}$ is a 3-adic integer with the 3-adic expansion $\tfrac{1}{5}=\overline{1210}2_3$. However, some rational numbers, such as $\tfrac{1}{p}$, cannot be written as a $p$-adic integer. Because of this, $p$-adic integers are generalized further to $p$-adic numbers:

**$p$-adic numbers** can be thought of as $p$-adic integers with *finitely many digits after the decimal point*. An example of a 3-adic number is

$$\ldots121012.102_3 = \cdots+1\cdot3^1+2\cdot3^0+1\cdot3^{-1}+0\cdot3^{-2}+2\cdot3^{-3}$$

Equivalently, every $p$-adic number is of the form $\tfrac x{p^k}$, where $x$ is a $p$-adic integer.

For any nonzero $p$-adic number $x$, its [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) $\tfrac{1}{x}$ is also a $p$-adic number, which can be computed using a variant of [long division](wikipedia://en/313384).[^o7] For this reason, the $p$-adic numbers form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), denoted $\mathbb Q_p$ or $\mathbf Q_p$.

### As a sequence of residues mod $p$<sup>*k*</sup> {#As_a_sequence_of_residues_mod_k}

Another way to define $p$-adic integers is by representing it as a sequence of residues $x_e\operatorname{mod} p^e$ for each integer $e$.[^o4] Here, each $x_i$ denotes an integer representative of a residue class modulo $p^i$

$$x = (x_1 \operatorname{mod} p, ~ x_2 \operatorname{mod} p^2, ~ x_3 \operatorname{mod} p^3, ~ \ldots)$$

satisfying the compatibility relations $x_i \equiv x_j ~ (\operatorname{mod} p^i)$ for $i < j$. In this notation, addition and multiplication of $p$-adic integers are defined component-wise:

$$x+y = (x_1+y_1 \operatorname{mod} p, ~ x_2+y_2 \operatorname{mod} p^2, ~ x_3+y_3 \operatorname{mod} p^3, ~ \ldots)$$

$$x\cdot y = (x_1\cdot y_1 \operatorname{mod} p, ~ x_2\cdot y_2 \operatorname{mod} p^2, ~ x_3\cdot y_3 \operatorname{mod} p^3, ~ \ldots)$$

This is equivalent to the base-$p$ definition, because the last $k$ digits of a base-$p$ expansion uniquely define its value mod $p$<sup>*k*</sup>, and vice versa.

This form can also explain why some rational numbers are $p$-adic integers, even if they are not integers. For example, $\tfrac{1}{5}$ is a 3-adic integer, because its 3-adic expansion consists of the [multiplicative inverses](wikipedia://en/9815338) of 5 mod 3, 3<sup>2</sup>, 3<sup>3</sup>, ...

$$\begin{aligned} \frac15 &= (\tfrac15 \operatorname{mod} 3, ~ \tfrac15 \operatorname{mod} 3^2, ~ \tfrac15 \operatorname{mod} 3^3, ~ \tfrac15 \operatorname{mod} 3^4, ~ \ldots)\\ &= (2 \operatorname{mod} 3, ~ 2 \operatorname{mod} 3^2, ~ 11 \operatorname{mod} 3^3, ~ 65 \operatorname{mod} 3^4, ~ \ldots) \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o10 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

There are several equivalent definitions of $p$-adic numbers. The two approaches given below are relatively elementary.

### As formal series in base $p$ {#As_formal_series_in_base}

A **$p$-adic integer** is often defined as a [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) of the form

$$r=\sum_{i=0}^\infty a_i p^i = a_0 + a_1 p + a_2 p^2 + a_3 p^3 + \cdots$$

where each $a_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}$ represents a "digit in base $p$".

A **$p$-adic unit** is a $p$-adic integer whose first digit is nonzero, i.e. $a_0\ne 0$. The set of all $p$-adic integers is usually denoted $\mathbb Z_p$.[^o12]

A **$p$-adic number** is then defined as a [formal Laurent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_Laurent_series) of the form

$$r=\sum_{i=v}^\infty a_i p^i = a_v p^v + a_{v+1} p^{v+1} + a_{v+2} p^{v+2} + a_{v+3} p^{v+3} + \cdots$$

where $v$ is a (possibly negative) integer, and each $a_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}$.[^o13] Equivalently, a $p$-adic number is anything of the form $\tfrac{x}{p^k}$, where $x$ is a $p$-adic integer.

The first index $v$ for which the digit $a_v$ is nonzero in $r$ is called the [$p$-adic valuation](wikipedia://en/16047618) of $r$, denoted $v_p(r)$. If $r=0$, then such an index does not exist, so by convention $v_p(0)=\infty$.

In this definition, addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of $p$-adic numbers are carried out similarly to numbers in base $p$, with "carries" or "borrows" moving from left to right rather than right to left.[^o14] As an example in $\mathbb Q_3$,

$$\begin{array}{lllllllllll} & & & _1 & & & & _1 & & _1 \\ & 2\cdot3^0 &+& 0\cdot3^1 &+& 1\cdot3^2 &+& 2\cdot3^3 &+& 1\cdot3^4 &+ \cdots \\ + & 1\cdot3^0 &+& 1\cdot3^1 &+& 2\cdot3^2 &+& 1\cdot3^3 &+& 0\cdot3^4 &+ \cdots \\\hline & 0\cdot3^0 &+& 2\cdot3^1 &+& 0\cdot3^2 &+& 1\cdot3^3 &+& 2\cdot3^4 &+ \cdots \end{array}$$

Division of $p$-adic numbers may also be carried out "formally" via [division of formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012#Division), with some care about having to "carry".[^o13]

With these operations, the set of $p$-adic numbers form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), denoted $\mathbb Q_p$.

### As equivalence classes {#As_equivalence_classes}

The $p$-adic numbers may also be defined as equivalence classes, in a similar way as the definition of real numbers as equivalence classes of [Cauchy sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_sequences). It is fundamentally based on the following lemma:

*Every nonzero rational number $r$ can be written $r=p^v\frac{m}{n},$ where $v$, $m$, and $n$ are integers and neither $m$ nor $n$ is divisible by $p$.*

The exponent $v$ is uniquely determined by $r$ and is called its **$p$-adic valuation**, denoted $v_p(r)$. The proof of the lemma results directly from the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556).

A **$p$-adic series** is a [formal Laurent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_Laurent_series) of the form

$$\sum_{i=v}^\infty r_i p^{i},$$

where $v$ is a (possibly negative) integer and the $r_i$ are rational numbers that either are zero or have a nonnegative valuation (that is, the denominator of $r_i$ is not divisible by $p$).

Every rational number may be viewed as a $p$-adic series with a single nonzero term, consisting of its factorization of the form $p^k\tfrac mn,$ with $m$ and $n$ both coprime with $p$.

Two $p$-adic series $\sum_{i=v}^\infty r_i p^{i}$ and $\sum_{i=w}^\infty s_i p^{i}$ are *equivalent* if there is an integer $N$ such that, for every integer $n>N,$ the rational number

$$\sum_{i=v}^n r_i p^{i} - \sum_{i=w}^n s_i p^{i}$$

is zero or has a $p$-adic valuation greater than $n$.

A $p$-adic series $\sum_{i=v}^\infty a_i p^{i}$ is *normalized* if either all $a_i$ are integers such that $0\le a_i <p,$ and $a_v >0,$ or all $a_i$ are zero. In the latter case, the series is called the *zero series*.

Every $p$-adic series is equivalent to exactly one normalized series. This normalized series is obtained by a sequence of transformations, which are equivalences of series; see § Normalization of a $p$-adic series, below.

In other words, the equivalence of $p$-adic series is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259), and each [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) contains exactly one normalized $p$-adic series.

The usual operations of series (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) are compatible with equivalence of $p$-adic series. That is, denoting the equivalence with $\sim$, if $S$, $T$ and $U$ are nonzero $p$-adic series such that $S\sim T,$ one has

$$\begin{aligned} S\pm U&\sim T\pm U,\\ SU&\sim TU,\\ 1/S&\sim 1/T. \end{aligned}$$

With this, the **$p$-adic numbers** are defined as the *equivalence classes* of $p$-adic series.

The uniqueness property of normalization, allows uniquely representing any $p$-adic number by the corresponding normalized $p$-adic series. The compatibility of the series equivalence leads almost immediately to basic properties of $p$-adic numbers:

- *Addition*, *multiplication* and [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of $p$-adic numbers are defined as for [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012), followed by the normalization of the result.
- With these operations, the $p$-adic numbers form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), which is an [extension field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field) of the rational numbers.
- The *valuation* of a nonzero $p$-adic number $x$, commonly denoted $v_p(x)$ is the exponent of $p$ in the first non zero term of the corresponding normalized series; the valuation of zero is $v_p(0)=+\infty$
- The *$p$-adic absolute value* of a nonzero $p$-adic number $x$, is $|x|_p=p^{-v(x)};$ for the zero $p$-adic number, one has $|0|_p=0.$

#### Normalization of a *p*-adic series {#Normalization_of_a_p-adic_series}

Starting with the series $\sum_{i=v}^\infty r_i p^{i},$ we wish to arrive at an equivalent series such that the $p$-adic valuation of $r_v$ is zero. For that, one considers the first nonzero $r_i.$ If its $p$-adic valuation is zero, it suffices to change $v$ into $i$, that is to start the summation from $v$. Otherwise, the $p$-adic valuation of $r_i$ is $j>0,$ and $r_i= p^js_i$ where the valuation of $s_i$ is zero; so, one gets an equivalent series by changing $r_i$ to $0$ and $r_{i+j}$ to $r_{i+j} + s_i.$ Iterating this process, one gets eventually, possibly after infinitely many steps, an equivalent series that either is the zero series or is a series such that the valuation of $r_v$ is zero.

Then, if the series is not normalized, consider the first nonzero $r_i$ that is not an integer in the interval $[0,p-1].$ Using [Bézout's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bézout's_lemma), write this as $r_i=a_i+ps_i$, where $a_i\in[0,p-1]$ and $s_i$ has nonnegative valuation. Then, one gets an equivalent series by replacing $r_i$ with $a_i,$ and adding $s_i$ to $r_{i+1}.$ Iterating this process, possibly infinitely many times, provides eventually the desired normalized $p$-adic series.

### Other equivalent definitions {#Other_equivalent_definitions}

Other equivalent definitions use [completion](wikipedia://en/9477975) of a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633) (see ), [completion of a metric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_of_a_metric_space) (see ), or [inverse limits](wikipedia://en/15109) (see ).

A $p$-adic number can be defined as a *normalized $p$-adic series*. Since there are other equivalent definitions that are commonly used, one says often that a normalized $p$-adic series *represents* a $p$-adic number, instead of saying that it *is* a $p$-adic number.

One can say also that any $p$-adic series represents a $p$-adic number, since every $p$-adic series is equivalent to a unique normalized $p$-adic series. This is useful for defining operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) of $p$-adic numbers: the result of such an operation is obtained by normalizing the result of the corresponding operation on series. This well defines operations on $p$-adic numbers, since the series operations are compatible with equivalence of $p$-adic series.

With these operations, $p$-adic numbers form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) called the **field of $p$-adic numbers** and denoted $\mathbb{Q}_p$ or $\mathbf Q_p.$ There is a unique [field homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_homomorphism) from the rational numbers into the $p$-adic numbers, which maps a rational number to its $p$-adic expansion. The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of this homomorphism is commonly identified with the field of rational numbers. This allows considering the $p$-adic numbers as an [extension field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field) of the rational numbers, and the rational numbers as a [subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subfield_%28mathematics%29) of the $p$-adic numbers.

The *valuation* of a nonzero $p$-adic number $x$, commonly denoted $v_p(x),$ is the exponent of $p$ in the first nonzero term of every $p$-adic series that represents $x$. By convention, $v_p(0)=\infty;$ that is, the valuation of zero is $\infty.$ This valuation is a [discrete valuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_valuation). The restriction of this valuation to the rational numbers is the $p$-adic valuation of $\mathbb{Q},$ that is, the exponent $v$ in the factorization of a rational number as $\tfrac nd p^v,$ with both $n$ and $d$ [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) with $p$.

## Notation {#Notation}

There are several different conventions for writing $p$-adic expansions. So far this article has used a notation for $p$-adic expansions in which [powers](wikipedia://en/99491) of $p$ increase from right to left. With this right-to-left notation the 3-adic expansion of $\tfrac15,$ for example, is written as

$$\frac15 = \dots 121012102_3.$$

When performing arithmetic in this notation, digits are [carried](wikipedia://en/1629687) to the left. It is also possible to write $p$-adic expansions so that the powers of $p$ increase from left to right, and digits are carried to the right. With this left-to-right notation the 3-adic expansion of $\tfrac15$ is

$$\frac15 = 2.01210121\dots_3 \mbox{ or } \frac1{15} = 20.1210121\dots_3.$$

$p$-adic expansions may be written with [other sets of digits](wikipedia://en/1181818) instead of $\{0,\,1,\,...,$ $p\,-\,1$}. For example, the $3$-adic expansion of $\tfrac15$ can be written using [balanced ternary](wikipedia://en/376757) digits $\{\underline{1},\,0,\,1$}, with $\underline{1}$ representing negative one, as

$$\frac15 = \dots\underline{1}11\underline{11}11\underline{11}11\underline{1}_{\text{3}} .$$

In fact any set of $p$ integers which are in distinct [residue classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residue_class) modulo $p$ may be used as $p$-adic digits. In number theory, [Teichmüller representatives](wikipedia://en/5573710#Motivation) are sometimes used as digits.[^o20]

**Quote notation** is a variant of the $p$-adic representation of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) that was proposed in 1979 by [Eric Hehner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Hehner) and [Nigel Horspool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Horspool) for implementing on computers the (exact) arithmetic with these numbers.[^o21] It can be used as a compact way to represent rational numbers, which have an infinite periodic sequence of digits. In this notation, a quote mark (') is used to separate the repeating part from the nonrepeating part.

$$\frac15=1210\,'2_3$$

:::

## *p*-adic expansion of rational numbers {#p-adic_expansion_of_rational_numbers}

The [decimal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_expansion) of a positive [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) $r$ is its representation as a [series](wikipedia://en/15287)

$$r = \sum_{i=k}^\infty a_i 10^{-i},$$

where $k$ is an integer and each $a_i$ is also an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) such that $0\le a_i <10.$ This expansion can be computed by [long division](wikipedia://en/313384) of the numerator by the denominator, which is itself based on the following theorem: If $r=\tfrac n d$ is a rational number such that $0\le r <1,$ there is an integer $a$ such that $0\le a <10,$ and $10r = a+r',$ with $0\le r'<1.$ The decimal expansion is obtained by repeatedly applying this result to the remainder $r'$ which in the iteration assumes the role of the original rational number $r$.

The $p$-*adic expansion* of a rational number can be computed similarly, but with a different division step. Suppose that $r=\tfrac{n}{d}$ is a rational number with nonnegative valuation (that is, $d$ is not divisible by $p$). The division step consists of writing

$$r = a + p\,r'$$

where $a$ is an integer such that $0\le a <p,$ and $r'$ has nonnegative valuation.

The integer $a$ can be computed as a [modular multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/9815338): $a=nd^{-1}\operatorname{mod}p$. Because of this, writing $r$ in this way is always possible, and such a representation is unique.

The $p$-adic expansion of a rational number is eventually [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848). [Conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), a series $\sum_{i=k}^\infty a_i p^i,$ with $0\le a_i <p$ converges (for the $p$-adic absolute value) to a rational number [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is eventually periodic; in this case, the series is the $p$-adic expansion of that rational number. The [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) is similar to that of the similar result for [repeating decimals](wikipedia://en/13612447).

::: {#o24 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Let us compute the 5-adic expansion of $\tfrac 13.$ We can write this number as $\tfrac13 = 2 + 5 \cdot \tfrac{-1}3$. Thus we use $a=2$ for the first step.

$$\frac13 = 2 + 5^1 \cdot \left(\frac{-1}3\right)$$

For the next step, we can write the "remainder" $\tfrac{-1}3$ as $\tfrac{-1}3 = 3 + 5 \cdot \tfrac{-2}3$. Thus we use $a=3$.

$$\frac13 = 2 + 3\cdot 5^1 + 5^2 \cdot \left(\frac{-2}3\right)$$

We can write the "remainder" $\tfrac{-2}3$ as $\tfrac{-2}3 = 1 + 5 \cdot \tfrac{-1}3$. Thus we use $a=1$.

$$\frac13 = 2 + 3\cdot 5^1 + 1\cdot5^2 + 5^3\cdot \left(\frac{-1}3\right)$$

Notice that we obtain the "remainder" $\tfrac{-1}3$ again, which means the digits can only repeat from this point on.

$$\frac13 = 2 + 3\cdot 5^1 + 1\cdot5^2 + 3\cdot 5^3 + 1\cdot 5^4 + 3\cdot 5^5 + 1\cdot 5^6 + \cdots$$

In the standard 5-adic notation, we can write this as

$$\frac 13= \ldots 1313132_5$$

with the [ellipsis](wikipedia://en/9596) $\ldots$ on the left hand side.

:::

## *p*-adic integers {#p-adic_integers}

The **$p$-adic integers** are the $p$-adic numbers with a nonnegative valuation.

A $p$-adic integer can be represented as a sequence

$$x = (x_1 \operatorname{mod} p, ~ x_2 \operatorname{mod} p^2, ~ x_3 \operatorname{mod} p^3, ~ \ldots)$$

of residues $x_e$ mod $p^e$ for each integer $e$, satisfying the compatibility relations $x_i \equiv x_j ~ (\operatorname{mod} p^i)$ for $i < j$.

Every [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) is a $p$-adic integer (including zero, since $0<\infty$). The rational numbers of the form $\tfrac nd p^k$ with $d$ coprime with $p$ and $k\ge 0$ are also $p$-adic integers (for the reason that $d$ has an inverse mod $p^e$ for every $e$).

The $p$-adic integers form a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), denoted $\mathbb{Z}_p$ or $\mathbf Z_p$, that has the following properties.

- It is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), since it is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of a field, or since the first term of the series representation of the product of two non zero $p$-adic series is the product of their first terms.
- The [units](wikipedia://en/289666) (invertible elements) of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ are the $p$-adic numbers of valuation zero.
- It is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039), such that each [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) is generated by a power of $p$.
- It is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) of [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) one, since its only [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) are the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) and the ideal generated by $p$, the unique [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164).
- It is a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633), since this results from the preceding properties.
- It is the [completion](wikipedia://en/9477975) of the local ring $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} = \bigl\{\tfrac nd \mathbin{\big|} n, d \in \mathbb{Z},\, d \not\in p\mathbb{Z} \bigr\},$ which is the [localization](wikipedia://en/252216) of $\mathbb{Z}$ at the prime ideal $p\mathbb{Z}.$

The last property provides a definition of the $p$-adic numbers that is equivalent to the above one: the field of the $p$-adic numbers is the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the completion of the localization of the integers at the prime ideal generated by $p$.

:::

## Topological properties {#Topological_properties}

*Image: 3-adic metric on Z mod 27 blue.svg.* Visual depiction of the 3-adic metric applied to the $\mathbf{Z}/27\mathbf{Z}$, the integers modulo 27

The $p$-adic valuation allows defining an [absolute value](wikipedia://en/5042667) on $p$-adic numbers: the $p$-adic absolute value of a nonzero $p$-adic number $x$ is

$$|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)},$$

where $v_p(x)$ is the $p$-adic valuation of $x$. The $p$-adic absolute value of $0$ is $|0|_p = 0.$ This is an absolute value that satisfies the [strong triangle inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_triangle_inequality) since, for every $x$ and $y$:

- $|x|_p = 0$ if and only if $x=0;$
- $$|x|_p\cdot |y|_p = |xy|_p;$$
- $$|x+y|_p\le \max\{|x|_p,|y|_p\} \le |x|_p + |y|_p.$$

Moreover, if $|x|_p \ne |y|_p,$ then $|x+y|_p = \max\bigl(|x|_p,|y|_p\bigr).$

This makes the $p$-adic numbers a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), and even an [ultrametric space](wikipedia://en/333996), with the $p$-adic distance defined by $d_p(x,y)=|x-y|_p.$

As a metric space, the $p$-adic numbers form the [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28metric_space%29) of the rational numbers equipped with the $p$-adic absolute value. This provides another way for defining the $p$-adic numbers.

As the metric is defined from a [discrete valuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_valuation), every [open ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) is also [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_ball). More precisely, the open ball $B_r(x) =\{y\mid d_p(x,y)<r\}$ equals the closed ball $\textstyle B_{p^{-v}}[x] =\{y\mid d_p(x,y)\le p^{-v}\},$ where $v$ is the least integer such that $\textstyle p^{-v}< r.$ Similarly, $\textstyle B_r[x] = B_{p^{-w}}(x),$ where $w$ is the greatest integer such that $\textstyle p^{-w}>r.$

This implies that the $p$-adic numbers $\mathbb Q_p$ form a [locally compact space](wikipedia://en/48632) ([locally compact field](wikipedia://en/35937890)), and the $p$-adic integers $\mathbb Z_p$—that is, the ball $B_1[0]=B_p(0)$—form a [compact space](wikipedia://en/6042).[^o29]

The space of 2-adic integers $\mathbb Z_2$ is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) $\mathcal C$.[^o30][^o31] This can be seen by considering the continuous 1-to-1 mapping $\psi:\mathbb Z_2\to \mathcal C$ defined by

$$\psi:~a_0+a_12+a_22^2+a_32^3+\cdots~\longmapsto~\frac{2a_0}3+\frac{2a_1}{3^2}+\frac{2a_2}{3^3}+\frac{2a_3}{3^4}+\cdots$$

Moreover, for any $p$, $\mathbb Z_p$ is homeomorphic to $\mathbb Z_2$, and therefore also homeomorphic to the Cantor set.[^o32]

The [Pontryagin dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_dual) of the group of $p$-adic integers is the [Prüfer $p$-group](wikipedia://en/4885031) $\mathbb Z(p^\infty)$, and the Pontryagin dual of the Prüfer $p$-group is the group of $p$-adic integers.[^o33]

:::

## Modular properties {#Modular_properties}

The [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $\mathbb{Z}_p/p^n\mathbb{Z}_p$ may be identified with the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ of the integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $p^n.$ This can be shown by remarking that every $p$-adic integer, represented by its normalized $p$-adic series, is congruent modulo $p^n$ with its [partial sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) $\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_ip^i,$ whose value is an integer in the interval $[0,p^n-1].$ A straightforward verification shows that this defines a [ring isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) from $\mathbb{Z}_p/p^n\mathbb{Z}_p$ to $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}.$

The [inverse limit](wikipedia://en/15109) of the rings $\mathbb{Z}_p/p^n\mathbb{Z}_p$ is defined as the ring formed by the sequences $a_0, a_1, \ldots$ such that $a_i \in \mathbb{Z}/p^i \mathbb{Z}$ and $a_i \equiv a_{i+1} \pmod {p^i}$ for every $i$.

The mapping that maps a normalized $p$-adic series to the sequence of its partial sums is a ring isomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}_p$ to the inverse limit of the $\mathbb{Z}_p/p^n\mathbb{Z}_p.$ This provides another way for defining $p$-adic integers ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an isomorphism).

This definition of $p$-adic integers is specially useful for practical computations, as allowing building $p$-adic integers by successive approximations.

For example, for computing the $p$-adic (multiplicative) inverse of an integer, one can use [Newton's method](wikipedia://en/22145), starting from the inverse modulo $p$; then, each Newton step computes the inverse modulo $p^{n^2}$ from the inverse modulo $p^n.$

The same method can be used for computing the $p$-adic [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of an integer that is a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091) modulo $p$. This seems to be the fastest known method for testing whether a large integer is a square: it suffices to test whether the given integer is the square of the value found in $\mathbb{Z}_p/p^n\mathbb{Z}_p$. Applying Newton's method to find the square root requires $p^n$ to be larger than twice the given integer, which is quickly satisfied.

[Hensel lifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hensel_lifting) is a similar method that allows to "lift" the factorization modulo $p$ of a polynomial with integer coefficients to a factorization modulo $p^n$ for large values of $n$. This is commonly used by [polynomial factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_factorization) algorithms.

## Cardinality {#Cardinality}

Both $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $\mathbb{Q}_p$ are [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248) and have the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901).[^o36] For $\mathbb{Z}_p,$ this results from the $p$-adic representation, which defines a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ on the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) $\{0,\ldots,p-1\}^\mathbb{N}.$ For $\mathbb{Q}_p$ this results from its expression as a [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite) [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of copies of $\mathbb{Z}_p$:

$$\mathbb{Q}_p=\bigcup_{i=0}^\infty \frac 1{p^i}\mathbb{Z}_p.$$

## Algebraic closure {#Algebraic_closure}

The field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ contains $\mathbb{Q}$ and is a field of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) $0$.

Because $0$ can be written as sum of squares, $\mathbb{Q}_p$ cannot be turned into an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430#Orderability_of_fields).

The field of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) $\mathbb{R}$ has only a single proper [algebraic extension](wikipedia://en/2125): the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) $\mathbb{C}$. In other words, this [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) is already [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018). By contrast, the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of $\mathbb{Q}_p$, denoted $\overline{\mathbb{Q}_p},$ has infinite degree,[^o38] that is, $\mathbb{Q}_p$ has infinitely many inequivalent algebraic extensions. Also contrasting the case of real numbers, although there is a unique extension of the $p$-adic valuation to $\overline{\mathbb{Q}_p},$ the latter is not (metrically) complete.[^o39][^o40]

Its (metric) completion is denoted $\mathbb{C}_p$ or $\Omega_p$,[^o40][^o41] and sometimes called the **complex $p$-adic numbers** by analogy to the complex numbers. The field $\mathbb{C}_p$ is algebraically closed.[^o40][^o42] However, unlike $\mathbb{C}$, this field is not [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact).[^o41]

The fields $\mathbb{C}_p$ and $\mathbb{C}$ are isomorphic, so we may regard $\mathbb{C}_p$ as $\mathbb{C}$ endowed with an exotic metric. The proof of existence of such a field isomorphism relies on the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), and does not provide an explicit example of such an isomorphism (that is, it is not [constructive](wikipedia://en/360113)).

If $K$ is any finite [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) of $\mathbb{Q}_p,$ the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) $\operatorname{Gal} \left(K/\mathbb{Q}_p \right)$ is [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012). Thus, the [absolute Galois group](wikipedia://en/2996781) ${\operatorname{Gal}} \bigl(\,\overline{\mathbb{Q}_p}/ \mathbb{Q}_p \bigr)$ is [prosolvable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosolvable).

## Multiplicative group {#Multiplicative_group}

The unit group $\mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ is contained in the multiplicative group $\mathbb{Q}_p^\times = \mathbb{Q}_p - \{0\}$. They have the same [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) (subgroup of elements of finite order). Hensel's lemma implies that the torsion subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}_p^\times$ maps surjectively to $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$. The kernel is $\{1\}$ when $p>2$ and $\{\pm 1\}$ when $p=2$. In particular, the torsion subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}_p^\times$ is cyclic of order $p-1$ when $p>2$, and equals $\{\pm 1\}$ when $p=2$. Therefore, for $n > 2$, the field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ contains the $n$th [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462) if and only if $n\,| p - 1$.[^o44]

Given a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $k$, let $\mathbb{Q}_p^{\times k}$ be the group of *k*th powers of elements of $\mathbb{Q}_p^\times$; then the index $(\mathbb{Q}_p^\times:\mathbb{Q}_p^{\times k})$ is finite.

::: {#o46 .statement cue=named-heading name="Local–global principle"}

## Local–global principle {#Local–global_principle}

[Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118)'s [local–global principle](wikipedia://en/489707) is said to hold for an equation if it can be solved over the rational numbers [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it can be solved over the real numbers and over the $p$-adic numbers for every prime $p$. This principle holds, for example, for equations given by [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478), but fails for higher polynomials in several indeterminates.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

The *p*-adic numbers have appeared in several fields of mathematics as well as physics.

### Analysis {#Analysis}

Similar to the more classical fields of [real](wikipedia://en/26478) and [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), which deal, respectively, with functions on the real and complex numbers, ***p*-adic analysis** studies functions on *p*-adic numbers. The theory of complex-valued numerical functions on the *p*-adic numbers is part of the theory of [locally compact groups](wikipedia://en/1651204) ([abstract harmonic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_harmonic_analysis)). The usual meaning taken for *p*-adic analysis is the theory of *p*-adic-valued functions on spaces of interest.

Applications of *p*-adic analysis have mainly been in number theory, where it has a significant role in [diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/502205) and [diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558). Some applications have required the development of *p*-adic [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713). In many ways *p*-adic analysis is less subtle than [classical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_analysis), since the [ultrametric inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrametric_inequality) means, for example, that convergence of [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) of *p*-adic numbers is much simpler. [Topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) over *p*-adic fields show distinctive features; for example aspects relating to [convexity](wikipedia://en/6292) and the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860) are different.

Two important concepts from *p*-adic analysis are [Mahler's theorem](wikipedia://en/408111), which characterizes every continuous *p*-adic function in terms of polynomials, and the [Volkenborn integral](wikipedia://en/41375311), which provides a method of [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) for *p*-adic functions.

### Hodge theory {#Hodge_theory}

***p*-adic Hodge theory** is a theory that provides a way to classify and study [*p*-adic Galois representations](wikipedia://en/486929) of [characteristic 0](wikipedia://en/1066621) [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375) with residual characteristic *p* (such as **Q**<sub>*p*</sub>). The theory has its beginnings in [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) and [John Tate](wikipedia://en/245933)'s study of [Tate modules](wikipedia://en/3094921) of [abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/247152) and the notion of [Hodge–Tate representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge–Tate_representation). Hodge–Tate representations are related to certain decompositions of *p*-adic [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) theories analogous to the [Hodge decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_decomposition), hence the name *p*-adic Hodge theory. Further developments were inspired by properties of *p*-adic Galois representations arising from the [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) of [varieties](wikipedia://en/248808). [Jean-Marc Fontaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Marc_Fontaine) introduced many of the basic concepts of the field.

### Teichmüller theory {#Teichmüller_theory}

***p*-adic Teichmüller theory** describes the "uniformization" of *p*-adic curves and their [moduli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_spaces), generalizing the usual [Teichmüller theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teichmüller_theory) that describes the [uniformization](wikipedia://en/288270) of [Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surfaces) and their moduli. It was introduced and developed by [Shinichi Mochizuki](wikipedia://en/36940578).

### Quantum physics {#Quantum_physics}

***p*-adic quantum mechanics** is a collection of related research efforts in [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics) that replace real numbers with *p*-adic numbers. Historically, this research was inspired by the discovery that the [Veneziano amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneziano_amplitude) of the open [bosonic string](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosonic_string), which is calculated using an integral over the real numbers, can be generalized to the *p*-adic numbers. This observation initiated the study of ***p*-adic string theory**.

:::

## Generalizations and related concepts {#Generalizations_and_related_concepts}

The reals and the $p$-adic numbers are the completions of the rationals; it is also possible to complete other fields, for instance general [algebraic number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822), in an analogous way. This will be described now.

Suppose *D* is a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) and *E* is its [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271). Pick a non-zero [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) *P* of *D*. If *x* is a non-zero element of *E*, then *xD* is a [fractional ideal](wikipedia://en/460700) and can be uniquely factored as a product of positive and negative powers of non-zero prime ideals of *D*. Therefore, writing ord<sub>*P*</sub>(*x*) for the exponent of *P* in this factorization gives a well-defined discrete valuation, and for any choice of number *c* greater than 1 we can set

$$|x|_P = c^{-\!\operatorname{ord}_P(x)}.$$

Completing with respect to this absolute value |⋅|<sub>*P*</sub> yields a field *E*<sub>*P*</sub>, the proper generalization of the field of *p*-adic numbers to this setting. The choice of *c* does not change the completion (different choices yield the same concept of Cauchy sequence, so the same completion). It is convenient, when the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) *D*/*P* is finite, to take for *c* the size of *D*/*P*.

For example, when *E* is a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) and *D* is its [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613), [Ostrowski's theorem](wikipedia://en/3360343) says that every non-trivial [non-Archimedean absolute value](wikipedia://en/5042667) on *E* arises as some |⋅|<sub>*P*</sub>. The remaining non-trivial absolute values on *E* arise from the different embeddings of *E* into the real or complex numbers. (In fact, the non-Archimedean absolute values can be considered as simply the different embeddings of *E* into the fields **C**<sub>*p*</sub>, thus putting the description of all the non-trivial absolute values of a number field on a common footing.)

Often, one needs to simultaneously keep track of all the above-mentioned completions when *E* is a number field (or more generally a [global field](wikipedia://en/288358)), which are seen as encoding "local" information. This is accomplished by [adele rings](wikipedia://en/247978) and [idele groups](wikipedia://en/1168614).

*p*-adic integers can be extended to [*p*-adic solenoids](wikipedia://en/2217712#p-adic_solenoids) $\mathbb{T}_p$. There is a map from $\mathbb{T}_p$ to the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) whose fibers are the *p*-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$, in analogy to how there is a map from $\mathbb{R}$ to the circle whose fibers are $\mathbb{Z}$.

The *p*-adic integers can also be extended to [profinite integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profinite_integers) $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, which can be understood as the [direct product of rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_product_of_rings)

$$\widehat{\mathbb{Z}} = \prod_p \mathbb{Z}_p.$$

Unlike the *p*-adic integers which only generalize the modulo over prime powers *p*<sup>*k*</sup>, the profinite integers generalizes the modulo over *all* natural numbers *n*. The [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) likewise implies a structure of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ for composite bases: for any *n* which has at least two distinct prime factors, the *n*-adic integer ring $\mathbb{Z}_n$ is isomorphic to $\prod_{p | n} \mathbb{Z}_p$.[^o55]
