# Symmetric-key algorithm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symmetric-key algorithm"}

*Image: Simple symmetric encryption-en.svg.* Symmetric-key encryption: the same key is used for both encryption and decryption

**Symmetric-key algorithms** are [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) for [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) that use the same [cryptographic keys](wikipedia://en/53039) for both the encryption of [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) and the decryption of [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560). The keys may be identical, or there may be a simple transformation to go between the two keys.[^o2] The keys, in practice, represent a [shared secret](wikipedia://en/7111246) between two or more parties that can be used to maintain a private information link.[^o3] The requirement that both parties have access to the secret key is one of the main drawbacks of [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric)-key encryption, in comparison to [asymmetric-key encryption](wikipedia://en/24222) (also known as public-key encryption).[^o4][^o5] However, symmetric-key encryption algorithms are usually better for bulk encryption. With the exception of the [one-time pad](wikipedia://en/22210) they have a smaller key size, which means less storage space and faster transmission. Due to this, asymmetric-key encryption is often used to exchange the secret key for symmetric-key encryption.[^o6][^o7][^o8]

:::

## Types {#Types}

Symmetric-key encryption can use either [stream ciphers](wikipedia://en/49007) or [block ciphers](wikipedia://en/4594).[^o10]

Stream ciphers encrypt the digits (typically [bytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte)), or letters (in substitution ciphers) of a message one at a time. An example is [ChaCha20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChaCha20). [Substitution ciphers](wikipedia://en/48356) are well-known ciphers, but can be easily decrypted using a [frequency table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_table).[^o11]

Block ciphers take a number of bits and encrypt them in a single unit, padding the plaintext to achieve a multiple of the block size. The [Advanced Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/1260) (AES) algorithm, approved by [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST) in December 2001, uses 128-bit blocks.

## Implementations {#Implementations}

Examples of popular symmetric-key algorithms include [Twofish](wikipedia://en/26508356), [Serpent](wikipedia://en/385154), [AES](wikipedia://en/1260) (Rijndael), [Camellia](wikipedia://en/532475), [Salsa20](wikipedia://en/2701329), [ChaCha20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChaCha20), [Blowfish](wikipedia://en/3940), [CAST5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAST5), [Kuznyechik](wikipedia://en/50158212), [RC4](wikipedia://en/25831), [DES](wikipedia://en/7978), [3DES](wikipedia://en/48375), [Skipjack](wikipedia://en/474702), [Safer](wikipedia://en/630497), and [IDEA](wikipedia://en/15076).[^o14]

:::

## Use as a cryptographic primitive {#Use_as_a_cryptographic_primitive}

Symmetric ciphers are commonly used to achieve other [cryptographic primitives](wikipedia://en/4928985) than just encryption.

Encrypting a message does not guarantee that it will remain unchanged while encrypted. Hence, often a [message authentication code](wikipedia://en/567523) is added to a ciphertext to ensure that changes to the ciphertext will be noted by the receiver. Message authentication codes can be constructed from an [AEAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AEAD) cipher (e.g. [AES-GCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AES-GCM)).

However, symmetric ciphers cannot be used for [non-repudiation](wikipedia://en/52491) purposes except by involving additional parties.[^o16] See the [ISO/IEC 13888-2 standard](http://www.iso.org/iso/iso_catalogue/catalogue_tc/catalogue_detail.htm?csnumber=44736).

Another application is to build [hash functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hash_function) from block ciphers. See [one-way compression function](wikipedia://en/3851622) for descriptions of several such methods.

## Construction of symmetric ciphers {#Construction_of_symmetric_ciphers}

Many modern block ciphers are based on a construction proposed by [Horst Feistel](wikipedia://en/530509). Feistel's construction makes it possible to build invertible functions from other functions that are themselves not invertible.

## Security of symmetric ciphers {#Security_of_symmetric_ciphers}

Symmetric ciphers have historically been susceptible to [known-plaintext attacks](wikipedia://en/517019), [chosen-plaintext attacks](wikipedia://en/63852), [differential cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/8536) and [linear cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/59469). Careful construction of the functions for each [round](wikipedia://en/73297602) can greatly reduce the chances of a successful attack. It is also possible to increase the key length or the rounds in the encryption process to better protect against attack. This, however, tends to increase the processing power and decrease the speed at which the process runs due to the amount of operations the system needs to do.[^o19]

Most modern symmetric-key algorithms appear to be resistant to the threat of [post-quantum cryptography](wikipedia://en/26605226).[^o20] [Quantum computers](wikipedia://en/25220) would exponentially increase the speed at which these ciphers can be decoded; notably, [Grover's algorithm](wikipedia://en/58498) would take the square-root of the time traditionally required for a [brute-force attack](wikipedia://en/53784), although these vulnerabilities can be compensated for by doubling key length.[^o21] For example, a 128 bit AES cipher would not be secure against such an attack as it would reduce the time required to test all possible iterations from over 10 quintillion years to about six months. By contrast, it would still take a quantum computer the same amount of time to decode a 256 bit AES cipher as it would a conventional computer to decode a 128 bit AES cipher.[^o22] For this reason, AES-256 is believed to be "quantum resistant".[^o23][^o24]

## Key management {#Key_management}

## Key establishment {#Key_establishment}

Symmetric-key algorithms require both the sender and the recipient of a message to have the same secret key. All early cryptographic systems required either the sender or the recipient to somehow receive a copy of that secret key over a physically secure channel.

Nearly all modern cryptographic systems still use symmetric-key algorithms internally to encrypt the bulk of the messages, but they eliminate the need for a physically secure channel by using [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903) or some other [public-key protocol](wikipedia://en/24222) to securely come to agreement on a fresh new secret key for each session/conversation (forward secrecy).

## Key generation {#Key_generation}

When used with asymmetric ciphers for key transfer, [pseudorandom key generators](wikipedia://en/182249) are nearly always used to generate the symmetric cipher session keys. However, lack of randomness in those generators or in their [initialization vectors](wikipedia://en/105971) is disastrous and has led to cryptanalytic breaks in the past. Therefore, it is essential that an implementation use a source of high [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445) for its initialization.[^o28][^o29][^o30]

## Reciprocal cipher {#Reciprocal_cipher}

A reciprocal cipher is a cipher where, just as one enters the [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) into the [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) system to get the [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560), one could enter the ciphertext into the same place in the system to get the plaintext. A reciprocal cipher is also sometimes referred as **self-reciprocal cipher**.[^o33][^o34]

Practically all mechanical cipher machines implement a reciprocal cipher, a [mathematical involution](wikipedia://en/293450) on each typed-in letter. Instead of designing two kinds of machines, one for encrypting and one for decrypting, all the machines can be identical and can be set up (keyed) the same way.[^o35]

Examples of reciprocal ciphers include:

- [Atbash](wikipedia://en/46265)
- [Beaufort cipher](wikipedia://en/4277010)[^o36]
- [Enigma machine](wikipedia://en/9256)[^o37]
- the self-reciprocal cipher which Marie Antoinette and [Axel von Fersen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axel_von_Fersen_the_Younger) communicated with.[^o38]
- the Porta polyalphabetic cipher which is self-reciprocal.[^o39]
- [Purple cipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_cipher)[^o40]
- [RC4](wikipedia://en/25831)
- [ROT13](wikipedia://en/48362)
- [XOR cipher](wikipedia://en/939985)
- [Vatsyayana cipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vatsyayana_cipher)

The majority of all modern ciphers can be classified as either a [stream cipher](wikipedia://en/49007), most of which use a reciprocal [XOR cipher](wikipedia://en/939985) combiner, or a [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594), most of which use a [Feistel cipher](wikipedia://en/352709) or [Lai–Massey scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lai–Massey_scheme) with a reciprocal transformation in each round.
