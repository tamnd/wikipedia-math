# Identity matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Identity matrix"}

matrix of ones

*Image: Math\_identify\_matrix\_3x3.png.* Example of a 3x3 identity matrix

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **identity matrix** of size $n$ is the $n\times n$ [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) with ones on the [main diagonal](wikipedia://en/194990) and zeros elsewhere. It has unique properties; for example when the identity matrix represents a [geometric transformation](wikipedia://en/43194879), the object remains unchanged by the transformation. In other contexts, it is analogous to multiplying by the number 1.

:::

## Terminology and notation {#Terminology_and_notation}

The identity matrix is often denoted by $I_n$, or simply by $I$ if the size is immaterial or can be trivially determined by the context.[^o3]

$$I_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\ I_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\ I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} ,\ \dots ,\ I_n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The term **unit matrix** has also been widely used,[^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7] but the term *identity matrix* is now standard.[^o8] The term *unit matrix* is ambiguous, because it is also used for a [matrix of ones](wikipedia://en/690246) and for any [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) of the [ring of all $n\times n$ matrices](wikipedia://en/487541).[^o9]

In some fields, such as [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) or [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the identity matrix is sometimes denoted by a boldface one, $\mathbf{1}$, or called "id" (short for identity). Less frequently, some mathematics books use $U$ or $E$ to represent the identity matrix, standing for "unit matrix"[^o4] and the German word Einheitsmatrix respectively.[^o10]

In terms of a notation that is sometimes used to concisely describe [diagonal matrices](wikipedia://en/174080), the identity matrix can be written as

$$I_n = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1, \dots, 1).$$

The identity matrix can also be written using the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) notation:[^o10]

$$(I_n)_{ij} = \delta_{ij}.$$

## Properties {#Properties}

When $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, it is a property of [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) that

$$I_m A = A I_n = A.$$

In particular, the identity matrix serves as the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) of the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) of all $n\times n$ matrices, and as the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\mathrm{GL}(n)$, which consists of all [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) $n\times n$ matrices under the matrix multiplication operation. In particular, the identity matrix is invertible. It is an [involutory matrix](wikipedia://en/7917643), equal to its own inverse. In this group, two square matrices have the identity matrix as their product exactly when they are the inverses of each other.

When $n\times n$ matrices are used to represent [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) from an $n$-dimensional vector space to itself, the identity matrix $I_n$ represents the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069), for whatever [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) was used in this representation.

The $i$th column of an identity matrix is the [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) $e_i$, a vector whose $i$th entry is 1 and 0 elsewhere. The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the identity matrix is 1, and its [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) is $n$.

The identity matrix is the only [idempotent matrix](wikipedia://en/2974863) with non-zero determinant. That is, it is the only matrix such that:

1. When multiplied by itself, the result is itself
1. All of its rows and columns are [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863).

The [principal square root](wikipedia://en/2929701) of an identity matrix is itself, and this is its only [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) square root. However, every identity matrix with at least two rows and columns has an infinitude of symmetric square roots.[^o13]

The [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of an identity matrix $I_n$ equals the size $n$, i.e.:

$$\operatorname{rank}(I_n) = n .$$
