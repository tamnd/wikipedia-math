# Epimorphism

*Image: Epimorphism scenarios.svg.* When $f$ is an epimorphism, the morphisms $g_1$ and $g_2$ are equal if and only if their compositions with $f$ are.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Epimorphism"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), an **epimorphism** is a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) *f* : *X* → *Y* that is [right-cancellative](wikipedia://en/490646) in the sense that, for all objects *Z* and all morphisms $g_{1}, g_{2}: Y \to Z$,

$$g_1 \circ f = g_2 \circ f \implies g_1 = g_2.$$

:::

Some authors use the adjective **epi** (an epimorphism is a morphism which is epi). Epimorphisms are categorical analogues of [onto or surjective functions](wikipedia://en/27873) (and in the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) the concept corresponds exactly to the surjective functions), but they may not exactly coincide in all contexts. The [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) of an epimorphism is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) (i.e. an epimorphism in a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) *C* is a monomorphism in the [dual category](wikipedia://en/310885) *C*<sup>op</sup>).

Epimorphism can be a subtly weaker condition than surjectivity. For example, in the category of rings, the inclusion $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Q}$ of integers into rational numbers is an epimorphism, since the images of integers under a homomorphism also determine the images of quotients of integers. In the category of [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637), an epimorphism is precisely a continuous function with [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) image, since the image of a [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) determines the image of its limit point: for example the inclusion $\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{R}$ of the [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) of rational numbers into the real number line.

Many authors in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) and [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) define an **epimorphism** simply as an *onto* or [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564). Every epimorphism in this algebraic sense is an epimorphism in the sense of category theory, but the converse is not true in all categories. In this article, the term "epimorphism" will be used in the sense of category theory given above. For more on this, see below.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In a [concrete category](wikipedia://en/167001) (in which each object has an underlying set), if the underlying function of a morphism is surjective, then the morphism is epi. In many concrete categories of interest the converse is also true. For example, in the following categories, the epimorphisms are exactly those morphisms that are surjective on the underlying sets:

- **[Set](wikipedia://en/26829)**: [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) and functions. To prove that every epimorphism $f : X \to Y$ in **Set** is surjective, we compose it with both the [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) $g_{1} : Y \to \{0, 1\}$ of the image $f(X)$ and the map $g_{2} : Y \to \{0, 1\}$ that is constant 1.
- **Rel**: sets with [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931) and relation-preserving functions. Here we can use the same proof as for **Set**, equipping $\{0, 1\}$ with the full relation $\{0, 1\}\times\{0, 1\}$.
- **Pos**: [partially ordered sets](wikipedia://en/23572) and [monotone functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function). If $f : (X, \le) \to (Y, \le)$ is not surjective, pick $y_{0}$ in $Y \backslash f(X)$ and let $g_{1} : Y \to \{0, 1\}$ be the characteristic function of $\{y\mid y_{0} \le y\}$ and $g_{2} : Y \to \{0, 1\}$ the characteristic function of $\{y | y_{0} < y\}$. These maps are monotone if $\{0, 1\}$ is given the standard ordering $0 < 1$.
- **[Grp](wikipedia://en/800092)**: [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) and [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396). The result that every epimorphism in **Grp** is surjective is due to [Otto Schreier](wikipedia://en/4355201) (he actually proved more, showing that every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) is an [equalizer](wikipedia://en/310767) using the [free product](wikipedia://en/360036) with one amalgamated subgroup); an [elementary proof](wikipedia://en/9318685) can be found in (Linderholm 1970).
- **FinGrp**: [finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_groups) and group homomorphisms. Also due to Schreier; the proof given in (Linderholm 1970) establishes this case as well.
- **[Ab](wikipedia://en/454748)**: [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) and group homomorphisms.
- **[$K$-Vect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_spaces)**: [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ and [$K$-linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation).
- **Mod**-$R$: [right modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ and [module homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/474118). This generalizes the two previous examples; to prove that every epimorphism $f : X \to Y$ in **Mod**-$R$ is surjective, we compose it with both the canonical [quotient map](wikipedia://en/364820) $g_{1} : Y \to Y/f(X)$ and the [zero map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_map) $g_{2} : Y \to Y/f(X)$.
- **[Top](wikipedia://en/435051)**: [topological spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_spaces) and [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122). To prove that every epimorphism in **Top** is surjective, we proceed exactly as in **Set**, giving {0, 1} the [indiscrete topology](wikipedia://en/382447), which ensures that all considered maps are continuous.
- **HComp**: [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) and continuous functions. If $f : X \to Y$ is not surjective, let $y \in Y - fX.$ Since $fX$ is closed, by [Urysohn's Lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urysohn's_Lemma) there is a continuous function $g_{1} : Y \to [0, 1]$ such that $g_{1}$ is $0$ on $fX$ and $1$ on $y$. We compose $f$ with both $g_{1}$ and the zero function $g_{2} : Y \to [0, 1].$

However, there are also many concrete categories of interest where epimorphisms fail to be surjective. A few examples are:

- In the [category of monoids](wikipedia://en/3955446), **Mon**, the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) **N** → **Z** is a non-surjective epimorphism. To see this, suppose that *g*<sub>1</sub> and *g*<sub>2</sub> are two distinct maps from **Z** to some monoid *M*. Then for some *n* in **Z**, *g*<sub>1</sub>(*n*) ≠ *g*<sub>2</sub>(*n*), so *g*<sub>1</sub>(−*n*) ≠ *g*<sub>2</sub>(−*n*). Either *n* or −*n* is in **N**, so the restrictions of *g*<sub>1</sub> and *g*<sub>2</sub> to **N** are unequal.
- In the category of algebras over commutative ring $R$, take $R[x]\to R[x,x^{-1}]$ the polyomials over $R$ included in the [Laurent polynomials](wikipedia://en/61351) (this is the morphism of [monoid rings](wikipedia://en/20781) corresponding to the above inclusion **N** → **Z**). This is an epimorphism since any homomorphism of algebras respects multiplicative inverse whenever it is defined, so the image of $x\in R[x]$ determines the image of any Laurent polynomial.
- In the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181), **Ring**, the inclusion map **Z** → **Q** is a non-surjective epimorphism; to see this, note that any [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) on **Q** is determined entirely by its action on **Z**, similar to the previous example. A similar argument shows that the natural ring homomorphism from any [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* to any one of its [localizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) is an epimorphism.
- In the [category of commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_commutative_rings), a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/11985661) homomorphism of rings *f* : *R* → *S* is an epimorphism if and only if for all [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) *P* of *R*, the ideal *Q* generated by *f*(*P*) is either *S* or is prime, and if *Q* is not *S*, the induced map [Frac](wikipedia://en/25271)(*R*/*P*) → Frac(*S*/*Q*) is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) ([EGA](wikipedia://en/471387) IV 17.2.6).
- In the category of Hausdorff spaces, **Haus**, the epimorphisms are precisely the continuous functions with [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) images. For example, the inclusion map **Q** → **R** is a non-surjective epimorphism.

The above differs from the case of monomorphisms where it is more frequently true that monomorphisms are precisely those whose underlying functions are [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective).

As for examples of epimorphisms in non-concrete categories:

- If a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) or [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is considered as a category with a single object (composition of morphisms given by multiplication), then the epimorphisms are precisely the right-cancellable elements.
- If a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) is considered as a category (objects are the vertices, morphisms are the paths, composition of morphisms is the concatenation of paths), then *every* morphism is an epimorphism.

## Properties {#Properties}

Every [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) is an epimorphism; indeed only a right-sided inverse is needed: suppose there exists a morphism $j : Y \to X$ such that *fj* = id<sub>*Y*</sub>. For any morphisms $h_1, h_2: Y \to Z$ where $h_1f = h_2f$, you have that $h_1 = h_1 id_Y = h_1fj = h_2fj = h_2$. A map with such a right-sided inverse is called a **[split epi](wikipedia://en/10630303)**. In an [elementary topos](wikipedia://en/815802), a map that is both a [monic morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monic_morphism) and an epimorphism is an isomorphism.

The composition of two epimorphisms is again an epimorphism. If the composition *fg* of two morphisms is an epimorphism, then *f* must be an epimorphism.

As some of the above examples show, the property of being an epimorphism is not determined by its behavior as a function, but also by the category of context. If *D* is a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) of *C*, then every morphism in *D* that is an epimorphism when considered as a morphism in *C* is also an epimorphism in *D*. However the converse need not hold; the smaller category can (and often will) have more epimorphisms.

As for most concepts in category theory, epimorphisms are preserved under [equivalences of categories](wikipedia://en/379051): given an equivalence *F* : *C* → *D*, a morphism *f* is an epimorphism in the category *C* if and only if *F*(*f*) is an epimorphism in *D*. A [duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duality_%28category_theory%29) between two categories turns epimorphisms into monomorphisms, and vice versa.

The definition of epimorphism may be reformulated to state that *f* : *X* → *Y* is an epimorphism if and only if the induced maps

$$\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Hom}(Y,Z) &\rightarrow& \operatorname{Hom}(X,Z)\\ g &\mapsto& gf\end{matrix}$$

are [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) for every choice of *Z*. This in turn is equivalent to the induced [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022)

$$\begin{matrix}\operatorname{Hom}(Y,-) &\rightarrow& \operatorname{Hom}(X,-)\end{matrix}$$

being a monomorphism in the [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) **Set**<sup>*C*</sup>.

Every [coequalizer](wikipedia://en/431940) is an epimorphism, a consequence of the uniqueness requirement in the definition of coequalizers. It follows in particular that every [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) is an epimorphism. The converse, namely that every epimorphism be a coequalizer, is not true in all categories.

In many categories it is possible to write every morphism as the composition of an epimorphism followed by a monomorphism. For instance, given a group homomorphism *f* : *G* → *H*, we can define the group *K* = im(*f*) and then write *f* as the composition of the surjective homomorphism *G* → *K* that is defined like *f*, followed by the injective homomorphism *K* → *H* that sends each element to itself. Such a factorization of an arbitrary morphism into an epimorphism followed by a monomorphism can be carried out in all [abelian categories](wikipedia://en/45063) and also in all the concrete categories mentioned above in (though not in all concrete categories).

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

*Image: Diagram-orthogonality-2.svg.* Diagram visualizing a strong epimorphism $\varepsilon$.

Among other useful concepts are *regular epimorphism*, *extremal epimorphism*, *immediate epimorphism*, *strong epimorphism*, and *split epimorphism*.

- An epimorphism is said to be **regular** if it is a [coequalizer](wikipedia://en/431940) of some pair of parallel morphisms.[^o8]
- An epimorphism *f* is said to be **strict** if it is a coequalizer of every pair of morphisms *g, h* such that $f \circ g = f \circ h$.[^o9]
- An epimorphism $\varepsilon$ is said to be **extremal**[^n1] if in each representation $\varepsilon=\mu\circ\varphi$, where $\mu$ is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538), the morphism $\mu$ is automatically an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828).
- An epimorphism $\varepsilon$ is said to be **immediate** if in each representation $\varepsilon=\mu\circ\varepsilon'$, where $\mu$ is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) and $\varepsilon'$ is an epimorphism, the morphism $\mu$ is automatically an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828).
- An epimorphism $\varepsilon:A\to B$ is said to be **strong**[^n1][^n2] if for any [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) $\mu:C\to D$ and any morphisms $\alpha:A\to C$ and $\beta:B\to D$ such that $\beta\circ\varepsilon=\mu\circ\alpha$, there exists a morphism $\delta:B\to C$ such that $\delta\circ\varepsilon=\alpha$ and $\mu\circ\delta=\beta$.
- An epimorphism $\varepsilon$ is said to be **split** if there exists a morphism $\mu$ such that $\varepsilon\circ\mu=1$ (in this case $\mu$ is called a right-sided inverse for $\varepsilon$).

There is also the notion of **homological epimorphism** in ring theory. A morphism *f*: *A* → *B* of rings is a homological epimorphism if it is an epimorphism and it induces a [full and faithful functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_and_faithful_functor) on [derived categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_categories): D(*f*) : D(*B*) → D(*A*).

A morphism that is both a monomorphism and an epimorphism is called a [bimorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimorphism). Every isomorphism is a bimorphism but the converse is not true in general. For example, the map from the [half-open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-open_interval) \[0,1) to the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) S<sup>1</sup> (thought of as a [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_subspace) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628)) that sends *x* to exp(2πi*x*) (see [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613)) is continuous and bijective but not a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) since the inverse map is not continuous at 1, so it is an instance of a bimorphism that is not an isomorphism in the category **Top**. Another example is the embedding $\mathbf{Q} \to \mathbf{R}$ in the category **Haus**; as noted above, it is a bimorphism, but it is not bijective and therefore not an isomorphism. Similarly, in the category of [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29), the map $\mathbf{Z} \to \mathbf{Q}$ is a bimorphism but not an isomorphism.

Epimorphisms are used to define abstract [quotient objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_object) in general categories: two epimorphisms *f*<sub>1</sub> : *X* → *Y*<sub>1</sub> and *f*<sub>2</sub> : *X* → *Y*<sub>2</sub> are said to be *equivalent* if there exists an isomorphism *j* : *Y*<sub>1</sub> → *Y*<sub>2</sub> with $j f_{1} = f_{2}.$ This is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259), and the equivalence classes are defined to be the quotient objects of *X*.

## Terminology {#Terminology}

The companion terms *epimorphism* and *[monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538)* were first introduced by [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394). Bourbaki uses *epimorphism* as shorthand for [surjective function](wikipedia://en/27873). Early category theorists believed that epimorphisms were the correct analogue of surjections in an arbitrary category, similar to how monomorphisms are a near analogue of injections. Unfortunately this is incorrect; strong or regular epimorphisms behave much more like surjections than do ordinary epimorphisms.

[Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574) attempted to create a distinction between *epimorphisms*, which were maps in a concrete category whose underlying set maps were surjective, and *epic morphisms*, which are epimorphisms in the categorical sense. However, this distinction never caught on.

It is a common misconception that epimorphism is a better concept than surjectivity. Unfortunately, this is rarely the case; epimorphisms can be very mysterious and have unexpected behavior. It is very difficult, for example, to classify all the epimorphisms of rings. In general, epimorphism is an unruly concept, related to surjectivity but fundamentally different.
