# Sawtooth wave

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Sawtooth wave"}

The **sawtooth wave** (or **saw wave**) is a kind of [non-sinusoidal waveform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-sinusoidal_waveform). It is so named based on its resemblance to the teeth of a plain-toothed [saw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saw) with a zero [rake angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rake_angle). A single sawtooth, or an intermittently triggered sawtooth, is called a **ramp waveform**.

:::

The convention is that a sawtooth wave ramps upward and then sharply drops. In a reverse (or inverse) sawtooth wave, the wave ramps downward and then sharply rises. It can also be considered the extreme case of an asymmetric [triangle wave](wikipedia://en/52151).[^o4]

The equivalent [piecewise linear functions](wikipedia://en/444224)

$$x(t) = t - \lfloor t \rfloor$$

$$x(t) = t \bmod 1$$

based on the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function) of time *t* is an example of a sawtooth wave with [period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_period) 1.

A more general form, in the range −1 to 1, and with period *p*, is

$$2\left({\frac t p} - \left\lfloor {\frac 1 2} + {\frac t p} \right\rfloor\right)$$

This sawtooth function has the same [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) as the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function.

While a [square wave](wikipedia://en/314652) is constructed from only odd harmonics, a sawtooth wave's sound is harsh and clear and its spectrum contains both even and odd [harmonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic) of the [fundamental frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_frequency). Because it contains all the integer harmonics, it is one of the best waveforms to use for [subtractive synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtractive_synthesis) of musical sounds, particularly bowed string instruments like violins and cellos, since the [slip-stick behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stick-slip_phenomenon) of the bow drives the strings with a sawtooth-like motion.[^o5]

A sawtooth can be constructed using [additive synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_synthesis). For period *p* and amplitude *a*, the following infinite [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) converge to a sawtooth and a reverse (inverse) sawtooth wave:

$$f = \frac{1}{p}$$

$$x_\text{sawtooth}(t) = - \frac{2 a}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} {(-1)}^{k} \frac{\sin(2\pi kft)}{k}$$

$$x_\text{reverse sawtooth}(t) = \frac{2a}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} {(-1)}^{k} \frac{\sin(2\pi kft)}{k}$$

In [digital](wikipedia://en/8276) synthesis, these series are only summed over *k* such that the highest harmonic, *N*<sub>max</sub>, is less than the [Nyquist frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyquist_frequency) (half the [sampling frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_frequency)). This summation can generally be more efficiently calculated with a [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512). If the waveform is digitally created directly in the time domain using a non-[bandlimited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandlimited) form, such as *y* = *x* − [floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function)(*x*), infinite harmonics are sampled and the resulting tone contains [aliasing](wikipedia://en/151474) distortion.

## Applications {#Applications}

- Sawtooth waves are known for their use in [electronic music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_music), such as the [Hoover sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_sound). The sawtooth and square waves are among the most common waveforms used to create sounds with subtractive [analog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtractive_synthesis) and [virtual analog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_analog) music synthesizers.
- Sawtooth waves are used in [switched-mode power supplies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switched-mode_power_supply). In the regulator chip the feedback signal from the output is continuously compared to a high-frequency sawtooth to generate a new duty cycle PWM signal on the output of the [comparator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparator).
- In the field of computer science, particularly in automation and robotics, $\mathrm{sawtooth}(\theta)=2\arctan(\tan(\frac{\theta}{2}))$ allows to calculate sums and differences of angles while avoiding discontinuities at 360° and 0°.
- The sawtooth wave is the form of the vertical and horizontal [deflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_%28physics%29) signals used to generate a [raster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raster_graphics) on [CRT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode-ray_tube)-based television or monitor screens. [Oscilloscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscilloscope) also use a sawtooth wave for their horizontal deflection, though they typically use [electrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic) deflection.
  - On the wave's "ramp", the magnetic field produced by the deflection yoke drags the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) beam across the face of the CRT, creating a [scan line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scan_line).
  - On the wave's "cliff", the magnetic field suddenly collapses, causing the electron beam to return to its resting position as quickly as possible.
  - The current applied to the deflection yoke is adjusted by various means (transformers, capacitors, center-tapped windings) so that the half-way voltage on the sawtooth's cliff is at the zero mark, meaning that a negative current will cause deflection in one direction, and a positive current deflection in the other; thus, a center-mounted deflection yoke can use the whole screen area to depict a trace. The horizontal frequency is 15.734 kHz on [NTSC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTSC), 15.625 kHz for [PAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PAL) and [SECAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SECAM).
  - The vertical deflection system operates the same way as the horizontal, though at a much lower frequency (59.94 Hz on [NTSC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NTSC), 50 Hz for PAL and SECAM).
  - The ramp portion of the wave must appear as a straight line. If otherwise, it indicates that the current isn't increasing linearly, and therefore that the magnetic field produced by the deflection yoke is not linear. As a result, the electron beam will accelerate during the non-linear portions. This would result in a television image "squished" in the direction of the non-linearity. Extreme cases will show marked brightness increases, since the electron beam spends more time on that side of the picture.
  - The first television receivers had controls allowing users to adjust the picture's vertical or horizontal linearity. Such controls were not present on later sets as the stability of electronic components had improved.
