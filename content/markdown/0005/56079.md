# Krull dimension

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Krull dimension"}

In [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), the **Krull dimension** of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R*, named after [Wolfgang Krull](wikipedia://en/964328), is the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the lengths of all [chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_%28order_theory%29) of [prime ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_ideals). The Krull dimension need not be finite even for a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129). More generally the Krull dimension can be defined for [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over possibly [non-commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring) as the [deviation](wikipedia://en/38289443) of the [poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) of submodules.

:::

The Krull dimension was introduced to provide an algebraic definition of the [dimension of an algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/253251): the dimension of the [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468) defined by an ideal *I* in a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) *R* is the Krull dimension of *R*/*I*.

A [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k* has Krull dimension 0; more generally, *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] has Krull dimension *n*. A [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) that is not a field has Krull dimension 1. A [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) has Krull dimension 0 if and only if every element of its [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235).

There are several other ways that have been used to define the dimension of a ring. Most of them coincide with the Krull dimension for Noetherian rings, but can differ for non-Noetherian rings.

## Explanation {#Explanation}

We say that a chain of prime ideals of the form $\mathfrak{p}_0\subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_1\subsetneq \ldots \subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_n$ has **length $n$**. That is, the length is the number of strict inclusions, not the number of primes; these differ by $1$. We define the **Krull dimension** of $R$ to be the supremum of the lengths of all chains of prime ideals in $R$.

Given a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ in $R$, we define the **height** of $\mathfrak{p}$, written $\operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak{p})$, to be the supremum of the lengths of all chains of prime ideals contained in $\mathfrak{p}$, meaning that $\mathfrak{p}_0\subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_1\subsetneq \ldots \subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_n = \mathfrak{p}$.[^o3] In other words, the height of $\mathfrak{p}$ is the Krull dimension of the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of $R$ at $\mathfrak{p}$. A prime ideal has height zero if and only if it is a [minimal prime ideal](wikipedia://en/16143832). The Krull dimension of a ring is the supremum of the heights of all maximal ideals, or those of all prime ideals. The height is also sometimes called the codimension, rank, or altitude of a prime ideal.

In a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129), every prime ideal has finite height. Nonetheless, [Nagata](wikipedia://en/3086117) gave an example of a Noetherian ring of infinite Krull dimension.[^o4] A ring is called **[catenary](wikipedia://en/10034460)** if any inclusion $\mathfrak{p}\subset \mathfrak{q}$ of prime ideals can be extended to a maximal chain of prime ideals between $\mathfrak{p}$ and $\mathfrak{q}$, and any two maximal chains between $\mathfrak{p}$ and $\mathfrak{q}$ have the same length. A ring is called [universally catenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_catenary) if any finitely generated algebra over it is catenary. Nagata gave an example of a Noetherian ring which is not catenary.[^o5]

In a Noetherian ring, a prime ideal has height at most $n$ if and only if it is a [minimal prime ideal](wikipedia://en/16143832) over an ideal generated by $n$ elements ([Krull's height theorem](wikipedia://en/542421) and its converse).[^o6] It implies that the [descending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_chain_condition) holds for prime ideals in such a way the lengths of the chains descending from a prime ideal are bounded by the number of generators of the prime.[^o7]

More generally, the height of an ideal $I$ is the infimum of the heights of all prime ideals containing $I$. In the language of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), this is the [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) of the subvariety of $\mathrm{Spec}(R)$ corresponding to $I$.[^o3]

## Schemes {#Schemes}

It follows readily from the definition of the [spectrum of a ring](wikipedia://en/37291) Spec(*R*), the space of prime ideals of *R* equipped with the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744), that the Krull dimension of *R* is equal to the dimension of its spectrum as a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), meaning the supremum of the lengths of all chains of [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_space) closed subsets. This follows immediately from the [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) between ideals of *R* and closed subsets of Spec(*R*) and the observation that, by the definition of Spec(*R*), each prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ of *R* corresponds to a generic point of the closed subset associated to $\mathfrak{p}$ by the Galois connection.

## Examples {#Examples}

- The dimension of a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) over a field *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] is the number of variables *n*. In the language of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), this says that the affine space of dimension *n* over a field has dimension *n*, as expected. In general, if *R* is a [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) ring of dimension *n*, then the dimension of *R*\[*x*\] is *n* + 1. If the Noetherian hypothesis is dropped, then *R*\[*x*\] can have dimension anywhere between *n* + 1 and 2*n* + 1.
- For example, the ideal $\mathfrak{p} = (y^2 - x, y) \subset \mathbb{C}[x,y]$ has height 2 since we can form the maximal ascending chain of prime ideals$(0)=\mathfrak{p}_0 \subsetneq (y^2 - x)= \mathfrak{p}_1 \subsetneq (y^2 - x, y) = \mathfrak{p}_2 = \mathfrak{p}$.
- Given an irreducible polynomial $f \in \mathbb{C}[x,y,z]$, the ideal $I = (f^3)$ is not prime (since $f\cdot f^2 \in I$, but neither of the factors are), but we can easily compute the height since the smallest prime ideal containing $I$ is just $(f)$.
- The ring of integers **Z** has dimension 1. More generally, any [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) that is not a field has dimension 1.
- An [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) is a field if and only if its Krull dimension is zero. [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132) that are not fields (for example, [discrete valuation rings](wikipedia://en/848633)) have dimension one.
- The Krull dimension of the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) is typically defined to be either $-\infty$ or $-1$. The zero ring is the only ring with a negative dimension.
- A ring is [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) if and only if it is [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) and its Krull dimension is ≤0.
- An [integral extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_extension) of a ring has the same dimension as the ring does.
- Let *R* be an algebra over a field *k* that is an integral domain. Then the Krull dimension of *R* is less than or equal to the transcendence degree of the field of fractions of *R* over *k*.[^o11] The equality holds if *R* is finitely generated as an algebra (for instance by the [Noether normalization lemma](wikipedia://en/3003553)).
- Let *R* be a Noetherian ring, *I* an ideal and $\operatorname{gr}_I(R) = \bigoplus_{k=0}^\infty I^k/I^{k+1}$ be the [associated graded ring](wikipedia://en/38279783) (geometers call it the ring of the [normal cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_cone) of *I*). Then $\operatorname{dim} \operatorname{gr}_I(R)$ is the supremum of the heights of maximal ideals of *R* containing *I*.[^o12]
- A commutative Noetherian ring of Krull dimension zero is a direct product of a finite number (possibly one) of [local rings](wikipedia://en/153391) of Krull dimension zero.
- A Noetherian local ring is called a [Cohen–Macaulay ring](wikipedia://en/497407) if its dimension is equal to its [depth](wikipedia://en/9770927). A [regular local ring](wikipedia://en/506959) is an example of such a ring.
- A Noetherian [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) is a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) if and only if every height 1 prime ideal is principal.[^o13]
- For a commutative Noetherian ring the three following conditions are equivalent: being a [reduced ring](wikipedia://en/539749) of Krull dimension zero, being a field or a [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of fields, being [von Neumann regular](wikipedia://en/907524).

## Of a module {#Of_a_module}

If $R$ is a commutative ring, and $M$ is an $R$-module, we define the Krull dimension of $M$ to be the Krull dimension of the quotient of $R$ making $M$ a [faithful module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_module). That is, we define it by the formula:

$$\dim_R M := \dim( R/{\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)})$$

where $\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)$, the [annihilator](wikipedia://en/328684), is the kernel of the natural map $R\to\operatorname{End}_R(M)$ of $R$ into the ring of $R$-linear endomorphisms of $M$.

In the language of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754), finitely generated modules are interpreted as [coherent sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_sheaves), or generalized finite rank [vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundles).

:::

## For non-commutative rings {#For_non-commutative_rings}

The Krull dimension of a module over a possibly non-commutative ring is defined as the [deviation](wikipedia://en/38289443) of the poset of submodules ordered by inclusion. For commutative Noetherian rings, this is the same as the definition using chains of prime ideals.[^o17] The two definitions can be different for commutative rings which are not Noetherian.
