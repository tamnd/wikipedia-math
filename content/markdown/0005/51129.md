# Nash embedding theorems

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nash embedding theorems"}

The **Nash embedding theorems** (or **imbedding theorems**), named after [John Forbes Nash Jr.](wikipedia://en/102567), state that every [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) can be isometrically [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) into some [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). [Isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) means preserving the length of every [path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifiable_path). For instance, bending but neither stretching nor tearing a page of paper gives an [isometric embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_embedding) of the page into three-dimensional Euclidean space because curves drawn on the page retain the same [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) however the page is bent.

:::

The first theorem is for [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable) ($C^{1}$) embeddings and the second for embeddings that are [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478) or [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) of class $C^{k}$, $3 \le k \le \infty$. These two theorems are very different from each other. The first theorem has a very simple proof but leads to some counterintuitive conclusions, while the second theorem has a technical and counterintuitive proof but leads to a less surprising result.

The *C*<sup>1</sup> theorem was published in 1954, and the $C^{k}$ theorem in 1956. The real analytic theorem was first treated by Nash in 1966; his argument was simplified considerably by (Greene Jacobowitz 1971). (A local version of this result was proved by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) and [Maurice Janet](wikipedia://en/50533623) in the 1920s.) In the real analytic case, the smoothing operators (see below) in the Nash inverse function argument can be replaced by Cauchy estimates. Nash's proof of the $C^{k}$ case was later extrapolated into the [h-principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-principle) and [Nash–Moser implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/2286045). A simpler proof of the second Nash embedding theorem was obtained by (Günther 1989) who reduced the set of nonlinear [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) to an elliptic system, to which the [contraction mapping theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraction_mapping_theorem) could be applied.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Nash–Kuiper theorem ( embedding theorem)"}

## Nash–Kuiper theorem ($C^{1}$ embedding theorem) {#Nash–Kuiper_theorem_(_embedding_theorem)}

Given an $m$-dimensional Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$, an *isometric embedding* is a continuously differentiable [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding) $f : M \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ such that the [pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508) of the Euclidean metric equals $g$. In analytical terms, this may be viewed (relative to a smooth [coordinate chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_chart) $x$) as a system of $\tfrac{1}{2}m(m + 1)$ many first-order [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) for $n$ unknown (real-valued) functions:

$$g_{ij}(x)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^n\frac{\partial f^\alpha}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial f^\alpha}{\partial x^j}.$$

If $n$ is less than $\tfrac{1}{2}m(m + 1)$, then there are more equations than unknowns. From this perspective, the existence of isometric embeddings given by the following theorem is considered surprising.

> **Nash–Kuiper theorem.** Let $(M, g)$ be an $m$-dimensional Riemannian manifold and $f : M \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ a [short](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_map) smooth embedding (or [immersion](wikipedia://en/6993953)) into Euclidean space $\mathbf{R}^{n}$, where $n \ge m + 1$. This map is not required to be isometric. Then there is a sequence of continuously differentiable isometric embeddings (or immersions) $M \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ of $g$ which [converge uniformly](wikipedia://en/50652) to $f$.

The theorem was originally proved by John Nash with the stronger assumption $n \ge m + 2$. His method was modified by [Nicolaas Kuiper](wikipedia://en/3344929) to obtain the theorem above.

The isometric embeddings produced by the Nash–Kuiper theorem are often considered counterintuitive and pathological. They often fail to be smoothly differentiable. For example, a [well-known theorem](wikipedia://en/11720315) of [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) asserts that the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane) cannot be smoothly isometrically immersed into $\mathbf{R}^{3}$. Any [Einstein manifold](wikipedia://en/644814) of negative [scalar curvature](wikipedia://en/285622) cannot be smoothly isometrically immersed as a hypersurface, and a theorem of [Shiing-Shen Chern](wikipedia://en/391905) and Kuiper even says that any [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) $m$-dimensional manifold of nonpositive [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) cannot be smoothly isometrically immersed in $\mathbf{R}^{2m-1}$. Furthermore, some smooth isometric embeddings exhibit rigidity phenomena which are violated by the largely unrestricted choice of $f$ in the Nash–Kuiper theorem. For example, the image of any smooth isometric hypersurface immersion of the round sphere must itself be a round sphere. By contrast, the Nash–Kuiper theorem ensures the existence of continuously differentiable isometric hypersurface immersions of the round sphere which are arbitrarily close to (for instance) a topological embedding of the sphere as a small [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381).

Any closed and oriented two-dimensional manifold can be smoothly embedded in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$. Any such embedding can be scaled by an arbitrarily small constant so as to become short, relative to any given Riemannian metric on the surface. It follows from the Nash–Kuiper theorem that there are continuously differentiable isometric embeddings of any such Riemannian surface where the radius of a circumscribed ball is arbitrarily small. By contrast, no negatively curved closed surface can even be smoothly isometrically embedded in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$. Moreover, for any smooth (or even $C^{2}$) isometric embedding of an arbitrary closed Riemannian surface, there is a quantitative (positive) lower bound on the radius of a circumscribed ball in terms of the surface area and curvature of the embedded metric.

In higher dimension, as follows from the [Whitney embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/477578), the Nash–Kuiper theorem shows that any closed $m$-dimensional Riemannian manifold admits an continuously differentiable isometric embedding into an *arbitrarily small neighborhood* in $2m$-dimensional Euclidean space. Although Whitney's theorem also applies to noncompact manifolds, such embeddings cannot simply be scaled by a small constant so as to become short. Nash proved that every $m$-dimensional Riemannian manifold admits a continuously differentiable isometric embedding into $\mathbf{R}^{2m+1}$.

At the time of Nash's work, his theorem was considered to be something of a mathematical curiosity. The result itself has not found major applications. However, Nash's method of proof was adapted by [Camillo De Lellis](wikipedia://en/39351243) and László Székelyhidi to construct low-regularity solutions, with prescribed [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327), of the [Euler equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_equation) from the mathematical study of [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988). In analytical terms, the Euler equations have a formal similarity to the isometric embedding equations, via the quadratic nonlinearity in the first derivatives of the unknown function. The ideas of Nash's proof were abstracted by [Mikhael Gromov](wikipedia://en/771562) to the principle of *convex integration*, with a corresponding [h-principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-principle). This was applied by [Stefan Müller](wikipedia://en/24863845) and [Vladimír Šverák](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimír_Šverák) to [Hilbert's nineteenth problem](wikipedia://en/2336226), constructing minimizers of minimal differentiability in the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882).

## *C*<sup>*k*</sup> embedding theorem {#Ck_embedding_theorem}

The technical statement appearing in Nash's original paper is as follows: if $M$ is a given $m$-dimensional Riemannian manifold (analytic or of class $C^{k}$, $3 \le k \le \infty$), then there exists a number *n* (with $n \le m(3m + 11)/2$ if *M* is a compact manifold, and with *n* ≤ *m*(*m* + 1)(3*m* + 11)/2 if *M* is a non-compact manifold) and an [isometric embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_embedding) $f : M \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ (also analytic or of class *C<sup>k</sup>*). That is $f$ is an [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119#Differential_topology) of $C^{k}$ manifolds and for every point $p$ of $M$, the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) $df_{p}$ is a [linear map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) from the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) $T_{p}M$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ that is compatible with the given [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856) on $T_{p}M$ and the standard [dot product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product) of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ in the following sense:

$$\langle u,v \rangle = df_p(u)\cdot df_p(v)$$

for all vectors $u$, $v$ in $T_{p}M$. When $n$ is larger than $\tfrac{1}{2}m(m + 1)$, this is an underdetermined system of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) (PDEs).

The Nash embedding theorem is a global theorem in the sense that the whole manifold is embedded into $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. A local embedding theorem is much simpler and can be proved using the [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005) of advanced calculus in a [coordinate neighborhood](wikipedia://en/2073470#Charts) of the manifold. The proof of the global embedding theorem relies on Nash's implicit function theorem for isometric embeddings. This theorem has been generalized by a number of other authors to abstract contexts, where it is known as [Nash–Moser theorem](wikipedia://en/2286045). The basic idea in the proof of Nash's implicit function theorem is the use of [Newton's method](wikipedia://en/22145) to construct solutions. The standard Newton's method fails to converge when applied to the system; Nash uses smoothing operators defined by [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) to make the Newton iteration converge: this is Newton's method with postconditioning. The fact that this technique furnishes a solution is in itself an [existence theorem](wikipedia://en/251177) and of independent interest. In other contexts, the [convergence of the standard Newton's method](wikipedia://en/10349343) had earlier been proved by [Leonid Kantorovitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Kantorovitch).

:::

## Extension to semi-Riemannian and Lorentzian cases {#Extension_to_semi-Riemannian_and_Lorentzian_cases}

The problem of isometric embeddability naturally extends to [semi-Riemannian metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-Riemannian_manifold), that is, non-degenerate metrics of arbitrary index. In 1970, Greene[^o9] and Clarke[^o10] independently demonstrated that Nash’s embedding theorem could be extended to indefinite metrics—and even degenerate ones—by algebraically reducing the problem to the Riemannian case.

Specifically, any smooth $n$-manifold $M^n$ endowed with a semi-Riemannian metric $g$ can be isometrically embedded into a semi-Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^N_\nu$ for a sufficiently large dimension $N$ and index $\nu$. Both authors also improved the Nash value of $N$ as a function of $n$: Greene utilized the implicit function theorem as formulated by Schwartz (1960)[^o11], while Clarke employed a technique inspired by Kuiper ((Kuiper 1955a), (Kuiper 1955b)).

A more restrictive problem arises when the index of the ambient semi-Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^N_\nu$ is fixed equal to the index of the metric $g$. This is particularly relevant for Lorentzian signature (case $\nu=1$) where the existence of an embedding relates to the [causality conditions](wikipedia://en/14346663)—properties that remain invariant under conformal transformations. Müller and Sánchez (2011)[^o14] provided a complete solution to this problem:

**(1) Isometric embedding criterion:** A Lorentzian $C^3$-manifold $(M,g)$ is isometrically embeddable in Lorentz-Minkowski spacetime $\mathbb{L}^N := \mathbb{R}^N_1$ if and only if it admits a steep temporal function. This is a smooth function $\tau$ whose gradient $\nabla \tau$ satisfies:

$$g(\nabla \tau, \nabla \tau) \leq -1$$

- **Necessity:** If such an embedding exists, the restriction of the natural time coordinate of $\mathbb{L}^N$ to $M$ yields a steep temporal function.
- **Sufficiency:** This is proven by applying a Nash embedding to a Riemannian metric constructed by reversing the sign of the Lorentzian metric in the direction of $\nabla \tau$ within the orthogonal decomposition $TM = \langle\nabla \tau\rangle \oplus \nabla \tau^\perp$.

**Observations:**

- The existence of $\tau$ implies that $g$ is [time-orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_orientability).
- $\tau$ serves as a temporal function for the spacetime obtained [time-orientating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_orientability) $(M,g)$ so that $\nabla \tau$ is past-directed. Consequently, this spacetime is [stably causal](wikipedia://en/14346663#Stable_causality).
- While not all stably causal spacetimes admit a steep temporal function, they all admit a conformal metric that does. Thus, all stably causal spacetimes are conformally embeddable in $\mathbb{L}^N$ for large $N$.

**(2) Global hyperbolicity:** Any [globally hyperbolic spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globally_hyperbolic_manifold) admits a steep temporal function.

This result was achieved by refining the techniques introduced by Bernal and Sánchez (2005) to construct Cauchy temporal functions. These functions were originally designed to prove the existence of [Bernal–Sánchez splittings](wikipedia://en/34265825#Bernal–Sánchez_smooth_and_orthogonal_splittings). Müller and Sánchez extended this to prove the existence of steep Cauchy temporal functions, which simultaneously ensure both the splitting and the isometric embedding.

**Observations:**

- **Conformal properties.** All metrics conformal to a globally hyperbolic one remain globally hyperbolic and are therefore isometrically embeddable in some $\mathbb{L}^N$. This property actually characterizes global hyperbolicity: any stably causal but non-globally hyperbolic spacetime admits a conformal metric that is not isometrically embeddable in any $\mathbb{L}^N$.
- **Historical note.** Although Clarke (1970) originally claimed result (2), Müller and Sánchez (2011) demonstrated in their Appendix that Clarke's technique contains a gap. Their approach, fundamentally different, aligns with the solution of the long-standing ["folk problem" regarding Geroch’s splitting](wikipedia://en/34265825#The_"folk"_problem).
