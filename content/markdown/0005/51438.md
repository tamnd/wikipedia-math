# Hypercomplex number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hypercomplex number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **hypercomplex number** is a traditional term for an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) of a finite-dimensional [unital](wikipedia://en/191788#Unital_algebra) [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). The study of hypercomplex numbers in the late 19th century forms the basis of modern [group representation](wikipedia://en/12695) theory.

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Common\_associative\_algebras\_over\_the\_reals.svg.* A [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) of some hypercomplex number systems and the corresponding lattice of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) generated by the basis elements

In the nineteenth century, [number systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_system) called [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), [tessarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessarine), [coquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquaternion), [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070), and [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) became established concepts in mathematical literature, extending the real and [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). The concept of a hypercomplex number covered them all, and called for a discipline to explain and classify them.

The cataloguing project began in 1872 when [Benjamin Peirce](wikipedia://en/364270) first published his *Linear Associative Algebra*, and was carried forward by his son [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117).[^o3] Most significantly, they identified the [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) and the [idempotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent_element_%28ring_theory%29) as useful hypercomplex numbers for classifications. The [Cayley–Dickson construction](wikipedia://en/175609) used [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450) to generate complex numbers, quaternions, and octonions out of the real number system. Hurwitz and Frobenius proved theorems that put limits on hypercomplexity: [Hurwitz's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurwitz's_theorem_%28normed_division_algebras%29) says finite-dimensional real [composition algebras](wikipedia://en/3861635) are the reals $\mathbb{R}$, the complexes $\mathbb{C}$, the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$, and the octonions $\mathbb{O}$, and the [Frobenius theorem](wikipedia://en/3966889) says the only real [associative division algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_division_algebra) are $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{H}$. In 1958 [J. Frank Adams](wikipedia://en/1223959) published a further generalization in terms of Hopf invariants on *H*-spaces that still limits the dimension to 1, 2, 4, or 8.[^o4]

It was [matrix algebra](wikipedia://en/20556859) that harnessed the hypercomplex systems. For instance, $2\times2$ [real matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_matrix) were found [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to [coquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquaternion). Soon the matrix paradigm began to explain several others as they were represented by matrices and their operations. In 1907 [Joseph Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494) showed that associative hypercomplex systems could be represented by [square matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_matrices), or [direct products](wikipedia://en/8887) of algebras of square matrices.[^o5][^o6] From that date the preferred term for a *hypercomplex system* became *[associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112)*, as seen in the title of Wedderburn's thesis at [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh). Note, however, that non-associative systems like octonions and [hyperbolic quaternions](wikipedia://en/1071730) represent another type of hypercomplex number.

As [Thomas Hawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_W._Hawkins_Jr.)[^o7] explains, the hypercomplex numbers are stepping stones to learning about [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) and [group representation](wikipedia://en/12695) theory. For instance, in 1929 [Emmy Noether](wikipedia://en/149896) wrote on "hypercomplex quantities and representation theory".[^o8] In 1973 [Kantor](wikipedia://en/30235234) and Solodovnikov published a textbook on hypercomplex numbers, which was translated in 1989.[^o9][^o10]

[Karen Parshall](wikipedia://en/36594635) has written a detailed exposition of the heyday of hypercomplex numbers,[^o11] including the role of mathematicians including [Theodor Molien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Molien)[^o12] and [Eduard Study](wikipedia://en/663997).[^o13] For the transition to [modern algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), [Bartel van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartel_van_der_Waerden) devotes thirty pages to hypercomplex numbers in his *History of Algebra*.[^o14]

::: {#o16 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A definition of a **hypercomplex number** is given by (Kantor Solodovnikov 1989) as an element of a [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra), but not necessarily [associative](wikipedia://en/1335) or [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390), finite-dimensional algebra over the real numbers. Elements are generated with real number coefficients $(a_0, \dots, a_n)$ for a basis $\{ 1, i_1, \dots, i_n \}$. Where possible, it is conventional to choose the basis so that $i_k^2 \in \{ -1, 0, +1 \}$. A technical approach to hypercomplex numbers directs attention first to those of [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) two.

:::

## Two-dimensional real algebras {#Two-dimensional_real_algebras}

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem:**[^o9][^o20][^o21] Up to isomorphism, there are exactly three 2-dimensional unital algebras over the reals: the ordinary [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559), and the [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169). In particular, every 2-dimensional unital algebra over the reals is associative and commutative.

Proof: Since the algebra is 2-dimensional, we can pick a basis $\{1,u\}$. Since the algebra is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under squaring, the non-real basis element $u$ squares to a linear combination of $1$ and $u$:

$$u^2 = a_0 + a_1 u$$

for some real numbers $a_0$ and $a_1$.

Using the common method of [completing the square](wikipedia://en/303500) by subtracting $a_1 u$ and adding the quadratic complement $a_1^2 / 4$ to both sides yields

$$u^2 - a_1 u + \frac{1}{4}a_1^2 = a_0 + \frac{1}{4}a_1^2.$$

Rewriting the left half of the above equation as $\left(u - \frac{1}{2}a_1\right)^2$ and then substituting in new element $\tilde u$, where $\tilde{u} = u - \tfrac{1}{2}a_1$, one obtains

$$\tilde{u}^2 = a_0 + \frac{1}{4}a_1^2.$$

The three cases depend on this real value:

- If $4a_0=-a_1^2$, the above formula yields $\tilde u^2=0$. Hence, $\tilde u$ can directly be identified with the [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) element $\varepsilon$ of the basis $\{ 1, ~\varepsilon \}$ of the dual numbers.
- If $4a_0>-a_1^2$, the above formula yields $\tilde u^2>0$. This leads to the split-complex numbers, which have normalized basis $\{ 1 , ~j \}$ with $j^2 = +1$. To obtain $j$ from $\tilde u$, the latter must be divided by the positive real number $\textstyle a \mathrel{:=} \sqrt{a_0 + \frac{1}{4}a_1^2}$, which has the same square as $\tilde u$ has.
- If $4a_0<-a_1^2$, the above formula yields $\tilde u^2<0$. This leads to the complex numbers, which have normalized basis $\{ 1 , ~i \}$ with $i^2 = -1$. To yield $i$ from $\tilde u$, the latter has to be divided by the positive real number $\textstyle a \mathrel{:=} \sqrt{-(a_0 + \frac{1}{4}a_1^2)}$, which squares to the negative of $\tilde u^2$.

The complex numbers are the only [quadratic algebra](wikipedia://en/23565253) that is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). [Split algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_algebra) such as the split-complex numbers that include non-real roots of 1 also contain [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent_element) $\frac{1}{2}(1 \pm j)$ and [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) $(1 + j)(1 - j) = 0$, so such algebras cannot be [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625). However, these properties can turn out to be very meaningful, for instance in representing a [light cone](wikipedia://en/310474) with a [null cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_cone).

In a 2004 edition of *[Mathematics Magazine](wikipedia://en/4230329)* the 2-dimensional real algebras have been styled the "generalized complex numbers".[^o22] The idea of [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) of four complex numbers can be extended to the 2-dimensional real algebras.[^o23]

:::

## Higher-dimensional examples (more than one non-real axis) {#Higher-dimensional_examples_(more_than_one_non-real_axis)}

### Clifford algebras {#Clifford_algebras}

A [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) is the unital associative algebra generated over an underlying vector space equipped with a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478). Over the real numbers this is equivalent to being able to define a symmetric scalar product, $u\cdot v=\tfrac12(uv+vu)$ that can be used to [orthogonalise](wikipedia://en/201611) the quadratic form, to give a basis $\{e_1,\,\dots,\,e_k\}$ such that:

$$\frac{1}{2} \left(e_i e_j + e_j e_i\right) = \begin{cases} -1, 0, +1 & i = j, \\ 0 & i \not = j. \end{cases}$$

Imposing closure under multiplication generates a multivector space spanned by a basis of $2^k$ elements, $\{1,\,e_1,\,e_2,\dots,\,e_1e_2,\,\dots,\, e_1e_2e_3,\,\dots\}$. These can be interpreted as the basis of a hypercomplex number system. Unlike the basis $\{e_1,\,\dots,\,e_k\}$, the remaining basis elements need not [anti-commute](wikipedia://en/294358), depending on how many simple exchanges must be carried out to swap the two factors. So $e_1e_2=-e_2e_1$, but $e_1(e_2e_3)=+(e_2e_3)e_1$.

Putting aside the bases which contain an element $e_i$ such that $e_i^2=0$ (i.e. directions in the original space over which the quadratic form was [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form)), the remaining Clifford algebras can be identified by the label $\text{Cl}_{p,q}(\mathbb R)$, indicating that the algebra is constructed from $p$ simple basis elements with $e_i^2=+1$, $q$ with $e_i^2=-1$, and where $\mathbb{R}$ indicates that this is to be a Clifford algebra over the reals—i.e. coefficients of elements of the algebra are to be real numbers.

These algebras, called [geometric algebras](wikipedia://en/12939), form a systematic set, which turn out to be very useful in physics problems that involve [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789), [phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29), or [spins](wikipedia://en/19593829), notably in [classical](wikipedia://en/19555586) and [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), [electromagnetic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_theory) and [relativity](wikipedia://en/30001).

Examples include: the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\text{Cl}_{0,1}(\mathbb R)$, [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) $\text{Cl}_{1,0}(\mathbb R)$, [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) $\text{Cl}_{0,2}(\mathbb R)$, [split-biquaternions](wikipedia://en/4166765) $\text{Cl}_{0,3}(\mathbb R)$, [split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265) $\text{Cl}_{1,1}(\mathbb R)\approx \text{Cl}_{2,0}(\mathbb R)$ (the natural algebra of two-dimensional Euclidean space); $\text{Cl}_{3,0}(\mathbb R)$ (the natural algebra of three-dimensional Euclidean space, and the algebra of the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868)); and the [spacetime algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime_algebra) $\text{Cl}_{1,3}(\mathbb R)$.

The elements of the algebra $\text{Cl}_{p,q}(\mathbb R)$ form an even subalgebra $\text{Cl}_{q+1,p}^{[0]}(\mathbb R)$ of the algebra $\text{Cl}_{q+1,p}(\mathbb R)$, which can be used to parametrise rotations in the larger algebra. There is thus a close connection between complex numbers and rotations in two-dimensional space; between quaternions and rotations in three-dimensional space; between split-complex numbers and (hyperbolic) rotations ([Lorentz transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformations)) in (1+1)-dimensional space, and so on.

Whereas Cayley–Dickson and split-complex constructs with eight or more dimensions are not associative with respect to multiplication, Clifford algebras retain associativity at any number of dimensions.

In 1995 [Ian R. Porteous](wikipedia://en/28112074) wrote on "The recognition of subalgebras" in his book on Clifford algebras. His Proposition 11.4 summarizes the hypercomplex cases:[^o26]

Let $A$ be a real associative algebra with unit element $1$. Then

- 1 generates $\mathbb{R}$ ([algebra of real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)),
- any two-dimensional subalgebra generated by an element $e_0$ of $A$ such that $e_0^2=-1$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}$ ([algebra of complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)),
- any two-dimensional subalgebra generated by an element $e_0$ of $A$ such that $e_0^2=1$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$ (pairs of real numbers with component-wise product, isomorphic to the [algebra of split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559)),
- any four-dimensional subalgebra generated by a set $\{e_0,\,e_1\}$ of mutually anti-commuting elements of $A$ such that $e_0 ^2 = e_1 ^2 = -1$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{H}$ ([algebra of quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440)),
- any four-dimensional subalgebra generated by a set $\{e_0,\,e_1\}$ of mutually anti-commuting elements of $A$ such that $e_0 ^2 = e_1 ^2 = 1$ is isomorphic to $\text{M}_2(\mathbb R)$ ($2\times2$ [real matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_matrices), [coquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquaternion)),
- any eight-dimensional subalgebra generated by a set $\{e_0,\,e_1,\,e_2\}$ of mutually anti-commuting elements of $A$ such that $e_0 ^2 = e_1 ^2 = e_2 ^2 = -1$ is isomorphic to ${}^2\mathbb H$ ([split-biquaternions](wikipedia://en/4166765)),
- any eight-dimensional subalgebra generated by a set $\{e_0,\,e_1,\,e_2\}$ of mutually anti-commuting elements of $A$ such that $e_0 ^2 = e_1 ^2 = e_2 ^2 = 1$ is isomorphic to $\text{M}_2(\mathbb C)$ ($2\times2$ complex matrices, [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070), [Pauli algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_algebra)).

### Cayley–Dickson construction {#Cayley–Dickson_construction}

*Image: Cayley\_Q8\_multiplication\_graph.svg.* Cayley [Q<sub>8</sub>](wikipedia://en/153130) graph of quaternion multiplication showing cycles of multiplication of *i* (red), *j* (green) and *k* (blue). After clicking on the diagram, hover over or click a path to highlight it.

All of the Clifford algebras $\text{Cl}_{p,q}(\mathbb R)$ apart from the real numbers, complex numbers and the quaternions contain non-real elements that square to +1; and so cannot be division algebras. A different approach to extending the complex numbers is taken by the [Cayley–Dickson construction](wikipedia://en/175609). This generates number systems of dimension $2^n,\, n=2,\,3,\,4,\,\dots$, with bases $\left\{1, i_1, \dots, i_{2^n-1}\right\}$, where all the non-real basis elements anti-commute and satisfy ${i_m}^2 = -1$. In 8 or more dimensions ($n\ge3$) these algebras are non-associative. In 16 or more dimensions ($n\ge4$) these algebras also have nonzero [zero-divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-divisor).

The first algebras in this sequence include the 4-dimensional [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), 8-dimensional [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436), and 16-dimensional [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434). An algebraic symmetry is lost with each increase in dimensionality: quaternion multiplication is not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), octonion multiplication is non-[associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), and the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) of [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434) is not multiplicative. After the sedenions are the 32-dimensional [trigintaduonions](wikipedia://en/25474499) (or 32-nions), the 64-dimensional sexagintaquatronions (or 64-nions), the 128-dimensional centumduodetrigintanions (or 128-nions), the 256-dimensional ducentiquinquagintasexions (or 256-nions), and *[ad infinitum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_infinitum)*, as summarized in the table below.[^o28]

|---|---|---|---|
| [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) | 1 | 2<sup>0</sup> | $\mathbb R$ |
| [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) | 2 | 2<sup>1</sup> | $\mathbb C$ |
| [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) | 4 | 2<sup>2</sup> | $\mathbb H$ |
| [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) | 8 | 2<sup>3</sup> | $\mathbb O$ |
| [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434) | 16 | 2<sup>4</sup> | $\mathbb S$ |
| [trigintaduonions](wikipedia://en/25474499) | 32 | 2<sup>5</sup> | $\mathbb T$ |

The Cayley–Dickson construction can be modified by inserting an extra sign at some stages. It then generates the "split algebras" in the collection of [composition algebras](wikipedia://en/3861635) instead of the division algebras:

[split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) with basis $\{ 1,\, i_1 \}$ satisfying $i_1^2 = +1$,

[split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265) with basis $\{ 1,\, i_1,\, i_2,\, i_3 \}$ satisfying $i_1^2 = -1,\, i_2^2 = i_3^2 = +1$, and

[split-octonions](wikipedia://en/3861649) with basis $\{ 1,\, i_1,\, \dots,\, i_7 \}$ satisfying $i_1^2 = i_2^2 = i_3^2 = -1$, $i_4^2 = i_5^2 = i_6^2 = i_7^2 = +1$.

Unlike the complex numbers, the split-complex numbers are not [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018), and further contain nontrivial [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) and nontrivial [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent). As with the quaternions, split-quaternions are not commutative, but further contain [nilpotents](wikipedia://en/252235); they are isomorphic to the [square matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_matrices) of dimension two. Split-octonions are non-associative and contain nilpotents.

### Tensor products {#Tensor_products}

The [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of any two algebras is another algebra, which can be used to produce many more examples of hypercomplex number systems.

In particular taking tensor products with the complex numbers (considered as algebras over the reals) leads to four-dimensional [bicomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/4802961) $\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{R} \mathbb{C}$ (isomorphic to tessarines $\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{R} D$), eight-dimensional [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070) $\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{R} \mathbb{H}$, and 16-dimensional [complex octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) $\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{R} \mathbb{O}$.

Another example is the tensor product of two quaternion algebras $\mathbb{H}^{\otimes 2}=\mathbb{H}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}$ (isomorphic to the Clifford algebra $\text{Cl}_{3,1}(\mathbb R)$ and to $4\times 4$ real matrices $M(4,\mathbb{R})$) leading to applications in relativistic physics.[^o37][^o38]

More generally, one defines $\mathbb{H}^{\otimes m}$ (and its subalgebras) as "hyperquaternion algebras". In particular, $\mathbb{H}^{\otimes 3}=M(4,\mathbb{H})$ yields a [quaternionic matrix](wikipedia://en/25826595) and its even subalgebra $\mathbb{H}^{\otimes 2} \otimes _\mathbb{R}\mathbb{C}$ ([Dirac algebra](wikipedia://en/10290343)).[^o39][^o40]

### Further examples {#Further_examples}

- [bicomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/4802961): a 4-dimensional vector space over the reals, 2-dimensional over the complex numbers, isomorphic to tessarines.
