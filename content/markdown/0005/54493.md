# Kuratowski's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kuratowski's theorem"}

*Image: GP92-Kuratowski.svg.* A subdivision of *K*<sub>3,3</sub> in the [generalized Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/23716451) *G*(9,2), showing that the graph is nonplanar.

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), **Kuratowski's theorem** is a mathematical [forbidden graph characterization](wikipedia://en/13547826) of [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), named after [Kazimierz Kuratowski](wikipedia://en/46075). It states that a finite graph is planar if and only if it does not contain a [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraph) that is a [subdivision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subdivision_%28graph_theory%29) of $K_5$ (the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on five [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899)) nor of $K_{3,3}$ (a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) on six vertices, three of which connect to each of the other three, also known as the [utility graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_graph)).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

A [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is a graph whose vertices can be represented by points in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), and whose edges can be represented by [simple curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_curve) in the same plane connecting the points representing their endpoints, such that no two curves intersect except at a common endpoint. Planar graphs are often [drawn](wikipedia://en/325813) with straight [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) representing their edges, but by [Fáry's theorem](wikipedia://en/6805386) allowing curved edges or requiring straight edges makes no difference to their graph-theoretic characterization.

A [subdivision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subdivision_%28graph_theory%29) of a graph is a graph formed by subdividing its edges into [paths](wikipedia://en/638889) of one or more edges. Kuratowski's theorem states that a finite graph $G$ is planar if it is not possible to subdivide the edges of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$, and then possibly add additional edges and vertices, to form a graph [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to $G$. Equivalently, a finite graph is planar if and only if it does not contain a subgraph that is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/353021) to $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$.

:::

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Kuratowski subgraph"}

## Kuratowski subgraphs {#Kuratowski_subgraphs}

If $G$ is a graph that contains a subgraph $H$ that is a subdivision of $K_5$ or $K_{3,3}$, then $H$ is known as a **Kuratowski subgraph** of $G$.[^o6] With this notation, Kuratowski's theorem can be expressed succinctly: a graph is planar if and only if it does not have a Kuratowski subgraph.

The two graphs $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$ are nonplanar, as may be shown either by a [case analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_cases) or an argument involving [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/148420). Additionally, subdividing a graph cannot turn a nonplanar graph into a planar graph: if a subdivision of a graph $G$ has a planar drawing, the paths of the subdivision form curves that may be used to represent the edges of $G$ itself. Therefore, a graph that contains a Kuratowski subgraph cannot be planar. The more difficult direction in proving Kuratowski's theorem is to show that, if a graph is nonplanar, it must contain a Kuratowski subgraph.

:::

## Algorithmic implications {#Algorithmic_implications}

A Kuratowski subgraph of a nonplanar graph can be found in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time), as measured by the size of the input graph.[^o8] This allows the correctness of a [planarity testing](wikipedia://en/15972636) algorithm to be verified for nonplanar inputs, as it is straightforward to test whether a given subgraph is or is not a Kuratowski subgraph.[^o9] Usually, non-planar graphs contain a large number of Kuratowski-subgraphs. The extraction of these subgraphs is needed, e.g., in [branch and cut](wikipedia://en/3237914) algorithms for crossing minimization. It is possible to extract a large number of Kuratowski subgraphs in time dependent on their total size.[^o10]

## History {#History}

[Kazimierz Kuratowski](wikipedia://en/46075) published his theorem in 1930.[^o13] The theorem was independently proved by [Orrin Frink](wikipedia://en/38498178) and [Paul Smith](wikipedia://en/4476957), also in 1930,[^o14] but their proof was never published. The special case of [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508) planar graphs (for which the only minimal forbidden subgraph is $K_{3,3}$) was also independently proved by [Karl Menger](wikipedia://en/149431) in 1930.[^o15] Since then, several new proofs of the theorem have been discovered.[^o16]

In the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union), Kuratowski's theorem was known as either the **Pontryagin–Kuratowski theorem** or the **Kuratowski–Pontryagin theorem**,[^o17] as the theorem was reportedly proved independently by [Lev Pontryagin](wikipedia://en/582075) around 1927.[^o18] However, as Pontryagin never published his proof, this usage has not spread to other places.[^o19]

:::

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Related results"}

## Related results {#Related_results}

A closely related result, [Wagner's theorem](wikipedia://en/3126130), characterizes the planar graphs by their [minors](wikipedia://en/353042) in terms of the same two forbidden graphs $K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$. Every Kuratowski subgraph is a special case of a minor of the same type, and while the reverse is not true, it is not difficult to find a Kuratowski subgraph (of one type or the other) from one of these two forbidden minors; therefore, these two theorems are equivalent.[^o22]

An extension is the [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769), stating that every class of graphs closed under taking minors (as the planar graphs are) can be characterized in an analogous way by a finite set of forbidden minors.

:::
