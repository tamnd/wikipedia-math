# Non-Euclidean geometry

*Image: noneuclid.svg.* Behavior of lines with a common perpendicular in each of the three types of geometry

branches

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-Euclidean geometry"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **non-Euclidean geometry** consists of two geometries based on [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) closely related to those that specify [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417). As Euclidean geometry lies at the intersection of [metric geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_geometry) and [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890), non-Euclidean geometry arises by either replacing the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) with an alternative, or consideration of [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) other than the [definite quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/20437320) associated with [metric geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_geometry). In the former case, one obtains [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) and [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020), the traditional non-Euclidean geometries. When [isotropic quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/10917170) are admitted, then there are affine planes associated with the planar algebras, which give rise to kinematic geometries that have also been called non-Euclidean geometry.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=named-heading name="Principles"}

## Principles {#Principles}

The essential difference between the metric geometries is the nature of [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) lines. [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s fifth postulate, the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493), is equivalent to [Playfair's postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playfair's_Postulate), which states that, within a two-dimensional plane, for any given line $l$ and a point *p*, which is not on $l$, there is exactly one line through *p* that does not intersect $l$. In hyperbolic geometry, by contrast, there are [infinitely](wikipedia://en/142679) many lines through *p* not intersecting $l$, while in elliptic geometry, any line through *p* intersects $l$.

Another way to describe the differences between these geometries is to consider two straight lines indefinitely extended in a two-dimensional plane that are both [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to a third line (in the same plane):

- In Euclidean geometry, the lines remain at a constant [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from each other (meaning that a line drawn perpendicular to one line at any point will intersect the other line and the length of the line segment joining the points of intersection remains constant) and are known as parallels.
- In hyperbolic geometry, they diverge from each other, increasing in distance as one moves further from the points of intersection with the common perpendicular; these lines are often called [ultraparallels](wikipedia://en/241291#Non-intersecting_/_parallel_lines).
- In elliptic geometry, the lines converge toward each other and intersect.

:::

## History {#History}

### Background {#Background}

[Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), named after the [Greek mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331), includes some of the oldest known mathematics, and geometries that deviated from this were not widely accepted as legitimate until the 19th century.

The debate that eventually led to the discovery of the non-Euclidean geometries began almost as soon as Euclid wrote *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*. In the *Elements*, Euclid begins with a limited number of assumptions (23 definitions, five common notions, and five postulates) and seeks to prove all the other results ([propositions](wikipedia://en/81094)) in the work. The most notorious of the postulates is often referred to as "Euclid's Fifth Postulate", or simply the *[parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493)*, which in Euclid's original formulation is:

> If a straight line falls on two straight lines in such a manner that the interior angles on the same side are together less than two right angles, then the straight lines, if produced indefinitely, meet on that side on which are the angles less than the two right angles.

Other mathematicians have devised simpler forms of this property. Regardless of the form of the postulate, however, it consistently appears more complicated than [Euclid's other postulates](wikipedia://en/9417#Axioms):

1. To draw a straight line from any point to any point.
1. To produce \[extend\] a finite straight line continuously in a straight line.
1. To describe a circle with any centre and distance \[radius\].
1. That all right angles are equal to one another.

For at least a thousand years, [geometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometer) were troubled by the disparate complexity of the fifth postulate, and believed it could be proved as a theorem from the other four. Many attempted to find a [proof by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction), including [Ibn al-Haytham](wikipedia://en/1645) (Alhazen, 11th century),[^o6] [Omar Khayyám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayyám) (12th century), [Nasīr al-Dīn al-Tūsī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasīr_al-Dīn_al-Tūsī) (13th century), and [Giovanni Girolamo Saccheri](wikipedia://en/30875270) (18th century).

The theorems of Ibn al-Haytham, Khayyam and al-Tusi on [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278), including the [Lambert quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3515519) and [Saccheri quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3818958), were "the first few theorems of the [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291) and the [elliptic geometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_geometry)". These theorems along with their alternative postulates, such as [Playfair's axiom](wikipedia://en/33731592), played an important role in the later development of non-Euclidean geometry. These early attempts at challenging the fifth postulate had a considerable influence on its development among later European geometers, including [Witelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witelo), [Levi ben Gerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_ben_Gerson), [Alfonso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abner_of_Burgos), [John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290) and Saccheri.[^o7] All of these early attempts made at trying to formulate non-Euclidean geometry, however, provided flawed proofs of the parallel postulate, depending on assumptions that are now recognized as essentially equivalent to the parallel postulate. These early attempts did, however, provide some early properties of the hyperbolic and elliptic geometries.

Khayyam, for example, tried to derive it from an equivalent postulate he formulated from "the principles of the Philosopher" ([Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)): "Two convergent straight lines intersect and it is impossible for two convergent straight lines to diverge in the direction in which they converge."[^o8] Khayyam then considered the three cases right, obtuse, and acute that the summit angles of a Saccheri quadrilateral can take and after proving a number of theorems about them, he correctly refuted the obtuse and acute cases based on his postulate and hence derived the classic postulate of Euclid, which he didn't realize was equivalent to his own postulate. Another example is al-Tusi's son, Sadr al-Din (sometimes known as "Pseudo-Tusi"), who wrote a book on the subject in 1298, based on al-Tusi's later thoughts, which presented another hypothesis equivalent to the parallel postulate. "He essentially revised both the Euclidean system of axioms and postulates and the proofs of many propositions from the *Elements*."[^o10][^o9] His work was published in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome) in 1594 and was studied by European geometers, including Saccheri[^o10] who criticised this work as well as that of Wallis.

[Giordano Vitale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Vitale), in his book *Euclide restituo* (1680, 1686), used the Saccheri quadrilateral to prove that if three points are equidistant on the base AB and the summit CD, then AB and CD are everywhere equidistant.

In a work titled *Euclides ab Omni Naevo Vindicatus* (*Euclid Freed from All Flaws*), published in 1733, Saccheri quickly discarded elliptic geometry as a possibility (some others of Euclid's axioms must be modified for elliptic geometry to work) and set to work proving a great number of results in hyperbolic geometry.

He finally reached a point where he believed that his results demonstrated the impossibility of hyperbolic geometry. His claim seems to have been based on Euclidean presuppositions, because no *logical* contradiction was present. In this attempt to prove Euclidean geometry he instead unintentionally discovered a new viable geometry, but did not realize it.

In 1766 [Johann Lambert](wikipedia://en/93085) wrote, but did not publish, *Theorie der Parallellinien* in which he attempted, as Saccheri did, to prove the fifth postulate. He worked with a figure now known as a *[Lambert quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3515519)*, a quadrilateral with three right angles (can be considered half of a [Saccheri quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3818958)). He quickly eliminated the possibility that the fourth angle is obtuse, as had Saccheri and Khayyam, and then proceeded to prove many theorems under the assumption of an acute angle. Unlike Saccheri, he never felt that he had reached a contradiction with this assumption. He had proved the non-Euclidean result that the sum of the angles in a triangle increases as the area of the triangle decreases, and this led him to speculate on the possibility of a model of the acute case on a sphere of imaginary radius. He did not carry this idea any further.

At this time it was widely believed that the universe worked according to the principles of Euclidean geometry.[^o13]

### Development of non-Euclidean geometry {#Development_of_non-Euclidean_geometry}

The beginning of the 19th century would finally witness decisive steps in the creation of non-Euclidean geometry. Circa 1813, [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) and independently around 1818, the German professor of law [Ferdinand Karl Schweikart](wikipedia://en/58742625)[^o15] had the germinal ideas of non-Euclidean geometry worked out. Schweikart published his results in his book of 1807 [^o16]. According to George Bruce Halsted [^o17], "Schweikart may be considered the first to publish a genuine conscious treatise on Non-Euclidean Geometry. This fixes the date of the first conscious creation and naming of the Non-Euclidean Geometry as between 1812 and 1816." Schweikart's nephew [Franz Taurinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Taurinus) did publish important results of hyperbolic trigonometry in two papers in 1825 and 1826, yet while admitting the internal consistency of hyperbolic geometry, he still believed in the special role of Euclidean geometry.[^o18]

Then, in 1829–1830 the [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) mathematician [Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Ivanovich_Lobachevsky) and in 1832 the [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary) mathematician [János Bolyai](wikipedia://en/98912) separately and independently published treatises on hyperbolic geometry. Consequently, hyperbolic geometry is called Lobachevskian or Bolyai-Lobachevskian geometry, as both mathematicians, independent of each other, are the basic authors of non-Euclidean geometry. [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) mentioned to Bolyai's father, when shown the younger Bolyai's work, that he had developed such a geometry several years before,[^o19] though he did not publish. While Lobachevsky created a non-Euclidean geometry by negating the parallel postulate, Bolyai worked out a geometry where both the Euclidean and the hyperbolic geometry are possible depending on a parameter *k*. Bolyai ends his work by mentioning that it is not possible to decide through mathematical reasoning alone if the geometry of the physical universe is Euclidean or non-Euclidean; this is a task for the physical sciences.

[Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980), in a famous lecture in 1854, founded the field of [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), discussing in particular the ideas now called [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric), and [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770). He constructed an infinite family of non-Euclidean geometries by giving a formula for a family of Riemannian metrics on the unit ball in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). The simplest of these is called [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020) and it is considered a non-Euclidean geometry due to its lack of parallel lines.[^o20]

By formulating the geometry in terms of a curvature [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965), Riemann allowed non-Euclidean geometry to apply to higher dimensions. Beltrami (1868) was the first to apply Riemann's geometry to spaces of negative curvature.

### Terminology {#Terminology}

It was Gauss who coined the term "non-Euclidean geometry".[^o23] He was referring to his own work, which today we call *hyperbolic geometry* or *Lobachevskian geometry*. Several modern authors still use the generic term *non-Euclidean geometry* to mean *hyperbolic geometry*.[^o24]

[Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) noted that distance between points inside a conic could be defined in terms of [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) and the projective [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) function. The method has become called the [Cayley–Klein metric](wikipedia://en/30794512) because [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) exploited it to describe the non-Euclidean geometries in articles[^o25] in 1871 and 1873 and later in book form. The Cayley–Klein metrics provided working models of hyperbolic and elliptic metric geometries, as well as Euclidean geometry.

Klein is responsible for the terms "hyperbolic" and "elliptic" (in his system he called Euclidean geometry *parabolic*, a term that generally fell out of use[^o26]). His influence has led to the current usage of the term "non-Euclidean geometry" to mean either "hyperbolic" or "elliptic" geometry.

There are some mathematicians who would extend the list of geometries that should be called "non-Euclidean" in various ways.[^o27]

There are many kinds of geometry that are quite different from Euclidean geometry but are also not necessarily included in the conventional meaning of "non-Euclidean geometry", such as more general instances of [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243).

:::

## Axiomatic basis of non-Euclidean geometry {#Axiomatic_basis_of_non-Euclidean_geometry}

Euclidean geometry can be axiomatically described in several ways. However, Euclid's original system of five postulates (axioms) is not one of these, as his proofs relied on several unstated assumptions that should also have been taken as axioms. [Hilbert's system](wikipedia://en/838151) consisting of 20 axioms[^o29] most closely follows the approach of Euclid and provides the justification for all of Euclid's proofs. Other systems, using different sets of [undefined terms](wikipedia://en/23706) obtain the same geometry by different paths. All approaches, however, have an axiom that is logically equivalent to Euclid's fifth postulate, the parallel postulate. [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) uses the Playfair axiom form, while [Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916), for instance, uses the axiom that says that, "There exists a pair of similar but not congruent triangles." In any of these systems, removal of the one axiom equivalent to the parallel postulate, in whatever form it takes, and leaving all the other axioms intact, produces [absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294). As the first 28 propositions of Euclid (in *The Elements*) do not require the use of the parallel postulate or anything equivalent to it, they are all true statements in absolute geometry.[^o30]

To obtain a non-Euclidean geometry, the parallel postulate (or its equivalent) *must* be replaced by its [negation](wikipedia://en/161019). Negating the [Playfair's axiom](wikipedia://en/33731592) form, since it is a compound statement (... there exists one and only one ...), can be done in two ways: either there will exist more than one line through the point parallel to the given line or there will exist no lines through the point parallel to the given line.

- In the first case, replacing the parallel postulate (or its equivalent) with the statement "In a plane, given a point P and a line $l$ not passing through P, there exist two lines through P, which do not meet $l$" and keeping all the other axioms, yields [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).[^o31]
- The second case is not dealt with as easily. Simply replacing the parallel postulate with the statement, "In a plane, given a point P and a line $l$ not passing through P, all the lines through P meet $l$", does not give a consistent set of axioms. This follows since parallel lines exist in absolute geometry,[^o32] but this statement says that there are no parallel lines. This problem was known (in a different guise) to Khayyam, Saccheri and Lambert and was the basis for their rejecting what was known as the "obtuse angle case". To obtain a consistent set of axioms that includes this axiom about having no parallel lines, some other axioms must be tweaked. These adjustments depend upon the axiom system used. Among others, these tweaks have the effect of modifying Euclid's second postulate from the statement that line segments can be extended indefinitely to the statement that lines are unbounded. [Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann)'s [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020) emerges as the most natural geometry satisfying this axiom.

## Models {#Models}

*Image: Triangles (spherical geometry).jpg.* On a sphere, the sum of the angles of a triangle is not equal to 180°. The surface of a sphere is not a Euclidean space, but locally the laws of the Euclidean geometry are good approximations. In a small triangle on the face of the earth, the sum of the angles is very near 180°.

**Models of non-Euclidean geometry** are [mathematical models](wikipedia://en/20590) of geometries that are non-Euclidean in the sense that it is not the case that exactly one line can be drawn [parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines) to a given line *l* through a point *A* that is not on *l*. In hyperbolic geometric models, by contrast, there are [infinitely](wikipedia://en/20110824) many lines through *A* parallel to *l*, and in elliptic geometric models, parallel lines do not exist. (See the entries on [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) and [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020) for more information.)

Euclidean geometry is modelled by our notion of a "flat [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852)." The simplest model for elliptic geometry is a sphere, where lines are "[great circles](wikipedia://en/48082)" (such as the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) or the [meridians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) on a [globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe)), and points opposite each other are identified (considered to be the same). The [pseudosphere](wikipedia://en/60766) has the appropriate [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) to model hyperbolic geometry.

### Elliptic geometry {#Elliptic_geometry}

The simplest model for [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020) is a sphere, where lines are "[great circles](wikipedia://en/48082)" (such as the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) or the [meridians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) on a [globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe)), and points opposite each other (called [antipodal points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodal_points)) are identified (considered the same). This is also one of the standard models of the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325). The difference is that as a model of elliptic geometry a metric is introduced permitting the measurement of lengths and angles, while as a model of the projective plane there is no such metric.

In the elliptic model, for any given line $l$ and a point *A*, which is not on $l$, all lines through *A* will intersect $l$.

### Hyperbolic geometry {#Hyperbolic_geometry}

Even after the work of Lobachevsky, Gauss, and Bolyai, the question remained: "Does such a model exist for [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291)?". The model for [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) was answered by [Eugenio Beltrami](wikipedia://en/543760), in 1868, who first showed that a surface called the [pseudosphere](wikipedia://en/60766) has the appropriate [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) to model a portion of [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) and in a second paper in the same year, defined the [Klein model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein_model), which models the entirety of hyperbolic space, and used this to show that Euclidean geometry and hyperbolic geometry were [equiconsistent](wikipedia://en/3435716) so that hyperbolic geometry was [logically consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logically_consistent) if and only if Euclidean geometry was. (The reverse implication follows from the [horosphere](wikipedia://en/13487128) model of Euclidean geometry.)

In the hyperbolic model, within a two-dimensional plane, for any given line $l$ and a point *A*, which is not on $l$, there are [infinitely](wikipedia://en/142679) many lines through *A* that do not intersect $l$.

In these models, the concepts of non-Euclidean geometries are represented by Euclidean objects in a Euclidean setting. This introduces a perceptual distortion wherein the straight lines of the non-Euclidean geometry are represented by Euclidean curves that visually bend. This "bending" is not a property of the non-Euclidean lines, only an artifice of the way they are represented.

### Three-dimensional non-Euclidean geometry {#Three-dimensional_non-Euclidean_geometry}

In three dimensions, there are eight models of geometries.[^o38] There are Euclidean, elliptic, and hyperbolic geometries, as in the two-dimensional case; mixed geometries that are partially Euclidean and partially hyperbolic or spherical; twisted versions of the mixed geometries; and one unusual geometry that is completely [anisotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropy) (i.e. every direction behaves differently).

:::

## Uncommon properties {#Uncommon_properties}

*Image: Lambert quadrilateral.svg.* Lambert quadrilateral in hyperbolic geometry

*Image: Saccheri quads.svg.* Saccheri quadrilaterals in the three geometries

Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries naturally have many similar properties, namely those that do not depend upon the nature of parallelism. This commonality is the subject of [absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294) (also called *neutral geometry*). However, the properties that distinguish one geometry from others have historically received the most attention.

Besides the behavior of lines with respect to a common perpendicular, mentioned in the introduction, we also have the following:

- A [Lambert quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3515519) is a quadrilateral with three right angles. The fourth angle of a Lambert quadrilateral is [acute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_angle) if the geometry is hyperbolic, a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956) if the geometry is Euclidean or [obtuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_angle) if the geometry is elliptic. Consequently, [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048) exist (a statement equivalent to the parallel postulate) only in Euclidean geometry.
- A [Saccheri quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3818958) is a quadrilateral with two sides of equal length, both perpendicular to a side called the *base*. The other two angles of a Saccheri quadrilateral are called the *summit angles* and they have equal measure. The summit angles of a Saccheri quadrilateral are acute if the geometry is hyperbolic, right angles if the geometry is Euclidean and obtuse angles if the geometry is elliptic.
- The sum of the measures of the angles of any triangle is less than 180° if the geometry is hyperbolic, equal to 180° if the geometry is Euclidean, and greater than 180° if the geometry is elliptic. The *defect* of a triangle is the numerical value (180° − the sum of the measures of the angles of the triangle). This result may also be stated as: the defect of triangles in hyperbolic geometry is positive, the defect of triangles in Euclidean geometry is zero, and the defect of triangles in elliptic geometry is negative.

## Importance {#Importance}

Before the models of a non-Euclidean plane were presented by Beltrami, Klein, and Poincaré, Euclidean geometry stood unchallenged as the [mathematical model](wikipedia://en/20590) of [space](wikipedia://en/27667). Furthermore, since the substance of the subject in [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484) was a chief exhibit of rationality, the Euclidean point of view represented absolute authority.

The discovery of the non-Euclidean geometries had a ripple effect which went far beyond the boundaries of mathematics and science. The philosopher [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)'s treatment of human knowledge had a special role for geometry. It was his prime example of synthetic [a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori) knowledge; not derived from the senses nor deduced through logic—our knowledge of space was a truth that we were born with. Unfortunately for Kant, his concept of this unalterably true geometry was Euclidean. Theology was also affected by the change from absolute truth to relative truth in the way that mathematics is related to the world around it, that was a result of this paradigm shift.[^o41]

Non-Euclidean geometry is an example of a [scientific revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm_shift) in the [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science), in which mathematicians and scientists changed the way they viewed their subjects.[^o42] Some geometers called [Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/648527) the "[Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus) of Geometry" due to the revolutionary character of his work.[^o43][^o44]

The existence of non-Euclidean geometries impacted the intellectual life of [Victorian England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_England) in many ways[^o45] and in particular was one of the leading factors that caused a re-examination of the teaching of geometry based on [Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107). This curriculum issue was hotly debated at the time and was even the subject of a book, *[Euclid and his Modern Rivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid_and_his_Modern_Rivals)*, written by Charles Lutwidge Dodgson (1832–1898) better known as [Lewis Carroll](wikipedia://en/26586083), the author of *[Alice in Wonderland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice's_Adventures_in_Wonderland)*.

## Planar algebras {#Planar_algebras}

In [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) is described with [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate):

$$C = \{ (x,y) : x,y \in \mathbb{R} \}$$

The [points](wikipedia://en/593693) are sometimes identified with generalized complex numbers $z = x + y \varepsilon$ where ε<sup>2</sup> ∈ $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

The Euclidean plane corresponds to the case $\varepsilon^{2} = -1$, an [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358). Since the modulus of $z$ is given by

$z z^\ast = (x + y \epsilon) (x - y \epsilon) = x^2 + y^2 ,$ this quantity is the square of the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between $z$ and the origin.

For instance, $\{z | z z* = 1\}$ is the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).

For planar algebra, non-Euclidean geometry arises in the other cases. When $\varepsilon^{2} = +1$, a [hyperbolic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_unit). Then $z$ is a [split-complex number](wikipedia://en/893559) and conventionally $\mathbf{j}$ replaces epsilon. Then

$$z z^\ast = (x + y\mathbf{j}) (x - y\mathbf{j}) = x^2 - y^2 \!$$

and $\{z | z z* = 1\}$ is the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122).

When $\varepsilon^{2} = 0$, then $z$ is a [dual number](wikipedia://en/42169).[^o47]

This approach to non-Euclidean geometry explains the non-Euclidean angles: the parameters of [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) in the dual number plane and [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) in the split-complex plane correspond to [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) in Euclidean geometry. Indeed, they each arise in [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241#Alternative_planar_decompositions) of a complex number $z$.[^o48]

## Kinematic geometries {#Kinematic_geometries}

Hyperbolic geometry found an application in [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914) with the [physical cosmology](wikipedia://en/5378) introduced by [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) in 1908. Minkowski introduced terms like [worldline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldline) and [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) into [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416). He realized that the [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095), of events one moment of proper time into the future, could be considered a [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) of three dimensions.[^o51][^o52] Already in the 1890s [Alexander Macfarlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Macfarlane) was charting this submanifold through his *Algebra of Physics* and [hyperbolic quaternions](wikipedia://en/1071730), though Macfarlane did not use cosmological language as Minkowski did in 1908. The relevant structure is now called the [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189) of hyperbolic geometry.

The non-Euclidean planar algebras support kinematic geometries in the plane. For instance, the [split-complex number](wikipedia://en/893559) *z* = e<sup>*a*j</sup> can represent a spacetime event one moment into the future of a [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference) of [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673) *a*. Furthermore, multiplication by *z* amounts to a [Lorentz boost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_boost) mapping the frame with rapidity zero to that with rapidity *a*.

Kinematic study makes use of the [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169) $z = x + y \epsilon, \quad \epsilon^2 = 0,$ to represent the classical description of motion in [absolute time and space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_time_and_space): The equations $x^\prime = x + vt,\quad t^\prime = t$ are equivalent to a [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405) in linear algebra:$\begin{pmatrix}x' \\ t' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & v \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x \\ t \end{pmatrix}.$

With dual numbers the mapping is $t^\prime + x^\prime \epsilon = (1 + v \epsilon)(t + x \epsilon) = t + (x + vt)\epsilon.$[^o53]

Another view of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) as a non-Euclidean geometry was advanced by [E. B. Wilson](wikipedia://en/11692574) and [Gilbert Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis) in *Proceedings of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences)* in 1912. They revamped the analytic geometry implicit in the split-complex number algebra into [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484) of premises and deductions.[^o54][^o55]

## Fiction {#Fiction}

Non-Euclidean geometry often makes appearances in works of [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) and [fantasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fantasy).

- In 1895, [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells) published the short story "The Remarkable Case of Davidson's Eyes". To appreciate this story one should know how [antipodal points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodal_points) on a sphere are identified in a model of the elliptic plane. In the story, in the midst of a thunderstorm, Sidney Davidson sees "Waves and a remarkably neat schooner" while working in an electrical laboratory at Harlow Technical College. At the story's close, Davidson proves to have witnessed H.M.S. *Fulmar* off [Antipodes Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipodes_Island).
- Non-Euclidean geometry is sometimes connected with the influence of the 20th-century [horror fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horror_fiction) writer [H. P. Lovecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft). In his works, many unnatural things follow their own unique laws of geometry: in Lovecraft's [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos), the sunken city of [R'lyeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R'lyeh) is characterized by its non-Euclidean geometry. It is heavily implied this is achieved as a side effect of not following the natural laws of this universe rather than simply using an alternate geometric model, as the sheer innate wrongness of it is said to be capable of driving those who look upon it insane.[^o57]
- The main character in [Robert Pirsig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Pirsig)'s *[Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen_and_the_Art_of_Motorcycle_Maintenance)* mentioned [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243) on multiple occasions.
- In *[The Brothers Karamazov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brothers_Karamazov)*, Dostoevsky discusses non-Euclidean geometry through his character Ivan.
- Christopher Priest's novel *[Inverted World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverted_World)* describes the struggle of living on a planet with the form of a rotating [pseudosphere](wikipedia://en/60766).
- Robert Heinlein's *[The Number of the Beast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Number_of_the_Beast_%28novel%29)* utilizes non-Euclidean geometry to explain instantaneous transport through space and time and between parallel and fictional universes.
- Zeno Rogue's *[HyperRogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HyperRogue)* is a [roguelike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roguelike) game set on the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane), allowing the player to experience many properties of this geometry. Many mechanics, quests, and locations are strongly dependent on the features of hyperbolic geometry.[^o58]
- In the [Renegade Legion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renegade_Legion) [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) setting for [FASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FASA)'s [wargame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wargame_%28video_games%29), [role-playing-game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-playing-game) and fiction, [faster-than-light travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faster-than-light_travel) and communications is possible through the use of Hsieh Ho's Polydimensional Non-Euclidean Geometry, published sometime in the middle of the 22nd century.
- In [Ian Stewart's](wikipedia://en/332183) *[Flatterland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatterland)*, the [protagonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protagonist) Victoria Line visits all kinds of non-Euclidean worlds.
