# Minkowski's theorem

*Image: MinkowskischerGitterpunktsatz.png.* An [elliptical](wikipedia://en/9277) set in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ illustrating Minkowski's theorem. With sufficient size, it must contain other integer points than zero.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Minkowski's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Minkowski's theorem** is the statement that every [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) in $\mathbb{R}^n$ which is symmetric with respect to the origin and which has [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) greater than $2^n$ contains a non-zero [integer point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_point) (meaning a point in $\mathbb{Z}^n$ that is not the origin). The theorem was [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) in 1889 and became the foundation of the branch of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) called the [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153). It can be extended from the integers to any [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) $L$ and to any symmetric convex set with volume greater than $2^nd(L)$, where $d(L)$ denotes the [covolume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covolume) of the lattice (the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of any of its bases).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Suppose that $L$ is a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) of [determinant](wikipedia://en/469246#Dividing_space_according_to_a_lattice) $d(L)$ in the $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $S$ is a [convex subset](wikipedia://en/6292) of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that is symmetric with respect to the origin, meaning that if $x$ is in $S$ then $-x$ is also in $S$. Minkowski's theorem states that if the volume of $S$ is strictly greater than $2^{n} d(L)$, then $S$ must contain at least one lattice point other than the origin. (Since the set $S$ is symmetric, it would then contain at least three lattice points: the origin 0 and a pair of points $\pm\,x$, where $x \in L \backslash 0$.)

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The simplest example of a lattice is the [integer lattice](wikipedia://en/3333402) $\mathbb{Z}^n$ of all points with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) coefficients; its determinant is 1. For $n = 2$, the theorem claims that a convex figure in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) symmetric about the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) and with [area](wikipedia://en/1209) greater than 4 encloses at least one lattice point in addition to the origin. The area bound is [sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_jargon#sharp): if $S$ is the interior of the square with vertices $(\pm1, \pm1)$ then $S$ is symmetric and convex, and has area 4, but the only lattice point it contains is the origin. This example, showing that the bound of the theorem is sharp, generalizes to [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783) in every dimension $n$.

:::

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The following argument proves Minkowski's theorem for the specific case of $L = \mathbb{Z}^2.$

::: {#o8 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof of the \\mathbb{Z}^2 case"}

**Proof of the $\mathbb{Z}^2$ case:** Consider the map

$$f: S \to \mathbb{R}^2/2L, \qquad (x,y) \mapsto (x \bmod 2, y \bmod 2)$$

Intuitively, this map cuts the plane into 2 by 2 squares, then stacks the squares on top of each other. Clearly $f\,(S)$ has area less than or equal to 4, because this set lies within a 2 by 2 square. Assume for a [contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction) that $f$ could be [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), which means the pieces of $S$ cut out by the squares stack up in a non-overlapping way. Because $f$ is locally area-preserving, this non-overlapping property would make it area-preserving for all of $S$, so the area of $f\,(S)$ would be the same as that of $S$, which is greater than 4. That is not the case, so the assumption must be false: $f$ is not injective, meaning that there exist at least two distinct points $p_{1}, p_{2}$ in $S$ that are mapped by $f$ to the same point: $f\,(p_{1}) = f\,(p_{2})$.

Because of the way $f$ was defined, the only way that $f\,(p_{1})$ can equal $f\,(p_{2})$ is for $p_{2}$ to equal $p_{1} + (2i, 2j)$ for some integers $i$ and $j$, not both zero. That is, the coordinates of the two points differ by two [even](wikipedia://en/143135) integers. Since $S$ is symmetric about the origin, $-p_{1}$ is also a point in $S$. Since $S$ is convex, the line segment between $-p_{1}$ and $p_{2}$ lies entirely in $S$, and in particular the midpoint of that segment lies in $S$. In other words,

$$\tfrac{1}{2}\left(-p_1 + p_2\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(-p_1 + p_1 + (2i, 2j)\right) = (i, j)$$

is a point in $S$. This point $(i, j)$ is an integer point, and is not the origin since $i$ and $j$ are not both zero. Therefore, $S$ contains a nonzero integer point.

**Remarks:**

- The argument above proves the theorem that any set of volume $>\!\det(L)$ contains two distinct points that differ by a lattice vector. This is a special case of [Blichfeldt's theorem](wikipedia://en/66127387).[^o9]
- The argument above highlights that the term $2^n \det(L)$ is the covolume of the lattice $2L$.
- To obtain a proof for general lattices, it suffices to prove Minkowski's theorem only for $\mathbb{Z}^n$; this is because every full-rank lattice can be written as $B\mathbb{Z}^n$ for some [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) $B$, and the properties of being convex and symmetric about the origin are preserved by linear transformations, while the covolume of $B\mathbb{Z}^n$ is $|\!\det(B)|$ and volume of a body scales by exactly $\frac{1}{\det(B)}$ under an application of $B^{-1}$.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Bounding the shortest vector {#Bounding_the_shortest_vector}

Minkowski's theorem gives an upper bound for the length of the shortest nonzero vector. This result has applications in lattice cryptography and number theory.

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem (Minkowski's bound on the shortest vector)"}

**Theorem (Minkowski's bound on the shortest vector):** Let $L$ be a lattice. Then there is a $x \in L \setminus \{0\}$ with $\|x\|_{\infty} \leq \left|\det(L)\right|^{1/n}$. In particular, by the standard comparison between $l_2$ and $l_{\infty}$ norms, $\|x\|_2 \leq \sqrt{n}\, \left|\det(L)\right|^{1/n}$.

::: {#o14 .proof cue=math-proof}

Let $l = \min \{ \|x\|_{\infty} : x \in L \setminus \{0\} \}$, and set $C = \{ y : \|y\|_{\infty} < l \}$. Then $\text{vol}(C) = (2l)^n$. If $(2l)^n > 2^n |d(L)|$, then $C$ contains a non-zero lattice point, which is a contradiction. Thus $l \leq | d(L)|^{1/n}$. Q.E.D.

:::

**Remarks:**

- The constant in the $L^2$ bound can be improved, for instance by taking the open ball of radius $< l$ as $C$ in the above argument. The optimal constant is known as the [Hermite constant](wikipedia://en/12528854).
- The bound given by the theorem can be very loose, as can be seen by considering the lattice generated by $(1,0), (0,n)$. But it cannot be further improved in the sense that there exists a global constant $c$ such that there exists an $n$-dimensional lattice $L$ satisfying $\| x\|_2 \geq c {\sqrt{n}}\cdot \left|\det(L)\right|^{1/n}$for all $x \in L \setminus \{0\}$. Furthermore, such lattice can be self-dual. [^o15]
- Even though Minkowski's theorem guarantees a short lattice vector within a certain magnitude bound, finding this vector is in general [a hard computational problem](wikipedia://en/18661117#Shortest_vector_problem_%28SVP%29). Finding the vector within a factor guaranteed by Minkowski's bound is [referred to as Minkowski's Vector Problem (MVP), and it is known that approximation SVP reduces to it](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/classes/sp07/cse206a/lec8.pdf) using [transference properties of the dual lattice.](wikipedia://en/1395200#Transference_theorems) The computational problem is also sometimes referred to as HermiteSVP.[^o16]
- The [LLL-basis reduction algorithm](wikipedia://en/1735228) can be seen as a weak but efficiently algorithmic version of Minkowski's bound on the shortest vector. This is because a $\delta$-LLL reduced basis $b_1, \ldots, b_n$ for $L$ has the property that $\|b_1\| \leq \left(\frac{1}{ \delta - .25}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{4}} \det(L)^{1/n}$; see these [lecture notes of Micciancio](http://cseweb.ucsd.edu/classes/sp14/cse206A-a/lec5.pdf) for more on this. As explained in,[^o16] proofs of bounds on the [Hermite constant](wikipedia://en/12528854) contain some of the key ideas in the LLL-reduction algorithm.

:::

### Applications to number theory {#Applications_to_number_theory}

#### Primes that are sums of two squares {#Primes_that_are_sums_of_two_squares}

The difficult implication in [Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares](wikipedia://en/1850216) can be proven using Minkowski's bound on the shortest vector.

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem:** Every [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) with $p \equiv 1 \mod 4$ can be written as a sum of two [squares](wikipedia://en/213919).

::: {#o20 .proof cue=math-proof}

Since $4 \,|\, p - 1$ and $a$ is a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091) modulo a prime $p$ if and only if $a^{\frac{p-1}{2}} = 1~(\text{mod}~p)$ (Euler's Criterion) there is a square root of $-1$ in $\mathbb{Z} / p\mathbb{Z}$; choose one and call one representative in $\mathbb{Z}$ for it $j$. Consider the lattice $L$ defined by the vectors $(1, j), (0,p)$, and let $B$ denote the associated [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859). The determinant of this lattice is $p$, whence Minkowski's bound tells us that there is a nonzero $x = (x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ with $0 < \|Bx\|_2^2 < 2p$. We have $\|Bx\|^2 = \|(x_1, jx_1 + px_2)\|^2 = x_1^2 + (jx_1 + px_2)^2$ and we define the integers $a = x_1, b = (jx_1 + px_2)$. Minkowski's bound tells us that $0 < a^2 + b^2 < 2p$, and simple [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) shows that $a^2 + b^2 = x_1^2 + (jx_1 + px_2)^2 = 0 \mod p$, and thus we conclude that $a^2 + b^2 = p$. Q.E.D.

:::

Additionally, the lattice perspective gives a computationally efficient approach to Fermat's theorem on sums of squares: First, recall that finding any nonzero vector with norm less than $2p$ in $L$, the lattice of the proof, gives a decomposition of $p$ as a sum of two squares. Such vectors can be found efficiently, for instance using [LLL-algorithm](wikipedia://en/1735228). In particular, if $b_1, b_2$ is a $3/4$-LLL reduced basis, then, by the property that $\|b_1\| \leq (\frac{1}{\delta - .25})^{\frac{n-1}{4}} \text{det}(B)^{1/n}$, $\|b_1\|^2 \leq \sqrt{2} p < 2p$. Thus, by running the LLL-lattice basis reduction algorithm with $\delta = 3/4$, we obtain a decomposition of $p$ as a sum of squares. Note that because every vector in $L$ has norm squared a multiple of $p$, the vector returned by the LLL-algorithm in this case is in fact a shortest vector.

:::

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Lagrange's four-square theorem"}

#### Lagrange's four-square theorem {#Lagrange's_four-square_theorem}

Minkowski's theorem is also useful to prove [Lagrange's four-square theorem](wikipedia://en/326395), which states that every [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) can be written as the sum of the squares of four natural numbers.

:::

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Dirichlet's theorem on simultaneous rational approximation"}

#### Dirichlet's theorem on simultaneous rational approximation {#Dirichlet's_theorem_on_simultaneous_rational_approximation}

Minkowski's theorem can be used to prove [Dirichlet's theorem on simultaneous rational approximation](wikipedia://en/2770230).

:::

#### Algebraic number theory {#Algebraic_number_theory}

Another application of Minkowski's theorem is the result that every class in the [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907) of a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) $K$ contains an [integral ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_ideal) of [norm](wikipedia://en/450555) not exceeding a certain bound, depending on $K$, called [Minkowski's bound](wikipedia://en/8491791): the finiteness of the [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28number_theory%29) of an algebraic number field follows immediately.

:::

## Complexity theory {#Complexity_theory}

The complexity of finding the point guaranteed by Minkowski's theorem, or the closely related Blichfeldt's theorem, have been studied from the perspective of [TFNP](wikipedia://en/4668618) search problems. In particular, it is known that a computational analogue of [Blichfeldt's theorem](wikipedia://en/66127387), a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) of the proof of Minkowski's theorem, is PPP-complete.[^o27] It is also known that the computational analogue of Minkowski's theorem is in the class PPP, and it was [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) to be PPP-complete.[^o28]
