# Free group

*Image: F2 Cayley Graph.svg.* Diagram showing the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) for the free group on two generators. Each vertex represents an element of the free group, and each edge represents multiplication by *a* or *b*.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Free group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **free group** $F_S$ over a given set $S$ consists of all [words](wikipedia://en/12792326) that can be built from members of $S$, considering two words to be different unless their equality follows from the [group axioms](wikipedia://en/19447#Definition) (e.g. $st=suu^{-1}t$ but $s\neq t^{-1}$ for $s,t,u\in S$). The members of $S$ are called **generators** of $F_S$, and the number of generators is the **rank** of the free group. An arbitrary [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ is called **free** if it is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to $F_S$ for some [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of $G$, that is, if there is a subset $S$ of $G$ such that every element of $G$ can be written in exactly one way as a product of finitely many elements of $S$ and their inverses (disregarding trivial variations such as $st=suu^{-1}t$).

:::

A related but different notion is a [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296); both notions are particular instances of a [free object](wikipedia://en/333835) from [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200). As such, free groups are defined by their [universal property](wikipedia://en/59735#Universal_property).

## History {#History}

Free groups first arose in the study of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), as examples of [Fuchsian groups](wikipedia://en/471365) (discrete groups acting by [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) on the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291)). In an 1882 paper, [Walther von Dyck](wikipedia://en/752612) pointed out that these groups have the simplest possible [presentations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation).[^o3] The algebraic study of free groups was initiated by [Jakob Nielsen](wikipedia://en/602516) in 1924, who gave them their name and established many of their basic properties.[^o4][^o5][^o6] [Max Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602) realized the connection with topology, and obtained the first proof of the full [Nielsen–Schreier theorem](wikipedia://en/18401725).[^o7] [Otto Schreier](wikipedia://en/4355201) published an algebraic proof of this result in 1927,[^o8] and [Kurt Reidemeister](wikipedia://en/520188) included a comprehensive treatment of free groups in his 1932 book on [combinatorial topology](wikipedia://en/363400).[^o9] Later on in the 1930s, [Wilhelm Magnus](wikipedia://en/8337666) discovered the connection between the [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) of free groups and [free Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/3174646).

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The group $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) is free of rank 1; a generating set is $S=\{1\}$. The integers are also a [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296), although all free groups of rank $\geq 2$ are non-abelian. A free group on a two-element set $S$ occurs in the proof of the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220) and is described there.

On the other hand, any nontrivial finite group cannot be free, since the elements of a free generating set of a free group have infinite order.

In [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of a [bouquet of *k* circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouquet_of_circles) (a set of *k* loops having only one point in common) is the free group on a set of *k* elements.

:::

## Construction {#Construction}

The **free group** $F_S$ with **free generating set** $S$ can be constructed as follows. $S$ is a set of symbols, and we suppose for every $s$ in $S$ there is a corresponding "inverse" symbol, $s^{-1}$, in a set $S^{-1}$. Let $T=S\cup S^{-1}$, and define a **[word](wikipedia://en/12792326)** in $S$ to be any written product of elements of $T$. That is, a word in $S$ is an element of the [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) generated by $T$. The empty word is the word with no symbols at all. For example, if $S=\{a,b,c\}$, then $T=\{a,a^{-1},b,b^{-1},c,c^{-1}\}$, and

$$a b^3 c^{-1} c a^{-1} c\,$$

is a word in $F_S$.

If an element of $S$ lies immediately next to its inverse, the word may be simplified by omitting the $c,c^{-1}$ pair:

$$a b^3 c^{-1} c a^{-1} c\;\;\longrightarrow\;\;a b^3 \, a^{-1} c.$$

A word that cannot be simplified further is called **reduced**.

The free group $F_S$ is defined to be the group of all reduced words in $S$, with [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474) of words (followed by reduction if necessary) as group operation. The identity is the empty word.

A reduced word is called **cyclically reduced** if its first and last letter are not inverse to each other. Every word is [conjugate](wikipedia://en/49176) to a cyclically reduced word, and a cyclically reduced conjugate of a cyclically reduced word is a cyclic permutation of the letters in the word. For instance $b^{-1}abcb$ is not cyclically reduced, but is conjugate to $abc$, which is cyclically reduced. The only cyclically reduced conjugates of $abc$ are $abc$, $bca$, and $cab$.

:::

## Universal property {#Universal_property}

The free group $F_S$ is the [universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_%28mathematics%29) group generated by the set $S$. This can be formalized by the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245): given any function $f$ from $S$ to a group $G$, there exists a unique [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $\varphi:F_S\to G$ making the following [diagram](wikipedia://en/138484) commute (where the unnamed mapping denotes the [inclusion](wikipedia://en/492516) from $S$ into $F_S$):

*Image: Free Group Universal.svg.*

That is, homomorphisms $F_S\to G$ are in one-to-one correspondence with functions $S\to G$. For a non-free group, the presence of [relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation) would restrict the possible images of the generators under a homomorphism.

To see how this relates to the constructive definition, think of the mapping from $S$ to $F_S$ as sending each symbol to a word consisting of that symbol. To construct $\varphi$ for the given $f$, first note that $\varphi$ sends the empty word to the identity of $G$ and it has to agree with $f$ on the elements of $S$. For the remaining words (consisting of more than one symbol), $\varphi$ can be uniquely extended, since it is a homomorphism, i.e., $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)$.

The above property characterizes free groups up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), and is sometimes used as an alternative definition. It is known as the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) of free groups, and the generating set $S$ is called a **basis** for $F_S$. The basis for a free group is not uniquely determined.

Being characterized by a universal property is the standard feature of [free objects](wikipedia://en/333835) in [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200). In the language of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the construction of the free group (similar to most constructions of free objects) is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) to the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092). This functor is [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) to the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) from groups to sets.

## Facts and theorems {#Facts_and_theorems}

Some properties of free groups follow readily from the definition:

1. Any group $G$ is the homomorphic image of some free group $F_S$. Let $S$ be a set of *[generators](wikipedia://en/99945)* of $G$. The natural map $\varphi$: $F_S\to G$ is an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539), which proves the claim. Equivalently, $G$ is isomorphic to a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of some free group $F_S$. If $S$ can be chosen to be finite here, then $G$ is called [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042). The kernel $\ker\varphi$ is the set of all *relations* in the [presentation](wikipedia://en/99494) of $G$; if $\ker\varphi$ can be generated by the conjugates of finitely many elements of $F$, then $G$ is finitely presented.
1. If $S$ has more than one element, then $F_S$ is not [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974), and in fact the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) of $F_S$ is trivial (that is, consists only of the identity element).
1. Two free groups $F_S$ and $F_T$ are isomorphic if and only if $S$ and $T$ have the same [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174). This cardinality is called the **rank** of the free group $F$. Thus for every [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173) *k*, there is, [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism, exactly one free group of rank *k*.
1. A free group of finite rank *n* > 1 has an [exponential](wikipedia://en/191933) [growth rate](wikipedia://en/498727) of order 2*n* − 1.

A few other related results are:

1. The [Nielsen–Schreier theorem](wikipedia://en/18401725): Every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of a free group is free. Furthermore, if the free group $F$ has rank *n* and the subgroup $H$ has [index](wikipedia://en/230581) *e* in $F$, then $H$ is free of rank 1 + *e*(*n–*1).
1. A free group of rank *k* clearly has subgroups of every rank less than *k*. Less obviously, a (*nonabelian!*) free group of rank at least 2 has subgroups of all [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) ranks.
1. The [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) of a free group of rank *k* > 1 has infinite rank; for example for $F(a,b)$, it is freely generated by the [commutators](wikipedia://en/7193) $[a^m,b^n]$ for non-zero *m* and *n*.
1. The free group in two elements is [SQ universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQ_universal); the above follows as any SQ universal group has subgroups of all countable ranks.
1. Any group that [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on a tree, [freely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_action) and preserving the [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_graph), is a free group of countable rank (given by 1 plus the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of the [quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) [graph](wikipedia://en/12401)).
1. The [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of a free group of finite rank, with respect to a free generating set, is a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) on which the group acts freely, preserving the orientation. As a topological space (a one-dimensional [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686)), this Cayley graph $\Gamma(F)$ is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414). For a finitely presented group $G$, the natural homomorphism defined above, $\varphi:F\to G$, defines a [covering map](wikipedia://en/250323) of Cayley graphs $\varphi^*:\Gamma(F)\to\Gamma(G)$, in fact a universal covering. Hence, the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the Cayley graph $\Gamma(G)$ is isomorphic to the kernel of $\varphi$, the normal subgroup of relations among the generators of $G$. The extreme case is when $G=\{e\}$, the trivial group, considered with as many generators as $F$, all of them trivial; the Cayley graph $\Gamma(G)$ is a bouquet of circles, and its fundamental group is $F$ itself.
1. Any subgroup of a free group, $H \subset F$, corresponds to a covering space of the bouquet of circles, namely to the [Schreier coset graph](wikipedia://en/24673785) of $F/H$. This can be used to give a topological proof of the Nielsen-Schreier theorem above.
1. The [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) approach to these results, given in the work by P.J. Higgins below, is related to the use of [covering spaces](wikipedia://en/250323) above. It allows more powerful results, for example on [Grushko's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grushko's_theorem), and a normal form for the fundamental groupoid of a graph of groups. In this approach there is considerable use of free groupoids on a directed graph.
1. [Grushko's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grushko's_theorem) has the consequence that if a subset $B$ of a free group $F$ on *n* elements generates $F$ and has *n* elements, then $B$ generates $F$ freely.

:::

## Free abelian group {#Free_abelian_group}

The free abelian group on a set $S$ is defined via its universal property in the analogous way, with obvious modifications: Consider a pair $(F,\varphi)$, where $F$ is an abelian group and $\varphi:S\to F$ is a function. $F$ is said to be the **free abelian group on $S$ with respect to $\varphi$** if for any abelian group $G$ and any function $\psi:S\to G$, there exists a unique homomorphism $f:F\to G$ such that

$$f(\varphi(s))=\psi(s),\ \forall s\in S.$$

The free abelian group on $S$ can be explicitly identified as the free group $F(S)$ modulo the subgroup generated by its commutators, $[F(S),F(S)]$, i.e. its [abelianisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelianisation). In other words, the free abelian group on $S$ is the set of words that are distinguished only up to the order of letters. The rank of a free group can therefore also be defined as the rank of its abelianisation as a free abelian group.

## Tarski's problems {#Tarski's_problems}

Around 1945, [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) asked whether the free groups on two or more generators have the same [first-order theory](wikipedia://en/19858), and whether this theory is [decidable](wikipedia://en/913118). (Sela 2006) answered the first question by showing that any two nonabelian free groups have the same first-order theory, and (Kharlampovich Myasnikov 2006) answered both questions, showing that this theory is decidable.

A similar unsolved (as of 2011) question in [free probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_probability_theory) asks whether the [von Neumann group algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_group_algebra) of any two non-abelian finitely generated free groups are isomorphic.
