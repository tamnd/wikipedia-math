# Scientific notation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Scientific notation"}

**Scientific notation** is a way of expressing [numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) that are too large or too small to be conveniently written in [decimal form](wikipedia://en/80503), since to do so would require writing out an inconveniently long string of digits. It may be referred to as **scientific form** or **standard index form**, or **standard form** in the United Kingdom. This [base ten](wikipedia://en/8214) notation is commonly used by scientists, mathematicians, and engineers, in part because it can simplify certain [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) operations. On [scientific calculators](wikipedia://en/439711), it is usually known as "SCI" display mode.

:::

In scientific notation, nonzero numbers are written in the form

or *m* times ten raised to the power of *n*, where *n* is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), and the [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) *m* is a nonzero [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) (usually between 1 and 10 in absolute value, and nearly always written as a [terminating decimal](wikipedia://en/8214)). The integer *n* is called the [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent) and the real number *m* is called the *[significand](wikipedia://en/419250)* or *mantissa*.[^o27] The term "mantissa" can be ambiguous where logarithms are involved, because it is also the traditional name of the [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532) of the [common logarithm](wikipedia://en/174482). If the number is negative then a minus sign precedes *m*, as in ordinary decimal notation. In normalized notation, the exponent is chosen so that the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) (modulus) of the significand *m* is at least 1 but less than 10.

[Decimal floating point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_floating_point) is a computer arithmetic system closely related to scientific notation.

## Styles {#Styles}

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Logarithmic notation"}

### Normalized notation {#Normalized_notation}

Any real number can be written in the form *m* in many ways: for example, 350 can be written as \3.5E2 or 35E1 or 350E0.

In *normalized* scientific notation (called "standard form" in the United Kingdom), the exponent *n* is chosen so that the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of *m* remains at least one and less than ten (1 ≤ $\lvert m\rvert$ \< 10). Thus 350 is written as \3.5E2. This form allows easy comparison of numbers: numbers with bigger exponents are (due to the normalization) larger than those with smaller exponents, and subtraction of exponents gives an estimate of the number of [orders of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude) separating the numbers. It is also the form that is required when using tables of [common logarithms](wikipedia://en/174482). In normalized notation, the exponent *n* is negative for a number with absolute value between 0 and 1 (e.g. 0.5 is written as 5E-1). The 10 and exponent are often omitted when the exponent is 0. For a series of numbers that are to be added or subtracted (or otherwise compared), it can be convenient to use the same value of *n* for all elements of the series.

Normalized scientific form is the typical form of expression of large numbers in many fields, unless an unnormalized or differently normalized form, such as [engineering notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_notation), is desired. Normalized scientific notation is often called **[exponential](wikipedia://en/99491) notation** – although the latter term is more general and also applies when *m* is not restricted to the range 1 to 10 (as in engineering notation for instance) and to [bases](wikipedia://en/701207) other than 10 (for example, 3.15).

:::

### Engineering notation {#Engineering_notation}

Engineering notation (often named "ENG" on scientific calculators) differs from normalized scientific notation in that the exponent *n* is restricted to [multiples](wikipedia://en/3912709) of 3. Consequently, the absolute value of *m* is in the range 1 ≤ |*m*| \< 1000, rather than 1 ≤ |*m*| \< 10. Though similar in concept, engineering notation is rarely called *scientific notation*. Engineering notation allows the numbers to explicitly match their corresponding [SI prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_prefixes), which facilitates reading and oral communication. For example, \12.5E-9 m can be read as "twelve-point-five nanometres" and written as \12.5 nm, while its scientific notation equivalent \1.25E-8 m would likely be read out as "one-point-two-five times ten-to-the-negative-eight metres".

[Calculators](wikipedia://en/7593) and [computer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program) typically present very large or small numbers using scientific notation, and some can be configured to uniformly present all numbers that way. Because [superscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superscript) exponents, like the ⟨<sup>7</sup>⟩ in 10<sup>7</sup>, can be inconvenient to display or type, the letter "E" or "e" (for "exponent") is often used to represent "times ten raised to the power of", so that the notation *m* E *n* for a decimal significand *m* and integer exponent *n* means the same as *m* × 10<sup>*n*</sup>. For example [\6.022E23](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_constant) is written as 6.022E23 or 6.022e23, and [\1.6E-35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_length) is written as 1.6E-35 or 1.6e-35. Sometimes the positive power is explicitly shown (6.022E+23 or 6.022e+23). While common in computer output, this abbreviated version of scientific notation is discouraged for published documents by some style guides.[^o28][^o11]

Most popular programming languages – including [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran), [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29)/[C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++), [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29), and [JavaScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JavaScript) – use this "E" notation, which comes from Fortran and was present in the first version released for the [IBM 704](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_704) in 1956.[^o29] The E notation was already used by the developers of [SHARE Operating System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHARE_Operating_System) (SOS) for the [IBM 709](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_709) in 1958.[^o30] Later versions of Fortran (at least since [FORTRAN IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FORTRAN_IV) as of 1961) also use "D" to signify [double precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_precision) numbers in scientific notation,[^o32] and newer Fortran compilers use "Q" to signify [quadruple precision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadruple_precision).[^o34] The [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412) programming language supports the use of either "E" or "D".

The [ALGOL 60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL) (1960) programming language uses a subscript ten "<sub>10</sub>" character instead of the letter "E", for example: `6.022<sub>10</sub>23`.[^o35][^o36] This presented a challenge for computer systems which did not provide such a character, so [ALGOL W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL_W) (1966) replaced the symbol by a single quote, e.g. `6.022'+23`,[^o37] and some Soviet ALGOL variants allowed the use of the Cyrillic letter "[ю](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu_%28Cyrillic%29)", e.g. 6.022ю+23. Subsequently, the [ALGOL 68](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL_68) programming language provided a choice of characters: E, e, \\, ⊥, or `<sub>10</sub>`.[^o38] The ALGOL "<sub>10</sub>" character was included in the Soviet [GOST 10859](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOST_10859) text encoding (1964), and was added to [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) 5.2 (2009) as 23E8.[^o39]

Some programming languages use other symbols. For instance, [Simula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simula) uses & (or && for [long](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_precision)), as in 6.022&23.[^o42] [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica) supports the shorthand notation 6.022\*^23 (reserving the letter E for the [mathematical constant *e*](wikipedia://en/9633)).

*Image: Avogadro's number in e notation.jpg.* A [Texas Instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Instruments) [TI-84 Plus](wikipedia://en/615169) calculator display showing the [Avogadro constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_constant) to three significant figures in E notation

The first [pocket calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocket_calculator) supporting scientific notation appeared in 1972.[^o58] To enter numbers in scientific notation calculators include a button labeled "EXP" or "×10<sup>*x*</sup>", among other variants. The displays of pocket calculators of the 1970s did not display an explicit symbol between significand and exponent; instead, one or more digits were left blank (e.g. `6.022 23`, as seen in the [HP-25](wikipedia://en/4402245)), or a pair of smaller and slightly raised digits were reserved for the exponent (e.g. `6.022 <sup>23</sup>`, as seen in the [Commodore PR100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_International)). In 1976, [Hewlett-Packard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard) calculator user Jim Davidson coined the term *decapower* for the scientific-notation exponent to distinguish it from "normal" exponents, and suggested the letter "D" as a separator between significand and exponent in typewritten numbers (for example, 6.022D23); these gained some currency in the programmable calculator user community.[^o31] The letters "E" or "D" were used as a scientific-notation separator by [Sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp_Corporation) [pocket computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocket_computer) released between 1987 and 1995, "E" used for 10-digit numbers and "D" used for 20-digit double-precision numbers.[^o33] The [Texas Instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Instruments) [TI-83](wikipedia://en/439710) and [TI-84](wikipedia://en/615169) series of calculators (1996–present) use a [small capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_caps) `<small>E</small>` for the separator.[^o41]

In 1962, Ronald O. Whitaker of Rowco Engineering Co. proposed a power-of-ten system nomenclature where the exponent would be circled, e.g. 6.022 × 10<sup>3</sup> would be written as "6.022③".[^o40]

:::

## Significant figures {#Significant_figures}

A significant figure is a digit in a number that adds to its precision. This includes all nonzero numbers, zeroes between significant digits, and zeroes [indicated to be significant](wikipedia://en/317062#Identifying_significant_digits). Leading and trailing zeroes are not significant digits, because they exist only to show the scale of the number. Unfortunately, this leads to ambiguity. The number 1230400 is usually read to have five significant figures: 1, 2, 3, 0, and 4, the final two zeroes serving only as placeholders and adding no precision. The same number, however, would be used if the last two digits were also measured precisely and found to equal 0 – seven significant figures.

When a number is converted into normalized scientific notation, it is scaled down to a number between 1 and 10. All of the significant digits remain, but the placeholding zeroes are no longer required. Thus 1230400 would become \1.2304E6 if it had five significant digits. If the number were known to six or seven significant figures, it would be shown as \1.23040E6 or \1.230400E6. Thus, an additional advantage of scientific notation is that the number of significant figures is unambiguous.

### Estimated final digits {#Estimated_final_digits}

It is customary in scientific measurement to record all the definitely known digits from the measurement and to estimate at least one additional digit if there is any information at all available on its value. The resulting number contains more information than it would without the extra digit, which may be considered a significant digit because it conveys some information leading to greater precision in measurements and in aggregations of measurements (adding them or multiplying them together).

More detailed information about the precision of a value written in scientific notation can be conveyed through additional notation. For instance, the accepted value of the mass of the [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) can be expressed as \1.67262192595E-27 ± (52) kg, which is shorthand for \1.67262192595E-27 ± \0.00000000052 kg. However, it is unclear whether an error expressed in this way (\5.2E-37 in this case) is the maximum possible error, [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520), or some other [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911).

## Use of spaces {#Use_of_spaces}

In normalized scientific notation, in E notation, and in engineering notation, the [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_%28punctuation%29) (which in [typesetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typesetting) may be represented by a normal width space or a [thin space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_space)) that is allowed *only* before and after "×" or in front of "E" is sometimes omitted, though it is less common to do so before the alphabetical character.[^o43]

## Further examples of scientific notation {#Further_examples_of_scientific_notation}

- An [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron)'s mass is about \0.000000000000000000000000000000910938356 kg.[^o44] In scientific notation, this is written \9.10938356E-31 kg.
- The [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) is about 5972400000000000000000000 kg.[^o45] In scientific notation, this is written \5.9724E24 kg.
- The [Earth's circumference](wikipedia://en/9228#Size_and_shape) is approximately 40000000 m.[^o46] In scientific notation, this is 4E7 m. In engineering notation, this is written 40E6 m. In [SI writing style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units), this may be written 40 Mm (*40 megametres*).
- An [inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inch) is defined as *exactly* \25.4 mm. Using scientific notation, this value can be uniformly expressed to any desired precision, from the nearest tenth of a [millimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter) \2.54E1 mm to the nearest [nanometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometer) \2.5400000E1 mm, or beyond.
- [Hyperinflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation) means that too much money is put into circulation, perhaps by printing banknotes, chasing too few goods. It is sometimes defined as inflation of 50% or more in a single month. In such conditions, money rapidly loses its value. Some countries have had events of inflation of 1 million percent or more in a single month, which usually results in the rapid abandonment of the currency. For example, in November 2008 the monthly inflation rate of the [Zimbabwean dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwean_dollar_%281980–2009%29) reached 79.6 billion percent (470% per day); the approximate value with three significant figures would be \7.96E10 %,[^o47][^o48] or more simply a rate of \7.96E8.

:::

## Converting numbers {#Converting_numbers}

Converting a number in these cases means to either convert the number into scientific notation form, convert it back into decimal form or to change the exponent part of the equation. None of these alter the actual number, only how it is expressed.

### Decimal to scientific {#Decimal_to_scientific}

First, move the decimal separator point sufficient places, *n*, to put the number's value within a desired range, between 1 and 10 for normalized notation. If the decimal was moved to the left, append `× 10''<sup>n</sup>''`; to the right, `× 10''<sup>−n</sup>''`. To represent the number 1230400 in normalized scientific notation, the decimal separator would be moved 6 digits to the left and `× 10<sup>6</sup>` appended, resulting in \1.2304E6. The number \-0.0040321 would have its decimal separator shifted 3 digits to the right instead of the left and yield \-4.0321E-3 as a result.

### Scientific to decimal {#Scientific_to_decimal}

Converting a number from scientific notation to decimal notation, first remove the `× 10''<sup>n</sup>''` on the end, then shift the decimal separator *n* digits to the right (positive *n*) or left (negative *n*). The number \1.2304E6 would have its decimal separator shifted 6 digits to the right and become 1230400, while \-4.0321E-3 would have its decimal separator moved 3 digits to the left and be \-0.0040321.

::: {#o21 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Decimal exponential notation"}

### Exponential {#Exponential}

Conversion between different scientific notation representations of the same number with different exponential values is achieved by performing opposite operations of multiplication or division by a power of ten on the significand and an subtraction or addition of one on the exponent part. The decimal separator in the significand is shifted *x* places to the left (or right) and *x* is added to (or subtracted from) the exponent, as shown below.

:::

## Basic operations {#Basic_operations}

Given two numbers in scientific notation,

$$x_0=m_0\times10^{n_0}$$

and

$$x_1=m_1\times10^{n_1}$$

[Multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) and [division](wikipedia://en/53696) are performed using the rules for operation with [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491):

$$x_0 x_1=m_0 m_1\times10^{n_0+n_1}$$

and

$$\frac{x_0}{x_1}=\frac{m_0}{m_1}\times10^{n_0-n_1}$$

Some examples are:

$$5.67\times10^{-5} \times 2.34\times10^2 \approx 13.3\times10^{-5+2} = 13.3\times10^{-3} = 1.33\times10^{-2}$$

and

$$\frac{2.34\times10^2}{5.67\times10^{-5}} \approx 0.413\times10^{2-(-5)} = 0.413\times10^{7} = 4.13\times10^6$$

[Addition](wikipedia://en/61338) and [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) require the numbers to be represented using the same exponential part, so that the significand can be simply added or subtracted:

Next, add or subtract the significands:

$$x_0 \pm x_1=(m_0\pm m_1)\times10^{n_0}$$

An example:

$$2.34\times10^{-5} + 5.67\times10^{-6} = 2.34\times10^{-5} + 0.567\times10^{-5} = 2.907\times10^{-5}$$

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Base-2 scientific notation"}

## Other bases {#Other_bases}

While base ten is normally used for scientific notation, powers of other bases can be used too,[^o49] base 2 being the next most commonly used one.

For example, in base-2 scientific notation, the number 1001<sub>b</sub> in [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) (=9<sub>d</sub>) is written as 1.001<sub>b</sub> × 2<sub>d</sub><sup>11<sub>b</sub></sup> or 1.001<sub>b</sub> × 10<sub>b</sub><sup>11<sub>b</sub></sup> using binary numbers (or shorter 1.001 × 10<sup>11</sup> if binary context is obvious). In E notation, this is written as 1.001<sub>b</sub>E11<sub>b</sub> (or shorter: 1.001E11) with the letter "E" now standing for "times two (10<sub>b</sub>) to the power" here. In order to better distinguish this base-2 exponent from a base-10 exponent, a base-2 exponent is sometimes also indicated by using the letter "B" instead of "E",[^o51] a shorthand notation originally proposed by [Bruce Alan Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Alan_Martin) of [Brookhaven National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookhaven_National_Laboratory) in 1968,[^o50] as in 1.001<sub>b</sub>B11<sub>b</sub> (or shorter: 1.001B11). For comparison, the same number in [decimal representation](wikipedia://en/3293594): 1.125 × 2<sup>3</sup> (using decimal representation), or 1.125B3 (still using decimal representation). Some calculators use a mixed representation for binary floating point numbers, where the exponent is displayed as decimal number even in binary mode, so the above becomes 1.001<sub>b</sub> × 10<sub>b</sub><sup>3<sub>d</sub></sup> or shorter 1.001B3.[^o51]

This is closely related to the base-2 [floating-point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-point) representation commonly used in computer arithmetic, and the usage of IEC [binary prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_prefixes) (e.g. 1B10 for 1×2<sup>10</sup> ([kibi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kibi-)), 1B20 for 1×2<sup>20</sup> ([mebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mebi-)), 1B30 for 1×2<sup>30</sup> ([gibi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibi-)), 1B40 for 1×2<sup>40</sup> ([tebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tebi-))).

Similar to "B" (or "b"[^o52]), the letters "H"[^o51] (or "h"[^o52]) and "O"[^o51] (or "o",[^o52] or "C"[^o51]) are sometimes also used to indicate *times 16 or 8 to the power* as in 1.25 = 1.40<sub>h</sub> × 10<sub>h</sub><sup>0<sub>h</sub></sup> = 1.40H0 = 1.40h0, or 98000 = 2.7732<sub>o</sub> × 10<sub>o</sub><sup>5<sub>o</sub></sup> = 2.7732o5 = 2.7732C5.[^o51]

Another similar convention to denote base-2 exponents is using a letter "P" (or "p", for "power"). In this notation the significand is always meant to be hexadecimal, whereas the exponent is always meant to be decimal.[^o53] This notation can be produced by implementations of the *[printf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printf)* family of functions following the [C99](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C99) specification and ([Single Unix Specification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_Unix_Specification)) [IEEE Std 1003.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Std_1003.1) [POSIX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/POSIX) standard, when using the *%a* or *%A* conversion specifiers.[^o53][^o54][^o55] Starting with [C++11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++11), [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++) I/O functions could parse and print the P notation as well. Meanwhile, the notation has been fully adopted by the language standard since [C++17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++17).[^o56] [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc.)'s [Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swift_%28programming_language%29) supports it as well.[^o57] It is also required by the [IEEE 754-2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_754-2008) binary floating-point standard. Example: 1.3DEp42 represents 1.3DE<sub>h</sub> × 2<sup>42</sup>.

[Engineering notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_notation) can be viewed as a base-1000 scientific notation.

:::
