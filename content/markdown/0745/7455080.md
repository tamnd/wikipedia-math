# Normal convergence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Normal convergence"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) **normal convergence** is a type of [convergence](wikipedia://en/1743431) for [series](wikipedia://en/15287) of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427). Like [absolute convergence](wikipedia://en/61532), it has the useful property that it is preserved when the order of summation is changed.

:::

## History {#History}

The concept of normal convergence was first introduced by [René Baire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Baire) in 1908 in his book *Leçons sur les théories générales de l'analyse*.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a set *S* and functions $f_n : S \to \mathbb{C}$ (or to any [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538)), the series

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty f_n(x)$$

is called **normally convergent** if the series of [uniform norms](wikipedia://en/349755) of the terms of the series converges, i.e.,

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty \|f_n\| := \sum_{n=0}^\infty \sup_{x \in S} |f_n(x)| < \infty.$$

:::

## Distinctions {#Distinctions}

Normal convergence implies [uniform absolute convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_absolute_convergence), i.e., uniform convergence of the series of nonnegative functions $\sum_{n=0}^\infty |f_n(x)|$; this fact is essentially the [Weierstrass M-test](wikipedia://en/444456). However, they should not be confused; to illustrate this, consider

$$f_n(x) = \begin{cases} 1/n, & x = n, \\ 0, & x \ne n. \end{cases}$$

Then the series $\sum_{n=0}^\infty |f_n(x)|$ is uniformly convergent (for any *ε* take *n* ≥ 1/*ε*), but the series of uniform norms is the [harmonic series](wikipedia://en/142488) and thus diverges. An example using continuous functions can be made by replacing these functions with bump functions of height 1/*n* and width 1 centered at each natural number *n*.

As well, normal convergence of a series is different from *norm-topology convergence*, i.e. convergence of the partial sum sequence in the topology induced by the uniform norm. Normal convergence implies norm-topology convergence if and only if the space of functions under consideration is [complete](wikipedia://en/3989) with respect to the uniform norm. (The converse does not hold even for complete function spaces: for example, consider the harmonic series as a sequence of constant functions).

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Local normal convergence {#Local_normal_convergence}

A series can be called "locally normally convergent on *X*" if each point *x* in *X* has a neighborhood *U* such that the series of functions *ƒ*<sub>*n*</sub> restricted to the domain *U*

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty f_n\mid_U$$

is normally convergent, i.e. such that

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty \| f_n\|_U < \infty$$

where the norm $\|\cdot\|_U$ is the supremum over the domain *U*.

### Compact normal convergence {#Compact_normal_convergence}

A series is said to be "normally convergent on compact subsets of *X*" or "compactly normally convergent on *X*" if for every compact subset *K* of *X*, the series of functions *ƒ*<sub>*n*</sub> restricted to *K*

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty f_n\mid_K$$

is normally convergent on *K*.

**Note**: if *X* is [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) (even in the weakest sense), local normal convergence and compact normal convergence are equivalent.

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Every normal convergent series is uniformly convergent, locally uniformly convergent, and compactly uniformly convergent. This is very important, since it assures that any re-arrangement of the series, any derivatives or integrals of the series, and sums and products with other convergent series will converge to the "correct" value.
- If $\sum_{n=0}^\infty f_n(x)$ is normally convergent to $f$, then any re-arrangement of the sequence (*ƒ*<sub>1</sub>, *ƒ*<sub>2</sub>, *ƒ*<sub>3</sub> ...) also converges normally to the same *ƒ*. That is, for every [bijection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijection_%28mathematics%29) $\tau: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$, $\sum_{n=0}^\infty f_{\tau(n)}(x)$ is normally convergent to $f$.

:::
