# List of long mathematical proofs

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of long mathematical proofs"}

This is a list of unusually long [mathematical proofs](wikipedia://en/82285). Such proofs often use [computational proof methods](wikipedia://en/2840305) and may be considered [non-surveyable](wikipedia://en/48740815).

:::

, the longest mathematical proof, measured by number of published journal pages, is the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445) with well over 10,000 pages. There are several proofs that would be far longer than this if the details of the computer calculations they depend on were published in full.

## Long proofs {#Long_proofs}

The length of unusually long proofs has increased with time. As a rough rule of thumb, 100 pages in 1900, or 200 pages in 1950, or 500 pages in 2000 is unusually long for a proof.

::: {#o3 .entry cue=list-entry name="Abel–Ruffini theorem"}

- 1799 – The [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518) was nearly proved by [Paolo Ruffini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Ruffini_%28mathematician%29), but his proof, spanning 500 pages, was mostly ignored and later, in 1824, [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573) published a proof that required just six pages.

:::

::: {#o4 .entry cue=list-entry name="Killing"}

- 1890 – [Killing](wikipedia://en/1531824)'s classification of [simple](wikipedia://en/59500649) [complex](wikipedia://en/62595897) [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944), including his discovery of the [exceptional Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/6573043), took 180 pages in 4 papers.

:::

::: {#o5 .entry cue=list-entry name="polygon of 65537 sides"}

- 1894 – The ruler-and-compass construction of a [polygon of 65537 sides](wikipedia://en/5024281) by [Johann Gustav Hermes](wikipedia://en/10448726) took over 200 pages.

:::

::: {#o6 .entry cue=list-entry name="Emanuel Lasker"}

- 1905 – [Emanuel Lasker](wikipedia://en/244649)'s original proof of the [Lasker–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–Noether_theorem) took 98 pages, but has since been simplified: modern proofs are less than a page long.

:::

::: {#o7 .entry cue=list-entry name="Odd order theorem"}

- 1963 – [Odd order theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_order_theorem) by Feit and Thompson was 255 pages long, which at the time was over 10 times as long as what had previously been considered a long paper in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).

:::

::: {#o8 .entry cue=list-entry name="Resolution of singularities"}

- 1964 – [Resolution of singularities](wikipedia://en/9914115). Hironaka's original proof was 216 pages long; it has since been simplified considerably down to about 10 or 20 pages.

:::

::: {#o9 .entry cue=list-entry name="Resolution of singularities"}

- 1966 – Abhyankar's proof of [resolution of singularities](wikipedia://en/9914115) for 3-folds in [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) greater than 6 covered about 500 pages in several papers. In 2009, Cutkosky simplified this to about 40 pages.

:::

::: {#o10 .entry cue=list-entry name="Discrete series representation"}

- 1966 – [Discrete series representations](wikipedia://en/7205312) of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945). Harish-Chandra's construction of these involved a long series of papers totaling around 500 pages. His later work on the Plancherel theorem for semisimple groups added another 150 pages to these.

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=list-entry name="Novikov"}

- 1968 – the [Novikov](wikipedia://en/1139033)–[Adian](wikipedia://en/5028401) proof solving [Burnside's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnside's_problem) on [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) infinite groups with finite [exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent_of_a_group) negatively. The three-part original paper is more than 300 pages long. (Britton later published a 282-page paper attempting to solve the problem, but his paper contained a serious gap.)

:::

::: {#o12 .entry cue=list-entry name="Fondements de la Géometrie Algébrique"}

- 1960-1970 – *[Fondements de la Géometrie Algébrique](wikipedia://en/7134874)*, *[Éléments de géométrie algébrique](wikipedia://en/471387)* and *[Séminaire de géométrie algébrique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Séminaire_de_géométrie_algébrique)*. Grothendieck's work on the foundations of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) covers many thousands of pages. Although this is not a proof of a single theorem, there are several theorems in it whose proofs depend on hundreds of earlier pages.

:::

::: {#o13 .entry cue=list-entry name="N-group theorem"}

- 1974 – [N-group theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-group_theorem). Thompson's classification of N-groups used 6 papers totaling about 400 pages, but also used earlier results of his such as the [odd order theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_order_theorem), which bring to total length up to more than 700 pages.

:::

::: {#o14 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ramanujan conjecture"}

- 1974 – [Ramanujan conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan_conjecture) and the [Weil conjectures](wikipedia://en/244705). While Deligne's final paper proving these conjectures were "only" about 30 pages long, it depended on background results in algebraic geometry and [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) that Deligne estimated to be about 2000 pages long.

:::

::: {#o15 .entry cue=list-entry name="4-color theorem"}

- 1974 – [4-color theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-color_theorem). Appel and Haken's proof of this took 139 pages, and also depended on long computer calculations.

:::

::: {#o16 .entry cue=list-entry name="Gorenstein–Harada theorem"}

- 1974 – The [Gorenstein–Harada theorem](wikipedia://en/33107560) classifying finite groups of sectional 2-rank at most 4 was 464 pages long.

:::

::: {#o17 .entry cue=list-entry name="Eisenstein series"}

- 1976 – [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458). [Langlands](wikipedia://en/25986)'s proof of the functional equation for Eisenstein series was 337 pages long.

:::

::: {#o18 .entry cue=list-entry name="Trichotomy theorem"}

- 1983 – [Trichotomy theorem](wikipedia://en/29598798). Gorenstein and Lyons's proof for the case of rank at least 4 was 731 pages long, and Aschbacher's proof of the rank 3 case adds another 159 pages, for a total of 890 pages.

:::

::: {#o19 .entry cue=list-entry name="Selberg trace formula"}

- 1983 – [Selberg trace formula](wikipedia://en/981694). Hejhal's proof of a general form of the Selberg trace formula consisted of 2 volumes with a total length of 1322 pages.

:::

::: {#o20 .entry cue=list-entry name="Arthur–Selberg trace formula"}

- [Arthur–Selberg trace formula](wikipedia://en/20419621). Arthur's proofs of the various versions of this cover several hundred pages spread over many papers.

:::

::: {#o21 .entry cue=list-entry name="Almgren's regularity theorem"}

- 2000 – [Almgren's regularity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almgren's_regularity_theorem). Almgren's proof was 955 pages long.

:::

::: {#o22 .entry cue=list-entry name="Lafforgue's theorem"}

- 2000 – [Lafforgue's theorem](wikipedia://en/32295019) on the Langlands conjecture for the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) over function fields. [Laurent Lafforgue](wikipedia://en/104485)'s proof of this was about 600 pages long, not counting many pages of background results.

:::

::: {#o23 .entry cue=list-entry name="Poincaré conjecture"}

- 2003 – [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798), [Geometrization theorem](wikipedia://en/220642), [Geometrization conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642). Perelman's original proofs of the Poincaré conjecture and the Geometrization conjecture were not lengthy, but were rather sketchy. Several other mathematicians have published proofs with the details filled in, which come to several hundred pages.

:::

::: {#o24 .entry cue=list-entry name="Quasithin group"}

- 2004 – [Quasithin groups](wikipedia://en/12939181). The classification of the [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) quasithin groups by Aschbacher and Smith was 1221 pages long, one of the longest single papers ever written.

:::

::: {#o25 .entry cue=list-entry name="Classification of finite simple groups"}

- 2004 – [Classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). The proof of this is spread out over hundreds of journal articles which makes it hard to estimate its total length, which is probably around 10,000 to 20,000 pages.

:::

::: {#o26 .entry cue=list-entry name="Robertson–Seymour theorem"}

- 2004 – [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769). The proof takes about 500 pages spread over about 20 papers.

:::

::: {#o27 .entry cue=list-entry name="Kepler conjecture"}

- 2005 – [Kepler conjecture](wikipedia://en/368311). [Hales](wikipedia://en/2388356)'s proof of this involves several hundred pages of published arguments, together with several gigabytes of computer calculations.

:::

::: {#o28 .entry cue=list-entry name="Strong perfect graph theorem"}

- 2006 – the [strong perfect graph theorem](wikipedia://en/744171), by [Maria Chudnovsky](wikipedia://en/19731900), [Neil Robertson](wikipedia://en/1575704), [Paul Seymour](wikipedia://en/1575708), and [Robin Thomas](wikipedia://en/24641679). The paper comprised 180 pages in the *[Annals of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/2785)*.

:::

## Long computer calculations {#Long_computer_calculations}

There are many mathematical theorems that have been checked by long computer calculations. If these were written out as proofs, many would be far longer than most of the proofs above. There is not really a clear distinction between computer calculations and proofs, as several of the proofs above, such as the 4-color theorem and the Kepler conjecture, use long computer calculations as well as many pages of mathematical argument. For the computer calculations in this section, the mathematical arguments are only a few pages long, and the length is due to long but routine calculations. Some typical examples of such theorems include:

::: {#o30 .entry cue=list-entry name="Sporadic simple groups"}

- Several proofs of the existence of [sporadic simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_simple_groups), such as the [Lyons group](wikipedia://en/1110773), originally used computer calculations with large [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) or with [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) on billions of symbols. In most cases, such as the [baby monster group](wikipedia://en/324799), the computer proofs were later replaced by shorter proofs avoiding computer calculations. Similarly, the calculation of the [maximal subgroups](wikipedia://en/1472649) of the larger sporadic groups uses a lot of computer calculations.

:::

::: {#o31 .entry cue=list-entry name="prime"}

- Proving that a particular number is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666)

:::

::: {#o32 .entry cue=list-entry name="Riemann hypothesis"}

- 2004 – Verification of the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125) for the first 10<sup>13</sup> [zeros](wikipedia://en/264210) of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).

:::

::: {#o33 .entry cue=list-entry name="Checkers"}

- 2007 – Verification that [checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkers) is a draw.

:::

::: {#o34 .entry cue=list-entry name="π"}

- Calculations of large numbers of digits of [π](wikipedia://en/23601).

:::

::: {#o35 .entry cue=list-entry name="Rubik's Cube"}

- 2010 – Showing that the [Rubik's Cube](wikipedia://en/25971) [can be solved in 20 moves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_solutions_for_Rubik's_Cube).

:::

- 2012 – Showing that Sudoku needs [at least 17 clues](https://arxiv.org/abs/1201.0749).

::: {#o36 .entry cue=list-entry name="Ternary Goldbach conjecture"}

- 2013 – [Ternary Goldbach conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternary_Goldbach_conjecture): Every [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) number greater than 5 can be expressed as the sum of three primes.

:::

::: {#o37 .entry cue=list-entry name="Erdős"}

- 2014 – Proof of [Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) [discrepancy conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/±1-sequence) for the particular case *C*=2: every [±1-sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/±1-sequence) of the length 1161 has a discrepancy at least 3; the original proof, generated by a [SAT solver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAT_solver), had a size of 13 gigabytes and was later reduced to 850 megabytes.

:::

::: {#o38 .entry cue=list-entry name="Boolean Pythagorean triples problem"}

- 2016 – Solving the [Boolean Pythagorean triples problem](wikipedia://en/50650284) required the generation of 200 terabytes of proof.[^o39]

:::

::: {#o40 .entry cue=list-entry name="Marijn Heule"}

- 2017 – [Marijn Heule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marijn_Heule), who coauthored solution to the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem, announced a 2 petabytes long proof that the 5th [Schur's number](wikipedia://en/1522286) is 160.[^o41]

:::

## Long proofs in mathematical logic {#Long_proofs_in_mathematical_logic}

[Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) showed how to find explicit examples of statements in formal systems that are provable in that system but whose shortest [proof](wikipedia://en/1197184) is absurdly long. For example, the statement:

"This statement cannot be proved in [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic) in less than a [googolplex](wikipedia://en/12365) symbols"

is provable in Peano arithmetic but the shortest proof has at least a googolplex symbols. It has a short proof in a more powerful system: in fact, it is easily provable in Peano arithmetic together with the statement that Peano arithmetic is consistent (which cannot be proved in Peano arithmetic by [Gödel's incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_incompleteness_theorem)).

In this argument, Peano arithmetic can be replaced by any more powerful consistent system, and a googolplex can be replaced by any number that can be described concisely in the system.

[Harvey Friedman](wikipedia://en/3038108) found some explicit natural examples of this phenomenon, giving some explicit statements in Peano arithmetic and other formal systems whose shortest proofs are ridiculously long (Smoryński 1982). For example, the statement

"there is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n* such that if there is a sequence of rooted trees *T*<sub>1</sub>, *T*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *T*<sub>*n*</sub> such that *T*<sub>*k*</sub> has at most *k*+10 vertices, then some tree can be homeomorphically [embedded](wikipedia://en/8149170) in a later one"

is provable in Peano arithmetic, but the shortest proof has length at least <sup>1000</sup>2, where <sup>0</sup>2 = 1 and <sup>*n*+1</sup>2 = 2<sup>(<sup>*n*</sup>2)</sup> ([tetrational](wikipedia://en/482379) growth). The statement is a special case of [Kruskal's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kruskal's_theorem) and has a short proof in [second order arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_order_arithmetic).
