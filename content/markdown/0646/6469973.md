# De Gua's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="De Gua's theorem"}

*Image: De\_gua\_theorem\_1.svg.* Tetrahedron with a right-angle corner in O

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **De Gua's theorem** is a three-dimensional analog of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) named after [Jean Paul de Gua de Malves](wikipedia://en/2680338). It states that if a [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606) has a right-angle corner (like the corner of a [cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_%28geometry%29)), then the square of the area of the face opposite the right-angle corner is the sum of the squares of the areas of the other three faces:

$$A_{ABC}^2 = A_{\color {blue} ABO}^2+A_{\color {green} ACO}^2+A_{\color {red} BCO}^2$$

De Gua's theorem can be applied for proving a special case of [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123).[^o2]

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) and de Gua's theorem are special cases ($n = 2, 3$) of a [general theorem](wikipedia://en/39781#Simplices_with_an_"orthogonal_corner") about [*n*-simplices](wikipedia://en/39781) with a [right-angle](wikipedia://en/76956) corner, proved by P. S. Donchian and [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) in 1935.[^o5] This, in turn, is a special case of [a yet more general theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034#Sets_of_m-dimensional_objects_in_n-dimensional_space) by Donald R. Conant and William A. Beyer (1974),[^o6] which can be stated as follows.

Let *U* be a [measurable](wikipedia://en/43327) subset of a *k*-dimensional [affine subspace](wikipedia://en/298834#Affine_subspaces) of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (so $k \le n$). For any subset $I \subseteq \{ 1, \ldots, n \}$ with exactly *k* elements, let $U_I$ be the [orthogonal projection](wikipedia://en/519182#Orthogonal_projections) of *U* onto the [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of $e_{i_1}, \ldots, e_{i_k}$, where $I = \{i_1, \ldots, i_k\}$ and $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ is the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) for $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then

$$\operatorname{vol}_k^2(U) = \sum_I \operatorname{vol}_k^2(U_I),$$

where $\operatorname{vol}_k(U)$ is the [*k*-dimensional volume](wikipedia://en/504109) of *U* and the sum is over all subsets $I \subseteq \{ 1, \ldots, n \}$ with exactly *k* elements.

De Gua's theorem and its generalisation (above) to *n*-simplices with right-angle corners correspond to the special case where *k* = *n*−1 and *U* is an (*n*−1)-simplex in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with vertices on the [co-ordinate axes](wikipedia://en/7706). For example, suppose $n = 3$, $k = 2$ and *U* is the [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) $\triangle ABC$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with vertices *A*, *B* and *C* lying on the $x_1$-, $x_2$- and $x_3$-axes, respectively. The subsets $I$ of $\{ 1, 2, 3 \}$ with exactly 2 elements are $\{ 2,3 \}$, $\{ 1,3 \}$ and $\{ 1,2 \}$. By definition, $U_{\{ 2,3 \}}$ is the orthogonal projection of $U = \triangle ABC$ onto the $x_2 x_3$-plane, so $U_{\{ 2,3 \}}$ is the triangle $\triangle OBC$ with vertices *O*, *B* and *C*, where *O* is the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432#Cartesian_coordinates) of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Similarly, $U_{\{ 1,3 \}} = \triangle AOC$ and $U_{\{ 1,2 \}} = \triangle ABO$, so the Conant–Beyer theorem says

$$\operatorname{vol}_2^2(\triangle ABC) = \operatorname{vol}_2^2(\triangle OBC) + \operatorname{vol}_2^2(\triangle AOC) + \operatorname{vol}_2^2(\triangle ABO),$$

which is de Gua's theorem.

The generalisation of de Gua's theorem to *n*-simplices with right-angle corners can also be obtained as a special case from the [Cayley–Menger determinant formula](wikipedia://en/2826108).

De Gua's theorem can also be generalized to arbitrary tetrahedra and to pyramids, similarly to how the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) generalises Pythagoras' theorem.[^o7][^o8]

:::

## History {#History}

Jean Paul de Gua de Malves (1713–1785) published the theorem in 1783, but around the same time a slightly more general version was published by another French mathematician, [Charles de Tinseau d'Amondans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Tinseau_d'Amondans) (1746–1818), as well. However the theorem had also been known much earlier to [Johann Faulhaber](wikipedia://en/9768822) (1580–1635) and [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) (1596–1650).[^o11]
