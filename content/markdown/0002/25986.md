# Robert Langlands

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Robert Langlands"}

**Robert Phelan Langlands** (; born October 6, 1936) is a Canadian mathematician.[^o9][^o3] He is best known as the founder of the [Langlands program](wikipedia://en/171905), a vast web of conjectures and results connecting [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) and [automorphic forms](wikipedia://en/782146) to the study of [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673) in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527),[^o4][^o5] for which he received the 2018 [Abel Prize](wikipedia://en/159285). He is emeritus professor and occupied [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s office at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in Princeton, until 2020 when he retired.[^o6]

:::

## Early life and career {#Early_life_and_career}

Langlands was born in New Westminster, British Columbia, Canada, in 1936 to Robert Langlands and Kathleen J Phelan. He has two younger sisters (Mary b. 1938; Sally b. 1941). In 1945, his family moved to [White Rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Rock,_British_Columbia), near the US border, where his parents had a building supply and construction business.[^o8][^o4][^o9]

He graduated from [Semiahmoo Secondary School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiahmoo_Secondary_School) and started enrolling at the [University of British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_British_Columbia) at the age of 16, receiving his undergraduate degree in mathematics in 1957;[^o10] he continued at UBC to receive a M.Sc. in 1958. He then went to [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University), where he received a Ph.D. in 1960.[^o11]

His first academic position was at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) from 1960 to 1967, where he worked as an associate professor.[^o4] He spent a year in Turkey at [METU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East_Technical_University) during 1967–68 in an office next to [Cahit Arf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cahit_Arf)'s.[^o12] He was a Miller Research Fellow at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), from 1964 to 1965, then was a professor at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) from 1967 to 1972. He was appointed Hermann Weyl Professor at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in 1972, and became professor emeritus in January 2007.[^o6]

## Research {#Research}

Langlands's Ph.D. thesis was on the analytical theory of [Lie](wikipedia://en/17945) [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799),[^o14] but he soon moved into [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), adapting the methods of [Harish-Chandra](wikipedia://en/373190) to the theory of [automorphic forms](wikipedia://en/782146). His first accomplishment in this field was a formula for the dimension of certain spaces of automorphic forms, in which particular types of Harish-Chandra's discrete series appeared.[^o15][^o16]

He next constructed an analytical theory of [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458) for [reductive groups](wikipedia://en/1088386) of [rank](wikipedia://en/395885#Tori) greater than one, thus extending work of [Hans Maass](wikipedia://en/19399645), Walter Roelcke, and [Atle Selberg](wikipedia://en/2021) from the early 1950s for rank one groups such as $\mathrm{SL}(2)$. This amounted to describing in general terms the continuous [spectra](wikipedia://en/20635289) of arithmetic quotients, and showing that all automorphic forms arise in terms of cusp forms and the residues of Eisenstein series induced from cusp forms on smaller subgroups. As a first application, he proved the [Weil conjecture on Tamagawa numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_conjecture_on_Tamagawa_numbers) for the large class of arbitrary simply connected [Chevalley groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley_group) defined over the rational numbers. Previously this had been known only in a few isolated cases and for certain classical groups where it could be shown by induction.[^o17]

As a second application of this work, he was able to show [meromorphic](wikipedia://en/80857) continuation for a large class of [$L$-functions](wikipedia://en/245283) arising in the theory of automorphic forms, not previously known to have them. These occurred in the constant terms of Eisenstein series, and meromorphicity as well as a weak [functional equation](wikipedia://en/595708) were a consequence of functional equations for Eisenstein series. This work led in turn, in the winter of 1966–67, to the now well known conjectures[^o18] making up what is often called the [Langlands program](wikipedia://en/171905). Very roughly speaking, they propose a huge generalization of previously known examples of reciprocity, including (a) classical [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695), in which characters of local and arithmetic abelian [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673) are identified with characters of local [multiplicative groups](wikipedia://en/1110742) and the idele quotient group, respectively; (b) earlier results of [Martin Eichler](wikipedia://en/4224478) and [Goro Shimura](wikipedia://en/174724) in which the [Hasse–Weil zeta functions](wikipedia://en/1157842) of arithmetic quotients of the [upper half plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_half_plane) are identified with $L$-functions occurring in [Hecke](wikipedia://en/1233990)'s theory of [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) automorphic forms. These conjectures were first posed in relatively complete form in a famous letter to Weil,[^o18] written in January 1967. It was in this letter that he introduced what has since become known as the $L$-group and along with it, the notion of functoriality.

The book by [Hervé Jacquet](wikipedia://en/25600925) and Langlands on $\mathrm{GL}(2)$ presented a theory of automorphic forms for the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\mathrm{GL}(2)$, establishing among other things the [Jacquet–Langlands correspondence](wikipedia://en/20606232) showing that functoriality was capable of explaining very precisely how automorphic forms for $\mathrm{GL}(2)$ related to those for [quaternion algebras](wikipedia://en/2776890). This book applied the adelic [trace formula](wikipedia://en/981694) for $\mathrm{GL}(2)$ and quaternion algebras to do this. Subsequently, [James Arthur](wikipedia://en/9525221), a student of Langlands while he was at Yale, successfully developed the trace formula for groups of higher rank. This has become a major tool in attacking functoriality in general, and in particular has been applied to demonstrating that the [Hasse–Weil zeta functions](wikipedia://en/1157842) of certain [Shimura varieties](wikipedia://en/10389861) are among the $L$-functions arising from automorphic forms.[^o19]

The functoriality conjecture is far from proven, but a special case (the octahedral [Artin conjecture](wikipedia://en/2774171#The_Artin_conjecture), proved by Langlands[^o20] and Tunnell[^o21]) was the starting point of [Andrew Wiles](wikipedia://en/2027)'s attack on the [Taniyama–Shimura conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taniyama–Shimura_conjecture) and [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953).

In the mid-1980s Langlands turned his attention[^o22] to [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), particularly the problems of [percolation](wikipedia://en/299184) and conformal invariance. In 1995, Langlands started a collaboration with [Bill Casselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Casselman_%28mathematician%29) at the [University of British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_British_Columbia) with the aim of posting nearly all of his writings—including publications, preprints, as well as selected correspondence—on the Internet. The correspondence includes a copy of the original letter to Weil that introduced the $L$-group. In recent years he has turned his attention back to automorphic forms, working in particular on a theme he calls "beyond [endoscopy](wikipedia://en/20546343)".[^o23]

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

Langlands has received the 1996 [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize) (which he shared with [Andrew Wiles](wikipedia://en/2027)),[^o25] the 2005 AMS [Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steele_Prize), the 1980 [Jeffery–Williams Prize](wikipedia://en/18739069), the 1988 [NAS Award in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAS_Award_in_Mathematics) from the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences),[^o26] the 2000 grande médaille de l'Académie des sciences de Paris, the 2006 [Nemmers Prize in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1252473), the 2007 [Shaw Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaw_Prize) in Mathematical Sciences (with [Richard Taylor](wikipedia://en/646931)) for his work on automorphic forms. In 2018, Langlands was awarded the [Abel Prize](wikipedia://en/159285) for "his visionary program connecting representation theory to number theory".[^o27]

He was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society_of_Canada) in 1972 and a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) in 1981.[^o28][^o29] In 2012, he became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o30] Langlands was elected as a member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1990.[^o31] He was elected as a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1993[^o32] and a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society) in 2004.[^o33]

Among other honorary degrees, in 2003, Langlands received a doctorate *honoris causa* from [Université Laval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_Laval).[^o34]

In 2019, Langlands was appointed a Companion of the [Order of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Canada).[^o35][^o36]

On January 10, 2020, Langlands was honoured at [Semiahmoo Secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiahmoo_Secondary_School), which installed a mural to celebrate his contributions to mathematics.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Langlands has been married to Charlotte Lorraine Cheverie (b 1935) since 1957. They have four children (2 daughters and 2 sons).[^o4] He holds Canadian and American citizenships.

In addition to his mathematical studies, Langlands likes to learn foreign languages, both for better understanding of foreign publications on his topic and as a hobby. He speaks English, French, Turkish and German, and reads (but does not speak) Russian.[^o38]
