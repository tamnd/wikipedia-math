# Slope

*Image: Wiki slope in 2d.svg.* Slope: $m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \tan(\theta)$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Slope"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **slope** or **gradient** of a [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29) is a number that describes the [direction](wikipedia://en/54516626) of the line on a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29).[^o2]

:::

It is commonly denoted by the letter *m*, and is defined as the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of the vertical change (rise) to the horizontal change (run) between any two distinct points on the line. It is not a direct distance or a direct angle, but a measure of their ratio.

The line may be physical, as set by a [road surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), pictorial as in a diagram of a road or roof, or abstract in pure mathematics. An application of the mathematical concept is found in the [grade](wikipedia://en/259162) or [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) in [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography) and [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering).

The *steepness*, incline, or grade of a line is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of its slope: greater absolute value indicates a steeper line. The line trend is defined as follows:

- An "increasing" or "ascending" line goes up from left to right and has positive slope: $m>0$.
- A "decreasing" or "descending" line goes down from left to right and has negative slope: $m<0$.

Special directions are:

- A "(square) [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251)" line has unit slope: $m=1$
- A "horizontal" line (the graph of a [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861)) has zero slope: **$m=0$**.
- A "vertical" line has undefined or infinite slope (see below).

If two points of a road have altitudes *y*<sub>1</sub> and *y*<sub>2</sub>, the rise is the difference (*y*<sub>2</sub> − *y*<sub>1</sub>) = Δ*y*. Neglecting the [Earth's curvature](wikipedia://en/233668), if the two points have horizontal distance *x*<sub>1</sub> and *x*<sub>2</sub> from a fixed point, the run is (*x*<sub>2</sub> − *x*<sub>1</sub>) = Δ*x*. The slope between the two points is the **difference ratio**:

$$m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}.$$

Through [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), the slope *m* of a line is related to its [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) of inclination *θ* by the [tangent function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_function)

$$m = \tan (\theta).$$

Thus, a 45° rising line has slope *m =* +1, and a 45° falling line has slope *m =* −1.

Generalizing this, [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416) defines the slope of a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) at a point as the slope of its [tangent line](wikipedia://en/31482) at that point. When the curve is approximated by a series of points, the slope of the curve may be approximated by the slope of the [secant line](wikipedia://en/241886) between two nearby points. When the curve is given as the graph of an [algebraic expression](wikipedia://en/4283745), calculus gives [formulas for the slope](wikipedia://en/7921) at each point. Slope is thus one of the central ideas of calculus and its applications to design.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

There seems to be no clear answer as to why the letter *m* is used for slope, but it first appears in English in [O'Brien](wikipedia://en/52691404) (1844)[^o5] who introduced the equation of a line as "*y* = *mx* + *b*", and it can also be found in [Todhunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Todhunter) (1888)[^o6] who wrote "*y* = *mx* + *c*".[^o7]

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Slope of lines illustrated.jpg.* Slope illustrated for *y* = (3/2)*x* − 1. Click on to enlarge

*Image: Gradient of a line in coordinates from -12x+2 to +12x+2.gif.* Slope of a line in coordinates system, from *f*(*x*) = −12*x* + 2 to *f*(*x*) = 12*x* + 2

The slope of a line in the plane containing the *x* and *y* axes is generally represented by the letter *m*,[^o10] and is defined as the change in the *y* coordinate divided by the corresponding change in the *x* coordinate, between two distinct points on the line. This is described by the following equation:

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{\text{vertical} \, \text{change} }{\text{horizontal} \, \text{change} }= \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}.$$

(The Greek letter *[delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_%28letter%29)*, Δ, is commonly used in mathematics to mean "difference" or "change".)

Given two points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$, the change in $x$ from one to the other is $x_2-x_1$ (*run*), while the change in $y$ is $y_2-y_1$ (*rise*). Substituting both quantities into the above equation generates the formula:

$$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}.$$

The formula fails for a vertical line, parallel to the $y$ axis (see [Division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663)), where the slope can be taken as [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824), so the slope of a vertical line is considered undefined.

::: {#o12 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Suppose a line runs through two points: *P* = (1, 2) and *Q* = (13, 8). By dividing the difference in $y$-coordinates by the difference in $x$-coordinates, one can obtain the slope of the line:

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{(8 - 2)}{(13 - 1)} = \frac{6}{12} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

Since the slope is positive, the direction of the line is increasing. Since |*m*| \< 1, the incline is not very steep (incline \< 45°).

As another example, consider a line which runs through the points (4, 15) and (3, 21). Then, the slope of the line is

$$m = \frac{ 21 - 15}{3 - 4} = \frac{6}{-1} = -6.$$

Since the slope is negative, the direction of the line is decreasing. Since |*m*| > 1, this decline is fairly steep (decline > 45°).

:::

## Algebra and geometry {#Algebra_and_geometry}

*Image: Slopes of Parallel and Perpendicular Lines.svg.* Slopes of parallel and perpendicular lines

If $y$ is a [linear function](wikipedia://en/152111) of $x$, then the coefficient of $x$ is the slope of the line created by plotting the function. Therefore, if the equation of the line is given in the form $y = mx + b$ then $m$ is the slope. This form of a line's equation is called the *slope-intercept form*, because $b$ can be interpreted as the [y-intercept](wikipedia://en/464331) of the line, that is, the $y$-coordinate where the line intersects the $y$-axis.; If the slope $m$ of a line and a point $(x_1, y_1)$ on the line are both known, then the equation of the line can be found using the [point-slope formula](wikipedia://en/17570#Point–slope_form): $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1).$; The slope of the line defined by the [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570) $ax + by + c = 0$ is $-\frac{a}{b}$.; Two lines are [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) if and only if they are not the same line (coincident) and either their slopes are equal or they both are vertical and therefore both have undefined slopes.; Two lines are [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) if the product of their slopes is −1 or one has a slope of 0 (a horizontal line) and the other has an undefined slope (a vertical line).; The angle θ between −90° and 90° that a line makes with the *x*-axis is related to the slope *m* as follows: $m = \tan(\theta)$ and $\theta = \arctan (m)$ (this is the inverse function of tangent; see [inverse trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/374220)).

::: {#o15 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

For example, consider a line running through points (2,8) and (3,20). This line has a slope, $m$, of

$$\frac {(20 - 8)}{(3 - 2)} = 12.$$

One can then write the line's equation, in point-slope form:

$$y - 8 = 12(x - 2) = 12x - 24.$$

or:

$$y = 12x - 16.$$

The angle θ between −90° and 90° that this line makes with the $x$-axis is

$$\theta = \arctan(12) \approx 85.2^{\circ} .$$

Consider the two lines: $y = -3x + 1$ and $y = -3x - 2$. Both lines have slope $m = -3$. They are not the same line. So they are parallel lines.

Consider the two lines $y = -3x + 1$ and $y = \tfrac{x}{3} - 2$. The slope of the first line is $m_{1} = -3$. The slope of the second line is $m_{2} = \tfrac{1}{3}$. The product of these two slopes is −1. So these two lines are perpendicular.

:::

## Statistics {#Statistics}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the gradient of the [least-squares regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares_regression) [best-fitting line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best-fitting_line) for a given [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) of data may be written as:

$$m = \frac{rs_y}{s_x},$$

This quantity *m* is called as the *[regression slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_slope)* for the line $y=mx+c$. The quantity $r$ is [Pearson's correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/221708), $s_y$ is the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) of the y-values and $s_x$ is the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) of the x-values. This may also be written as a ratio of [covariances](wikipedia://en/157059):[^o17]

$$m = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(Y,X)}{\operatorname{cov}(X,X)}$$

## Calculus {#Calculus}

*Image: Tangent function animation.gif.* At each point, the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is the slope of a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) that is [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) at that point. Note: the derivative at point A is [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) where green and dash–dot, [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) where red and dashed, and [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28number%29) where black and solid.

The concept of a slope is central to [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416). For non-linear functions, the rate of change varies along the curve. The [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the function at a point is the slope of the line [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the curve at the point and is thus equal to the rate of change of the function at that point.

If we let Δ*x* and Δ*y* be the distances (along the *x* and *y* axes, respectively) between two points on a curve, then the slope given by the above definition,

$$m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x},$$

is the slope of a [secant line](wikipedia://en/241886) to the curve. For a line, the secant between any two points is the line itself, but this is not the case for any other type of curve.

For example, the slope of the secant intersecting *y* = *x*<sup>2</sup> at (0,0) and (3,9) is 3. (The slope of the tangent at *x* = $\tfrac{3}{2}$ is also 3 − *a* consequence of the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662).)

By moving the two points closer together so that Δ*y* and Δ*x* decrease, the secant line more closely approximates a tangent line to the curve, and as such the slope of the secant approaches that of the tangent. Using [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), we can determine the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759), or the value that Δ*y*/Δ*x* approaches as Δ*y* and Δ*x* get closer to [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero); it follows that this limit is the exact slope of the tangent. If *y* is dependent on *x*, then it is sufficient to take the limit where only Δ*x* approaches zero. Therefore, the slope of the tangent is the limit of Δ*y*/Δ*x* as Δ*x* approaches zero, or d*y*/d*x*. We call this limit the [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28calculus%29).

$$\frac{\mathrm dy}{\mathrm dx} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$$

The value of the derivative at a specific point on the function provides us with the slope of the tangent at that precise location. For example, let *y* = *x*<sup>2</sup>. A point on this function is (−2,4). The derivative of this function is $\tfrac{dy}{dx}$ = 2*x*. So the slope of the line tangent to *y* at (−2,4) is 2 ⋅ (−2) = −4. The equation of this tangent line is: *y* − 4 = (−4)(*x* − (−2)) or *y* = −4*x* − 4.

## Difference of slopes {#Difference_of_slopes}

*Image: Missing\_square\_puzzle.svg.* The illusion of a paradox of area is dispelled by comparing slopes where blue and red triangles meet.

An extension of the idea of angle follows from the difference of slopes. Consider the [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405)

$$(u,v) = (x,y) \begin{pmatrix}1 & v \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then $(1,0)$ is mapped to $(1,v)$. The slope of $(1,0)$ is zero and the slope of $(1,v)$ is $v$. The shear mapping added a slope of $v$. For two points on $\{(1,y):y\in\mathbb{R}\}$ with slopes $m$ and $n$, the image

$$(1,y)\begin{pmatrix}1 & v \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = (1, y + v)$$

has slope increased by $v$, but the difference $n-m$ of slopes is the same before and after the shear. This invariance of slope differences makes slope an angular [invariant measure](wikipedia://en/7635201), on a par with circular angle (invariant under rotation) and [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981), with invariance group of [squeeze mappings](wikipedia://en/1140043).[^o20]

## Slope (pitch) of a roof {#Slope_(pitch)_of_a_roof}

The slope of a roof, traditionally and commonly called the [roof pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_pitch), in carpentry and architecture in the US is commonly described in terms of integer fractions of one foot (geometric tangent, rise over run), a legacy of British imperial measure. Other units are in use in other locales, with similar conventions. For details, see [roof pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_pitch).

## Slope of a road or railway {#Slope_of_a_road_or_railway}

There are two common ways to describe the steepness of a [road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road) or [railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_tracks). One is by the angle between 0° and 90° (in degrees), and the other is by the slope in a percentage. See also [steep grade railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steep_grade_railway) and [rack railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rack_railway).

The formulae for converting a slope given as a percentage into an angle in degrees and vice versa are:

$\text{angle} = \arctan \left( \frac{\text{slope}}{100\%} \right)$ (this is the inverse function of tangent; see [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261))

and

$$\mbox{slope} = 100\% \times \tan( \mbox{angle}),$$

where *angle* is in degrees and the trigonometric functions operate in degrees. For example, a slope of 100[%](wikipedia://en/1712680) or 1000[‰](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_mil) is an angle of 45°.

A third way is to give one unit of rise in say 10, 20, 50 or 100 horizontal units, e.g. 1:10. 1:20, 1:50 or 1:100 (or "1 *in* 10", "1 *in* 20", etc.) 1:10 is steeper than 1:20. For example, steepness of 20% means 1:5 or an incline with angle 11.3°.

Roads and railways have both longitudinal slopes and cross slopes.

## Other uses {#Other_uses}

The concept of a slope or gradient is also used as a basis for developing other applications in mathematics:

- [Gradient descent](wikipedia://en/201489), a first-order iterative optimization algorithm for finding the minimum of a function
- [Gradient theorem](wikipedia://en/5337437), theorem that a line integral through a gradient field can be evaluated by evaluating the original scalar field at the endpoints of the curve
- [Gradient method](wikipedia://en/15094152), an algorithm to solve problems with search directions defined by the gradient of the function at the current point
- [Conjugate gradient method](wikipedia://en/1448821), an algorithm for the numerical solution of particular systems of linear equations
- [Nonlinear conjugate gradient method](wikipedia://en/8980593), generalizes the conjugate gradient method to nonlinear optimization
- [Stochastic gradient descent](wikipedia://en/1180641), iterative method for optimizing a differentiable objective function
