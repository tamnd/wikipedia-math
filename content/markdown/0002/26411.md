# Ring homomorphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ring homomorphism"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **ring homomorphism** is a structure-preserving [function](wikipedia://en/185427) between two [rings](wikipedia://en/48404). More explicitly, if *R* and *S* are rings, then a ring homomorphism is a function $f : R \to S$ that preserves addition, multiplication and [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity); that is,[^n1][^n2][^n3][^n4][^n5]

:::

$$\begin{aligned} f(a+b)&= f(a) + f(b),\\ f(ab) &= f(a)f(b), \\ f(1) &= 1, \end{aligned}$$

for all *a*, *b* in *R*.

These conditions imply that additive inverses and the additive identity are also preserved (see [Group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396)).

If, in addition, is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942), then its [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) <sup>−1</sup> is also a ring homomorphism. In this case, is called a **ring isomorphism**, and the rings *R* and *S* are said to be *isomorphic*. From the standpoint of ring theory, isomorphic rings have exactly the same properties.

If *R* and *S* are [rngs](wikipedia://en/3405004), then the corresponding notion is that of a **rng homomorphism**, defined as above except without the third condition *f*(1<sub>*R*</sub>) = 1<sub>*S*</sub>. A rng homomorphism between (unital) rings need not be a ring homomorphism.

The [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two ring homomorphisms is a ring homomorphism. It follows that the rings form a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) with ring homomorphisms as [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) (see *[Category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181)*). In particular, one obtains the notions of ring endomorphism, ring isomorphism, and ring automorphism.

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Let *f* : *R* → *S* be a ring homomorphism. Then, directly from these definitions, one can deduce:

- *f*(0<sub>*R*</sub>) = 0<sub>*S*</sub>.
- *f*(−*a*) = −*f*(*a*) for all *a* in *R*.
- For any [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) *a* in *R*, *f*(*a*) is a unit element such that *f*(*a*)<sup>−1</sup> = *f*(*a*<sup>−1</sup>). In particular, *f* induces a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from the (multiplicative) group of units of *R* to the (multiplicative) group of units of *S* (or of im(*f*)).
- The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of *f*, denoted im(*f*), is a subring of *S*.
- The [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of *f*, defined as ker(*f*) = $\{a\text{ in } R | f(a) = 0_{S}\}$, is a [two-sided ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_ideal) in *R*. Every two-sided ideal in a ring *R* is the kernel of some ring homomorphism.
- A homomorphism is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) if and only if its kernel is the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal).
- The [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of *S* [divides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divides) the characteristic of *R*. This can sometimes be used to show that between certain rings *R* and *S*, no ring homomorphism *R* → *S* exists.
- If *R<sub>p</sub>* is the smallest [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) contained in *R* and *S<sub>p</sub>* is the smallest subring contained in *S*, then every ring homomorphism *f* : *R* → *S* induces a ring homomorphism *f<sub>p</sub>* : *R<sub>p</sub>* → *S<sub>p</sub>*.
- If *R* is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) and *S* is not the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874), then is injective.
- If both *R* and *S* are [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), then im(*f*) is a subfield of *S*, so *S* can be viewed as a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) of *R*.
- If *I* is an ideal of *S* then <sup>−1</sup>(*I*) is an ideal of *R*.
- If *R* and *S* are commutative and *P* is a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) of *S* then <sup>−1</sup>(*P*) is a prime ideal of *R*.
- If *R* and *S* are commutative, *M* is a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of *S*, and is surjective, then <sup>−1</sup>(*M*) is a maximal ideal of *R*.
- If *R* and *S* are commutative and *S* is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), then ker(*f*) is a prime ideal of *R*.
- If *R* and *S* are commutative, *S* is a field, and is surjective, then ker(*f*) is a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of *R*.
- If is surjective, *P* is prime (maximal) ideal in *R* and ker(*f*) ⊆ *P*, then *f*(*P*) is prime (maximal) ideal in *S*.

Moreover,

- The composition of ring homomorphisms *S* → *T* and *R* → *S* is a ring homomorphism *R* → *T*.
- For each ring *R*, the identity map *R* → *R* is a ring homomorphism.
- Therefore, the class of all rings together with ring homomorphisms forms a category, the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181).
- The zero map *R* → *S* that sends every element of *R* to 0 is a ring homomorphism only if *S* is the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) (the ring whose only element is zero).
- For every ring *R*, there is a unique ring homomorphism **Z** → *R*. This says that the ring of integers is an [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object) in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of rings.
- For every ring *R*, there is a unique ring homomorphism from *R* to the zero ring. This says that the zero ring is a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) in the category of rings.
- As the initial object is not isomorphic to the terminal object, there is no [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object) in the category of rings; in particular, the zero ring is not a zero object in the category of rings.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The function *f* : **Z** → **Z**/*n***Z**, defined by *f*(*a*) = \[*a*\]<sub>*n*</sub> = *a* mod *n* is a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) ring homomorphism with kernel *n***Z** (see *[Modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087)*).
- The [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) **C** → **C** is a ring homomorphism (this is an example of a ring automorphism).
- For a ring *R* of prime characteristic *p*, *R* → *R*, *x* → <sup>*p*</sup> is a ring endomorphism called the [Frobenius endomorphism](wikipedia://en/1088270).
- If *R* and *S* are rings, the zero function from *R* to *S* is a ring homomorphism if and only if *S* is the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) (otherwise it fails to map 1<sub>*R*</sub> to 1<sub>*S*</sub>). On the other hand, the zero function is always a rng homomorphism.
- If **R**\[*X*\] denotes the ring of all [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in the variable *X* with coefficients in the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) **R**, and **C** denotes the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), then the function *f* : **R**\[*X*\] → **C** defined by *f*(*p*) = *p*(*i*) (substitute the imaginary unit *i* for the variable *X* in the polynomial *p*) is a surjective ring homomorphism. The kernel of *f* consists of all polynomials in **R**\[*X*\] that are divisible by <sup>2</sup> + 1.
- If *f* : *R* → *S* is a ring homomorphism between the rings *R* and *S*, then *f* induces a ring homomorphism between the [matrix rings](wikipedia://en/487541) M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) → M<sub>*n*</sub>(*S*).
- Let *V* be a vector space over a field *k*. Then the map *ρ* : *k* → End(*V*) given by *ρ*(*a*)*v* = *av* is a ring homomorphism. More generally, given an abelian group *M*, a module structure on *M* over a ring *R* is equivalent to giving a ring homomorphism *R* → End(*M*).
- A unital [algebra homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) between unital [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112) over a commutative ring *R* is a ring homomorphism that is also [*R*-linear](wikipedia://en/474118).

:::

## Non-examples {#Non-examples}

- The function *f* : **Z**/6**Z** → **Z**/6**Z** defined by *f*(\[*a*\]<sub>6</sub>) = \[4*a*\]<sub>6</sub> is not a ring homomorphism, but is a rng homomorphism (and rng endomorphism), with kernel 3**Z**/6**Z** and image 2**Z**/6**Z** (which is isomorphic to **Z**/3**Z**).
- There is no ring homomorphism **Z**/*n***Z** → **Z** for any *n* ≥ 1.
- If *R* and *S* are rings, the inclusion *R* → *R* × *S* that sends each *r* to (*r*,0) is a rng homomorphism, but not a ring homomorphism (if *S* is not the zero ring), since it does not map the multiplicative identity 1 of *R* to the multiplicative identity (1,1) of *R* × *S*.

:::

## Category of rings {#Category_of_rings}

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ring isomorphism"}

### Endomorphisms, isomorphisms, and automorphisms {#Endomorphisms,_isomorphisms,_and_automorphisms}

- A **ring endomorphism** is a ring homomorphism from a ring to itself.
- A **ring isomorphism** is a ring homomorphism having a 2-sided inverse that is also a ring homomorphism. One can prove that a ring homomorphism is an isomorphism if and only if it is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) as a function on the underlying sets. If there exists a ring isomorphism between two rings *R* and *S*, then *R* and *S* are called **isomorphic**. Isomorphic rings differ only by a relabeling of elements. Example: Up to isomorphism, there are four rings of order 4. (This means that there are four pairwise non-isomorphic rings of order 4 such that every other ring of order 4 is isomorphic to one of them.) On the other hand, up to isomorphism, there are eleven [rngs](wikipedia://en/3405004) of order 4.
- A **ring automorphism** is a ring isomorphism from a ring to itself.

:::

### Monomorphisms and epimorphisms {#Monomorphisms_and_epimorphisms}

Injective ring homomorphisms are identical to [monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) in the category of rings: If *f* : *R* → *S* is a monomorphism that is not injective, then it sends some *r*<sub>1</sub> and *r*<sub>2</sub> to the same element of *S*. Consider the two maps *g*<sub>1</sub> and *g*<sub>2</sub> from **Z**\[*x*\] to *R* that map *x* to *r*<sub>1</sub> and *r*<sub>2</sub>, respectively; *f* ∘ *g*<sub>1</sub> and *f* ∘ *g*<sub>2</sub> are identical, but since is a monomorphism this is impossible.

However, surjective ring homomorphisms are vastly different from [epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/59539) in the category of rings. For example, the inclusion **Z** ⊆ **Q** with the identity mapping is a ring epimorphism, but not a surjection. However, every ring epimorphism is also a [strong epimorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_epimorphism), the converse being true in every category.
