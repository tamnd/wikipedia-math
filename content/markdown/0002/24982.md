# Psychometrics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Psychometrics"}

**Psychometrics** is a field of study within [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) concerned with the theory and technique of [measurement](wikipedia://en/19022). Psychometrics generally covers specialized fields within psychology and education devoted to testing, measurement, assessment, and related activities.[^o2] Psychometrics is concerned with the objective measurement of [latent constructs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_variable) that cannot be directly observed. Examples of latent constructs include [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence), [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality) factors (e.g., [introversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introversion)), [mental disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder), and [educational achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_measurement).[^o3] The levels of individuals on nonobservable latent variables are [inferred](wikipedia://en/27577) through [mathematical modeling](wikipedia://en/20590) based on what is observed from individuals' responses to items on tests and scales.[^o3]

:::

Practitioners are described as **psychometricians**, although not all who engage in psychometric research go by this title. Most psychometricians are [psychologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychologists) with advanced graduate training in psychometrics and measurement theory. According to the *Dictionary of Psychology,* a psychometrician "is an individual with a theoretical knowledge of measurement techniques who is qualified to develop, evaluate, and improve psychological tests."[^o4] In addition to traditional academic institutions, psychometricians also work for organizations, such as [Pearson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_Education) and the [Educational Testing Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_Testing_Service) as well as independent consultants. Some psychometric researchers focus on the construction and validation of assessment instruments, including [surveys](wikipedia://en/27578), [scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance), and [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-ended_question)- or [closed-ended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-ended_question) [questionnaires](wikipedia://en/956483). Others focus on research relating to measurement theory (e.g., [item response theory](wikipedia://en/420159), [intraclass correlation](wikipedia://en/11391832)) or specialize as [learning and development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_and_development) professionals.

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word psychometry derives from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): ψυχή, *psukhē*, "spirit, soul" and μέτρον, *metron*, "measure". The American academic [Joseph Rodes Buchanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Rodes_Buchanan) is credited as having first coined the word "psychometry" in 1842 in connection with his investigation of [paranormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychometry_%28paranormal%29) phenomena rather than the rational quantification of [psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological) criteria.

## Historical foundation {#Historical_foundation}

Rational psychological testing has come from two streams of thought: the first, from [Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Darwin), [Galton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Francis_Galton), and [Cattell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_McKeen_Cattell), on the measurement of individual differences and the second, from [Herbart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.E._Herbart), [Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.H._Weber), [Fechner](wikipedia://en/203499), and [Wundt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wundt) and their psychophysical measurements of a similar construct. The second set of individuals and their research is what has led to the development of [experimental psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_psychology) and standardized testing.[^o8]

### Victorian stream {#Victorian_stream}

Charles Darwin was the inspiration behind Francis Galton, a scientist who advanced the development of psychometrics. In 1859, Darwin published his book *[On the Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Origin_of_Species)*. Darwin described the role of natural selection in the emergence, over time, of different populations of species of plants and animals. The book showed how individual members of a [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species) differ among themselves and how they possess characteristics that are more or less adaptive to their environment. Those with more adaptive characteristics are more likely to survive to procreate and give rise to another generation. Those with less adaptive characteristics are less likely. These ideas stimulated Galton's interest in the study of human beings and how they differ one from another and how to measure those differences.

Galton wrote a book entitled *[Hereditary Genius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_Genius)* which was first published in 1869. The book described different characteristics that people possess and how those characteristics make some more "fit" than others. Today these differences, such as sensory and motor functioning (reaction time, visual acuity, and physical strength), are important domains of scientific psychology. Much of the early theoretical and applied work in psychometrics was undertaken in an attempt to measure [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_%28trait%29). Galton, often referred to as "the father of psychometrics," devised and included mental tests among his [anthropometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropometric) measures. [James McKeen Cattell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_McKeen_Cattell), a pioneer in the field of psychometrics, went on to extend Galton's work. Cattell coined the term *mental test*, and is responsible for research and knowledge that ultimately led to the development of modern tests.[^o8]

### German stream {#German_stream}

The origin of psychometrics also has connections to the related field of [psychophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychophysics). Around the same time that Darwin, Galton, and Cattell were making their discoveries, Herbart was also interested in "unlocking the mysteries of human consciousness" through the scientific method.[^o8] Herbart was responsible for creating mathematical models of the mind, which were influential in educational practices for years to come.

[E.H. Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Heinrich_Weber) built upon Herbart's work and tried to prove the existence of a psychological threshold, saying that a minimum stimulus was necessary to activate a [sensory system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensory_nervous_system). After Weber, [G.T. Fechner](wikipedia://en/203499) expanded upon the knowledge he gleaned from Herbart and Weber to devise the law that the strength of a sensation grows as the logarithm of the stimulus intensity. A follower of Weber and Fechner, [Wilhelm Wundt](wikipedia://en/34128) is credited with founding the science of psychology. It is Wundt's influence that paved the way for others to develop psychological testing.[^o8]

### 20th century {#20th_century}

In 1936, the psychometrician [L. L. Thurstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._L._Thurstone), a co-founder and first president of the Psychometric Society, developed and applied a theoretical approach to measurement referred to as the [law of comparative judgment](wikipedia://en/2251120), an approach that has close connections to the psychophysical theory of [Ernst Heinrich Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Heinrich_Weber) and [Gustav Fechner](wikipedia://en/203499). In addition, Spearman and Thurstone both made important contributions to the theory and application of [factor analysis](wikipedia://en/253492), a statistical method developed and used extensively in psychometrics.[^o11] In the late 1950s, [Leopold Szondi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Szondi) made a historical and epistemological assessment of the impact of statistical thinking on psychology during the previous few decades: "in the last decades, the specifically psychological thinking has been almost completely suppressed and removed, and replaced by a statistical thinking. Precisely here we see the cancer of testology and testomania of today."[^o12]

More recently, psychometric theory has been applied in the measurement of personality, [attitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attitude_%28psychology%29) and [beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief), and [academic achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_achievement). These latent constructs cannot truly be measured, and much of the research and science in this discipline has been developed in an attempt to measure these constructs as close to the true score as possible.

Figures who made significant contributions to psychometrics include Paul Horst, [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695), Henry F. Kaiser, [Carl Brigham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Brigham), [L. L. Thurstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._L._Thurstone), [E. L. Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._L._Thorndike), [Georg Rasch](wikipedia://en/3253022), [Eugene Galanter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Galanter), [Johnson O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_O'Connor), [Frederic M. Lord](wikipedia://en/16006063), [Ledyard R Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ledyard_R_Tucker), [Louis Guttman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Guttman), and [Jane Loevinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Loevinger).

## Definition of measurement in the social sciences {#Definition_of_measurement_in_the_social_sciences}

The definition of measurement in the social sciences has a long history. A current widespread definition, proposed by [Stanley Smith Stevens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Smith_Stevens), is that measurement is "the assignment of numerals to objects or events according to some rule". This definition was introduced in a 1946 *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_%28journal%29)* article in which Stevens proposed four [levels of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels_of_measurement).[^o14] Although widely adopted, this definition differs in important respects from the more classical definition of measurement adopted in the physical sciences, namely that scientific measurement entails "the estimation or discovery of the ratio of some magnitude of a quantitative attribute to a unit of the same attribute" (p. 358)[^o15]

Indeed, Stevens's definition of measurement was put forward in response to the British Ferguson Committee, whose chair, A. Ferguson, was a physicist. The committee was appointed in 1932 by the British Association for the Advancement of Science to investigate the possibility of quantitatively estimating sensory events. Although its chair and other members were physicists, the committee also included several psychologists. The committee's report highlighted the importance of the definition of measurement. While Stevens's response was to propose a new definition, which has had considerable influence in the field, this was by no means the only response to the report. Another, notably different, response was to accept the classical definition, as reflected in the following statement:

Measurement in psychology and physics are in no sense different. Physicists can measure when they can find the operations by which they may meet the necessary criteria; psychologists have to do the same. They need not worry about the mysterious differences between the meaning of measurement in the two sciences (Reese, 1943, p. 49).[^o16]

These divergent responses are reflected in alternative approaches to measurement. For example, methods based on [covariance matrices](wikipedia://en/191752) are typically employed on the premise that numbers, such as raw scores derived from assessments, are measurements. Such approaches implicitly entail Stevens's definition of measurement, which requires only that numbers are *assigned* according to some rule. The main research task, then, is generally considered to be the discovery of associations between scores, and of factors posited to underlie such associations.[^o17]

On the other hand, when measurement models such as the [Rasch model](wikipedia://en/2161615) are employed, numbers are not assigned based on a rule. Instead, in keeping with Reese's statement above, specific criteria for measurement are stated, and the goal is to construct procedures or operations that provide data that meet the relevant criteria. Measurements are estimated based on the models, and tests are conducted to ascertain whether the relevant criteria have been met.

## Instruments and procedures {#Instruments_and_procedures}

The first psychometric instruments were designed to measure [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_%28trait%29).[^o19] One early approach to measuring intelligence was the test developed in France by [Alfred Binet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Binet) and [Theodore Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Simon). That test was known as the Test Binet-Simon. The French test was adapted for use in the U.S. by [Lewis Terman](wikipedia://en/1583393) of Stanford University, and named the [Stanford-Binet IQ test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford-Binet_IQ_test).

Another major focus in psychometrics has been on [personality testing](wikipedia://en/609194). There has been a range of theoretical approaches to conceptualizing and measuring personality, though there is no widely agreed upon theory. Some of the better-known instruments include the [Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Multiphasic_Personality_Inventory), the [Five-Factor Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_five_personality_traits) (or "Big 5") and tools such as [Personality and Preference Inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_and_Preference_Inventory) and the [Myers–Briggs Type Indicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myers–Briggs_Type_Indicator). Attitudes have also been studied extensively using psychometric approaches.[^o20] An alternative method involves the application of unfolding measurement models, the most general being the Hyperbolic Cosine Model (Andrich & Luo, 1993).[^o21]

## Theoretical approaches {#Theoretical_approaches}

Psychometricians have developed a number of different measurement theories. These include [classical test theory](wikipedia://en/419859) (CTT) and [item response theory](wikipedia://en/420159) (IRT).[^o23][^o24] An approach that seems mathematically to be similar to IRT but also quite distinctive, in terms of its origins and features, is represented by the [Rasch model](wikipedia://en/2161615) for measurement. The development of the Rasch model, and the broader class of models to which it belongs, was explicitly founded on requirements of measurement in the physical sciences.[^o25]

Psychometricians have also developed methods for working with large matrices of correlations and covariances. Techniques in this general tradition include [factor analysis](wikipedia://en/253492),[^o26] a method of determining the underlying dimensions of data. One of the main challenges faced by users of factor analysis is a lack of consensus on appropriate procedures for [determining the number of latent factors](wikipedia://en/253492#Criteria_for_determining_the_number_of_factors).[^o27] A usual procedure is to stop factoring when [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) drop below one because the original sphere shrinks. The lack of clear cutting points concerns other multivariate methods as well.[^o28]

[Multidimensional scaling](wikipedia://en/398786)[^o29] is a method for finding a simple representation for data with a large number of latent dimensions. [Cluster analysis](wikipedia://en/669675) is an approach to finding objects that are like each other. Factor analysis, multidimensional scaling, and cluster analysis are all multivariate descriptive methods used to distill from large amounts of data simpler structures.

More recently, [structural equation modeling](wikipedia://en/2007748)[^o30] and [path analysis](wikipedia://en/590893) represent more sophisticated approaches to working with large [covariance matrices](wikipedia://en/191752). These methods allow statistically sophisticated models to be fitted to data and tested to determine if they are adequate fits. Because at a granular level psychometric research is concerned with the extent and nature of multidimensionality in each of the items of interest, a relatively new procedure known as bi-factor analysis[^o31][^o32][^o33] can be helpful. Bi-factor analysis can decompose "an item's systematic variance in terms of, ideally, two sources, a general factor and one source of additional systematic variance."[^o34]

### Key concepts {#Key_concepts}

Key concepts in classical test theory are [reliability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliability_%28psychometric%29) and [validity](wikipedia://en/21526661). A reliable measure is one that measures a construct consistently across time, individuals, and situations. A valid measure is one that measures what it is intended to measure. Reliability is necessary, but not sufficient, for validity.

Both reliability and validity can be assessed statistically. Consistency over repeated measures of the same test can be assessed with the Pearson correlation coefficient, and is often called *test-retest reliability.*[^o36] Similarly, the equivalence of different versions of the same measure can be indexed by a [Pearson correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient), and is called *equivalent forms reliability* or a similar term.[^o36]

Internal consistency, which addresses the homogeneity of a single test form, may be assessed by correlating performance on two halves of a test, which is termed *split-half reliability*; the value of this [Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient) for two half-tests is adjusted with the [Spearman–Brown prediction formula](wikipedia://en/409802) to correspond to the correlation between two full-length tests.[^o36] Perhaps the most commonly used index of reliability is [Cronbach's α](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cronbach's_α), which is equivalent to the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of all possible split-half coefficients. Other approaches include the [intraclass correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intra-class_correlation), which is the ratio of variance of measurements of a given target to the variance of all targets.

There are a number of different forms of validity. [Criterion-related validity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criterion_validity) refers to the extent to which a test or scale predicts a sample of behavior, i.e., the criterion, that is "external to the measuring instrument itself."[^o37] That external sample of behavior can be many things including another test; college grade point average as when the high school SAT is used to predict performance in college; and even behavior that occurred in the past, for example, when a test of current psychological symptoms is used to predict the occurrence of past victimization (which would accurately represent postdiction). When the criterion measure is collected at the same time as the measure being validated the goal is to establish *[concurrent validity](wikipedia://en/2846373)*; when the criterion is collected later the goal is to establish *[predictive validity](wikipedia://en/794350)*. A measure has *[construct validity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construct_validity)* if it is related to measures of other constructs as required by theory. *[Content validity](wikipedia://en/2229899)* is a demonstration that the items of a test do an adequate job of covering the domain being measured. In a personnel selection example, test content is based on a defined statement or set of statements of knowledge, skill, ability, or other characteristics obtained from a *[job analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_analysis)*.

[Item response theory](wikipedia://en/420159) models the relationship between [latent traits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_trait) and responses to test items. Among other advantages, IRT provides a basis for obtaining an estimate of the location of a test-taker on a given latent trait as well as the standard error of measurement of that location. For example, a university student's knowledge of history can be deduced from his or her score on a university test and then be compared reliably with a high school student's knowledge deduced from a less difficult test. Scores derived by classical test theory do not have this characteristic, and assessment of actual ability (rather than ability relative to other test-takers) must be assessed by comparing scores to those of a "norm group" randomly selected from the population. In fact, all measures derived from classical test theory are dependent on the sample tested, while, in principle, those derived from item response theory are not.

## Standards of quality {#Standards_of_quality}

The considerations of [validity](wikipedia://en/239140) and [reliability](wikipedia://en/239138) typically are viewed as essential elements for determining the [quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_%28business%29) of any test. However, professional and practitioner associations have frequently placed these concerns within broader contexts when developing [standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standards_organization) and evaluating overall test quality within a given context. A concern in many applied research settings is whether the metric of a given psychological inventory is meaningful or arbitrary.[^o39]

### Testing standards {#Testing_standards}

In 2014, the American Educational Research Association (AERA), American Psychological Association (APA), and National Council on Measurement in Education (NCME) published a revision of the *[Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing](wikipedia://en/4593659)*,[^o41] which describes standards for test development, evaluation, and use. The *Standards* cover essential topics in testing including validity, reliability/errors of measurement, and fairness in testing. The book also establishes standards related to testing operations—including test design and development, scores, scales, norms, score linking, cut scores, test administration, scoring, reporting, score interpretation, test documentation, and rights and responsibilities of test takers and test users. Finally, the *Standards* cover topics related to testing applications, including [psychological testing and assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_testing), workplace testing and [credentialing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_certification), [educational testing and assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_%28student_assessment%29), and testing in [program evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_evaluation) and public policy.

### Evaluation standards {#Evaluation_standards}

In the field of [evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaluation), and in particular [educational evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_evaluation), the [Joint Committee on Standards for Educational Evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Committee_on_Standards_for_Educational_Evaluation)[^o43] has published three sets of standards for evaluations. *The Personnel Evaluation Standards*[^o44] was published in 1988, *The Program Evaluation Standards* (2nd edition)[^o45] was published in 1994, and *The Student Evaluation Standards*[^o46] was published in 2003.

Each publication presents and elaborates a set of standards for use in a variety of educational settings. The standards provide guidelines for designing, implementing, assessing, and improving the identified form of evaluation.[^o47] Each of the standards has been placed in one of four fundamental categories to promote educational evaluations that are proper, useful, feasible, and accurate. In these sets of standards, validity and reliability considerations are covered under the accuracy topic. For example, the student accuracy standards help ensure that student evaluations will provide sound, accurate, and credible information about student learning and performance.

## Controversy and criticism {#Controversy_and_criticism}

Because psychometrics is based on [latent psychological processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_variable) measured through [correlations](wikipedia://en/157057) and statistical inference, there has long been controversy surrounding the extent to which psychological traits can truly be measured with scientific precision.[^o50] Critics, including practitioners in the [physical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_sciences), argue that constructs such as intelligence, personality, emotional stability, or psychological adjustment are abstract human experiences rather than directly observable physical properties. As a result, psychometric measurement depends heavily on statistical modelling, probability theory, sampling assumptions, correlations, and interpretive frameworks rather than exact measurement in the physical-science sense. Statistical conclusions in psychology are therefore probabilistic rather than absolute, meaning that test scores represent estimated interpretations shaped by theory, methodology, culture, environment, and context.[^o51] Critics further argue that the historical foundations of psychometrics complicate claims of neutrality and objectivity. Influential intelligence theorists such as [Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072), [Charles Spearman](wikipedia://en/1290799), and [Lewis Terman](wikipedia://en/1583393) were advocates of [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics), and their assumptions significantly shaped early theories of intelligence and human difference.[^o52][^o53] Critics therefore argue that intelligence testing cannot be fully separated from the racial and social assumptions embedded within its historical development. Although [IQ](wikipedia://en/14892) testing continues to be widely used in psychology and education, critics increasingly question the assumption that intelligence can be measured as a culturally neutral and objective biological trait. [David Wechsler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Wechsler) defined intelligence as the "aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment," yet critics note that different cultures, socioeconomic conditions, educational systems, and social environments may value different forms of reasoning, adaptation, communication, and problem-solving.

The *Standards for Educational and Psychological Measurement* states that "validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores entailed by proposed uses of tests".[^o54] Simply put, a test is not valid unless it is interpreted and used in the manner for which it was designed.[^o55] Even tests with acceptable reliability and validity remain dependent upon theoretical assumptions regarding what is being measured and how those measurements should be interpreted. Critics argue that psychometric testing developed primarily within Western and white-majority academic traditions has often constructed standards of "normality" around dominant cultural experiences while positioning deviation from those norms as deficient or abnormal. In continuing to rely upon such measures without sufficiently critiquing the assumptions embedded within them, psychology may unintentionally legitimize [racialized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racialized) interpretations of intelligence, achievement, behaviour, and mental health. Such assumptions can contribute to unequal educational placement, biased clinical interpretation, discriminatory occupational screening, and disparities in healthcare treatment. [Lee Cronbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Cronbach) noted in *[American Psychologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Psychologist)* (1957) that "correlational psychology, though fully as old as experimentation, was slower to mature. It qualifies equally as a discipline, however, because it asks a distinctive type of question and has technical methods of examining whether the question has been properly put and the data properly interpreted." He further argued that "The correlation method, for its part, can study what man has not learned to control or can never hope to control ... A true federation of the disciplines is required. Kept independent, they can give only wrong answers or no answers at all regarding certain important problems."[^o56]

Two major categories of tools used to measure [personality traits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_traits) are [objective tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_test) and [projective measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_test). Examples include the [Big Five Inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Five_personality_traits) (BFI), [Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Multiphasic_Personality_Inventory) (MMPI-2), [Rorschach Inkblot test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rorschach_test), [Neurotic Personality Questionnaire KON-2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotic_Personality_Questionnaire_KON-2006),[^o57] and [Eysenck Personality Questionnaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eysenck_Personality_Questionnaire). Some psychometric measures demonstrate acceptable [reliability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliability_%28research_methods%29) and [validity](wikipedia://en/21526661), while others remain controversial. [The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myers–Briggs_Type_Indicator) (MBTI), for example, has been criticized for questionable validity. Psychometric specialist [Robert Hogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hogan_%28psychologist%29) wrote that "Most personality psychologists regard the MBTI as little more than an elaborate Chinese fortune cookie."[^o58]

## Non-human: animals and machines {#Non-human:_animals_and_machines}

Psychometrics addresses *human* abilities, attitudes, traits, and educational evolution. Notably, the study of behavior, mental processes, and abilities of non-human *animals* is usually addressed by [comparative psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_psychology), or with a continuum between non-human animals and the rest of animals by [evolutionary psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_psychology). Nonetheless, there are some advocators for a more gradual transition between the approach taken for humans and the approach taken for (non-human) animals.[^o60][^o61][^o62][^o63]

The evaluation of abilities, traits and learning evolution of *machines* has been mostly unrelated to the case of humans and non-human animals, with specific approaches in the area of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence). A more integrated approach, under the name of [universal psychometrics](wikipedia://en/69898089), has also been proposed.[^o64][^o65]
