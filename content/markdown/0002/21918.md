# Normal subgroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Normal subgroup"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **normal subgroup** (also known as an **invariant subgroup** or **self-conjugate subgroup**)[^n1] is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) that is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under [conjugation](wikipedia://en/61839) by members of the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of which it is a part. In other words, a subgroup $N$ of the group $G$ is normal in $G$ if and only if $gng^{-1} \in N$ for all $g \in G$ and $n \in N.$ The usual notation for this relation is $N \triangleleft G.$

:::

Normal subgroups are important because they (and only they) can be used to construct [quotient groups](wikipedia://en/11526) of the given group. Furthermore, the normal subgroups of $G$ are precisely the [kernels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_of_a_homomorphism) of [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) with [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) $G,$ which means that they can be used to internally classify those homomorphisms.

[Évariste Galois](wikipedia://en/9815) was the first to realize the importance of the existence of normal subgroups.[^n2]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $N$ of a group $G$ is called a **normal subgroup** of $G$ if it is invariant under [conjugation](wikipedia://en/61839); that is, the conjugation of an element of $N$ by an element of $G$ is always in $N.$[^n3] The usual notation for this relation is $N \triangleleft G.$

### Equivalent conditions {#Equivalent_conditions}

For any subgroup $N$ of $G,$ the following conditions are [equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) to $N$ being a normal subgroup of $G.$ Therefore, any one of them may be taken as the definition.

- The image of conjugation of $N$ by any element of $G$ is a subset of $N,$[^n4] i.e., $gNg^{-1}\subseteq N$ for all $g\in G$.
- The image of conjugation of $N$ by any element of $G$ is equal to $N,$[^n4] i.e., $gNg^{-1}= N$ for all $g\in G$.
- For all $g \in G,$ the left and right [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) $gN$ and $Ng$ are equal.[^n4]
- The sets of left and right [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) of $N$ in $G$ coincide.[^n4]
- Multiplication in $G$ preserves the equivalence relation "is in the same left coset as". That is, for every $g,g',h,h'\in G$ satisfying $g N = g' N$ and $h N = h' N$, we have $(g h) N = (g' h') N.$
- There exists a group on the set of left cosets of $N$ where multiplication of any two left cosets $gN$ and $hN$ yields the left coset $(gh)N$. (This group is called the *quotient group* of $G$ *modulo* $N$, denoted $G/N$.)
- $N$ is a [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176) of $G.$[^n2]
- $N$ is preserved by the [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839) of $G.$[^n5]
- There is some [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $G \to H$ whose [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is $N.$[^n2]
- There exists a group homomorphism $\phi:G \to H$ whose [fibers](wikipedia://en/6603892) form a group where the identity element is $N$ and multiplication of any two fibers $\phi^{-1}(h_1)$ and $\phi^{-1}(h_2)$ yields the fiber $\phi^{-1}(h_1 h_2)$. (This group is the same group $G/N$ mentioned above.)
- There is some [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167) on $G$ for which the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) is $N$.
- For all $n\in N$ and $g\in G,$ the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) $[n,g] = n^{-1} g^{-1} n g$ is in $N.$
- Any two elements commute modulo the normal subgroup membership relation. That is, for all $g, h \in G,$ $g h \in N$ if and only if $h g \in N.$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

For any group $G,$ the trivial subgroup $\{ e \}$ consisting of just the identity element of $G$ is always a normal subgroup of $G.$ Likewise, $G$ itself is always a normal subgroup of $G.$ (If these are the only normal subgroups, then $G$ is said to be [simple](wikipedia://en/47600).)[^n6] Other named normal subgroups of an arbitrary group include the [center of the group](wikipedia://en/7125) (the set of elements that commute with all other elements) and the [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) $[G,G].$[^n7][^n8] More generally, since conjugation is an isomorphism, any [characteristic subgroup](wikipedia://en/7198) is a normal subgroup.[^n9]

If $G$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) then every subgroup $N$ of $G$ is normal, because $gN = \{gn\}_{n\in N} = \{ng\}_{n\in N} = Ng.$ More generally, for any group $G$, every subgroup of the *center* $Z(G)$ of $G$ is normal in $G$. (In the special case that $G$ is abelian, the center is all of $G$, hence the fact that all subgroups of an abelian group are normal.) A group that is not abelian but for which every subgroup is normal is called a [Hamiltonian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_group).[^n10]

A concrete example of a normal subgroup is the subgroup $N = \{(1), (123), (132)\}$ of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_3,$ consisting of the identity and both three-cycles. In particular, one can check that every coset of $N$ is either equal to $N$ itself or is equal to $(12)N = \{ (12), (23), (13)\}.$ On the other hand, the subgroup $H = \{(1), (12)\}$ is not normal in $S_3$ since $(123)H = \{(123), (13) \} \neq \{(123), (23) \} = H(123).$[^n11] This illustrates the general fact that any subgroup $H \leq G$ of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) two is normal.

As an example of a normal subgroup within a [matrix group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_group), consider the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbf{R})$ of all invertible $n\times n$ matrices with real entries under the operation of matrix multiplication and its subgroup $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbf{R})$ of all $n\times n$ matrices of [determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant_%28mathematics%29) 1 (the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823)). To see why the subgroup $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbf{R})$ is normal in $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbf{R})$, consider any matrix $X$ in $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbf{R})$ and any invertible matrix $A$. Then using the two important identities $\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)$ and $\det(A^{-1})=\det(A)^{-1}$, one has that $\det(AXA^{-1}) = \det(A) \det(X) \det(A)^{-1} = \det(X) = 1$, and so $AXA^{-1} \in \mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbf{R})$ as well. This means $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbf{R})$ is closed under conjugation in $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbf{R})$, so it is a normal subgroup.

In the [Rubik's Cube group](wikipedia://en/2467690), the subgroups consisting of operations which only affect the orientations of either the corner pieces or the edge pieces are normal.[^n12]

The [translation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_group) is a normal subgroup of the [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164) in any dimension.[^n13] This means: applying a rigid transformation, followed by a translation and then the inverse rigid transformation, has the same effect as a single translation. By contrast, the subgroup of all [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) about the origin is *not* a normal subgroup of the Euclidean group, as long as the dimension is at least 2: first translating, then rotating about the origin, and then translating back will typically not fix the origin and will therefore not have the same effect as a single rotation about the origin.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- If $H$ is a normal subgroup of $G,$ and $K$ is a subgroup of $G$ containing $H,$ then $H$ is a normal subgroup of $K.$[^n14]
- A normal subgroup of a normal subgroup of a group need not be normal in the group. That is, normality is not a [transitive relation](wikipedia://en/200463). The smallest group exhibiting this phenomenon is the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of order 8.[^n15] However, a [characteristic subgroup](wikipedia://en/7198) of a normal subgroup is normal.[^n16] A group in which normality is transitive is called a [T-group](wikipedia://en/10545261).[^n17]
- The two groups $G$ and $H$ are normal subgroups of their [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) $G \times H.$
- If the group $G$ is a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) $G = N \rtimes H,$ then $N$ is normal in $G,$ though $H$ need not be normal in $G.$
- If $M$ and $N$ are normal subgroups of an additive group $G$ such that $G = M + N$ and $M \cap N = \{0\}$, then $G = M \oplus N.$[^n18]
- Normality is preserved under surjective homomorphisms;[^n19] that is, if $G \to H$ is a surjective group homomorphism and $N$ is normal in $G,$ then the image $f(N)$ is normal in $H.$
- Normality is preserved by taking [inverse images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_image);[^n19] that is, if $G \to H$ is a group homomorphism and $N$ is normal in $H,$ then the inverse image $f^{-1}(N)$ is normal in $G.$
- Normality is preserved on taking [direct products](wikipedia://en/3706015);[^n20] that is, if $N_1 \triangleleft G_1$ and $N_2 \triangleleft G_2,$ then $N_1 \times N_2\; \triangleleft \;G_1 \times G_2.$
- Every subgroup of [index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_%28group_theory%29) 2 is normal. More generally, a subgroup, $H,$ of finite index, $n,$ in $G$ contains a subgroup, $K,$ normal in $G$ and of index dividing $n!$ called the [normal core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_core). In particular, if $p$ is the smallest prime dividing the order of $G,$ then every subgroup of index $p$ is normal.[^n21]
- The fact that normal subgroups of $G$ are precisely the kernels of group homomorphisms defined on $G$ accounts for some of the importance of normal subgroups; they are a way to internally classify all homomorphisms defined on a group. For example, a non-identity finite group is [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) if and only if it is isomorphic to all of its non-identity homomorphic images,[^n22] a finite group is [perfect](wikipedia://en/585271) if and only if it has no normal subgroups of prime [index](wikipedia://en/230581), and a group is [imperfect](wikipedia://en/4939209) if and only if the [derived subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_subgroup) is not supplemented by any proper normal subgroup.

### Lattice of normal subgroups {#Lattice_of_normal_subgroups}

Given two normal subgroups, $N$ and $M,$ of $G,$ their intersection $N\cap M$and their [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_of_subgroups) $N M = \{n m : n \in N\; \text{ and }\; m \in M \}$ are also normal subgroups of $G.$

The normal subgroups of $G$ form a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) under [subset inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subset_inclusion) with [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element), $\{ e \},$ and [greatest element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_element), $G.$ The [meet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet_%28lattice_theory%29) of two normal subgroups, $N$ and $M,$ in this lattice is their intersection and the [join](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Join_%28lattice_theory%29) is their product.

The lattice is [complete](wikipedia://en/44777) and [modular](wikipedia://en/1089311).[^n20]

:::

## Normal subgroups, quotient groups and homomorphisms {#Normal_subgroups,_quotient_groups_and_homomorphisms}

If $N$ is a normal subgroup, we can define a multiplication on cosets as follows:

$$\left(a_1 N\right) \left(a_2 N\right) := \left(a_1 a_2\right) N.$$

This relation defines a mapping $G/N\times G/N \to G/N.$ To show that this mapping is well-defined, one needs to prove that the choice of representative elements $a_1, a_2$ does not affect the result. To this end, consider some other representative elements $a_1'\in a_1 N, a_2' \in a_2 N.$ Then there are $n_1, n_2\in N$ such that $a_1' = a_1 n_1, a_2' = a_2 n_2.$ It follows that

$$a_1' a_2' N = a_1 n_1 a_2 n_2 N =a_1 a_2 n_1' n_2 N=a_1 a_2 N,$$

where we also used the fact that $N$ is a normal subgroup, and therefore there is $n_1'\in N$ such that $n_1 a_2 = a_2 n_1'.$ This proves that this product is a well-defined mapping between cosets.

With this operation, the set of cosets is itself a group, called the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) and denoted with $G/N.$ There is a natural [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396), $f : G \to G/N,$ given by $f(a) = a N.$ This homomorphism maps $N$ into the identity element of $G/N,$ which is the coset $e N = N,$[^n23] that is, $\ker(f) = N.$

In general, a group homomorphism, $f : G \to H$ sends subgroups of $G$ to subgroups of $H.$ Also, the preimage of any subgroup of $H$ is a subgroup of $G.$ We call the preimage of the trivial group $\{ e \}$ in $H$ the **[kernel](wikipedia://en/45240)** of the homomorphism and denote it by $\ker f.$ As it turns out, the kernel is always normal and the image of $G, f(G),$ is always [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $G / \ker f$ (the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem)).[^n24] In fact, this correspondence is a bijection between the set of all quotient groups of $G, G / N,$ and the set of all homomorphic images of $G$ ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism).[^n25] It is also easy to see that the kernel of the quotient map, $f : G \to G/N,$ is $N$ itself, so the normal subgroups are precisely the kernels of homomorphisms with [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) $G.$[^n26]
