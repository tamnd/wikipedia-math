# Roman numerals

*Image: CuttySarkRomNum.jpg.* Roman numerals on stern of the ship showing [draught](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draft_%28hull%29) in [feet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_%28unit%29). The numbers range from 13 to 22, from bottom to top.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Roman numerals"}

**Roman numerals** are a [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170) that originated in [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) and remained the usual way of writing [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) throughout Europe well into the [Late Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages). Like many other ancient numeral systems, Roman numerals are based on the [additive](wikipedia://en/61338) principle: a number is written by concatenating individual symbols, each representing a fixed value, and the value of the resulting numeral phrase is the sum of the individual values of each letter. The modern style of Roman numerals uses only seven letters from the [Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet) as symbols: [I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I) meaning 1, [V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V) meaning 5, [X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X) meaning 10, [L](wikipedia://en/4860524) meaning 50, [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C) meaning 100, [D](wikipedia://en/8123) meaning 500, and [M](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M) meaning 1000. For example, the Roman numeral XXVII represents the number $10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 27$. When a smaller numeral symbol precedes a larger one, [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) is implied; for example, the notation IV represents $5 - 1 = 4$ and IX represents $10 - 1 = 9$.

:::

The use of Roman numerals has continued long after the [decline of the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire). From the 14th century on, Roman numerals began to be replaced by the [positional](wikipedia://en/437052) [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371); however, this process was gradual, and the use of Roman numerals has persisted in some contexts, such as on [clock faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock_face). For instance, on the clock of [Big Ben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ben) (designed in 1852), the hours from 1 to 12 are written as:

**I, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, XII**

Other common uses include year numbers on monuments and buildings and copyright dates on the title screens of films and television programmes. MCM signifies $1000 + 1000 - 100 = 1900$, so 1912 is written MCMXII. For the years of the century, MM indicates 2000, so (the current year) is written .

## Description {#Description}

Roman numerals use different symbols for each power of ten, and there is no zero symbol, in contrast with the [place value notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_value_notation) of Arabic numerals (in which place-keeping zeros enable the same digit to represent different powers of ten).

This allows some flexibility in notation, and there has never been an official or universally accepted standard for Roman numerals. Usage varied greatly in ancient Rome and became thoroughly chaotic in medieval times. The more recent restoration of a largely "classical" notation has gained popularity among some, while variant forms are used by some modern writers as seeking more "flexibility".[^o3] Roman numerals may be considered legally binding expressions of a number, as in U.S. copyright law before the [Berne Convention Implementation Act of 1988](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berne_Convention_Implementation_Act_of_1988) (where an "incorrect" or ambiguous numeral in a [copyright notice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_notice) could invalidate a copyright claim or affect the termination date of the copyright period).[^o4]

### Standard form {#Standard_form}

The following table displays how Roman numerals are usually written in modern times:[^o6]

*Individual decimal places*

::: {#o7 .table cue=table name="Individual decimal places"}

|  | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Units |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | M | C | X | I |
| 2 | MM | CC | XX | II |
| 3 | MMM | CCC | XXX | III |
| 4 |  | CD | XL | IV |
| 5 |  | D | L | V |
| 6 |  | DC | LX | VI |
| 7 |  | DCC | LXX | VII |
| 8 |  | DCCC | LXXX | VIII |
| 9 |  | CM | XC | IX |

:::

The numerals for 4 (IV) and 9 (IX) are written using [subtractive notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtractive_notation),[^o8] where the smaller symbol (I) is *subtracted* from the larger one (V or X), instead of IIII and VIIII. Subtractive notation is also used for 40 (XL), 90 (XC), 400 (CD) and 900 (CM).[^o9] These are the only subtractive forms in standard use.

A number containing two or more decimal digits is built by appending the Roman numeral equivalent for each, from highest to lowest, as in the following examples:

- 39 = XXX + IX = **XXXIX**.
- 246 = CC + XL + VI = **CCXLVI**.
- 789 = DCC + LXXX + IX = **DCCLXXXIX**.
- 2,421 = MM + CD + XX + I = **MMCDXXI**.

Any missing place (represented by a zero in the place-value equivalent) is omitted, as in Latin (and English) speech:

- 160 = C + LX = **CLX**
- 207 = CC + VII = **CCVII**
- 1,009 = M + IX = **MIX**
- 1,066 = M + LX + VI = **MLXVI**[^o10][^o11]

The largest number that can be represented in this manner is 3,999 (**MMMCMXCIX**), but this is sufficient for the values for which Roman numerals are commonly used today, such as year numbers:

- 1776 = M + DCC + LXX + VI = **MDCCLXXVI** (the date written on the book held by the [Statue of Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Liberty)).
- 1918 = M + CM + X + VIII = **MCMXVIII** (the first year of the [Spanish flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu) pandemic)
- 1944 = M + CM + XL + IV = **MCMXLIV** (erroneous copyright notice of the 1954 movie *[The Last Time I Saw Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Time_I_Saw_Paris#Copyright)*)[^o4]
- \= (this year)

For larger numbers (4,000 and larger): Both before and after the introduction of Arabic numerals in the West, from ancient times through medieval and modern, users of Roman numerals have used various means to write larger numbers (see below).

### Other forms {#Other_forms}

Forms exist that vary in one way or another from the general standard represented above.

#### Other additive forms {#Other_additive_forms}

*Image: BadSalzdetfurthBadenburgerStr060529.jpg.* A [clock face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock_face) with the Roman numerals typical for clocks, in [Bad Salzdetfurth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Salzdetfurth), Germany

While subtractive notation for 4, 40, and 400 (IV, XL, and CD) has been the usual form since Roman times , [additive notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_notation) to represent these numbers (IIII, XXXX, and CCCC)[^o14] very frequently continued to be used, including in compound numbers like 24 (XXIIII),[^o15] 74 (LXXIIII),[^o16] and 490 (CCCCLXXXX).[^o17] The additive forms for 9, 90, and 900 (VIIII,[^o14] LXXXX,[^o18] and DCCCC[^o19]) have also been used, although less often.

The two conventions could be mixed in the same document or inscription, even in the same numeral. For example, on the numbered gates to the [Colosseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colosseum), IIII is systematically used instead of IV, but subtractive notation is used for XL; consequently, gate 44 is labelled XLIIII.[^o20][^o21]

Especially on tombstones and other funerary inscriptions, 5 and 50 have been occasionally written IIIII and XXXXX instead of V and L, and there are instances such as IIIIII and XXXXXX rather than VI or LX.[^o22][^o23]

Modern [clock faces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock_face) that use Roman numerals still very often use IIII for four o'clock but IX for nine o'clock, a practice that goes back to very early clocks such as the [Wells Cathedral clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wells_Cathedral_clock) of the late 14th century.[^o24][^o25][^o26] However, this is far from universal: for example, the clock of [Elizabeth Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Tower) at the Palace of Westminster uses a subtractive IV for 4 o'clock.[^o25]

*Image: AdmiraltyArchLondonCloseup.jpg.* The year number on [Admiralty Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_Arch), London. The year 1910 is rendered as MDCCCCX, rather than the more usual MCMX

Several monumental inscriptions created in the early 20th century use variant forms for "1900" (usually written MCM). These vary from MDCCCCX for 1910 as seen on [Admiralty Arch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Admiralty_Arch), London, to the more unusual, if not unique MDCDIII for 1903, on the north entrance to the [Saint Louis Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Louis_Art_Museum).[^o27]

*Image: Epitaph des Marcus Caelius.JPG.* Epitaph of [centurion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centurion) Marcus Caelius, showing "XIIX"

#### Other subtractive forms {#Other_subtractive_forms}

There are numerous historical examples of IIX being used for 8; for example, XIIX was used by officers of the [Legio XVIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legio_XVIII) (18th Roman legion) to write their number.[^o29][^o30] The notation appears prominently on the [cenotaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cenotaph) of their senior [centurion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centurion) [Marcus Caelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Caelius) (c. 45 BC – 9 AD). On the publicly displayed official Roman calendars known as [Fasti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasti), XIIX is used for the 18 days to the next [Kalends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calends), and XXIIX for the 28 days in February. The latter can be seen on the sole extant pre-Julian calendar, the [Fasti Antiates Maiores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasti_Antiates_Maiores).[^o31] There are historical examples of other subtractive forms: IIIXX for 17,[^o32] IIXX for 18,[^o33] IIIC for 97,[^o34] IIC for 98,[^o35][^o36] and IC for 99.[^o37] A possible explanation is that the word for 18 in Latin is duodeviginti—literally "two from twenty"⁠—while 98 is duodecentum (two from hundred) and 99 is undecentum (one from hundred).[^o38] However, the explanation does not seem to apply to IIIXX and IIIC, since the Latin words for 17 and 97 were septendecim (seven ten) and nonaginta septem (ninety seven), respectively.

The ROMAN() function in [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel) supports multiple subtraction modes depending on the "Form" setting. For example, the number "499" (usually CDXCIX) can be rendered as LDVLIV ((500-50)+(50-5)+(5-1)), XDIX ((500-10)+(10-1)), VDIV ((500-5)+(5-1)) or ID (500-1). The relevant Microsoft help page offers no explanation for this function other than to describe its output as "more concise".[^o39]

#### Non-standard variants {#Non-standard_variants}

*Image: Padlock,\_Athlone.jpg.* Padlock used on the north [gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_gate) of the Irish town of [Athlone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athlone). "1613" in the date is rendered XVIXIII, (literally "16, 13") instead of MDCXIII.

*Image: Excerpt from BnF ms. 1433 fr., fol. 24r.png.* Excerpt from [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliothèque_nationale_de_France).[^o41] The Roman numeral for 500 is rendered as C, instead of D.

There are also historical examples of other additive and multiplicative forms, and forms which seem to reflect spoken phrases. Some of these variants may have been regarded as errors even by contemporaries.

- IIXX was how people associated with the [XXII Roman Legion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legio_XXII_Deiotariana) used to write their number. The practice may have been due to a common way to say "twenty-second" in Latin, namely duo et vice(n)sima (literally "two and twentieth") rather than the "regular" vice(n)sima secunda (twenty second).[^o42] Apparently, at least one ancient [stonecutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonecutter) mistakenly thought that the IIXX of "22nd Legion" stood for 18, and "corrected" it to XVIII.[^o42]
- Other numerals that do not fit the usual patterns—such as VXL for 45, instead of the usual XLV—may be due to scribal errors, or the writer's lack of familiarity with the system, rather than being genuine variant usage.

### Zero {#Zero}

As a non-[positional numeral system](wikipedia://en/437052), Roman numerals have no "place-keeping" [zeros.](wikipedia://en/34513) Furthermore, the system as used by the Romans lacked a numeral for the number [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) itself (that is, what remains after 1 is subtracted from 1). The word la (the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) word meaning "none") was used to represent 0, although the earliest attested instances are medieval. For instance [Dionysius Exiguus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysius_Exiguus) used nulla alongside Roman numerals in a manuscript from 525 AD.[^o44][^o45] About 725, [Bede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bede) or one of his colleagues used the letter N, the initial of nulla or of la (the Latin word for "nothing") for 0, in a table of [epacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epacts), all written in Roman numerals.[^o46]

The use of N to indicate "none" long survived in the historic [apothecaries' system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothecaries'_system) of measurement: used well into the 20th century to designate quantities in pharmaceutical prescriptions.[^o47]

In later times, the Arabic numeral "0" has been used as a zero to open enumerations with Roman numbers. Examples include the 24-hour [Shepherd Gate Clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepherd_Gate_Clock) from 1852 and [tarot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot) packs such as the 15th-century [Sola Busca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sola_Busca_tarot) and the 20th century [Rider–Waite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rider-Waite_Tarot) packs.

### Fractions {#Fractions}

*Image: Vecchi 003.jpg.* A [triens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triens) coin ($\tfrac{1}{3}$ or $\tfrac{4}{12}$ of an as). Note the four dots (**····**) indicating its value.

*Image: Semisse.jpg.* A [semis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semis) coin ($\tfrac{1}{2}$ or $\tfrac{6}{12}$ of an as). Note the S indicating its value.

The base "Roman fraction" is S, indicating $\tfrac{1}{2}$. The use of S (as in VIIS to indicate $7\tfrac{1}{2}$) is attested in some ancient inscriptions[^o50] and in the now rare apothecaries' system (usually in the form SS):[^o47] but while Roman numerals for [whole numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) are essentially [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214), S does not correspond to $\tfrac{5}{10}$, as one might expect, but $\tfrac{6}{12}$.

The Romans used a [duodecimal](wikipedia://en/8400) rather than a decimal system for [fractions](wikipedia://en/19727024), as the [divisibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisibility) of twelve (12 = 2<sup>2</sup> × 3) makes it easier to handle the common [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) of $\tfrac{1}{3}$ and $\tfrac{1}{4}$ than does a system based on ten (10 = 2 × 5). Notation for fractions other than $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is mainly found on surviving [Roman coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_coin), many of which had values that were duodecimal fractions of the unit [as](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_%28coin%29). Fractions less than $\tfrac{1}{2}$ are indicated by a dot (**·**) for each uncia "twelfth", the source of the English words *inch* and *ounce*; dots are repeated for fractions up to five twelfths. Six twelfths (one half), is S for semis "half". *Uncia* dots were added to S for fractions from seven to eleven twelfths, just as tallies were added to V for whole numbers from six to nine.[^o51] The arrangement of the dots was variable and not necessarily [linear](wikipedia://en/91591). Five dots arranged like (**⁙**) (as on the face of a [die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice)) are known as a [quincunx](wikipedia://en/174906), from the name of the Roman fraction/coin. The Latin words sextans and quadrans are the source of the English words *[sextant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextant)* and *[quadrant](wikipedia://en/18315778)*.

Each fraction from $\tfrac{1}{12}$ to $\tfrac{12}{12}$ had a name in Roman times; these corresponded to the names of the related coins:

### Large numbers {#Large_numbers}

The modern form can only write numbers up to 3999, and without M in early Roman times only numbers up to 899 could be written. Various schemes have been used over time to write larger numbers.

::: {#o58 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="IƆƆ"}

#### Apostrophus {#Apostrophus}

*Image: Westerkerk MDCXXX.jpg.* "1630" on the [Westerkerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westerkerk) in Amsterdam. "M" and "D" are given archaic apostrophus form.

Using the apostrophus method,[^o59] 500 is written as IↃ, while 1,000 is written as CIↃ.[^n1] This system of encasing numbers to denote thousands (imagine the Cs and Ↄs as parentheses) had its origins in Etruscan numeral usage.

Each additional set of C and Ↄ surrounding CIↃ raises the value by a factor of ten: CCIↃↃ represents 10,000 and CCCIↃↃↃ represents 100,000. Similarly, each additional Ↄ to the right of IↃ raises the value by a factor of ten: IↃↃ represents 5,000 and IↃↃↃ represents 50,000. Numerals larger than CCCIↃↃↃ do not occur.[^o89]

*Image: Roman numerals Bungus 1584-1585.png.* Page from a 16th-century manual, showing a mixture of apostrophus and vinculum numbers (see in particular the ways of writing 10,000).

- **IↃ** = 500
- **CIↃ** = 1,000
- **IↃↃ** = 5,000
- **CCIↃↃ** = 10,000
- **IↃↃↃ** = 50,000
- **CCCIↃↃↃ** = 100,000

Sometimes IↃ (500) is reduced to D, CIↃ (1,000) to ↀ, IↃↃ (5,000) to ↁ; CCIↃↃ (10,000) to ↂ; IↃↃↃ (50,000) to ↇ; and CCCIↃↃↃ (100,000) to ↈ.[^o60] It is likely CIↃ (1000) influenced the later M.

[John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290) is often credited with introducing [the $\langle \infty\rangle$ symbol](wikipedia://en/1605389) for [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), and one conjecture is his basing it on ↀ, since 1,000 was [hyperbolically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbole) used to represent very large numbers.

:::

#### Vinculum {#Vinculum}

Using the [vinculum](wikipedia://en/673457), conventional Roman numerals are multiplied by 1,000 by adding a "bar" or "overline", thus:[^o60]

- **IV** = 4,000
- **XXV** = 25,000

The *vinculum*came into use in the [late Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic),[^o62] and it was a common alternative to the apostrophic ↀ during the Imperial era around the Roman world (M for '1000' was not in use until the Medieval period).[^o63][^o64] It continued in use in the Middle Ages, though it became known more commonly as titulus,[^o65] and it appears in modern editions of classical and medieval Latin texts.[^o66][^o68]

In an extension of the vinculum, a three-sided box (now sometimes printed as two vertical lines and a vinculum) is used to multiply by 100,000,[^o67][^o62] thus:

- **XIII XXXII p.** = 1,332,000 paces (1,332 [Roman miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mile#Roman)).[^o68]

Vinculum notation is distinct from the custom of adding an overline to a numeral simply to indicate that it is a number. Both usages can be seen on Roman inscriptions of the same period and general location, such as on the [Antonine Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonine_Wall).[^o69][^o70]

#### Other {#Other}

- There are some examples of year numbers after 1000 written as two Roman numerals 1–99, e.g. 1613 as XVIXIII, corresponding to the common reading "sixteen thirteen" of such year numbers in English, or 1519 as XCXIX as in [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) *quinze-cent-dix-neuf* (fifteen-hundred and nineteen), and similar readings in other languages.[^o72]
- In some French texts from the 15th century and later, one finds constructions like IIII<sup>XX</sup>XIX for 99, reflecting the French reading of that number as quatre-vingt-dix-neuf (four-score and nineteen).[^o72] Similarly, in some English documents one finds, for example, 77 written as "iii<sup>xx</sup>xvii" (which could be read "three-score and seventeen").[^o73]
- A medieval accounting text from 1301 renders numbers like 13,573 as "XIII. M. V. C. III. XX. XIII", that is, "13×1000 + 5×100 + 3×20 + 13".[^o74]

:::

## Origin {#Origin}

The system is closely associated with the ancient [city-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City-state) of Rome and the Empire that it created. However, due to the scarcity of surviving examples, the origins of the system are obscure and there are several competing theories, all largely conjectural.

### Etruscan numerals {#Etruscan_numerals}

Rome was founded sometime between 850 and 750 BC, next to the southern edge of the Etruscan domain, which covered a large part of north-central Italy.

The Roman numerals, in particular, are directly derived from the [Etruscan number symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_numerals): $\langle \text{𐌠}\rangle$, $\langle \text{𐌡}\rangle$, $\langle \text{𐌢}\rangle$, $\langle \text{𐌣}\rangle$, and $\langle \text{𐌟}\rangle$ for 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 (they had more symbols for larger numbers, but it is unknown which symbol represents which number). As in the basic Roman system, the Etruscans wrote the symbols that added to the desired number, from higher to lower value. Thus, the number 87, for example, would be written 50 + 10 + 10 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 𐌣𐌢𐌢𐌢𐌡𐌠𐌠 (this would appear as 𐌠𐌠𐌡𐌢𐌢𐌢𐌣 since [Etruscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscan_language) was written from right to left.)[^o77]

The symbols $\langle \text{𐌠}\rangle$ and $\langle \text{𐌡}\rangle$ resembled letters of the Etruscan alphabet, but $\langle \text{𐌢}\rangle$, $\langle \text{𐌣}\rangle$, and $\langle \text{𐌟}\rangle$ did not. The Etruscans used the subtractive notation, too, but not like the Romans. They wrote 17, 18, and 19 as 𐌠𐌠𐌠𐌢𐌢, 𐌠𐌠𐌢𐌢, and 𐌠𐌢𐌢, mirroring the way they spoke those numbers ("three from twenty", etc.); and similarly for 27, 28, 29, 37, 38, etc. However, they did not write 𐌠𐌡 for 4 (nor 𐌢𐌣 for 40), and wrote 𐌡𐌠𐌠, 𐌡𐌠𐌠𐌠 and 𐌡𐌠𐌠𐌠𐌠 for 7, 8, and 9, respectively.[^o77]

::: {#o79 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Early Roman numerals"}

### Early Roman numerals {#Early_Roman_numerals}

*Image: Borne milliaire Via Egnatia Galikos (crop).jpg.* Milestone of the 2nd century BC, with 260 written as CCↆX, using the 'anchor' mark for fifty.

The early Roman numerals for 1, 10, and 100 were the Etruscan ones: $\langle \text{𐌠}\rangle$, $\langle \text{𐌢}\rangle$, and $\langle \text{𐌟}\rangle$. The symbols for 5 and 50 changed from $\langle \text{𐌡}\rangle$ and $\langle \text{𐌣}\rangle$ to $\langle V\rangle$ and $\langle \text{ↆ}\rangle$ at some point. The latter had flattened to $\langle \perp\rangle$ (an inverted T) by the time of [Augustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustus), and soon afterwards became identified with the graphically similar letter .[^o89]

The symbol for 100 was written variously as $\langle \text{𐌟}\rangle$ or $\langle \text{Ↄ}\operatorname{IC}\rangle$, and was then abbreviated to or , with (which matched the Latin letter *C*) finally winning out. It might have helped that *C* was the initial letter of CENTUM, Latin for "hundred".

The numbers 500 and 1000 were denoted by V or X overlaid with a box or circle. Thus, 500 was like a Ɔ superimposed on a ⋌ or ⊢, making it look like Þ. It became D or Ð by the time of Augustus, under the graphic influence of the letter D. It was later identified as the letter D; an alternative symbol for "thousand" was a CIↃ, and half of a thousand or "five hundred" is the right half of the symbol, IↃ, and this may have been converted into D.[^n1]

The notation for 1000 was a circled or boxed X: Ⓧ, ⊗, ⊕, and by Augustan times was partially identified with the Greek letter Φ *[phi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi)*. Over time, the symbol changed to Ψ and ↀ. The latter symbol further evolved into ∞, then ⋈, and eventually changed to M under the influence of the Latin word *mille* "thousand".[^o89]

According to Paul Kayser, the basic numerical symbols were I, X, 𐌟 and Φ (or ⊕) and the intermediate ones were derived by taking half of those (half an X is V, half a 𐌟 is ↆ and half a Φ/⊕ is D). Then 𐌟 and ↆ developed as mentioned above.[^o80]

:::

### Classical Roman numerals {#Classical_Roman_numerals}

The [Colosseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colosseum) was constructed in Rome in CE 72–80,[^o82] and while the original perimeter wall has largely disappeared, the numbered entrances from XXIII (23) to LIIII (54) survive,[^o83] to demonstrate that in Imperial times Roman numerals had already assumed their classical form: [as largely standardised in current use](wikipedia://en/25657#Standard_form). The most obvious anomaly ([a common one that persisted for centuries](wikipedia://en/25657#Other_additive_forms)) is the inconsistent use of subtractive notation - while XL is used for 40, IV is avoided in favour of IIII: in fact, gate 44 is labelled XLIIII.

## Use in the Middle Ages and Renaissance {#Use_in_the_Middle_Ages_and_Renaissance}

[Lower case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_case), or *minuscule*, letters were developed in the Middle Ages, well after the demise of the [Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire), and since that time lower-case versions of Roman numbers have also been commonly used: i, ii, iii, iv, and so on.

*Image: Excerpt from BnF ms. 23112 fr., fol. 343v.png.* 13th century example of iiij.

Since the Middle Ages, a "j" has sometimes been substituted for the final "i" of a "lower-case" Roman numeral, such as "iij" for 3 or "vij" for 7. This "j" can be considered a [swash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swash_%28typography%29) variant of "i". Into the early 20th century, the use of a final "j" was still sometimes used in [medical prescriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_prescription) to prevent tampering with or misinterpretation of a number after it was written.

[Chronograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronogram), messages with dates encoded into them, were popular during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) era. The chronogram would be a phrase containing the letters I, V, X, L, C, D, and M. By putting these letters together, the reader would obtain a number, usually indicating a particular year.[^o85]

A superscript "o" (sometimes written directly above the symbol) was sometimes used as an [ordinal indicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_indicator).[^o86]

### "Medieval Roman numerals" {#"Medieval_Roman_numerals"}

Scribal abbreviation Numerals in documents and inscriptions from the Middle Ages sometimes include additional symbols, which today are called "medieval Roman numerals". Some simply substitute another letter for the standard one (such as "A" for "V", or "Q" for "D"), while others serve as abbreviations for compound numerals ("O" for "XI", or "F" for "XL"). This system was occasionally extended to include letters and numbers from other scripts, with Adriano Cappelli's comprehensive record of scribal numeral forms (published 1912) recording the use of forms of [omega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omega) and [sampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampi) for 800 and 900 respectively, as they represented in the [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_alphabet) numeral system, as well as combinations of Latin letters, early Roman symbols and Hindu-Arabic numerals.[^o54] Although the medieval Roman numeral values for the letters of the Latin alphabet are still listed today in some dictionaries under the entries for those letters, their usage even at the time was inconsistent and never standard, and—with the aforementioned exception of M being accepted as a standard modern Roman numeral—they are not used in any meaningful capacity in modern times.

## Modern use {#Modern_use}

*Image: Year 1575 in Arabic and Roman numbers.jpg.* Year 1575 in Arabic and Roman numbers

By the 11th century, Arabic numerals had been introduced into Europe from [al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus), by way of [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) traders and arithmetic treatises. Roman numerals, however, proved very persistent, remaining in common use in the West well into the 14th and 15th centuries, even in accounting and other business records (where the actual calculations would have been made using an [abacus](wikipedia://en/655)). Replacement by their more convenient "Arabic" equivalents was quite gradual, and Roman numerals are still used today in certain contexts. A few examples of their current use are:

*Image: Carlos IV Coin.jpg.* Spanish [Real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_real) using IIII instead of IV as regnal number of [Charles IV of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_IV_of_Spain).

- Names of monarchs and popes, e.g. [Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II) of the United Kingdom, [Pope Leo XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Leo_XIV). These are referred to as [regnal numbers](wikipedia://en/296235) and are usually read as [ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_numbers_%28linguistics%29); e.g. II is pronounced "the second". This tradition began in Europe sporadically in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), gaining widespread use in England during the reign of [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII). Previously, the monarch was not known by numeral but by an [epithet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epithet) such as [Edward the Confessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_the_Confessor). Some monarchs (e.g. [Charles IV of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_IV_of_Spain), [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV) of France and [William IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_IV) of Great Britain) seem to have preferred the use of IIII instead of IV on their coinage (see illustration).
- [Generational suffixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generational_suffixes), particularly in the U.S., for people sharing the same name across generations, such as [William Howard Taft IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Howard_Taft_IV). These are also usually read as ordinals.
- In the [French Republican Calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Republican_Calendar#Converting_from_the_Gregorian_Calendar), initiated during the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution), years were numbered by Roman numerals – from the year I (1792) when this calendar was introduced to the year XIV (1805) when it was abandoned.
- *Image: Cambridge Main Public Library 1888.jpg.* The year of construction of the [Cambridge Public Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Public_Library) (Massachusetts, U.S.), 1888, displayed in "standard" Roman numerals on its facade. The year of production of films, television shows and other works of art within the work itself. Outside reference to the work will use regular Arabic numerals.
- Hour marks on [timepieces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timepiece). In this context, 4 is often written IIII.
- The year of construction on building [façades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Façade) and [cornerstones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornerstone).
- Page numbering of prefaces and introductions of books, and sometimes of appendices and annexes, too.
- Book volume and chapter numbers, as well as the several acts within a play (e.g. Act iii, Scene 2).
- [Sequels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequel) to some films, video games, and other works (as in *[Rocky II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_II)*, *[Grand Theft Auto V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Theft_Auto_V)*, *[Myst III: Exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myst_III:_Exile)*).
- [Outlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_%28summary%29) that use numbers to show hierarchical relationships.
- Occurrences of a recurring grand event, for instance:
  - The [Summer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_Olympic_Games) and [Winter Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Olympic_Games) (e.g. the [XXI Olympic Winter Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_Winter_Olympics); the [Games of the XXX Olympiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Summer_Olympics)).
  - The [Super Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Bowl), the annual championship game of the [National Football League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Football_League) (e.g. [Super Bowl XLII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Bowl_XLII); [Super Bowl 50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Bowl_50) was a one-time exception[^o93]).
  - [WrestleMania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WrestleMania), the annual [professional wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professional_wrestling) event for [WWE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WWE) (e.g. [WrestleMania XXX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WrestleMania_XXX)). This usage has also been inconsistent.
- [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) and [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II).

### Specific disciplines {#Specific_disciplines}

In [astronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronautics), [United States rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_orbital_launch_systems#United_States) model variants are sometimes designated by Roman numerals, e.g. [Titan I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_I), [Titan II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_II), [Titan III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_III), [Saturn I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_I), [Saturn V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_V).

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), the [natural satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite) or "moons" of the [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) are [designated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naming_of_moons#Roman_numeral_designations) by capital Roman numerals appended to the planet's name. For example, [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_%28moon%29)'s designation is [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn) VI.[^o95]

In [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), Roman numerals are sometimes used to denote the [groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_%28periodic_table%29) of the [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table), but this has officially been deprecated in favour of Arabic numerals.[^o96] They are also used in the [IUPAC nomenclature of inorganic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUPAC_nomenclature_of_inorganic_chemistry), for the [oxidation number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_number) of [cations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cation) which can take on several different positive charges. They are also used for naming [phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28matter%29) of [polymorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_%28materials_science%29) [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal), such as [ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice).

In [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education), school grades (in the sense of year-groups rather than test scores) are sometimes referred to by a Roman numeral; for example, "grade IX" is sometimes seen for "grade 9".

*Image: 9th Aero Squadron AEF.jpg.* Stylised "IX" represents "9" in unit emblem of [9th Aero Squadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9th_Aero_Squadron) AEF, 1918.

In [entomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entomology), the broods of the thirteen- and seventeen-year [periodical cicadas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodical_cicadas) are identified by Roman numerals.

In [graphic design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphic_design), stylised Roman numerals may represent numeric values.

In [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law), Roman numerals are commonly used to help organize legal codes as part of an [alphanumeric outline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_%28list%29#Alphanumeric_outline). In citing [UK Acts of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Parliament_%28United_Kingdom%29) within a given year (a given [session](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_session) until 1963), the chapter of a [local act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_and_personal_acts_of_Parliament_%28United_Kingdom%29#Local_acts) is given in lowercase Roman numerals, whereas that of a [public act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Act_of_Parliament_%28United_Kingdom%29#Public_general_acts) has plain Arabic numerals and a [personal act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_and_personal_acts_of_Parliament_%28United_Kingdom%29#Personal_acts) has [italic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_type) Arabic numerals.[^o97]

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) (including [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), and [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176)), when a graph includes negative numbers, its quadrants are named using I, II, III, and IV.[^o98] These quadrant names signify positive numbers on both axes, negative numbers on the x-axis, negative numbers on both axes, and negative numbers on the y-axis, respectively. The use of Roman numerals to designate quadrants avoids confusion, since Arabic numerals are used for the actual data represented in the graph.

In [military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military) unit designation, Roman numerals are often used to distinguish between units at different levels. This reduces possible confusion, especially when viewing operational or strategic level maps. In particular, army corps are often numbered using Roman numerals (for example, the American XVIII Airborne Corps or the Nazi III Panzerkorps) with Arabic numerals being used for divisions and armies.

In [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), Roman numerals are used in several contexts:

- [Movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movement_%28music%29) are often numbered using Roman numerals.
- In [Roman numeral analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numeral_analysis), [harmonic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_%28music%29) is identified using Roman numerals.
- Individual strings of [stringed instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stringed_instruments), such as the [violin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Violin), are often denoted by Roman numerals, with higher numbers denoting lower strings.

In [pharmacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacy), Roman numerals were used with the now largely obsolete [apothecaries' system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothecaries'_system) of measurement: including SS to denote "one half" and N to denote "zero".[^o47][^o99]

In [photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photography), Roman numerals (with zero) are used to denote varying levels of brightness when using the [Zone System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_System).

In [seismology](wikipedia://en/28935), Roman numerals are used to designate degrees of the [Mercalli intensity scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercalli_intensity_scale) of earthquakes.

*Image: Eire Frankng I-IV-1986.jpg.* Example of postage stamp from Ireland (Éire) franked using Roman numeral for the month

In [sport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Team_sport) the team containing the "top" players and representing a nation or province, a [club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_club) or a school at the highest level in (say) [rugby union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rugby_union) is often called the "1st XV", while a lower-ranking [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket) or [American football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_football) team might be the "3rd XI".

In [tarot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarot), Roman numerals (with zero) are often used to denote the cards of the [Major Arcana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_Arcana).

In [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland), Roman numerals were used until the late 1980s to indicate the month on postage [Franking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franking). In documents, Roman numerals are sometimes still used to indicate the month to avoid confusion over day/month/year or month/day/year formats.

In [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology) and [biblical scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_scholarship), the [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint) is often referred to as LXX, as this translation of the [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament) into Greek is named for the legendary number of its translators (septuaginta being Latin for "seventy").

### Modern use in European languages other than English {#Modern_use_in_European_languages_other_than_English}

Some uses that are rare or never seen in English-speaking countries may be relatively common in parts of [continental Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Europe) and in other regions (e.g. [Latin America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_America)) that use a European language other than English. For instance:

Capital or [small capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_caps) Roman numerals are widely used in [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages) to denote centuries, e.g. the French *xviii<sup>e</sup> siècle*[^o101] and the Spanish *siglo xviii* for "18th century". Some Slavic and Turkic languages (especially in and adjacent to Russia) similarly favour Roman numerals (e.g. Russian XVIII век, Azeri XVIII əsr or Polish wiek XVIII[^o102]). On the other hand, in [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language) and some [Central European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe) Slavic languages, like most [Germanic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages), one writes "18." (with a period) before the local word for "century" (e.g. Turkish \18. yüzyıl, Czech \18. století).

When typing on Russian typewriters, the Roman-numeral "V" was replaced with "У" because the letter "V" is absent in the Russian Cyrillic alphabet. Additionally, the Roman-numeral "I" was replaced with "1", since this letter had been removed from the Russian alphabet by the [1918 reform of orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reforms_of_Russian_orthography); in typewritten texts, 1 could look as `1` or `I` depending on the typewriter model. The numbers "II" and "III" were replaced with "П" and "Ш" respectively. For "X", the Cyrillic letter "Х" was used. For example, XVIII was typed as ХУШ. This style is sometimes maintained even when typing on a computer, either out of habit or due to the inconvenience of switching between Latin and Russian script for one or two letters. The numerals "L", "C", "D", and "M" were rarely used in Russian typewritten texts, although some Russian books on typewriting accepted replacing them by the Cyrillic letters "Л", "С", "Д", and "М".[^o103][^o104] Roman numerals are quite a common way for writing ordinal numbers in Russian texts. The usage of Roman numerals to indicate occurrences of a recurring grand event in Russian is more common than in English; for example, they were used for Congresses of the [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union). Besides the cases mentioned above, Roman numerals are used in Russian for declensions and conjugations, [blood types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_type) (the notations O, A, B, AB and I, II, III, IV are both well known in Russia), half-years and quarters of a year, etc.

Mixed Roman and Arabic numerals are sometimes used in numeric representations of dates (especially in formal letters and official documents, but also on tombstones). The month is written in Roman numerals, while the day is in Arabic numerals: "4.VI.1789" and "VI.4.1789" both refer unambiguously to 4 June 1789.

*Image: DarboLaikas.jpg.* Business hours table on a shop window in [Vilnius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilnius), Lithuania.

Roman numerals are sometimes used to represent the days of the week in hours-of-operation signs displayed in windows or on doors of businesses,[^o106] and sometimes in railway and bus timetables. Monday, taken as the first day of the week, is represented by I. Sunday is represented by VII. The hours of operation signs are tables composed of two columns where the left column is the day of the week in Roman numerals and the right column is a range of hours of operation from starting time to closing time. In the example case (left), the business opens from 10 a.m. to 7 p.m. on weekdays, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. on Saturdays and is [closed on Sundays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_shopping). Note that the listing uses 24-hour time.

*Image: Roma - strada statale 4 - segnale di progressiva ettometrica 17+900.jpg.* Sign at 17.9 km on route SS4 [Salaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Via_Salaria), north of Rome, Italy.

Roman numerals may also be used for [floor numbering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_numbering).[^o107][^o108] For instance, apartments in central [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam) are indicated as 138-III, with both an Arabic numeral (number of the block or house) and a Roman numeral (floor number). The apartment on the ground floor is indicated as 138-huis.

In Italy, where roads outside built-up areas have [kilometre signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milestone), major roads and motorways also mark 100-metre subdivisionals, using Roman numerals from I to IX for the smaller intervals. The sign thus marks 17.9 km.

Certain Romance-speaking countries use Roman numerals to designate assemblies of their national legislatures. For instance, the composition of the [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy) [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Italy) from 2018 to 2022 (elected in the [2018 Italian general election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Italian_general_election)) is called the [XVIII Legislature of the Italian Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature_XVIII_of_Italy) (or more commonly the "XVIII Legislature").

A notable exception to the use of Roman numerals in Europe is in Greece, where [Greek numerals](wikipedia://en/166697) (based on the Greek alphabet) are generally used in contexts where Roman numerals would be used elsewhere.

## Unicode {#Unicode}

The "[Number Forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_Forms)" block of the [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) computer character set standard has a number of [Roman numeral symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerals_in_Unicode#Roman_numerals) in the range of [code points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode_code_point) from U+2160 to U+2188.[^o110] This range includes both upper- and lowercase numerals, as well as pre-combined characters for numbers up to 12. One justification for the existence of pre-combined numbers is to facilitate the setting of multiple-letter numbers (such as VIII) on a single horizontal line in Asian vertical text. The Unicode standard, however, includes special Roman numeral code points for compatibility only, stating that "\[f\]or most purposes, it is preferable to compose the Roman numerals from sequences of the appropriate Latin letters".[^o111] The block also includes some apostrophus symbols for large numbers, an old variant of "L" (50) similar to the Etruscan character, the [Claudian letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudian_letter) "reversed C", etc.[^o112]
