# Self-similarity

*Image: KochSnowGif16 800x500 2.gif.* A [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959) has an infinitely repeating self-similarity when it is magnified.

*Image: Standard self-similarity.png.* Standard (trivial) self-similarity[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Self-similarity"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **self-similar** object is exactly or approximately [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) to a part of itself (i.e., the whole has the same shape as one or more of the parts). Many objects in the real world, such as [coastlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastline), are statistically self-similar: parts of them show the same statistical properties at many scales.[^o3] Self-similarity is a typical property of [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913). [Scale invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance) is an exact form of self-similarity where at any magnification there is a smaller piece of the object that is [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) to the whole. For instance, a side of the [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959) is both [symmetrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetrical) and scale-invariant; it can be continually magnified 3x without changing shape.

:::

[Peitgen](wikipedia://en/1562451) *et al.* explain the concept as such:

If parts of a figure are small replicas of the whole, then the figure is called *self-similar*....A figure is *strictly self-similar* if the figure can be decomposed into parts which are exact replicas of the whole. Any arbitrary part contains an exact replica of the whole figure.[^o4]Since mathematically, a fractal may show self-similarity under arbitrary magnification, it is impossible to recreate this physically. Peitgen *et al.* suggest studying self-similarity using approximations:In order to give an operational meaning to the property of self-similarity, we are necessarily restricted to dealing with finite approximations of the limit figure. This is done using the method which we will call box self-similarity where measurements are made on finite stages of the figure using grids of various sizes.[^o5]

This vocabulary was introduced by [Benoit Mandelbrot](wikipedia://en/3999) in 1964.[^o6]

## Self-affinity {#Self-affinity}

*Image: Self-affine set.png.* A self-affine fractal with [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) = 1.8272

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **self-affinity** is a feature of a [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) whose pieces are [scaled](wikipedia://en/651752) by different amounts in the *x* and *y* directions. This means that to appreciate the self-similarity of these fractal objects, they have to be rescaled using an [anisotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropic) [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449).[^o9]

:::

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is self-similar if there exists a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) *S* indexing a set of non-[surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) $\{ f_s : s\in S \}$ for which[^o12]

$$X=\bigcup_{s\in S} f_s(X)$$

If $X\subset Y$, we call *X* self-similar if it is the only [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty_set) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of *Y* such that the equation above holds for $\{ f_s : s\in S \}$. We call.[^o13]

$$\mathfrak{L}=(X,S,\{ f_s : s\in S \} )$$

a *self-similar structure*. The homeomorphisms may be [iterated](wikipedia://en/1448702), resulting in an [iterated function system](wikipedia://en/505225). The composition of functions creates the algebraic structure of a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652). When the set *S* has only two elements, the monoid is known as the [dyadic monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyadic_monoid). The dyadic monoid can be visualized as an infinite [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321); more generally, if the set *S* has *p* elements, then the monoid may be represented as a [p-adic](wikipedia://en/51423) tree.

The group of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the dyadic monoid is the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019); the automorphisms can be pictured as [hyperbolic rotations](wikipedia://en/1264398) of the binary tree.

A more general notion than self-similarity is [self-affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-affinity).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Feigenbaumzoom.gif.* Self-similarity in the [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562) shown by zooming in on the Feigenbaum point at (−1.401155189..., 0)

*Image: Fractal fern explained.png.* An image of the [Barnsley fern](wikipedia://en/25721564) which exhibits [affine](wikipedia://en/38449) self-similarity

The [Cantor discontinuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor_discontinuum) is self-similar since any of its closed subsets is a continuous image of the discontinuum.[^o16]

The [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562) is also self-similar around [Misiurewicz points](wikipedia://en/9612488).

Self-similarity has important consequences for the design of computer networks, as typical network traffic has self-similar properties. For example, in [teletraffic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teletraffic_engineering), [packet switched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packet_switched) data traffic patterns seem to be statistically self-similar.[^o17] This property means that simple models using a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) are inaccurate, and networks designed without taking self-similarity into account are likely to function in unexpected ways.[^o17]

Similarly, [stock market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_market) movements are described as displaying [self-affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-affinity), i.e. they appear self-similar when transformed via an appropriate [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) for the level of detail being shown.[^o18] [Andrew Lo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Lo) describes stock market log return self-similarity in [econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390).[^o19]

[Finite subdivision rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_subdivision_rules) are a powerful technique for building self-similar sets, including the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) and the [Sierpinski triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_triangle).[^o20]

Some [space filling curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_filling_curves), such as the [Peano curve](wikipedia://en/358330) and [Moore curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore_curve), also feature properties of self-similarity.[^o21]

*Image: RepeatedBarycentricSubdivision.png.* A triangle subdivided repeatedly using [barycentric subdivision](wikipedia://en/583651). The complement of the large circles becomes a [Sierpinski carpet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_carpet).

### In cybernetics {#In_cybernetics}

The [viable system model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viable_system_model) of [Stafford Beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stafford_Beer) is an organizational model with an affine self-similar hierarchy, where a given viable system is one element of the System One of a viable system one recursive level higher up, and for whom the elements of its System One are viable systems one recursive level lower down.

### In nature {#In_nature}

*Image: Flickr - cyclonebill - Romanesco.jpg.* Close-up of a [Romanesco broccoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesco_broccoli)

Self-similarity can be found in nature, as well. Plants, such as [Romanesco broccoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanesco_broccoli), exhibit strong self-similarity.

### In music {#In_music}

- Strict [canons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_%28music%29) display various types and amounts of self-similarity, as do sections of [fugues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fugue_%28music%29).
- A [Shepard tone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shepard_tone) is self-similar in the frequency or wavelength domains.
- The Danish composer [Per Nørgård](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_Nørgård) made use of a self-similar [integer sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) named the [infinity series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity_series) in much of his music.
- In the research field of [music information retrieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_information_retrieval), self-similarity commonly refers to the fact that music often consists of parts that are repeated in time.[^o25] In other words, music is self-similar under temporal translation, rather than (or in addition to) under scaling.[^o26]
