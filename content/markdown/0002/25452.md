# Richard Wagner

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Richard Wagner"}

**Wilhelm Richard Wagner** ( ; ; 22 May 1813 – 13 February 1883) was a German composer, theatre director, essayist, and conductor, best known for his [operas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operas), although his mature works are often referred to as [*music dramas*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musikdrama). Unlike most composers, Wagner wrote both the [libretti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libretto) and the music for all of his stage works. He first achieved recognition with works in the Romantic tradition of [Carl Maria von Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Maria_von_Weber) and [Giacomo Meyerbeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giacomo_Meyerbeer), but revolutionised the genre through his concept of the *[Gesamtkunstwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesamtkunstwerk)* ("total work of art"), which sought to unite poetic, musical, visual, and dramatic elements. In that approach, the drama unfolds as a continuously sung narrative, with the music evolving organically from the text rather than alternating between [arias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aria) and [recitatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recitative). Wagner outlined those ideas in a [series of essays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prose_works_by_Richard_Wagner) published between 1849 and 1852, most fully realising them in the first half of his four-opera cycle *[Der Ring des Nibelungen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Ring_des_Nibelungen)* (*The Ring of the [Nibelung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nibelung)*).

:::

Wagner's compositions, particularly in his later period, have complex [textures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_%28music%29), rich [harmonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony) and [orchestration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchestration), and elaborate [leitmotifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitmotif)—musical phrases associated with individual characters, places, ideas, or plot elements. His advances in musical language, such as extreme [chromaticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromaticism) and quickly shifting [tonal centres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonality), greatly influenced the development of classical music; his *[Tristan und Isolde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_und_Isolde)* is regarded as an important precursor to [modernist music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism_%28music%29). Later in life, he softened his ideological stance against traditional operatic forms (e.g., arias, ensembles and [choruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir)), reintroducing them into his last few stage works, including *[Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Meistersinger_von_Nürnberg)* (*The Mastersingers of Nuremberg*) and *[Parsifal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsifal)*.

To fully realise his artistic vision, Wagner had his own [opera house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_house) built to his specifications: the [Bayreuth Festspielhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth_Festspielhaus), which featured many innovations designed to immerse the audience in the drama. It hosted the premieres of *The Ring* and *Parsifal*, and remains entirely devoted to staging his [mature works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth_canon) at the annual [Bayreuth Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth_Festival). After Wagner's death, his wife [Cosima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosima_Wagner) assumed leadership; it has since remained under the management of [their descendants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_family).

Wagner's unorthodox operas, provocative essays, and contentious personal conduct engendered [considerable controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controversies_surrounding_Richard_Wagner) during his lifetime, and continue to do so. Declared a "genius" by some and a "disease" by others, his views on religion, politics, and society remain debated—most notably the extent to which his [antisemitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitism) finds expression in his stage and prose works. Despite this, his operas and music remain central to the repertoire of major opera houses and concert halls worldwide. His ideas can be traced across many art forms throughout the 20th century; his influence extended beyond composition into conducting, philosophy, literature, the visual arts, and theatre.

## Biography {#Biography}

### Early years {#Early_years}

*Image: Wagnerbruhl.jpg.* Wagner's birthplace, at 3, [the Brühl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brühl_%28Leipzig%29), Leipzig

Richard Wagner was born on 22 May 1813 to an [ethnic German family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_family_tree) in [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig), then part of the [Confederation of the Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederation_of_the_Rhine). His family lived at No 3, the [Brühl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brühl_%28Leipzig%29) (*The House of the Red and White Lions*) in Leipzig's [Jewish quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_quarter_%28diaspora%29). He was baptised at [St. Thomas Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Thomas_Church,_Leipzig). He was the ninth, and youngest, child of Carl Friedrich Wagner, a clerk in the Leipzig police service, and his wife, [Johanna Rosine Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johanna_Rosine_Wagner), the daughter of a baker.[^n1][^n2] Wagner's father Carl died of [typhoid fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoid_fever) six months after Richard's birth. Afterwards, his mother Johanna lived with Carl's friend, the actor and playwright [Ludwig Geyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Geyer).[^n3] In August 1814 Johanna and Geyer probably married, although no documentation of this has been found in the Leipzig church registers.[^n4] She and her family moved to Geyer's residence in [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresden). Until he was fourteen, Wagner was known as Wilhelm Richard Geyer. He almost certainly thought that Geyer was his biological father.[^n5]

Geyer's love of the theatre came to be shared by his stepson, and Wagner took part in his performances. In his autobiography [*Mein Leben*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mein_Leben_%28Wagner%29) Wagner recalled once playing the part of an angel.[^n6] In late 1820, Wagner was enrolled at Pastor Wetzel's school at Possendorf, near Dresden, where he received some piano instruction from his Latin teacher.[^n7] He struggled to play a proper [scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_%28music%29) at the keyboard and preferred playing theatre overtures [by ear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_music_by_ear). Following Geyer's death in 1821, Richard was sent to the [Kreuzschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kreuzschule), the boarding school of the [Dresdner Kreuzchor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresdner_Kreuzchor), at the expense of Geyer's brother.[^n8] At the age of nine he was hugely impressed by the [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_fiction) elements of [Carl Maria von Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Maria_von_Weber)'s opera *[Der Freischütz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Freischütz)*, which he saw Weber conduct.[^n9] At this period Wagner entertained ambitions as a playwright. His first creative effort, listed in the *[Wagner-Werk-Verzeichnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner-Werk-Verzeichnis)* (the standard listing of Wagner's works) as WWV 1, was a tragedy called *[Leubald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leubald)*. Begun when he was in school in 1826, the play was strongly influenced by [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare) and [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe). Wagner was determined to set it to music and persuaded his family to allow him music lessons.[^n10]

By 1827, the family had returned to Leipzig. Wagner's first lessons in [harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony) were taken during 1828–1831 with Christian Gottlieb Müller.[^n11] In January 1828 he first heard [Beethoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_van_Beethoven)'s [7th Symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._7_%28Beethoven%29) and then, in March, the same composer's [9th Symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._9_%28Beethoven%29), both at the [Gewandhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gewandhaus). Beethoven became a major inspiration, and Wagner wrote a piano transcription of the 9th Symphony.[^n12] He was also greatly impressed by a performance of [Mozart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Amadeus_Mozart)'s [Requiem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Requiem_%28Mozart%29).[^n13] Wagner's early [piano sonatas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_sonata) and his first attempts at orchestral [overtures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overture) date from this period.[^n14]

In 1829 he saw a performance by [dramatic soprano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramatic_soprano) [Wilhelmine Schröder-Devrient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelmine_Schröder-Devrient), who became his ideal of the fusion of drama and music in opera. In *Mein Leben* Wagner wrote, "When I look back across my entire life I find no event to place beside this in the impression it produced on me," and claimed that the "profoundly human and ecstatic performance of this incomparable artist" kindled in him an "almost demonic fire".[^n15]

In 1831, Wagner enrolled at the [Leipzig University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_University), where he became a member of the Saxon [student fraternity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studentenverbindung).[^n16] He took composition lessons with the [Thomaskantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomaskantor) [Theodor Weinlig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Theodor_Weinlig).[^n17] Weinlig was so impressed with Wagner's musical ability that he refused any payment for his lessons. He arranged for his pupil's Piano Sonata in B-flat major (which was consequently dedicated to him) to be published as Wagner's Op. 1. A year later, Wagner composed his [Symphony in C major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_in_C_major_%28Wagner%29), a Beethovenesque work performed in Prague in 1832[^n18] and at the Leipzig Gewandhaus in 1833.[^n19] He then began to work on an opera, *[Die Hochzeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Hochzeit)* (*The Wedding*), which he never completed.[^n20]

### Early career and marriage (1833–1842) {#Early_career_and_marriage_(1833–1842)}

*Image: Minna.jpg.* [Wilhelmine "Minna" Planer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minna_Planer) (1835), by Alexander von Otterstedt

In 1833, Wagner's brother Albert managed to obtain for him a position as choirmaster at the [Theatre Würzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainfranken_Theater_Würzburg).[^n21] In the same year, at the age of 20, Wagner composed his first complete opera, *[Die Feen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Feen)* (*The Fairies*). This work, which imitated the style of Weber, went unproduced until half a century later, when it was premiered in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich) shortly after the composer's death in 1883.[^n22]

Having returned to Leipzig in 1834, Wagner held a brief appointment as musical director at the opera house in [Magdeburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdeburg)[^n23] during which he wrote *[Das Liebesverbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Liebesverbot)* (*The Ban on Love*), based on Shakespeare's *[Measure for Measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_for_Measure)*. This was staged at Magdeburg in 1836 but closed before the second performance; this, together with the financial collapse of the theatre company employing him, left the composer in bankruptcy.[^n24][^n25] Wagner had fallen for one of the leading ladies at Magdeburg, the actress [Christine Wilhelmine "Minna" Planer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minna_Planer),[^n26] and after the disaster of *Das Liebesverbot* he followed her to [Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg), where she helped him to get an engagement at the theatre.[^n27] The two married in [Tragheim Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragheim_Church) on 24 November 1836.[^n28] In May 1837, Minna left Wagner for another man,[^n29] and this was only the first débâcle of a tempestuous marriage. In June 1837, Wagner moved to [Riga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riga) (then in the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire)), where he became music director of the local opera;[^n30] having in this capacity engaged Minna's sister Amalie (also a singer) for the theatre, he presently resumed relations with Minna during 1838.[^n31]

By 1839, the couple had amassed such large debts that they fled Riga on the run from creditors.[^n32] Debts plagued Wagner for most of his life.[^n33] Initially the pair took a stormy sea passage to London,[^n34] from which Wagner drew the inspiration for his opera *[Der fliegende Holländer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_fliegende_Holländer)* (*The Flying Dutchman*), with a plot based on a sketch by [Heinrich Heine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Heine).[^n35] The Wagners settled in Paris in September 1839[^n26] and stayed there until 1842. During these years, Wagner is believed to have attended [François Delsarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Delsarte)'s "Cours d'esthétique appliquée," which arguably influenced his aesthetic writings and compositional style.[^o44] Wagner made a scant living by writing articles and short novelettes such as *A pilgrimage to Beethoven*, which sketched his growing concept of "music drama", and *An end in Paris*, where he depicts his own miseries as a German musician in the French metropolis.[^n36] He also provided arrangements of operas by other composers, largely on behalf of the [Schlesinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Schlesinger) publishing house. During this stay he completed his third and fourth operas *[Rienzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rienzi)* and *Der fliegende Holländer*.[^n36]

### Dresden (1842–1849) {#Dresden_(1842–1849)}

*Image: Richard Wagner c.1840.jpg.* Wagner c. 1840, by Ernest Benedikt Kietz

Wagner had completed *Rienzi* in 1840. With the strong support of [Giacomo Meyerbeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giacomo_Meyerbeer),[^n37] it was accepted for performance by the Dresden [Court Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semperoper#History) (*Hofoper*) in the [Kingdom of Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Saxony), and in 1842 Wagner moved to Dresden. His relief at returning to Germany was recorded in his "[Autobiographic Sketch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autobiographic_Sketch_%28Wagner%29)" of 1842, where he wrote that, en route from Paris, "For the first time I saw the [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine)—with hot tears in my eyes, I, poor artist, swore eternal fidelity to my German fatherland."[^n38] *Rienzi* was staged to considerable acclaim on 20 October.[^n39]

Wagner lived in Dresden for the next six years, eventually being appointed the Royal Saxon Court Conductor.[^n40] During this period, he staged there *Der fliegende Holländer* (2 January 1843)[^n41] and [*Tannhäuser*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tannhäuser_%28opera%29) (19 October 1845),[^n42] the first two of his three middle-period operas. Wagner also mixed with artistic circles in Dresden, including the composer [Ferdinand Hiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Hiller) and the architect [Gottfried Semper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Semper).[^n43][^n44]

Wagner's involvement in [left-wing politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing_politics) abruptly ended his welcome in Dresden. Wagner was active among [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist) German nationalists there, regularly receiving such guests as the conductor and radical editor [August Röckel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Röckel) and the Russian [anarchist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism) [Mikhail Bakunin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Bakunin).[^n45] He was also influenced by the ideas of [Pierre-Joseph Proudhon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon) and [Ludwig Feuerbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Feuerbach).[^n46] Widespread discontent came to a head in 1849, when the unsuccessful [May Uprising in Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Uprising_in_Dresden) broke out, in which Wagner played a [minor supporting role](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/May_Uprising_in_Dresden#Prominent_figures_amongst_the_revolutionaries). Warrants were issued for the revolutionaries' arrest. Wagner had to flee, first visiting Paris and then settling in [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich)[^n47] where he at first took refuge with a friend, [Alexander Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Müller_%28composer%29).[^o59]

### In exile: Switzerland (1849–1858) {#In_exile:_Switzerland_(1849–1858)}

*Image: Richard Wagners Steckbrief 1849.jpg.* Warrant for the arrest of Richard Wagner, issued on 16 May 1849

Wagner was to spend the next twelve years in exile from Germany. He had completed *[Lohengrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lohengrin_%28opera%29)*, the last of his middle-period operas, before the Dresden uprising, and now wrote desperately to his friend [Franz Liszt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Liszt) to have it staged in his absence. Liszt conducted the premiere in [Weimar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar) in August 1850.[^o61]

Nevertheless, Wagner was in grim personal straits, isolated from the German musical world and without any regular income. In 1850, Julie, the wife of his friend Karl Ritter, began to pay him a small pension which she maintained until 1859. With help from her friend Jessie Laussot, this was to have been augmented to an annual sum of 3,000 [thalers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thaler) per year, but the plan was abandoned when Wagner began an affair with Mme. Laussot. Wagner even plotted an elopement with her in 1850, which her husband prevented.[^n48][^n49] Meanwhile, Wagner's wife Minna, who had disliked the operas he had written after *Rienzi*, was falling into a deepening [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_%28mood_disorder%29). Wagner fell victim to ill health, according to [Ernest Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Newman) "largely a matter of overwrought nerves", which made it difficult for him to continue writing.[^n50]

Wagner's primary published output during his first years in Zürich was a set of essays. In "[The Artwork of the Future](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Artwork_of_the_Future)" (1849), he described a vision of opera as *[Gesamtkunstwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesamtkunstwerk)* (total work of art), in which music, song, dance, poetry, visual arts and stagecraft were unified. "[Judaism in Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism_in_Music)" (1850) was the first of Wagner's writings to feature [antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitism) views.[^n51] In this polemic Wagner asserted—often with vulgar, abusive language—that Jews lived as "outsiders" amid European societies and were disconnected from the national spirit ([*Volksgeist*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geist#Volksgeist)) of these countries, thus capable of producing only shallow and artificial imitations of European art music, despite having achieved technical proficiency in its study. According to Wagner, Jews such as Meyerbeer commercialised music catered to the masses in order to achieve fame and financial success, rather than creating genuine works of art.[^n52]

In "[Opera and Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_and_Drama)" (1851), Wagner described the [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) of music drama that he was using to create the *Ring* cycle. Before leaving Dresden, Wagner had drafted a scenario that eventually became *[Der Ring des Nibelungen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Ring_des_Nibelungen)*. He initially [wrote the libretto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Ring_des_Nibelungen:_Composition_of_the_poem) for a single opera, *Siegfrieds Tod* (*Siegfried's Death*), in 1848. After arriving in Zürich, he expanded the story with *Der junge Siegfried* (*Young Siegfried*), which explored the [hero's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigurd) background. He completed the text of the cycle by writing the libretti for *[Die Walküre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Walküre)* (*The [Valkyrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valkyrie)*) and *[Das Rheingold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Rheingold)* (*The Rhine Gold*) and revising the other libretti to conform to his new concept, completing them in 1852.[^n53] The concept of opera expressed in "Opera and Drama" and in other essays effectively renounced all the operas he had previously written through *Lohengrin.* Partly in an attempt to explain his change of views, Wagner published in 1851 the autobiographical "[A Communication to My Friends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Communication_to_My_Friends)".[^o68] This included his first public announcement of what was to become the *Ring* cycle:

> I shall never write an *Opera* more. As I have no wish to invent an arbitrary title for my works, I will call them Dramas ...
>
> I propose to produce my myth in three complete dramas, preceded by a lengthy Prelude (Vorspiel)....
>
> At a specially-appointed Festival, I propose, some future time, to produce those three Dramas with their Prelude, *in the course of three days and a fore-evening* \[emphasis in original\].[^n54]

Wagner began composing the music for *Das Rheingold* between November 1853 and September 1854, following it immediately with *Die Walküre* (written between June 1854 and March 1856).[^n55] He began work on the third *Ring* drama, which he now called simply *[Siegfried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_%28opera%29)*, probably in September 1856, but by June 1857 he had completed only the first two acts. He decided to put the work aside to concentrate on a new idea: *[Tristan und Isolde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_und_Isolde)*,[^n56] based on the [Arthurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_of_Britain) love story *[Tristan and Iseult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_and_Iseult)*.

*Image: Mathilde Wesendonck by Karl Ferdinand Sohn, 1850.jpg.* Portrait of [Mathilde Wesendonck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathilde_Wesendonck) (1850) by [Karl Ferdinand Sohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Ferdinand_Sohn)

One source of inspiration for *Tristan und Isolde* was the philosophy of [Arthur Schopenhauer](wikipedia://en/700), notably his *[The World as Will and Representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Representation)*, to which Wagner had been introduced in 1854 by his poet friend [Georg Herwegh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Herwegh). Wagner later called this the most important event of his life.[^n57] His personal circumstances certainly made him an easy convert to what he understood to be Schopenhauer's philosophy, sometimes categorised as "[philosophical pessimism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_pessimism)". He remained an adherent of Schopenhauer for the rest of his life.[^o73]

One of Schopenhauer's doctrines was that music held a supreme role in the arts as a direct expression of the world's essence, namely, blind, impulsive will.[^n58] This doctrine contradicted Wagner's view, expressed in "Opera and Drama", that the music in opera had to be subservient to the drama. Wagner scholars have argued that Schopenhauer's influence caused Wagner to assign a more commanding role to music in his later operas, including the latter half of the *Ring* cycle, which he had yet to compose.[^o75] Aspects of Schopenhauerian doctrine found their way into Wagner's subsequent libretti.

A second source of inspiration was Wagner's infatuation with the poet-writer [Mathilde Wesendonck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathilde_Wesendonck), the wife of the silk merchant Otto Wesendonck. Wagner met the Wesendoncks, who were both great admirers of his music, in Zürich in 1852. From May 1853 onwards Wesendonck made several loans to Wagner to finance his household expenses in Zürich,[^n59] and in 1857 placed a cottage on his estate at Wagner's disposal,[^n60][^n61] which became known as the *Asyl* ("asylum" or "place of rest"). During this period, Wagner's growing passion for his patron's wife inspired him to put aside work on the *Ring* cycle (which was not resumed for the next twelve years) and begin work on *Tristan*.[^n62] While planning the opera, Wagner composed the *[Wesendonck Lieder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesendonck_Lieder)*, five songs for voice and piano, setting poems by Mathilde. Two of these settings are explicitly subtitled by Wagner as "studies for *Tristan und Isolde*".[^n63]

Among the conducting engagements that Wagner undertook for revenue during this period, he gave several concerts in 1855 with the [Philharmonic Society of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Philharmonic_Society), including one before [Queen Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Victoria).[^n64] The Queen enjoyed his *Tannhäuser* overture and spoke with Wagner after the concert, writing in her diary that Wagner was "short, very quiet, wears spectacles & has a very finely-developed forehead, a hooked nose & projecting chin."[^o82]

### In exile: Venice and Paris (1858–1862) {#In_exile:_Venice_and_Paris_(1858–1862)}

Wagner's uneasy affair with Mathilde collapsed in 1858, when Minna intercepted a letter to Mathilde from him.[^n65] After the resulting confrontation with Minna, Wagner left Zürich alone, bound for [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice), where he rented an apartment in the [Palazzo Giustinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palazzo_Giustinian_%28Dorsoduro%29), while Minna returned to Germany.[^n66] Wagner's attitude to Minna had changed; the editor of his correspondence with her, John Burk, has said that she was to him "an invalid, to be treated with kindness and consideration, but, except at a distance, \[was\] a menace to his peace of mind."[^n67] Wagner continued his correspondence with Mathilde and his friendship with her husband Otto, who maintained his financial support of the composer. In an 1859 letter to Mathilde, Wagner wrote, half-satirically, of *Tristan*: "Child! This Tristan is turning into something *terrible*. This final act!!!—I fear the opera will be banned ... only mediocre performances can save me! Perfectly good ones will be bound to drive people mad."[^o87]

*Image: Richard Wagner, Paris, 1861.jpg.* Wagner in Paris, 1861

In November 1859, Wagner once again moved to Paris to oversee production of a new revision of *Tannhäuser*, staged thanks to the efforts of Princess [Pauline von Metternich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_von_Metternich), whose husband, [Richard von Metternich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_von_Metternich), was the Austrian ambassador in Paris. The performances of the Paris *Tannhäuser* in 1861 were [a notable fiasco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tannhäuser_%28opera%29#The_Paris_première). This was partly a consequence of the conservative tastes of the [Jockey Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jockey-Club_de_Paris), which organised demonstrations in the theatre to protest at the presentation of the ballet feature in Act 1 (instead of its traditional location in the second act); but the opportunity was also exploited by those who wanted to use the occasion as a veiled political protest against the pro-Austrian policies of [Napoleon III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_III).[^n68] It was during this visit that Wagner met the French poet [Charles Baudelaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Baudelaire), who wrote an appreciative brochure, "Richard Wagner et Tannhäuser à Paris".[^n69] The opera was withdrawn after the third performance and Wagner left Paris soon after.[^n70] He had sought a reconciliation with Minna during this Paris visit, and although she joined him there, the reunion was not successful and they again parted from each other when Wagner left.[^n71]

### Return and resurgence (1862–1871) {#Return_and_resurgence_(1862–1871)}

The political ban that had been placed on Wagner in the [German Confederation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Confederation) after he had fled Dresden was fully lifted in 1862. The composer settled in [Biebrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biebrich_%28Wiesbaden%29), on the Rhine near [Wiesbaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiesbaden) in [Hesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesse).[^n72] Here Minna visited him for the last time: they parted irrevocably,[^n73] though Wagner continued to give financial support to her while she lived in Dresden until her death in 1866.[^n74]

*Image: De 20 jarige Ludwig II in kroningsmantel door Ferdinand von Piloty 1865.jpg.* Portrait of [Ludwig II of Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_II_of_Bavaria) about the time when he first met Wagner, by Ferdinand von Piloty, 1865

In Biebrich, Wagner, at last, began work on *Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg*, his only mature comedy. Wagner wrote a first draft of the libretto in 1845,[^n75] and he had resolved to develop it during a visit he had made to Venice with the Wesendoncks in 1860, where he was inspired by [Titian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titian)'s painting *[The Assumption of the Virgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assumption_of_the_Virgin_%28Titian%29)*.[^n76] Throughout this period (1861–1864) Wagner sought to have *Tristan und Isolde* produced in Vienna.[^n77] Despite many rehearsals, the opera remained unperformed, and gained a reputation as being "impossible" to sing, which added to Wagner's financial problems.[^n78]

Wagner's fortunes took a dramatic upturn in 1864, when [King Ludwig II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_II_of_Bavaria) succeeded to the throne of [Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavaria) at the age of 18. The young king, an ardent admirer of Wagner's music, had the composer brought to Munich.[^n79] The King, who was homosexual[^o101], expressed in his correspondence a passionate personal adoration for Wagner, and Wagner in his responses had no scruples about feigning reciprocal feelings.[^n80][^n81] Ludwig settled Wagner's considerable debts[^n82] and proposed to stage *Tristan*, *Die Meistersinger*, the *Ring*, and the other operas Wagner planned.[^n83] Wagner also began to dictate his autobiography, *Mein Leben*, at the King's request.[^n84] Wagner noted that his rescue by Ludwig coincided with news of the death of his earlier mentor (but later supposed enemy) [Giacomo Meyerbeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giacomo_Meyerbeer), and regretted that "this operatic master, who had done me so much harm, should not have lived to see this day."[^n85]

After grave difficulties in rehearsal, *Tristan und Isolde* premiered at the [National Theatre Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theatre_Munich) on 10 June 1865, the first Wagner opera premiere in almost 15 years. (The premiere had been scheduled for 15 May, but was delayed by bailiffs acting for Wagner's creditors,[^n86] and also because the Isolde, [Malvina Schnorr von Carolsfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malvina_Garrigues), was hoarse and needed time to recover.) The conductor of this premiere was [Hans von Bülow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_von_Bülow), whose wife, [Cosima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosima_Wagner), had given birth in April that year to a daughter, named [Isolde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolde_Beidler), a child not of Bülow but of Wagner.[^n87]

Cosima was 24 years younger than Wagner and was herself illegitimate, the daughter of the Countess [Marie d'Agoult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_d'Agoult), who had left her husband for [Franz Liszt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Liszt).[^n88] Liszt initially disapproved of his daughter's involvement with Wagner, though nevertheless the two men were friends.[^n89] The indiscreet affair scandalised Munich, and Wagner also fell into disfavour with many leading members of the court, who were suspicious of his influence on the King.[^n90] In December 1865, Ludwig was finally forced to ask the composer to leave Munich.[^n91] He apparently also toyed with the idea of abdicating to follow his hero into exile, but Wagner quickly dissuaded him.[^n92]

*Image: Fritz Luckhardt - Richard y Cosima Wagner (9 de mayo de 1872, Viena).jpg.* Richard and Cosima Wagner, photographed in 1872

Ludwig installed Wagner at the [Villa Tribschen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Tribschen), beside Switzerland's [Lake Lucerne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Lucerne).[^n93] *Die Meistersinger* was completed at Tribschen in 1867, and premiered in Munich on 21 June the following year.[^n75] At Ludwig's insistence, "special previews" of the first two works of the *Ring*, *Das Rheingold* and *Die Walküre*, were performed at Munich in 1869 and 1870,[^n94] but Wagner retained his dream, first expressed in "A Communication to My Friends", to present the first complete cycle at a special festival with a new, dedicated, [opera house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_house).[^o118]

Minna died of a heart attack on 25 January 1866 in Dresden. Wagner did not attend the funeral.[^n95] Following Minna's death Cosima wrote to Hans von Bülow several times asking him to grant her a divorce, but Bülow refused to concede this. He consented only after she had two more children with Wagner: another daughter, named [Eva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eva_Chamberlain), after the heroine of *Meistersinger*, and a son [Siegfried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_Wagner), named after the hero of the *Ring*. The divorce was finally sanctioned, after delays in the legal process, by a Berlin court on 18 July 1870.[^n96] Richard and Cosima's wedding took place on 25 August 1870.[^n97] On Christmas Day 1870, Wagner arranged a surprise performance (its premiere) of the *[Siegfried Idyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_Idyll)* for Cosima's birthday.[^n98] The marriage to Cosima lasted to the end of Wagner's life.

Wagner, settled into his new-found domesticity, turned his energies towards completing the *Ring* cycle. He had not abandoned polemics: he republished his 1850 pamphlet "Judaism in Music", originally issued under a pseudonym, under his own name in 1869, extending the introduction and adding a lengthy final section. The publication led to several public protests at early performances of *Die Meistersinger* in Vienna and Mannheim.[^n99]

### Bayreuth (1871–1876) {#Bayreuth_(1871–1876)}

In 1871, Wagner decided to move to [Bayreuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth), which was to be the location of his new opera house.[^n100] The town council donated a large plot of land—the "Green Hill"—as a site for the theatre. The Wagners moved to the town the following year, and the foundation stone for the [Bayreuth Festspielhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth_Festspielhaus) ("Festival Theatre") was laid. Wagner initially announced the first Bayreuth Festival, at which for the first time the *Ring* cycle was presented complete, for 1873,[^n101] but since Ludwig had declined to finance the project, the start of building was delayed and the proposed date for the festival was deferred. To raise funds for the construction, "[Wagner societies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Wagner_Societies)" were formed in several cities,[^n102] and Wagner began touring Germany conducting concerts.[^n103] By the spring of 1873, only a third of the required funds had been raised; further pleas to Ludwig were initially ignored, but early in 1874, with the project on the verge of collapse, the King relented and provided a loan.[^n104][^n105] The full building programme included the family home, "[Wahnfried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahnfried)", into which Wagner, with Cosima and the children, moved from their temporary accommodation on 18 April 1874.[^n106][^n107] The theatre was completed in 1875, and the festival was scheduled for the following year. Commenting on the struggle to finish the building, Wagner remarked to Cosima: "Each stone is red with my blood and yours."[^o133]

*Image: Festspielhaus Bayreuth 1900.jpg.* The [Bayreuth Festspielhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth_Festspielhaus): [photochrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochrom) print of c. 1895

For the design of the Festspielhaus, Wagner appropriated some of the ideas of his former colleague, Gottfried Semper, which he had previously solicited for a proposed new opera house in Munich.[^n101] Wagner was responsible for several theatrical innovations at Bayreuth; these include darkening the auditorium during performances, and placing the orchestra in a pit out of view of the audience.[^n108]

The Festspielhaus finally opened on 13 August 1876 with *Das Rheingold*, at last taking its place as the first evening of the complete *Ring* cycle; the 1876 [Bayreuth Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth_Festival) therefore saw the premiere of the complete cycle, performed as a sequence as the composer had intended.[^n109] The 1876 Festival consisted of three full *Ring* cycles (under the baton of [Hans Richter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Richter_%28conductor%29)).[^n110] At the end, critical reactions ranged between that of the Norwegian composer [Edvard Grieg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edvard_Grieg), who thought the work "divinely composed", and that of the French newspaper [Le Figaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Figaro), which called the music "the dream of a lunatic".[^n111] The disillusioned included Wagner's (then) friend [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche), who, having published his eulogistic essay "Richard Wagner in Bayreuth" before the festival as part of his *[Untimely Meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untimely_Meditations)*, was bitterly disappointed by what he saw as Wagner's pandering to increasingly exclusivist German nationalism; his breach with Wagner began at this time.[^n112] The festival firmly established Wagner as an artist of European, and indeed world, importance: attendees included [Kaiser Wilhelm I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser_Wilhelm_I), the Emperor [Pedro II of Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_II_of_Brazil), [Anton Bruckner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Bruckner), [Camille Saint-Saëns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camille_Saint-Saëns) and [Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyotr_Ilyich_Tchaikovsky).[^n113]

Wagner was far from satisfied with the Festival; Cosima recorded that months later his attitude towards the productions was "Never again, never again!"[^n114] Moreover, the festival finished with a deficit of about 150,000 marks.[^o142] The expenses of Bayreuth and of Wahnfried meant that Wagner still sought further sources of income by conducting or taking on commissions such as the *Centennial March* for America, for which he received \$5,000.[^n115][^n116][^o145]

### Last years (1876–1883) {#Last_years_(1876–1883)}

Following the first Bayreuth Festival, Wagner began work on *[Parsifal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsifal)*, his final opera. The composition took four years, much of which Wagner spent in Italy for health reasons.[^n117] From 1876 to 1878 Wagner also embarked on the last of his documented emotional liaisons, this time with [Judith Gautier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Gautier), whom he had met at the 1876 Festival.[^n118] Wagner was also much troubled by problems of financing *Parsifal*, and by the prospect of the work being performed by other theatres than Bayreuth. He was once again assisted by the liberality of King Ludwig, but was still forced by his personal financial situation in 1877 to sell the rights of several of his unpublished works (including the *Siegfried Idyll*) to the publisher [Schott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schott_Music).[^n119]

*Image: WahnfriedBayreuth11.JPG.* The Wagner grave in the Wahnfried garden; in 1977 Cosima's ashes were placed alongside Wagner's body.

Wagner wrote several articles in his later years, often on political topics, and often [reactionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactionary) in tone, repudiating some of his earlier, more liberal, views. These include "Religion and Art" (1880) and "Heroism and Christianity" (1881), which were printed in the journal *[Bayreuther Blätter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuther_Blätter)*, published by his supporter [Hans von Wolzogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_von_Wolzogen).[^o150] Wagner's sudden interest in Christianity at this period, which infuses *Parsifal*, was contemporary with his increasing alignment with [German nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_nationalism), and required on his part, and the part of his associates, "the rewriting of some recent Wagnerian history", so as to represent, for example, the *Ring* as a work reflecting Christian ideals.[^n120] Many of these later articles, including "What is German?" (1878, but based on a draft written in the 1860s),[^n121] repeated Wagner's antisemitic preoccupations.

Wagner completed *Parsifal* in January 1882, and a second Bayreuth Festival was held for the new opera, which premiered on 26 May.[^n122] Wagner was by this time extremely ill, having suffered a series of increasingly severe [angina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angina) attacks.[^n123] During the sixteenth and final performance of *Parsifal* on 29 August, he entered the pit unseen during act 3, took the baton from conductor [Hermann Levi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Levi), and led the performance to its conclusion.[^n124]

After the festival, the Wagner family journeyed to [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) for the winter. Wagner died of a heart attack at the age of 69 on 13 February 1883 at [Ca' Vendramin Calergi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ca'_Vendramin_Calergi), a 16th-century [palazzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palazzo) on the [Grand Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_%28Venice%29).[^n125] The legend that the attack was prompted by an argument with Cosima over Wagner's supposedly amorous interest in the singer [Carrie Pringle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrie_Pringle), who had been a Flower-maiden in *Parsifal* at Bayreuth, is without credible evidence.[^n126] After a funerary [gondola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondola) bore Wagner's remains over the Grand Canal, his body was taken to Germany where it was buried in the garden of the Villa Wahnfried in Bayreuth.[^n127]

## Works {#Works}

Wagner's musical output is listed by the *Wagner-Werk-Verzeichnis* (WWV) as comprising 113 works, including fragments and projects.[^o160] The first complete scholarly edition of his musical works in print was commenced in 1970 under the aegis of the [Bavarian Academy of Fine Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavarian_Academy_of_Fine_Arts) and the [Akademie der Wissenschaften und der Literatur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akademie_der_Wissenschaften_und_der_Literatur) of [Mainz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainz), and is presently under the editorship of [Egon Voss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egon_Voss). It will consist of 21 volumes (57 books) of music and 10 volumes (13 books) of relevant documents and texts. As at October 2017, three volumes remain to be published. The publisher is [Schott Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schott_Music).[^n128]

### Operas {#Operas}

*Image: Siegfried leitmotif.jpg.* [Leitmotif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitmotif) associated with the horn-call of the hero of Wagner's opera [*Siegfried*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_%28opera%29)

Wagner's operatic works are his primary artistic legacy. Unlike most opera composers, who generally left the task of writing the [libretto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libretto) (the text and lyrics) to others, Wagner wrote his own libretti, which he referred to as "poems".[^n129]

From 1849 onwards, he urged a new concept of opera often referred to as "music drama" (although he later rejected this term),[^n130] in which all musical, poetic and dramatic elements were to be fused together—the *[Gesamtkunstwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesamtkunstwerk)*. Wagner developed a compositional style in which the importance of the orchestra is equal to that of the singers. The orchestra's dramatic role in the later operas includes the use of [leitmotifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitmotif), musical phrases that can be interpreted as announcing specific characters, locales, and plot elements; their complex interweaving and evolution illuminate the progression of the drama.[^n131] These operas are still, despite Wagner's reservations, referred to by many writers[^o166] as "music dramas".[^o167]

#### Early works (to 1842) {#Early_works_(to_1842)}

Wagner's earliest attempts at opera were often uncompleted. Abandoned works include [a pastoral opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Laune_des_Verliebten) based on [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe)'s *Die Laune des Verliebten* (*The Infatuated Lover's Caprice*), written at the age of 17,[^n20] *[Die Hochzeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Hochzeit)* (*The Wedding*), on which Wagner worked in 1832,[^n20] and the [singspiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singspiel) *[Männerlist größer als Frauenlist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Männerlist_größer_als_Frauenlist)* (*Men are More Cunning than Women*, 1837–1838). *[Die Feen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Feen)* (*The Fairies*, 1833) was not performed in the composer's lifetime,[^n22] and *[Das Liebesverbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Liebesverbot)* (*The Ban on Love*, 1836) was withdrawn after its first performance.[^n24] *[Rienzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rienzi)* (1842) was Wagner's first opera to be successfully staged.[^n132] The compositional style of these early works was conventional—the relatively more sophisticated *Rienzi* showing the clear influence of [Grand Opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Opera) *à la* [Spontini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspare_Spontini) and Meyerbeer—and did not exhibit the innovations that would mark Wagner's place in musical history. Later in life, Wagner said that he did not consider these works to be part of his *oeuvre*,[^n133] and they have been performed only rarely in the last hundred years, although the overture to *Rienzi* is an occasional concert-hall piece. *Die Feen*, *Das Liebesverbot*, and *Rienzi* were performed at both Leipzig and Bayreuth in 2013 to mark the composer's bicentenary.[^o175]

#### "Romantic operas" (1843–1851) {#"Romantic_operas"_(1843–1851)}

*Image: Der fliegende Holländer.jpg.* Opening of overture to *Der fliegende Holländer* in Wagner's hand and with his notes to the publisher

Wagner's middle stage output began with *[Der fliegende Holländer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_fliegende_Holländer)* (*The Flying Dutchman*, 1843), followed by *[Tannhäuser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tannhäuser_%28opera%29)* (1845) and *[Lohengrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lohengrin_%28opera%29)* (1850). These three operas are sometimes referred to as Wagner's "romantic operas".[^o177] They reinforced the reputation, among the public in Germany and beyond, that Wagner had begun to establish with *Rienzi*. Although distancing himself from the style of these operas from 1849 onwards, he nevertheless reworked both *Der fliegende Holländer* and *Tannhäuser* on several occasions. These three operas are considered to represent a significant developmental stage in Wagner's musical and operatic maturity as regards thematic handling, portrayal of emotions and orchestration.[^n134] They are the earliest works included in the [Bayreuth canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayreuth_canon), the mature operas that Cosima staged at the Bayreuth Festival after Wagner's death in accordance with his wishes.[^n135] All three (including the differing versions of *Der fliegende Holländer* and *Tannhäuser*) continue to be regularly performed throughout the world and have been frequently recorded. They were also the operas by which his fame spread during his lifetime.

#### "Music dramas" (1851–1882) {#"Music_dramas"_(1851–1882)}

##### Starting the *Ring* {#Starting_the_Ring}

*Image: Ring22.jpg.* [Brünnhilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brünnhilde) the [Valkyrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valkyrie), as illustrated by [Arthur Rackham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Rackham) (1910)

Wagner's late dramas are considered his masterpieces. *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, commonly referred to as the *Ring* or "*Ring* cycle", is a set of four operas based loosely on figures and elements of [Germanic mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_mythology)—particularly from the later [Norse mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_mythology)—notably the [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse) *[Poetic Edda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetic_Edda)* and *[Volsunga Saga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volsunga_Saga)*, and the [Middle High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_High_German) *[Nibelungenlied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nibelungenlied)*.[^o182] Wagner specifically developed the libretti for these operas according to his interpretation of *[Stabreim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliterative_verse#Old_High_German_and_Old_Saxon)*, highly alliterative rhyming verse-pairs used in old Germanic poetry.[^n136] They were also influenced by Wagner's concepts of [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) drama, in which [tetralogies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetralogy) were a component of [Athenian festivals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_festivals), and which he had amply discussed in his essay "[Oper und Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oper_und_Drama)".[^n137]

The first two components of the *Ring* cycle were *[Das Rheingold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Rheingold)* (*The Rhinegold*), which was completed in 1854, and *[Die Walküre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Walküre)* (*The Valkyrie*), which was finished in 1856. In *Das Rheingold*, with its "relentlessly talky 'realism' \[and\] the absence of lyrical '[numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_%28music%29),[^n138] Wagner came very close to the musical ideals of his 1849–1851 essays. *Die Walküre*, which contains what is virtually a traditional [aria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aria) (Siegmund's *Winterstürme* in the first act), and the quasi-[choral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choral_music) appearance of the [Valkyries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valkyrie) themselves, shows more "operatic" traits, but has been assessed by Barry Millington as "the music drama that most satisfactorily embodies the theoretical principles of 'Oper und Drama'... A thoroughgoing synthesis of poetry and music is achieved without any notable sacrifice in musical expression."[^n139]

##### *Tristan und Isolde* and *Die Meistersinger* {#Tristan_und_Isolde_and_Die_Meistersinger}

While composing the opera *[Siegfried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_%28opera%29)*, the third part of the *Ring* cycle, Wagner interrupted work on it and between 1857 and 1864 wrote the tragic love story *[Tristan und Isolde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_und_Isolde)* and his only mature comedy *[Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Meistersinger_von_Nürnberg)* (*The Mastersingers of Nuremberg*), two works that are also part of the regular operatic canon.[^n140] [Harold C. Schonberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_C._Schonberg) writes: "The opening chords of Tristan were to the last half of the nineteenth century what \[Beethoven's\] [*Eroica*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._3_%28Beethoven%29) and Ninth Symphonies had been to the first half—a breakaway, a new concept... In *Tristan*, harmonic relations are pushed to their breaking point, and twentieth century scholars see in the opera the beginnings of [atonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonality)."[^n141]

*Image: Betz Franz.png.* [Franz Betz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Betz) (by Fritz Luckhardt), who created the role of Hans Sachs in *Die Meistersinger*, and sang Wotan in the first complete *Ring* cycle

*Tristan* is often granted a special place in musical history; many see it as the beginning of the move away from conventional [harmony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony) and [tonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonality) and consider that it lays the groundwork for the direction of classical music in the 20th century.[^n75][^n142][^n143] Wagner felt that his musico-dramatical theories were most perfectly realised in this work with its use of "the art of transition" between dramatic elements and the balance achieved between vocal and orchestral lines.[^n144] Completed in 1859, the work was given its first performance in Munich, conducted by Bülow, in June 1865.[^n145]

*Die Meistersinger* was originally conceived by Wagner in 1845 as a sort of comic pendant to *Tannhäuser*.[^n146] Like *Tristan*, it was premiered in Munich under the baton of Bülow, on 21 June 1868, and became an immediate success.[^n147] Millington describes *Meistersinger* as "a rich, perceptive music drama widely admired for its warm humanity",[^n148] but its strong German [nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism) overtones have led some to cite it as an example of Wagner's reactionary politics and antisemitism.[^o198]

##### Completing the *Ring* {#Completing_the_Ring}

When Wagner returned to writing the music for the last act of *Siegfried* and for *[Götterdämmerung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Götterdämmerung)* (*Twilight of the Gods*) as the final part of the *Ring*, his style had changed once more to something more recognisable as "operatic" than the aural world of *Rheingold* and *Walküre*, though it was still thoroughly stamped with his own originality as a composer and suffused with leitmotifs.[^n149] This was in part because the libretti of the four *Ring* operas had been written in reverse order, so that the book for *Götterdämmerung* was conceived more "traditionally" than that of *Rheingold*;[^n150] still, the self-imposed strictures of the *Gesamtkunstwerk* had become relaxed. The differences also result from Wagner's development as a composer during the period in which he wrote *Tristan*, *Meistersinger* and the Paris version of *Tannhäuser*.[^n151] From Act 3 of *Siegfried* onwards, the *Ring* becomes more [chromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromaticism) melodically, more complex harmonically and more developmental in its treatment of leitmotifs.[^n152]

Wagner took 26 years from writing the first draft of a libretto in 1848 until he completed *Götterdämmerung* in 1874. The *Ring* takes about 15 hours to perform[^n153] and is the only undertaking of such size to be regularly presented on the world's stages.

##### *Parsifal* {#Parsifal}

Wagner's final opera, *[Parsifal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsifal)* (1882), which was his only work written especially for his Bayreuth Festspielhaus and which is described in the score as a "*Bühnenweihfestspiel*" ("festival play for the consecration of the stage"), has a storyline suggested by elements of the legend of the [Holy Grail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Grail). It also carries elements of [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) renunciation suggested by Wagner's readings of Schopenhauer.[^n154] Wagner described it to Cosima as his "last card".[^n155] It remains controversial because of its treatment of Christianity, its eroticism, and its expression, as perceived by some commentators, of German nationalism and antisemitism.[^n156] Despite the composer's own description of the opera to King Ludwig as "this most Christian of works",[^n157] Ulrike Kienzle has commented that "Wagner's turn to Christian mythology, upon which the imagery and spiritual contents of *Parsifal* rest, is idiosyncratic and contradicts Christian [dogma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma) in many ways."[^n158] Musically the opera has been held to represent a continuing development of the composer's style, and Millington describes it as "a diaphanous score of unearthly beauty and refinement".[^n26]

### Non-operatic music {#Non-operatic_music}

*Image: Gill Wagner.jpg.* [André Gill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Gill) suggesting that Wagner's music was ear-splitting. Cover of *[L'Éclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Éclipse)* 18 April 1869.

Apart from his operas, Wagner composed relatively few pieces of music. These include his [Symphony in C major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_in_C_major_%28Wagner%29) (written at the age of 19), the *[Faust Overture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faust_Overture)* (the only completed part of an intended symphony on the subject), some [concert overtures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_overture), and choral and piano pieces.[^n159] His most commonly performed work that is not an extract from an opera is the *[Siegfried Idyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_Idyll)* for chamber orchestra, which has several motifs in common with the *Ring* cycle.[^n160] The *[Wesendonck Lieder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesendonck_Lieder)* are also often performed, either in the original piano version or with orchestral accompaniment. More rarely performed are the *American Centennial March* (1876), and *[Das Liebesmahl der Apostel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Liebesmahl_der_Apostel)* (*The Love Feast of the Apostles*), a piece for male choruses and orchestra composed in 1843 for the city of Dresden.[^n161]

After completing *Parsifal*, Wagner expressed his intention to turn to the writing of symphonies,[^n162] and several sketches dating from the late 1870s and early 1880s have been identified as work towards this end.[^n163] The overtures and certain orchestral passages from Wagner's middle- and late-stage operas are commonly played as concert pieces. For most of these, Wagner wrote or rewrote short passages to ensure musical coherence. The "[Bridal Chorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridal_Chorus)" from *Lohengrin* is frequently played as the bride's processional [wedding march](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding_march) in English-speaking countries.[^n164]

### Prose writings {#Prose_writings}

Wagner was an extremely prolific writer, authoring many books, poems, and articles, as well as voluminous correspondence. His writings covered a wide range of topics, including autobiography, politics, philosophy, and detailed analyses of his own operas.

Wagner planned for a collected edition of his publications as early as 1865;[^n165] he believed that such an edition would help the world understand his intellectual development and artistic aims.[^n166] The first such edition was published between 1871 and 1883, but was doctored to suppress or alter articles that were an embarrassment to him (e.g. those praising Meyerbeer), or by altering dates on some articles to reinforce Wagner's own account of his progress.[^n167] Wagner's autobiography *Mein Leben* was originally published for close friends only in a very small edition (15–18 copies per volume) in four volumes between 1870 and 1880. The first public edition (with many passages suppressed by Cosima) appeared in 1911; the first attempt at a full edition (in German) appeared in 1963.[^n168]

There have been modern complete or partial editions of Wagner's writings,[^n169] including a centennial edition in German edited by [Dieter Borchmeyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dieter_Borchmeyer) (which, however, omitted the essay "[Das Judenthum in der Musik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Judenthum_in_der_Musik)" and *Mein Leben*).[^n170] The English translations of Wagner's prose in eight volumes by [William Ashton Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ashton_Ellis) (1892–1899) are still in print and commonly used, despite their deficiencies.[^n171] The first complete historical and critical edition of Wagner's prose works was launched in 2013 at the Institute for Music Research at the [University of Würzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Würzburg); this will result in at least eight volumes of text and several volumes of commentary, totalling over 5,000 pages. It was originally anticipated that the project will be completed by 2030.[^o227]

A complete edition of Wagner's correspondence, estimated to amount to between 10,000 and 12,000 items, is underway under the supervision of the University of Würzburg. As of January 2021, 25 volumes have appeared, covering the period to 1873.[^o228]

## Influence and legacy {#Influence_and_legacy}

### Influence on music {#Influence_on_music}

Wagner's later musical style introduced new ideas in harmony, melodic process (leitmotif) and operatic structure. Notably from *Tristan und Isolde* onwards, he explored the limits of the traditional tonal system, which gave keys and chords their identity, pointing the way to [atonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonality) in the 20th century. Some music historians date the beginning of [modern classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_classical_music) to the first notes of *Tristan*, which include the so-called [Tristan chord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_chord).[^n172][^n173] Schonberg writes "More than any other composer in history up to that point, Wagner made the orchestra an equal partner in the drama. It is in Wagner's big, resonantly scored orchestra where much of the opera is explained—where psychological changes of the characters, their motivations, drives, desires, loves, hates, are all explained."[^n174]

*Image: Photo of Gustav Mahler by Moritz Nähr 01.jpg.* Gustav Mahler in 1907

Wagner inspired great devotion. For an extended period, many composers were inclined to align themselves with or against Wagner's music. [Anton Bruckner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Bruckner) and [Hugo Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Wolf) were greatly indebted to him, as were [César Franck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/César_Franck), [Henri Duparc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Duparc_%28composer%29), [Ernest Chausson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Chausson), [Jules Massenet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Massenet), [Richard Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Strauss), [Alexander von Zemlinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Zemlinsky), [Hans Pfitzner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Pfitzner) and many others.[^o234] [Gustav Mahler](wikipedia://en/65174) was devoted to Wagner and his music: he sought him out on his 1875 visit to Vienna at the age of 15,[^n175] and became a renowned Wagner conductor;[^n176] his compositions were seen by [Richard Taruskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Taruskin) as extending Wagner's "maximalization" of "the temporal and the sonorous" in music to the world of the symphony.[^n177] The harmonic revolutions of [Claude Debussy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Debussy) and [Arnold Schoenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Schoenberg) (both of whose *oeuvres* contain examples of tonal and [atonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonality) modernism) have often been traced back to *Tristan* and *Parsifal*.[^n178][^n179] The Italian form of operatic [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_%28theatre%29) known as [verismo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verismo) owed much to the Wagnerian concept of musical form.[^n180]

Wagner made a major contribution to the principles and practice of conducting. His essay "About Conducting" (1869)[^n181] advanced [Hector Berlioz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector_Berlioz)'s technique of conducting and claimed that conducting was a means by which a musical work could be re-interpreted, rather than simply a mechanism for achieving orchestral unison. He exemplified this approach in his own conducting, which was significantly more flexible than the disciplined approach of [Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn); in his view, this also justified practices that were later frowned upon, such as the rewriting of scores.[^n182] [Wilhelm Furtwängler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Furtwängler) felt that Wagner and Bülow, through their interpretative approach, inspired a whole new generation of conductors (including Furtwängler himself).[^n183]

Among those from the late 20th century and beyond claiming inspiration from Wagner's music are the German band [Rammstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rammstein);[^n184] [Jim Steinman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Steinman), who wrote songs for [Meat Loaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meat_Loaf), [Bonnie Tyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonnie_Tyler), [Air Supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Supply), [Celine Dion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celine_Dion) and others;[^o245] and the electronic composer [Klaus Schulze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Schulze), whose 1975 album *[Timewind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timewind)* consists of two 30-minute tracks, "Bayreuth Return" and "Wahnfried 1883". [Joey DeMaio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joey_DeMaio) of the band [Manowar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manowar) has described Wagner as "the father of [heavy metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_metal_music)".[^n185] The Slovenian group [Laibach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laibach_%28band%29) created the 2009 suite *VolksWagner*, using material from Wagner's operas.[^o247] [Phil Spector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Spector)'s [Wall of Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_of_Sound) recording technique was, it has been claimed, heavily influenced by Wagner.[^n186]

### Influence on literature, philosophy and the visual arts {#Influence_on_literature,_philosophy_and_the_visual_arts}

*Image: Nietzsche1882.jpg.* Friedrich Nietzsche in 1882

Wagner's influence on literature and philosophy is significant. Millington has commented:

> \[Wagner's\] protean abundance meant that he could inspire the use of literary motif in many a novel employing interior [monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monologue); ... the [Symbolists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_%28arts%29) saw him as a mystic hierophant; the [Decadents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decadent_movement) found many a frisson in his work.[^n187]

[Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche) was a member of Wagner's inner circle during the early 1870s. Nietzsche's first published work, *[The Birth of Tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birth_of_Tragedy)*, proposed Wagner's music as the [Dionysian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysian) "rebirth" of European culture in opposition to [Apollonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonian_and_Dionysian) rationalist "decadence". Nietzsche broke with Wagner following the first Bayreuth Festival in 1876, believing that Wagner's final phase represented a pandering to Christian pieties and a surrender to the new [German Reich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Empire).[^n188] Nevertheless, in *[Thus Spoke Zarathustra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thus_Spoke_Zarathustra)*, Nietzsche alluded to Wagner as the "old sorcerer", a reference to the captivating power of Wagner's music.[^o252] Nietzsche expressed his displeasure with the later Wagner in *[The Case of Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Case_of_Wagner)* and *[Nietzsche contra Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nietzsche_contra_Wagner)*.[^n188]

The poets [Charles Baudelaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Baudelaire), [Stéphane Mallarmé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stéphane_Mallarmé) and [Paul Verlaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Verlaine) worshipped Wagner.[^n189] [Édouard Dujardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Édouard_Dujardin), whose influential novel *[Les Lauriers sont coupés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Lauriers_sont_coupés)* is in the form of an interior monologue inspired by Wagnerian music, founded a journal dedicated to Wagner, *[La Revue wagnérienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Revue_wagnérienne)*, to which [J. K. Huysmans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._K._Huysmans) and [Téodor de Wyzewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Téodor_de_Wyzewa) contributed.[^n190] In a list of major cultural figures influenced by Wagner, [Bryan Magee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Magee) includes [D. H. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._H._Lawrence), [Aubrey Beardsley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aubrey_Beardsley), [Romain Rolland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romain_Rolland), [Gérard de Nerval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gérard_de_Nerval), [Pierre-Auguste Renoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Auguste_Renoir), and [Rainer Maria Rilke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Maria_Rilke).[^n191]

In the 20th century, [W. H. Auden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._H._Auden) once called Wagner "perhaps the greatest genius that ever lived",[^o257] while [Thomas Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann)[^n188] and [Marcel Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust)[^n192] were heavily influenced by him and discussed Wagner in their novels. He is also discussed in some of the works of [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce),[^n193] as well as [W. E. B. Du Bois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._E._B._Du_Bois), who featured *Lohengrin* in *[The Souls of Black Folk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Souls_of_Black_Folk)*.[^n194] Wagnerian themes inhabit [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot)'s *[The Waste Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Waste_Land)*, which contains lines from *Tristan und Isolde* and *Götterdämmerung*; and Verlaine's poem on *Parsifal*.[^n195]

Many of Wagner's concepts, including his speculation about dreams, predated their investigation by [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud).[^n196] Wagner had publicly analysed the Oedipus myth before Freud was born in terms of its psychological significance, insisting that incestuous desires are natural and normal, and perceptively exhibiting the relationship between sexuality and anxiety.[^n197] [Georg Groddeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Groddeck) considered the *Ring* as the first manual of psychoanalysis.[^n198]

### Influence on cinema {#Influence_on_cinema}

Wagner's use of leitmotifs and the integrated musical expression they can enable has influenced many 20th- and 21st-century [film scores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_score). The critic [Theodor Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Adorno) has noted that the Wagnerian leitmotif "leads directly to [cinema music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_music) where the sole function of the leitmotif is to announce heroes or situations so as to allow the audience to orient itself more easily".[^n199] Film scores citing Wagnerian themes include the *[Looney Tunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Looney_Tunes)* short *[What's Opera, Doc?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What's_Opera,_Doc?)* and [Francis Ford Coppola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Ford_Coppola)'s *[Apocalypse Now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypse_Now)*, which both feature a version of the [Ride of the Valkyries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ride_of_the_Valkyries);[^o268] [Trevor Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trevor_Jones_%28composer%29)'s soundtrack to [John Boorman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Boorman)'s film [*Excalibur*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excalibur_%28film%29);[^n200] and the 2011 films *[A Dangerous Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Dangerous_Method)* (dir. [David Cronenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cronenberg)) and *[Melancholia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melancholia_%282011_film%29)* (dir. [Lars von Trier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_von_Trier)).[^o270] [Hans-Jürgen Syberberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans-Jürgen_Syberberg)'s 1977 film *[Hitler: A Film from Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitler:_A_Film_from_Germany)*'s visual style and set design are strongly inspired by *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, musical excerpts from which are frequently used in the film's soundtrack.[^o271]

### Opponents and supporters {#Opponents_and_supporters}

*Image: Hanslick.jpg.* Eduard Hanslick

Not all reactions to Wagner were positive. For a time, German musical life divided into two factions, supporters of Wagner and supporters of [Johannes Brahms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Brahms); the latter, with the support of the powerful critic [Eduard Hanslick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Hanslick) (of whom Beckmesser in *Meistersinger* is in part a caricature), championed traditional forms and led the conservative front against Wagnerian innovations.[^n201] They were supported by the conservative leanings of some German music schools, including the [conservatories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_school) at [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Music_and_Theatre_Leipzig) under [Ignaz Moscheles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignaz_Moscheles) and at [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochschule_für_Musik_und_Tanz_Köln) under the direction of Ferdinand Hiller.[^n202] Another Wagner detractor was the French composer [Charles-Valentin Alkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles-Valentin_Alkan), who wrote to Hiller after attending Wagner's Paris concert on 25 January 1860, at which Wagner conducted the overtures to *Der fliegende Holländer* and *Tannhäuser*, the preludes to *Lohengrin* and *Tristan und Isolde*, and six other extracts from *Tannhäuser* and *Lohengrin*: "I had imagined that I was going to meet music of an innovative kind but was astonished to find a pale imitation of Berlioz ... I do not like all the music of Berlioz while appreciating his marvellous understanding of certain instrumental effects ... but here he was imitated and caricatured ... Wagner is not a musician, he is a disease."[^o275]

Even those who, like Debussy, opposed Wagner ("this old poisoner")[^o276] could not deny his influence. Indeed, Debussy was one of many composers, including Tchaikovsky, who felt the need to break with Wagner precisely because his influence was so unmistakable and overwhelming. "Golliwogg's Cakewalk" from Debussy's *[Children's Corner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children's_Corner)* piano suite contains a deliberately tongue-in-cheek quotation from the opening bars of *Tristan*.[^n203] Others who proved resistant to Wagner's operas included [Gioachino Rossini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gioachino_Rossini), who said "Wagner has wonderful moments, and dreadful quarters of an hour."[^o278] In the 20th century Wagner's music was parodied by [Paul Hindemith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Hindemith) and [Hanns Eisler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanns_Eisler), among others.[^n204]

Wagner's followers (known as Wagnerians or Wagnerites)[^o280] have formed many societies dedicated to Wagner's life and work.[^o281]

### Film and stage portrayals {#Film_and_stage_portrayals}

Wagner has been the subject of many biographical films. The earliest was a silent film made by [Carl Froelich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Froelich) in 1913 and featured in the title role the composer [Giuseppe Becce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Becce), who also wrote the score for the film (as Wagner's music, still in copyright, was not available).[^n205] Other film portrayals of Wagner include: [Alan Badel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Badel) in *[Magic Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_Fire)* (1955), [Lyndon Brook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_Brook) in *[Song Without End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_Without_End)* (1960), [Trevor Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trevor_Howard) in *[Ludwig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_%28film%29)* (1972), [Paul Nicholas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nicholas) in *[Lisztomania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisztomania_%28film%29)* (1975), and [Richard Burton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Burton) in *[Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_%28film%29)* (1983).

[Jonathan Harvey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Harvey_%28composer%29)'s opera *[Wagner Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner_Dream)* (2007) intertwines the events surrounding Wagner's death with the story of Wagner's uncompleted opera outline *[Die Sieger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Sieger) (The Victors)*.[^n206]

### Bayreuth Festival {#Bayreuth_Festival}

Since Wagner's death, the Bayreuth Festival, which has become an annual event, has been successively directed by his widow, his son Siegfried, the latter's widow [Winifred Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winifred_Wagner), their two sons [Wieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wieland_Wagner) and [Wolfgang Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Wagner), and, presently, two of the composer's great-granddaughters, [Eva Wagner-Pasquier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eva_Wagner-Pasquier) and [Katharina Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharina_Wagner).[^o286] Since 1973, the festival has been overseen by the [Richard-Wagner-Stiftung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner_Foundation) (Richard Wagner Foundation), the members of which include some of Wagner's descendants.[^o287]

### Nazi appropriation {#Nazi_appropriation}

[Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler) was an admirer of Wagner's music and saw in his operas an embodiment of his own vision of the German nation; in a 1922 speech he claimed that Wagner's works glorified "the heroic Teutonic nature ... Greatness lies in the heroic."[^o289] Hitler visited Bayreuth frequently from 1923 onwards and attended the productions at the theatre.[^n207] There continues to be debate about the extent to which Wagner's views might have influenced [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) thinking. [Houston Stewart Chamberlain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston_Stewart_Chamberlain) (1855–1927), who married Wagner's daughter Eva in 1908 but never met Wagner, was the author of *[The Foundations of the Nineteenth Century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Foundations_of_the_Nineteenth_Century)*, approved by the Nazi movement.[^n208] Chamberlain met Hitler several times between 1923 and 1927 in Bayreuth, but cannot credibly be regarded as a conduit of Wagner's own views.[^o292] The Nazis used those parts of Wagner's thought that were useful for propaganda and ignored or suppressed the rest.[^o293]

While Bayreuth presented a useful front for Nazi culture, and Wagner's music was used at many Nazi events,[^o294] the Nazi hierarchy as a whole did not share Hitler's enthusiasm for Wagner's operas and resented attending these lengthy epics at Hitler's insistence.[^n209] Some Nazi ideologists, most notably [Alfred Rosenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Rosenberg), rejected *Parsifal* as excessively Christian and pacifist.[^o296]

Guido Fackler has researched evidence that indicates that it is possible that Wagner's music was used at the [Dachau concentration camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dachau_concentration_camp) in 1933–1934 to "reeducate" [political prisoners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_prisoner) by exposure to "national music".[^o297] There has been no evidence to support claims, sometimes made,[^o298] that his music was played at [Nazi death camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extermination_camp) during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), and Pamela Potter has noted that Wagner's music was explicitly off-limits in the camps.

Because of the associations of Wagner with antisemitism and Nazism, the [performance of his music in the State of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner's_music_in_Israel) has been a source of controversy.[^o299]

## Views {#Views}

Wagner's operas, writings, politics, beliefs and unorthodox lifestyle made him a controversial figure during his lifetime.[^n210] Following his death, debate about his ideas and their interpretation, particularly in Germany during the 20th century, has continued.

### Racism and antisemitism {#Racism_and_antisemitism}

*Image: Richard Wagner's Prose Works.jpg.* Wagner's [prose works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_prose_works_by_Richard_Wagner), the complete William Ashton Ellis English translations volumes I through VIII

Wagner's hostile writings on Jews, including [*Jewishness in Music*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Judenthum_in_der_Musik), correspond to some existing trends of thought in Germany during the 19th century.[^o303] Despite his very public views on this topic, throughout his life Wagner had Jewish friends, colleagues and supporters.[^n211][^n212] There have been frequent suggestions that [antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitic) stereotypes are represented in Wagner's operas. The characters of [Alberich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberich) and Mime in the *Ring*, Sixtus Beckmesser in *Die Meistersinger*, and Klingsor in *Parsifal* are sometimes claimed as Jewish representations, though they are not identified as such in the librettos of these operas.[^o306] The topic is further complicated by claims, which may have been credited by Wagner, that he himself was of Jewish ancestry, via his supposed father Geyer; however, there is no evidence that Geyer had Jewish ancestors.[^n213][^n214]

Late in his life, in the early 1880s, Wagner developed a friendship with [Arthur de Gobineau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_de_Gobineau), whose [racialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racialist) philosophy was used by the [Nazi Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party).[^n215] Their friendship was tempestuous; while Gobineau considered the world's outlook one of hopeless decline through irreparable blood tainting, Wagner maintained belief that humanity could be united through [self-knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Know_thyself), spiritual awakening and cultural renewal, transcending biological racial distinctions—as outlined in his essay *Know Thyself* (1881).[^n216]

### Other interpretations {#Other_interpretations}

Wagner's ideas are amenable to socialist interpretations; many of his ideas on art were being formulated at the time of his revolutionary inclinations in the 1840s. Thus, for example, [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw) wrote in *[The Perfect Wagnerite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Perfect_Wagnerite)* (1883):

> \[Wagner's\] picture of Niblunghome under the reign of Alberic is a poetic vision of unregulated industrial capitalism as it was made known in Germany in the middle of the 19th century by [Engels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engels)'s book *[The Condition of the Working Class in England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Condition_of_the_Working_Class_in_England)*.[^n217]

Left-wing interpretations of Wagner also inform the writings of [Theodor Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Adorno) among other Wagner critics. [Walter Benjamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin) gave Wagner as an example of "bourgeois false consciousness", alienating art from its social context.[^n218] [György Lukács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/György_Lukács) contended that the ideas of the early Wagner represented the ideology of the "true socialists" (*wahre Sozialisten*), a movement referenced in [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)'s *[Communist Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Manifesto)* as belonging to the left wing of German [bourgeois radicalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeois_radicalism) and associated with [Feuerbachianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feuerbachianism) and [Karl Theodor Ferdinand Grün](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Theodor_Ferdinand_Grün),[^o314] while [Anatoly Lunacharsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Lunacharsky) said about the later Wagner: "The circle is complete. The revolutionary has become a reactionary. The rebellious petty bourgeois now kisses the slipper of the Pope, the keeper of order."[^o315]

The writer [Robert Donington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Donington) has produced a detailed, if controversial, [Jungian interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungian_interpretation) of the *Ring* cycle, described as "an approach to Wagner by way of his symbols", which, for example, sees the character of the goddess Fricka as part of her husband Wotan's "inner femininity".[^n219] Millington notes that [Jean-Jacques Nattiez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Nattiez) has also applied [psychoanalytical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalytical) techniques in an evaluation of Wagner's life and works.[^n220][^n221]
