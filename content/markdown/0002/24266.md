# Profinite group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Profinite group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **profinite group** is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) that is in a certain sense assembled from a system of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707).

:::

The idea of using a profinite group is to provide a "uniform", or "synoptic", view of an entire system of finite groups. Properties of the profinite group are generally speaking uniform properties of the system. For example, the profinite group is finitely generated (as a topological group) if and only if there exists $d\in\mathbb{N}$ such that every group in the system can be [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by $d$ elements.[^o2] Many theorems about finite groups can be readily generalised to profinite groups; examples are [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150) and the [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993).[^o3]

To construct a profinite group one needs a system of finite groups and [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) between them. Without loss of generality, these homomorphisms can be assumed to be [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873), in which case the finite groups will appear as [quotient groups](wikipedia://en/11526) of the resulting profinite group; in a sense, these quotients approximate the profinite group.

Important examples of profinite groups are the [additive groups](wikipedia://en/2974) of [$p$-adic integers](wikipedia://en/51423) and the [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673) of infinite-degree [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634).

Every profinite group is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [totally disconnected](wikipedia://en/303356). A non-compact generalization of the concept is that of [locally profinite groups](wikipedia://en/32286640). Even more general are the [totally disconnected groups](wikipedia://en/4947490).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Profinite groups can be defined in either of two equivalent ways.

### First definition (constructive) {#First_definition_(constructive)}

A profinite group is a topological group that is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the [inverse limit](wikipedia://en/15109) of an [inverse system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_system) of [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) finite groups.[^o7] In this context, an inverse system consists of a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165) $(I, \leq),$ an [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of finite groups $\{G_i: i \in I\},$ each having the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology), and a family of [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) $\{f^j_i : G_j \to G_i \mid i, j \in I, i \leq j\}$ such that $f_i^i$ is the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) on $G_i$ and the collection satisfies the composition property $f^j_i \circ f^k_j = f^k_i$ whenever $i\leq j\leq k.$ The inverse limit is the set:

$$\varprojlim G_i = \left\{(g_i)_{i \in I} \in {\textstyle\prod\limits_{i \in I}} G_i : f^j_i (g_j) = g_i \text{ for all } i\leq j\right\}$$

equipped with the [relative](wikipedia://en/910926) [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910).

One can also define the inverse limit in terms of a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245). In [categorical](wikipedia://en/5869) terms, this is a special case of a [cofiltered limit](wikipedia://en/4322449) construction.

### Second definition {#Second_definition}

A profinite group is a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), [compact](wikipedia://en/750326) and [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected) topological group:[^o9] that is, a topological group that is also a [Stone space](wikipedia://en/380219).

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Profinite completion"}

### Profinite completion {#Profinite_completion}

Given an arbitrary group $G$, there is a related profinite group $\widehat{G},$ the profinite completion of $G$.[^o9] It is defined as the inverse limit of the groups $G/N$, where $N$ runs through the [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) in $G$ of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581) (these normal subgroups are [partially ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) by inclusion, which translates into an inverse system of natural homomorphisms between the quotients).

There is a natural homomorphism $\eta : G \to \widehat{G}$, and the image of $G$ under this homomorphism is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $\widehat{G}$. The homomorphism $\eta$ is injective if and only if the group $G$ is [residually finite](wikipedia://en/5780875) (i.e., $\bigcap N = 1$, where the intersection runs through all normal subgroups $N$ of finite index).

The homomorphism $\eta$ is characterized by the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245): given any profinite group $H$ and any continuous group homomorphism $f : G \rightarrow H$ where $G$ is given the smallest topology compatible with group operations in which its normal subgroups of finite index are open, there exists a unique [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) group homomorphism $g : \widehat{G} \rightarrow H$ with $f = g \eta$.

:::

### Equivalence {#Equivalence}

Any group constructed by the first definition satisfies the axioms in the second definition.

Conversely, any group $G$ satisfying the axioms in the second definition can be constructed as an inverse limit according to the first definition using the inverse limit $\varprojlim G/N$ where $N$ ranges through the open [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) of $G$ ordered by (reverse) inclusion. If $G$ is topologically finitely generated then it is in addition equal to its own profinite completion.[^o13]

### Surjective systems {#Surjective_systems}

In practice, the inverse system of finite groups is almost always surjective inverse system, meaning that all its maps are surjective. Without loss of generality, it suffices to consider only surjective systems since given any inverse system, it is possible to first construct its profinite group $G,$ and then reconstruct it as its own profinite completion.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- Finite groups are profinite, if given the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology).
- The group of [$p$-adic integers](wikipedia://en/51423) $\mathbb{Z}_p$ under addition is profinite (in fact procyclic). It is the inverse limit of the finite groups $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ where $n$ ranges over all [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) and the natural maps $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p^m\mathbb{Z}$ for $n \ge m.$ The topology on this profinite group is the same as the topology arising from the $p$-adic valuation on $\mathbb{Z}_p.$
- The group of [profinite integers](wikipedia://en/46531203) $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is the profinite completion of $\mathbb{Z}.$ In detail, it is the inverse limit of the finite groups $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ where $n = 1,2,3,\dots$ with the modulo maps $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ for $m\,|\,n.$ This group is the product of all the groups $\mathbb{Z}_p,$ and it is the [absolute Galois group](wikipedia://en/2996781) of any [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).
- The [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) of [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634) of infinite degree gives rise naturally to Galois groups that are profinite. Specifically, if $L / K$ is a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064), consider the group $G = \operatorname{Gal}(L / K)$ consisting of all [field automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_automorphism) of $L$ that keep all elements of $K$ fixed. This group is the inverse limit of the finite groups $\operatorname{Gal}(F / K),$ where $F$ ranges over all intermediate fields such that $F / K$ is a finite Galois extension. For the limit process, the restriction homomorphisms $\operatorname{Gal}(F_1 / K) \to \operatorname{Gal}(F_2 / K)$ are used, where $F_2 \subseteq F_1.$ The topology obtained on $\operatorname{Gal}(L / K)$ is known as the *[Krull topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_topology)* after [Wolfgang Krull](wikipedia://en/964328). (Waterhouse 1974) showed that every profinite group is isomorphic to one arising from the Galois theory of some field $K,$ but one cannot (yet) control which field $K$ will be in this case. In fact, for many fields $K$ one does not know in general precisely which [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) occur as Galois groups over $K.$ This is the [inverse Galois problem](wikipedia://en/739166) for a field $K.$ (For some fields $K$ the inverse Galois problem is settled, such as the field of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in one variable over the complex numbers.) Not every profinite group occurs as an [absolute Galois group](wikipedia://en/2996781) of a field.[^o18]
- The [étale fundamental groups considered in algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/3022906) are also profinite groups, roughly speaking because the algebra can only 'see' finite coverings of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808). The [fundamental groups](wikipedia://en/11004) of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), however, are in general not profinite: for any prescribed group, there is a 2-dimensional [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022) whose fundamental group equals it.
- The automorphism group of a [locally finite rooted tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_finite_rooted_tree) is profinite.

:::

## Properties and facts {#Properties_and_facts}

- Every [product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of (arbitrarily many) profinite groups is profinite; the topology arising from the profiniteness agrees with the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910). The inverse limit of an inverse system of profinite groups with continuous transition maps is profinite and the inverse limit functor is [exact](wikipedia://en/453945) on the category of profinite groups. Further, being profinite is an extension property.
- Every [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) subgroup of a profinite group is itself profinite; the topology arising from the profiniteness agrees with the [subspace topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29). If $N$ is a closed normal subgroup of a profinite group $G,$ then the [factor group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_group) $G / N$ is profinite; the topology arising from the profiniteness agrees with the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology).
- Since every profinite group $G$ is compact Hausdorff, there exists a [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) on $G,$ which allows us to measure the "size" of subsets of $G,$ compute certain [probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilities), and [integrate](wikipedia://en/15532) functions on $G.$
- A subgroup of a profinite group is open if and only if it is closed and has finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581).
- According to a theorem of [Nikolay Nikolov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Nikolov_%28mathematician%29) and [Dan Segal](wikipedia://en/9276503), in any topologically finitely generated profinite group (that is, a profinite group that has a [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [finitely generated subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_generated_subgroup)) the subgroups of finite index are open. This generalizes an earlier analogous result of [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) for topologically finitely generated [pro-$p$ groups](wikipedia://en/1842075). The proof uses the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445).
- As an easy corollary of the Nikolov–Segal result above, any surjective discrete group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ between profinite groups $G$ and $H$ is continuous as long as $G$ is topologically finitely generated. Indeed, any open subgroup of $H$ is of finite index, so its preimage in $G$ is also of finite index, and hence it must be open.
- Suppose $G$ and $H$ are topologically finitely generated profinite groups that are isomorphic as discrete groups by an isomorphism $\iota.$ Then $\iota$ is bijective and continuous by the above result. Furthermore, $\iota^{-1}$ is also continuous, so $\iota$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660). Therefore the topology on a topologically finitely generated profinite group is uniquely determined by its algebraic structure.

## Ind-finite groups {#Ind-finite_groups}

There is a notion of , which is the conceptual [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) to profinite groups; i.e. a group $G$ is ind-finite if it is the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of an [inductive system](wikipedia://en/211566#Formal_definition) of finite groups. (In particular, it is an [ind-group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ind-group).) The usual terminology is different: a group $G$ is called [locally finite](wikipedia://en/4939270) if every finitely generated [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) is finite. This is equivalent, in fact, to being 'ind-finite'.

By applying [Pontryagin duality](wikipedia://en/366136), one can see that [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) profinite groups are in duality with locally finite discrete abelian groups. The latter are just the abelian [torsion groups](wikipedia://en/3568695).

::: {#o22 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Projective profinite group"}

## Projective profinite groups {#Projective_profinite_groups}

A profinite group is projective profinite groups if it has the [lifting property](wikipedia://en/53659730) for every extension. This is equivalent to saying that $G$ is projective if for every surjective morphism from a profinite $H \to G$ there is a [section](wikipedia://en/10630303) $G \to H.$[^o23][^o24]

Projectivity for a profinite group $G$ is equivalent to either of the two properties:[^o23]

- the [cohomological dimension](wikipedia://en/9880197) $\operatorname{cd}(G) \leq 1;$
- for every prime $p$ the Sylow $p$-subgroups of $G$ are free pro-$p$-groups.

Every projective profinite group can be realized as an [absolute Galois group](wikipedia://en/2996781) of a [pseudo algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/3696313). This result is due to [Alexander Lubotzky](wikipedia://en/22754130) and [Lou van den Dries](wikipedia://en/55143215).[^o25]

:::

::: {#o27 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Procyclic group"}

## Procyclic group {#Procyclic_group}

A profinite group $G$ is procyclic group if it is topologically generated by a single element $\sigma;$ that is, if $G = \overline{\langle \sigma \rangle},$ the closure of the subgroup $\langle \sigma \rangle = \left\{\sigma^n: n \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}.$[^o28]

A topological group $G$ is procyclic if and only if $G \cong {\textstyle\prod\limits_{p\in S}} G_p$ where $p$ ranges over some set of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) $S$ and $G_p$ is isomorphic to either $\mathbb{Z}_p$ or $\mathbb{Z}/p^n \mathbb{Z}, n \in \mathbb{N}.$[^o29]

:::
