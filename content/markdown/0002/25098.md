# Phase velocity

*Image: Wave group.gif.* [Frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650) in groups of [gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_wave) on the surface of deep water. The \#dd0000 red square moves with the phase velocity, and the \#77ac30 green circles propagate with the [group velocity](wikipedia://en/12778). In this deep-water case, *the phase velocity is twice the group velocity*. The red square overtakes two green circles when moving from the left to the right of the figure. New waves seem to emerge at the back of a wave group, grow in amplitude until they are at the center of the group, and vanish at the wave group front. For surface gravity waves, the water particle velocities are much smaller than the phase velocity, in most cases.

*Image: Wave packet propagation (phase faster than group, nondispersive).gif.* Propagation of a [wave packet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_packet) demonstrating a phase velocity greater than the group velocity.

*Image: Wave opposite-group-phase-velocity.gif.* This shows a wave with the group velocity and phase velocity going in different directions.[^o1] The group velocity is positive (i.e., the [envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envelope_%28waves%29) of the wave moves rightward), while the phase velocity is negative (i.e., the peaks and troughs move leftward).

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Phase velocity"}

The **phase velocity** of a [wave](wikipedia://en/33516) is the speed of any [wavefront](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefront), a surface of constant [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29). This is the [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) at which the phase of any constant-[frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) component of the wave travels. For such a [spectral component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_component), any given phase of the wave (for example, the [crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_%28physics%29)) will appear to travel at the phase velocity. The phase velocity of light waves is not a physically meaningful quantity and is not related to information transfer.[^o3]

:::

## Sinusoidal or plane waves {#Sinusoidal_or_plane_waves}

For a simple sinusoidal wave the phase velocity is given in terms of the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) $\lambda$ (lambda) and [time period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_period) $T$ as

$$v_\mathrm{p} = \frac{\lambda}{T}.$$

Equivalently, in terms of the wave's [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) $\omega$, which specifies angular change per unit of time, and [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber) (or angular wave number) $k$, which represent the angular change per unit of space,[^o3]

$$v_\mathrm{p} = \frac{\omega}{k}.$$

## Beats {#Beats}

The previous definition of phase velocity has been demonstrated for an isolated wave. However, such a definition can be extended to a beat of waves, or to a signal composed of multiple waves. For this it is necessary to mathematically write the beat or signal as a low frequency envelope multiplying a carrier. Thus the phase velocity of the carrier determines the phase velocity of the wave set.[^o6]

## Dispersion {#Dispersion}

In the context of electromagnetics and optics, the frequency is some function $\omega(k)$ of the wave number, so in general, the phase velocity and the group velocity depend on specific medium and frequency. The ratio between the speed of light *c* and the phase velocity *v*<sub>*p*</sub> is known as the [refractive index](wikipedia://en/25880), $n = c / v_{p} = ck / \omega$.

In this way, we can obtain another form for group velocity for electromagnetics. Writing $n = n(\omega)$, a quick way to derive this form is to observe

$$k = \frac{1}{c} \omega n(\omega) \implies dk = \frac{1}{c}\left(n(\omega) + \omega \frac{\partial}{\partial \omega}n(\omega)\right)d\omega.$$

We can then rearrange the above to obtain

$$v_g = \frac{\partial w}{\partial k} = \frac{c}{n+\omega\frac{\partial n}{\partial \omega}}.$$

From this formula, we see that the group velocity is equal to the phase velocity only when the refractive index is independent of frequency $\partial n / \partial\omega = 0$. When this occurs, the medium is called non-dispersive, as opposed to [dispersive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersion_%28optics%29), where various properties of the medium depend on the frequency $\omega$. The relation $\omega(k)$ is known as the [dispersion relation](wikipedia://en/853778) of the medium.
