# Skewness

*Image: SkewedDistribution.png.* Example distribution with positive skewness. The data presented is from experiments on wheat grass growth.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Skewness"}

**Skewness** in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) is a measure of the asymmetry of the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) about its mean. Similarly to [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848), it provides insights into [shape-related characteristics](wikipedia://en/368684) of a distribution. The skewness value can be positive, zero, negative, or undefined.

:::

For a [unimodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodal) distribution (a distribution with a single peak), negative skew commonly indicates that the tail is on the left side of the distribution, and positive skew indicates that the tail is on the right. In cases where one tail is long but the other tail is thick, skewness does not obey a simple rule. For example, a zero value in skewness means that the tails on both sides of the mean balance out overall; this is the case for a symmetric distribution but can also be true for an asymmetric distribution where one tail is long and thin, and the other is short but thick. Thus, the symmetry of a distribution cannot be inferred using only its skewness; the [distribution shape](wikipedia://en/9330700) must be taken into account.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

::: {#o3 .definition cue=dfni name="tails"}

Consider the two distributions in the figure. Within each graph, the values on the right side of the distribution taper differently from the values on the left side. These tapering sides are called **tails** and they provide a visual means to determine which of the two kinds of skewness a distribution has:

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=dfni name="negative skew"}

1. **negative skew**: The left tail is longer; the mass of the distribution is concentrated on the right of the figure. The distribution is said to be left-skewed, left-tailed or skewed to the left, despite the fact that the curve itself appears to be skewed or leaning to the right; "left" refers instead to the left tail being drawn out and, often, the mean being skewed to the left of a typical center of the data. A left-skewed distribution usually appears as a right-leaning curve.[^o5]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=dfni name="positive skew"}

1. **positive skew**: The right tail is longer; the mass of the distribution is concentrated on the left of the figure. The distribution is said to be right-skewed, right-tailed or skewed to the right, despite the fact that the curve itself appears to be skewed or leaning to the left; "right" refers instead to the right tail being drawn out and, often, the mean being skewed to the right of a typical center of the data. A right-skewed distribution usually appears as a left-leaning curve.[^o5]

:::

*Image: Negative and positive skew diagrams (English).svg.*

Skewness in a data series may sometimes be observed not only graphically but by simple inspection of the values. For instance, consider the numeric sequence (49, 50, 51), whose values are evenly distributed around a central value of 50. We can transform this sequence into a negatively skewed distribution by adding a value far below the mean, which is probably a negative [outlier](wikipedia://en/160951), e.g. (40, 49, 50, 51). Therefore, the mean of the sequence becomes 47.5, and the median is 49.5. Based on the formula of [nonparametric skew](wikipedia://en/34502950), defined as $(\mu - \nu)/\sigma,$ the skew is negative. Similarly, we can make the sequence positively skewed by adding a value far above the mean, which is probably a positive outlier, e.g. (49, 50, 51, 60), where the mean is 52.5, and the median is 50.5.

As mentioned earlier, a unimodal distribution with zero value of skewness does not imply that this distribution is symmetric necessarily. However, a symmetric unimodal or multimodal distribution always has zero skewness.

*Image: Asymmetric Distribution with Zero Skewness.jpg.* Example of an asymmetric distribution with zero skewness. This figure serves as a counterexample that zero skewness does not imply symmetric distribution necessarily. (Skewness was calculated by Pearson's moment coefficient of skewness.)

## Relationship of mean and median {#Relationship_of_mean_and_median}

The skewness is not directly related to the relationship between the mean and median: a distribution with negative skew can have its mean greater than or less than the median, and likewise for positive skew.[^o8]

*Image: Relationship between mean and median under different skewness.png.* A general relationship of mean and median under differently skewed unimodal distribution.

In the older notion of [nonparametric skew](wikipedia://en/34502950), defined as $(\mu - \nu)/\sigma,$ where $\mu$ is the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean), $\nu$ is the [median](wikipedia://en/18837), and $\sigma$ is the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), the skewness is defined in terms of this relationship: positive/right nonparametric skew means the mean is greater than (to the right of) the median, while negative/left nonparametric skew means the mean is less than (to the left of) the median. However, the modern definition of skewness and the traditional nonparametric definition do not always have the same sign: while they agree for some families of distributions, they differ in some of the cases, and conflating them is misleading.

If the distribution is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/39284103), then the mean is equal to the median, and the distribution has zero skewness.[^o9] If the distribution is both symmetric and [unimodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodal_distribution), then the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) = [median](wikipedia://en/18837) = [mode](wikipedia://en/1432127). This is the case of a coin toss or the series 1,2,3,4,... Note, however, that the converse is not true in general, i.e. zero skewness (defined below) does not imply that the mean is equal to the median.

A 2005 journal article points out:[^o8]

> Many textbooks teach a rule of thumb stating that the mean is right of the median under right skew, and left of the median under left skew. This rule fails with surprising frequency. It can fail in [multimodal distributions](wikipedia://en/1068378), or in distributions where one tail is [long](wikipedia://en/1385393) but the other is [heavy](wikipedia://en/8092200). Most commonly, though, the rule fails in discrete distributions where the areas to the left and right of the median are not equal. Such distributions not only contradict the textbook relationship between mean, median, and skew, they also contradict the textbook interpretation of the median.

*Image: Positive skewness with mean less than median.png.* Distribution of adult residents across US households

For example, in the distribution of adult residents across US households, the skew is to the right. However, since the majority of cases is less than or equal to the mode, which is also the median, the mean sits in the heavier left tail. As a result, the rule of thumb that the mean is right of the median under right skew failed.[^o8]

## Definition {#Definition}

### Fisher's moment coefficient of skewness {#Fisher's_moment_coefficient_of_skewness}

The skewness $\gamma_1$ of a random variable $X$ is the third [standardized moment](wikipedia://en/202713) $\tilde{\mu}_3$, defined as:[^o14][^o13]

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma_1 &:= \tilde{\mu}_3 \equiv \frac{\mu_3}{\sigma^3}\\ &= \operatorname{E}\left[\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^3 \right] = \frac{\operatorname{E}\left[(X-\mu)^3\right]}{\left( \operatorname{E}\left[ (X-\mu)^2 \right] \right)^{3/2}}\\ &= \frac{\kappa_3}{\kappa_2^{3/2}} \end{aligned}$$

where $\mu$ is the mean, $\sigma$ is the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), E is the [expectation operator](wikipedia://en/9653), $\mu_{3}$ is the third [central moment](wikipedia://en/19983), and $\kappa_{t}$ are the $t$-th [cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684). It is sometimes referred to as **Pearson's moment coefficient of skewness**,[^o13] or simply the **moment coefficient of skewness**,[^o14] but should not be confused with Pearson's other skewness statistics (see below). The last equality expresses skewness in terms of the ratio of the third cumulant $\kappa_{3}$ to the 1.5th power of the second cumulant $\kappa_{2}$. This is analogous to the definition of [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848) as the fourth cumulant normalized by the square of the second cumulant. The skewness is also sometimes denoted $\operatorname{Skew}[X]$.

If $\sigma$ is finite and $\mu$ is finite too, then skewness can be expressed in terms of the non-central moment $E[X^{3}]$ by expanding the previous formula:

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{\mu}_3 &= \operatorname{E}\left[\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^3 \right] \\ &= \frac{\operatorname{E}[X^3] - 3\mu\operatorname E[X^2] + 3\mu^2\operatorname E[X] - \mu^3}{\sigma^3}\\ &= \frac{\operatorname{E}[X^3] - 3\mu(\operatorname E[X^2] -\mu\operatorname E[X]) - \mu^3}{\sigma^3}\\ &= \frac{\operatorname{E}[X^3] - 3\mu\sigma^2 - \mu^3}{\sigma^3}. \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o16 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Skewness can be infinite, as when

$$\Pr(X=x) = \begin{cases} x^{-2} & \text{ for } x >1, \\[2pt] 0 & \text{ for } x < 1, \end{cases}$$

where the third cumulants are infinite, or as when

$$\Pr[X<x] = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} (1-x)^{-3} & \text{ for } x < 0, \\[2pt] \frac{1}{2} (1+x)^{-3} & \text{ for } x > 0. \end{cases}$$

where the third cumulant is undefined.

Examples of distributions with finite skewness include the following.

- A [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) and any other symmetric distribution with finite third moment has a skewness of 0
- A [half-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/15906455) has a skewness just below 1
- An [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) has a skewness of 2
- A [lognormal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lognormal_distribution) can have a skewness of any positive value, depending on its parameters

:::

### Sample skewness {#Sample_skewness}

For a sample of *n* values, two natural estimators of the population skewness are[^o19]

$$b_1 = \frac{m_3}{s^3} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^3}{\left[\tfrac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2 \right]^{3/2}}$$

and

$$g_1 = \frac{m_3}{m_2^{3/2}} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar{x})^3}{\left[\tfrac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2 \right]^{3/2}},$$

where $\bar{x}$ is the [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean), $s$ is the [sample standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590#Corrected_sample_standard_deviation), $m_{2}$ is the (biased) sample second central [moment](wikipedia://en/368684), and $m_{3}$ is the (biased) sample third central moment.[^o19] $g_1$ is a [method of moments](wikipedia://en/2175504) estimator.

Another common definition of the *sample skewness* is[^o19][^o20]

$$\begin{aligned} G_1 & = \frac{k_3}{k_2^{3/2}} = \frac{n^2}{(n-1)(n-2)}\; b_1 = \frac{\sqrt{n(n-1)}}{n-2}\; g_1, \\ \end{aligned}$$

where $k_3$ is the unique symmetric unbiased estimator of the third [cumulant](wikipedia://en/359684) and $k_2 = s^2$ is the symmetric unbiased estimator of the second cumulant (i.e. the [sample variance](wikipedia://en/32344#Population_variance_and_sample_variance)). This adjusted Fisher–Pearson standardized moment coefficient $G_1$ is the version found in [Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel) and several statistical packages including [Minitab](wikipedia://en/1773375), [SAS](wikipedia://en/990677) and [SPSS](wikipedia://en/179088).[^o20]

Under the assumption that the underlying random variable $X$ is normally distributed, it can be shown that all three ratios $b_1$, $g_1$ and $G_1$ are unbiased and [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569) estimators of the population skewness $\gamma_1=0$, with $\sqrt{n} b_1 \mathrel{\xrightarrow{d}} N(0, 6)$, i.e., their distributions converge to a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 6 ([Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824), 1930).[^o19] The variance of the sample skewness is thus approximately $6/n$ for sufficiently large samples. More precisely, in a random sample of size *n* from a normal distribution,[^o21][^o22]

$$\operatorname{var}(G_1)= \frac{6n ( n - 1 )}{ ( n - 2 )( n + 1 )( n + 3 ) } .$$

In normal samples, $b_1$ has the smaller variance of the three estimators, with[^o19]

$$\operatorname{var}(b_1) < \operatorname{var} (g_1) < \operatorname{var}(G_1).$$

For non-normal distributions, $b_1$, $g_1$ and $G_1$ are generally [biased estimators](wikipedia://en/8450479) of the population skewness $\gamma_1$; their expected values can even have the opposite sign from the true skewness. For instance, a mixed distribution consisting of very thin Gaussians centred at −99, 0.5, and 2 with weights 0.01, 0.66, and 0.33 has a skewness $\gamma_1$ of about −9.77, but in a sample of 3 $G_1$ has an expected value of about 0.32, since usually all three samples are in the positive-valued part of the distribution, which is skewed the other way.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

Skewness is a descriptive statistic that can be used in conjunction with the [histogram](wikipedia://en/13266) and the normal [quantile plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantile_plot) to characterize the data or distribution.

Skewness indicates the direction and relative magnitude of a distribution's deviation from the normal distribution.

With pronounced skewness, standard statistical inference procedures such as a [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) for a mean will be not only incorrect, in the sense that the true coverage level will differ from the nominal (e.g., 95%) level, but they will also result in unequal error probabilities on each side.

Skewness can be used to obtain approximate probabilities and quantiles of distributions (such as [value at risk](wikipedia://en/148633) in finance) via the [Cornish–Fisher expansion](wikipedia://en/37730111).

Many models assume normal distribution; i.e., data are symmetric about the mean. The normal distribution has a skewness of zero. But in reality, data points may not be perfectly symmetric. So, an understanding of the skewness of the dataset indicates whether deviations from the mean are going to be positive or negative.

[D'Agostino's K-squared test](wikipedia://en/11686201) is a [goodness-of-fit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodness-of-fit) [normality test](wikipedia://en/8617378) based on sample skewness and sample kurtosis.

:::

### Quantile-based measures {#Quantile-based_measures}

Bowley's measure of skewness (from 1901),[^o34][^o35] also called **Yule's coefficient** (from 1912)[^o36][^o37] is defined as:

$$\frac{\frac{Q(3/4) + Q(1/4)}{2} - Q(1/2)}{\frac{Q(3/4) - Q(1/4)}{2}} = \frac{Q(3/4) + Q(1/4) - 2 Q(1/2)}{Q(3/4) - Q(1/4)},$$

where *Q* is the [quantile function](wikipedia://en/10167616) (i.e., the inverse of the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793)). The numerator is difference between the average of the upper and lower quartiles (a measure of location) and the median (another measure of location), while the denominator is the [semi-interquartile range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-interquartile_range) $\frac{Q(3/4)-Q(1/4)}{2}$, which for symmetric distributions is equal to the [MAD](wikipedia://en/344242) measure of [dispersion](wikipedia://en/23773247).

Other names for this measure are Galton's measure of skewness,[^o38] the Yule–Kendall index[^o39] and the quartile skewness,[^o40]

Similarly, Kelly's measure of skewness is defined as[^o41]

$$\frac{Q(9/10) + Q(1/10) - 2 Q(1/2)}{Q(9/10) - Q(1/10)}.$$

A more general formulation of a skewness function was described by Groeneveld, R. A. and Meeden, G. (1984):[^o42][^o43][^o44]

$$\gamma( u ) = \frac{ Q( u ) +Q( 1 - u )-2Q( 1 / 2 ) }{Q( u ) -Q( 1 - u ) }$$

The function $\gamma(u)$ satisfies $-1 \le \gamma(u) \le 1$ and is well defined without requiring the existence of any moments of the distribution.[^o42] Bowley's measure of skewness is $\gamma(u)$ evaluated at $u = 3/4$ while Kelly's measure of skewness is $\gamma(u)$ evaluated at $u = 9/10$. This definition leads to a corresponding overall measure of skewness[^o45] defined as the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of this over the range $1/2 \le u < 1$. Another measure can be obtained by integrating the numerator and denominator of this expression.[^o42]

Quantile-based skewness measures are at first glance easy to interpret, but they often show significantly larger sample variations than moment-based methods. This means that often samples from a symmetric distribution (like the uniform distribution) have a large quantile-based skewness, just by chance.

:::

### Groeneveld and Meeden's coefficient {#Groeneveld_and_Meeden's_coefficient}

Groeneveld and Meeden have suggested, as an alternative measure of skewness,[^o42]

$$\operatorname{skew}(X) = \frac{\mu - \nu}{ \operatorname E( | X - \nu | ) },$$

where $\mu$ is the mean, $\nu$ is the median, $\lvert ...\rvert$ is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991), and $E()$ is the expectation operator. This is closely related in form to [Pearson's second skewness coefficient](wikipedia://en/28212#Pearson.27s_second_skewness_coefficient_.28median_skewness.29).

Use of [L-moments](wikipedia://en/24350828) in place of moments provides a measure of skewness known as the L-skewness.[^o48]

### Distance skewness {#Distance_skewness}

A value of skewness equal to zero does not imply that the probability distribution is symmetric. Thus there is a need for another measure of asymmetry that has this property: such a measure was introduced in 2000.[^o50] It is called **distance skewness** and denoted by $\operatorname{dSkew}$. If *X* is a random variable taking values in the $d$-dimensional Euclidean space, $X$ has finite expectation, $X$ is an independent identically distributed copy of $X$, and $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the norm in the Euclidean space, then a simple *measure of asymmetry* with respect to location parameter $\theta$ is

$$\operatorname{dSkew}(X) := 1 - \frac{\operatorname{E}\|X-X'\|}{\operatorname{E}\|X+X'-2 \theta\|} \text{ if } \Pr(X=\theta)\ne 1$$

and $\operatorname{dSkew}(X) := 0$ for $X = \theta$ (with probability 1). Distance skewness is always between 0 and 1, equals 0 if and only if *X* is diagonally symmetric with respect to $\theta$ ($X$ and $2\theta - X$ have the same probability distribution) and equals 1 if and only if *X* is a constant *c* ($c \neq \theta$) with probability one.[^o51] Thus there is a simple consistent [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test) of diagonal symmetry based on the **sample distance skewness**:

$$\operatorname{dSkew}_n(X) := 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j} \|x_i-x_j\| }{\sum_{i,j} \|x_i+x_j-2\theta \|}.$$

### Medcouple {#Medcouple}

The [medcouple](wikipedia://en/46325747) is a scale-invariant robust measure of skewness, with a [breakdown point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakdown_point) of 25%.[^o53] It is the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of the values of the kernel function

$$h(x_i, x_j) = \frac{ (x_i - x_m) - (x_m - x_j)}{x_i - x_j}$$

taken over all couples $(x_i, x_j)$ such that $x_i \geq x_m \geq x_j$, where $x_m$ is the median of the [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) $\{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}$. It can be seen as the median of all possible quantile skewness measures.
