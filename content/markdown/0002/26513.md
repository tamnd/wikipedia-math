# Reverse Polish notation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Reverse Polish notation"}

*Image: Typing the calculation for "8 times 6" into a pocket calculator HP-32SII which uses RPN logic.webm.* Video: Keys pressed for calculating eight times six on a [HP-32SII](wikipedia://en/898457) (employing RPN) from 1991

**Reverse Polish notation** (**RPN**), also known as **reverse Łukasiewicz notation**, **Polish postfix notation** or simply **postfix notation**, is a mathematical notation in which [operators](wikipedia://en/4140245) follow their [operands](wikipedia://en/22656) (e.g. $3\ 4\ +$), in contrast to the more common [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014) (in which operators are placed *between* operands, e.g. $3 + 4$), as well as [prefix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_notation) (in which operators precede their operands, e.g. $+\ 3\ 4$). The notation does not need any parentheses as long as each operator has a fixed [number of operands](wikipedia://en/42301).

:::

Here are some examples to illustrate the differences between the aforementioned types of notation:

::: {#o2 .table cue=table}

| Infix notation | Postfix notation | Prefix notation |
|---|---|---|
| $3 + 4$ | $3\ 4\ +$ | $+\ 3\ 4$ |
| $1 - 2 \times 3$ | $1\ 2\ 3 \times\ -$ | $-\ 1 \times 2\ 3$ |
| $(1 - 2) \times 3$ | $1\ 2 - 3\ \times$ | $\times - 1\ 2\ 3$ |

:::

The term *postfix notation* describes the general scheme in mathematics and computer sciences, whereas the term *reverse Polish notation* typically refers specifically to the method used to enter calculations into hardware or software calculators, which often have additional [side effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side_effect_%28computer_science%29) and implications depending on the actual implementation involving a [stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_%28data_structure%29). The description "Polish" refers to the [nationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationality) of [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician) [Jan Łukasiewicz](wikipedia://en/305064),[^o34][^o35] who invented [Polish notation](wikipedia://en/25056) in 1924.[^o33][^o55][^o37][^o36]

The first computer to use postfix notation, though it long remained essentially unknown outside of Germany, was [Konrad Zuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse)'s [Z3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z3_%28computer%29) in 1941[^o78][^o93] as well as his [Z4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z4_%28computer%29) in 1945. The reverse Polish scheme was again proposed in 1954 by [Arthur Burks](wikipedia://en/6479641), Don Warren, and Jesse Wright[^o56] and was independently reinvented by [Friedrich L. Bauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_L._Bauer) and [Edsger W. Dijkstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edsger_W._Dijkstra) in the early 1960s to reduce [computer memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory) access and use the [stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_%28data_structure%29) to evaluate [expressions](wikipedia://en/609125). The [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) and notation for this scheme were extended by the philosopher and computer scientist [Charles L. Hamblin](wikipedia://en/19501790) in the mid-1950s.[^o28][^o29][^o30][^o31][^o58][^o57]

During the 1970s and 1980s, [Hewlett-Packard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard) used RPN in all of their desktop and hand-held calculators, and has continued to use it in some models into the 2020s.[^o42][^o38] In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), reverse Polish notation is used in [stack-oriented programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack-oriented_programming_languages) such as [Forth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forth_%28programming_language%29), [dc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dc_%28programming_language%29), [Factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_%28programming_language%29), [STOIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STOIC), [PostScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PostScript), [RPL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RPL_%28programming_language%29), and [Joy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joy_%28programming_language%29).

## Explanation {#Explanation}

In reverse Polish notation, the [operators](wikipedia://en/4140245) follow their [operands](wikipedia://en/22656). For example, to add 3 and 4 together, the expression is 3 4 + rather than 3 + 4. The conventional notation expression 3 − 4 + 5 becomes 3 4 − 5 + in reverse Polish notation: 4 is first subtracted from 3, then 5 is added to it.

The computation may be illustrated as such:

$$\begin{aligned} \ & 3\ 4\ -\ 5\ + \\ =\ & (-1)\ 5\ + \\ =\ & 4 \end{aligned}$$

The concept of a *[stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_%28data_structure%29)*, a last-in/first-out construct, is integral to the left-to-right evaluation of RPN. In the example 3 4 −, first the 3 is put onto the stack, then the 4; the 4 is now on top and the 3 below it. The subtraction operator removes the top two items from the stack, performs 3 − 4, and puts the result of −1 onto the stack.

Common language in this context refers to items being pushed onto the stack when added and popped or removed from the stack when taken off.

The advantage of reverse Polish notation is that it removes the need for order of operations and parentheses that are required by [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014) and can be evaluated linearly, left-to-right. For example, the infix expression (3 + 4) × (5 + 6) becomes 3 4 + 5 6 + × in reverse Polish notation.

## Practical implications {#Practical_implications}

Reverse Polish notation has been compared to how one had to work through problems with a [slide rule](wikipedia://en/28743).[^o96]

In comparison, testing of reverse Polish notation with algebraic notation, reverse Polish has been found to lead to faster calculations, for two reasons. The first reason is that reverse Polish calculators do not need expressions to be parenthesized, so fewer operations need to be entered to perform typical calculations. Additionally, users of reverse Polish calculators made fewer mistakes than for other types of calculators.[^o59][^o39] Later research clarified that the increased speed from reverse Polish notation may be attributed to the smaller number of keystrokes needed to enter this notation, rather than to a smaller cognitive load on its users.[^o60] However, anecdotal evidence suggests that reverse Polish notation is more difficult for users who previously learned algebraic notation.[^o39]

## Converting from infix notation {#Converting_from_infix_notation}

[Edsger W. Dijkstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edsger_W._Dijkstra) invented the [shunting-yard algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shunting-yard_algorithm) to convert infix expressions to postfix expressions (reverse Polish notation), so named because its operation resembles that of a [railroad shunting yard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_yard).

There are other ways of producing postfix expressions from infix expressions. Most [operator-precedence parsers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator-precedence_parser) can be modified to produce postfix expressions; in particular, once an [abstract syntax tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_syntax_tree) has been constructed, the corresponding postfix expression is given by a simple [post-order traversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-order_traversal) of that tree.

## Implementations {#Implementations}

### Hardware calculators {#Hardware_calculators}

#### Early history {#Early_history}

The first computer implementing a form of reverse Polish notation (but without the name and also without a [stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_machine)), was [Konrad Zuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse)'s [Z3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z3_%28computer%29), which he started to construct in 1938 and demonstrated publicly on 12 May 1941.[^o80][^o82][^o84][^o85] In dialog mode, it allowed operators to enter two operands followed by the desired operation. It was destroyed on 21 December 1943 in a bombing raid.[^o82] With Zuse's help a first replica was built in 1961.[^o82] The 1945 [Z4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z4_%28computer%29) also added a 2-level [stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_machine).[^o89][^o92]

Other early computers to implement architectures enabling reverse Polish notation were the [English Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Electric_Company)'s [KDF9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Electric_KDF9) machine, which was announced in 1960 and commercially available in 1963,[^o32] and the [Burroughs B5000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burroughs_B5000), announced in 1961 and also delivered in 1963:

The KDF9 designers drew ideas from Hamblin's [GEORGE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GEORGE_%28high-level_language_system%29) (General Order Generator),[^o28][^o29][^o31][^o90][^o91][^o92] a high-level programming language written for a [DEUCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Electric_DEUCE) computer installed at [The New South Wales University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_South_Wales_University_of_Technology), Kensington, Australia, in 1957.[^o28][^o29][^o31][^o32]

One of the designers of the B5000, [Robert S. Barton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_S._Barton), later wrote that he developed reverse Polish notation independently of Hamblin sometime in 1958 after reading a 1954 textbook on symbolic logic by [Irving Copi](wikipedia://en/4352005),[^o61][^o62][^o40] where he found a reference to Polish notation,[^o40] which made him read the works of Jan Łukasiewicz as well,[^o40] and before he was aware of Hamblin's work.

[Friden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friden,_Inc.) introduced reverse Polish notation to the desktop calculator market with the [EC-130](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friden_EC-130), designed by [Robert "Bob" Appleby Ragen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Ragen),[^o41] supporting a four-level stack[^o37] in June 1963.[^o63] The successor [EC-132](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friden_EC-132) added a square root function in April 1965.[^o64] Around 1966, the [Monroe Epic](wikipedia://en/7910068) calculator supported an unnamed input scheme resembling RPN as well.[^o37]

#### Hewlett-Packard {#Hewlett-Packard}

*Image: Hewlett-Packard No Equals hat.jpg.* A promotional Hewlett-Packard "No Equals" hat from the 1980s – both a boast and a reference to RPN

[Hewlett-Packard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard) engineers designed the [9100A Desktop Calculator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard_9100A) in 1968 with reverse Polish notation[^o42] with only three stack levels with working registers *X* ("keyboard"), *Y* ("accumulate") and visible storage register *Z* ("temporary"),[^o43][^o76] a reverse Polish notation variant later referred to as *three-level RPN*.[^o97] This calculator popularized reverse Polish notation among the scientific and engineering communities. The [HP-35](wikipedia://en/449629), the world's first handheld scientific [calculator](wikipedia://en/7593),[^o42] introduced the classical *four-level RPN* with its specific ruleset of the so-called *operational (memory) stack*[^o46][^o26] (later also called *automatic memory stack*[^o45][^o77][^o26]) in 1972.[^o44] In this scheme, the Enter ↑ key duplicates values into Y under certain conditions (*automatic stack lift* with *temporary stack lift disable*), and the top register *T* ("top") gets duplicated on drops (*top copy on pop* aka *top stack level repetition*) in order to ease some calculations and to save keystrokes.[^o77] HP used reverse Polish notation on every handheld calculator it sold, whether scientific, financial, or programmable, until it introduced the [HP-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewlett-Packard_HP-10) [adding machine](wikipedia://en/74133) calculator in 1977. By this time, HP was the leading manufacturer of calculators for professionals, including engineers and accountants.

Later calculators with LCDs in the early 1980s, such as the [HP-10C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-10C), [HP-11C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-11C), [HP-15C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-15C), [HP-16C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-16C), and the financial [HP-12C](wikipedia://en/952015) calculator also used reverse Polish notation. In 1988, Hewlett-Packard introduced a business calculator, the [HP-19B](wikipedia://en/30220535), without reverse Polish notation, but its 1990 successor, the [HP-19BII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-19BII), gave users the option of using algebraic or reverse Polish notation again.

In 1986,[^o95][^o94] HP introduced [RPL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RPL_%28programming_language%29), an object-oriented successor to reverse Polish notation. It deviates from classical reverse Polish notation by using a dynamic stack only limited by the amount of available memory (instead of three or four fixed levels) and which could hold all kinds of data objects (including symbols, strings, lists, matrices, graphics, programs, etc.) instead of just numbers. The system would display an error message when running out of memory instead of just dropping values off the stack on overflow as with fixed-sized stacks.[^o98] It also changed the behaviour of the stack to no longer duplicate the top register on drops (since in an unlimited stack there is no longer a top register) and the behaviour of the Enter ↑ key so that it no longer duplicated values into Y, which had shown to sometimes cause confusion among users not familiar with the specific properties of the *automatic memory stack*. From 1990 to 2003, HP manufactured the [HP-48 series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-48_series) of graphing RPL calculators, followed by the [HP-49 series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-49_series) between 1999 and 2008. The last RPL calculator was named [HP 50g](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_50g), introduced in 2006 and discontinued in 2015. However, there are several community efforts like [newRPL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NewRPL) or [DB48X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DB48X) to recreate RPL on modern calculators.

As of 2011, Hewlett-Packard was offering the calculator models 12C, 12C Platinum, [17bII+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_17bII+), [20b](wikipedia://en/22821822), [30b](wikipedia://en/28301176), [33s](wikipedia://en/630171), [35s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_35s), [48gII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_48gII) (RPL) and 50g (RPL) which support reverse Polish notation.[^o65]

While calculators emulating classical models continued to support classical reverse Polish notation, new reverse Polish notation models feature a variant of reverse Polish notation, where the Enter ↑ key behaves as in RPL. This latter variant is sometimes known as *entry RPN*.[^o66]

In 2013, the [HP Prime](wikipedia://en/40988986) introduced a *128-level* form of entry RPN called *advanced RPN*. In contrast to RPL with its dynamic stack, it just drops values off the stack on overflow like other fixed-sized stacks do.[^o98] However, like RPL, it does not emulate the behaviour of a classical operational RPN stack to duplicate the top register on drops.

In late 2017, the list of active models supporting reverse Polish notation included only the 12C, 12C Platinum, 17bii+, 35s, and Prime. By July 2023, only the 12C, 12C Platinum, the [HP 15C Collector's Edition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP_15C_Collector's_Edition), and the Prime remain active models supporting RPN.

#### Sinclair Radionics {#Sinclair_Radionics}

In Britain, [Clive Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clive_Sinclair)'s [Sinclair Scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinclair_Scientific) (1974) and [Scientific Programmable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinclair_Scientific_Programmable) (1975) models used reverse Polish notation.[^o47][^o48]

#### Commodore {#Commodore}

In 1974, [Commodore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_Business_Machines) produced the [Minuteman \*6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_MM6) (MM6) without an Enter ↑ key and the [Minuteman \*6X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_MM6X) (MM6X) with an Enter ↑ key, both implementing a form of *two-level RPN*. The [SR4921 RPN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_SR4921_RPN) came with a variant of *four-level RPN* with stack levels named X, Y, Z, and W (rather than T) and an Ent key (for "entry"). In contrast to Hewlett-Packard's reverse Polish notation implementation, W filled with 0 instead of its contents being duplicated on stack drops.[^o70]

#### Prinztronic {#Prinztronic}

**Prinz** and **Prinztronic** were own-brand trade names of the British [Dixons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dixons_Retail) photographic and electronic goods stores retail chain, later rebranded as [Currys Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currys_Digital) stores, and became part of DSG International. A variety of calculator models was sold in the 1970s under the Prinztronic brand, all made for them by other companies.

Among these was the PROGRAM[^o71] Programmable Scientific Calculator which featured reverse Polish notation.

#### Heathkit {#Heathkit}

The [Aircraft Navigation Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_Navigation_Computer) [Heathkit OC-1401](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathkit_OC-1401)/[OCW-1401](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heathkit_OCW-1401) used *five-level RPN* in 1978.

#### Soviet Union / Semico {#Soviet_Union_/_Semico}

[Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) programmable calculators ([MK-52](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MK-52), [MK-61](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MK-61), [B3-34](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elektronika_B3-34) and earlier [B3-21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B3-21)[^o72] models) used reverse Polish notation for both automatic mode and programming. Modern Russian calculators [MK-161](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MK-161)[^o73] and [MK-152](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MK-152),[^o74] designed and manufactured in [Novosibirsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novosibirsk) since 2007 and offered by [Semico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semico),[^o75] are backwards compatible with them. Their extended architecture is also based on reverse Polish notation.

#### Community-developed hardware-based calculators {#Community-developed_hardware-based_calculators}

An eight-level stack was suggested by John A. Ball in 1978.[^o37]

The community-developed calculators [WP&nbsp;34S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP&nbsp;34S) (2011), [WP&nbsp;31S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP&nbsp;31S) (2014) and [WP&nbsp;34C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP&nbsp;34C) (2015), which are based on the [HP&nbsp;20b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;20b)/[HP&nbsp;30b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP&nbsp;30b) hardware platform, support classical Hewlett-Packard-style reverse Polish notation supporting automatic stack lift behaviour of the Enter ↑ key and top register copies on pops, but switchable between a four- and an eight-level operational stack.

In addition to the optional support for an eight-level stack, the newer [SwissMicros DM42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SwissMicros_DM42)-based [WP&nbsp;43S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP&nbsp;43S) as well as the [WP&nbsp;43C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WP&nbsp;43C) (2019) / [C43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C43_%28calculator%29) (2022) / [C47](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C47_%28calculator%29) (2023) derivatives support data types for stack objects (real numbers, infinite integers, finite integers, complex numbers, strings, matrices, dates and times). The latter three variants can also be switched between *classical* and *entry RPN* behaviour of the Enter ↑ key, a feature often requested by the community.[^o99] They also support a rarely seen [significant figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_figure) mode, which had already been available as a compile-time option for the WP 34S and WP 31S.[^o100][^o101]

Since 2021, the [HP-42S](wikipedia://en/730770) simulator [Free42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free42) version 3 can be enabled to support a dynamic RPN stack only limited by the amount of available memory instead of the classical 4-level stack. This feature was incorporated as a selectable function into the DM42 since firmware DMCP-3.21 / DM42-3.18.[^o19][^o20]
