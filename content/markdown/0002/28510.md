# Second-order predicate

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Second-order predicate"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a **second-order predicate** is a [predicate](wikipedia://en/285109) that takes a [first-order predicate](wikipedia://en/11344) as an argument.[^o2] Compare [higher-order predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_predicate).

:::

The idea of second order predication was introduced by the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) mathematician and philosopher [Frege](wikipedia://en/48416). It is based on his idea that a predicate such as "is a philosopher" designates a [concept, rather than an object](wikipedia://en/300920).[^o3] Sometimes a concept can itself be the subject of a proposition, such as in "There are no [Bosnian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina) philosophers". In this case, we are not saying anything of any Bosnian philosophers, but of the concept "is a Bosnian philosopher" that it is not [satisfied](wikipedia://en/23401166). Thus the predicate "is not satisfied" attributes something to the concept "is a Bosnian philosopher", and is thus a second-level predicate.

This idea is the basis of Frege's theory of [number](wikipedia://en/21690).[^o4]
