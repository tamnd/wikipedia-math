# Natural language

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Natural language"}

A **natural language** or **ordinary language** is any [spoken language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoken_language) or [signed language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_language) used organically in a human community, first emerging without conscious premeditation and subject to: replication across generations of people in the community, regional expansion or contraction, and gradual [internal and structural changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_change).[^o2] The vast majority of languages in the world are natural languages. As a category, natural language includes both [standard dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_dialect) (ones with high social [prestige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_prestige)) as well as [nonstandard or vernacular dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular). Even an [official language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_language) with a regulating academy such as [Standard French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_French), overseen by the [Académie Française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_Française), is still classified as a natural language (e.g. in the field of [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing)), as its [prescriptive aspects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_prescription) do not make it regulated enough to be considered a constructed or [controlled natural language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_natural_language). Linguists broadly consider [writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing) to be a static visual representation of a particular natural language, though, in many cases in highly [literate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literate) modern societies, [writing itself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Written_language) is also now subject to the natural processes of widely spoken natural languages.

:::

Excluded from the definition of natural language are: [artificial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_language) and [constructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructed_language) languages, such as those developed for [works of fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiction); languages of [formal logic](wikipedia://en/10939), such as those in [computer programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming);[^o3] and non-human [communication systems in nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biocommunication_%28science%29), such as [whale vocalizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_vocalization) or [honey bees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey_bee)' [waggle dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waggle_dance).[^o4] The academic consensus is that particular [key features](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_language#Aspects_of_human_language) prevent [animal communication systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_communication) from being classified as languages at all. Certain [systems of human communication with no native speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_language_%28disambiguation%29), as sometimes used in cross-cultural contexts, are also not natural languages.

## Controlled languages {#Controlled_languages}

Controlled natural languages are subsets of natural languages whose grammars and dictionaries have been restricted to reduce [ambiguity](wikipedia://en/677) and complexity. This may be accomplished by decreasing usage of [superlative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison_%28grammar%29) or [adverbial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adverbial) forms, or [irregular verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irregular_verbs). Typical purposes for developing and implementing a controlled natural language are to aid understanding by non-native speakers or to ease computer processing. An example of a widely used controlled natural language is [Simplified Technical English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Technical_English), which was originally developed for [aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_engineering) and [avionics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics) industry manuals.

## International constructed languages {#International_constructed_languages}

Being constructed, [International auxiliary languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language) such as [Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto) and [Interlingua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlingua) are not considered natural languages, with the possible exception of true native speakers of such languages.[^o7] Natural languages evolve, through fluctuations in vocabulary and syntax, to incrementally improve human communication. In contrast, Esperanto was created by Polish ophthalmologist [L. L. Zamenhof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._L._Zamenhof) in the late 19th century.

Some natural languages have become organically "standardized" through the synthesis of two or more pre-existing natural languages over a relatively short period of time through the development of a [pidgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pidgin), which is not considered a language, into a stable [creole language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creole_language). A creole such as [Haitian Creole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haitian_Creole) has its own grammar, vocabulary and literature. It is spoken by over 10 million people worldwide and is one of the two official languages of the [Republic of Haiti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Haiti).

As of 1996, there were 350 attested families with one or more [native speakers of Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_speakers_of_Esperanto). [Latino sine flexione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latino_sine_flexione), another international auxiliary language, is no longer widely spoken.
