# M. C. Escher

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="M. C. Escher"}

**Maurits Cornelis Escher** (;[^o3] ; 17 June 1898 – 27 March 1972) was a Dutch graphic artist who made [woodcuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut), [lithographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithography), and [mezzotints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mezzotint), many of which were [inspired by mathematics](wikipedia://en/22468661). Despite wide popular interest, for most of his life Escher was neglected in the art world, even in his native Netherlands. He was 70 before a retrospective exhibition was held. In the late twentieth century, he became more widely appreciated, and in the twenty-first century he has been celebrated in exhibitions around the world.

:::

His work features mathematical objects and operations including [impossible objects](wikipedia://en/56110), explorations of infinity, [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829), [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741), [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405), [truncated](wikipedia://en/4224429) and [stellated polyhedra](wikipedia://en/180253), [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), and [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671). Although Escher believed he had no mathematical ability, he interacted with the mathematicians [George Pólya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya), [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193), and [Donald Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter), and the [crystallographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography) [Friedrich Haag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Haag_%28crystallographer%29), and conducted his own research into tessellation.

Early in his career, he drew inspiration from nature, making studies of insects, landscapes, and plants such as [lichens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lichen), all of which he used as details in his artworks. He traveled in Italy and Spain, sketching buildings, townscapes, architecture and the tilings of the [Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra) and [the Mezquita of Cordoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque–Cathedral_of_Córdoba), and became steadily more interested in their [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181).

Escher's art became well known among scientists and mathematicians, and in popular culture, especially after it was featured by [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) in his April 1966 [Mathematical Games column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Games_column) in *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*. Apart from being used in a variety of technical papers, his work has appeared on the covers of many books and albums. He was one of the major inspirations for [Douglas Hofstadter](wikipedia://en/8758)'s [Pulitzer Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize)–winning 1979 book *[Gödel, Escher, Bach](wikipedia://en/48103)*.

## Early life {#Early_life}

*Image: Leeuwarden - Keramiekmuseum Princessehof.jpg.* Escher's birth house, now part of the [Princessehof Ceramics Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princessehof_Ceramics_Museum), in [Leeuwarden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeuwarden), [Friesland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friesland), the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands)

Maurits Cornelis Escher was born on 17 June 1898 in [Leeuwarden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeuwarden), [Friesland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friesland), the Netherlands, in a house that forms part of the [Princessehof Ceramics Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princessehof_Ceramics_Museum) today. He was the youngest son of the civil engineer [George Arnold Escher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Arnold_Escher) and his second wife, Sara Gleichman. In 1903, the family moved to [Arnhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnhem), where he attended primary and secondary school until 1918.[^o5][^o6] Known to his friends and family as "Mauk", he was a sickly child and was placed in a special school at the age of seven; he failed the second grade.[^o7] Although he excelled at drawing, his grades were generally poor. He took [carpentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpentry) and piano lessons until he was thirteen years old.[^o5][^o6]

In 1918, he went to the [Technical College of Delft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delft_University_of_Technology).[^o5][^o6] From 1919 to 1922, Escher attended the [Haarlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haarlem) School of Architecture and Decorative Arts, learning drawing and the art of making [woodcuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut).[^o5] He briefly studied [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), but he failed a number of subjects (due partly to a persistent skin infection) and switched to [decorative arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorative_arts),[^o7] studying under the graphic artist [Samuel Jessurun de Mesquita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Jessurun_de_Mesquita).[^o8]

## Study journeys {#Study_journeys}

*Image: Alhambra-p3-closeup.jpg.* [Moorish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish) [tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellations) including this one at the [Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra) inspired Escher's work with tilings of the plane. He made sketches of this and other Alhambra patterns in 1936.[^o10]

In 1922, an important year of his life, Escher traveled through Italy, visiting [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence), [San Gimignano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Gimignano), [Volterra](wikipedia://en/548647), [Siena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siena), and [Ravello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravello). In the same year, he traveled through Spain, visiting [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid), [Toledo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toledo,_Spain), and [Granada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granada).[^o5] He was impressed by the Italian countryside and, in Granada, by the [Moorish architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorish_architecture) of the fourteenth-century [Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra). The intricate decorative designs of the Alhambra, based on [geometrical](wikipedia://en/18973446) [symmetries](wikipedia://en/53741) featuring interlocking repetitive patterns in the coloured tiles or sculpted into the walls and ceilings, triggered his interest in the mathematics of [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) and became a powerful influence on his work.[^o11][^o12]

*Image: Escher Alhambra Tessellation Sketch.jpg.* Escher's painstaking[^o51] study of the same Moorish tiling in the Alhambra, 1936, demonstrates his growing interest in tessellation.

Escher returned to Italy and lived in [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome) from 1923 to 1935. While in Italy, Escher met Jetta Umiker – a Swiss woman, like himself attracted to Italy – whom he married in 1924. The couple settled in Rome where their first son, Giorgio (George) Arnaldo Escher, named after his grandfather, was born. Escher and Jetta later had two more sons – Arthur and Jan.[^o5][^o6]

He travelled frequently, visiting (among other places) [Viterbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viterbo) in 1926, the [Abruzzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abruzzo) in 1927 and 1929, [Corsica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsica) in 1928 and 1933, [Calabria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabria) in 1930, the [Amalfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalfi) coast in 1931 and 1934, and [Gargano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gargano) and [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily) in 1932 and 1935. The townscapes and landscapes of these places feature prominently in his artworks. In May and June 1936, Escher travelled back to Spain, revisiting the Alhambra and spending days at a time making detailed drawings of its mosaic patterns. It was here that he became fascinated, to the point of obsession, with tessellation, explaining:[^o8]

It remains an extremely absorbing activity, a real mania to which I have become addicted, and from which I sometimes find it hard to tear myself away.[^o51]

The sketches he made in the Alhambra formed a major source for his work from that time on.[^o51] He studied the architecture of the [Mezquita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque–Cathedral_of_Córdoba), the Moorish mosque of Cordoba. This turned out to be the last of his long study journeys; after 1937, his artworks were created in his studio rather than in the field. His art correspondingly changed sharply from being mainly observational, with a strong emphasis on the realistic details of things seen in nature and architecture, to being the product of his geometric analysis and his visual imagination. All the same, even his early work already shows his interest in the nature of space, the unusual, perspective, and multiple points of view.[^o8][^o51]

## Later life {#Later_life}

In 1935, the political climate in Italy under [Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini) became unacceptable to Escher. He had no interest in politics, finding it impossible to involve himself with any ideals other than the expressions of his own concepts through his own particular medium, but he was averse to fanaticism and hypocrisy. When his eldest son, George, was forced at the age of nine to wear a [Ballila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_Nazionale_Balilla) uniform in school, the family left Italy and moved to [Château-d'Œx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Château-d'Œx), Switzerland, where they remained for two years.[^o14]

The Netherlands post office had Escher design a [semi-postal stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-postal_stamp) for the "Air Fund" (Dutch: *Het Nationaal Luchtvaartfonds*) in 1935, and again in 1949 he designed Dutch stamps. These were for the 75th anniversary of the [Universal Postal Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Postal_Union); a different design was used by [Suriname](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suriname) and the [Netherlands Antilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands_Antilles) for the same commemoration.[^o15]

Escher, who had been very fond of and inspired by the landscapes in Italy, was decidedly unhappy in Switzerland. In 1937, the family moved again, to [Uccle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uccle) (Ukkel), a suburb of [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels), Belgium.[^o5][^o6] [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) forced them to move in January 1941, this time to [Baarn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baarn), Netherlands, where Escher lived until 1970.[^o5] Most of Escher's best-known works date from this period. The sometimes cloudy, cold, and wet weather of the Netherlands allowed him to focus intently on his work.[^o5] After 1953, Escher lectured widely. A planned series of lectures in North America in 1962 was cancelled after an illness, and he stopped creating artworks for a time,[^o5] but the illustrations and text for the lectures were later published as part of the book *Escher on Escher*.[^o16] He was awarded the Knighthood of the [Order of Orange-Nassau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Orange-Nassau) in 1955;[^o5] in 1967, he was made an Officer.[^o17]

In July 1969, he finished his last work, a large woodcut with threefold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) called *[Snakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snakes_%28M._C._Escher%29)*, in which snakes wind through a pattern of linked rings. These shrink to infinity toward both the center and the edge of a circle. It was exceptionally elaborate, being printed using three blocks, each rotated three times about the center of the image and precisely aligned to avoid gaps and overlaps, for a total of nine print operations for each finished print. The image encapsulates Escher's love of symmetry; of interlocking patterns; and, at the end of his life, of his approach to infinity.[^o18][^o19][^o20] The care that Escher took in creating and printing this woodcut can be seen in a video recording.[^o21]

Escher moved to the [Rosa Spier Huis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_Spier_Huis) in [Laren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laren,_North_Holland) in 1970, an artists' retirement home in which he had his own studio. He died in a hospital in [Hilversum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilversum) on 27 March 1972, aged 73.[^o5][^o6] He is buried at the New Cemetery in Baarn.[^o22][^o23]

## Mathematically inspired work {#Mathematically_inspired_work}

Much of Escher's work is inescapably mathematical. This has caused a disconnect between his fame among mathematicians and the general public, and the lack of esteem with which he has been viewed in the art world.[^o25][^o31] His originality and mastery of graphic techniques are respected, but his works have been thought too intellectual and insufficiently lyrical. Movements such as [conceptual art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_art) have, to a degree, reversed the art world's attitude to intellectuality and lyricism, but this did not rehabilitate Escher, because traditional critics still disliked his narrative themes and his use of perspective. However, these same qualities made his work highly attractive to the public.[^o25]

Escher is not the first artist to explore mathematical themes: J. L. Locher, a previous director of the [Kunstmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunstmuseum_Den_Haag) in [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague), pointed out that [Parmigianino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmigianino) (1503–1540) had explored spherical geometry and reflection in his 1524 *[Self-portrait in a Convex Mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Portrait_in_a_Convex_Mirror_%28Parmigianino%29)*, depicting his own image in a curved mirror, while [William Hogarth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hogarth)'s 1754 *[Satire on False Perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satire_on_False_Perspective)* foreshadows Escher's playful exploration of errors in perspective.[^o26][^o77] Another early artistic forerunner is [Giovanni Battista Piranesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Piranesi) (1720–1778), whose dark "fantastical"[^o27] prints such as *The Drawbridge* in his [*Carceri* ("Prisons")](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_Prisons) sequence depict perspectives of complex architecture with many stairs and ramps, peopled by walking figures.[^o27][^o28] Escher greatly admired Piranesi and had several of Piranesi's prints hanging in his studio.[^o29][^o30]

Only with 20th century movements such as [Cubism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubism), [De Stijl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Stijl), [Dadaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dada), and [Surrealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrealism) did mainstream art start to explore Escher-like ways of looking at the world with multiple simultaneous viewpoints.[^o25] However, although Escher had much in common with, for example, [Magritte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magritte)'s surrealism and [Op art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op_art), he did not make contact with any of these movements.[^o31][^o32]

### Tessellation {#Tessellation}

In his early years, Escher sketched landscapes and nature. He sketched insects such as ants, bees, grasshoppers, and mantises,[^o34] which appeared frequently in his later work. His early love of [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) and Italian landscapes and of nature created an interest in tessellation, which he called *[Regular Division of the Plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_Division_of_the_Plane)*; this became the title of his 1958 book, complete with reproductions of a series of woodcuts based on tessellations of the plane, in which he described the systematic buildup of mathematical designs in his artworks. He wrote, "[crystallographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography) have opened the gate leading to an extensive domain".[^o35]

*Image: Study of Regular Division of the Plane with Reptiles.jpg.* Hexagonal tessellation with animals: *Study of Regular Division of the Plane with Reptiles* (1939). Escher reused the design in his 1943 lithograph *[Reptiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptiles_%28M._C._Escher%29)*.

After his 1936 journey to the [Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra) and to [La Mezquita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque–Cathedral_of_Córdoba), [Cordoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Córdoba,_Andalusia), where he sketched the [Moorish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moors) architecture and the tessellated mosaic decorations,[^o36] Escher began to explore tessellation using geometric grids as the basis for his sketches. He then extended these to form complex interlocking designs, for example with animals such as birds, fish, and reptiles.[^o37] One of his first attempts at a tessellation was his pencil, India ink, and watercolour *Study of Regular Division of the Plane with Reptiles* (1939), constructed on a hexagonal grid. The heads of the red, green, and white reptiles meet at a vertex; the tails, legs, and sides of the animals interlock exactly. It was used as the basis for his 1943 lithograph *[Reptiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptiles_%28M._C._Escher%29)*.[^o38]

His first study of mathematics began with papers by [George Pólya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya)[^o39] and by the crystallographer [Friedrich Haag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Haag_%28crystallographer%29)[^o40] on plane [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186), sent to him by his brother [Berend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berend_George_Escher), a geologist.[^o56] He carefully studied the 17 canonical [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214) and created periodic tilings with 43 drawings of different types of symmetry. From this point on, he developed a mathematical approach to expressions of symmetry in his artworks using his own notation. Starting in 1937, he created woodcuts based on the 17 groups. His *[Metamorphosis I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphosis_I)* (1937) began a series of designs that told a story through the use of pictures. In *Metamorphosis I*, he transformed [convex polygons](wikipedia://en/660019) into regular patterns in a plane to form a human motif. He extended the approach in his piece *[Metamorphosis III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamorphosis_III)*, which is almost seven metres long.[^o51][^o41]

In 1941 and 1942, Escher summarised his findings for his own artistic use in a sketchbook, which he labeled (following Haag) *Regelmatige vlakverdeling in asymmetrische congruente veelhoeken* ("Regular division of the plane with asymmetric congruent polygons").[^o42] The mathematician [Doris Schattschneider](wikipedia://en/39957951) unequivocally described this notebook as recording "a methodical investigation that can only be termed mathematical research."[^o56] She defined the research questions he was following as

(1) What are the possible shapes for a tile that can produce a regular division of the plane, that is, a tile that can fill the plane with its congruent images such that every tile is surrounded in the same manner? (2) Moreover, in what ways are the edges of such a tile related to each other by [isometries](wikipedia://en/1101694)?[^o56]

### Geometries {#Geometries}

Although Escher did not have mathematical training – his understanding of mathematics was largely visual and intuitive – his [art had a strong mathematical component](wikipedia://en/22468661), and several of the worlds that he drew were built around impossible objects. After 1924, Escher turned to sketching landscapes in Italy and [Corsica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsica) with irregular [perspectives](wikipedia://en/14909067) that are impossible in natural form. His first print of an impossible reality was *[Still Life and Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Still_Life_and_Street)* (1937); impossible stairs and multiple visual and gravitational perspectives feature in popular works such as *[Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_%28M._C._Escher%29)* (1953). *[House of Stairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Stairs)* (1951) attracted the interest of the mathematician [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193) and his father, the biologist [Lionel Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Penrose). In 1956, they published a paper, "Impossible Objects: A Special Type of Visual Illusion" and later sent Escher a copy. Escher replied, admiring the Penroses' [continuously rising flights of steps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose_stairs), and enclosed a print of *[Ascending and Descending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascending_and_Descending)* (1960). The paper contained the tribar or [Penrose triangle](wikipedia://en/56108), which Escher used repeatedly in his lithograph of a building that appears to function as a [perpetual motion](wikipedia://en/54077) machine, *[Waterfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall_%28M._C._Escher%29)* (1961).[^o44][^o45][^o46][^o47]

Escher was interested enough in [Hieronymus Bosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieronymus_Bosch)'s 1500 triptych *[The Garden of Earthly Delights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Garden_of_Earthly_Delights)* to re-create part of its right-hand panel, *Hell*, as a lithograph in 1935. He reused the figure of a [Medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) woman in a two-pointed headdress and a long gown in his lithograph *[Belvedere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belvedere_%28M._C._Escher%29)* in 1958; the image is, like many of his other "extraordinary invented places",[^o48] peopled with "[jesters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jester), knaves, and contemplators".[^o48] Thus, Escher not only was interested in possible or impossible geometry but was, in his own words, a "reality enthusiast";[^o48] he combined "formal astonishment with a vivid and idiosyncratic vision".[^o48]

Escher worked primarily in the media of [lithographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithography) and [woodcuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodcut), although the few [mezzotints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mezzotint) he made are considered to be masterpieces of the technique. In his graphic art, he portrayed mathematical relationships among shapes, figures, and space. Integrated into his prints were mirror images of cones, spheres, cubes, rings, and spirals.[^o49]

Escher was fascinated by mathematical objects such as the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817), which has only one surface. His wood engraving *Möbius Strip II* (1963) depicts a chain of ants marching forever over what, at any one place, are the two opposite faces of the object—which are seen on inspection to be parts of the strip's single surface. In Escher's own words:[^o50]

An endless ring-shaped band usually has two distinct surfaces, one inside and one outside. Yet on this strip nine red ants crawl after each other and travel the front side as well as the reverse side. Therefore the strip has only one surface.[^o50]

The mathematical influence in his work became prominent after 1936, when, having boldly asked the Adria Shipping Company if he could sail with them as travelling artist in return for making drawings of their ships, they surprisingly agreed, and he sailed the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea), becoming interested in order and symmetry. Escher described this journey, including his repeat visit to the Alhambra, as "the richest source of inspiration I have ever tapped".[^o51]

Escher's interest in [curvilinear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvilinear_perspective) was encouraged by his friend and "kindred spirit",[^o52] the art historian and artist Albert Flocon, in another example of constructive mutual influence. Flocon identified Escher as a "thinking artist"[^o52] alongside [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca), [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer), [Wenzel Jamnitzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenzel_Jamnitzer), [Abraham Bosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Bosse), [Girard Desargues](wikipedia://en/358484), and [Père Nicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Père_Nicon).[^o52] Flocon was delighted by Escher's *Grafiek en tekeningen* ("Graphics and Drawings"), which he read in 1959. This stimulated Flocon and André Barre to correspond with Escher and to write the book *La Perspective curviligne* ("[Curvilinear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvilinear_perspective)").[^o53]

### Platonic and other solids {#Platonic_and_other_solids}

*Image: Universiteit Twente Mesa Plus Escher Object.jpg.* Sculpture of a [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843), as in Escher's 1952 work *[Gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation_%28M._C._Escher%29)* ([University of Twente](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Twente))

Escher often incorporated three-dimensional objects such as spheres, [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905), including tetrahedrons and cubes, into his works, as well as mathematical objects such as [cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) and [stellated polyhedra](wikipedia://en/180253). In the print [*Reptiles*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reptiles_%28M._C._Escher%29), he combined two- and three-dimensional images. In one of his papers, Escher emphasized the importance of dimensionality:

The flat shape irritates me — I feel like telling my objects, you are too fictitious, lying there next to each other static and frozen: do something, come off the paper and show me what you are capable of! ... So I make them come out of the plane. ... My objects ... may finally return to the plane and disappear into their place of origin.[^o55]

Escher's artwork is especially well-liked by mathematicians such as [Doris Schattschneider](wikipedia://en/39957951) and scientists such as [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193), who enjoy his use of [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) and [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) distortions.[^o56] For example, in *[Gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation_%28M._C._Escher%29)*, animals climb around a [stellated](wikipedia://en/180253) [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407).[^o57]

The two towers of *Waterfall* impossible building are topped with compound polyhedra, one a [compound of three cubes](wikipedia://en/14809983), the other a stellated [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631) now known as [Escher's solid](wikipedia://en/33808818). Escher had used this solid in his 1948 woodcut *[Stars](wikipedia://en/1170784)*, which contains all five of the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) and various stellated solids, representing stars; the central solid is animated by [chameleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chameleon) climbing through the frame as it whirls in space. Escher possessed a 6 cm [refracting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope) and was a keen-enough amateur [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) to have recorded observations of [binary stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star).[^o58][^o59][^o60]

### Levels of reality {#Levels_of_reality}

Escher's artistic expression was created from images in his mind, rather than directly from observations and travels to other countries. His interest in the multiple levels of reality in art is seen in works such as *[Drawing Hands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawing_Hands)* (1948), where two hands are shown, each drawing the other. The critic Steven Poole commented that

It is a neat depiction of one of Escher's enduring fascinations: the contrast between the two-dimensional flatness of a sheet of paper and the illusion of three-dimensional volume that can be created with certain marks. In *Drawing Hands*, space and the flat plane coexist, each born from and returning to the other, the black magic of the artistic illusion made creepily manifest.[^o48]

### Infinity and hyperbolic geometry {#Infinity_and_hyperbolic_geometry}

*Image: Schattschneider Reconstruction of Escher's Coxeter Diagram.jpg.* [Doris Schattschneider](wikipedia://en/39957951)'s reconstruction of the diagram of hyperbolic tiling sent by Escher to the mathematician [Donald Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter)[^o56]

In 1954, the International Congress of Mathematicians met in Amsterdam, and N. G. de Bruin organised a display of Escher's work at the Stedelijk Museum for the participants. Both Roger Penrose and [H. S. M. Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter) were deeply impressed with Escher's intuitive grasp of mathematics. Inspired by *Relativity*, Penrose devised his [tribar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose_tribar), and his father, Lionel Penrose, devised an endless staircase. Roger Penrose sent sketches of both objects to Escher, and the cycle of invention was closed when Escher then created the [perpetual motion](wikipedia://en/54077) machine of *Waterfall* and the endless march of the monk-figures of *Ascending and Descending*.[^o56] In 1957 Coxeter obtained Escher's permission to use two of his drawings in his paper "Crystal symmetry and its generalizations".[^o56][^o63] He sent Escher a copy of the paper; Escher recorded that Coxeter's figure of a hyperbolic tessellation "gave me quite a shock": the infinite regular repetition of the tiles in the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Models_of_the_hyperbolic_plane), growing rapidly smaller towards the edge of the circle, was precisely what he wanted to allow him to represent [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824) on a two-dimensional plane.[^o56][^o64]

Escher carefully studied Coxeter's figure, marking it up to analyse the successively smaller circles with which (he deduced) it had been constructed. He then constructed a diagram, which he sent to Coxeter, showing his analysis; Coxeter confirmed it was correct, but disappointed Escher with his highly technical reply. All the same, Escher persisted with [hyperbolic tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_tiling), which he called "Coxetering".[^o56] Among the results were the series of wood engravings *Circle Limit I–IV*.[^o56] In 1959, Coxeter published his finding that these works were extraordinarily accurate: "Escher got it absolutely right to the millimeter".[^o65]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

*Image: Gevel Escher in Het Paleis 300 dpi.jpg.* The [Escher Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escher_Museum) in [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague). The poster shows a detail from *[Day and Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Day_and_Night_%28M._C._Escher%29)*, 1938.

### In art collections {#In_art_collections}

The Escher intellectual property is controlled by the M.C. Escher Company, while exhibitions of his artworks are managed separately by the M.C. Escher Foundation.

The primary institutional collections of original works by M.C. Escher are exhibited at the [Escher Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escher_Museum) in [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague) (which are part of the large collection of the Kunstmuseum there[^o68]); the [National Gallery of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Art) (Washington, DC);[^o69] the [National Gallery of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Canada) (Ottawa);[^o70] the [Israel Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Museum) (Jerusalem);[^o71] and the [Huis ten Bosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huis_Ten_Bosch_%28theme_park%29) (Nagasaki, Japan).[^o72]

### Exhibitions {#Exhibitions}

*Image: Escher Poster Dulwich Picture Gallery 2015.jpg.* Poster advertising the first major exhibition of Escher's work in Britain ([Dulwich Picture Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulwich_Picture_Gallery), 14 October 2015 – 17 January 2016). The image, which shows Escher and his interest in geometric distortion and multiple levels of distance from reality, is based on his *[Hand with Reflecting Sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_with_Reflecting_Sphere)*, 1935.[^o78][^o77]

Despite wide popular interest, Escher was for a long time somewhat neglected in the art world; even in his native Netherlands, he was 70 before a retrospective exhibition was held.[^o48] In the twenty-first century, major exhibitions have been held in cities around the world.[^o74][^o76][^o80] An exhibition of his work in Rio de Janeiro attracted more than 573,000 visitors in 2011;[^o74] its daily visitor count of 9,677 made it the most visited museum exhibition of the year, anywhere in the world.[^o75] No major exhibition of Escher's work was held in Britain until 2015, when the [Scottish National Gallery of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_National_Gallery_of_Modern_Art) ran one in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh) from June to September 2015,[^o76] moving in October 2015 to the [Dulwich Picture Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulwich_Picture_Gallery), London. The exhibition poster is based on *[Hand with Reflecting Sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_with_Reflecting_Sphere)*, 1935, which shows Escher in his house reflected in a handheld sphere, thus illustrating the artist, his interest in [levels of reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics) in art (e.g., is the hand in the foreground more real than the reflected one?), [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405), and [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026).[^o77][^o78][^o79] The exhibition moved to Italy in 2015–2016, attracting over 500,000 visitors in Rome and Bologna,[^o80] and then [Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan).[^o81][^o82][^o83]

In 2023, the [Kunstmuseum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunstmuseum_Den_Haag) in the Hague created a large retrospective of Escher, entitled 'Escher – Other World'.[^o84]

### In mathematics and science {#In_mathematics_and_science}

*Image: Leeuwarden - Tegeltableau Escher.jpg.* Wall tableau of one of Escher's bird tessellations at the [Princessehof Ceramics Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princessehof_Ceramics_Museum) in Leeuwarden

[Doris Schattschneider](wikipedia://en/39957951) identifies eleven strands of mathematical and scientific research anticipated or directly inspired by Escher. These are the classification of regular tilings using the edge relationships of tiles: two-color and two-motif tilings ([counterchange symmetry or antisymmetry](wikipedia://en/73498718)); color symmetry (in [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography)); metamorphosis or [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) change; covering surfaces with symmetric patterns; Escher's algorithm (for generating patterns using decorated squares); creating tile shapes; local versus global definitions of regularity; symmetry of a tiling induced by the symmetry of a tile; orderliness not induced by symmetry groups; the filling of the central void in Escher's lithograph *[Print Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_Gallery_%28M._C._Escher%29)* by H. Lenstra and B. de Smit.[^o56]

The [Pulitzer Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize)-winning[^o86] 1979 book *[Gödel, Escher, Bach](wikipedia://en/48103)* by [Douglas Hofstadter](wikipedia://en/8758)[^o87] discusses the ideas of self-reference and [strange loops](wikipedia://en/47929) expressed in Escher's art. The [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid) [4444 Escher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4444_Escher) was named in Escher's honor in 1985.[^o88]

### In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

Escher's fame in popular culture grew when his work was featured by [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) in his April 1966 ["Mathematical Games" column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Games_column) in *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*.[^o90] Escher's works have appeared on many album covers including [The Scaffold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scaffold)'s 1969 *L the P* with *Ascending and Descending*; [Mott the Hoople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mott_the_Hoople)'s eponymous 1969 record with *Reptiles*, [Beaver & Krause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaver_&_Krause)'s 1970 *In A Wild Sanctuary* with *Three Worlds*; and [Mandrake Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandrake_Memorial)'s 1970 *Puzzle* with *House of Stairs* and (inside) *Curl Up*. His works have similarly been used on many book covers, including some editions of [Edwin Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Abbott_Abbott)'s *Flatland*, which used *Three Spheres*; [E. H. Gombrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Gombrich)'s *Meditations on a Hobby Horse* with *Horseman*; Pamela Hall's *Heads You Lose* with *Plane Filling 1*; Patrick A. Horton's *Mastering the Power of Story* with *Drawing Hands*; [Erich Gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Gamma) et al.'s *Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-oriented software* with *Swans*; and Arthur Markman's *Knowledge Representation* with *Reptiles*. The "World of Escher" markets [posters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poster), [neckties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necktie), [T-shirts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-shirt), and [jigsaw puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jigsaw_puzzle) of Escher's artworks.[^o91] Both Austria and the Netherlands have issued [postage stamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp) commemorating the artist and his works.[^o15] In 1996, [Byron Preiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byron_Preiss) and [Harry N. Abrams Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrams_Books) published *[Escher Interactive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escher_Interactive)* for [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows), a CD-ROM that contained a digital gallery and interactive games inspired by Escher's work.[^o92] In 2019, Robin Lutz wrote and directed the documentary film *M.C. Escher: Journey To Infinity*, telling the story of Escher's life and art.[^o93][^o94]
