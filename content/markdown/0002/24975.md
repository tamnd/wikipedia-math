# Piezoelectricity

*Image: Piezoelectric balance presented by Pierre Curie to Lord Kelvin, Hunterian Museum, Glasgow.jpg.* Piezoelectric balance presented by [Pierre Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Curie) to [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin), [Hunterian Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunterian_Museum_and_Art_Gallery), [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Piezoelectricity"}

**Piezoelectricity** (, ) is the [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) that accumulates in certain [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) materials—such as [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal), certain [ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic), and [biological matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_matter) such as [bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bone), [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), and various [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein)—in response to applied [mechanical stress](wikipedia://en/228107).[^o3][^o4][^o5]

:::

The piezoelectric effect results from the linear [electromechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromechanical) interaction between the mechanical and electrical states in crystalline materials with no [inversion symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrosymmetry).[^o6] The piezoelectric effect is a [reversible process](wikipedia://en/416681): [materials exhibiting the piezoelectric effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_piezoelectric_materials) also exhibit the reverse piezoelectric effect, the internal generation of a mechanical strain resulting from an applied [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092). For example, [lead zirconate titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_zirconate_titanate) crystals will generate measurable piezoelectricity when their static structure is [deformed](wikipedia://en/19194778) by about 0.1% of the original dimension. Conversely, those same crystals will change about 0.1% of their static dimension when an external electric field is applied. The inverse piezoelectric effect is used in the production of [ultrasound waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasound).[^o7]

French physicists [Jacques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Curie) and [Pierre Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Curie) discovered piezoelectricity in 1880.[^o18] The piezoelectric effect is exploited in many forms, including the production and detection of sound,[^o8] piezoelectric [inkjet printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inkjet_printing), generation of high voltage electricity, as a [clock generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock_generator) in electronic devices, in [microbalances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbalance), to drive an [ultrasonic nozzle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasonic_nozzle), and in ultrafine focusing of optical assemblies. It forms the basis for [scanning probe microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_probe_microscopy) that resolve images at the scale of [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom). It is used in the [pickups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickup_%28music_technology%29) of some [electronically amplified guitars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickup_%28music_technology%29#Piezoelectric_pickups) and as [triggers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigger_%28drums%29) in most modern [electronic drums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_drum).[^o9][^o10] The piezoelectric effect also finds everyday uses, such as generating sparks to ignite gas cooking and heating devices, torches, and [cigarette lighters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighters).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word *piezoelectricity* means [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity) resulting from [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure). It is derived grc (an ancient source of static electricity).[^o12][^o13] The German form of the word (Piezoelektrizität) was coined in 1881 by the German physicist [Wilhelm Gottlieb Hankel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Gottlieb_Hankel); the English word was derived from German in 1883.[^o12][^o14]

## History {#History}

### Discovery and early research {#Discovery_and_early_research}

The [pyroelectric effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroelectricity), by which a material generates an [electric potential](wikipedia://en/59615) in response to a temperature change, was studied by [Carl Linnaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus) and [Franz Aepinus](wikipedia://en/604168) in the mid-18th century. Drawing on this knowledge, both [René Just Haüy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Just_Haüy) and [Antoine César Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_César_Becquerel) posited a relationship between [mechanical stress](wikipedia://en/228107) and electric charge; however, experiments by both proved inconclusive.[^o17]

*Image: Top view of Curie piezo electric compensator.jpg.* View of piezo crystal in the top of a Curie compensator in the Museum of Scotland.

The first demonstration of the direct piezoelectric effect was in 1880 by the brothers [Pierre Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Curie) and [Jacques Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Curie).[^o18] They combined their knowledge of [pyroelectricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroelectricity) with their understanding of the underlying crystal structures that gave rise to pyroelectricity to predict crystal behavior, and demonstrated the effect using crystals of [tourmaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourmaline), [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz), [topaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topaz), [cane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_cane) [sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar), and [Rochelle salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochelle_salt) (sodium potassium tartrate tetrahydrate). Quartz and Rochelle salt exhibited the most piezoelectricity.

*Image: SchemaPiezo.gif.* A piezoelectric disk generates a voltage when deformed (change in shape is greatly exaggerated).

The Curies, however, did not predict the converse piezoelectric effect. The converse effect was mathematically deduced from fundamental thermodynamic principles by [Gabriel Lippmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Lippmann) in 1881.[^o19] The Curies immediately confirmed the existence of the converse effect,[^o20] and went on to obtain quantitative proof of the complete reversibility of electro-elasto-mechanical deformations in piezoelectric crystals.

For the next few decades, piezoelectricity remained something of a laboratory curiosity, though it was a vital tool in the discovery of [polonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium) and radium by Pierre and [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie) in 1898. More work was done to explore and define the crystal structures that exhibited piezoelectricity. This culminated in 1910 with the publication of [Woldemar Voigt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woldemar_Voigt)'s *Lehrbuch der Kristallphysik* (*Textbook on Crystal Physics*),[^o21] which described the 20 natural crystal classes capable of piezoelectricity, and rigorously defined the piezoelectric constants using [tensor analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_analysis).

### World War I and inter-war years {#World_War_I_and_inter-war_years}

The first practical application for piezoelectric devices was [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar), first developed during [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I). The superior performance of piezoelectric devices, operating at ultrasonic frequencies, superseded the earlier [Fessenden oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fessenden_oscillator). In [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) in 1917, [Paul Langevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Langevin) and his coworkers developed an [ultrasonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasound) [submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine) detector.[^o23] The detector consisted of a [transducer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transducer), made of thin quartz crystals carefully glued between two steel plates, and a [hydrophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophone) to detect the returned [echo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echo_%28phenomenon%29). By emitting a high-frequency pulse from the transducer, and measuring the amount of time it takes to hear an echo from the sound waves bouncing off an object, one can calculate the distance to that object.

Piezoelectric devices found homes in many fields. Ceramic [phonograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonograph) cartridges simplified player design, were cheap and accurate, and made record players cheaper to maintain and easier to build. The development of the [ultrasonic transducer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasonic_transducer) allowed for easy measurement of viscosity and elasticity in fluids and solids, resulting in huge advances in materials research. Ultrasonic [time-domain reflectometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-domain_reflectometer) (which send an ultrasonic pulse through a material and measure reflections from discontinuities) could find flaws inside cast metal and stone objects, improving structural safety.

### World War II and post-war {#World_War_II_and_post-war}

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), independent research groups in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR), and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) discovered a new class of synthetic materials, called [ferroelectrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferroelectricity), which exhibited piezoelectric constants many times higher than natural materials. This led to intense research to develop [barium titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_titanate) and later lead zirconate titanate materials with specific properties for particular applications.

One significant example of the use of piezoelectric crystals was developed by [Bell Telephone Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Telephone_Laboratories). Following World War I, Frederick R. Lack, working in radio telephony in the engineering department, developed the "AT cut" crystal, a crystal that operated through a wide range of temperatures. Lack's crystal did not need the heavy accessories previous crystal used, facilitating its use on the aircraft. This development allowed Allied air forces to engage in coordinated mass attacks through the use of aviation radio.

Development of piezoelectric devices and materials in the United States was kept within the companies doing the development, mostly due to the wartime beginnings of the field, and in the interests of securing profitable patents. New materials were the first to be developed—[quartz crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz_crystals) were the first commercially exploited piezoelectric material, but scientists searched for higher-performance materials. Despite the advances in materials and the maturation of manufacturing processes, the United States market did not grow as quickly as Japan's did. Without many new applications, the growth of the United States' piezoelectric industry suffered.

In contrast, Japanese manufacturers shared their information, quickly overcoming technical and manufacturing challenges and creating new markets. In Japan, a temperature stable crystal cut was developed by [Issac Koga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issac_Koga). Japanese efforts in materials research created piezoceramic materials competitive to the United States materials but free of expensive patent restrictions. Major Japanese piezoelectric developments included new designs of piezoceramic filters for [radios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio) and [televisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Televisions), piezo buzzers and audio transducers that can connect directly to electronic circuits, and the [piezoelectric igniter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezo_ignition), which generates sparks for small engine ignition systems and gas-grill lighters, by compressing a ceramic disc. Ultrasonic transducers that transmit sound waves through air had existed for quite some time but first saw major commercial use in early television remote controls. These transducers now are mounted on several [car](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile) models as an [echolocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_location) device, helping the driver determine the distance from the car to any objects that may be in its path.

## Mechanism {#Mechanism}

*Image: Piezo bending principle.svg.* Piezoelectric plate used to convert [audio signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_signal) to sound waves

The nature of the piezoelectric effect is closely related to the occurrence of [electric dipole moments](wikipedia://en/30876071) in solids. These may be either induced for [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ions) on [crystal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_lattice) sites with asymmetric charge surroundings (as in [BaTiO<sub>3</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_titanate) and [PZTs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PZT)) or directly carried by molecular groups (as in [cane sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cane_sugar)). The dipole density or [polarization](wikipedia://en/698648) (dimensionality \[C·m/m<sup>3</sup>\] ) may easily be calculated for [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystals) by summing up the dipole moments per volume of the crystallographic [unit cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_cell).[^o27] As every dipole is a vector, the dipole density ***P*** is a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641). Dipoles near each other tend to be aligned in regions called [Weiss domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain). The domains are usually randomly oriented, but can be aligned using the process of *poling* (not the same as [magnetic poling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet#Magnetizing_ferromagnets)), a process by which a strong electric field is applied across the material, usually at elevated temperatures. Not all piezoelectric materials can be poled.[^o28]

Of importance for the piezoelectric effect is the change of polarization ***P*** when applying a [mechanical stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_stress). This might either be caused by a reconfiguration of the dipole-inducing surrounding or by re-orientation of molecular dipole moments under the influence of the external stress. Piezoelectricity may then manifest in a variation of the polarization strength, its direction or both, with the details depending on: 1. the orientation of ***P*** within the crystal; 2. [crystal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_symmetry); and 3. the applied mechanical stress. The change in ***P*** appears as a variation of surface [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) upon the crystal faces, i.e. as a variation of the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) extending between the faces caused by a change in dipole density in the bulk. For example, a 1 cm<sup>3</sup> cube of quartz with 2 kN (500 lbf) of correctly applied force can produce a voltage of 12500 [V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt).[^o29]

Piezoelectric materials also show the opposite effect, called the **converse piezoelectric effect**, where the application of an electrical field creates mechanical deformation in the crystal.

### Mathematical description {#Mathematical_description}

Linear piezoelectricity is the combined effect of

- The linear electrical behavior of the material:

$\mathbf{D} = \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{E} \quad \implies$ [$\quad D_i = \sum_j \varepsilon_{ij}\,E_j \;$](wikipedia://en/246160)

where **D** is the electric flux density[^o31][^o32] ([electric displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_displacement)), **ε** is the [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) (free-body dielectric constant), **E** is the [electric field strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field_strength), and [$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 0$](wikipedia://en/8328) , [$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = \mathbf{0}$](wikipedia://en/6123).

- [Hooke's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law) for linear elastic materials:

$$\boldsymbol{S}=\mathsf{s}\,\boldsymbol{T} \quad \implies \quad S_{ij} = \sum_{k,\ell} s_{ijk\ell} \,T_{k\ell} \;$$

where **S** is the linearized [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_%28materials_science%29), **s** is [compliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compliance_%28mechanics%29) under short-circuit conditions, **T** is [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28physics%29), and

$$\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{T} = \mathbf{0} \,\,,\, \boldsymbol{S} = \frac{\nabla \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{u} \nabla}{2},$$

where **u** is the *displacement vector*.

These may be combined into so-called *coupled equations*, of which the **strain-charge form** is:[^o33]

$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{S} &= \mathsf{s}\,\boldsymbol{T} + \mathfrak{d}^t\,\mathbf{E}\ && \implies \quad S_{ij} = \sum_{k,\ell} s_{ijk\ell} \,T_{k\ell} + \sum_k d^t_{ijk} \,E_k, \\[6pt] \mathbf{D} &= \mathfrak{d}\,\boldsymbol{T} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\,\mathbf{E} && \implies \quad D_i = \sum_{j, k} d_{ijk} \,T_{jk} + \sum_j \varepsilon_{ij}\,E_j, \end{aligned}$$

where $\mathfrak{d}$ is the piezoelectric tensor and the superscript t stands for its transpose. Due to the symmetry of $\mathfrak{d}$, $d^t_{ijk}=d_{kji}=d_{kij}$.

In matrix form,

$$\begin{aligned} \{S\} &= \left [s^E \right ]\{T\}+[d^\mathrm{t}]\{E\}, \\[6pt] \{D\} &= [d]\{T\}+\left [ \varepsilon^T \right ] \{E\}, \end{aligned}$$

where \[*d*\] is the matrix for the direct piezoelectric effect and \[*d*${}^{t}$\] is the matrix for the converse piezoelectric effect. The superscript *E* indicates a zero, or constant, electric field; the superscript *T* indicates a zero, or constant, stress field; and the superscript t stands for [transposition](wikipedia://en/173844) of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859).

Notice that the third order tensor $\mathfrak{d}$ maps vectors into symmetric matrices. There are no non-trivial rotation-invariant tensors that have this property, which is why there are no isotropic piezoelectric materials.

The strain-charge for a material of the [4mm](wikipedia://en/198204) (C<sub>4v</sub>) [crystal class](wikipedia://en/456410) (such as a poled piezoelectric ceramic such as tetragonal PZT or BaTiO<sub>3</sub>) as well as the [6mm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_crystal_system#Hexagonal_crystal_system) crystal class may also be written as (ANSI IEEE 176):

$$\begin{aligned} & \begin{bmatrix} S_1 \\ S_2 \\ S_3 \\ S_4 \\ S_5 \\ S_6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} s_{11}^E & s_{12}^E & s_{13}^E & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ s_{21}^E & s_{22}^E & s_{23}^E & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ s_{31}^E & s_{32}^E & s_{33}^E & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & s_{44}^E & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & s_{55}^E & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & s_{66}^E=2\left(s_{11}^E-s_{12}^E\right) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} T_1 \\ T_2 \\ T_3 \\ T_4 \\ T_5 \\ T_6 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & d_{31} \\ 0 & 0 & d_{32} \\ 0 & 0 & d_{33} \\ 0 & d_{24} & 0 \\ d_{15} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} E_1 \\ E_2 \\ E_3 \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt] & \begin{bmatrix} D_1 \\ D_2 \\ D_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & d_{15} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & d_{24} & 0 & 0 \\ d_{31} & d_{32} & d_{33} & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} T_1 \\ T_2 \\ T_3 \\ T_4 \\ T_5 \\ T_6 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} {\varepsilon}_{11} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & {\varepsilon}_{22} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & {\varepsilon}_{33} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} E_1 \\ E_2 \\ E_3 \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

where the first equation represents the relationship for the converse piezoelectric effect and the latter for the direct piezoelectric effect.[^o34]

Although the above equations are the most used form in literature, some comments about the notation are necessary. Generally, *D* and *E* are [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29), that is, [Cartesian tensors](wikipedia://en/1824845) of rank 1; and permittivity *ε* is a Cartesian tensor of rank 2. Strain and stress are, in principle, also rank-2 [tensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensors). But conventionally, because strain and stress are all symmetric tensors, the subscript of strain and stress can be relabeled in the following fashion: 11 → 1; 22 → 2; 33 → 3; 23 → 4; 13 → 5; 12 → 6. (Different conventions may be used by different authors in literature. For example, some use 12 → 4; 23 → 5; 31 → 6 instead.) That is why *S* and *T* appear to have the "vector form" of six components. Consequently, *s* appears to be a 6-by-6 matrix instead of a rank-3 tensor. Such a relabeled notation is often called [Voigt notation](wikipedia://en/1436668). Whether the shear strain components *S*<sub>4</sub>, *S*<sub>5</sub>, *S*<sub>6</sub> are tensor components or engineering strains is another question. In the equation above, they must be engineering strains for the 6,6 coefficient of the compliance matrix to be written as shown, i.e., 2(*s*${}^{E}_{11}$ − *s*${}^{E}_{12}$). Engineering shear strains are double the value of the corresponding tensor shear, such as *S*<sub>6</sub> = 2*S*<sub>12</sub> and so on. This also means that *s*<sub>66</sub> = $\tfrac{1}{G_{12}}$, where *G*<sub>12</sub> is the [shear modulus](wikipedia://en/1575447).

In total, there are four piezoelectric coefficients, *d<sub>ij</sub>*, *e<sub>ij</sub>*, *g<sub>ij</sub>*, and *h<sub>ij</sub>* defined as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} d_{ij} &= \phantom{+} \left ( \frac{\partial D_i}{\partial T_j} \right )^E &&= \phantom{+} \left ( \frac{\partial S_j}{\partial E_i} \right )^T \\[6pt] e_{ij} &= \phantom{+} \left ( \frac{\partial D_i}{\partial S_j} \right )^E &&= -\left ( \frac{\partial T_j}{\partial E_i} \right )^S \\[6pt] g_{ij} &= -\left ( \frac{\partial E_i}{\partial T_j} \right )^D &&= \phantom{+} \left ( \frac{\partial S_j}{\partial D_i} \right )^T \\[6pt] h_{ij} &= -\left ( \frac{\partial E_i}{\partial S_j} \right )^D &&= -\left ( \frac{\partial T_j}{\partial D_i} \right )^S \end{aligned}$$

where the first set of four terms corresponds to the direct piezoelectric effect and the second set of four terms corresponds to the converse piezoelectric effect. The equality between the direct piezoelectric tensor and the transpose of the converse piezoelectric tensor originates from the [Maxwell relations](wikipedia://en/921525) of thermodynamics.[^o35] For those piezoelectric crystals for which the polarization is of the crystal-field induced type, a formalism has been worked out that allows for the calculation of piezoelectrical coefficients *d<sub>ij</sub>* from electrostatic lattice constants or higher-order [Madelung constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madelung_constant).[^o27]

## Crystal classes {#Crystal_classes}

*Image: Capacitor schematic with dielectric.svg.* Any spatially separated charge will result in an [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092), and therefore an [electric potential](wikipedia://en/59615). Shown here is a standard dielectric in a [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor). In a piezoelectric device, mechanical stress, instead of an externally applied voltage, causes the charge separation in the individual atoms of the material.

Of the 32 [crystal classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_class), 21 are non-[centrosymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrosymmetric) (not having a centre of symmetry), and of these, 20 exhibit direct piezoelectricity[^o37] (the 21st is the cubic class 432). Ten of these represent the polar crystal classes,[^o38] which show a spontaneous polarization without mechanical stress due to a non-vanishing electric dipole moment associated with their unit cell, and which exhibit [pyroelectricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroelectricity). If the dipole moment can be reversed by applying an external electric field, the material is said to be [ferroelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferroelectric).

- The 10 polar (pyroelectric) crystal classes: 1, 2, m, mm2, 4, 4mm, 3, 3m, 6, 6mm.
- The other 10 piezoelectric crystal classes: 222, $\overline{4}$, 422, $\overline{4}$2m, 32, $\overline{6}$, 622, $\overline{6}$2m, 23, $\overline{4}$3m.

For polar crystals, for which ***P*** ≠ 0 holds without applying a mechanical load, the piezoelectric effect manifests itself by changing the magnitude or the direction of ***P*** or both.

For the nonpolar but piezoelectric crystals, on the other hand, a polarization ***P*** different from zero is only elicited by applying a mechanical load. For them the stress can be imagined to transform the material from a nonpolar crystal class (***P*** = 0) to a polar one,[^o27] having ***P*** ≠ 0.

## Materials {#Materials}

Many materials exhibit piezoelectricity. Examples include:

### Crystalline materials {#Crystalline_materials}

- [Langasite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langasite) (La<sub>3</sub>Ga<sub>5</sub>SiO<sub>14</sub>) – a quartz-analogous crystal
- [Gallium orthophosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallium_orthophosphate) (GaPO<sub>4</sub>) – a quartz-analogous crystal
- [Lithium niobate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_niobate) (LiNbO<sub>3</sub>)
- [Lithium tantalate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_tantalate) (LiTaO<sub>3</sub>)
- [Quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz)
- [Berlinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlinite) (AlPO<sub>4</sub>) – a rare [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate) [mineral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral) that is structurally identical to quartz
- [Rochelle salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_sodium_tartrate)
- [Topaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topaz) – piezoelectricity in topaz can probably be attributed to ordering of the (F,OH) in its lattice, which is otherwise centrosymmetric: orthorhombic bipyramidal (mmm). Topaz has anomalous optical properties, which are attributed to such ordering.[^o41]
- [Tourmaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourmaline)-group minerals
- [Lead titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_titanate) (PbTiO<sub>3</sub>) – although it occurs in nature as mineral macedonite,[^o42][^o43] it is synthesized for research and applications.

### Ceramics {#Ceramics}

*Image: Perovskite.svg.* Tetragonal unit cell of lead titanate

Ceramics with randomly oriented grains must be ferroelectric to exhibit piezoelectricity.[^o45] The occurrence of [abnormal grain growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abnormal_grain_growth) (AGG) in sintered polycrystalline piezoelectric ceramics has detrimental effects on the piezoelectric performance in such systems and should be avoided, as the microstructure in piezoceramics exhibiting AGG tends to consist of few abnormally large elongated grains in a matrix of randomly oriented finer grains. Macroscopic piezoelectricity is possible in textured polycrystalline non-ferroelectric piezoelectric materials, such as AlN and ZnO. The families of ceramics with [perovskite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perovskite_%28structure%29), [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten)-[bronze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze), and related structures exhibit piezoelectricity:

- [Lead zirconate titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_zirconate_titanate) (Pb\[Zr\_{*x*}Ti\_{1−*x*}\]O3 with 0 ≤ *x* ≤ 1) – more commonly known as PZT, the most common piezoelectric ceramic in use today.
- [Potassium niobate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_niobate) (KNbO<sub>3</sub>)[^o46]
- [Sodium tungstate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_tungstate) (Na<sub>2</sub>WO<sub>3</sub>)
- Ba<sub>2</sub>NaNb<sub>5</sub>O<sub>5</sub>
- Pb<sub>2</sub>KNb<sub>5</sub>O<sub>15</sub>
- [Zinc oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc_oxide) (ZnO) – [Wurtzite structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurtzite_crystal_structure). While single crystals of ZnO are piezoelectric and pyroelectric, polycrystalline (ceramic) ZnO with randomly oriented grains exhibits neither piezoelectric nor pyroelectric effect. Not being ferroelectric, polycrystalline ZnO cannot be poled like barium titanate or PZT. Ceramics and polycrystalline thin films of ZnO may exhibit macroscopic piezoelectricity and pyroelectricity only if they are [textured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texture_%28crystalline%29) (grains are preferentially oriented), such that the piezoelectric and pyroelectric responses of all individual grains do not cancel. This is readily accomplished in polycrystalline thin films.[^o34]

### Lead-free piezoceramics {#Lead-free_piezoceramics}

- [Sodium potassium niobate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_potassium_niobate) ((K,Na)NbO<sub>3</sub>). This material is also known as NKN or KNN. In 2004, a group of Japanese researchers led by Yasuyoshi Saito discovered a sodium potassium niobate composition with properties close to those of PZT, including a high *T*<sub>C</sub>.[^o48] Certain compositions of this material have been shown to retain a high mechanical quality factor (*Q*<sub>m</sub> ≈ 900) with increasing vibration levels, whereas the mechanical quality factor of hard PZT degrades in such conditions. This fact makes NKN a promising replacement for high power resonance applications, such as piezoelectric transformers.[^o49]
- [Bismuth ferrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth_ferrite) (BiFeO<sub>3</sub>) – a promising candidate for the replacement of lead-based ceramics.
- Sodium niobate (NaNbO<sub>3</sub>)
- [Barium titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_titanate) (BaTiO<sub>3</sub>) – Barium titanate was the first piezoelectric ceramic discovered.
- [Bismuth titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bismuth_titanate) (Bi<sub>4</sub>Ti<sub>3</sub>O<sub>12</sub>)
- [Sodium bismuth titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_bismuth_titanate) (NaBi(TiO<sub>3</sub>)<sub>2</sub>)

The fabrication of lead-free piezoceramics pose multiple challenges, from an environmental standpoint and their ability to replicate the properties of their lead-based counterparts. By removing the lead component of the piezoceramic, the risk of toxicity to humans decreases, but the mining and extraction of the materials can be harmful to the environment.[^o50] Analysis of the environmental profile of PZT versus sodium potassium niobate (NKN or KNN) shows that across the four indicators considered (primary energy consumption, toxicological footprint, eco-indicator 99, and input-output upstream greenhouse gas emissions), KNN is actually more harmful to the environment. Most of the concerns with KNN, specifically its Nb<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> component, are in the early phase of its life cycle before it reaches manufacturers. Since the harmful impacts are focused on these early phases, some actions can be taken to minimize the effects. Returning the land as close to its original form after Nb<sub>2</sub>O<sub>5</sub> mining via dam deconstruction or replacing a stockpile of utilizable soil are known aids for any extraction event. For minimizing air quality effects, modeling and simulation still needs to occur to fully understand what mitigation methods are required. The extraction of lead-free piezoceramic components has not grown to a significant scale at this time, but from early analysis, experts encourage caution when it comes to environmental effects.

Fabricating lead-free piezoceramics faces the challenge of maintaining the performance and stability of their lead-based counterparts. In general, the main fabrication challenge is creating the "[morphotropic phase boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphotropic_phase_boundary) (MPBs)" that provide the materials with their stable piezoelectric properties without introducing the "polymorphic phase boundaries (PPBs)" that decrease the temperature stability of the material.[^o51] New phase boundaries are created by varying additive concentrations so that the [phase transition](wikipedia://en/54423) temperatures converge at room temperature. The introduction of the MPB improves piezoelectric properties, but if a PPB is introduced, the material becomes negatively affected by temperature. Research is ongoing to control the type of phase boundaries that are introduced through phase engineering, diffusing phase transitions, domain engineering, and chemical modification.

### III–V and II–VI semiconductors {#III–V_and_II–VI_semiconductors}

A piezoelectric potential can be created in any bulk or nanostructured semiconductor crystal having non central symmetry, such as the [Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_semiconductor_materials#Types_of_semiconductor_materials) [III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_group)–[V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_group) and [II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_12_element)–[VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcogen) materials, due to polarization of ions under applied stress and strain. This property is common to both the [zincblende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zincblende) and [wurtzite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurtzite) crystal structures. To first order, there is only one independent piezoelectric coefficient in [zincblende](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zincblende), called e<sub>14</sub>, coupled to shear components of the strain. In [wurtzite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurtzite), there are instead three independent piezoelectric coefficients: *e*<sub>31</sub>, *e*<sub>33</sub> and *e*<sub>15</sub>. The semiconductors where the strongest piezoelectricity is observed are those commonly found in the [wurtzite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurtzite) structure, i.e. [GaN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GaN), [InN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InN), [AlN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlN) and [ZnO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZnO) (see [piezotronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezotronics)).

Since 2006, there have also been a number of reports of strong [non linear piezoelectric effects in polar semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non_linear_piezoelectric_effects_in_polar_semiconductors).[^o53] Such effects are generally recognized to be at least important if not of the same order of magnitude as the first order approximation.

### Polymers {#Polymers}

The piezo-response of [polymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer) is not as high as the response for ceramics; however, polymers hold properties that ceramics do not. Over the last few decades, non-toxic, piezoelectric polymers have been studied and applied due to their flexibility and smaller [acoustical impedance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_impedance).[^o55] Other properties that make these materials significant include their [biocompatibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biocompatibility), [biodegradability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodegradation), low cost, and low power consumption compared to other piezo-materials (ceramics, etc.).[^o56]

Piezoelectric polymers can be classified by bulk polymers, voided charged polymers ("piezoelectrets"), and polymer composites. A piezo-response observed by bulk polymers is mostly due to its molecular structure. There are two types of bulk polymers: [amorphous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorphous_solid) and [semi-crystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-crystalline_polymer). Examples of semi-crystalline polymers are [polyvinylidene fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvinylidene_fluoride) (PVDF) and its [copolymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copolymer), [polyamides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyamide), and [parylene-C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parylene). Non-crystalline polymers, such as [polyimide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyimide) and [polyvinylidene chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvinylidene_chloride) (PVDC), fall under amorphous bulk polymers. Voided charged polymers exhibit the piezoelectric effect due to charge induced by poling of a porous polymeric film. Under an electric field, charges form on the surface of the voids forming dipoles. Electric responses can be caused by any deformation of these voids. The piezoelectric effect can also be observed in polymer composites by integrating piezoelectric ceramic particles into a polymer film. A polymer does not have to be piezo-active to be an effective material for a polymer composite.[^o56] In this case, a material could be made up of an inert matrix with a separate piezo-active component.

PVDF exhibits piezoelectricity several times greater than quartz. The piezo-response observed from PVDF is about 20–30 pC/N. That is an order of 5–50 times less than that of piezoelectric ceramic lead zirconate titanate (PZT).[^o55][^o56] The thermal stability of the piezoelectric effect of polymers in the PVDF family (i.e. vinylidene fluoride co-poly trifluoroethylene) goes up to 125 °C. Some applications of PVDF are pressure sensors, hydrophones, and shock wave sensors.[^o55]

Due to their flexibility, piezoelectric composites have been proposed as energy harvesters and nanogenerators. In 2018, it was reported by Zhu et al. that a piezoelectric response of about 17 pC/N could be obtained from PDMS/PZT nanocomposite at 60% porosity.[^o57] Another PDMS nanocomposite was reported in 2017, in which BaTiO<sub>3</sub> was integrated into PDMS to make a stretchable, transparent nanogenerator for self-powered physiological monitoring.[^o58] In 2016, polar molecules were introduced into a polyurethane foam in which high responses of up to 244 pC/N were reported.[^o59]

### Other materials {#Other_materials}

Most materials exhibit at least weak piezoelectric responses. Trivial examples include [sucrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucrose) (table sugar), [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), and viral proteins, including those from [bacteriophage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage).[^o61][^o62] An actuator based on wood fibers ([cellulose fibers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose_fiber)) has been reported.[^o56] D<sub>33</sub> responses for cellular [polypropylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polypropylene) are around 200 pC/N. Some applications of cellular polypropylene are musical key pads, microphones, and ultrasound-based echolocation systems.[^o55] Recently, a single amino acid, β-glycine, displayed a high piezoelectric response of 178 pm V<sup>−1</sup> compared with other biological materials.[^o63]

[Ionic liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_liquid) were recently identified as the first piezoelectric liquid.[^o64]

## Application {#Application}

### High voltage and power sources {#High_voltage_and_power_sources}

Direct piezoelectricity of some substances, like quartz, can generate [potential differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_difference) of thousands of volts.

*Image: Butane lighter piezo (3).jpg.* Butane lighter piezo

- The best-known application is the electric [cigarette lighter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighter): pressing the button causes a spring-loaded hammer to hit a piezoelectric crystal, producing a sufficiently high-voltage [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) that flows across a small [spark gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark_gap), thus heating and igniting the gas. The portable sparkers used to ignite [gas stoves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_stove) work the same way, and many types of gas burners now have built-in piezo-based ignition systems.
- A similar idea is being researched by [DARPA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DARPA) in the United States in a project called [energy harvesting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_harvesting), which includes an attempt to power battlefield equipment by piezoelectric generators embedded in [soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldier)' boots. However, these energy harvesting sources by association affect the body. DARPA's effort to harness 1–2 watts from continuous shoe impact while walking were abandoned due to the impracticality and the discomfort from the additional energy expended by a person wearing the shoes. Other energy harvesting ideas include [Crowd Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowd_Farm), harvesting the energy from human movements in train stations or other public places[^o68][^o69] and converting a dance floor to generate electricity.[^o70] Vibrations from industrial machinery can also be harvested by piezoelectric materials to charge batteries for backup supplies or to power low-power microprocessors and wireless radios.[^o71][^o72]
- A piezoelectric [transformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer) is a type of AC voltage multiplier. Unlike a conventional transformer, which uses magnetic coupling between input and output, the piezoelectric transformer uses [acoustic coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_coupling). An input voltage is applied across a short length of a bar of piezoceramic material such as [PZT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_zirconate_titanate), creating an alternating stress in the bar by the inverse piezoelectric effect and causing the whole bar to vibrate. The vibration frequency is chosen to be the [resonant](wikipedia://en/41660) frequency of the block, typically in the 100 [kilohertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilohertz) to 1 megahertz range. A higher output voltage is then generated across another section of the bar by the piezoelectric effect. Step-up ratios of more than 1,000:1 have been demonstrated. An extra feature of this transformer is that, by operating it above its resonant frequency, it can be made to appear as an [inductive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor) load, which is useful in circuits that require a controlled soft start.[^o73] These devices can be used in DC–AC inverters to drive [cold cathode fluorescent lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_cathode_fluorescent_lamp). Piezo transformers are some of the most compact high voltage sources.

### Sensors {#Sensors}

*Image: Piezoelectric pickup1.jpg.* Piezoelectric disk used as a [guitar pickup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar_pickup)

*Image: RPG-7 detached.jpg.* Many rocket-propelled grenades used a piezoelectric fuse. Pictured, a Russian [RPG-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RPG-7)[^o75]

The principle of operation of a piezoelectric [sensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor) is that a physical dimension, transformed into a force, acts on two opposing faces of the sensing element. Depending on the design of a sensor, different "modes" to load the piezoelectric element can be used: longitudinal, transversal and shear.

Detection of pressure variations in the form of sound is the most common sensor application, e.g. piezoelectric [microphones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microphone) (sound waves bend the piezoelectric material, creating a changing voltage) and piezoelectric [pickups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pick_up_%28music_technology%29) for [acoustic-electric guitars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic-electric_guitar). A piezo sensor attached to the body of an instrument is known as a [contact microphone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_microphone).

Piezoelectric sensors especially are used with high frequency sound in ultrasonic transducers for medical imaging and also industrial [nondestructive testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondestructive_testing) (NDT).

For many sensing techniques, the sensor can act as both a sensor and an actuator—often the term *transducer* is preferred when the device acts in this dual capacity, but most piezo devices have this property of reversibility whether it is used or not. Ultrasonic transducers, for example, can inject ultrasound waves into the body, receive the returned wave, and convert it to an electrical signal (a voltage). Most medical ultrasound transducers are piezoelectric.

In addition to those mentioned above, various sensor and transducer applications include:

- Piezoelectric elements are also used in the detection and generation of sonar waves.
- Piezoelectric materials are used in single-axis and dual-axis tilt sensing.[^o76]
- Power monitoring in high power applications (e.g. medical treatment, [sonochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonochemistry) and industrial processing).
- [Piezoelectric microbalances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezoelectric_microbalance) are used as very sensitive chemical and biological sensors.
- Piezoelectrics are sometimes used in [strain gauges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_gauge). More commonly however, a [Piezoresistive effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezoresistive_effect) element is used.
- A piezoelectric transducer was used in the penetrometer instrument on the [Huygens Probe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens_Probe).
- Piezoelectric [transducers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transducer) are used in [electronic drum pads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_drum) to detect the impact of the drummer's sticks, and to detect muscle movements in medical [acceleromyography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleromyography).
- Automotive [engine management systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engine_Control_Unit) use piezoelectric transducers to detect Engine knock (Knock Sensor, KS), also known as detonation, at certain hertz frequencies. A piezoelectric transducer is also used in fuel injection systems to measure manifold absolute pressure (MAP sensor) to determine engine load, and ultimately the fuel injectors milliseconds of on time.
- Ultrasonic piezo sensors are used in the detection of acoustic emissions in [acoustic emission testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustic_emission_testing).
- Piezoelectric transducers can be used in transit-time [ultrasonic flow meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasonic_flow_meters).

### Actuators {#Actuators}

*Image: 2007-07-24 Piezoelectric buzzer.jpg.* Metal disk with piezoelectric disk attached, used in a [buzzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buzzer)

As very high electric fields correspond to only tiny changes in the width of the crystal, this width can be changed with better-than-[μm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre) precision, making piezo crystals the most important tool for positioning objects with extreme accuracy—thus their use in [actuators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuators).[^o78] Multilayer ceramics, using layers thinner than 100 μm, allow reaching high electric fields with voltage lower than 150 V. These ceramics are used within two kinds of actuators: direct piezo actuators and [amplified piezoelectric actuators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplified_piezoelectric_actuator). While direct actuator's stroke is generally lower than 100 μm, amplified piezo actuators can reach millimeter strokes.

- [Loudspeakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezoelectric_loudspeaker): Voltage is converted to mechanical movement of a metallic diaphragm.
- [Ultrasonic cleaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasonic_cleaning) usually uses piezoelectric elements to produce intense sound waves in liquid.
- [Piezoelectric motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezoelectric_motor): Piezoelectric elements apply a directional force to an [axle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axle), causing it to rotate. Due to the extremely small distances involved, the piezo motor is viewed as a high-precision replacement for the [stepper motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepper_motor).
- Piezoelectric elements can be used in [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser) mirror alignment, where their ability to move a large mass (the mirror mount) over microscopic distances is exploited to electronically align some laser mirrors. By precisely controlling the distance between mirrors, the laser electronics can accurately maintain optical conditions inside the laser cavity to optimize the beam output.
- A related application is the [acousto-optic modulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acousto-optic_modulator), a device that scatters light off soundwaves in a crystal, generated by piezoelectric elements. This is useful for fine-tuning a laser's frequency.
- [Atomic force microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_force_microscope) and [scanning tunneling microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_tunneling_microscope) employ converse piezoelectricity to keep the sensing needle close to the specimen.[^o79]
- [Inkjet printers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inkjet_printer): On many inkjet printers, piezoelectric crystals are used to drive the ejection of ink from the inkjet print head towards the paper.
- [Diesel engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diesel_engine): High-performance [common rail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_rail) diesel engines use piezoelectric [fuel injectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuel_injector), first developed by [Robert Bosch GmbH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bosch_GmbH), instead of the more common [solenoid valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solenoid_valve) devices.
- Active vibration control using amplified actuators.
- [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) shutters.
- XY stages for micro scanning used in infrared cameras.
- Moving the patient precisely inside active [CT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_computed_tomography) and [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging) scanners where the strong radiation or magnetism precludes electric motors.[^o80]
- [Crystal earpieces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_earpiece) are sometimes used in old or low power radios.
- [High-intensity focused ultrasound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-intensity_focused_ultrasound) for localized heating or creating a localized [cavitation](wikipedia://en/7807) can be achieved, for example, in patient's body or in an industrial chemical process.
- [Refreshable braille display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refreshable_braille_display). A small crystal is expanded by applying a current that moves a lever to raise individual braille cells.
- Piezoelectric actuator. A single crystal or a number of crystals are expanded by applying a voltage for moving and controlling a mechanism or system.[^o78]
- Piezoelectric actuators are used for fine servo positioning in hard disc drives.[^o81][^o82]

### Frequency standard {#Frequency_standard}

The piezoelectrical properties of quartz are useful as a [standard of frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_standard).

- [Quartz clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz_clock) employ a [crystal oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_oscillator) made from a quartz crystal that uses a combination of both direct and converse piezoelectricity to generate a regularly timed series of electrical pulses that is used to mark time. The quartz crystal (like any [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29) material) has a precisely defined [natural frequency](wikipedia://en/41660) (caused by its shape and size) at which it prefers to [oscillate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillator), and this is used to stabilize the frequency of a periodic voltage applied to the crystal.
- The same principle is used in some [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio) [transmitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmitter) and [receivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receiver_%28radio%29), and in [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) where it creates a [clock pulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock_pulse). Both of these usually use a [frequency multiplier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_multiplier) to reach gigahertz ranges.

### Piezoelectric motors {#Piezoelectric_motors}

*Image: Slip-stick actuator operation.svg.* A stick-slip actuator

Types of piezoelectric motor include:

- The [ultrasonic motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasonic_motor) used for [auto-focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto-focus) in [reflex cameras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_lens_reflex)
- [Inchworm motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inchworm_motor) for linear motion
- Rectangular four-quadrant motors with high power density (2.5 [W](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt)/cm<sup>3</sup>) and speed ranging from 10 nm/s to 800 mm/s.
- Stepping piezo motor, using [stick-slip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stick-slip) effect.

Aside from the stepping stick-slip motor, all these motors work on the same principle. Driven by dual orthogonal vibration modes with a [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) difference of 90°, the contact point between two surfaces vibrates in an [elliptical](wikipedia://en/9277) path, producing a [frictional](wikipedia://en/11062) force between the surfaces. Usually, one surface is fixed, causing the other to move. In most piezoelectric motors, the piezoelectric crystal is excited by a [sine wave](wikipedia://en/324749) signal at the resonant frequency of the motor. Using the resonance effect, a much lower voltage can be used to produce a high vibration amplitude.

A stick-slip motor works using the inertia of a mass and the friction of a clamp. Such motors can be very small. Some are used for camera sensor displacement, thus allowing an anti-shake function.

### Reduction of vibrations and noise {#Reduction_of_vibrations_and_noise}

Different teams of researchers have been investigating ways to reduce vibrations in materials by attaching piezo elements to the material. When the material is bent by a vibration in one direction, the vibration-reduction system responds to the bend and sends electric power to the piezo element to bend in the other direction. Applications to flexible structures, such as shells and plates, have also been studied for nearly three decades.[^o86]

### Surgery {#Surgery}

[Piezosurgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piezosurgery)[^o88] is a minimally invasive technique that aims to cut a target tissue with little damage to neighboring tissues. For example, Hoigne *et al.*[^o89] uses frequencies in the range 25–29 kHz, causing microvibrations of 60–210 μm. It has the ability to cut mineralized tissue without cutting neurovascular tissue and other soft tissue, thereby maintaining a blood-free operating area, better visibility and greater precision.[^o90]

### Piezoelectric metamaterials with electro-momentum couplings {#Piezoelectric_metamaterials_with_electro-momentum_couplings}

In 2019, Pernas-Salomón and Shmuel developed a dynamic homogenization method by which they were the first to show that piezoelectric composites exhibit an effective coupling between linear momentum and the electric field, which they termed electro-momentum coupling.[^o92] Because homogeneous piezoelectric materials do not exhibit this coupling, such composites belong to the class of [metamaterials](wikipedia://en/906878)—artificial media engineered to display extraordinary effective properties, in magnitude or in kind.[^o93] Electro-momentum coupling is analogous to Willis coupling in elastic composites, which couples linear momentum to strain and was discovered by J. R. Willis.[^o94][^o95][^o96] The local part of these couplings—like piezoelectric coupling—emerges from broken symmetries.[^o97][^o98] Piezoelectric metamaterials with electro-momentum coupling offer a mechanism for wave manipulation akin to Willis coupling: they impart a direction-dependent phase shift that enables wavefront shaping, with the added advantage of electrical tunability.[^o97]
