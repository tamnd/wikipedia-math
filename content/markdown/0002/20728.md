# Mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics"}

The **mathematical formulations of quantum mechanics** are those mathematical formalisms that permit a [rigorous](wikipedia://en/655974#Mathematics) description of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). This mathematical formalism uses mainly a part of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), especially [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), which are a kind of [linear space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_space). Such are distinguished from mathematical formalisms for physics theories developed prior to the early 1900s by the use of abstract mathematical structures, such as infinite-dimensional [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) ([*L*<sup>2</sup> space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L2_space) mainly), and [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on these spaces. In brief, values of physical [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) such as [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) and [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) were no longer considered as values of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), but as [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue); more precisely as [spectral values](wikipedia://en/206137) of linear [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29) in Hilbert space.[^n1]

:::

These formulations of quantum mechanics continue to be used today. At the heart of the description are ideas of *[quantum state](wikipedia://en/30876419)* and *quantum observables*, which are radically different from those used in previous [models](wikipedia://en/20590) of physical reality. While the mathematics permits calculation of many quantities that can be measured experimentally, there is a definite theoretical limit to values that can be simultaneously measured. This limitation was first elucidated by [Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisenberg_uncertainty_relations) through a [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535), and is represented mathematically in the new formalism by the [non-commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative) of operators representing quantum observables.

Prior to the development of quantum mechanics as a separate [theory](wikipedia://en/30746), the mathematics used in physics consisted mainly of formal [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), beginning with [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), and increasing in complexity up to [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564). [Probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) was used in [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481). Geometric intuition played a strong role in the first two and, accordingly, [theories of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_physics) were formulated entirely in terms of differential geometric concepts. The phenomenology of quantum physics arose roughly between 1895 and 1915, and for the 10 to 15 years before the development of quantum mechanics (around 1925) physicists continued to think of quantum theory within the confines of what is now called [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics), and in particular within the same mathematical structures. The most sophisticated example of this is the [Sommerfeld–Wilson–Ishiwara quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sommerfeld–Wilson–Ishiwara_quantization) rule, which was formulated entirely on the classical [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).

## History of the formalism {#History_of_the_formalism}

### The "old quantum theory" and the need for new mathematics {#The_"old_quantum_theory"_and_the_need_for_new_mathematics}

In the 1890s, [Planck](wikipedia://en/19848) was able to derive the [blackbody spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbody_spectrum), which was later used to avoid the classical [ultraviolet catastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet_catastrophe) by making the unorthodox assumption that, in the interaction of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426) with [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter), energy could only be exchanged in discrete units which he called [quanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum).[^o5] Planck postulated a direct proportionality between the frequency of radiation and the quantum of energy at that frequency. The proportionality constant, $h$, is now called the [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant) in his honor.

In 1905, [Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) explained certain features of the [photoelectric effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectric_effect) by assuming that Planck's energy quanta were actual particles, which were later dubbed [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photons).

*Image: Bohr atom model (mul).svg.* light at the right frequency

All of these developments were [phenomenological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_%28particle_physics%29) and challenged the theoretical physics of the time. [Bohr and Sommerfeld](wikipedia://en/1188104) went on to modify [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) in an attempt to deduce the [Bohr model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_model) from first principles. They proposed that, of all closed classical orbits traced by a mechanical system in its phase space, only the ones that enclosed an area which was a multiple of the Planck constant were actually allowed. The most sophisticated version of this formalism was the so-called [Sommerfeld–Wilson–Ishiwara quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sommerfeld–Wilson–Ishiwara_quantization). Although the Bohr model of the hydrogen atom could be explained in this way, the spectrum of the helium atom (classically an unsolvable [3-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-body_problem)) could not be predicted. The mathematical status of quantum theory remained uncertain for some time.

In 1923, [de Broglie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_de_Broglie) proposed that [wave–particle duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave–particle_duality) applied not only to photons but to electrons and every other physical system.

The situation changed rapidly in the years 1925–1930, when working mathematical foundations were found through the groundbreaking work of [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schrödinger), [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg), [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born), [Pascual Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascual_Jordan), and the foundational work of [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) and [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742), and it became possible to unify several different approaches in terms of a fresh set of ideas. The physical interpretation of the theory was also clarified in these years after [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg) discovered the uncertainty relations and [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr) introduced the idea of [complementarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_%28physics%29).

### The "new quantum theory" {#The_"new_quantum_theory"}

Werner Heisenberg's [matrix mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_mechanics) was the first successful attempt at replicating the observed quantization of [atomic spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_spectra). Later in the same year, Schrödinger created his [wave mechanics](wikipedia://en/59874). Schrödinger's formalism was considered easier to understand, visualize and calculate as it led to [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations), which physicists were already familiar with solving. Within a year, it was shown that the two theories were equivalent.

Schrödinger himself initially did not understand the fundamental probabilistic nature of quantum mechanics, as he thought that the [absolute square](wikipedia://en/991#Complex_numbers) of the wave function of an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) should be interpreted as the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) of an object smeared out over an extended, possibly infinite, [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of space. It was [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born) who introduced the interpretation of the [absolute square](wikipedia://en/991#Complex_numbers) of the wave function as the probability distribution of the position of a [*pointlike* object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_particle). Born's idea was soon taken over by Niels Bohr in Copenhagen who then became the "father" of the [Copenhagen interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_interpretation) of quantum mechanics. Schrödinger's [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) can be seen to be closely related to the classical [Hamilton–Jacobi equation](wikipedia://en/897539). The correspondence to classical mechanics was even more explicit, although somewhat more formal, in Heisenberg's matrix mechanics. In his PhD thesis project, [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742)[^n2] discovered that the equation for the operators in the [Heisenberg representation](wikipedia://en/306706), as it is now called, closely translates to classical equations for the dynamics of certain quantities in the Hamiltonian formalism of classical mechanics, when one expresses them through [Poisson brackets](wikipedia://en/294340), a procedure now known as [canonical quantization](wikipedia://en/869590).

Already before Schrödinger, the young postdoctoral fellow Werner Heisenberg invented his [matrix mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_mechanics), which was the first correct quantum mechanics – the essential breakthrough. Heisenberg's matrix mechanics formulation was based on algebras of infinite matrices, a very radical formulation in light of the mathematics of classical physics, although he started from the index-terminology of the experimentalists of that time, not even aware that his "index-schemes" were matrices, as Born soon pointed out to him. In fact, in these early years, [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) was not generally popular with physicists in its present form.

Although Schrödinger himself after a year proved the equivalence of his wave-mechanics and Heisenberg's matrix mechanics, the reconciliation of the two approaches and their modern abstraction as motions in Hilbert space is generally attributed to Paul Dirac, who wrote a lucid account in his 1930 classic *[The Principles of Quantum Mechanics](wikipedia://en/32975011)*. He is the third, and possibly most important, pillar of that field (he soon was the only one to have discovered a relativistic generalization of the theory). In his above-mentioned account, he introduced the [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542), together with an abstract formulation in terms of the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) used in functional analysis; he showed that Schrödinger's and Heisenberg's approaches were two different representations of the same theory, and found a third, most general one, which represented the dynamics of the system. His work was particularly fruitful in many types of generalizations of the field.

The first complete mathematical formulation of this approach, known as the [Dirac–von Neumann axioms](wikipedia://en/39062473), is generally credited to [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942)'s 1932 book *[Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics](wikipedia://en/45154254)*, although [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) had already referred to Hilbert spaces (which he called *unitary spaces*) in his 1927 classic paper and [1928 book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gruppentheorie_und_Quantenmechanik). It was developed in parallel with a new approach to the mathematical [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) based on linear operators rather than the [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) that were [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s approach a generation earlier. Though theories of quantum mechanics continue to evolve to this day, there is a basic framework for the mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics which underlies most approaches and can be traced back to the mathematical work of John von Neumann. In other words, discussions about [*interpretation* of the theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretation_of_quantum_mechanics), and extensions to it, are now mostly conducted on the basis of shared assumptions about the mathematical foundations.

### Later developments {#Later_developments}

The application of the new quantum theory to electromagnetism resulted in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), which was developed starting around 1930. Quantum field theory has driven the development of more sophisticated formulations of quantum mechanics, of which the ones presented here are simple special cases.

- [Path integral formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation)
- [Phase-space formulation](wikipedia://en/36053570) of quantum mechanics & [geometric quantization](wikipedia://en/1146267)
- [quantum field theory in curved spacetime](wikipedia://en/1057601)
- [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/293622), [algebraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_quantum_physics) and [constructive quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/1231204)
- [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184) [formalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formalism_%28mathematics%29)
- [Generalized statistical model of quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/3793084)

A related topic is the relationship to classical mechanics. Any new physical theory is supposed to reduce to successful old theories in some approximation. For quantum mechanics, this translates into the need to study the so-called [classical limit of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit_of_quantum_mechanics). Also, as Bohr emphasized, human cognitive abilities and language are inextricably linked to the classical realm, and so classical descriptions are intuitively more accessible than quantum ones. In particular, [quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_%28physics%29), namely the construction of a quantum theory whose classical limit is a given and known classical theory, becomes an important area of quantum physics in itself.

Finally, some of the originators of quantum theory (notably Einstein and Schrödinger) were unhappy with what they thought were the philosophical implications of quantum mechanics. In particular, Einstein took the position that quantum mechanics must be incomplete, which motivated research into so-called [hidden-variable theories](wikipedia://en/211602). The issue of hidden variables has become in part an experimental issue with the help of [quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_optics).

## Postulates of quantum mechanics {#Postulates_of_quantum_mechanics}

A physical system is generally described by three basic ingredients: [states](wikipedia://en/30876419); [observables](wikipedia://en/294248); and [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29) (or law of [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739)) or, more generally, a [group of physical symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_invariance). A classical description can be given in a fairly direct way by a phase space [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28abstract%29) of mechanics: states are points in a phase space formulated by [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356), observables are real-valued functions on it, time evolution is given by a one-parameter [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of symplectic transformations of the phase space, and physical symmetries are realized by symplectic transformations. A quantum description normally consists of a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) of states, observables are [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) on the space of states, time evolution is given by a [one-parameter group](wikipedia://en/893698) of unitary transformations on the Hilbert space of states, and physical symmetries are realized by [unitary transformations](wikipedia://en/236007). (It is possible, to map this Hilbert-space picture to a [phase space formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space_formulation), invertibly. See below.)

The following summary of the mathematical framework of quantum mechanics can be partly traced back to the [Dirac–von Neumann axioms](wikipedia://en/39062473).[^n3]

### Description of the state of a system {#Description_of_the_state_of_a_system}

Each isolated physical system is associated with a (topologically) [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$ with [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) $\langle\varphi|\psi\rangle$.

The state of an isolated physical system is represented, at a fixed time $t$, by a state vector $|\psi \rangle$ belonging to a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ called the *state space*.

[Separability](wikipedia://en/20598932#Separable_spaces) is a mathematically convenient hypothesis, with the physical interpretation that the state is uniquely determined by countably many observations. Quantum states can be identified with [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) in $H$, where two vectors (of length 1) represent the same state if they differ only by a [phase factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_factor):[^n4][^n5]

$$|\psi_k \rangle \sim |\psi_l\rangle \;\; \Leftrightarrow \;\; |\psi_k \rangle = e^{i\alpha} |\psi_l\rangle, \quad\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}.$$

As such, a quantum state is an element of a [projective Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/796760), conventionally termed a ["ray"](wikipedia://en/3570657#Rays_and_ray_space).[^n6][^n7]

Accompanying Postulate I is the composite system postulate:[^n8]

The Hilbert space of a composite system is the Hilbert space [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of the state spaces associated with the component systems. For a non-relativistic system consisting of a finite number of distinguishable particles, the component systems are the individual particles.

In the presence of [quantum entanglement](wikipedia://en/25336), the quantum state of the composite system cannot be factored as a tensor product of states of its local constituents; Instead, it is expressed as a sum, or [superposition](wikipedia://en/82728), of tensor products of states of component subsystems. A subsystem in an entangled composite system generally cannot be described by a state vector (or a ray), but instead is described by a [density operator](wikipedia://en/62844); Such quantum state is known as a [mixed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_state_%28physics%29). The [density operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_operator) of a mixed state is a [trace class](wikipedia://en/192266), nonnegative ([positive semi-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semi-definite_matrix)) [self-adjoint](wikipedia://en/187408) operator $\rho$ normalized to be of [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) 1. In turn, any [density operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_operator) of a mixed state can be represented as a subsystem of a larger composite system in a pure state (see [purification theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger–HJW_theorem)).

In the absence of quantum entanglement, the quantum state of the composite system is called a [separable state](wikipedia://en/2833933). The density matrix of a bipartite system in a separable state can be expressed as $\rho=\sum_k p_k \rho_1^k \otimes \rho_2^k$, where $\; \sum_k p_k = 1$. If there is only a single non-zero $p_k$, then the state can be expressed just as $\rho = \rho_1 \otimes \rho_2 ,$ and is called simply separable or product state.

### Measurement on a system {#Measurement_on_a_system}

#### Description of physical quantities {#Description_of_physical_quantities}

Physical observables are represented by [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) matrices on $H$. Since these operators are Hermitian, their [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) are always real, and represent the possible outcomes/results from measuring the corresponding observable. If the spectrum of the observable is [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_spectrum), then the possible results are *quantized*.

Every measurable physical quantity $\mathcal{A}$ is described by a Hermitian operator $A$ acting in the state space $\mathcal{H}$. This operator is an observable, meaning that its [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvectors) form a basis for $\mathcal{H}$. The result of measuring a physical quantity $\mathcal{A}$ must be one of the eigenvalues of the corresponding observable $A$.

#### Results of measurement {#Results_of_measurement}

By spectral theory, we can associate a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) to the values of $A$ in any state $\psi$. We can also show that the possible values of the observable $A$ in any state must belong to the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_an_operator) of $A$. The [expectation value](wikipedia://en/9653) (in the sense of probability theory) of the observable $A$ for the system in state represented by the unit vector $\psi$ ∈ *H* is $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$. If we represent the state $\psi$ in the basis formed by the eigenvectors of $A$, then the square of the modulus of the component attached to a given eigenvector is the probability of observing its corresponding eigenvalue.

When the physical quantity $\mathcal{A}$ is measured on a system in a normalized state $|\psi\rangle$, the probability of obtaining an eigenvalue (denoted $a_n$ for discrete spectra and $\alpha$ for continuous spectra) of the corresponding observable $A$ is given by the *amplitude squared* of the appropriate wave function (projection onto corresponding eigenspace).

$$\begin{alignedat}{3}\mathbb{P}(a_n)&= |\langle a_n|\psi \rangle|^2 &&\,\,\text{(Discrete, nondegenerate spectrum)} \\ \mathbb{P}(a_n) &= \sum_i^{g_n} |\langle a_n^i|\psi \rangle|^2 &&\,\, \text{(Discrete, degenerate spectrum)} \\ d\mathbb{P}(\alpha) &= |\langle \alpha | \psi \rangle |^2 d\alpha &&\,\, \text{(Continuous, nondegenerate spectrum)} \end{alignedat}$$

For a mixed state $\rho$, the expected value of $A$ in the state $\rho$ is $\operatorname{tr}(A\rho)$, and the probability of obtaining an eigenvalue $a_n$ in a discrete, nondegenerate spectrum of the corresponding observable $A$ is given by $\mathbb P(a_n)=\operatorname{tr}(|a_n\rangle\langle a_n|\rho)=\langle a_n|\rho|a_n\rangle$.

If the eigenvalue $a_n$ has [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_energy_levels), orthonormal eigenvectors $\{|a_{n1}\rangle,|a_{n2}\rangle, \dots , |a_{nm}\rangle\}$, then the [projection operator](wikipedia://en/519182) onto the eigensubspace can be defined as the identity operator in the eigensubspace:

$$P_n=|a_{n1}\rangle\langle a_{n1}|+|a_{n2}\rangle\langle a_{n2}| + \dots + |a_{nm}\rangle\langle a_{nm}|,$$

and then $\mathbb P(a_n)=\operatorname{tr}(P_n\rho)$.

Postulates II.a and II.b are collectively known as the [Born rule](wikipedia://en/4313746) of quantum mechanics.

#### Effect of measurement on the state {#Effect_of_measurement_on_the_state}

When a measurement is performed, only one result is obtained (according to some [interpretations of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics)). This is modeled mathematically as the processing of additional information from the measurement, confining the probabilities of an immediate second measurement of the same observable. In the case of a discrete, non-degenerate spectrum, two sequential measurements of the same observable will always give the same value assuming the second immediately follows the first. Therefore, the state vector must change as a result of measurement, and *collapse* onto the eigensubspace associated with the eigenvalue measured. If the measurement of the physical quantity $\mathcal{A}$ on the system in the state $|\psi\rangle$ gives the result $a_n$, then the state of the system immediately after the measurement is the normalized projection of $|\psi\rangle$ onto the eigensubspace associated with $a_n$ $|\psi\rangle \quad \overset{a_n}{\Longrightarrow}\quad \frac{P_n|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle \psi | P_n | \psi \rangle}}$

For a mixed state $\rho$, after obtaining an eigenvalue $a_n$ in a discrete, nondegenerate spectrum of the corresponding observable $A$, the updated state is given by $\rho'=\frac{P_n\rho P_n^\dagger}{\operatorname{tr}(P_n\rho P_n^\dagger)}$. If the eigenvalue $a_n$ has degenerate, orthonormal eigenvectors $\{|a_{n1}\rangle,|a_{n2}\rangle, \dots ,|a_{nm}\rangle\}$, then the [projection operator](wikipedia://en/519182) onto the eigensubspace is $P_n=|a_{n1}\rangle\langle a_{n1}|+|a_{n2}\rangle\langle a_{n2}| + \dots + |a_{nm}\rangle\langle a_{nm}|$.

Postulates II.c is sometimes called the "state update rule" or "collapse rule"; Together with the Born rule (Postulates II.a and II.b), they form a complete representation of [measurements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_in_quantum_mechanics), and are sometimes collectively called the measurement postulate(s).

Note that the [projection-valued measures](wikipedia://en/1046155) (PVM) described in the measurement postulate(s) can be generalized to [positive operator-valued measures](wikipedia://en/3793084) (POVM), which is the most general kind of measurement in quantum mechanics. A POVM can be understood as the effect on a component subsystem when a PVM is performed on a larger, composite system (see [Naimark's dilation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naimark's_dilation_theorem)).

### Time evolution of a system {#Time_evolution_of_a_system}

The Schrödinger equation describes how a state vector evolves in time. Depending on the text, it may be derived from some other assumptions, motivated on heuristic grounds, or asserted as a postulate. Derivations include using the [de Broglie relation between wavelength and momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave) or [path integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_between_Schrödinger's_equation_and_the_path_integral_formulation_of_quantum_mechanics).

The time evolution of the state vector $|\psi(t)\rangle$ is governed by the Schrödinger equation, where $H(t)$ is the observable associated with the total energy of the system (called the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381)) $i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle$

Equivalently, the time evolution postulate can be stated as:

The time evolution of a [closed system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_system#In_quantum_physics) is described by a [unitary transformation](wikipedia://en/58071309) on the initial state. $|\psi(t)\rangle = U(t;t_0)|\psi(t_0)\rangle$

For a closed system in a mixed state $\rho$, the time evolution is $\rho(t)=U(t;t_0)\rho(t_0) U^\dagger(t;t_0)$.

The evolution of an [open quantum system](wikipedia://en/1079106) can be described by [quantum operations](wikipedia://en/666107) (in an [operator sum](wikipedia://en/666107#Statement_of_the_theorem) formalism) and [quantum instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_instrument), and generally does not have to be unitary.

### Other implications of the postulates {#Other_implications_of_the_postulates}

- Physical symmetries act on the Hilbert space of quantum states [unitarily](wikipedia://en/225999) or [antiunitarily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiunitary) due to [Wigner's theorem](wikipedia://en/3570657) ([supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/224636) is another matter entirely).
- Density operators are those that are in the closure of the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the one-dimensional orthogonal projectors. Conversely, one-dimensional orthogonal projectors are [extreme points](wikipedia://en/454968) of the set of density operators. Physicists also call one-dimensional orthogonal projectors *pure states* and other density operators *mixed states*.
- One can in this formalism state Heisenberg's [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883) and [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) it as a theorem, although the exact historical sequence of events, concerning who derived what and under which framework, is the subject of historical investigations outside the scope of this article.

Furthermore, to the postulates of quantum mechanics one should also add basic statements on the properties of [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) and Pauli's [exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669), see below.

### Spin {#Spin}

In addition to their other properties, all particles possess a quantity called [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829), an *intrinsic angular momentum*. Despite the name, particles do not literally spin around an axis, and quantum mechanical spin has no correspondence in classical physics. In the position representation, a spinless wavefunction has position $\mathbf{r}$ and time $t$ as continuous variables, $\psi = \psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$. For spin wavefunctions the spin is an additional discrete variable: $\psi = \psi(\mathbf{r}, t, \sigma)$, where $\sigma$ takes the values;

$$\sigma = -S \hbar , -(S-1) \hbar , \dots, 0, \dots ,+(S-1) \hbar ,+S \hbar \,.$$

That is, the state of a single particle with spin $S$ is represented by a $(2S + 1)$-component [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) of complex-valued wave functions.

Two classes of particles with *very different* behaviour are [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) which have integer spin ($S = 0, 1, 2, ...$), and [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) possessing half-integer spin ($S = \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{5}{2}, ...$).

### Symmetrization postulate {#Symmetrization_postulate}

In quantum mechanics, two particles can be distinguished from one another using two methods. By performing a measurement of intrinsic properties of each particle, particles of different types can be distinguished. Otherwise, if the particles are identical, their trajectories can be tracked which distinguishes the particles based on the locality of each particle. While the second method is permitted in classical mechanics, (i.e. all classical particles are treated with distinguishability), the same cannot be said for quantum mechanical particles since the process is infeasible due to the fundamental uncertainty principles that govern small scales. Hence the requirement of indistinguishability of quantum particles is presented by the symmetrization postulate. The postulate is applicable to a system of bosons or fermions, for example, in predicting the spectra of [helium atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium_atom). The postulate, explained in the following sections, can be stated as follows:

The wavefunction of a system of *N* identical particles (in 3D) is either totally symmetric (bosons) or totally antisymmetric (fermions) under interchange of any pair of particles.

Exceptions can occur when the particles are constrained to two spatial dimensions where existence of particles known as [anyons](wikipedia://en/367654) are possible which are said to have a continuum of statistical properties spanning the range between fermions and bosons.[^n9] The connection between behaviour of identical particles and their spin is given by [spin statistics theorem](wikipedia://en/352827).

It can be shown that two particles localized in different regions of space can still be represented using a symmetrized/antisymmetrized wavefunction and that independent treatment of these wavefunctions gives the same result.[^n10] Hence the symmetrization postulate is applicable in the general case of a system of identical particles.

#### Exchange Degeneracy {#Exchange_Degeneracy}

In a system of identical particles, let *P* be known as exchange operator that acts on the wavefunction as:

$$P \bigg(\cdots|\psi\rangle |\phi\rangle \cdots\bigg) \equiv \cdots |\phi\rangle |\psi\rangle \cdots$$

If a physical system of identical particles is given, wavefunction of all particles can be well known from observation but these cannot be labelled to each particle. Thus, the above exchanged wavefunction represents the same physical state as the original state which implies that the wavefunction is not unique. This is known as exchange degeneracy.[^n11]

More generally, consider a linear combination of such states, $|\Psi\rangle$. For the best representation of the physical system, we expect this to be an eigenvector of *P* since exchange operator is not excepted to give completely different vectors in projective Hilbert space. Since $P^2 = 1$, the possible eigenvalues of *P* are +1 and −1. The $|\Psi\rangle$ states for identical particle system are represented as symmetric for +1 eigenvalue or antisymmetric for -1 eigenvalue as follows:

$$P|\cdots n_i,n_j \cdots; S\rangle = + |\cdots n_i,n_j \cdots; S\rangle$$

$$P|\cdots n_i, n_j \cdots; A\rangle = - |\cdots n_i, n_j \cdots; A\rangle$$

The explicit symmetric/antisymmetric form of $|\Psi\rangle$ is [constructed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identical_particles#Wavefunction_representation) using a symmetrizer or [antisymmetrizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisymmetrizer) operator. Particles that form symmetric states are called [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) and those that form antisymmetric states are called as fermions. The relation of spin with this classification is given from [spin statistics theorem](wikipedia://en/352827) which shows that integer spin particles are bosons and half integer spin particles are fermions.

::: {#o31 .statement cue=named-heading name="Pauli exclusion principle"}

#### Pauli exclusion principle {#Pauli_exclusion_principle}

The property of spin relates to another basic property concerning systems of $N$ identical particles: the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669), which is a consequence of the following permutation behaviour of an $N$-particle wave function; again in the position representation one must postulate that for the transposition of any two of the $N$ particles one always should have

::: {#o32 .equation cue=equation-box-1 name="Pauli principle"}

**Pauli principle.**

:::

i.e., on [transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28mathematics%29) of the arguments of any two particles the wavefunction should *reproduce*, apart from a prefactor $(-1)^{2S}$ which is $+1$ for bosons, but ($-1$) for [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermions). Electrons are fermions with $S = 1/2$; quanta of light are bosons with $S = 1$.

Due to the form of anti-symmetrized wavefunction:

$$\Psi^{(A)}_{n_1 \cdots n_N} (x_1, \ldots, x_N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}} \left| \begin{matrix} \psi_{n_1}(x_1) & \psi_{n_1}(x_2) & \cdots & \psi_{n_1}(x_N) \\ \psi_{n_2}(x_1) & \psi_{n_2}(x_2) & \cdots & \psi_{n_2}(x_N) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \psi_{n_N}(x_1) & \psi_{n_N}(x_2) & \cdots & \psi_{n_N}(x_N) \\ \end{matrix} \right|$$

if the wavefunction of each particle is completely determined by a set of quantum numbers, then two fermions cannot share the same set of quantum numbers since the resulting function cannot be anti-symmetrized (i.e. above formula gives zero). The same cannot be said of bosons since their wavefunction is:

$$|x_1 x_2 \cdots x_N; S \rangle = \frac{\prod_j n_j!}{N!} \sum_p \left|x_{p(1)}\right\rangle \left|x_{p(2)}\right\rangle \cdots \left|x_{p(N)}\right\rangle$$

where $n_j$ is the number of particles with same wavefunction.

:::

#### Exceptions for symmetrization postulate {#Exceptions_for_symmetrization_postulate}

In nonrelativistic quantum mechanics all particles are either bosons or [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermions); in relativistic quantum theories also ["supersymmetric"](wikipedia://en/224636) theories exist, where a particle is a linear combination of a bosonic and a fermionic part. Only in dimension $d = 2$ can one construct entities where $(-1)^{2S}$ is replaced by an arbitrary complex number with magnitude 1, called [anyons](wikipedia://en/367654). In relativistic quantum mechanics, spin statistic theorem can prove that under certain set of assumptions that the integer spins particles are classified as bosons and half spin particles are classified as [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion). Anyons which form neither symmetric nor antisymmetric states are said to have fractional spin.

Although *spin* and the *Pauli principle* can only be derived from relativistic generalizations of quantum mechanics, the properties mentioned in the last two paragraphs belong to the basic postulates already in the non-relativistic limit. Especially, many important properties in natural science, e.g. the [periodic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_system) of chemistry, are consequences of the two properties.

### Representations {#Representations}

The original form of the Schrödinger equation depends on choosing a particular representation of Heisenberg's [canonical commutation relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_commutation_relations). The [Stone–von Neumann theorem](wikipedia://en/744569) dictates that all irreducible representations of the finite-dimensional Heisenberg commutation relations are unitarily equivalent. A systematic understanding of its consequences has led to the [phase space formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space_formulation) of quantum mechanics, which works in full [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) instead of [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), so then with a more intuitive link to the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit) thereof. This picture also simplifies considerations of [quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_%28physics%29), the deformation extension from classical to quantum mechanics.

The [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719) is an exactly solvable system where the different representations are easily compared. There, apart from the Heisenberg, or Schrödinger (position or momentum), or phase-space representations, one also encounters the Fock (number) representation and the [Segal–Bargmann (Fock-space or coherent state) representation](wikipedia://en/34205013) (named after [Irving Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Segal) and [Valentine Bargmann](wikipedia://en/6850609)). All four are unitarily equivalent.

:::

### Time as an operator {#Time_as_an_operator}

The framework presented so far singles out time as *the* parameter that everything depends on. It is possible to formulate mechanics in such a way that time becomes itself an observable associated with a self-adjoint operator. At the classical level, it is possible to arbitrarily parameterize the trajectories of particles in terms of an unphysical parameter $s$, and in that case the time *t* becomes an additional generalized coordinate of the physical system. At the quantum level, translations in $s$ would be generated by a "Hamiltonian" $H - E$, where *E* is the energy operator and $H$ is the "ordinary" Hamiltonian. However, since *s* is an unphysical parameter, *physical* states must be left invariant by "*s*-evolution", and so the physical state space is the kernel of $H - E$ (this requires the use of a [rigged Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/393048) and a renormalization of the norm).

This is related to the [quantization of constrained systems](wikipedia://en/13657747) and [quantization of gauge theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_of_gauge_theories). It is also possible to formulate a quantum theory of "events" where time becomes an observable.[^n12]

## Problem of measurement {#Problem_of_measurement}

The picture given in the preceding paragraphs is sufficient for description of a completely isolated system. However, it fails to account for one of the main differences between quantum mechanics and classical mechanics, that is, the effects of [measurement](wikipedia://en/19022).[^n13] The von Neumann description of quantum measurement of an observable $A$, when the system is prepared in a pure state $\psi$ is the following (note, however, that von Neumann's description dates back to the 1930s and is based on experiments as performed during that time – more specifically the [Compton–Simon experiment](wikipedia://en/55236); it is not applicable to most present-day measurements within the quantum domain):

- Let $A$ have spectral resolution $$A = \int \lambda \, d \operatorname{E}_A(\lambda),$$ where $E_{A}$ is the [resolution of the identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_of_the_identity) (also called [projection-valued measure](wikipedia://en/1046155)) associated with $A$. Then the probability of the measurement outcome lying in an interval $B$ of $\mathbf{R}$ is $|E_{A}(B) \psi|^{2}$. In other words, the probability is obtained by integrating the characteristic function of $B$ against the countably additive measure $$\langle \psi \mid \operatorname{E}_A \psi \rangle.$$
- If the measured value is contained in $B$, then immediately after the measurement, the system will be in the (generally non-normalized) state $E_{A}(B)\psi$. If the measured value does not lie in $B$, replace $B$ by its complement for the above state.

For example, suppose the state space is the $n$-dimensional complex Hilbert space $\mathbf{C}^{n}$ and $A$ is a Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_{i}$, with corresponding eigenvectors $\psi_{i}$. The projection-valued measure associated with $A$, $E_{A}$, is then

$$\operatorname{E}_A (B) = | \psi_i\rangle \langle \psi_i|,$$

where $B$ is a Borel set containing only the single eigenvalue $\lambda_{i}$. If the system is prepared in state

$$| \psi \rangle$$

Then the probability of a measurement returning the value $\lambda_{i}$ can be calculated by integrating the spectral measure

$$\langle \psi \mid \operatorname{E}_A \psi \rangle$$

over $B_{i}$. This gives trivially

$$\langle \psi| \psi_i\rangle \langle \psi_i \mid \psi \rangle = | \langle \psi \mid \psi_i\rangle | ^2.$$

The characteristic property of the von Neumann measurement scheme is that repeating the same measurement will give the same results. This is also called the *projection postulate*.

A more general formulation replaces the projection-valued measure with a positive-operator valued measure (POVM). To illustrate, take again the finite-dimensional case. Here we would replace the rank-1 projections

$$| \psi_i\rangle \langle \psi_i|$$

by a finite set of positive operators

$$F_i F_i^*$$

whose sum is still the identity operator as before (the resolution of identity). Just as a set of possible outcomes $\{\lambda_{1} ... \lambda_{n}\}$ is associated to a projection-valued measure, the same can be said for a POVM. Suppose the measurement outcome is $\lambda_{i}$. Instead of collapsing to the (unnormalized) state

$$| \psi_i\rangle \langle \psi_i |\psi\rangle$$

after the measurement, the system now will be in the state

$$F_i |\psi\rangle.$$

Since the $F_{i} F_{i}*$ operators need not be mutually orthogonal projections, the projection postulate of von Neumann no longer holds.

The same formulation applies to general [mixed states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_state_%28physics%29).

In von Neumann's approach, the state transformation due to measurement is distinct from that due to time evolution in several ways. For example, time evolution is deterministic and unitary whereas measurement is non-deterministic and non-unitary. However, since both types of state transformation take one quantum state to another, this difference was viewed by many as unsatisfactory. The POVM formalism views measurement as one among many other [quantum operations](wikipedia://en/666107), which are described by [completely positive maps](wikipedia://en/6083511) which do not increase the trace.

## List of mathematical tools {#List_of_mathematical_tools}

Part of the folklore of the subject concerns the [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) textbook [Methods of Mathematical Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methods_of_Mathematical_Physics) put together by [Richard Courant](wikipedia://en/715238) from [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302)'s [Göttingen University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen_University) courses. The story is told (by mathematicians) that physicists had dismissed the material as not interesting in the current research areas, until the advent of Schrödinger's equation. At that point it was realised that the mathematics of the new quantum mechanics was already laid out in it. It is also said that Heisenberg had consulted Hilbert about his matrix mechanics, and Hilbert observed that his own experience with infinite-dimensional matrices had derived from differential equations, advice which Heisenberg ignored, missing the opportunity to unify the theory as Weyl and Dirac did a few years later. Whatever the basis of the anecdotes, the mathematics of the theory was conventional at the time, whereas the physics was radically new.

The main tools include:

- [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422): [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers), [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector), [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue)
- [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180): [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713)
- [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations): [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations), [separation of variables](wikipedia://en/387750), [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations), [Sturm–Liouville theory](wikipedia://en/490990), [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601)
- [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147): [Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/52247)
