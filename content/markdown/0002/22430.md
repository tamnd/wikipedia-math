# Ordered field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ordered field"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **ordered field** is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) together with a [total ordering](wikipedia://en/30330) of its elements that is compatible with the field operations. Basic examples of ordered fields are the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) and the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), both with their standard orderings.

:::

Every [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634) of an ordered field is also an ordered field in the inherited order. Every ordered field contains an ordered subfield that is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). Every [Dedekind-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind-complete) ordered field is isomorphic to the reals. [Squares](wikipedia://en/659942) are necessarily non-negative in an ordered field. This implies that the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) cannot be ordered since the square of the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) *i* is −1 (which is negative in any ordered field). [Finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) cannot be ordered.

Historically, the [axiomatization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatization) of an ordered field was abstracted gradually from the real numbers, by mathematicians including [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), [Otto Hölder](wikipedia://en/191652) and [Hans Hahn](wikipedia://en/1075212). This grew eventually into the [Artin–Schreier theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Schreier_theorem) of ordered fields and [formally real fields](wikipedia://en/1067223).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

There are two equivalent common definitions of an ordered field. The definition of **total order** appeared first historically and is a [first-order](wikipedia://en/10983) axiomatization of the ordering $\leq$ as a [binary predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_predicate). Artin and Schreier gave the definition in terms of **positive cone** in 1926, which axiomatizes the subcollection of nonnegative elements. Although the latter is higher-order, viewing positive cones as maximal prepositive cones provides a larger context in which field orderings are extremal partial orderings.

### Total order {#Total_order}

A [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $(F, +, \cdot\,)$ together with a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330#Strict_total_order) $\leq$ on $F$ is an **ordered field** if the order satisfies the following properties for all $a, b, c \in F:$

- if $a \leq b$ then $a + c \leq b + c,$ and
- if $0 \leq a$ and $0 \leq b$ then $0 \leq a \cdot b.$

As usual, we write $a < b$ for $a\le b$ and $a\ne b$. The notations $b\ge a$ and $b> a$ stand for $a\le b$ and $a < b$, respectively. Elements $a\in F$ with $a>0$ are called positive.

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Positive cone of an ordered field"}

### Positive cone {#Positive_cone}

A **prepositive cone** or **preordering** of a field $F$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $P \subseteq F$ that has the following properties:[^o7]

- For $x$ and $y$ in $P,$ both $x + y$ and $x \cdot y$ are in $P.$
- If $x \in F,$ then $x^2 \in P.$ In particular, $0 = 0^2 \in P$ and $1 = 1^2 \in P.$
- The element $- 1$ is not in $P.$

A **preordered field** is a field equipped with a preordering $P.$ Its non-zero elements $P^*$ form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the multiplicative group of $F.$

If in addition, the set $F$ is the union of $P$ and $- P,$ we call $P$ a **positive cone** of $F.$ The non-zero elements of $P$ are called the **positive** elements of $F.$

An ordered field is a field $F$ together with a positive cone $P.$

The preorderings on $F$ are precisely the intersections of families of positive cones on $F.$ The positive cones are the maximal preorderings.[^o7]

:::

### Equivalence of the two definitions {#Equivalence_of_the_two_definitions}

Let $F$ be a field. There is a bijection between the field orderings of $F$ and the positive cones of $F.$

Given a field ordering ≤ as in the first definition, the set of elements such that $x \geq 0$ forms a positive cone of $F.$ Conversely, given a positive cone $P$ of $F$ as in the second definition, one can associate a total ordering $\leq_P$ on $F$ by setting $x \leq_P y$ to mean $y - x \in P.$ This total ordering $\leq_P$ satisfies the properties of the first definition.

:::

## Examples of ordered fields {#Examples_of_ordered_fields}

Examples of ordered fields are:

- the field $\mathbb{Q}$ of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) with its standard ordering (which is also its only ordering);
- the field $\mathbb{R}$ of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) with its standard ordering (which is also its only ordering);
- any subfield of an ordered field, such as the real [algebraic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_numbers) or the [computable numbers](wikipedia://en/6206), becomes an ordered field by restricting the ordering to the subfield;
- the field $\mathbb{Q}(x)$ of [rational functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_functions) $p(x)/q(x)$, where $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ are [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) with rational coefficients and $q(x) \ne 0$, can be made into an ordered field by fixing a real [transcendental number](wikipedia://en/30325) $\alpha$ and defining $p(x)/q(x) > 0$ if and only if $p(\alpha)/q(\alpha) > 0$. This is equivalent to embedding $\mathbb{Q}(x)$ into $\mathbb{R}$ via $x\mapsto \alpha$ and restricting the ordering of $\mathbb{R}$ to an ordering of the image of $\mathbb{Q}(x)$. In this fashion, we get many different orderings of $\mathbb{Q}(x)$.
- the field $\mathbb{R}(x)$ of [rational functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_functions) $p(x)/q(x)$, where $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ are [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) with real coefficients and $q(x) \ne 0$, can be made into an ordered field by defining $p(x)/q(x) > 0$ to mean that $p_n/q_m > 0$, where $p_n \neq 0$ and $q_m \neq 0$ are the leading coefficients of $p(x) = p_n x^n + \dots + p_0$ and $q(x) = q_m x^m + \dots + q_0$, respectively. Equivalently: for rational functions $f(x), g(x)\in \mathbb{R}(x)$ we have $f(x) < g(x)$ if and only if $f(t) < g(t)$ for all sufficiently large $t\in\mathbb{R}$. In this ordered field the polynomial $p(x)=x$ is greater than any constant polynomial and the ordered field is not [Archimedean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_field).
- The field $\mathbb{R}((x))$ of [formal Laurent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_Laurent_series) with real coefficients, where *x* is taken to be infinitesimal and positive
- the [transseries](wikipedia://en/58151408)
- [real closed fields](wikipedia://en/1066093)
- the [superreal numbers](wikipedia://en/1065871)
- the [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429)

The [surreal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surreal_numbers) form a [proper class](wikipedia://en/20696) rather than a [set](wikipedia://en/26691), but otherwise obey the axioms of an ordered field. Every ordered field can be embedded into the surreal numbers.

## Properties of ordered fields {#Properties_of_ordered_fields}

*Image: Invariance of less-than-relation by multiplication with positive number.svg.* The property $a > 0 \land x < y \Rightarrow ax < ay$

*Image: Translation invariance of less-than-relation.svg.* The property $x < y \Rightarrow a+x < a+y$

For every *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* in *F*:

- Either −*a* ≤ 0 ≤ *a* or *a* ≤ 0 ≤ −*a*.
- One can "add inequalities": if *a* ≤ *b* and *c* ≤ *d*, then *a* + *c* ≤ *b* + *d*.
- One can "multiply inequalities with positive elements": if *a* ≤ *b* and 0 ≤ *c*, then *ac* ≤ *bc*.
- "Multiplying with negatives flips an inequality": if *a* ≤ *b* and c ≤ 0, then *ac* ≥ *bc*.
- If *a* \< *b* and *a*, *b* > 0, then 1/*b* \< 1/*a*.
- Squares are non-negative: 0 ≤ *a*<sup>2</sup> for all *a* in *F*. In particular, since 1=1<sup>2</sup>, it follows that 0 ≤ 1. Since 0 ≠ 1, we conclude 0 \< 1.
- An ordered field has [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 0. (Since 1 > 0, then 1 + 1 > 0, and 1 + 1 + 1 > 0, etc., and no finite sum of ones can equal zero.) In particular, finite fields cannot be ordered.
- Every non-trivial sum of squares is nonzero. Equivalently: $\textstyle \sum_{k=1}^n a_k^2 = 0 \; \Longrightarrow \; \forall k \; \colon a_k = 0 .$[^o14][^o15]

Every subfield of an ordered field is also an ordered field (inheriting the induced ordering). The smallest subfield is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the [rationals](wikipedia://en/19727024) (as for any other field of characteristic 0), and the order on this rational subfield is the same as the order of the rationals themselves.

If every element of an ordered field lies between two elements of its rational subfield, then the field is said to be *[Archimedean](wikipedia://en/264158)*. Otherwise, such field is a [non-Archimedean ordered field](wikipedia://en/31046646) and contains [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990) as well as infinite elements larger than any rational. For example, the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) form an Archimedean field, but [hyperreal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperreal_numbers) form a non-Archimedean field, because it [extends](wikipedia://en/11634) real numbers with elements greater than any standard [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474).[^o11]

An ordered field *F* is isomorphic to the real number field **R** if and only if every non-empty subset of *F* with an upper bound in *F* has a [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound) in *F*. This property implies that the field is Archimedean.

### Vector spaces over an ordered field {#Vector_spaces_over_an_ordered_field}

[Vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) (particularly, [*n*-spaces](wikipedia://en/1463006#Coordinate_space)) over an ordered field exhibit some special properties and have some specific structures, namely: [orientation](wikipedia://en/1391942), [convexity](wikipedia://en/3237201), and [positively-definite inner product](wikipedia://en/14856). See [Real coordinate space#Geometric properties and uses](wikipedia://en/1482083#Geometric_properties_and_uses) for discussion of those properties of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, which can be generalized to vector spaces over other ordered fields.

## Orderability of fields {#Orderability_of_fields}

Every ordered field is a [formally real field](wikipedia://en/1067223), i.e., 0 cannot be written as a sum of nonzero squares.[^o14][^o15]

Conversely, every formally real field can be equipped with a compatible total order, that will turn it into an ordered field. (This order need not be uniquely determined.) The proof uses [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442).[^o16]

[Finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) and more generally fields of positive [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) cannot be turned into ordered fields, as shown above. The [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) also cannot be turned into an ordered field, as −1 is a square of the imaginary unit *i*. Also, the [*p*-adic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_numbers) cannot be ordered, since according to [Hensel's lemma](wikipedia://en/1633368#Examples) **Q**<sub>2</sub> contains a square root of −7, thus 1<sup>2</sup> + 1<sup>2</sup> + 1<sup>2</sup> + 2<sup>2</sup> + $\sqrt{-7}$<sup>2</sup> = 0, and **Q**<sub>*p*</sub> (*p* > 2) contains a square root of 1 − *p*, thus (*p* − 1)⋅1<sup>2</sup> + ($\sqrt{1 - p}$)<sup>2</sup> = 0.[^o17]

## Topology induced by the order {#Topology_induced_by_the_order}

If *F* is equipped with the [order topology](wikipedia://en/213650) arising from the total order ≤, then the axioms guarantee that the operations + and × are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29), so that *F* is a [topological field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_field).

::: {#o20 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Harrison set"}

## Harrison topology {#Harrison_topology}

The **Harrison topology** is a topology on the set of orderings *X*<sub>*F*</sub> of a formally real field *F*. Each order can be regarded as a multiplicative group homomorphism from *F*<sup>∗</sup> onto ±1. Giving ±1 the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology) and ±1<sup>*F*</sup> the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) induces the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) on *X*<sub>*F*</sub>. The **Harrison sets** $H(a) = \{ P \in X_F : a \in P \}$ form a [subbasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subbasis) for the Harrison topology. The product is a [Boolean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_space) ([compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) and [totally disconnected](wikipedia://en/303356)), and *X*<sub>*F*</sub> is a closed subset, hence again Boolean.[^o21][^o22]

:::

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Fan (order)"}

## Fans and superordered fields {#Fans_and_superordered_fields}

A **fan** on *F* is a preordering *T* with the property that if *S* is a subgroup of index 2 in *F*<sup>∗</sup> containing *T* − {0} and not containing −1 then *S* is an ordering (that is, *S* is closed under addition).[^o25] A **superordered field** is a totally real field in which the set of sums of squares forms a fan.[^o26]

:::
