# Redshift

*Image: Redshift.svg.* [Absorption lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line) in the [visible spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum) of a [supercluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercluster) of distant galaxies (right), as compared to absorption lines in the visible spectrum of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) (left). Arrows indicate redshift. Wavelength increases up towards the red and beyond (frequency decreases).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Redshift"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a **redshift** is an increase in the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125), or equivalently, a decrease in the [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779), of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426) (such as [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light)). The opposite change, a decrease in wavelength and increase in frequency and energy, is known as a blueshift.

:::

Three forms of redshift occur in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) and [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology): [Doppler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect) redshifts due to the relative motions of radiation sources, [gravitational redshift](wikipedia://en/10346) as radiation escapes from [gravitational potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential), and cosmological redshifts caused by the [universe expanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_of_the_universe). The value of a redshift is often denoted by the letter $z$, corresponding to the fractional change in wavelength (positive for redshifts, negative for blueshifts), and by the wavelength ratio $1 + z$ (which is greater than 1 for redshifts and less than 1 for blueshifts). Automated astronomical redshift surveys are an important tool for learning about the large-scale structure of the universe. Redshift and blueshift can also be related to [photon energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_energy) and, via [Planck's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck's_law), to a corresponding [blackbody temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbody_temperature).

Examples of strong redshifting are a [gamma ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray) perceived as an [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray), or initially visible light perceived as [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave). The initial 3000 [kelvin](wikipedia://en/19593121) (K) radiation from the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116) has redshifted far down to become the 3 K [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376). Subtler redshifts are seen in the [spectroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_spectroscopy) observations of [astronomical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object), and are used in terrestrial technologies such as [Doppler radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_radar) and [radar guns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_gun). [Gravitational waves](wikipedia://en/8111079), which also travel at [the speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), are subject to the same redshift phenomena.[^o2]

Other physical processes exist that can lead to a shift in the frequency of electromagnetic radiation, including [scattering](wikipedia://en/164483) and [optical effects](wikipedia://en/2460242); however, the resulting changes are distinguishable from (astronomical) redshift and are not generally referred to as such.

## History {#History}

The history of the subject began in the 19th century, with the development of [classical wave mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) and the exploration of phenomena which are associated with the [Doppler effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect). The effect is named after the Austrian mathematician [Christian Doppler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Doppler), who offered the first known physical explanation for the phenomenon in 1842.[^o4][^o5] In 1845, the hypothesis was tested and confirmed for [sound waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_wave) by the Dutch scientist [Christophorus Buys Ballot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._H._D._Buys_Ballot).[^o6] Doppler correctly predicted that the phenomenon would apply to all [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) and, in particular, suggested that the varying [colors of stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_classification#Conventional_colour_description) could be attributed to their motion with respect to the Earth.[^o7]

Unaware of Doppler's work, French physicist [Hippolyte Fizeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippolyte_Fizeau) suggested in 1848 that a shift in [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line) from stars might be used to measure their motion relative to Earth.[^o5] In 1850, [François-Napoléon-Marie Moigno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François-Napoléon-Marie_Moigno) analysed both Doppler's and Fizeau's ideas in a publication read by both [James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) and [William Huggins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Huggins), who initially stuck to the idea that the color of stars related to their chemistry, however by 1868, Huggins was the first to determine the velocity of a star moving away from the Earth by the analysis of spectral shifts.[^o8][^o5]

In 1871, optical redshift was confirmed when the phenomenon was observed in [Fraunhofer lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraunhofer_lines), using solar rotation, about 0.1 Å in the red.[^o9] In 1887, [Hermann Carl Vogel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Carl_Vogel) and [Julius Scheiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Scheiner) discovered the "annual Doppler effect", the yearly change in the Doppler shift of stars located near the ecliptic, due to the orbital velocity of the Earth.[^o10] In 1901, [Aristarkh Belopolsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarkh_Belopolsky) verified optical redshift in the laboratory using a system of rotating mirrors.[^o11][^o9]

Beginning with observations in 1912, [Vesto Slipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesto_Slipher) discovered that the [Andromeda Galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_Galaxy) had a blue shift, indicating that it was moving towards the Earth.[^o12] Slipher first reported his measurement in the inaugural volume of the *[Lowell Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lowell_Observatory) Bulletin*.[^o13] Three years later, he wrote a review in the journal *[Popular Astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_Astronomy_%28US_magazine%29)*.[^o14] In it he stated that "the early discovery that the great Andromeda spiral had the quite exceptional velocity of –300 km\[/s\] showed the means then available, capable of investigating not only the spectra of the spirals but their velocities as well."[^o14] Slipher reported the velocities for 15 [spiral nebulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_galaxy#Spiral_nebula) spread across the entire [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239), all but three having observable "positive" (that is recessional) velocities.[^o12]

Until 1923 the nature of the nebulae was unclear. By that year [Edwin Hubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble) had established that these were [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) and worked out a procedure to measure distance based on the period-luminosity relation of variable [Cepheids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cepheids) stars. This made it possible to test a prediction by [Willem de Sitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_de_Sitter) in 1917 that redshift would be correlated with distance. In 1929 Hubble combined his distance estimates with redshift data from Slipher's reports and measurements by [Milton Humason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Humason) to report an approximate relationship between the redshift and distance, a result now called [Hubble's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble's_law).[^o12][^o15][^o16]

Theories relating to the redshift-distance relation also evolved during the 1920s. The solution to the equations of general relativity described by de Sitter contained no matter, but in 1922 [Alexander Friedmann](wikipedia://en/728892) derived dynamic solutions, now called the [Friedmann equations](wikipedia://en/1147994), based on frictionless fluid models.[^o17] Independently [Georges Lemaître](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Lemaître) derived similar equations in 1927 and his analysis became widely known around the time of Hubble's key publication.[^o12]

By early 1930 the combination of the redshift measurements and theoretical models established a major breakthrough in the new science of cosmology: the universe had a history and its expansion could be investigated with physical models backed up with observational astronomy.[^o12]

When cosmological redshifts were first discovered, [Fritz Zwicky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Zwicky) proposed an effect known as [tired light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tired_light). However this model has largely been ruled out by timescale stretch observations in [type Ia supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_Ia_supernovae).[^o18]

[Arthur Eddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eddington) used the term "red shift" as early as 1923, which is the oldest example of the term reported by the *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)*.[^o19] Willem de Sitter used the single-word version *redshift* in 1934.[^o21]

In the 1960s the discovery of [quasars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasars), which appear as very blue point sources and thus were initially thought to be unusual stars, led to the idea that they were as bright as they were because they were closer than their redshift data indicated. A flurry of theoretical and observational work concluded that these objects were very powerful but distant astronomical objects.[^o12]

## Physical origins {#Physical_origins}

Redshifts are differences between two wavelength measurements and wavelengths are a property of both the photons and the measuring equipment. Thus redshifts characterise differences between two measurement locations. These differences are commonly organised in three groups, attributed to relative motion between the source and the observer, to the expansion of the universe, and to gravity.[^o23] The following sections explain these groups.

### Doppler effect {#Doppler_effect}

*Image: Suzredshift.gif.* [Doppler effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect), yellow (c. 575 [nm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometre) wavelength) ball appears greenish (blueshift to c. 565 nm wavelength) approaching observer, turns [orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_%28colour%29) (redshift to c. 585 nm wavelength) as it passes, and returns to yellow when motion stops. To observe such a change in colour, the object would have to be travelling at approximately 5,200 [km/s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_per_second), or about 32 times faster than the speed record for the [fastest space probe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parker_Solar_Probe).

*Image: Redshift blueshift.svg.* Redshift and blueshift

If a source of the light is moving away from an observer, then redshift ($z > 0$) occurs; if the source moves towards the observer, then [blueshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueshift) ($z < 0$) occurs. This is true for all electromagnetic waves and is explained by the [Doppler effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect). Consequently, this type of redshift is called the *Doppler redshift*. If the source moves away from the observer with [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) $v$, which is much less than the speed of light ($v \ll c$), the redshift is given by

$$z \approx \frac{v}{c}$$

where $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) (since $\gamma \approx 1$). In the classical Doppler effect, the frequency of the source is not modified, but the recessional motion causes the illusion of a lower frequency.

A more complete treatment of the Doppler redshift requires considering relativistic effects associated with motion of sources close to the speed of light. A complete derivation of the effect can be found in the article on the [relativistic Doppler effect](wikipedia://en/408026). In brief, objects moving close to the speed of light will experience deviations from the above formula due to the [time dilation](wikipedia://en/297839) of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) which can be corrected for by introducing the [Lorentz factor](wikipedia://en/732446) $\gamma$ into the classical Doppler formula as follows (for motion solely in the line of sight):

$$1 + z = \left(1 + \frac{v}{c}\right) \gamma.$$

This phenomenon was first observed in a 1938 experiment performed by [Herbert E. Ives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_E._Ives) and G. R. Stilwell, called the [Ives–Stilwell experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ives–Stilwell_experiment).[^o25]

Since the Lorentz factor is dependent only on the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301) of the velocity, this causes the redshift associated with the relativistic correction to be independent of the orientation of the source movement. In contrast, the classical part of the formula is dependent on the [projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_resolute) of the movement of the source into the [line-of-sight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-of-sight_propagation) which yields different results for different orientations. If $\theta$ is the angle between the direction of relative motion and the direction of emission in the observer's frame[^o26] (zero angle is directly away from the observer), the full form for the relativistic Doppler effect becomes:

$$1+ z = \frac{1 + v \cos (\theta)/c}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$$

and for motion solely in the line of sight ($\theta = 0^{\circ}$), this equation reduces to:

$$1 + z = \sqrt{\frac{1+v/c}{1-v/c}}$$

For the special case that the light is moving at [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956) ($\theta = 90^{\circ}$) to the direction of relative motion in the observer's frame,[^o27] the relativistic redshift is known as the [transverse redshift](wikipedia://en/408026), and a redshift:

$$1 + z = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}$$

is measured, even though the object is not moving away from the observer. Even when the source is moving towards the observer, if there is a transverse component to the motion then there is some speed at which the dilation just cancels the expected blueshift and at higher speed the approaching source will be redshifted.[^o28]

### Cosmological {#Cosmological}

The observations of increasing redshifts from more and more distant galaxies can be modelled assuming a [homogeneous and isotropic universe](wikipedia://en/173937) combined with [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024). This cosmological redshift can be written as a function of $a$, the time-dependent cosmic [scale factor](wikipedia://en/921168):[^o30]

$$1+z = \frac{a_\mathrm{now}}{a_\mathrm{then}} = \frac{a_0}{a(t)}$$

The scale factor is [monotonically increasing](wikipedia://en/48260) as time passes. Thus $z$ is positive, close to zero for local stars, and increasing for distant galaxies that appear redshifted.

Using a [Friedmann–Robertson–Walker model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedmann–Robertson–Walker_model) of the expansion of the universe, redshift can be related to the age of an observed object, the so-called *[cosmic time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_time)–redshift relation*. Denote a density ratio as $\Omega_{0}$:

$$\Omega_0 = \frac {\rho}{ \rho_\text{crit}} \ ,$$

with $\rho_{\operatorname{crit}}$ the critical density demarcating a universe that eventually crunches from one that simply expands. This density is about three hydrogen atoms per cubic meter of space.[^o31] At large redshifts, $1 + z > \Omega_{0}^{-1}$, one finds:

$$t(z) \approx \frac {2}{3 H_0 {\Omega_0}^{1/2} } \left( \frac{1}{z^{3/2}}\ \right),$$

where $t$ is age of the object after the big bang, $H_{0}$ is the present-day [Hubble constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_constant), and $z$ is the redshift.[^o32][^o33] For these large redshifts, the age of the universe, $t(z)$, is small, meaning that the light was emitted when the universe was young.

The **cosmological redshift** is commonly attributed to stretching of the wavelengths of photons due to the stretching of space. This interpretation can be misleading. As required by [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), the cosmological expansion of space has no effect on local physics. There is no term related to expansion in [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) that govern light propagation. The cosmological redshift can be interpreted as an accumulation of infinitesimal Doppler shifts along the trajectory of the light.[^o34]

There are several websites for calculating various times and distances from redshift, as the precise calculations require numerical integrals for most values of the parameters.[^o35][^o36]

#### Distinguishing between cosmological and local effects {#Distinguishing_between_cosmological_and_local_effects}

The redshift of a galaxy includes both a component related to [recessional velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recessional_velocity) from expansion of the universe, and a component related to the [peculiar motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peculiar_motion) of the galaxy with respect to its local universe.[^o38] The redshift due to expansion of the universe depends upon the recessional velocity in a fashion determined by the cosmological model chosen to describe the expansion of the universe, which is very different from how Doppler redshift depends upon local velocity.[^o39] Describing the cosmological expansion origin of redshift, cosmologist [Edward Robert Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Robert_Harrison) said, "Light leaves a galaxy, which is stationary in its local region of space, and is eventually received by observers who are stationary in their own local region of space. Between the galaxy and the observer, light travels through vast regions of expanding space. As a result, all wavelengths of the light are stretched by the expansion of space. It is as simple as that..."[^o40] [Steven Weinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Weinberg) clarified, "The increase of wavelength from emission to absorption of light does not depend on the rate of change of $a(t)$ \[the [scale factor](wikipedia://en/921168)\] at the times of emission or absorption, but on the increase of $a(t)$ in the whole period from emission to absorption."[^o41]

#### Decomposition {#Decomposition}

Redshifts or blueshifts cannot be simply added. A galaxy with an observed redshift of $z$ will have a part due to cosmological redshift, $\bar{z}$, and part due to the galaxy's peculiar velocity, $z_\mathrm{p}$, related by:[^o43]

$$(1+z) = (1+\bar{z})(1+ z_\mathrm{p})$$

or

$$z_\mathrm{p} = \frac{z-\bar{z}}{1+\bar{z}}$$

Then the galaxies peculiar velocity, $v_\mathrm{p}$ will be

$$v_\mathrm{p} = cz_\mathrm{p}$$

with $c$ being the speed of light. This relationship between velocity and redshift assumes nonrelativistic velocity. For [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray) or [jets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysical_jet) from [active galactic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_galactic_nuclei), a relativistic relation is needed.[^o43]

### Gravitational redshift {#Gravitational_redshift}

In the theory of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), there is time dilation within a gravitational well. Light emitted within the well will appear to have fewer cycles per second when measured outside of the well, due to differences in the two clocks.[^o46] This is known as the [gravitational redshift](wikipedia://en/10346) or *Einstein shift*.[^o47] The theoretical derivation of this effect follows from the [Schwarzschild solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_solution) of the [Einstein equations](wikipedia://en/424540) which yields the following formula for redshift associated with a photon travelling in the [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field) of an [uncharged](wikipedia://en/9804), [nonrotating](wikipedia://en/39789), [spherically symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_symmetry) mass:

$$1+z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{2GM}{rc^2}}},$$

where

- $G$ is the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant),
- $M$ is the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of the object creating the gravitational field,
- $r$ is the radial coordinate of the source (which is analogous to the classical distance from the center of the object, but is actually a [Schwarzschild coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_coordinates)), and
- $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light).

This gravitational redshift result can be derived from the assumptions of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) and the [equivalence principle](wikipedia://en/857235); the full theory of general relativity is not required.[^o48]

The effect is very small but measurable on Earth using the [Mössbauer effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mössbauer_effect) and was first observed in the [Pound–Rebka experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound–Rebka_experiment).[^o49] However, it is significant near a [black hole](wikipedia://en/4650), and as an object approaches the [event horizon](wikipedia://en/29320146) the red shift becomes infinite. It is also the dominant cause of large angular-scale temperature fluctuations in the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) radiation (see [Sachs–Wolfe effect](wikipedia://en/437988)).[^o50]

:::

## Measurement {#Measurement}

*Image: High-redshift galaxy candidates in the Hubble Ultra Deep Field 2012.jpg.* High-redshift galaxy candidates in the [Hubble Ultra Deep Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Ultra_Deep_Field), 2012[^o57]

Using a telescope and a [spectrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrometer), the variation in intensity of star light with frequency can be measured. The resulting spectrum can be compared to the spectrum from hot gases expected in stars, such as [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), in a laboratory on Earth. As illustrated with the idealised spectrum in the top-right, to determine the redshift, features in the two spectra such as [absorption lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line), [emission lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line), or other variations in light intensity may be shifted.

Redshift (and blueshift) may be characterised by the relative difference between the observed and emitted wavelengths (or frequency) of an object. In astronomy, it is customary to refer to this change using a [dimensionless quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity) called $z$. If $\lambda$ represents wavelength and $f$ represents frequency (note, $\lambda f = c$ where $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light)), then $z$ is defined by the equations:[^o58]

*Calculation of redshift, $z$*

::: {#o59 .table cue=table name="Calculation of redshift, z"}

| Based on wavelength | Based on frequency |
|---|---|
| $z = \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{obsv}} - \lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}}$ | $z = \frac{f_{\mathrm{emit}} - f_{\mathrm{obsv}}}{f_{\mathrm{obsv}}}$ |
| $1+z = \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{obsv}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}}$ | $1+z = \frac{f_{\mathrm{emit}}}{f_{\mathrm{obsv}}}$ |

:::

[Doppler effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect) blueshifts ($z < 0$) are associated with objects approaching (moving closer to) the observer with the light shifting to greater [energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy). Conversely, Doppler effect redshifts ($z > 0$) are associated with objects receding (moving away) from the observer with the light shifting to lower energies. Likewise, gravitational blueshifts are associated with light emitted from a source residing within a weaker [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field) as observed from within a stronger gravitational field, while gravitational redshifting implies the opposite conditions.

## Observations in astronomy {#Observations_in_astronomy}

*Image: Look-back time by redshift.png.* The [lookback time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookback_time) by observed redshift up to $z$ = 20 using parameters of the [Planck mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_mission) in the [standard model of cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda_CDM).[^o62] There are websites for calculating distances from redshift.[^o35][^o36]

The redshift observed in astronomy can be measured because the [emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_spectrum) and [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_spectroscopy) spectra for [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) are distinctive and well known, calibrated from [spectroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopic) experiments in [laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratories) on Earth. When the redshifts of various absorption and emission lines from a single astronomical object are measured, $z$ is found to be remarkably constant. Although distant objects may be slightly blurred and lines broadened, it is by no more than can be explained by [thermal](wikipedia://en/64204) or mechanical [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion) of the source. For these reasons and others, the consensus among astronomers is that the redshifts they observe are due to some combination of the three established forms of Doppler-like redshifts.

Spectroscopy, as a measurement, is considerably more difficult than simple [photometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photometry_%28astronomy%29), which measures the [brightness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightness) of astronomical objects through certain [filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_filter). When photometric data is all that is available (for example, the [Hubble Deep Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Deep_Field) and the [Hubble Ultra Deep Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Ultra_Deep_Field)), astronomers rely on a technique for measuring [photometric redshifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photometric_redshift).[^o63] Due to the broad wavelength ranges in photometric filters and the necessary assumptions about the nature of the spectrum at the light-source, [errors](wikipedia://en/22346) for these sorts of measurements can range up to $\delta z = 0.5$, and are much less reliable than spectroscopic determinations.[^o64]

However, photometry does at least allow a qualitative characterisation of a redshift. For example, if a Sun-like spectrum had a redshift of $z = 1$, it would be brightest in the [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared) (1000 nm) rather than at the blue-green (500 nm) color associated with the peak of its [blackbody](wikipedia://en/44364) spectrum, and the light intensity will be reduced in the filter by a factor of four, $(1 + z)^{2}$. Both the photon count rate and the photon energy are redshifted. (See [K correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K_correction) for more details on the photometric consequences of redshift.)

Determining the redshift of an object with spectroscopy requires the wavelength of the emitted light in the rest frame of the source. Astronomical applications rely on distinct spectral lines. Redshifts cannot be calculated by looking at unidentified features whose rest-frame frequency is unknown, or with a spectrum that is featureless or [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182) (random fluctuations in a spectrum). Thus [gamma-ray bursts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_burst) themselves cannot be used for reliable redshift measurements, but optical afterglow associated with the burst can be analysed for redshifts.[^o65]

### Local observations {#Local_observations}

In nearby objects (within our [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way) galaxy) observed redshifts are almost always related to the [line-of-sight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-of-sight_propagation) velocities associated with the objects being observed. Observations of such redshifts and blueshifts enable astronomers to measure [velocities](wikipedia://en/20491903) and parametrise the [masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of the [orbiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) in [spectroscopic binaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopic_binaries). Similarly, small redshifts and blueshifts detected in the spectroscopic measurements of individual stars are one way astronomers have been able to [diagnose and measure](wikipedia://en/7290120#Radial_velocity) the presence and characteristics of [planetary systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exoplanet) around other stars and have even made very [detailed differential measurements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rossiter–McLaughlin_effect) of redshifts during [planetary transits](wikipedia://en/7290120) to determine precise orbital parameters. Some approaches are able to track the redshift variations in multiple objects at once.[^o67]

Finely detailed measurements of redshifts are used in [helioseismology](wikipedia://en/408801) to determine the precise movements of the [photosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosphere) of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun).[^o68] Redshifts have also been used to make the first measurements of the [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) rates of [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet),[^o69] velocities of [interstellar clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_cloud),[^o70] the [rotation of galaxies](wikipedia://en/183083),[^n1] and the [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29) of [accretion](wikipedia://en/48400112) onto [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star) and [black holes](wikipedia://en/4650) which exhibit both Doppler and gravitational redshifts.[^o71] The [temperatures](wikipedia://en/20647050) of various emitting and absorbing objects can be obtained by measuring [Doppler broadening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_broadening)—effectively redshifts and blueshifts over a single emission or absorption line.[^o72] By measuring the broadening and shifts of the 21-centimeter [hydrogen line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_line) in different directions, astronomers have been able to measure the [recessional velocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recessional_velocity) of [interstellar gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_gas), which in turn reveals the [rotation curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_curve) of our Milky Way.[^n1] Similar measurements have been performed on other galaxies, such as [Andromeda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_Galaxy).[^n1]

### Extragalactic observations {#Extragalactic_observations}

The most distant objects exhibit larger redshifts corresponding to the [Hubble flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_flow) of the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe). The largest-observed redshift, corresponding to the greatest distance and furthest back in time, is that of the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) radiation; the [numerical value of its redshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble's_law#Redshift_velocity) is about $z = 1089$ ($z = 0$ corresponds to present time), and it shows the state of the universe about 13.8 billion years ago,[^o74] and 379,000 years after the initial moments of the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116).

The luminous point-like cores of [quasars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar) were the first "high-redshift" ($z > 0.1$) objects discovered before the improvement of telescopes allowed for the discovery of other high-redshift galaxies.[^o75]

For galaxies more distant than the [Local Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Group) and the nearby [Virgo Cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_Cluster), but within a thousand mega[parsecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsec) or so, the redshift is approximately proportional to the galaxy's distance. This correlation was first observed by [Edwin Hubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble) and has come to be known as [Hubble's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble's_law). [Vesto Slipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesto_Slipher) was the first to discover galactic redshifts, in about 1912, while Hubble correlated Slipher's measurements with distances he [measured by other means](wikipedia://en/471487) to formulate his law.[^n2] Because it is usually not known how [luminous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity) objects are, measuring the redshift is easier than more direct distance measurements, so redshift is sometimes in practice converted to a crude distance measurement using Hubble's law.[^o77]

[Gravitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation) interactions of galaxies with each other and clusters cause a significant [scatter](wikipedia://en/32344) in the normal plot of the Hubble diagram. The [peculiar velocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peculiar_velocity) associated with galaxies superimpose a rough trace of the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of [virialised objects](wikipedia://en/32664) in the universe. This effect leads to such phenomena as nearby galaxies (such as the [Andromeda Galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_Galaxy)) exhibiting blueshifts as we fall towards a common [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter), and redshift maps of clusters showing a [fingers of god](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingers_of_god) effect due to the scatter of peculiar velocities in a roughly spherical distribution.[^n3] These "redshift-space distortions" can be used as a cosmological probe in their own right, providing information on how structure formed in the universe,[^o79] and how gravity behaves on large scales.[^o80]

The Hubble law's linear relationship between distance and redshift assumes that the rate of expansion of the universe is constant. However, when the universe was much younger, the expansion rate, and thus the Hubble "constant", was larger than it is today. For more distant galaxies, then, whose light has been travelling to us for much longer times, the approximation of constant expansion rate fails, and the Hubble law becomes a non-linear integral relationship and dependent on the history of the expansion rate since the emission of the light from the galaxy in question. Observations of the redshift-distance relationship can be used, then, to determine the expansion history of the universe and thus the matter and energy content.[^o81]

It was long believed that the expansion rate has been continuously decreasing since the Big Bang, but observations beginning in 1988 of the redshift-distance relationship using [Type Ia supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_Ia_supernova) have suggested that in comparatively recent times the expansion rate of the universe has [begun to accelerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerating_expansion_of_the_universe).[^o82]

### Highest redshifts {#Highest_redshifts}

*Image: Comoving distance and lookback time (Planck 2018).png.* [Comoving distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_and_proper_distances) and [lookback time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookback_time) for the Planck 2018 cosmology parameters, from redshift 0 to 15, with distance (blue solid line) on the left axis, and time (orange dashed line) on the right. Note that the time that has passed (in billions of years) from a given redshift until now is not the same as the distance (in giga light years) light would have travelled from that redshift, due to the expansion of the universe over the intervening period.

Records for the largest observed redshift have been set and broken repeatedly as observation technology has advanced.[^o84] The most reliable redshifts are from observing [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line).[^o85] Galaxies that have held the record for largest spectroscopic redshift include [GN-z11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GN-z11) with a redshift of $z = 11.1$,[^o86] corresponding to 400 million years after the Big Bang; [JADES-GS-z14-0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JADES-GS-z14-0) at $z = 14.32$ (290 million years);[^o87] and [MoM-z14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MoM-z14) at $z = 14.44$ (280 million years).[^o88]

Slightly less reliable are [Lyman-break](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyman-break_galaxy) redshifts, such as the lensed galaxy A1689-zD1 at a redshift $z = 7.5$.[^o89][^o90] The most distant-observed [gamma-ray burst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_burst) with a spectroscopic redshift measurement was [GRB 090423](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRB_090423), which had a redshift of $z = 8.2$.[^o91] The most distant-known quasar, [UHZ1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UHZ1), is at $z = 10.1$.[^o92][^o93] The highest-known redshift radio galaxy, [ILT J2336+1842](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ILT_J2336+1842), is at a redshift $z = 6.6$,[^o94] and molecular material ([carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide)) has been detected as far out as $z = 6.9$.[^o95]

*Extremely red objects* (EROs) are [astronomical sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_astronomy#Astronomical_sources) of radiation that radiate energy in the red and near infrared part of the electromagnetic spectrum. These may be starburst galaxies that have a high redshift accompanied by reddening from intervening dust, or they could be highly redshifted elliptical galaxies with an older (and therefore redder) stellar population.[^o96] Objects that are even redder than EROs are termed *hyper extremely red objects* (HEROs).[^o97]

In June 2015, astronomers reported evidence for [Population III stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_population#Population_III_stars) in the [Cosmos Redshift 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos_Redshift_7) [galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) at $z = 6.60$. Such stars are likely to have existed in the very early universe (i.e., at high redshift), and may have started the production of [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) heavier than [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) that are needed for the later formation of [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) and [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life) as we know it.[^o98][^o99]

Nonetheless, relic sources post far higher redshifts than even the furthest astronomical objects observed. The [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) has a redshift of $z = 1089$, corresponding to an age of approximately 379,000 years after the Big Bang and a [proper distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_and_proper_distances) of more than 46 billion light-years.[^o100] This redshift corresponds to a shift in average temperature from 3000 K down to 3 K.[^o101] The yet-to-be-observed first light from the oldest [Population III stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_III_stars), not long after atoms first formed and the CMB ceased to be absorbed almost completely, may have redshifts in the range of $20 < z < 100$.[^o102] Other high-redshift events predicted by physics but not presently observable are the [cosmic neutrino background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_neutrino_background) from about two seconds after the Big Bang (and a redshift in excess of $z > 10^{10}$)[^o103] and the cosmic [gravitational wave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave_background) emitted directly from [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_%28cosmology%29) at a redshift in excess of $z > 10^{25}$.[^o104]

### Redshift surveys {#Redshift_surveys}

*Image: 2dfgrs.png.* Rendering of the 2dFGRS data

With advent of [automated telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotic_telescope) and improvements in [spectroscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_spectroscopy), a number of collaborations have been made to map the universe in redshift space. By combining redshift with angular position data, a redshift survey maps the 3D distribution of matter within a field of the sky. These observations are used to measure properties of the [large-scale structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe) of the universe. The [Great Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CfA2_Great_Wall), a vast [supercluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercluster) of galaxies over 500 million [light-years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year) wide, provides a dramatic example of a large-scale structure that redshift surveys can detect.[^o106]

The first redshift survey was the [CfA Redshift Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CfA_Redshift_Survey), started in 1977 with the initial data collection completed in 1982.[^o107] More recently, the [2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2dF_Galaxy_Redshift_Survey) determined the large-scale structure of one section of the universe, measuring redshifts for over 220,000 galaxies; data collection was completed in 2002, and the final [data set](wikipedia://en/8495) was released 30 June 2003.[^o108][^o109] The [Sloan Digital Sky Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Digital_Sky_Survey) (SDSS) began collecting data in 1998[^o110] and published its eighteenth data release in 2023.[^o111] SSDS has measured redshifts for galaxies as high as 0.8, and has recorded over 100,000 [quasars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar) at $z = 3$ and beyond.[^o112] The [DEEP2 Redshift Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEEP2_Redshift_Survey) used the [Keck telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keck_telescopes) with the "DEIMOS" [spectrograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrograph); a follow-up to the pilot program DEEP1, DEEP2 was designed to measure faint galaxies with redshifts 0.7 and above, and it recorded redshifts of over 38,000 objects by its conclusion in 2013.[^o113][^o114]

## Effects from physical optics or radiative transfer {#Effects_from_physical_optics_or_radiative_transfer}

The interactions and phenomena summarised in the subjects of [radiative transfer](wikipedia://en/2925371) and [physical optics](wikipedia://en/2460242) can result in shifts in the wavelength and frequency of electromagnetic radiation. In such cases, the shifts correspond to a physical energy transfer to matter or other photons rather than being by a transformation between reference frames. Such shifts can be from such physical phenomena as [coherence effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_effect) or the [scattering](wikipedia://en/164483) of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426) whether from [charged](wikipedia://en/9804) [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle), from [particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulates), or from fluctuations of the [index of refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_of_refraction) in a [dielectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric) medium as occurs in the radio phenomenon of [radio whistlers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistler_%28radio%29).[^n1] While such phenomena are sometimes referred to as "redshifts" and "blueshifts", in astrophysics light–matter interactions that result in energy shifts in the radiation field are generally referred to as "reddening" rather than "redshifting" which, as a term, is normally reserved for the effects discussed above.[^n1]

In many circumstances scattering causes radiation to redden because [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) results in the predominance of many low-[energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) photons over few high-energy ones (while [conserving total energy](wikipedia://en/67088)).[^n1] Except possibly under carefully controlled conditions, scattering does not produce the same relative change in wavelength across the whole spectrum; that is, any calculated $z$ is generally a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of wavelength. Furthermore, scattering from [random](wikipedia://en/19196523) [media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter) generally occurs at many [angles](wikipedia://en/1196), and $z$ is a function of the scattering angle. If multiple scattering occurs, or the scattering particles have relative motion, then there is generally distortion of [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line) as well.[^n1]

In [interstellar astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_medium), [visible spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum) can appear redder due to scattering processes in a phenomenon referred to as [interstellar reddening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_reddening)[^n1]—similarly [Rayleigh scattering](wikipedia://en/26176) causes the [atmospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) reddening of the Sun seen in the sunrise or sunset and causes the rest of the sky to have a blue colour. This phenomenon is distinct from redshifting because the [spectroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopic) lines are not shifted to other wavelengths in reddened objects and there is an additional [dimming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction_%28astronomy%29) and distortion associated with the phenomenon due to photons being scattered in and out of the [line of sight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-of-sight_propagation).[^o116]

## Blueshift {#Blueshift}

The opposite of a redshift is a **blueshift**. A blueshift is any decrease in [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) (increase in [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy)), with a corresponding increase in frequency, of an [electromagnetic wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave). In [visible light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light), this shifts a color towards the blue end of the spectrum.

::: {#o120 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Doppler blueshift"}

### Doppler blueshift {#Doppler_blueshift}

[Doppler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect) blueshift is caused by movement of a source towards the observer. The term applies to any decrease in wavelength and increase in frequency caused by relative motion, even outside the [visible spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum). Only objects moving at near-[relativistic speeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_speed) toward the observer are noticeably bluer to the [naked eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_eye), but the wavelength of any reflected or emitted photon or other particle is shortened in the direction of travel.[^o121]

Doppler blueshift is used in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) to determine relative motion:

- The [Andromeda Galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_Galaxy) is moving toward our own [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way) [galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) within the [Local Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Group); thus, when observed from Earth, its light is undergoing a blueshift.[^o122]
- Components of a [binary star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star) system will be blueshifted when moving towards Earth
- When observing spiral galaxies, the side spinning toward us will have a slight blueshift *relative to* the side spinning away from us (see [Tully–Fisher relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tully–Fisher_relation)).
- [Blazars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blazar) are known to propel [relativistic jets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_jet) toward us, emitting [synchrotron radiation](wikipedia://en/170808) and [bremsstrahlung](wikipedia://en/54147) that appears blueshifted.[^o123]
- Nearby stars such as [Barnard's Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard's_Star) are moving toward us, resulting in a very small blueshift.

:::

### Gravitational blueshift {#Gravitational_blueshift}

*Image: Gravitional well.jpg.* [Matter waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_waves) (protons, electrons, photons, etc.) falling into a [gravity well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_well) become more energetic and undergo observer-independent blueshifting.

Unlike the *relative* Doppler blueshift, caused by movement of a source towards the observer and thus dependent on the received angle of the photon, gravitational blueshift is *absolute* and does not depend on the received angle of the photon:

Photons climbing out of a gravitating object become less energetic. This loss of energy is known as a "redshifting", as photons in the visible spectrum would appear more red. Similarly, photons falling into a gravitational field become more energetic and exhibit a blueshifting. ... Note that the magnitude of the redshifting (blueshifting) effect is not a function of the emitted angle or the received angle of the photon—it depends only on how far radially the photon had to climb out of (fall into) the potential well.[^o125][^o126]

It is a natural consequence of [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) and [mass–energy equivalence](wikipedia://en/422481), and was confirmed experimentally in 1959 with the [Pound–Rebka experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pound–Rebka_experiment). Gravitational blueshift contributes to [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) (CMB) anisotropy via the [Sachs–Wolfe effect](wikipedia://en/437988): when a gravitational well evolves while a photon is passing, the amount of blueshift on approach will differ from the amount of [gravitational redshift](wikipedia://en/10346) as it leaves the region.[^o127]

#### Blue outliers {#Blue_outliers}

There are far-away [active galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_galaxies) that show a blueshift in their [\[O III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_ionized_oxygen)\] emission [lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_spectrum). One of the largest blueshifts is found in the narrow-line [quasar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar), [PG 1543+489](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PG_1543+489), which has a relative velocity of −1150 km/s. These types of galaxies are called "blue outliers".[^o129]

### Cosmological blueshift {#Cosmological_blueshift}

In a hypothetical universe undergoing a runaway [Big Crunch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Crunch) contraction, a cosmological blueshift would be observed, with galaxies further away being increasingly blueshifted—the exact opposite of the actually observed [cosmological redshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_redshift) in the present [expanding universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expanding_universe).[^o131]
