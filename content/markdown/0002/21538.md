# Normed vector space

*Image: Mathematical Spaces.svg.* Hierarchy of mathematical spaces. [Inner product spaces](wikipedia://en/14856) are a subset of normed vector spaces, which are a subset of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018), which in turn are a subset of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Normed vector space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **normed vector space** or **normed space** is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), typically over the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) numbers, on which a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) is defined.[^o2] A norm is a generalization of the intuitive notion of "length" in the physical world. If $V$ is a vector space over $K$, where $K$ is a field equal to $\mathbb R$ or to $\mathbb C$, then a norm on $V$ is a map $V\to\mathbb R$, typically denoted by $\lVert\cdot \rVert$, satisfying the following four axioms:

:::

1. Non-negativity: for every $x\in V$,$\; \lVert x \rVert \ge 0$.
1. Positive definiteness: for every $x \in V$, $\; \lVert x\rVert=0$ if and only if $x$ is the zero vector.
1. Absolute homogeneity: for every $\lambda\in K$ and $x\in V$, $$\lVert \lambda x \rVert = |\lambda|\, \lVert x\rVert$$
1. [Triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941): for every $x\in V$ and $y\in V$, $$\|x+y\| \leq \|x\| + \|y\|.$$

If $V$ is a real or complex vector space as above, and $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ is a norm on $V$, then the ordered pair $(V,\lVert\cdot \rVert)$ is called a normed vector space. If it is clear from context which norm is intended, then it is common to denote the normed vector space simply by $V$.

A norm induces a [distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), called its [(norm) induced metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_induced_metric), by the formula

$$d(x,y) = \|y-x\|.$$

which makes any normed vector space into a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) and a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752). If this metric space is [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) then the normed space is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989). Every normed vector space can be "uniquely extended" to a Banach space, which makes normed spaces intimately related to Banach spaces. Every Banach space is a normed space but the converse is not true. For example, the set of the [finite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_sequence) of real numbers can be normed with the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm), but it is not complete for this norm.

An [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) is a normed vector space whose norm is the square root of the inner product of a vector and itself. The [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm) of a [Euclidean vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector_space) is a special case that allows defining [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) by the formula

$$d(A, B) = \|\overrightarrow{AB}\|.$$

The study of normed spaces and Banach spaces is a fundamental part of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a major subfield of mathematics.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **normed vector space** is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) equipped with a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534). A **seminormed vector space** is a vector space equipped with a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067).

A useful [variation of the triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941#Reverse_triangle_inequality) is

$$\|x-y\| \geq | \|x\| - \|y\| |$$

for any vectors $x$ and $y.$

This also shows that a vector norm is a ([uniformly](wikipedia://en/32337)) [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122).

Property 3 depends on a choice of norm $|\alpha|$ on the field of scalars. When the scalar field is $\mathbb{R}$ (or more generally a subset of $\mathbb{C}$), this is usually taken to be the ordinary [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991), but other choices are possible. For example, for a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$ one could take $|\alpha|$ to be the [$p$-adic absolute value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_absolute_value).

:::

## Topological structure {#Topological_structure}

If $(V, \|\,\cdot\,\|)$ is a normed vector space, the norm $\|\,\cdot\,\|$ induces a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) (a notion of *distance*) and therefore a [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) on $V.$ This metric is defined in the natural way: the distance between two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ is given by $\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|.$ This topology is precisely the weakest topology which makes $\|\,\cdot\,\|$ continuous and which is compatible with the linear structure of $V$ in the following sense:

1. The vector addition $\,+\, : V \times V \to V$ is jointly continuous with respect to this topology. This follows directly from the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941).
1. The scalar multiplication $\,\cdot\, : \mathbb{K} \times V \to V,$ where $\mathbb{K}$ is the underlying scalar field of $V,$ is jointly continuous. This follows from the triangle inequality and homogeneity of the norm.

Similarly, for any seminormed vector space we can define the distance between two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ as $\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|.$ This turns the seminormed space into a [pseudometric space](wikipedia://en/68503) (notice this is weaker than a metric) and allows the definition of notions such as [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) and [convergence](wikipedia://en/285759). To put it more abstractly every seminormed vector space is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) and thus carries a [topological structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_structure) which is induced by the semi-norm.

Of special interest are [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) normed spaces, which are known as [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). Every normed vector space $V$ sits as a dense subspace inside some Banach space; this Banach space is essentially uniquely defined by $V$ and is called the [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_completion) of $V.$

Two norms on the same vector space are called [equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_norm) if they define the same [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_%28structure%29). On a finite-dimensional vector space (but not infinite-dimensional vector spaces), all norms are equivalent (although the resulting metric spaces need not be the same)[^o6] And since any Euclidean space is complete, we can thus conclude that all finite-dimensional normed vector spaces are Banach spaces.

A normed vector space $V$ is [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) if and only if the unit ball $B = \{ x : \|x\| \leq 1\}$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), which is the case if and only if $V$ is finite-dimensional; this is a consequence of [Riesz's lemma](wikipedia://en/5584743). (In fact, a more general result is true: a topological vector space is locally compact if and only if it is finite-dimensional. The point here is that we don't assume the topology comes from a norm.)

The topology of a seminormed vector space has many nice properties. Given a [neighbourhood system](wikipedia://en/1714290) $\mathcal{N}(0)$ around 0 we can construct all other neighbourhood systems as

$$\mathcal{N}(x) = x + \mathcal{N}(0) := \{x + N : N \in \mathcal{N}(0)\}$$

with

$$x + N := \{x + n : n \in N\}.$$

Moreover, there exists a [neighbourhood basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood_basis) for the origin consisting of [absorbing](wikipedia://en/1728179) and [convex sets](wikipedia://en/6292). As this property is very useful in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), generalizations of normed vector spaces with this property are studied under the name [locally convex spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex_space).

A norm (or [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067)) $\|\cdot\|$ on a topological vector space $(X, \tau)$ is continuous if and only if the topology $\tau_{\|\cdot\|}$ that $\|\cdot\|$ induces on $X$ is [coarser](wikipedia://en/393069) than $\tau$ (meaning, $\tau_{\|\cdot\|} \subseteq \tau$), which happens if and only if there exists some open ball $B$ in $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ (such as maybe $\{x \in X : \|x\| < 1\}$ for example) that is open in $(X, \tau)$ (said different, such that $B \in \tau$).

## Normable spaces {#Normable_spaces}

A [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $(X, \tau)$ is called **normable** if there exists a norm $\| \cdot \|$ on $X$ such that the canonical metric $(x, y) \mapsto \|y-x\|$ induces the topology $\tau$ on $X.$ The following theorem is due to [Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161):[^n1]

**[Kolmogorov's normability criterion](wikipedia://en/55284288)**: A Hausdorff topological vector space is normable if and only if there exists a convex, [von Neumann bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_bounded) neighborhood of $0 \in X.$

A product of a family of normable spaces is normable if and only if only finitely many of the spaces are non-trivial (that is, $\neq \{ 0 \}$).[^n1] Furthermore, the quotient of a normable space $X$ by a closed vector subspace $C$ is normable, and if in addition $X$'s topology is given by a norm $\|\,\cdot,\|$ then the map $X/C \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $x + C \mapsto \inf_{c \in C} \|x + c\|$ is a well defined norm on $X / C$ that induces the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) on $X / C.$[^n2]

Furthermore, $X$ is finite-dimensional if and only if $X^{\prime}_{\sigma}$ is normable (here $X^{\prime}_{\sigma}$ denotes $X^{\prime}$ endowed with the [weak-\* topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology)).

The topology $\tau$ of the [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) $C^{\infty}(K),$ as defined in the article on [spaces of test functions and distributions](wikipedia://en/67789038), is defined by a countable family of norms but it is not a normable space because there does not exist any norm $\|\cdot\|$ on $C^{\infty}(K)$ such that the topology that this norm induces is equal to $\tau.$

Even if a metrizable topological vector space has a topology that is defined by a family of norms, then it may nevertheless still fail to be [normable space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) (meaning that its topology can not be defined by any single norm). An example of such a space is the [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) $C^{\infty}(K),$ whose definition can be found in the article on [spaces of test functions and distributions](wikipedia://en/67789038), because its topology $\tau$ is defined by a countable family of norms but it is not a normable space because there does not exist any norm $\|\cdot\|$ on $C^{\infty}(K)$ such that the topology this norm induces is equal to $\tau.$ In fact, the topology of a [locally convex space](wikipedia://en/518844) $X$ can be a defined by a family of norms on $X$ if and only if there exists at least one continuous norm on $X.$[^n3]

:::

## Linear maps and dual spaces {#Linear_maps_and_dual_spaces}

The most important maps between two normed vector spaces are the [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [linear maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation). Together with these maps, normed vector spaces form a [category](wikipedia://en/5869).

The norm is a continuous function on its vector space. All linear maps between finite-dimensional vector spaces are also continuous.

An *isometry* between two normed vector spaces is a linear map $f$ which preserves the norm (meaning $\|f(\mathbf{v})\| = \|\mathbf{v}\|$ for all vectors $\mathbf{v}$). Isometries are always continuous and [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective). A [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) isometry between the normed vector spaces $V$ and $W$ is called an *isometric isomorphism*, and $V$ and $W$ are called *isometrically isomorphic*. Isometrically isomorphic normed vector spaces are identical for all practical purposes.

When speaking of normed vector spaces, we augment the notion of [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) to take the norm into account. The dual $V^{\prime}$ of a normed vector space $V$ is the space of all *continuous* linear maps from $V$ to the base field (the complexes or the reals) — such linear maps are called "functionals". The norm of a functional $\varphi$ is defined as the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of $|\varphi(\mathbf{v})|$ where $\mathbf{v}$ ranges over all unit vectors (that is, vectors of norm $1$) in $V.$ This turns $V^{\prime}$ into a normed vector space. An important theorem about continuous linear functionals on normed vector spaces is the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860).

## Normed spaces as quotient spaces of seminormed spaces {#Normed_spaces_as_quotient_spaces_of_seminormed_spaces}

The definition of many normed spaces (in particular, [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989)) involves a seminorm defined on a vector space and then the normed space is defined as the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) by the subspace of elements of seminorm zero. For instance, with the [$L^p$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194), the function defined by

$$\|f\|_p = \left( \int |f(x)|^p \;dx \right)^{1/p}$$

is a seminorm on the vector space of all functions on which the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288) on the right hand side is defined and finite. However, the seminorm is equal to zero for any function [supported](wikipedia://en/381013) on a set of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) zero. These functions form a subspace which we "quotient out", making them equivalent to the zero function.

## Finite product spaces {#Finite_product_spaces}

Given $n$ seminormed spaces $\left(X_i, q_i\right)$ with seminorms $q_i : X_i \to \mathbb{R},$ denote the [product space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space) by

$$X := \prod_{i=1}^n X_i$$

where vector addition defined as

$$\left(x_1,\ldots,x_n\right) + \left(y_1,\ldots,y_n\right) := \left(x_1 + y_1, \ldots, x_n + y_n\right)$$

and scalar multiplication defined as

$$\alpha \left(x_1,\ldots,x_n\right) := \left(\alpha x_1, \ldots, \alpha x_n\right).$$

Define a new function $q : X \to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$q\left(x_1,\ldots,x_n\right) := \sum_{i=1}^n q_i\left(x_i\right),$$

which is a seminorm on $X.$ The function $q$ is a norm if and only if all $q_i$ are norms.

More generally, for each real $p \geq 1$ the map $q : X \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$q\left(x_1,\ldots,x_n\right) := \left(\sum_{i=1}^n q_i\left(x_i\right)^p\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}$$

is a semi norm. For each $p$ this defines the same topological space.

A straightforward argument involving elementary linear algebra shows that the only finite-dimensional seminormed spaces are those arising as the product space of a normed space and a space with trivial seminorm. Consequently, many of the more interesting examples and applications of seminormed spaces occur for infinite-dimensional vector spaces.
