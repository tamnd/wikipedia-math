# Pseudorandomness

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudorandomness"}

A **pseudorandom** sequence of numbers is one that appears to be [statistically random](wikipedia://en/2551773), despite having been produced by a completely [deterministic](wikipedia://en/522958) and repeatable process.[^o2] [Pseudorandom number generators](wikipedia://en/45524) are often used in computer programming, as traditional sources of randomness available to humans (such as rolling dice) rely on physical processes not readily available to computer programs, although developments in [hardware random number generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardware_random_number_generator) technology have challenged this.

:::

## Background {#Background}

The generation of random numbers has many uses, such as for [random sampling](wikipedia://en/160361), [Monte Carlo methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_methods), [board games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_game), or [gambling](wikipedia://en/11921). In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), however, most processes, such as gravitational acceleration, are deterministic, meaning that they always produce the same outcome from the same starting point. Some notable exceptions are [radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) and [quantum measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_measurement), which are both modeled as being truly random processes in the underlying physics. Since these processes are not practical sources of random numbers, pseudorandom numbers are used, which ideally have the unpredictability of a truly random sequence, despite being generated by a deterministic process.[^o4]

In many applications, the deterministic process is a [computer algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) called a [pseudorandom number generator](wikipedia://en/45524), which must first be provided with a number called a [random seed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_seed). Since the same seed will yield the same sequence every time, it is important that the seed be well chosen and kept hidden, especially in [security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_security) applications, where the pattern's unpredictability is a critical feature.[^o5]

In some cases where it is important for the sequence to be demonstrably unpredictable, physical sources of random numbers have been used, such as radioactive decay, atmospheric electromagnetic noise harvested from a radio tuned between stations, or intermixed timings of [keystrokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystroke_dynamics).[^o2][^o6] The time investment needed to obtain these numbers leads to a compromise: using some of these physics readings as a seed for a pseudorandom number generator.

## History {#History}

Before modern computing, researchers requiring random numbers would either generate them through various means ([dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice), [cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_cards), [roulette wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roulette),[^o8] etc.) or use existing random number tables.

The first attempt to provide researchers with a ready supply of random digits was in 1927, when the Cambridge University Press published a table of 41,600 digits developed by [L.H.C. Tippett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L.H.C._Tippett). In 1947, the [RAND Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND_Corporation) generated numbers by the electronic simulation of a roulette wheel;[^o8] the results were eventually published in 1955 as *[A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates](wikipedia://en/1354812)*.

## In computational complexity {#In_computational_complexity}

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392), a [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) is **pseudorandom** against a class of adversaries if no adversary from the class can distinguish it from the uniform distribution with significant advantage.[^o10] This notion of pseudorandomness is studied in [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543) and has applications to [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432).

Formally, let *S* and *T* be finite sets and let **F** = {*f*: *S* → *T*} be a class of functions. A [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) **D** over *S* is ε-**pseudorandom** against **F** if for every *f* in **F**, the [statistical distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_variation_distance) between the distributions $f(X)$ and $f(Y)$, where $X$ is sampled from **D** and $Y$ is sampled from the [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28discrete%29) on *S*, is at most ε.

In typical applications, the class **F** describes a model of computation with bounded resources and one is interested in designing distributions **D** with certain properties that are pseudorandom against **F**. The distribution **D** is often specified as the output of a [pseudorandom generator](wikipedia://en/1812809).[^o11]
