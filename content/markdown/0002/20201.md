# Musical notation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Musical notation"}

*Image: Bachlut1.png.* Hand-written musical notation by [J. S. Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Sebastian_Bach) (1685–1750). This is the beginning of the Prelude from the Suite for [Lute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lute) in G minor, BWV 995 (transcription of [Cello Suite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cello_Suites_%28Bach%29) No. 5 in C minor, BWV 1011).

**Musical notation** is any system used to visually represent music. Systems of notation generally represent the elements of a [piece of music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piece_of_music) that are considered important for its performance in the context of a given musical tradition. The process of interpreting musical notation is often referred to as **reading music**.

:::

Distinct methods of notation have been invented throughout history by various cultures. Much information about [ancient music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_music) notation is fragmentary. Even in the same time frames, different styles of music and different cultures use different music notation methods.

For example, [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_music) performers most often use [sheet music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_music) using [staves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staff_%28music%29), [time signatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_signature), [key signatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_signature), and [noteheads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notehead) for writing and deciphering [pieces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_composition). But even so, there are far more systems than just that. For instance, in professional [country music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Country_music), the [Nashville Number System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nashville_Number_System) is the main method, and for [string instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_instrument) such as [guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar), it is quite common for [tablature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tablature) to be used by players.

Musical notation uses ancient and [modern symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_musical_symbols) made upon any media such as stone, [clay tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_tablet), [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus), [parchment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parchment) or [manuscript paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript_paper); printed using a [printing press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press) (c. 1400), a [computer printer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_printer) (c. 1980) or other [printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_engraving) or [modern copying technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photocopier).

Although many ancient cultures used symbols to represent [melodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melody) and [rhythms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm), none of them were particularly comprehensive, which has limited today's understanding of their music. The direct ancestor of the modern [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_culture) system of notation emerged in [medieval Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), in the context of the [Christian Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church)'s attempts to standardize the performance of [plainsong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plainsong) melodies so that chants could be standardized across different areas. Notation [developed further during the Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_music) and [Baroque music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_music) eras. In the [Classical period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_period_%28music%29) (1750–1820) and the [Romantic music era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romantic_music_era) (1820–1900), notation continued to develop as the [technology for musical instruments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_technology_%28mechanical%29) advanced. In the [contemporary classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_classical_music) of the 20th and 21st centuries, music notation has evolved further, with the introduction of [graphical notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_notation_%28music%29) by some modern composers and the use, since the 1980s, of computer-based [scorewriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorewriter) programs for notating music. Music notation has been adapted to many kinds of music, including [classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_music), [popular music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_music), and [traditional music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_music).

## History {#History}

### Ancient Near East {#Ancient_Near_East}

*Image: Hurritische hymne.gif.* A tablet with the Hymn to Nikkal inscribed[^o5]

The earliest form of musical notation can be found in a [cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform_script) tablet that was created at [Nippur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippur), in [Babylonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia) (today's [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq)), in about 1400 BCE. The [tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_tablet) represents fragmentary instructions for performing music, showing it was composed in [harmonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmony) of [thirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) using a [diatonic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diatonic_scale).[^n1]

A tablet from about 1250 BCE shows a more developed form of notation.[^n2] Although the interpretation of the notation system is still controversial, it is clear that the notation indicates the names of strings on a [lyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyre), the [tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_tuning) of which is described in other tablets.[^n3] Research indicates these notations had dual purposes for liturgical and secular musical pieces since music was essential in both religious ceremonies and courtly activities.[^o9] Although they are fragmentary, these tablets represent the earliest notated [melodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melody) found anywhere in the world.[^n4]

*Image: Delphichymn.jpg.* A photograph of the original stone at Delphi containing the second of the two [Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns) to [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo). The music notation is the line of occasional symbols *above* the main, uninterrupted line of Greek lettering.

### Ancient Greece {#Ancient_Greece}

[Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_ancient_Greece) musical notation was in use from at least the 6th century BCE until approximately the 4th century CE; only few complete compositions ([Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph), two Invocations to the Muse by Mesomedes, the Bellermann exercises) and around 40 fragmentary pieces using this notation survive.

The notation for sung music consists of letter symbols for the [pitches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_%28music%29), placed above text syllables. Rhythm is indicated in a rudimentary way only, with long and short symbols. The [Seikilos epitaph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seikilos_epitaph) has been variously dated between the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE.

Three hymns by [Mesomedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesomedes) of [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete) exist in [manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript). The [Delphic Hymns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphic_Hymns), dated to the 2nd century BCE also use this notation, but they are not completely preserved.

Ancient Greek notation appears to have fallen out of use around the time of the [decline of the Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decline_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire).

### Byzantine Empire {#Byzantine_Empire}

*Image: Stavropoleos-anastasimatar-macarie-viena-1823-p11.png.* Byzantine music notation in the first edition (1823) of Macarie Ieromonahul's *anastasimatarion*, a hymnal with daily chant (including resurrection troparia called *apolytikia anastasima*) in [oktoechos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octoechos_%28liturgy%29#Types_of_octoechos_books) order, each section began with the evening psalm 140 (here section of echos protos with Romanian written in Cyrillic script)

[Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) music once included music for court ceremonies, but has only survived as vocal [church music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_music) within various [Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodoxy) traditions of monodic ([monophonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monophony)) chant written down in Byzantine round notation (see Macarie's *[anastasimatarion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octoechos_%28liturgy%29)* with the Greek text translated into [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language) and transliterated into its corresponding [Cyrillic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Cyrillic_alphabet)).[^o13]

Since the 6th century, Greek theoretical categories (*melos*, *genos*, *[harmonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonia)*, *systema*) played a key role in the understanding and transmission of Byzantine music, The tradition of [Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damascus), especially, had a strong impact on the pre-Islamic [Near East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_East) comparable to that of [Persian music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_traditional_music). The earliest evidence are [papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus) fragments of Greek *tropologia*. These fragments present the hymn text following a modal signature or key (like "ΠΛ Α" for *echos plagios protos* or "Β" for *echos devteros*).

Unlike Western notation, Byzantine [neumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neume) used since the 10th century were always related to [modal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_%28music%29) steps (same modal degree, one degree lower, two degrees higher, etc.) in relation to such a clef or modal key ([modal signatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enechema)). Originally this key or the incipit of a common melody was enough to indicate a certain [melodic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melodic_pattern) given within the [echos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echos). Next to [ekphonetic notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekphonetic_notation), only used in [lectionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lectionary) to indicate formulas used during scriptural lessons, melodic notation developed not earlier than between the 9th and the 10th century, when a *theta* (θ), *oxeia* (/) or *diple* (//) were written under a certain syllable of the text whenever a longer [melisma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melisma) was expected. This primitive form was called "theta" or "diple notation".

The evolution of this notation can be observed in Greek monastic chant books like those of the [*sticherarion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sticheron) and the [*heirmologion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irmologion). Chartres notation was used on [Mount Athos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Athos) and Constantinople, and Coislin notation was used within the patriarchates of Jerusalem and Alexandria, while another gestic notation was originally used for the *asmatikon* (choir book) and *kontakarion* (book of the soloist or monophonaris) of the Constantinopolitan cathedral rite. The earliest books which have survived, are "kondakars" in Slavonic translation which already show a notation system known as [Kondakarian notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_music#The_Kievan_Rus'_and_the_earliest_manuscripts_of_the_cathedral_rite).[^o14] Like the [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet), notational signs are ordered left to right (though the direction could be adapted, like in certain [Syriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_alphabet) manuscripts). The question of rhythm was entirely based on *cheironomia* (the interpretation of so-called great signs which derived from different chant books). These great signs (μεγάλα σῃμάδια) indicated well-known melodic phrases given by gestures of the [choirleaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor_%28Christianity%29) of the cathedral rite. They existed once as part of an oral tradition, developed into Kondakarian notation and were integrated, during the 13th century, into Byzantine round notation as a kind of universal notation system.[^o15]

Today, the main difference between Western and Eastern neumes is that Eastern notation symbols are "differential" rather than absolute, i.e., they indicate pitch steps (rising, falling or at the same step), and the musicians must deduce the specific intervals based on the musical context and their current pitch. These step symbols themselves, or better "phonic neumes", resemble brush strokes and are colloquially called *gántzoi* ('hooks') in [modern Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek).

Notes as pitch classes or modal keys (usually memorised by modal signatures) are represented in written form only between these neumes (in manuscripts usually written in red ink). In modern notation they simply serve as optional reminders, with modal and tempo directions added when necessary. In Papadic notation medial signatures usually meant a temporary change into another *echos*.

The so-called "great signs" were once related to cheironomic signs; according to modern interpretations they are understood as embellishments and microtonal attractions (pitch changes smaller than a [semitone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone)), both essential in Byzantine chant.[^o16]

*Image: ChrysanthosTable.png.* Chrysanthos's *Kanonion* with a comparison between Ancient Greek tetraphonia (column 1), Western [Solfeggio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solfeggio), the *Papadic Parallage* (ascending: column 3 and 4; descending: column 5 and 6) according to the *trochos system*, and his heptaphonic *parallage* according to the New Method (syllables in the fore-last and *martyriai* in the last column)[^n5])

Since [Chrysanthos of Madytos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysanthos_of_Madytos) there are seven standard note names used for "solfège" (*parallagē*) *pá, vú, g<sup>h</sup>á, d<sup>h</sup>i, ké, zō, nē*, while the older practice still used the four *enechemata* or intonation formulas of the four echoi given by the modal signatures, the authentic or *kyrioi* in ascending direction, and the plagal or *plagioi* in descending direction ([Papadic Octoechos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papadic_Octoechos)).[^o18] With exception of *vú and zō,* they do roughly correspond to Western solmization syllables as *re, mi, fa, sol, la, si, do*. Byzantine music uses the eight natural, non-tempered scales whose elements were identified by *Ēkhoi*, "sounds", exclusively, and therefore the absolute pitch of each note may slightly vary each time, depending on the particular *Ēkhos* used. Byzantine notation is still used in many Orthodox Churches. Sometimes cantors also use transcriptions into Western or Kievan staff notation while adding non-notatable embellishment material from memory and "sliding" into the natural scales from experience. However, even in modern neume editions since the reform of Chrysanthos, many details rely on oral tradition passed down by traditional masters.

### 13th-century Near East {#13th-century_Near_East}

In 1252, [Safi al-Din al-Urmawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safi_al-Din_al-Urmawi) developed a form of musical notation where [rhythms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm) were represented by [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) shapes. Many subsequent scholars of rhythm have sought to develop graphical geometrical notations. For example, a similar geometric system was published in 1987 by Kjell Gustafson, whose method represents a rhythm as a two-dimensional graph.[^n6] Rhythmic notation during its early stages developed Eastern musical traditions while simultaneously establishing concepts that Western music used to build its notation systems later on.[^o21]

### Early Europe {#Early_Europe}

*Image: Head of Christ1.jpg.* Music notation from an early 14th-century English [Missal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missal)

The scholar and music theorist [Isidore of Seville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Seville), while writing in the early 7th century, wrote that "unless sounds are held by the memory of man, they perish, because they cannot be written down."[^n7] By the middle of the 9th century, however, a form of neumatic notation began to develop in monasteries in Europe as a [mnemonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemonic) device for [Gregorian chant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_chant), using symbols known as [neumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neume); the earliest surviving musical notation of this type is in the *Musica Disciplina* of [Aurelian of Réôme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurelian_of_Réôme), from about 850. There are scattered survivals from the [Iberian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iberian_Peninsula) before this time, of a type of notation known as [Visigothic neumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neume), but its few surviving fragments have not yet been deciphered.[^n8] The problem with this notation was that it only showed melodic contours and consequently the music could not be read by someone who did not know the music already.

*Image: EarlyMusicNotation.JPG.* Early music notation in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin), clearly marking neumes (date unknown, [Florentian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence,_Italy))

Notation had developed far enough to notate melody, but there was still no system for notating rhythm. A mid-13th-century treatise, *[De Mensurabili Musica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Mensurabili_Musica)*, explains a set of six [rhythmic modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythmic_modes) that were in use at the time,[^n9] although it is not clear how they were formed. These rhythmic modes were all in triple time and rather limited the rhythm in chant to six different repeating patterns. This was a flaw seen by German music theorist [Franco of Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_of_Cologne) and summarised as part of his treatise *Ars Cantus Mensurabilis* (the art of measured chant, or [mensural notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mensural_notation)). He suggested that individual notes could have their own rhythms represented by the shape of the note. Not until the 14th century did something like the present system of fixed note lengths arise. The use of regular measures (bars) became commonplace by the end of the 17th century.

The founder of what is now considered the standard music staff was [Guido d'Arezzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo),[^n10] an Italian Benedictine monk who lived from about 991 until after 1033. He taught the use of [solmization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solmization) syllables based on a hymn to [Saint John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_John_the_Baptist), which begins [*Ut queant laxis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ut_Queant_Laxis) and was written by the [Lombard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombards) historian [Paul the Deacon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_the_Deacon). The first stanza is:

1. ***Ut****queant laxis*
1. ***re****sonare fibris*
1. ***Mi****ra gestorum*
1. ***fa****muli tuorum*,
1. ***Sol****ve polluti*
1. **la**bii reatum,
1. **S**ancte **I**ohannes.

Guido used the first syllable of each line, *Ut*, *Re*, *Mi*, *Fa*, *Sol*, *La*, and *Si*, to read notated music in terms of [hexachords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guidonian_hand#Hexachord_in_Middle_Ages); they were not note names, and each could, depending on context, be applied to any note. In the 17th century, *Ut* was changed in most countries except France to the easily singable, open syllable Do, believed to have been taken either from the name of the Italian theorist [Giovanni Battista **Do**ni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Doni), or from the Latin word ***Do****minus*, meaning *Lord*.[^n11]

Christian monks developed the first forms of modern European musical notation in order to standardize liturgy throughout the worldwide Church,[^n12] and an enormous body of religious music has been composed for it through the ages. This led directly to the emergence and development of European classical music, and its many derivatives. The [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque) style, which encompassed music, art, and architecture, was particularly encouraged by the post-Reformation Catholic Church as such forms offered a means of religious expression that was stirring and emotional, intended to stimulate religious fervor.[^n13]

## Modern staff notation {#Modern_staff_notation}

*Image: Chopin Prelude 7.png.* An example of modern musical notation: Prelude, Op. 28, No. 7, by [Frédéric Chopin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frédéric_Chopin) Chopin Prelude 7.mid

Modern music notation is used by musicians of many different genres throughout the world. The staff (or stave, in British English) consists of 5 parallel horizontal lines that act as a framework upon which pitches are indicated by placing oval note-heads on (i.e. crossing) the staff lines, between the lines (*i.e.* in the spaces) or above and below the staff using small additional lines called [ledger lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ledger_line). Notation is read from left to right, which makes setting music for right-to-left scripts difficult. The **pitch** of a note is indicated by the vertical position of the note-head within the staff, and can be modified by [accidentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accidental_%28music%29). The **duration** (note length or [note value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_value)) is indicated by the form of the note-head or with the addition of a note-stem plus beams or flags. A stemless hollow oval is a [whole note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_note) or semibreve, a hollow rectangle or stemless hollow oval with one or two vertical lines on both sides is a [double whole note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_whole_note) or breve. A stemmed hollow oval is a [half note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_note) or minim. Solid ovals always use stems, and can indicate [quarter notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_note) (crotchets) or, with added beams or flags, smaller subdivisions. Additional symbols such as [dots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dotted_note) and [ties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tie_%28music%29) can lengthen the duration of a note.

A staff of written music generally begins with a [clef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clef), which indicates the pitch-range of the staff. The [treble clef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treble_clef) or G clef was originally a letter G and it identifies the second line up on the five-line staff as the note G above middle C. The [bass clef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_clef) or F clef identifies the second line down as the note F below middle C. While the treble and bass clef are the most widely used, other clefs, which identify middle C, are used for some instruments, such as the [alto clef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alto_clef) (for [viola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola) and [alto trombone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alto_trombone)) and the [tenor clef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenor_clef) (used for some [cello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cello), [bassoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bassoon), [tenor trombone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trombone), and [double bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bass) music). Some instruments use mainly one clef, such as violin and flute which use [treble clef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treble_clef), and [double bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bass) and [tuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuba) which use [bass clef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_clef). Some instruments, such as [piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano) and [pipe organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipe_organ), regularly use both treble and bass clefs.

Following the clef, the [key signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_signature) is a group of 0 to 7 [sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp_%28music%29) ([♯](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp_%28music%29)) or [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_%28music%29) ([♭](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_%28music%29)) signs placed on the staff to indicate the [key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_%28music%29) of the piece or song by specifying that certain notes are sharp or flat throughout the piece, unless otherwise indicated with [accidentals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accidental_%28music%29) added before certain notes. When a flat ([♭](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_%28music%29)) sign is placed before a note, the pitch of the note is lowered by one semitone. Similarly, a sharp sign ([♯](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp_%28music%29)) raises the pitch by one semitone. For example, a sharp on the note D would raise it to [D♯](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D♯_%28musical_note%29) while a flat would lower it to [D♭](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D♭_%28musical_note%29). [Double sharps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_sharp) and [double flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_flat) are less common, but they are used. A double sharp is placed before a note to make it two semitones higher, a double flat makes it two semitones lower. A [natural sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sign) placed before a note renders that note in its "natural" form, which means that any sharp or flat applied to that note from the key signature or an accidental is cancelled. Sometimes a [courtesy accidental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtesy_accidental) is used in music where it is not technically required, to remind the musician of what pitch is required.

Following the key signature is the [time signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_signature). The time signature typically consists of two numbers, with one of the most common being time. The top "4" indicates that there are four beats per measure (also called [bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_%28music%29)). The bottom "4" indicates that each of those beats are quarter notes. Measures divide the piece into groups of [beats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_%28music%29), and the time signatures specify those groupings. time is used so often that it is also called "[common time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_time)", and it may be indicated with commontime rather than numbers. Other frequently used time signatures are time (three beats per bar, with each beat being a quarter note); time (two beats per bar, with each beat being a quarter note); time (six beats per bar, with each beat being an eighth note) and time (twelve beats per bar, with each beat being an eighth note; in practice, the eighth notes are typically put into four groups of three eighth notes. time is a [compound time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_time) type of time signature). Many other time signatures exist, such as time or time.

Many short [classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_music) pieces from the [Classical era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_period_%28music%29) and songs from [traditional music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_music) and [popular music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_music) are in one time signature for much or all of the piece. Music from the [Romantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romantic_music) era and later, particularly [contemporary classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_classical_music) and [rock music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_music) genres such as [progressive rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_rock) and the [hardcore punk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardcore_punk) subgenre [mathcore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathcore), may use [mixed meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_meter); songs or pieces change from one meter to another, for example alternating between bars of time and time.

Directions to the player regarding matters such as [tempo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempo) (e.g., [*andante*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andante_%28tempo%29)) and [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28music%29) (e.g., *[forte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forte_%28music%29)*) appear above or below the staff. Terms indicating the [musical expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_expression) or "feel" of a song or piece are indicated at the beginning of the piece and at any points where the mood changes (e.g., *gelassen*)

For vocal music, lyrics are written near the pitches of the melody. Since standard musical notation runs from left to right, when the lyrics are in a language that is written from [right to left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidirectional_text), such Arabic or Hebrew, the general flow of the text is from left to right, but each syllable is written from right to left. There were also attempts to write scores in such languages fully from right to left, for example by the [Abraham Zevi Idelsohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Zevi_Idelsohn), who published Hebrew songbooks[^o32] and by the American [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers) educator Rolla Foley, who published a collection of Arabic songs.[^o33]

For short pauses (breaths), [retakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retake) (retakes are indicated with a ' mark) are added.

In music for [ensembles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_ensemble), a "[score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_music)" shows music for all players together, with the staves for the different instruments and/or voices stacked vertically. The [conductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conducting) uses the score while leading an [orchestra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchestra), [concert band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concert_band), [choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir) or other large ensemble. Individual performers in an ensemble play from "parts" which contain only the music played by an individual musician. A score can be constructed from a complete set of parts and vice versa. The process was laborious and time consuming when parts were hand-copied from the score, but since the development of [scorewriter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scorewriter) computer software in the 1980s, a score stored electronically can have parts automatically prepared by the program and printed out quickly and inexpensively using a computer printer.

### Variations on staff notation {#Variations_on_staff_notation}

*Image: Lead-sheet-wikipedia.svg.* A lead sheet

*Image: Chord chart.svg.* A chord chart. Chord chart.mid

- [Percussion notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percussion_notation) conventions are varied because of the wide range of percussion instruments. Percussion instruments are generally grouped into two categories: pitched (e.g. [glockenspiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glockenspiel) or [tubular bells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tubular_bells)) and non-pitched (e.g. [bass drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_drum) and [snare drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snare_drum)). The notation of non-pitched percussion instruments is less standardized. Pitched instruments use standard Western classical notation for the pitches and rhythms. In general, notation for unpitched percussion uses the five line staff, with different lines and spaces representing different [drum kit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_kit) instruments. Standard Western rhythmic notation is used to indicate the rhythm.
- [Figured bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figured_bass) notation originated in [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque_music) [*basso continuo*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figured_bass) parts. It is also used extensively in [accordion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accordion) notation. The bass notes of the music are conventionally notated, along with numbers and other signs that determine which chords the harpsichordist, organist or lutenist should improvise. It does not, however, specify the exact pitches of the harmony, leaving that for the performer to improvise.
- A [lead sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead_sheet) specifies only the melody, lyrics and harmony, using one staff with [chord symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_notation) placed above and lyrics below. It is used to capture the essential elements of a [popular song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_music) without specifying how the song should be arranged or performed.[^o35]
- A [chord chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_chart) or "chart" contains little or no melodic or voice-leading information at all, but provides basic harmonic information about the [chord progression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chord_progression). Some chord charts also contain rhythmic information, indicated using [slash notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_notation) for full beats and rhythmic notation for rhythms. This is the most common kind of written music used by professional [session musicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Session_musician) playing [jazz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz) or other forms of [popular music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_popular_music_genres) and is intended primarily for the [rhythm section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_section) (usually containing [piano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano), [guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar), [bass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bass_guitar) and [drums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum)).
- Simpler chord charts for songs may contain only the chord changes, placed above the lyrics where they occur. Such charts depend on prior knowledge of the melody, and are used as reminders in performance or informal [group singing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_singing). Some chord charts intended for [rhythm section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_section) accompanists contain only the chord progression.
- The [shape note](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_note) system is found in some church hymnals, [sheet music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_music), and song books, especially in the [Southern United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_United_States). Instead of the customary elliptical note head, note heads of various shapes are used to show the position of the note on the major scale. [*The Sacred Harp*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Harp) is one of the most popular tune books using shape notes.

## In various countries {#In_various_countries}

### Korea {#Korea}

*Image: 시용향악보 (時用鄕樂譜).jpg.* *Jeongganbo musical notation system*

*Jeongganbo* is a traditional musical notation system created during the time of [Sejong the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sejong_the_Great). It was the first East Asian system to represent rhythm, pitch, and time.[^n14][^o39] Among various kinds of Korean traditional music, *Jeong-gan-bo* targets a particular genre, *Jeong-ak* (정악, 正樂).

*Jeong-gan-bo* specifies the pitch by writing the note's name down in a box called *jeong-gan*. One *jeong-gan* is one beat each, and it can be split into two, three or more to hold half beats, quarter beats, and more.

Also, there are many markings indicating things such as ornaments. Most of these were later created by Ki-su Kim.

### India {#India}

*Image: Bhat notation1.jpg.* Indian music, early 20th century.

The *[Samaveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samaveda)* text (1200 BCE – 1000 BCE) contains what are probably the world's oldest surviving melodies.[^o41] The musical notation is written usually immediately above, sometimes within, the line of *Samaveda* text, either in syllabic or numerical form depending on the Samavedic *Sakha* (school).[^o42] The Indian scholar and musical theorist [Pingala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingala) (c. 200 BCE), in his *Chanda Sutra*, used marks denoting long and short syllables to indicate meters in Sanskrit poetry.

A rock inscription circa 7th–8th century CE at [Kudumiyanmalai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kudumiyanmalai), Tamil Nadu contains an early example of a musical notation. It was first identified and published by archaeologist/epigraphist [D. R. Bhandarkar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._R._Bhandarkar).[^n15] Written in the *Pallava-grantha* script of the 7th century, it contains 38 horizontal lines of notation inscribed on a rectangular rock face (dimensions of around 13 by 14 feet). Each line of the notation contains 64 characters (characters representing musical notes), written in groups of four notes. The basic characters for the seven notes, *sa*, *ri*, *ga*, *ma*, *pa*, *dha*, *ni*, are seen to be suffixed with the vowels *a*, *i*, *u*, *e*. For example, in the place of *sa*, any one of *sa*, *si*, *su* or *se* is used. Similarly, in place of *ri*, any one of *ra*, *ri*, *ru* or *re* is used. Horizontal lines divide the notation into 7 sections. Each section contains 4 to 7 lines of notation, with a title indicating its musical 'mode'. These modes may have been popular from at least the 6th century CE and were incorporated into the Indian *raga* system that developed later. But some of the unusual features seen in this notation have been given several non-conclusive interpretations by scholars.[^n16]

In the notation of Indian *[rāga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rāga)*, a solfege-like system called [*sargam*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swara) is used. As in Western solfege, there are names for the seven basic pitches of a major scale (*Shadja*, *Rishabha*, *Gandhara*, *Madhyama*, *Panchama*, *Dhaivata* and *Nishada*, usually shortened to *Sa*, *Re*, *Ga*, *Ma*, *Pa*, *Dha*, *Ni*). The tonic of any scale is named *Sa*, and the dominant *Pa*. *Sa* is fixed in any scale, and *Pa* is fixed at a fifth above it (a [Pythagorean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_tuning) fifth rather than an [equal-tempered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_temperament) fifth). These two notes are known as *achala swar* ('fixed notes').

Each of the other five notes, *Re*, *Ga*, *Ma*, *Dha* and *Ni*, can take a 'regular' (*shuddha*) pitch, which is equivalent to its pitch in a standard major scale (thus, *shuddha Re*, the second degree of the scale, is a whole-step higher than *Sa*), or an altered pitch, either a half-step above or half-step below the shuddha pitch. *Re*, *Ga*, *Dha* and *Ni* all have altered partners that are a half-step lower (*komal* or "flat") (thus, *komal Re* is a half-step higher than *Sa*).

*Ma* has an altered partner that is a half-step higher (*teevra* or "sharp") (thus, *teevra Ma* is an augmented fourth above *Sa*). *Re*, *Ga*, *Ma*, *Dha* and *Ni* are called vikrut swar ('movable notes'). In the written system of Indian notation devised by Ravi Shankar, the pitches are represented by Western letters. Capital letters are used for the *achala swar*, and for the higher variety of all the *vikrut swar*. Lowercase letters are used for the lower variety of the *vikrut swar*.

Other systems exist for non-twelve-tone [equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_temperament) and non-Western music, such as the Indian *[Swaralipi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaralipi)*.

### Russia {#Russia}

*Image: Example of hooks and banners notation.PNG.* An example of Znamenny notation with so-called "red marks", Russia, 1884. "Thy [Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus) we honour, oh Lord, and Thy holy Resurrection we praise."

*Image: Kryuki.jpg.* Hand-drawn *[lubok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubok)* featuring 'hook and banner notation'

Znamenny Chant is a singing tradition used in the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church) which uses a "hook and banner" notation. Znamenny Chant is [unison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unison), [melismatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melisma) [liturgical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgy) singing that has its own specific notation, called the *stolp* notation. The symbols used in the *stolp* notation are called *ru* (крюки, 'hooks') or *ru* (знамёна, 'banners'). Often the names of the signs are used to refer to the *stolp* notation. *Znamenny* melodies are part of a system, consisting of Eight Modes (intonation structures; called *glasy*); the melodies are characterized by fluency and well-balancedness.[^n17] There exist several types of Znamenny Chant: the so-called *Stolpovoy*, *Malyj* (Little) and *Bolshoy* (Great) Znamenny Chant. Ruthenian Chant (*[Prostopinije](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostopinije)*) is sometimes considered a sub-division of the Znamenny Chant tradition, with the [Muscovite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Duchy_of_Moscow) Chant (Znamenny Chant proper) being the second branch of the same musical continuum.

Znamenny Chants are not written with notes (the so-called linear notation), but with special signs, called *Znamëna* (Russian for "marks", "banners") or *Kryuki* ("hooks"), as some shapes of these signs resemble hooks. Each sign may include the following components: a large black hook or a black stroke, several smaller black 'points' and 'commas' and lines near the hook or crossing the hook. Some signs may mean only one note, some 2 to 4 notes, and some a whole melody of more than 10 notes with a complicated rhythmic structure. The *stolp* notation was developed in [Kievan Rus'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kievan_Rus') as an [East Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Slavs) refinement of the [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_music) [neumatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neumatic) musical notation.

The most notable feature of this notation system is that it records transitions of the melody, rather than [notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_note). The signs also represent the mood and indicate how this part of melody is to be sung (tempo, strength, devotion, meekness, etc.). Every sign has its own name and also features as a spiritual symbol. For example, there is a specific sign, called "little dove" (Russian: голубчик (*golubchik*)), which represents two rising sounds, but which is also a symbol of the [Holy Ghost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Ghost). Gradually, the system became more and more complicated. This system was also ambiguous, so that almost no one, except the most trained and educated singers, could sing an unknown melody at sight. The signs only helped to reproduce the melody, not coding it in an unambiguous way. (See Byzantine Empire)

### China {#China}

*Image: Qinnotation.png.* Chinese [*Guqin* notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guqin_notation), 1425

The earliest known examples of text referring to music in China are inscriptions on musical instruments found in the Tomb of the [Marquis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquess) Yi of Zeng (d. 433 BCE). Sets of 41 chimestones and 65 bells bore lengthy inscriptions concerning pitches, scales, and transposition. The bells still sound the pitches that their inscriptions refer to. Although no notated musical compositions were found, the inscriptions indicate that the system was sufficiently advanced to allow for musical notation. Two systems of pitch nomenclature existed, one for relative pitch and one for absolute pitch. For relative pitch, a [solmization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solmization) system was used.[^n18]

[*Gongche* notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gongche_notation) used Chinese characters for the names of the scale.

### Japan {#Japan}

*Image: Tempyo Biwa Fu.jpg.* *Tempyō Biwa Fu* 天平琵琶譜 (circa 738 AD), musical notation for [Biwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biwa). ([Shōsōin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shōsōin), at Nara, Japan)

Japanese music is highly diversified, and therefore requires various systems of notation. In Japanese *[shakuhachi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakuhachi)* music, for example, glissandos and timbres are often more significant than distinct pitches, whereas *[taiko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiko)* notation focuses on discrete strokes.

[Ryukyuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryukyu_Islands) [sanshin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanshin) music uses *[kunkunshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunkunshi)*, a notation system of [kanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanji) with each character corresponding to a finger position on a particular string.

### Indonesia {#Indonesia}

Notation plays a relatively minor role in the oral traditions of [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia). However, in [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java) and [Bali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bali), several systems were developed starting in the late 19th century, initially for archival purposes. Today the most widespread are cipher notations ("not *angka*" in the broadest sense) in which the pitches are represented with the numbers 1 to 7, with 1 corresponding to either the highest note of a particular octave, as in [Sundanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundanese_script) [gamelan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan), or the lowest, as in the [*kepatihan* notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepatihan_notation) of [Javanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java) [gamelan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan).

Notes in the ranges outside the central octave are represented with one or more dots above or below each number. For the most part, these cipher notations are mainly used to notate the skeletal melody (the *[balungan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balungan)*) and vocal parts ([*gerongan*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerong)), although transcriptions of the elaborative instrument variations are sometimes used for analysis and teaching. Drum parts are notated with a system of symbols largely based on letters representing the vocables used to learn and remember drumming patterns; these symbols are typically laid out in a grid underneath the skeletal melody for a specific or generic piece.

The symbols used for drum notation (as well as the vocables represented) are highly variable from place to place and performer to performer. In addition to these current systems, two older notations used a kind of staff: the [Solonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surakarta) script could capture the flexible rhythms of the [*pesinden*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sindhen) with a squiggle on a horizontal staff, while in [Yogyakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogyakarta), a ladder-like vertical staff allowed notation of the *balungan* by dots and also included important drum strokes. In Bali, there are a few books published of *[Gamelan gender wayang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamelan_gender_wayang)* pieces employing alphabetical notation in the old Balinese script.

Composers and scholars both Indonesian and foreign have also mapped the *[slendro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slendro)* and *[pelog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelog)* [tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_tuning) systems of gamelan onto the Western staff, with and without various symbols for [microtones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microtonal_music). The Dutch composer [Ton de Leeuw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ton_de_Leeuw) also invented a three-line staff for his composition *Gending*. However, these systems do not enjoy widespread use.

In the second half of the twentieth century, Indonesian musicians and scholars extended cipher notation to other oral traditions, and a [diatonic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diatonic_and_chromatic) cipher notation has become common for notating Western-related genres (church hymns, popular songs, and so forth). Unlike the cipher notation for gamelan music, which uses a "fixed-Do" (that is, 1 always corresponds to the same pitch, within the natural variability of gamelan tuning), Indonesian diatonic cipher notation is "movable-Do" notation, so scores must indicate which pitch corresponds to the number 1 (for example, "1=C").

### Israel {#Israel}

*Image: Example of biblical Hebrew trope.svg.* Example of biblical Hebrew Trope

Ancient Jewish texts include a series of marks assigning musical cantillation notes. Known in Hebrew as *Ta'amim* and Yiddish as Trope, there are records of these marks from the 6th and 7th centuries, having been passed down as a tradition for Jewish prayers and texts. Traditionally a series of marks written above and around the accompanying Hebrew texts, Ta’amim marks represent a short musical motif. Ta’amim tells the reader how to sing or chant the words aloud. It acts as a guide for the sentence structure, showing which words connect and where pauses go, and they mark which syllable gets the emphasis. Throughout the Jewish diaspora there are variations in the accompanying melodies.

## Other systems and practices {#Other_systems_and_practices}

### Cipher notation {#Cipher_notation}

*Image: AmazingGraceNumberedMusicalNotation.png.* "[Amazing Grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazing_Grace)" in numbered notation.

Cipher notation systems assigning Arabic numerals to the [major scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_scale) degrees have been used at least since the Iberian organ tablatures of the 16th-century and include such exotic adaptations as *[Siffernotskrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siffernotskrift)*. The one most widely in use today is the Chinese *Jianpu*, discussed in the [main article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numbered_musical_notation). Numerals can also be assigned to different scale systems, as in the Javanese *[kepatihan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepatihan)* notation described above.

### Solfège {#Solfège}

Solfège is a way of assigning syllables to names of the musical scale. In order, they are today: *Do*, *Re*, *Mi*, *Fa*, *Sol*, *La*, *Ti*, *Do'* (for the octave). The classic variation is: *Do*, *Re*, *Mi*, *Fa*, *Sol*, *La*, *Si*, *Do'*. The first Western system of functional names for the musical notes was introduced by [Guido of Arezzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_of_Arezzo) (c. 991 – after 1033), using the beginning syllables of the first six musical lines of the Latin hymn *[Ut queant laxis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ut_queant_laxis)*. The original sequence was *Ut*, *Re*, *Mi*, *Fa*, *Sol*, *La*, where each verse started a scale note higher. "Ut" later became "Do". The equivalent syllables used in Indian music are: *Sa*, *Re*, *Ga*, *Ma*, *Pa*, *Dha*, *Ni*. See also: [solfège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solfège), [sargam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swara), [Kodály hand signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodály_method#Hand_signs).

[Tonic sol-fa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonic_sol-fa) is a type of notation using the initial letters of solfège.

### Letter notation {#Letter_notation}

The notes of the 12-tone scale can be written by their letter names A–G, possibly with a trailing [accidental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accidental_%28music%29), such as Asharp or Bflat.

#### ABC {#ABC}

[ABC notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_notation) is a compact format using plain text characters, readable by computers and by humans. More than 100,000 tunes are now transcribed in this format.[^o62]

### Tablature {#Tablature}

Tablature was first used in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_music) for organ music and later in the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_music) for [lute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lute) music.[^n19] In most lute tablatures, a staff is used, but instead of pitch values, the lines of the staff represent the strings of the instrument. The [frets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fret) to finger are written on each line, indicated by letters or numbers. Rhythm is written separately with one or another variation of standard note values indicating the duration of the fastest moving part. Few seem to have remarked on the fact that tablature combines in one notation system both the physical and technical requirements of play (the lines and symbols on them and in relation to each other representing the actual performance actions) with the unfolding of the music itself (the lines of tablature taken horizontally represent the actual temporal unfolding of the music). In later periods, lute and guitar music was written with standard notation. Tablature caught interest again in the late 20th century for popular [guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitar) music and other fretted instruments, being easy to transcribe and share over the internet in [ASCII format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCII_tab).

### Piano-roll-based notations {#Piano-roll-based_notations}

Some chromatic systems have been created taking advantage of the layout of black and white keys of the standard piano keyboard. The "staff" is most widely referred to as "[piano roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_roll#In_digital_audio_workstations)", created by extending the black and white piano keys.

#### Klavar notation {#Klavar_notation}

Klavarskribo (sometimes shortened to **klavar**) is a music notation system that was introduced in 1931 by the [Dutchman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands) Cornelis Pot. The name means "keyboard writing" in [Esperanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esperanto). It differs from conventional music notation in a number of ways and is intended to be easily readable. Many klavar readers are from the Netherlands.

### Chromatic staff notations {#Chromatic_staff_notations}

Over the past three centuries, hundreds of music notation systems have been proposed as alternatives to traditional Western music notation. Many of these systems seek to improve upon traditional notation by using a "chromatic staff" in which each of the 12 pitch classes has its own unique place on the staff. An example is Jacques-Daniel Rochat's [*Dodeka* music notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodeka_music_notation).[^o68][^n20] These notation systems do not require the use of standard key signatures, accidentals, or clef signs. They also represent interval relationships more consistently and accurately than traditional notation, e.g. major 3rds appear wider than minor 3rds. Many of these systems are described and illustrated in Gardner Read's *Source Book of Proposed Music Notation Reforms*.

### Graphic notation {#Graphic_notation}

The term "graphic notation" refers to the contemporary use of non-traditional symbols and text to convey information about the performance of a piece of music. Composers such as [Johanna Beyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johanna_Beyer), [Christian Wolff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Wolff_%28composer%29), [Carmen Barradas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmen_Barradas), [Earle Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earle_Brown), [Yoko Ono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoko_Ono), [Anthony Braxton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Braxton), [John Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage), [Morton Feldman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morton_Feldman), [Cathy Berberian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathy_Berberian), [Graciela Castillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graciela_Castillo), [Krzysztof Penderecki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krzysztof_Penderecki), [Cornelius Cardew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Cardew), [Pauline Oliveros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Oliveros) and [Roger Reynolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Reynolds) are among the early generation of composers who innovated forms of graphic notation beginning in the mid-twentieth century.[^o71] The book *[Notations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notations)*, edited by John Cage and [Alison Knowles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alison_Knowles) and published by Something Else Press in 1969, compiles many examples of this kind of notation.

### Simplified music notation {#Simplified_music_notation}

[Simplified music notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Music_Notation) is an alternative form of musical notation designed to make [sight-reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sight_reading) easier. It is based on [classical staff notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_musical_symbols), but incorporates [sharps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp_%28music%29) and [flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_%28music%29) into the shape of the [note heads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_heads). [Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_note) such as [double sharps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp_%28music%29) and [double flats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_%28music%29) are written at the [pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_%28music%29) they are actually played at, but preceded by [symbols](wikipedia://en/37673) called "history signs" that show they have been [transposed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28music%29).

### Modified Stave Notation {#Modified_Stave_Notation}

[Modified Stave Notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_Stave_Notation) (MSN) is an alternative way of notating music for people who cannot easily read ordinary musical notation even if it is enlarged.

### Parsons code {#Parsons_code}

Parsons code is used to encode music so that it can be easily searched.

### Braille music {#Braille_music}

Braille music is a complete, well developed, and internationally accepted musical notation system that has symbols and notational conventions quite independent of print music notation. It is linear in nature, similar to a printed language and different from the two-dimensional nature of standard printed music notation. To a degree, Braille music resembles musical markup languages[^o76] such as [MusicXML](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MusicXML)[^o77] or [NIFF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notation_Interchange_File_Format).

### Integer notation {#Integer_notation}

In [integer notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_notation), or the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) model of pitch, all [pitch classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_class) and [intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) between pitch classes are designated using the numbers 0 through 11.

### Rap notation {#Rap_notation}

The standard form of rap notation is the "flow diagram", where [rappers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rappers) line up their lyrics underneath "beat numbers".[^n21] Hip-hop scholars also make use of the same flow diagrams that rappers use: the books *How to Rap* and *How to Rap 2* extensively use the diagrams to explain rap's triplets, flams, rests, rhyme schemes, runs of rhyme, and breaking rhyme patterns, among other techniques.[^n22] Similar systems are used by musicologists Adam Krims in his book *Rap Music and the Poetics of Identity*[^n23] and Kyle Adams in his work on rap's flow.[^n24] As rap usually revolves around a strong 4/4 beat,[^n25] with certain syllables aligned to the beat, all the notational systems have a similar structure: they all have four beat numbers at the top of the diagram, so that syllables can be written in-line with the beat.[^n25]

### Tin Whistle Fingering Charts {#Tin_Whistle_Fingering_Charts}

It is used for six-hole woodwind instruments, basically for Irish folk songs. Tin whistle tabs are particularly useful for those unfamiliar with sheet music notation.

## Perspectives of musical notation in composition and musical performance {#Perspectives_of_musical_notation_in_composition_and_musical_performance}

According to Philip Tagg and [Richard Middleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Middleton_%28musicologist%29), musicology and to a degree European-influenced musical practice suffer from a "notational centricity", a methodology slanted by the characteristics of notation.[^n26][^n27] A variety of 20th- and 21st-century composers have dealt with this problem, either by adapting standard Western musical notation or by using graphic notation. These include [George Crumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Crumb), [Luciano Berio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luciano_Berio), [Krzysztof Penderecki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krzysztof_Penderecki), [Earl Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earle_Brown), [John Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage), [Witold Lutoslawski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witold_Lutoslawski), and others.[^n28][^n29]
