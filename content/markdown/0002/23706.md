# Primitive notion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Primitive notion"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), and [formal systems](wikipedia://en/396102), a **primitive notion** is a concept that is not defined in terms of previously defined concepts. It is often motivated informally, usually by an appeal to [intuition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_%28knowledge%29) or taken to be [self-evident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-evident). In an [axiomatic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_theory), relations between primitive notions are restricted by [axioms](wikipedia://en/928).[^o2] Some authors refer to the latter as "defining" primitive notions by one or more axioms, but this can be misleading. Formal theories cannot dispense with primitive notions, under pain of [infinite regress](wikipedia://en/3004668).

:::

For example, in contemporary geometry, *[point](wikipedia://en/593693)*, *[line](wikipedia://en/946975)*, and *contains* are some primitive notions.

## Details {#Details}

[Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) explained the role of primitive notions as follows:[^o4]

When we set out to construct a given discipline, we distinguish, first of all, a certain small group of expressions of this discipline that seem to us to be immediately understandable; the expressions in this group we call PRIMITIVE TERMS or UNDEFINED TERMS, and we employ them without explaining their meanings. At the same time we adopt the principle: not to employ any of the other expressions of the discipline under consideration, unless its meaning has first been determined with the help of primitive terms and of such expressions of the discipline whose meanings have been explained previously. The sentence which determines the meaning of a term in this way is called a DEFINITION,...

An inevitable regress to primitive notions in the [theory of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_knowledge) was explained by [Gilbert de B. Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_de_B._Robinson):

To a non-mathematician it often comes as a surprise that it is impossible to define explicitly all the terms which are used. This is not a superficial problem but lies at the root of all knowledge; it is necessary to begin somewhere, and to make progress one must clearly state those elements and relations which are undefined and those properties which are taken for granted.[^o5]

## Examples {#Examples}

The necessity for primitive notions is illustrated in several axiomatic foundations in mathematics:

- [Set theory](wikipedia://en/27553): The concept of the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) is an example of a primitive notion. As [Mary Tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Tiles) writes:[^o8] \[The\] 'definition' of 'set' is less a definition than an attempt at explication of something which is being given the status of a primitive, undefined, term. As evidence, she quotes [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989): "A set is formed by the grouping together of single objects into a whole. A set is a plurality thought of as a unit."
- [Naive set theory](wikipedia://en/4944): The [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is a primitive notion. To assert that it exists would be an implicit [axiom](wikipedia://en/928).
- [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic): The [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637) and the number [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) are primitive notions. Since Peano arithmetic is useful in regards to properties of the numbers, the objects that the primitive notions represent may not strictly matter.[^o9]
- Arithmetic of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438): Typically, primitive notions are: real number, two [binary operations](wikipedia://en/3948): [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), numbers 0 and 1, ordering.
- [Axiomatic systems](wikipedia://en/188401): The primitive notions will depend upon the set of axioms chosen for the system. [Alessandro Padoa](wikipedia://en/2452868) discussed this selection at the [International Congress of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Philosophy) in Paris in 1900.[^o10] The notions themselves may not necessarily need to be stated; Susan Haack (1978) writes, "A set of axioms is sometimes said to give an implicit definition of its primitive terms."[^o11]
- [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417): Under [Hilbert's axiom system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_axiom_system) the primitive notions are *point, line, plane, congruence, betweenness*, and *incidence*.
- [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417): Under [Peano's axiom system](wikipedia://en/21317755#Pasch_and_Peano) the primitive notions are *point, segment*, and *motion*.

## Russell's primitives {#Russell's_primitives}

In his book on [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439), *[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics)* [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) used the following notions: for class-calculus ([set theory](wikipedia://en/27553)), he used [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558), taking [set membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_membership) as a primitive notion. To establish sets, he also establishes [propositional functions](wikipedia://en/229146) as primitive, as well as the phrase "such that" as used in [set builder notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_builder_notation). (pp 18,9) Regarding relations, Russell takes as primitive notions the [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020) and [complementary relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_relation) of a given *xRy*. Furthermore, logical products of relations and [relative products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_product) of relations are primitive. (p 25) As for denotation of objects by description, Russell acknowledges that a primitive notion is involved. (p 27) The thesis of Russell’s book is "Pure mathematics uses only a few notions, and these are logical constants." (p xxi)
