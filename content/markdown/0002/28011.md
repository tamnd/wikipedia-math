# Subgroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subgroup"}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a subset of a group G is a subgroup of G if the members of that subset form a group with respect to the group operation in G.

:::

Formally, given a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ under a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) ∗, a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $H$ of $G$ is called a **subgroup** of $G$ if $H$ also forms a group under the operation ∗. More precisely, $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ if the [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) of ∗ to $H \times H$ is a group operation on $H$. This is often denoted $H \le G$, read as "$H$ is a subgroup of $G$".

The **trivial subgroup** of any group is the subgroup {*e*} consisting of just the identity element.[^n1]

A **proper subgroup** of a group $G$ is a subgroup $H$ which is a [proper subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $G$ (that is, $H \ne G$). This is often represented notationally by $H < G$, read as "$H$ is a proper subgroup of $G$". Some authors also exclude the trivial group from being proper (that is, $H \ne \{e\}$).[^n2][^n3]

If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then $G$ is sometimes called an **overgroup** of $H$.

The same definitions apply more generally when $G$ is an arbitrary [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799), but this article will only deal with subgroups of groups.

## Subgroup tests {#Subgroup_tests}

Suppose that $G$ is a group, and $H$ is a subset of $G$. For now, assume that the group operation of $G$ is written multiplicatively, denoted by juxtaposition.

- Then $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $H$ is nonempty and [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under products and inverses. *Closed under products* means that for every $a$ and $b$ in $H$, the product $ab$ is in $H$. *Closed under inverses* means that for every $a$ in $H$, the inverse $a^{-1}$ is in $H$. These two conditions can be combined into one, that for every $a$ and $b$ in $H$, the element $ab^{-1}$ is in $H$, but it is more natural and usually just as easy to test the two closure conditions separately.[^n4]
- When $H$ is *finite*, the test can be simplified: $H$ is a subgroup if and only if it is nonempty and closed under products. These conditions alone imply that every element $a$ of $H$ generates a finite cyclic subgroup of $H$, say of order $n$, and then the inverse of $a$ is $a^{n-1}$.[^n4]

If the group operation is instead denoted by addition, then *closed under products* should be replaced by *closed under addition*, which is the condition that for every $a$ and $b$ in $H$, the sum $a + b$ is in $H$, and *closed under inverses* should be edited to say that for every $a$ in $H$, the inverse $-a$ is in $H$.

:::

## Basic properties of subgroups {#Basic_properties_of_subgroups}

- The [identity](wikipedia://en/14962) of a subgroup is the identity of the group: if $G$ is a group with identity $e_{G}$, and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ with identity $e_{H}$, then $e_{H} = e_{G}$.
- The [inverse](wikipedia://en/45199) of an element in a subgroup is the inverse of the element in the group: if $H$ is a subgroup of a group $G$, and $a$ and $b$ are elements of $H$ such that $ab = ba = e_{H}$, then $ab = ba = e_{G}$.
- If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, then the inclusion map $H \to G$ sending each element $a$ of $H$ to itself is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564).
- The [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of subgroups $A$ and $B$ of $G$ is again a subgroup of $G$.[^n5] For example, the intersection of the $x$-axis and $y$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$ under addition is the trivial subgroup. More generally, the intersection of an arbitrary collection of subgroups of $G$ is a subgroup of $G$.
- The [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of subgroups $A$ and $B$ is a subgroup if and only if $A \subseteq B$ or $B \subseteq A$. A non-example: $2\mathbb{Z} \cup 3\mathbb{Z}$ is not a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z},$ because 2 and 3 are elements of this subset whose sum, 5, is not in the subset. Similarly, the union of the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^2.$
- If $S$ is a subset of $G$, then there exists a smallest subgroup containing $S$, namely the intersection of all of subgroups containing $S$; it is denoted by $\langle S\rangle$ and is called the [subgroup generated by $S$](wikipedia://en/99945). An element of $G$ is in $\langle S\rangle$ if and only if it is a finite product of elements of $S$ and their inverses, possibly repeated.[^n6]
- Every element $a$ of a group $G$ generates a cyclic subgroup $\langle a\rangle$. If $\langle a\rangle$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ ([the integers $\bmod n$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers_modulo_n)) for some positive integer $n$, then $n$ is the smallest positive integer for which $a^{n} = e$, and $n$ is called the *order* of $a$. If $\langle a\rangle$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z},$ then $a$ is said to have *infinite order*.
- The subgroups of any given group form a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) under inclusion, called the [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172). (While the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) here is the usual set-theoretic intersection, the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of a set of subgroups is the subgroup *generated by* the set-theoretic union of the subgroups, not the set-theoretic union itself.) If $e$ is the identity of $G$, then the trivial group $\{e\}$ is the [minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) subgroup of $G$, while the [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) subgroup is the group $G$ itself.

*Image: Left cosets of Z 2 in Z 8.svg.* $G$ is the group $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z},$ the [integers mod 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers_modulo_n) under addition. The subgroup $H$ contains only 0 and 4, and is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}.$ There are four left cosets of $H$: $H$ itself, $1 + H$, $2 + H$, and $3 + H$ (written using additive notation since this is an [additive group](wikipedia://en/2974)). Together they partition the entire group $G$ into equal-size, non-overlapping sets. The index $[G : H]$ is 4.

## Cosets and Lagrange's theorem {#Cosets_and_Lagrange's_theorem}

Given a subgroup $H$ and some $a$ in $G$, we define the **left [coset](wikipedia://en/98759)** $aH = \{ah : h\text{ in } H\}.$ Because $a$ is invertible, the map $\varphi : H \to aH$ given by $\varphi(h) = ah$ is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942). Furthermore, every element of $G$ is contained in precisely one left coset of $H$; the left cosets are the equivalence classes corresponding to the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $a_{1} \sim a_{2}$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $a_1^{-1}a_2$ is in $H$. The number of left cosets of $H$ is called the [index](wikipedia://en/230581) of $H$ in $G$ and is denoted by $[G : H]$.

[Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150) states that for a finite group $G$ and a subgroup $H$,

$$[ G : H ] = { |G| \over |H| }$$

where $\lvert G\rvert$ and $\lvert H\rvert$ denote the [orders](wikipedia://en/493527) of $G$ and $H$, respectively. In particular, the order of every subgroup of $G$ (and the order of every element of $G$) must be a [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of $\lvert G\rvert$.[^o14][^n7]

**Right cosets** are defined analogously: $Ha = \{ha : h\text{ in } H\}.$ They are also the equivalence classes for a suitable equivalence relation and their number is equal to $[G : H]$.

If $aH = Ha$ for every $a$ in $G$, then $H$ is said to be a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918). Every subgroup of index 2 is normal: the left cosets, and also the right cosets, are simply the subgroup and its complement. More generally, if $p$ is the lowest prime dividing the order of a finite group $G$, then any subgroup of index $p$ (if such exists) is normal.

:::

## Example: Subgroups of Z<sub>8</sub> {#Example:_Subgroups_of_Z8}

Let $G$ be the finite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327)

$$\mathrm{Z}_8 = \{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}$$

under addition modulo 8. The subset $\{0,2,4,6\}$ consisting of multiples of 2 is a subgroup of $\mathrm{Z}_8$. More generally, for each divisor $d$ of 8, the multiples of $d$ form a subgroup. Explicitly, for $d=1,2,4,8$, these subgroups are $\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}, \{0,2,4,6\}, \{0,4\}, \{0\}$.

In general, for any positive integer $n$, one can describe all subgroups of the finite cyclic group $\mathrm{Z}_n$ similarly: for each divisor $d$ of $n$, the multiples of $d$ in $\mathrm{Z}_n$ form a subgroup of order $n/d$, and every subgroup arises in this way.

Subgroups of cyclic groups are cyclic.[^n8]

## Example: Subgroups of S<sub>4</sub> {#Example:_Subgroups_of_S4}

The [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{4}$ is the group whose elements are the [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of $\{1,2,3,4\}$. Below are all its subgroups, ordered by cardinality.

### 24 elements {#24_elements}

Like each group, $S_{4}$ is a subgroup of itself.

### 12 elements {#12_elements}

The [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_{4}$ consists of all the even permutations in $S_{4}$. Since it is of index 2, it is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918).

### 8 elements {#8_elements}

There are three subgroups of order 8, each isomorphic to the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) $D_{4}$, the group of symmetries of a square.

Labeling the vertices of a square $1,2,3,4$ clockwise lets one view $D_{4}$ as a subgroup of $S_{4}$. This subgroup is generated by the 90-degree clockwise rotation and by the reflection in the diagonal axis joining vertices 1 and 3; these are the permutations $(1234)$ and $(24)$.

Up to symmetries of the square, there are three different ways to label the vertices of a square, distinguished by which pairs of numbers appear on opposite corners. In the labeling above, 1 and 3 were opposite, and 2 and 4 were opposite; another choice has 1 and 4 opposite, and 2 and 3 opposite; the third choice has 1 and 2 opposite, and 3 and 4 opposite. The three labelings give rise to three different subgroups of order 8 in $S_{4}$, conjugate to each other, each isomorphic to $D_{4}$.

### 6 elements {#6_elements}

There are four subgroups of order 6, each isomorphic to $S_{3}$. Each is the [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_subgroup) of one of the elements of $\{1,2,3,4\}$. For example, the stabilizer of 4 is the group of permutations in $S_{4}$ that map 4 to 4, while permuting $\{1,2,3\}$ in an arbitrary way; it is generated by the permutations $(12)$ and $(123)$, for instance. The four subgroups of order 6 are conjugate to each other.

### 4 elements {#4_elements}

There are seven subgroups of order 4, falling into three conjugacy classes of subgroups:

- The subset $\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$ is a normal subgroup isomorphic to the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) $V_{4}$.
- The group generated by $(12)$ and $(34)$ is another subgroup isomorphic to $V_{4}$, but it is not normal. Instead it has conjugates, namely the group generated by $(13)$ and $(24)$ and the group generated by $(14)$ and $(23)$.
- Each of the six 4-cycles in $S_{4}$ generates a cyclic subgroup of order 4, but each 4-cycle generates the same subgroup as its inverse, so there are only three distinct subgroups of this type. These three subgroups are conjugate to each other because all 4-cycles in $S_{4}$ are conjugate to each other.

### 3 elements {#3_elements}

There are four subgroups of order 3, each generated by a 3-cycle. There are eight 3-cycles in $S_{4}$, but each generates the same subgroup as its inverse. The resulting four subgroups are conjugate to each other.

### 2 elements {#2_elements}

There are nine subgroups of order 2, falling into two conjugacy classes of subgroups:

- Each of the $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ [transpositions](wikipedia://en/449166#Transpositions) (2-cycles) generates a subgroup of order 2. These six subgroups are conjugate.
- Each of the double-transpositions $(12)(34)$, $(13)(24)$, $(14)(23)$ generates a subgroup of order 2. These three subgroups are conjugate.

### 1 element {#1_element}

The [trivial subgroup](wikipedia://en/193059) is the unique subgroup of order 1.

## Other examples {#Other_examples}

- The even integers form a subgroup $2\mathbb{Z}$ of the [integer ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_ring) $\mathbb{Z}:$ the sum of two even integers is even, and the negative of an even integer is even.
- Every [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977#Definitions) in a ring $R$ is a subgroup of the additive group of $R$.
- Every [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) is a subgroup of the additive group of vectors.
- In an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), the elements of finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527) form a subgroup called the [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052).
