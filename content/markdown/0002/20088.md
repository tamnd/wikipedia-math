# Myriad

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Myriad"}

In the context of numeric naming systems for [powers of ten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powers_of_ten), **myriad** is the quantity ten thousand ([10,000](wikipedia://en/493491)). Idiomatically, in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), *myriad* is an adjective used to mean that a group of things has [indefinitely large quantity](wikipedia://en/538913).[^o2]

:::

*Myriad* derives from the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) for ten thousand (μυριάς) and is used with this meaning in [literal translations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language), [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) or [Sinospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinospheric) languages ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language), [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language), [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language), and [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language)), and in reference to ancient [Greek numerals](wikipedia://en/166697).

The term *myriad* is also used in the form "a myriad" for a 100 km × 100 km square (10,000 km²) the grid size of the British [Ordnance Survey National Grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordnance_Survey_National_Grid) and the US [Military Grid Reference System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Grid_Reference_System). It contains 100 [hectads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hectad_%28unit_of_area%29).

## History {#History}

The [Aegean numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegean_numerals) of the [Minoan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoans) and [Mycenaean civilizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_civilization) included a symbol composed of a circle with four dashes 𐄫 to denote tens of thousands.[^o4]

In classical [Greek numerals](wikipedia://en/166697), myriad was written as a capital [mu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu_%28letter%29): **Μ**. To distinguish this numeral from letters, it was sometimes given an [overbar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overbar): $\overline{M}$. Multiples were written above this sign. For example 45820000 is 4,582×10,000 or 45,820,000.

The etymology of *myriad* is uncertain. It has been variously connected to [PIE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European) *\*meu-* ("damp") in reference to the waves of the sea and to Greek *myrmex* (μύρμηξ, "ant") in reference to their swarms.[^o5]

In his *[Sand Reckoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sand_Reckoner)*, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) used "myriad myriad" ($\overline{\operatorname{MM}}$, one hundred million) as the basis for a numeration system of large powers of ten, which he used to count grains of sand.[^o6]

## In English {#In_English}

*Myriad* may be used either as an [adjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjective) (there are myriad people outside) or as a [noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun) (there is a myriad of people outside),[^o8] but there are small differences. The former might imply that it is a diverse group of people whereas the latter usually does not.

Despite its usual meaning (a large, unspecified quantity), *myriad* is sometimes used in English to mean ten thousand although usually restricted to translation from other languages like [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) and [Chinese](wikipedia://en/5781) where quantities are grouped by 10,000. Such use permits the translator to remain closer to the original text and avoid unwieldy mentions of "tens of thousands". For example, "the original number of the crews supplied by the several nations I find to have been twenty-four myriads"[^o9] and "What is the distance between one bridge and another? Twelve myriads of [parasangs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasang)".[^o10]

## In European languages {#In_European_languages}

Most European languages include a variation of *myriad* with a similar meaning to the English word.

Additionally, the [prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix) *[myria-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myria-)* indicating multiplication times ten thousand (×10<sup>4</sup>), was part of the original [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_system) adopted by [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_Republic) in 1795.[^o12] Although it was not retained after the 11th [CGPM conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conférence_générale_des_poids_et_mesures) in 1960, *[myriameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myriameter)* is sometimes still encountered as a translation of the [Scandinavian mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scandinavian_mile) ([Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language) & [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language): *mil*) of 10 km, or in some classifications of wavelengths as the adjective *[myriametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-low_frequency)*. The *[myriagramme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myriagramme)* (10 kg) was a French approximation of the [avoirdupois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avoirdupois) *quartier* of 25 lb and the *[myriaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myriaton)* appears in [Isaac Asimov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Asimov)'s *[Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_and_Empire)* novel trilogy.

In [modern Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek), the word "myriad" is rarely used to denote 10,000, but a million is *ekatommyrio* (εκατομμύριο, *lit.* 'hundred myriad') and a [thousand million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_E9) is *disekatommyrio* (δισεκατομμύριο, *lit.* 'twice hundred myriad').

## In East Asian languages {#In_East_Asian_languages}

In [East Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_culture), the traditional numeral systems of [China](wikipedia://en/5781), [Korea](wikipedia://en/994834), and [Japan](wikipedia://en/146615) are all [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214)-based but grouped into ten thousands rather than thousands. The [character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character) for myriad is 萬 in [traditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_characters) script and 万 in [simplified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_characters) form in both [mainland China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese_character#Mainland_China) and [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_script_reform); its pronunciation varies between languages ([Mandarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_Chinese): *wàn*, [Hakka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hakka_language): *wan*<sup>5</sup>, [Minnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnan_language): *bān*, [Cantonese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantonese_language): *maan*<sup>6</sup>, [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language) and [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language): *man*, [Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnamese_language): *vạn*, [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_language): หมื่น *muen* and [Khmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khmer_language): ម៉ឺន *meun*).

Because of this grouping into fours, higher orders of numbers are provided by the [powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28math%29) of 10,000 rather than 1,000: In China, 10,000<sup>2</sup> was 萬萬 in ancient texts but is now called 億 and sometimes written as 1,0000,0000; 10,000<sup>3</sup> is 1,0000,0000,0000 or 兆; 10,000<sup>4</sup> is 1,0000,0000,0000,0000 or 京; and so on. Conversely, Chinese, Japanese, and Korean generally do not have native words for powers of one thousand: what is called "one million" in English is "100萬" (100 myriad) in the [Sinosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinosphere), and "one billion" in English is "十億" (ten [myllion](wikipedia://en/306043)) or "十萬萬" (ten myriad myriad) in the Sinosphere. Unusually, Vietnam employs its former translation of 兆, *một triệu*, to mean 1,000,000 rather than the Chinese figure. Similarly, the Chinese government has adapted the word 兆 to mean the scientific prefix [mega-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mega-), but transliterations are used instead for [giga-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giga-), [tera-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tera-), and other larger prefixes. This has caused confusion in areas closely related to China such as [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong) and [Macau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macau), where 兆 is still largely used to mean 10,000<sup>3</sup>.

萬 and 万 are also frequently employed colloquially in expressions, [clichés](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliché), and *[chengyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chengyu)* (idioms) in the senses of "vast", "numerous", "numberless", and "infinite". A [skeleton key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton_key) is a 万 ("myriad-use key"),[^o14] the [emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_of_China) was the "lord of myriad [chariots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot)" (萬乘之主),[^o15] the [Great Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall) is called 万 ("Myriad-[mile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_mile) Long Wall"), [Zhu Xi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Xi)'s statement 月 ("the moon reflects in myriad rivers") had the sense of supporting greater [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism) in [Chinese philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_philosophy),[^o16] and Ha Qiongwen's popular 1959 [propaganda poster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda_in_the_People's_Republic_of_China) 毛, meaning "Long live [Chairman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Central_Military_Commission_%28China%29) [Mao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong)", literally reads as "\[May\] Chairman Mao \[live to be\] [10,000 years old](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_thousand_years)".[^o17] Its literary use may thus mean something huge and plentiful.[^o18]p. 60

## In Old Turkic {#In_Old_Turkic}

A similar term is the [Old Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Turkic) word *[tümän](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumen_%28unit%29)*,[^o20] whose variant forms remain in use for "ten thousand" among modern [Mongolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_language), [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language).[^o21][^o22] According to Sir [Gerard Clauson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_Clauson) (1891–1974), it was likely borrowed from [Tokharian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tocharian_language) *tmān*, which may have been borrowed in turn from [Old Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Chinese) *tman* 萬 > *wan*.[^o23]

## In Hebrew {#In_Hebrew}

In [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew) the word רבבה (pronounced "revava") means 10,000, and is the highest number represented in Hebrew. Its sources go back to biblical times.[^o25]
