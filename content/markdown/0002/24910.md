# Product topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Product topology"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **product space** is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of a family of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) equipped with a [natural topology](wikipedia://en/11934923) called the **product topology**. This topology differs from another, perhaps more natural-seeming, topology called the [box topology](wikipedia://en/954333), which can also be given to a product space and which [agrees](wikipedia://en/393069) with the product topology when the product is over only finitely many spaces. However, the product topology is "correct" in that it makes the product space a [categorical product](wikipedia://en/237868) of its factors, whereas the box topology is too [fine](wikipedia://en/393069); in that sense the product topology is the natural topology on the Cartesian product.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Throughout, $I$ will be some non-empty [index set](wikipedia://en/393055) and for every index $i \in I,$ let $X_i$ be a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). Denote the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of the sets $X_i$ by

$$X := \prod X_{\bullet} := \prod_{i \in I} X_i$$

and for every index $i \in I$, denote the $i$-th **[canonical projection](wikipedia://en/3913145)** by

$$\begin{aligned} p_i :\ \prod_{j \in I} X_j &\to X_i, \\[3mu] (x_j)_{j \in I} &\mapsto x_i. \\ \end{aligned}$$

The **product topology**, sometimes called the **Tychonoff topology**, on $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$ is defined to be the [coarsest topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarsest_topology) (that is, the topology with the fewest open sets) for which all the projections $p_i : \prod X_{\bullet} \to X_i$ are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29). It is the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123) on $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$ with respect to the family of projections $\left\{p_i\mathbin{\big\vert} i \in I\right\}$. The Cartesian product $X := \prod_{i \in I} X_i$ endowed with the product topology is called the **product space**. The open sets in the product topology are the unions of (finitely many or infinitely many) sets of the form $\prod_{i \in I} U_i$, where each $U_i$ is open in $X_i$ and $U_i \neq X_i$ for only finitely many $i$. In particular, for a finite product (in particular, for the product of two topological spaces), the set of all Cartesian products between one basis element from each $X_i$ gives a basis for the product topology of $\prod_{i\in I} X_i$. That is, for a finite product, the set of all $\prod_{i \in I} U_i$, where each $U_i$ is an element of the (chosen) basis of $X_i$, is a basis for the product topology of $\prod_{i\in I} X_i$.

The product topology on $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$ is the topology [generated](wikipedia://en/59220) by the sets of the form $p_i^{-1}\left(U\right)$, where $i \in I$ and $U$ is an open subset of $X_i$. A [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $X$ is open if and only if it is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of (possibly infinitely many) [intersections](wikipedia://en/23476429) of finitely many sets of the form $p_i^{-1}\left(U\right)$. The $p_i^{-1}\left(U\right)$'s are sometimes called [open cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cylinder), and their intersections are [cylinder sets](wikipedia://en/2613658).

The product topology is also called the [topology of pointwise convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_of_pointwise_convergence) because a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) (or more generally, a [net](wikipedia://en/22170)) in $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$ converges if and only if all its projections to the spaces $X_i$ converge. Explicitly, a sequence $s_{\bullet} = \left(s_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ (respectively, a net $s_{\bullet} = \left(s_a\right)_{a \in A}$) converges to a given point $x \in \prod_{i \in I} X_i$ if and only if $p_i\left(s_{\bullet}\right) \to p_i(x)$ in $X_i$ for every index $i \in I$, where $p_i\left(s_{\bullet}\right) := p_i \circ s_{\bullet}$ denotes $\left(p_i\left(s_n\right)\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ (respectively, denotes $\left(p_i\left(s_a\right)\right)_{a \in A}$). In particular, if $X_i = \mathbb{R}$ for all $i$, then the Cartesian product is the space $\prod_{i \in I} \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^I$ of all [real-valued functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-valued_functions) on $I$, and convergence in the product topology is the same as [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198) of functions.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

If the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ is endowed with its [standard topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_topology) then the product topology on the product of $n$ copies of $\mathbb{R}$ is equal to the ordinary [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) on $\mathbb{R}^n.$ (Because $n$ is finite, this is also equivalent to the [box topology](wikipedia://en/954333) on $\mathbb{R}^n.$)

The [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to the product of [countably many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) copies of the [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) $\{ 0, 1 \}$ and the space of [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689) is homeomorphic to the product of countably many copies of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), where again each copy carries the discrete topology.

Several additional examples are given in the article on the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123).

## Properties {#Properties}

The set of Cartesian products between the open sets of the topologies of each $X_i$ forms a basis for what is called the [box topology](wikipedia://en/954333) on $X.$ In general, the box topology is [finer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finer_topology) than the product topology, but for finite products they coincide.

The product space $X,$ together with the canonical projections, can be characterized by the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245): if $Y$ is a topological space, and for every $i \in I,$ $f_i : Y \to X_i$ is a continuous map, then there exists precisely one continuous map $f : Y \to X$ such that for each $i \in I$ the following diagram [commutes](wikipedia://en/138484):

This shows that the product space is a [product](wikipedia://en/237868) in the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051). It follows from the above universal property that a map $f : Y \to X$ is continuous [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $f_i = p_i \circ f$ is continuous for all $i \in I.$ In many cases it is easier to check that the component functions $f_i$ are continuous. Checking whether a map $X \to Y$ is continuous is usually more difficult; one tries to use the fact that the $p_i$ are continuous in some way.

In addition to being continuous, the canonical projections $p_i : X \to X_i$ are [open maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map). This means that any open subset of the product space remains open when projected down to the $X_i.$ The converse is not true: if $W$ is a [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of the product space whose projections down to all the $X_i$ are open, then $W$ need not be open in $X$ (consider for instance $W = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus (0, 1)^2.$) The canonical projections are not generally [closed maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_map) (consider for example the closed set $\left\{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : xy = 1\right\},$ whose projections onto both axes are $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$).

Suppose $\prod_{i \in I} S_i$ is a product of arbitrary subsets, where $S_i \subseteq X_i$ for every $i \in I.$ If all $S_i$ are non-empty then $\prod_{i \in I} S_i$ is a closed subset of the product space $X$ if and only if every $S_i$ is a closed subset of $X_i.$ More generally, the closure of the product $\prod_{i \in I} S_i$ of arbitrary subsets in the product space $X$ is equal to the product of the closures:[^n1]

$${\operatorname{Cl}_X}\Bigl(\prod_{i \in I} S_i\Bigr) = \prod_{i \in I} \bigl({\operatorname{Cl}_{X_i}} S_i\bigr).$$

Any product of [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) is again a Hausdorff space; this comes from a simple preservation of disjoint open neighborhoods under the inverse of canonical projections from $X_i$'s to $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$. This line of argument is used for showing that other [topological invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant) are preserved under the product topology.

[Tychonoff's theorem](wikipedia://en/82739), which is equivalent to the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), states that any product of [compact spaces](wikipedia://en/6042) is a compact space. A specialization of [Tychonoff's theorem](wikipedia://en/82739) that requires only [the ultrafilter lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_ultrafilter_lemma) (and not the full strength of the axiom of choice) states that any product of compact [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) spaces is a compact space.

If $z = \left(z_i\right)_{i \in I} \in X$ is fixed then the set

$$\left\{ x = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I} \in X \mathbin{\big\vert} x_i = z_i \text{ for all but finitely many } i \right\}$$

is a [dense subset](wikipedia://en/23523594) of the product space $X$.[^n1]

## Relation to other topological notions {#Relation_to_other_topological_notions}

**Separation**

- Every product of [T<sub>0</sub> spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T0_space) is T<sub>0</sub>.
- Every product of [T<sub>1</sub> spaces](wikipedia://en/148721) is T<sub>1</sub>.
- Every product of [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) is Hausdorff.
- Every product of [regular spaces](wikipedia://en/53058) is regular.
- Every product of [Tychonoff spaces](wikipedia://en/30554) is Tychonoff.
- A product of [normal spaces](wikipedia://en/48629) need not be normal. An example of this is the product of the set X of ordinals less than the [first uncountable ordinal](wikipedia://en/20860540) and the set Y which is the union of X with $\omega_1$. Both spaces have the [order topology](wikipedia://en/213650) and are normal, yet $X \times Y$ is not normal.[^o11]

**Compactness**

- Every product of compact spaces is compact ([Tychonoff's theorem](wikipedia://en/82739)).
- A product of [locally compact spaces](wikipedia://en/48632) need not be locally compact. However, an arbitrary product of locally compact spaces where all but finitely many are compact is locally compact (This condition is sufficient and necessary).

**Connectedness**

- Every product of [connected](wikipedia://en/87141) (resp. path-connected) spaces is connected (resp. path-connected).
- Every product of [hereditarily disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditarily_disconnected) spaces is hereditarily disconnected.

**Metric spaces**

- Every countable product of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) is a [metrizable space](wikipedia://en/19738).

## Axiom of choice {#Axiom_of_choice}

One of many ways to express the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) is to say that it is equivalent to the statement that the Cartesian product of a collection of non-empty sets is non-empty.[^o14] The proof that this is equivalent to the statement of the axiom in terms of choice functions is immediate: one needs only to pick an element from each set to find a representative in the product. Conversely, a representative of the product is a set which contains exactly one element from each component.

The axiom of choice occurs again in the study of (topological) product spaces; for example, [Tychonoff's theorem](wikipedia://en/82739) on compact sets is a more complex and subtle example of a statement that requires the axiom of choice and is equivalent to it in its most general formulation,[^o15] and shows why the product topology may be considered the more useful topology to put on a Cartesian product.
