# Field of fractions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Field of fractions"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **field of fractions** of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) is the smallest [field](wikipedia://en/10603) in which it can be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119). The construction of the field of fractions is modeled on the relationship between the integral domain of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). Intuitively, it consists of ratios between integral domain elements.

:::

The field of fractions of an integral domain $R$ is sometimes denoted by $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ or $\operatorname{Quot}(R)$, and the construction is sometimes also called the **fraction field**, **field of quotients**, or **quotient field** of $R$. All four are in common usage, but are not to be confused with the [quotient of a ring by an ideal](wikipedia://en/184635), which is a quite different concept. For a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) that is not an integral domain, the analogous construction is called the [localization](wikipedia://en/252216) or ring of quotients.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given an integral domain $R$ and letting $R^* = R \setminus \{0\}$, we define an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on $R \times R^*$ by letting $(n,d) \sim (m,b)$ whenever $nb = md$. We denote the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of $(n,d)$ by $\frac{n}{d}$. This notion of equivalence is motivated by the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$, which have the same property with respect to the underlying [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers.

Then the **field of fractions** is the set $\text{Frac}(R) = (R \times R^*)/\sim$ with addition given by

$$\frac{n}{d} + \frac{m}{b} = \frac{nb+md}{db}$$

and multiplication given by

$$\frac{n}{d} \cdot \frac{m}{b} = \frac{nm}{db}.$$

One may check that these operations are well-defined and that, for any integral domain $R$, $\text{Frac}(R)$ is indeed a field. In particular, for $n,d \neq 0$, the multiplicative inverse of $\frac{n}{d}$ is as expected: $\frac{d}{n} \cdot \frac{n}{d} = 1$.

The embedding of $R$ in $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ maps each $n$ in $R$ to the fraction $\frac{en}{e}$ for any nonzero $e\in R$ (the equivalence class is independent of the choice $e$). This is modeled on the identity $\frac{n}{1}=n$.

The field of fractions of $R$ is characterized by the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245):

if $h: R \to F$ is an [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from $R$ into a field $F$, then there exists a unique ring homomorphism $g: \operatorname{Frac}(R) \to F$ that extends $h$.

There is a [categorical](wikipedia://en/5869) interpretation of this construction. Let $\mathbf{C}$ be the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of integral domains and injective ring maps. The [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from $\mathbf{C}$ to the [category of fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_fields) that takes every integral domain to its fraction field and every homomorphism to the induced map on fields (which exists by the universal property) is the [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) of the [inclusion functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_functor) from the category of fields to $\mathbf{C}$. Thus the category of fields (which is a full subcategory) is a [reflective subcategory](wikipedia://en/3967424) of $\mathbf{C}$.

A [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) is not required for the role of the integral domain; this construction can be applied to any [nonzero](wikipedia://en/1872874) commutative [rng](wikipedia://en/3405004) $R$ with no nonzero [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441). The embedding is given by $r\mapsto\frac{rs}{s}$ for any nonzero $s\in R$.[^o4]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The field of fractions of the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties) is the field of [rationals](wikipedia://en/19727024): $\mathbb{Q} = \operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z})$.
- Let $R:=\{a+b\mathrm{i} \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ be the ring of [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628). Then $\operatorname{Frac}(R)=\{c+d\mathrm{i}\mid c,d\in\mathbb{Q}\}$, the field of [Gaussian rationals](wikipedia://en/318052).
- The field of fractions of a field is canonically [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the field itself.
- For any field $k$, the field of fractions of the one-variable [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $k[t]$ is the [rational function field](wikipedia://en/361210) $k(t)$.[^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10]
- For any field $k$, the field of fractions of the [formal power series ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_power_series_ring) $k[\![t]\!]$ is the field of [formal Laurent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_Laurent_series) $k(\!(t)\!)$.
- The field of fractions of the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) ring of half-line functions yields a [space of operators](wikipedia://en/51363226), including the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021), [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886), and [integral operator](wikipedia://en/2649421). This construction gives an alternate representation of the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) that does not depend explicitly on an integral transform.[^o11]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Localization {#Localization}

For any [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $R$ and any [multiplicative set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_set) $S$ in $R$, the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) $S^{-1}R$ is the [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) consisting of [fractions](wikipedia://en/1704824)

$$\frac{r}{s}$$

with $r\in R$ and $s\in S$, where now $(r,s)$ is equivalent to $(r',s')$ if and only if there exists $t\in S$ such that $t(rs'-r's)=0$.

Two special cases of this are notable:

- If $S$ is the complement of a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) $P$, then $S^{-1}R$ is also denoted $R_P$. When $R$ is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) and $P$ is the zero ideal, $R_P$ is the field of fractions of $R$.
- If $S$ is the set of non-[zero-divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-divisor) in $R$, then $S^{-1}R$ is called the [total quotient ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_quotient_ring). The [total quotient ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_quotient_ring) of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) is its field of fractions, but the [total quotient ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_quotient_ring) is defined for any [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346).

Note that it is permitted for $S$ to contain 0, but in that case $S^{-1}R$ will be the [trivial ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_ring).

### Semifield of fractions {#Semifield_of_fractions}

The **semifield of fractions** of a [commutative semiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_semiring) in which every nonzero element is (multiplicatively) cancellative is the smallest [semifield](wikipedia://en/10417428) in which it can be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119). (Note that, unlike the case of rings, a semiring with no [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) can still have nonzero elements that are not cancellative. For example, let $\mathbb{T}$ denote the [tropical semiring](wikipedia://en/3892336) and let $R=\mathbb{T}[X]$ be the [polynomial semiring](wikipedia://en/373065#polynomial_rigs) over $\mathbb{T}$. Then $R$ has no zero divisors, but the element $1+X$ is not cancellative because $(1+X)(1+X+X^2)=1+X+X^2+X^3=(1+X)(1+X^2)$).

The elements of the semifield of fractions of the commutative [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468) $R$ are [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) written as

$$\frac{a}{b}$$

with $a$ and $b$ in $R$ and $b\neq 0$.
