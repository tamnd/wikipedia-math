# National Security Agency

NSA

1

::: {#o9 .subject cue=lead name="National Security Agency"}

The **National Security Agency** (**NSA**) is an [intelligence agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_agency) of the [United States Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense), under the authority of the [director of national intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_National_Intelligence) (DNI). The NSA is responsible for global monitoring, collection, and processing of information and data for intelligence and [counterintelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterintelligence) purposes, specializing in a discipline known as [signals intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_intelligence) (SIGINT). The NSA is also tasked with the [protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_assurance) of U.S. communications networks and [information systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_systems).[^o10][^o70] The NSA relies on a variety of measures to accomplish its mission, the majority of which are [clandestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clandestine_operations).[^o11] The NSA has roughly 32,000 employees.[^o12]

:::

Originating as a unit to decipher coded communications in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), it was officially formed as the NSA by President [Harry S. Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman) in 1952. Between then and the end of the Cold War, it became the largest of the [U.S. intelligence organizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._intelligence_community#Organization) in terms of personnel and budget. Still, information available as of 2013 indicates that the [Central Intelligence Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Intelligence_Agency) (CIA) pulled ahead in this regard, with a budget of \$14.7 billion.[^o5][^o13] The NSA currently conducts [worldwide mass data collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_surveillance) and has been known to physically [bug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bugging) electronic systems as one method to this end.[^o14] The NSA is also alleged to have been behind such attack software as [Stuxnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuxnet), which severely damaged [Iran's nuclear program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_program_of_Iran).[^o15][^o16] The NSA, alongside the CIA, maintains a physical presence in many countries across the globe; the CIA/NSA joint [Special Collection Service](wikipedia://en/8629252) (a highly classified intelligence team) inserts eavesdropping devices in high-value targets (such as presidential palaces or embassies). SCS collection tactics allegedly encompass "close surveillance, burglary, wiretapping, \[and\] breaking".[^o17]

Unlike the CIA and the [Defense Intelligence Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Intelligence_Agency) (DIA), both of which specialize primarily in foreign [human espionage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clandestine_human_intelligence), the NSA does not publicly conduct [human intelligence gathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_intelligence_%28intelligence_gathering%29). The NSA is entrusted with assisting with and coordinating SIGINT elements for other government organizations—which Executive Order prevents from engaging in such activities on their own.[^o18] As part of these responsibilities, the agency has a co-located organization called the [Central Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Security_Service) (CSS), which facilitates cooperation between the NSA and other U.S. defense [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) components. To further ensure streamlined communication between the signals [intelligence community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Intelligence_Community) divisions, the [NSA director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_the_National_Security_Agency) simultaneously serves as the Commander of the [United States Cyber Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Cyber_Command) and as Chief of the Central Security Service.

The NSA's actions have been a matter of political controversy on several occasions, including its role in providing intelligence during the [Gulf of Tonkin incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin_incident), which contributed to the [escalation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin_Resolution) of U.S. involvement in the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War).[^o19] Declassified documents later revealed that the NSA misinterpreted or overstated signals intelligence, leading to reports of a second [North Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Vietnam) attack that likely never occurred.[^o20] The agency has also received scrutiny for spying on anti–Vietnam War leaders and the agency's participation in [economic espionage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_espionage). In 2013, the NSA had many of its secret surveillance programs [revealed to the public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surveillance_disclosures_%282013–present%29) by [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden), a former NSA contractor. According to the leaked documents, the NSA intercepts and stores the communications of over a billion people worldwide, including United States citizens. The documents also revealed that the NSA tracks hundreds of millions of people's movements using cell phone [metadata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metadata). Internationally, research has pointed to the NSA's ability to surveil the domestic Internet traffic of foreign countries through "[boomerang routing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_Sovereignty#Protection_of_National_Traffic)".[^o21]

## History {#History}

### Formation {#Formation}

The origins of the National Security Agency can be traced back to April 28, 1917, three weeks after the [U.S. Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress) declared war on Germany in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I). A [code](wikipedia://en/838989) and [cipher](wikipedia://en/5244) decryption unit was established as the Cable and Telegraph Section, which was also known as the Cipher Bureau.[^o24] It was headquartered in Washington, D.C., and was part of the war effort under the executive branch without direct congressional authorization. During the war, it was relocated in the army's organizational chart several times. On July 5, 1917, [Herbert O. Yardley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_O._Yardley) was assigned to head the unit. At that point, the unit consisted of Yardley and two civilian clerks. It absorbed the Navy's [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) functions in July 1918. World War I ended on [November 11, 1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice_of_11_November_1918), and the army cryptographic section of Military Intelligence (MI-8) moved to New York City on May 20, 1919, where it continued intelligence activities as the Code Compilation Company under the direction of Yardley.[^o25][^o26]

### The Black Chamber {#The_Black_Chamber}

*Image: Black Chamber cryptanalytic work sheet for solving Japanese diplomatic cipher, 1919 - National Cryptologic Museum - DSC07698.JPG.* Black Chamber cryptanalytic work sheet for solving Japanese diplomatic cipher, 1919

After the disbandment of the [U.S. Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army) cryptographic section of military intelligence known as MI-8, the U.S. government created the Cipher Bureau, also known as [Black Chamber](wikipedia://en/991864), in 1919. The Black Chamber was the United States' first peacetime [cryptanalytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalytic) organization.[^o28] Jointly funded by the Army and the State Department, the Cipher Bureau was disguised as a New York City [commercial code](wikipedia://en/28739060) company; it produced and sold such codes for business use. Its true mission, however, was to break the communications (chiefly diplomatic) of other nations. At the [Washington Naval Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Naval_Conference), it aided American negotiators by providing them with the decrypted traffic of many of the conference delegations, including the [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan). The Black Chamber successfully persuaded [Western Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Union), the largest U.S. [telegram company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegram_company) at the time, as well as several other communications companies, to illegally give the Black Chamber access to cable traffic of foreign embassies and consulates.[^o29] Soon, these companies publicly discontinued their collaboration. Despite the Chamber's initial successes, it was shut down in 1929 by U.S. secretary of state [Henry L. Stimson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_L._Stimson), who defended his decision by stating, "Gentlemen do not read each other's mail."[^o30]

### World War II and its aftermath {#World_War_II_and_its_aftermath}

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), the [Signal Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_Intelligence_Service) (SIS) was created to intercept and decipher the communications of the [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers).[^o33] When the war ended, the SIS was reorganized as the [Army Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Security_Agency) (ASA), and it was placed under the leadership of the Director of Military Intelligence.[^o33]

On May 20, 1949, all cryptologic activities were centralized under a national organization called the Armed Forces Security Agency (AFSA).[^o33] This organization was originally established within the [U.S. Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense) under the command of the [Joint Chiefs of Staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Chiefs_of_Staff).[^o34] The AFSA was tasked with directing the Department of Defense communications and electronic intelligence activities, except those of U.S. [military intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_intelligence) units.[^o34] However, the AFSA was unable to centralize [communications intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COMINT) and failed to coordinate with civilian agencies that shared its interests, such as the [Department of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_State), the [Central Intelligence Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Intelligence_Agency) (CIA) and the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation) (FBI).[^o34] In December 1951, President [Harry S. Truman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_S._Truman) ordered a panel to investigate how AFSA had failed to achieve its goals. The results of the investigation led to improvements and its redesignation as the National Security Agency.[^o35]

The [National Security Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Security_Council) issued a memorandum of October 24, 1952, that revised [National Security Council Intelligence Directive (NSCID) 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Council_Intelligence_Directives). On the same day, Truman issued a second memorandum that called for the establishment of the NSA.[^o36] The actual establishment of the NSA was done by a November 4 memo by [Robert A. Lovett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_A._Lovett), the [Secretary of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_Defense), changing the name of the AFSA to the NSA, and making the new agency responsible for all communications intelligence.[^o37] Since President Truman's memo was a [classified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classified_information) document,[^o36] the existence of the NSA was not known to the public at that time. Due to its ultra-secrecy, the U.S. intelligence community referred to the NSA as "No Such Agency".[^o38]

:::

### Vietnam War {#Vietnam_War}

In the 1960s, the NSA played a key role in expanding American commitment to the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War) by providing evidence of a [North Vietnamese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Vietnam) attack on the American Naval destroyer Maddox during the [Gulf of Tonkin incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Tonkin_incident).[^o40] A secret operation, code-named "[MINARET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_MINARET)", was set up by the NSA to monitor the phone communications of Senators [Frank Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Church) and [Howard Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Baker), as well as key leaders of the [civil rights movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement), including [Martin Luther King Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr.), and prominent U.S. journalists and athletes who criticized the Vietnam War.[^o41] However, the project turned out to be controversial, and an internal review by the NSA concluded that its Minaret program was "disreputable if not outright illegal".[^o41]

The NSA has mounted a major effort to secure tactical communications among U.S. armed forces during the war with mixed success. The [NESTOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NESTOR_%28encryption%29) family of compatible [secure voice](wikipedia://en/1560035) systems it developed was widely deployed during the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War), with about 30,000 NESTOR sets produced. However, a variety of technical and operational problems limited their use, allowing the North Vietnamese to exploit and intercept U.S. communications.[^o42]

### Church Committee hearings {#Church_Committee_hearings}

In the aftermath of the [Watergate scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watergate_scandal), a congressional hearing in 1975 led by Senator [Frank Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Church)[^o44] revealed that the NSA, in collaboration with Britain's SIGINT intelligence agency, [Government Communications Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Headquarters) (GCHQ), had routinely intercepted the international communications of prominent anti-Vietnam war leaders such as [Jane Fonda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Fonda) and Dr. [Benjamin Spock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Spock).[^o45] The NSA tracked these individuals in a secret filing system that was destroyed in 1974.[^o46] Following the resignation of President [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon), there were several investigations into suspected misuse of FBI, CIA and NSA facilities.[^o49] Senator Frank Church uncovered previously unknown activity,[^o49] such as a CIA plot (ordered by the administration of President [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy)) to assassinate [Fidel Castro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro).[^o47] The investigation also uncovered NSA's wiretaps on targeted U.S. citizens.[^o48] After the Church Committee hearings, the [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Act) of 1978 was passed. This was designed to limit the practice of [mass surveillance in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_surveillance_in_the_United_States).[^o49]

### 1980s to 1990s {#1980s_to_1990s}

In 1986, the NSA intercepted the communications of the Libyan government during the immediate aftermath of the [Berlin discotheque bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986_Berlin_discotheque_bombing). The [White House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House) asserted that the NSA interception had provided "irrefutable" evidence that Libya was behind the bombing, which U.S. President [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan) cited as a justification for the [1986 United States bombing of Libya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1986_United_States_bombing_of_Libya).[^o51][^o52]

In 1999, a multi-year investigation by the European Parliament highlighted the NSA's role in economic espionage in a report entitled 'Development of Surveillance Technology and Risk of Abuse of Economic Information'.[^o53] That year, the NSA founded the [NSA Hall of Honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA_Hall_of_Honor), a memorial at the [National Cryptologic Museum](wikipedia://en/734289) in Fort Meade, Maryland.[^o54] The memorial is a, "tribute to the pioneers and heroes who have made significant and long-lasting contributions to American cryptology".[^o54] NSA employees must be retired for more than fifteen years to qualify for the memorial.[^o54]

NSA's infrastructure deteriorated in the 1990s as defense budget cuts resulted in maintenance deferrals. On January 24, 2000, NSA headquarters suffered a total network outage for three days caused by an overloaded network. Incoming traffic was successfully stored on agency servers, but it could not be directed and processed. The agency carried out emergency repairs for \$3 million to get the system running again (some incoming traffic was also directed instead to Britain's [GCHQ](wikipedia://en/12884) for the time being). Director [Michael Hayden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Hayden_%28general%29) called the outage a "wake-up call" for the need to invest in the agency's infrastructure.[^o55]

In the 1990s the defensive arm of the NSA—the Information Assurance Directorate (IAD)—started working more openly; the first public technical talk by an NSA scientist at a major cryptography conference was J. Solinas' presentation on efficient [Elliptic Curve Cryptography](wikipedia://en/9966) algorithms at Crypto 1997.[^o56] The IAD's cooperative approach to academia and industry culminated in its support for a [transparent process](wikipedia://en/99431) for replacing the outdated [Data Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/7978) (DES) by an [Advanced Encryption Standard](wikipedia://en/1260) (AES). Cybersecurity policy expert [Susan Landau](wikipedia://en/19313149) attributes the NSA's harmonious collaboration with industry and academia in the selection of the AES in 2000—and the Agency's support for the choice of a strong encryption algorithm designed by Europeans rather than by Americans—to [Brian Snow](wikipedia://en/20915550), who was the Technical Director of IAD and represented the NSA as cochairman of the Technical Working Group for the AES competition, and Michael Jacobs, who headed IAD at the time.[^o57]

After the [terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11_attacks), the NSA believed that it had public support for a dramatic expansion of its surveillance activities.[^o58] According to [Neal Koblitz](wikipedia://en/1587628) and [Alfred Menezes](wikipedia://en/4356319), the period when the NSA was a trusted partner with academia and industry in the development of cryptographic standards started to come to an end when, as part of the change in the NSA in the post-September 11 era, Snow was replaced as Technical Director, Jacobs retired, and IAD could no longer effectively oppose proposed actions by the offensive arm of the NSA.[^o59]

### War on terror {#War_on_terror}

In the aftermath of the [September 11 attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11_attacks), the NSA created new IT systems to deal with the flood of information from new technologies like the Internet and cell phones. [ThinThread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ThinThread) contained advanced [data mining](wikipedia://en/42253) capabilities. It also had a "privacy mechanism"; surveillance was stored encrypted; decryption required a warrant. The research done under this program may have contributed to the technology used in later systems. ThinThread was canceled when Michael Hayden chose [Trailblazer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailblazer_Project), which did not include ThinThread's privacy system.[^o61]

[Trailblazer Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trailblazer_Project) ramped up in 2002 and was worked on by [Science Applications International Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Applications_International_Corporation) (SAIC), [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing), [Computer Sciences Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Sciences_Corporation), [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM), and [Litton Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litton_Industries). Some NSA [whistleblowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whistleblower) complained internally about major problems surrounding Trailblazer. This led to investigations by Congress and the NSA and DoD [Inspectors General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspectors_General). The project was canceled in early 2004. [Turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence_%28NSA%29) started in 2005. It was developed in small, inexpensive "test" pieces, rather than one grand plan like Trailblazer. It also included offensive cyber-warfare capabilities, like injecting [malware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malware) into remote computers. Congress criticized Turbulence in 2007 for having similar bureaucratic problems as Trailblazer.[^o62] It was to be a realization of information processing at higher speeds in cyberspace.[^o63]

### Global surveillance program disclosures {#Global_surveillance_program_disclosures}

The massive extent of the NSA's spying, both foreign and domestic, was revealed to the public in a series of detailed disclosures of internal NSA documents beginning in June 2013. Most of the disclosures were leaked by former NSA contractor [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden). On September 4, 2020, the NSA's surveillance program was ruled unlawful by the [US Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Courts_of_Appeals). The court also added that the US intelligence leaders, who publicly defended it, were not telling the truth.[^o65]

## Mission {#Mission}

NSA's [eavesdropping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eavesdropping) mission includes radio broadcasting, both from various organizations and individuals, the Internet, telephone calls, and other intercepted forms of communication. Its secure communications mission includes military, diplomatic, and all other sensitive, confidential, or secret government communications.[^o67]

According to a 2010 article in *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post)*, "every day, collection systems at the National Security Agency intercept and store 1.7 billion e-mails, phone calls and other types of communications. The NSA sorts a fraction of those into 70 separate databases."[^o68]

Because of its listening task, NSA/CSS has been heavily involved in [cryptanalytic](wikipedia://en/5715) research, continuing the work of predecessor agencies which had broken many World War II [codes](wikipedia://en/838989) and [ciphers](wikipedia://en/5244) (see, for instance, [Purple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_%28cipher_machine%29), [Venona project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venona_project), and [JN-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JN-25)). In 2004, NSA [Central Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Security_Service) and the [National Cyber Security Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Cyber_Security_Division) of the [Department of Homeland Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Homeland_Security) (DHS) agreed to expand the NSA Centers of Academic Excellence in Information Assurance Education Program.[^o69]

As part of the [National Security Presidential Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Directive) 54/Homeland Security Presidential Directive 23 (NSPD 54), signed on January 8, 2008, by President Bush, the NSA became the lead agency to monitor and protect all of the federal government's computer networks from [cyber-terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyber-terrorism).[^o70] A part of the NSA's mission is to serve as a [combat support agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_support_agency) for the Department of Defense.[^o71]

## Operations {#Operations}

Operations by the National Security Agency can be divided into three types:

- Collection overseas, which falls under the responsibility of the Global Access Operations (GAO) division.
- Domestic collection, which falls under the responsibility of the [Special Source Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Source_Operations) (SSO) division.
- Hacking operations, which fall under the responsibility of the [Tailored Access Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailored_Access_Operations) (TAO) division.

### Collection overseas {#Collection_overseas}

#### Echelon {#Echelon}

"Echelon" was created in the incubator of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War).[^o75] Today it is a [legacy system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_system), and several NSA stations are closing.[^o242] NSA/CSS, in combination with the equivalent agencies in the United Kingdom ([Government Communications Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Headquarters)), Canada ([Communications Security Establishment](wikipedia://en/231712)), Australia ([Australian Signals Directorate](wikipedia://en/37127)), and New Zealand ([Government Communications Security Bureau](wikipedia://en/661849)), otherwise known as the [UKUSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKUSA_Agreement) group,[^o76] was reported to be in command of the operation of the so-called [ECHELON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECHELON) system. Its capabilities were suspected to include the ability to monitor a large proportion of the world's transmitted civilian telephone, fax, and data traffic.[^o77]

During the early 1970s, the first of what became more than eight large satellite communications dishes were installed at Menwith Hill.[^o78] Investigative journalist [Duncan Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncan_Campbell_%28journalist,_born_1952%29) reported in 1988 on the "[ECHELON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECHELON)" surveillance program, an extension of the [UKUSA Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKUSA_Agreement) on global signals intelligence [SIGINT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIGINT), and detailed how the eavesdropping operations worked.[^o79] On November 3, 1999, the BBC reported that they had confirmation from the Australian Government of the existence of a powerful "global spying network" code-named Echelon, that could "eavesdrop on every single phone call, fax or e-mail, anywhere on the planet" with Britain and the United States as the chief protagonists. They confirmed that Menwith Hill was "linked directly to the headquarters of the US National Security Agency (NSA) at Fort Meade in Maryland".[^o80] NSA's United States Signals Intelligence Directive 18 (USSID 18) strictly prohibited the interception or collection of information about "... [U.S. persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._person), entities, corporations or organizations...." without explicit written legal permission from the [United States Attorney General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Attorney_General) when the subject is located abroad, or the [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Court) when within U.S. borders. Alleged Echelon-related activities, including its use for motives other than national security, including political and [industrial espionage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_espionage), received criticism from countries outside the UKUSA alliance.[^o81]

#### Other SIGINT overseas operations {#Other_SIGINT_overseas_operations}

The NSA was also involved in planning to blackmail people with "[SEXINT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEXINT)", intelligence gained about a potential target's sexual activity and preferences. Those targeted had not committed any apparent crime nor were they charged with one.[^o83] To support its [facial recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_recognition_system) program, the NSA is intercepting "millions of images per day".[^o84] The [Real Time Regional Gateway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Time_Regional_Gateway) is a data collection program introduced in 2005 in Iraq by the NSA during the [Iraq War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq_War) that consisted of gathering all electronic communication, storing it, then searching and otherwise analyzing it. It was effective in providing information about Iraqi insurgents who had eluded less comprehensive techniques.[^o85] This "collect it all" strategy introduced by NSA director, [Keith B. Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_B._Alexander), is believed by [Glenn Greenwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Greenwald) of *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* to be the model for the comprehensive worldwide mass archiving of communications which NSA is engaged in as of 2013.[^o86]

A dedicated unit of the NSA locates targets for the [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA) for extrajudicial assassination in the Middle East.[^o87] The NSA has also spied extensively on the European Union, the United Nations, and numerous governments including allies and trading partners in Europe, South America, and Asia.[^o88][^o89] In June 2015, [WikiLeaks](wikipedia://en/8877168) published documents showing that NSA spied on French companies.[^o90] WikiLeaks also published documents showing that NSA spied on federal German ministries since the 1990s.[^o91][^o92] Even Germany's Chancellor [Angela Merkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Merkel)'s cellphones and phones of her predecessors had been intercepted.[^o93]

#### Boundless Informant {#Boundless_Informant}

In June 2013, [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden) revealed that between February 8 and March 8, 2013, the NSA collected about 124.8 billion telephone data items and 97.1 billion computer data items throughout the world, as was displayed in charts from an internal NSA tool codenamed [Boundless Informant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundless_Informant). Initially, it was reported that some of these data reflected eavesdropping on citizens in countries like Germany, Spain, and France,[^o95] but later on, it became clear that those data were collected by European agencies during military missions abroad and were subsequently shared with NSA.

#### Bypassing encryption {#Bypassing_encryption}

In 2013, reporters uncovered a secret memo that claims the NSA created and pushed for the adoption of the [Dual EC DRBG](wikipedia://en/14259066) encryption standard that contained built-in vulnerabilities in 2006 to the United States [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) (NIST), and the [International Organization for Standardization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Organization_for_Standardization) (aka ISO).[^o97][^o99] This memo appears to give credence to previous speculation by cryptographers at [Microsoft Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Research).[^o98] [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden) claims that the NSA often bypasses the encryption process altogether by lifting information before encryption or after decryption.[^o99]

[XKeyscore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XKeyscore) rules (as specified in a file xkeyscorerules100.txt, sourced by German TV stations [NDR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norddeutscher_Rundfunk) and [WDR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westdeutscher_Rundfunk), who claim to have excerpts from its source code) reveal that the NSA tracks users of privacy-enhancing software tools, including [Tor](wikipedia://en/20556944); an anonymous email service provided by the [MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Computer_Science_and_Artificial_Intelligence_Laboratory) (CSAIL) in Cambridge, Massachusetts; and readers of the *[Linux Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux_Journal)*.[^o100][^o101]

#### Software backdoors {#Software_backdoors}

[Linus Torvalds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Torvalds), the founder of [Linux kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux_kernel), joked during a [LinuxCon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LinuxCon) keynote on September 18, 2013, that the NSA, who is the founder of [SELinux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SELinux), wanted a backdoor in the kernel.[^o103] However, later, Linus' father, a [Member of the European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_the_European_Parliament) (MEP), revealed that the NSA actually did this.[^o104]

When my oldest son was asked the same question: "Has he been approached by the NSA about backdoors?" he said "No", but at the same time he nodded. Then he was sort of in the legal free. He had given the right answer, everybody understood that the NSA had approached him. [IBM Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Notes) was the first widely adopted software product to use [public key cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key_cryptography) for client-server and server–server authentication and encryption of data. Until US laws regulating encryption were changed in 2000, IBM and [Lotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotus_Development) were prohibited from exporting versions of Notes that supported [symmetric encryption](wikipedia://en/53042) keys that were longer than 40 bits. In 1997, Lotus negotiated an agreement with the NSA that allowed the export of a version that supported stronger keys with 64 bits, but 24 of the bits were encrypted with a special key and included in the message to provide a "workload reduction factor" for the NSA. This strengthened the protection for users of Notes outside the US against private-sector [industrial espionage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_espionage), but not against spying by the US government.[^o106][^o107]

#### Boomerang routing {#Boomerang_routing}

While it is assumed that foreign transmissions terminating in the U.S. (such as a non-U.S. citizen accessing a U.S. website) subject non-U.S. citizens to NSA surveillance, recent research into boomerang routing has raised new concerns about the NSA's ability to surveil the domestic Internet traffic of foreign countries.[^o21] Boomerang routing occurs when an Internet transmission that originates and terminates in a single country transits another. Research at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) has suggested that approximately 25% of Canadian domestic traffic may be subject to NSA surveillance activities as a result of the boomerang routing of Canadian [Internet service providers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_service_provider).[^o21]

#### Implanting hardware equipment {#Implanting_hardware_equipment}

A document included in the NSA files released with [Glenn Greenwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_Greenwald)'s book *[No Place to Hide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Place_to_Hide_%28Greenwald_book%29)* details how the agency's [Tailored Access Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailored_Access_Operations) (TAO) and other NSA units gained access to hardware equipment. They intercepted [routers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Router_%28computing%29), [servers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Server_%28computing%29), and other [network hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_hardware) equipment being shipped to organizations targeted for surveillance and installing covert implant firmware onto them before they are delivered. This was described by an NSA manager as "some of the most productive operations in TAO because they preposition access points into hard target networks around the world."[^o110]

Computers that were seized by the NSA due to [interdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdiction) are often modified with a physical device known as Cottonmouth.[^o111] It is a device that can be inserted at the USB port of a computer to establish remote access to the targeted machine. According to the NSA's Tailored Access Operations (TAO) group implant catalog, after implanting Cottonmouth, the NSA can establish a [network bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_bridge) "that allows the NSA to load exploit software onto modified computers as well as allowing the NSA to relay commands and data between hardware and software implants."[^o112]

### Domestic collection {#Domestic_collection}

NSA's mission, as outlined in [Executive Order 12333](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_Order_12333) in 1981, is to collect information that constitutes "foreign intelligence or counterintelligence" while *not* "acquiring information concerning the domestic activities of [United States persons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_person)". NSA has declared that it relies on the FBI to collect information on foreign intelligence activities within the borders of the United States while confining its activities within the United States to the embassies and missions of foreign nations.[^o114]

The appearance of a 'Domestic Surveillance Directorate' of the NSA was soon exposed as a hoax in 2013.[^o115][^o116] NSA's domestic surveillance activities are limited by the requirements imposed by the [Fourth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_U.S._Constitution). The [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Court) for example held in October 2011, citing multiple Supreme Court precedents, that the Fourth Amendment prohibitions against unreasonable searches and seizures apply to the contents of all communications, whatever the means, because "a person's private communications are akin to personal papers."[^o117] However, these protections do not apply to non-U.S. persons located outside of U.S. borders, so the NSA's foreign surveillance efforts are subject to far fewer limitations under U.S. law.[^o118] The specific requirements for domestic surveillance operations are contained in the [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Act) of 1978 (FISA), which does not extend protection to non-U.S. citizens located outside of [U.S. territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._territory).[^o118]

#### President's Surveillance Program {#President's_Surveillance_Program}

[George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush), president during the [9/11 terrorist attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_11_attacks), approved the [Patriot Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_Act) shortly after the attacks to take anti-terrorist security measures. [Titles 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_I_of_the_Patriot_Act), [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_II_of_the_Patriot_Act), and [9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_IX_of_the_Patriot_Act) specifically authorized measures that would be taken by the NSA. These titles granted enhanced domestic security against terrorism, surveillance procedures, and improved intelligence, respectively. On March 10, 2004, there was a debate between President Bush and White House Counsel [Alberto Gonzales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Gonzales), Attorney General [John Ashcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ashcroft), and Acting Attorney General [James Comey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Comey). The Attorneys General were unsure if the NSA's programs could be considered constitutional. They threatened to resign over the matter, but ultimately the NSA's programs continued.[^o120] On March 11, 2004, President Bush signed a new authorization for mass surveillance of Internet records, in addition to the surveillance of phone records. This allowed the president to be able to override laws such as the [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Act), which protected civilians from mass surveillance. In addition to this, President Bush also signed that the measures of mass surveillance were also retroactively in place.[^o426][^o121]

One such surveillance program, authorized by the U.S. Signals Intelligence Directive 18 of President George Bush, was the Highlander Project undertaken for the National Security Agency by the U.S. Army [513th Military Intelligence Brigade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/513th_Military_Intelligence_Brigade). NSA relayed telephone (including cell phone) conversations obtained from ground, airborne, and satellite monitoring stations to various U.S. Army Signal Intelligence Officers, including the [201st Military Intelligence Battalion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/201st_Expeditionary_Military_Intelligence_Brigade). Conversations of citizens of the U.S. were intercepted, along with those of other nations.[^o122] Proponents of the surveillance program claim that the President has [executive authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_executive_theory) to order such action, arguing that laws such as FISA are overridden by the President's Constitutional powers. In addition, some argued that FISA was implicitly overridden by a subsequent statute, the [Authorization for Use of Military Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorization_for_Use_of_Military_Force_Against_Terrorists), although the Supreme Court's ruling in *[Hamdan v. Rumsfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamdan_v._Rumsfeld)* deprecates this view.[^o123]

#### The PRISM program {#The_PRISM_program}

*Image: Prism-slide-8.jpg.* PRISM: a [clandestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clandestine_operation) [surveillance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surveillance) programs under which the NSA collects large amounts of user data from companies such as [Facebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook) and [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft).

Under the [PRISM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRISM_%28surveillance_program%29) program, which started in 2007,[^o125][^o126] NSA gathers Internet communications from foreign targets from nine major U.S. Internet-based communication service providers: [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft),[^o127] [Yahoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahoo), [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google), [Facebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook), [PalTalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PalTalk), [AOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AOL), [Skype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skype), [YouTube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube), and [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc.). Data gathered include email, videos, photos, [VoIP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VoIP) chats such as Skype, and file transfers.

Former NSA director General Keith Alexander claimed that in September 2009 the NSA prevented [Najibullah Zazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Najibullah_Zazi) and his friends from carrying out a terrorist attack.[^o128] However, no evidence has been presented demonstrating that the NSA has ever been instrumental in preventing a terrorist attack.[^o129][^o130][^o131][^o132]

::: {#o134 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="FASCIA (database)"}

#### The FASCIA database {#The_FASCIA_database}

**FASCIA** is a [database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database) created and used by the U.S. National Security Agency that contains trillions of device-location records that are collected from a variety of sources.[^o135] Its existence was revealed during the 2013 [global surveillance disclosure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surveillance_disclosure) by [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden).[^o136]

The FASCIA database stores various types of information, including [Location Area Codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location_area_identity) (LACs), [Cell Tower IDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_ID) (CeLLIDs), [Visitor Location Registers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visitor_Location_Register) (VLRs), [International Mobile Station Equipment Identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Mobile_Station_Equipment_Identity) (IMEIs) and [MSISDNs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MSISDN) (Mobile Subscriber Integrated Services Digital Network-Numbers).[^o135][^o136] Over about seven months, more than 27 [terabytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terabyte) of location data were collected and stored in the database.[^o137]

:::

#### Commercial Solutions for Classified (CSfC) {#Commercial_Solutions_for_Classified_(CSfC)}

Commercial Solutions for Classified (CSfC) is a key component of the NSA's commercial cybersecurity strategy. CSfC-validated commercial products are proven to meet rigorous security requirements for protection of classified National Security Systems (NSS) data. Once validated, the [Department of Defense (DoD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense), Intelligence Community, Military Services, and other U.S. government agencies are able to implement these commercial hardware and software technologies into their data protection and cybersecurity solutions.[^o139]

### Hacking operations {#Hacking_operations}

Besides the more traditional ways of eavesdropping to collect signals intelligence, the NSA is also engaged in [hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hacker_%28computer_security%29) computers, smartphones, and their networks. A division that conducts such operations is the [Tailored Access Operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailored_Access_Operations) (TAO) division, which has been active since at least circa 1998.[^o141]

According to the *[Foreign Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Policy)* magazine, "... the Office of Tailored Access Operations, or TAO, has successfully penetrated Chinese computer and telecommunications systems for almost 15 years, generating some of the best and most reliable intelligence information about what is going on inside the People's Republic of China."[^o142][^o143] In an interview with *[Wired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wired_%28magazine%29)* magazine, Edward Snowden said the Tailored Access Operations division accidentally caused Syria's internet blackout in 2012.[^o144]

## Organizational structure {#Organizational_structure}

*Image: GEN Joshua M. Rudd.jpg.* [Joshua M. Rudd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_M._Rudd), the director of the NSA

The NSA is led by the [Director of the National Security Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_the_National_Security_Agency) (DIRNSA), who also serves as Chief of the [Central Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Security_Service) (CHCSS) and Commander of the [United States Cyber Command](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Cyber_Command) (USCYBERCOM) and is the highest-ranking military official of these organizations. He is assisted by a [Deputy Director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deputy_Director_of_the_National_Security_Agency), who is the highest-ranking civilian within the NSA/CSS. NSA also has an [Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspector_General), head of the Office of the Inspector General (OIG);[^o146] a [General Counsel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Counsel), head of the Office of the General Counsel (OGC); and a Director of Compliance, who is head of the Office of the Director of Compliance (ODOC).[^o147] The [National Security Agency Office of Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Agency_Office_of_Inspector_General) has worked on cases in collaboration with the [United States Department of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice) and the [Central Intelligence Agency Office of Inspector General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Intelligence_Agency_Office_of_Inspector_General).[^o148] Unlike other intelligence organizations such as the [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Intelligence_Agency) or [DIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Intelligence_Agency), the NSA has always been particularly reticent concerning its internal organizational structure.

As of the mid-1990s, the National Security Agency was organized into five Directorates:

- The Operations Directorate, which was responsible for SIGINT collection and processing.
- The Technology and Systems Directorate, which develops new technologies for SIGINT collection and processing.
- The Information Systems Security Directorate, which was responsible for NSA's communications and [information security](wikipedia://en/15036) missions.
- The Plans, Policy, and Programs Directorate, which provided staff support and general direction for the Agency.
- The Support Services Directorate, which provided logistical and administrative support activities.[^o149]

Each of these directorates consisted of several groups or elements, designated by a letter. There were for example the A Group, which was responsible for all SIGINT operations against the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, and the G Group, which was responsible for SIGINT related to all non-communist countries. These groups were divided into units designated by an additional number, like unit A5 for breaking Soviet codes, and G6, being the office for the Middle East, North Africa, Cuba, and Central and South America.[^o150][^o151]

### Directorates {#Directorates}

, NSA has about a dozen directorates, which are designated by a letter, although not all of them are publicly known.[^o153]

In the year 2000, a leadership team was formed consisting of the director, the deputy director, and the directors of the Signals Intelligence (SID), the Information Assurance (IAD) and the Technical Directorate (TD). The chiefs of other main NSA divisions became associate directors of the senior leadership team.[^o154] After President George W. Bush initiated the [President's Surveillance Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President's_Surveillance_Program) (PSP) in 2001, the NSA created a 24-hour Metadata Analysis Center (MAC), followed in 2004 by the Advanced Analysis Division (AAD), with the mission of analyzing content, Internet metadata and telephone metadata. Both units were part of the Signals Intelligence Directorate.[^o155]

In 2016, a proposal combined the Signals Intelligence Directorate with the Information Assurance Directorate into a Directorate of Operations.[^o156]

### NSANet {#NSANet}

*Image: Intel GreenDoor.jpg.* *Behind the Green Door* – Secure communications room with separate computer terminals for access to [SIPRNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIPRNet), GWAN, [NSANet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSANet), and [JWICS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JWICS)

NSANet stands for National Security Agency Network and is the official NSA [intranet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intranet).[^o159] It is a classified network,[^o160] for information up to the level of [TS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classified_information)/[SCI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitive_Compartmented_Information)[^o161] to support the use and sharing of intelligence data between NSA and the signals intelligence agencies of the four other nations of the [Five Eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Eyes) partnership. The management of NSANet has been delegated to the [Central Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Security_Service) Texas (CSSTEXAS).[^o162]

NSANet is a highly secured computer network consisting of fiber-optic and satellite communication channels that are almost completely separated from the public Internet. The network allows NSA personnel and civilian and military intelligence analysts anywhere in the world to have access to the agency's systems and databases. This access is tightly controlled and monitored. For example, every keystroke is logged, activities are audited at random, and downloading and printing of documents from NSANet are recorded.[^o163] In 1998, NSANet, along with [NIPRNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIPRNet) and [SIPRNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIPRNet), had "significant problems with poor search capabilities, unorganized data, and old information".[^o164] In 2004, the network was reported to have used over twenty [commercial off-the-shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_off-the-shelf) operating systems.[^o165] Some universities that do highly sensitive research are allowed to connect to it.[^o166] The thousands of Top Secret internal NSA documents that were taken by [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden) in 2013 were stored in "a file-sharing location on the NSA's intranet site"; so, they could easily be read online by NSA personnel. Everyone with a TS/SCI clearance had access to these documents. As a system administrator, Snowden was responsible for moving accidentally misplaced highly sensitive documents to safer storage locations.[^o167]

:::

### Watch centers {#Watch_centers}

The NSA maintains at least two watch centers:

- [National Security Operations Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Operations_Center) (NSOC), which is the NSA's current operations center and focal point for time-sensitive SIGINT reporting for the United States SIGINT System (USSS). This center was established in 1968 as the National SIGINT Watch Center (NSWC) and was renamed into National SIGINT Operations Center (NSOC) in 1973. This "nerve center of the NSA" got its current name in 1996.
- NSA/CSS Threat Operations Center (NTOC), which is the primary NSA/CSS partner for [Department of Homeland Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Homeland_Security) response to cyber incidents. The NTOC establishes real-time network awareness and threat characterization capabilities to forecast, alert, and attribute malicious activity and enable the coordination of Computer Network Operations. The NTOC was established in 2004 as a joint Information Assurance and Signals Intelligence project.[^o169]

::: {#o171 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="NSA Police"}

### NSA Police {#NSA_Police}

The NSA has its law enforcement team, known as the *NSA Police* (and formerly as *NSA Security Protective Force*) which provides law enforcement services, emergency response, and physical security to its officials and properties.[^o172]

NSA Police are armed federal officers. NSA Police has a K9 division, which generally conducts explosive detection screening of mail, vehicles, and cargo entering NSA grounds.[^o173] They use marked vehicles to carry out patrols.[^o174]

## Employees {#Employees}

The number of NSA employees is officially classified[^o176] but there are several sources providing estimates. In 1961, the NSA had 59,000 military and civilian employees, which grew to 93,067 in 1969, of which 19,300 worked at the headquarters at Fort Meade. In the early 1980s, NSA had roughly 50,000 military and civilian personnel. By 1989 this number had grown again to 75,000, of which 25,000 worked at the NSA headquarters. Between 1990 and 1995 the NSA's budget and workforce were cut by one-third, which led to a substantial loss of experience.[^o177]

In 2012, the NSA said more than 30,000 employees worked at Fort Meade and other facilities.[^o3] In 2012, [John C. Inglis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Inglis), the deputy director, said that the total number of NSA employees is "somewhere between 37,000 and one billion" as a joke,[^o176] and stated that the agency is "probably the biggest employer of [introverts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraversion_and_introversion)."[^o176] In 2013 *[Der Spiegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Spiegel)* stated that the NSA had 40,000 employees.[^o178] More widely, it has been described as the world's largest single employer of mathematicians.[^o179] Some NSA employees form part of the workforce of the [National Reconnaissance Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Reconnaissance_Office) (NRO), the agency that provides the NSA with satellite [signals intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_intelligence). As of 2013 about 1,000 [system administrators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_administrator) work for the NSA.[^o182]

### Personnel security {#Personnel_security}

The NSA received criticism early on in 1960 after [two agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_and_Mitchell_defection) had defected to the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union). Investigations by the [House Un-American Activities Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Un-American_Activities_Committee) and a special subcommittee of the [United States House Committee on Armed Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Committee_on_Armed_Services) revealed severe cases of ignorance of personnel security regulations, prompting the former personnel director and the director of security to step down and leading to the adoption of stricter security practices.[^o181] Nonetheless, security breaches reoccurred only a year later when in an issue of *[Izvestia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izvestia)* of July 23, 1963, a former NSA employee published several cryptologic secrets. The very same day, an NSA clerk-messenger committed suicide as ongoing investigations disclosed that he had sold secret information to the Soviets regularly. The reluctance of congressional houses to look into these affairs prompted a journalist to write, "If a similar series of tragic blunders occurred in any ordinary agency of Government an aroused public would insist that those responsible be officially censured, demoted, or fired." [David Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Kahn_%28writer%29) criticized the NSA's tactics of concealing its doings as smug and the Congress' blind faith in the agency's right-doing as shortsighted and pointed out the necessity of surveillance by the Congress to prevent abuse of power.[^o181]

[Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden)'s leaking of the existence of [PRISM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRISM_%28surveillance_program%29) in 2013 caused the NSA to institute a "[two-man rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-man_rule)", where two system administrators are required to be present when one accesses certain sensitive information.[^o182] Snowden claims he suggested such a rule in 2009.[^o183]

#### Polygraphing {#Polygraphing}

*Image: DOD polygraph brochure.pdf.* [Defense Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Security_Service) (DSS) polygraph brochure given to NSA applicants

The NSA conducts [polygraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygraph) tests of employees. For new employees, the tests are meant to discover enemy spies who are applying to the NSA and to uncover any information that could make an applicant pliant to coercion.[^o185] As part of the latter, historically *EPQs* or "embarrassing personal questions" about sexual behavior had been included in the NSA polygraph.[^o185] The NSA also conducts five-year periodic reinvestigation polygraphs of employees, focusing on counterintelligence programs. In addition, the NSA conducts periodic polygraph investigations to find spies and leakers; those who refuse to take them may receive "termination of employment", according to a 1982 memorandum from the director of the NSA.[^o186]

*Image: NSApolygraphvideo.webm.* NSA-produced video on the polygraph process

There are also "special access examination" polygraphs for employees who wish to work in highly sensitive areas, and those polygraphs cover counterintelligence questions and some questions about behavior.[^o186] NSA's brochure states that the average test length is between two and four hours.[^o187] A 1983 report of the [Office of Technology Assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Technology_Assessment) stated that "It appears that the NSA \[National Security Agency\] (and possibly CIA) use the polygraph not to determine deception or truthfulness per se, but as a technique of interrogation to encourage admissions."[^o188] Sometimes applicants in the polygraph process confess to committing felonies such as murder, rape, and selling of illegal drugs. Between 1974 and 1979, of the 20,511 job applicants who took polygraph tests, 695 (3.4%) confessed to previous felony crimes; almost all of those crimes had been undetected.[^o185]

In 2010 the NSA produced a video explaining its polygraph process.[^o189] The video, ten minutes long, is titled "The Truth About the Polygraph" and was posted to the Web site of the [Defense Security Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Security_Service). Jeff Stein of *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post)* said that the video portrays "various applicants, or actors playing them—it's not clear—describing everything bad they had heard about the test, the implication being that none of it is true."[^o190] AntiPolygraph.org argues that the NSA-produced video omits some information about the polygraph process; it produced a video responding to the NSA video.[^o189][^o191] George Maschke, the founder of the Web site, accused the NSA polygraph video of being "[Orwellian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orwellian)".[^o190]

In 2013, an article indicated that after [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden) revealed his identity in 2013, the NSA began requiring polygraphing of employees once per quarter.[^o192]

### Arbitrary firing {#Arbitrary_firing}

The number of exemptions from legal requirements has been criticized. When in 1964 Congress was hearing a bill giving the director of the NSA the power to fire at will any employee, *The Washington Post* wrote: "This is the very definition of arbitrariness. It means that an employee could be discharged and disgraced based on anonymous allegations without the slightest opportunity to defend himself." Yet, the bill was accepted by an overwhelming majority.[^o181] Also, every person hired to a job in the US after 2007, at any private organization, state or federal government agency, *must* be reported to the [New Hire Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hire_Registry), ostensibly to look for [child support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_support) evaders, *except* that employees of an intelligence agency may be excluded from reporting if the director deems it necessary for national security reasons.[^o194]

## Facilities {#Facilities}

::: {#o197 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="NSA building"}

### Headquarters {#Headquarters}

#### History of headquarters {#History_of_headquarters}

*Image: NSA-Fort Meade-1950.png.* Headquarters at [Fort Meade](wikipedia://en/129313) circa 1950s

When the agency was first established, its headquarters and cryptographic center were in the Naval Security Station in Washington, D.C. The COMINT functions were located in [Arlington Hall](wikipedia://en/340801) in [Northern Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Virginia), which served as the headquarters of the [U.S. Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army)'s cryptographic operations.[^o199] Because the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) had detonated a nuclear bomb and because the facilities were crowded, the federal government wanted to move several agencies, including the AFSA/NSA. A planning committee considered [Fort Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Knox), but [Fort Meade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_George_G._Meade), [Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland), was ultimately chosen as NSA headquarters because it was far enough away from Washington, D.C. in case of a nuclear strike and was close enough so its employees would not have to move their families.[^o200]

Construction of additional buildings began after the agency occupied buildings at Fort Meade in the late 1950s, which they soon outgrew.[^o200] In 1963 the new headquarters building, nine stories tall, opened. NSA workers referred to the building as the "Headquarters Building" and since the NSA management occupied the top floor, workers used "Ninth Floor" to refer to their leaders.[^o201] COMSEC remained in Washington, D.C., until its new building was completed in 1968.[^o200] In September 1986, the Operations 2A and 2B buildings, both copper-shielded to prevent [eavesdropping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eavesdropping), opened with a dedication by President [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan).[^o202] The four NSA buildings became known as the "Big Four."[^o202] The NSA director moved to 2B when it opened.[^o202]

*Image: National Security Agency, 2013.jpg.* National Security Agency headquarters in Fort Meade, 2013

Headquarters for the National Security Agency are in [Fort George G. Meade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_George_G._Meade), Maryland, although it is separate from other compounds and agencies that are based within this same military installation. Fort Meade is about 20 southwest of [Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore),[^o203] and 25 northeast of Washington, D.C.[^o204] The NSA has two dedicated exits off [Baltimore–Washington Parkway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore–Washington_Parkway). The Eastbound exit from the Parkway (heading toward Baltimore) is open to the public and provides employee access to its main campus and public access to the National Cryptology Museum. The Westbound side exit, (heading toward Washington) is labeled "NSA Employees Only".[^o205][^o206] The exit may only be used by people with the proper clearances, and security vehicles parked along the road guard the entrance.[^o207]

NSA is the largest employer in the state of Maryland, and two-thirds of its personnel work at Fort Meade.[^o224] Built on 350 acre[^o208] of Fort Meade's 5000 acre,[^o209] the site has 1,300 buildings and an estimated 18,000 parking spaces.[^o204][^o210]

The main NSA headquarters and operations building is what [James Bamford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bamford), author of *[Body of Secrets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_Secrets)*, describes as "a modern boxy structure" that appears similar to "any stylish office building."[^o211] The building is covered with one-way dark glass, which is lined with copper shielding to prevent espionage by trapping in signals and sounds.[^o211] It contains 3000000 sqft, or more than 68 acre, of floor space; Bamford said that the [U.S. Capitol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Capitol) "could easily fit inside it four times over."[^o211]

The facility has over 100 watchposts,[^o212] one of them being the visitor control center, a two-story area that serves as the entrance.[^o211] At the entrance, a white pentagonal structure,[^o213] visitor badges are issued to visitors and security clearances of employees are checked.[^o214] The visitor center includes a painting of the NSA seal.[^o213]

The OPS2A building, the tallest building in the NSA complex and the location of much of the agency's operations directorate is accessible from the visitor center. Bamford described it as a "dark glass [Rubik's Cube](wikipedia://en/25971)".[^o215] The facility's "red corridor" houses non-security operations such as concessions and the drug store. The name refers to the "red badge" which is worn by someone without a security clearance. The NSA headquarters includes a cafeteria, a credit union, ticket counters for airlines and entertainment, a barbershop, and a bank.[^o213] NSA headquarters has its own post office, fire department, and police force.[^o229][^o216][^o217]

The employees at the NSA headquarters reside in various places in the [Baltimore-Washington area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore-Washington_area), including [Annapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annapolis,_Maryland), Baltimore, and [Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia,_Maryland) in Maryland and the District of Columbia, including the [Georgetown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgetown,_District_of_Columbia) community.[^o218] The NSA maintains a shuttle service from the [Odenton station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odenton_station) of [MARC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MARC_Train) to its Visitor Control Center and has done so since 2005.[^o219]

#### Energy consumption {#Energy_consumption}

*Image: NeverSleeps 071310.jpg.* Due to massive amounts of [data processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_processing), NSA is the largest electricity consumer in Maryland.[^o224]

Following a major power outage in 2000, in 2003, and follow-ups through 2007, *[The Baltimore Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Baltimore_Sun)* reported that the NSA was at risk of electrical overload because of insufficient internal electrical infrastructure at Fort Meade to support the amount of equipment being installed. This problem was apparently recognized in the 1990s but not made a priority, and "now the agency's ability to keep its operations going is threatened."[^o221]

On August 6, 2006, *[The Baltimore Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Baltimore_Sun)* reported that the NSA had completely maxed out the grid and that Baltimore Gas & Electric (BGE, now [Constellation Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation_Energy)) was unable to sell them any more power.[^o222] NSA decided to move some of its operations to a new satellite facility. BGE provided NSA with 65 to 75 [megawatts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrawatt-hour) at Fort Meade in 2007 and expected that an increase of 10 to 15 megawatts would be needed later that year.[^o223] In 2011, the NSA was Maryland's largest consumer of power.[^o224] In 2007, as BGE's largest customer, NSA bought as much electricity as [Annapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annapolis,_Maryland), the capital city of Maryland.[^o221] One estimate put the potential for power consumption by the new [Utah Data Center](wikipedia://en/30341295) at 40 million per year.[^o240]

#### Computing assets {#Computing_assets}

In 1995, *[The Baltimore Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Baltimore_Sun)* reported that the NSA is the owner of the single largest group of [supercomputers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer).[^o226] NSA held a groundbreaking ceremony at Fort Meade in May 2013 for its High-Performance Computing Center 2, expected to open in 2016.[^o227] Called Site M, the center has a 150-megawatt power substation, 14 administrative buildings and 10 parking garages.[^o229] It cost US3.2 billion and covers 227 acre.[^o229] The center is 1800000 sqft[^o229] and initially uses 60 megawatts of electricity.[^o228] Increments II and III are expected to be completed by 2030 and would quadruple the space, covering 5800000 sqft with 60 buildings and 40 parking garages.[^o229] [Defense contractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_contractor) are also establishing or expanding [cybersecurity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybersecurity) facilities near the NSA and around the [Washington metropolitan area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_metropolitan_area).[^o229]

### National Computer Security Center {#National_Computer_Security_Center}

The DoD Computer Security Center was founded in 1981 and renamed the National Computer Security Center (NCSC) in 1985. NCSC was responsible for computer security throughout the federal government.[^o232] NCSC was part of NSA,[^o233] and during the late 1980s and the 1990s, NSA and NCSC published [Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trusted_Computer_System_Evaluation_Criteria) in a six-foot high [Rainbow Series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow_Series) of books that detailed trusted computing and network platform specifications.[^o234] The Rainbow books were replaced by the [Common Criteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Criteria), however, in the early 2000s.[^o234]

### Other facilities {#Other_facilities}

*Image: Buckley AFB.png.* [Buckley Space Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckley_Space_Force_Base) in Colorado

*Image: Utah Data Center Panorama (cropped).jpg.* [Utah Data Center](wikipedia://en/30341295)

NSA had facilities at [Friendship Annex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friendship_Annex) (FANX) in [Linthicum, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linthicum,_Maryland), which is a 20 to 25-minute drive from Fort Meade;[^o236] the [Aerospace Data Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_Data_Facility-Colorado) at [Buckley Space Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckley_Space_Force_Base) in [Aurora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora,_Colorado), Colorado; NSA Texas in the [Texas Cryptology Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Cryptology_Center) at [Lackland Air Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lackland_Air_Force_Base) in [San Antonio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio), Texas; NSA Georgia, [Georgia Cryptologic Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgia_Cryptologic_Center), Fort Gordon, [Augusta, Georgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusta,_Georgia); NSA Hawaii, [Hawaii Cryptologic Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawaii_Cryptologic_Center) in [Honolulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honolulu); the [Multiprogram Research Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiprogram_Research_Facility) in [Oak Ridge, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Ridge,_Tennessee), and elsewhere.[^o218][^o240]

In 2009, to protect its assets and access more electricity, NSA sought to decentralize and expand its existing facilities in Fort Meade and Menwith Hill,[^o237] the latter expansion expected to be completed by 2015.[^o238]

On January 6, 2011, a groundbreaking ceremony was held to begin construction on the NSA's first Comprehensive National Cyber-security Initiative (CNCI) Data Center, known as the "[Utah Data Center](wikipedia://en/30341295)" for short. The \$1.5B data center is being built at [Camp Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_Williams), Utah, located 25 mi south of [Salt Lake City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_City), and will help support the agency's National Cyber-security Initiative.[^o239] It is expected to be operational by September 2013.[^o240] Construction of Utah Data Center finished in May 2019.[^o241]

In 2012, NSA collected intelligence from four [geostationary satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_satellite).[^o240] Satellite receivers were at [Roaring Creek Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roaring_Creek_Station) in [Catawissa, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catawissa,_Pennsylvania) and [Salt Creek Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Creek_Station) in [Arbuckle, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbuckle,_California).[^o240] It operated ten to twenty [taps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_tapping) on U.S. telecom switches. NSA had installations in several U.S. states and from them observed intercepts from Europe, the Middle East, North Africa, Latin America, and Asia.[^o240] The *[Yakima Herald-Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakima_Herald-Republic)* cited Bamford, saying that many of NSA's bases for its Echelon program were a [legacy system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_system), using outdated, 1990s technology.[^o242] In 2004, NSA closed its operations at [Bad Aibling Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Aibling_Station) (Field Station 81) in [Bad Aibling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Aibling), Germany.[^o243] In 2012, NSA began to move some of its operations at Yakima Research Station, [Yakima Training Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakima_Training_Center), in Washington state to Colorado, planning to leave Yakima closed.[^o244] During 2013, NSA also intended to close operations at [Sugar Grove, West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Grove,_West_Virginia).[^o242]

### Global stations {#Global_stations}

*Image: Menwith-hill-radomes.jpg.* [RAF Menwith Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Menwith_Hill) has the largest NSA presence in the United Kingdom.[^o238]

Following the[^o246] [UKUSA Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UKUSA_Agreement) between the [Five Eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Eyes) that cooperated on [signals intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signals_intelligence) and [ECHELON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ECHELON),[^o247] NSA stations were built at [GCHQ Bude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GCHQ_Bude) in [Morwenstow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morwenstow), United Kingdom; [Geraldton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geraldton), [Pine Gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine_Gap) and [Shoal Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoal_Bay_Receiving_Station), Australia; [Leitrim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFS_Leitrim) and [Ottawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa), Ontario, Canada; [Misawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misawa,_Aomori), Japan; and [Waihopai](wikipedia://en/661849#Waihopai_station) and [Tangimoana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangimoana_Station),[^o248] New Zealand.[^o249]

NSA operates [RAF Menwith Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Menwith_Hill) in North Yorkshire, United Kingdom, which was, according to [BBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News) in 2007, the largest electronic monitoring station in the world.[^o250] Planned in 1954, and opened in 1960, the base covered 562 acre in 1999.[^o251] The agency's [European Cryptologic Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_Complex) (ECC), with 240 employees in 2011, is headquartered at a US military compound in [Griesheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griesheim_%28Hesse%29), near [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt) in Germany. A 2011 NSA report indicates that the ECC is responsible for the "largest analysis and productivity in Europe" and focuses on various priorities, including Africa, Europe, the Middle East, and counterterrorism operations.[^o252]

Since the mid-1980s, the NSA and Taiwan's [National Security Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Bureau_%28Taiwan%29) have jointly operated a signals intelligence (SIGINT) [listening station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listening_station) at [Yangmingshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangmingshan).[^o253][^o254][^o255]

In 2013, a new Consolidated Intelligence Center, also to be used by NSA, is being built at the headquarters of the [United States Army Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Europe) in [Wiesbaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiesbaden), Germany.[^o256] NSA's partnership with [Bundesnachrichtendienst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundesnachrichtendienst) (BND), the German foreign intelligence service, was confirmed by BND president [Gerhard Schindler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Schindler).[^o256]

#### Thailand {#Thailand}

Thailand is a "3rd party partner" of the NSA along with nine other nations.[^o258] These are non-English-speaking countries that have made security agreements for the exchange of SIGINT raw material and end product reports. Thailand is the site of at least two US SIGINT collection stations. One is at the [US Embassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Embassy) in [Bangkok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok), an NSA-[CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA) Joint Special Collection Service (JSCS) unit. It presumably eavesdrops on foreign consulates, embassies, governmental communications, and other targets of opportunity.[^o259]

The second installation is a FORNSAT (foreign satellite interception) station in the Thai city of [Khon Kaen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khon_Kaen). It is codenamed INDRA, but has also been referred to as LEMONWOOD.[^o259] The station is approximately 40 ha in size and consists of a large 3,700–4,600 m<sup>2</sup> (40,000–50,000 ft<sup>2</sup>) operations building on the west side of the ops compound and four [radome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radome)-enclosed [parabolic antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_antenna). Possibly two of the radome-enclosed antennas are used for SATCOM intercept and two antennas are used for relaying the intercepted material back to the NSA. There is also a PUSHER-type circularly-disposed antenna array (CDAA) just north of the ops compound.[^o260][^o261] NSA activated Khon Kaen in October 1979. Its mission was to eavesdrop on the radio traffic of [Chinese army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Liberation_Army) and [air force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Liberation_Army_Air_Force) units in southern China, especially in and around the city of [Kunming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunming) in Yunnan. In the late 1970s, the base consisted only of a small CDAA antenna array that was remote-controlled via satellite from the NSA listening post at [Kunia, Hawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunia_Camp,_Hawaii), and a small force of civilian contractors from [Bendix Field Engineering Corp.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bendix_Corporation) whose job it was to keep the antenna array and satellite relay facilities up and running 24/7.[^o260] According to the papers of the late General William Odom, the INDRA facility was upgraded in 1986 with a new British-made PUSHER CDAA antenna as part of an overall upgrade of NSA and Thai SIGINT facilities whose objective was to spy on the neighboring communist nations of Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia.[^o260] The base fell into disrepair in the 1990s as China and Vietnam became more friendly towards the US, and by 2002 archived satellite imagery showed that the PUSHER CDAA antenna had been torn down, perhaps indicating that the base had been closed. At some point in the period since [9/11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9/11), the Khon Kaen base was reactivated and expanded to include a sizeable SATCOM intercept mission. It is likely that the NSA presence at Khon Kaen is relatively small, and that most of the work is done by civilian contractors.[^o260]

## Research and development {#Research_and_development}

NSA has been involved in debates about public policy, both indirectly as a behind-the-scenes adviser to other departments, and directly during and after [Vice Admiral Bobby Ray Inman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Ray_Inman)'s directorship. NSA was a major player in the debates of the 1990s regarding the [export of cryptography in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Export_of_cryptography_in_the_United_States). Restrictions on export were reduced but not eliminated in 1996. Its secure government communications work has involved the NSA in numerous technology areas, including the design of specialized communications [hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware) and software, production of dedicated [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) at the [Ft. Meade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Meade,_Maryland) chip fabrication plant), and advanced [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) research. For 50 years, the NSA designed and built most of its in-house computer equipment, but from the 1990s until about 2003 (when the U.S. Congress curtailed the practice), the agency contracted with the private sector in the fields of research and equipment.[^o263]

### Data Encryption Standard {#Data_Encryption_Standard}

*Image: Frostburg.jpg.* [FROSTBURG](wikipedia://en/3764514) was the NSA's first [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer), used from 1991 to 1997.

NSA was embroiled in some controversy concerning its involvement in the creation of the Data Encryption Standard (DES), a standard and public [block cipher](wikipedia://en/4594) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) used by the [U.S. government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._government) and banking community.[^o265] During the development of DES by [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) in the 1970s, NSA recommended changes to some details of the design. There was suspicion that these changes had weakened the algorithm sufficiently to enable the agency to eavesdrop if required, including speculation that a critical component—the so-called [S-boxes](wikipedia://en/240991)—had been altered to insert a "[backdoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backdoor_%28computing%29)" and that the reduction in key length might have made it feasible for NSA to discover DES keys using massive computing power. It has since been observed that the S-boxes in DES are particularly resilient against [differential cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/8536), a technique that was not publicly discovered until the late 1980s but known to the IBM DES team.

### Advanced Encryption Standard {#Advanced_Encryption_Standard}

The involvement of the NSA in selecting a successor to the Data Encryption Standard (DES), the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), was limited to [hardware performance testing](wikipedia://en/99431).[^o267] NSA has subsequently certified AES for protection of classified information when used in NSA-approved systems.[^o268]

### NSA encryption systems {#NSA_encryption_systems}

- [KL-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KL-7) ADONIS off-line rotor encryption machine (post-WWII – 1980s)[^o271][^o272]
- [KW-26](wikipedia://en/1006090) ROMULUS electronic in-line teletypewriter encryptor (1960s–1980s)[^o273]
- [KW-37](wikipedia://en/1009397) JASON fleet broadcast encryptor (1960s–1990s)[^o272]
- KY-57 [VINSON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VINSON) tactical radio voice encryptor[^o273]
- [KG-84](wikipedia://en/1637220) Dedicated Data Encryption/Decryption[^o273]
- [STU-III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STU-III) secure telephone unit,[^o273] phased out by the [STE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_Terminal_Equipment)[^o274]

The NSA oversees encryption in the following systems that are in use today:

- [EKMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EKMS) Electronic Key Management System[^o275]
- [Fortezza](wikipedia://en/797842) encryption based on portable crypto token in [PC Card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PC_Card) format[^o276]

The NSA has specified [Suite A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA_Suite_A_Cryptography) and [Suite B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA_Suite_B_Cryptography) cryptographic algorithm suites to be used in U.S. government systems; the Suite B algorithms are a subset of those previously specified by [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) and are expected to serve for most information protection purposes, while the Suite A algorithms are secret and are intended for especially high levels of protection.[^o268]

### SHA {#SHA}

The widely used [SHA-1](wikipedia://en/26672) and [SHA-2](wikipedia://en/1638777) hash functions were designed by NSA. SHA-1 is a slight modification of the weaker [SHA-0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SHA-0) algorithm, also designed by NSA in 1993. This small modification was suggested by the NSA two years later, with no justification other than the fact that it provides additional security. An attack for SHA-0 that does not apply to the revised algorithm was indeed found between 1998 and 2005 by academic cryptographers. Because of weaknesses and key length restrictions in SHA-1, NIST deprecates its use for [digital signatures](wikipedia://en/59644) and approves only the newer SHA-2 algorithms for such applications from 2013 on.[^o280]

A new hash standard, [SHA-3](wikipedia://en/20394543), has recently been selected through the [competition](wikipedia://en/20051952) concluded on October 2, 2012, with the selection of [Keccak](wikipedia://en/20394543) as the algorithm. The process to select SHA-3 was similar to the one held in choosing the AES, but some doubts have been cast over it,[^o281][^o282] since fundamental modifications have been made to Keccak to turn it into a standard.[^o283] These changes potentially undermine the cryptanalysis performed during the competition and reduce the security levels of the algorithm.[^o281]

### Clipper chip {#Clipper_chip}

Because of concerns that widespread use of strong cryptography would hamper government use of [wiretaps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_tapping), the NSA proposed the concept of [key escrow](wikipedia://en/17114) in 1993 and introduced the Clipper chip that would offer stronger protection than DES but would allow access to encrypted data by authorized law enforcement officials.[^o285] The proposal was strongly opposed and key escrow requirements ultimately went nowhere.[^o286] However, NSA's [Fortezza](wikipedia://en/797842) hardware-based encryption cards, created for the Clipper project, are still used within government, and NSA ultimately declassified and published the design of the [Skipjack cipher](wikipedia://en/474702) used on the cards.[^o287][^o288]

### Dual EC DRBG random number generator crypto trojan {#Dual_EC_DRBG_random_number_generator_crypto_trojan}

NSA promoted the inclusion of a random number generator called [Dual EC DRBG](wikipedia://en/14259066) in the U.S. [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology)'s 2007 guidelines. This led to speculation of a [backdoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backdoor_%28computing%29) which would allow NSA access to data encrypted by systems using that [pseudorandom number generator](wikipedia://en/45524) (PRNG).[^o290]

This is now deemed to be plausible based on the fact that output of next iterations of PRNG can provably be determined if relation between two internal [Elliptic Curve](wikipedia://en/9966) points is known.[^o291][^o292] Both NIST and [RSA](wikipedia://en/301240) are now officially recommending against the use of this PRNG.[^o293][^o294]

### Perfect Citizen {#Perfect_Citizen}

Perfect Citizen is a program to perform [vulnerability assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulnerability_assessment_%28computing%29) by the NSA in the American [critical infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_infrastructure).[^o296][^o297] It was originally reported to be a program to develop a system of sensors to detect cyber attacks on critical infrastructure computer networks in both the private and public sector through a [network monitoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_monitoring) system named *Einstein*.[^o298][^o299] It is funded by the [Comprehensive National Cybersecurity Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comprehensive_National_Cybersecurity_Initiative) and thus far [Raytheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raytheon_Technologies) has received a contract for up to \$100 million for the initial stage.

### Academic research {#Academic_research}

The NSA has invested many millions of dollars in academic research under grant code prefix *MDA904*, resulting in over 3,000 papers The NSA publishes its documents through various publications.

- *Cryptolog* is published monthly by PI, Techniques, and Standards, for the Personnel of Operations. Declassified issues are available online.[^o302]
- The *Cryptologic Almanac* is a cryptology [academic journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_journal) published internally by the NSA.[^o303] It publishes short vignettes about NSA or NSA-related topics. A selection of articles published are available to the public online.[^o304]
- *Cryptologic Quarterly* was the combined result of the merger of *NSA Technical Journal* and *Cryptologic Spectrum* in 1981. It expanded its coverage to cover a larger segment of NSA readership.
- *Cryptologic Spectrum* was a [cryptology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptology) journal published internally by the NSA.[^o303] It was established in 1969, until consolidation with the *NSA Technical Journal* in 1981. A selection of articles published between 1969 and 1981 are available to the public online.[^o304] The journal had been [classified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classified_information_in_the_United_States) until its tables of contents were published online in September 2006 following a [Freedom of Information Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_Information_Act_%28United_States%29) request in 2003.[^o305]
- The *NSA Technical Journal* was established in 1954 by [Ralph J. Canine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_J._Canine) to "foster the exchange of ideas and create an 'intellectual community' within the Agency".[^o306] In 1981, the publication was consolidated with *Cryptologic Spectrum* into a single publication, called *Cryptologic Quarterly*.

Despite this, the NSA/CSS has, at times, attempted to restrict the publication of academic research into cryptography; for example, the [Khufu and Khafre](wikipedia://en/634695) block ciphers were voluntarily withheld in response to an NSA request to do so. In response to a [FOIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_information_in_the_United_States) lawsuit, in 2013 the NSA released the 643-page research paper titled, "Untangling the Web: A Guide to Internet Research",[^o307] written and compiled by NSA employees to assist other NSA workers in searching for information of interest to the agency on the public Internet.[^o308]

### Patents {#Patents}

NSA can file for a patent from the [U.S. Patent and Trademark Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Patent_and_Trademark_Office) under [gag order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gag_order). Unlike normal patents, these are not revealed to the public and do not expire. However, if the Patent Office receives an application for an identical patent from a third party, they will reveal the NSA's patent and officially grant it to the NSA for the full term on that date.[^o310]

One of NSA's published patents describes a method of [geographically locating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geolocation) an individual computer site in an Internet-like network, based on the [latency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latency_%28engineering%29) of multiple network connections.[^o311] Although no public patent exists, NSA is reported to have used a similar locating technology called trilateralization that allows real-time tracking of an individual's location, including altitude from ground level, using data obtained from cellphone towers.[^o312]

## Insignia and memorials {#Insignia_and_memorials}

*Image: National Security Agency.svg.*

The [heraldic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldic) insignia of NSA consists of an [eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bald_eagle) inside a circle, grasping a [key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_%28lock%29) in its talons.[^o315] The eagle represents the agency's national mission.[^o315] Its breast features a shield with bands of red and white, taken from the [Great Seal of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Seal_of_the_United_States) and representing Congress.[^o315] The key is taken from the emblem of [Saint Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Peter) and represents security.[^o315]

When the NSA was created, the agency had no emblem and used that of the Department of Defense.[^o316] The agency adopted its first of two emblems in 1963.[^o316] The current NSA insignia has been in use since 1965, when then-[Director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_the_National_Security_Agency), LTG [Marshall S. Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Carter) ([USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army)) ordered the creation of a device to represent the agency.[^o317] The NSA's flag consists of the agency's seal on a light blue background.

*Image: They Served In Silence - National Cryptologic Museum - DSC07636.JPG.* National Cryptologic Memorial

Crews associated with NSA missions have been involved in several dangerous and deadly situations.[^o318] The [USS *Liberty* incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Liberty_incident) in 1967 and [USS *Pueblo* incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Pueblo_%28AGER-2%29) in 1968 are examples of the losses endured during the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War).[^o318] The National Security Agency/Central Security Service Cryptologic Memorial honors and remembers the fallen personnel, both military and civilian, of these intelligence missions.[^o319] It is made of black granite, and has 171 names carved into it, [^o319] It is located at NSA headquarters. A tradition of declassifying the stories of the fallen was begun in 2001.[^o319]

## Constitutionality, legality, and privacy concerning operations {#Constitutionality,_legality,_and_privacy_concerning_operations}

In the United States, at least since 2001,[^o321] there has been legal controversy over what signal intelligence can be used for and how much freedom the National Security Agency has to use signal intelligence.[^o322] In 2015, the government made slight changes in how it uses and collects certain types of data,[^o323] specifically phone records. The government was not analyzing the phone records as of early 2019.[^o324] The surveillance programs were deemed unlawful in September 2020 in a court of appeals case.[^o65]

### Warrantless surveillance {#Warrantless_surveillance}

On December 16, 2005, *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* reported that under [White House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House) pressure and with an [executive order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_order) from President [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush), the National Security Agency, in an attempt to thwart terrorism, had been tapping phone calls made to persons outside the country, without obtaining [warrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warrant_%28law%29) from the [United States Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Court), a secret court created for that purpose under the [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Act) (FISA).[^o121]

### [Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden) {#Edward_Snowden}

Edward Snowden is a former American intelligence contractor who revealed in 2013 the existence of secret wide-ranging information-gathering programs conducted by the National Security Agency (NSA).[^o327] More specifically, Snowden released information that demonstrated how the United States government was gathering immense amounts of personal communications, emails, phone locations, web histories and more of American citizens without their knowledge.[^o328] One of Snowden's primary motivators for releasing this information was fear of a surveillance state developing as a result of the infrastructure being created by the NSA. As Snowden recounts, "I believe that, at this point in history, the greatest danger to our freedom and way of life comes from the reasonable fear of omniscient State powers kept in check by nothing more than policy documents... It is not that I do not value intelligence, but that I oppose . . . omniscient, automatic, mass surveillance. . . . That seems to me a greater threat to the institutions of free society than missed intelligence reports, and unworthy of the costs."[^o329]

In March 2014, Army General [Martin Dempsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Dempsey), [Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Joint_Chiefs_of_Staff), told the [House Armed Services Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_Committee_on_Armed_Services), "The vast majority of the documents that Snowden ... exfiltrated from our highest levels of security ... had nothing to do with exposing government oversight of domestic activities. The vast majority of those were related to our military capabilities, operations, tactics, techniques, and procedures."[^o330] When asked in a May 2014 interview to quantify the number of documents Snowden stole, retired NSA director Keith Alexander said there was no accurate way of counting what he took, but Snowden may have downloaded more than a million documents.[^o331]

### Other surveillance programs {#Other_surveillance_programs}

On January 17, 2006, the [Center for Constitutional Rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Constitutional_Rights) filed a lawsuit, [CCR v. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CCR_v._Bush), against the [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush) presidency. The lawsuit challenged the National Security Agency's (NSA's) surveillance of people within the U.S., including the interception of CCR emails without securing a warrant first.[^o333][^o334]

In the August 2006 case *[ACLU v. NSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACLU_v._NSA)*, [U.S. District Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._District_Court) Judge [Anna Diggs Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Diggs_Taylor) concluded that NSA's warrantless surveillance program was both illegal and unconstitutional. On July 6, 2007, the [6th Circuit Court of Appeals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/6th_Circuit_Court_of_Appeals) vacated the decision because the ACLU lacked standing to bring the suit.[^o335]

In September 2008, the [Electronic Frontier Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_Frontier_Foundation) (EFF) filed a [class action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_action) lawsuit against the NSA and several high-ranking officials of the [Bush administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_George_W._Bush),[^o336] charging an "illegal and unconstitutional program of dragnet communications surveillance,"[^o337] based on documentation provided by former [AT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT&T) technician [Mark Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Klein).[^o338]

As a result of the [USA Freedom Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Freedom_Act) passed by [Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Congress) in June 2015, the NSA had to shut down its bulk phone surveillance program on November 29 of the same year. The USA Freedom Act forbids the NSA to collect metadata and content of phone calls unless it has a warrant for terrorism investigation. In that case, the agency must ask the [telecom companies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_company) for the record, which will only be kept for six months. The NSA's use of large telecom companies to assist it with its surveillance efforts has caused several privacy concerns.[^o339]

### AT&T Internet monitoring {#AT&T_Internet_monitoring}

In May 2008, [Mark Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Klein), a former [AT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT&T_Inc.) employee, alleged that his company had cooperated with NSA in installing [Narus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narus_%28company%29) hardware to replace the FBI [Carnivore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnivore_%28software%29) program, to monitor network communications including traffic between U.S. citizens.[^o341]

### Data mining {#Data_mining}

NSA was reported in 2008 to use its computing capability to analyze "transactional" data that it regularly acquires from other government agencies, which gather it under their jurisdictional authorities.[^o343]

A 2013 advisory group for the Obama administration, seeking to reform NSA spying programs following the revelations of documents released by Edward J. Snowden,[^o344] mentioned in 'Recommendation 30' on page 37, "...that the National Security Council staff should manage an interagency process to review regularly the activities of the US Government regarding attacks that exploit a previously unknown vulnerability in a computer application." Retired cybersecurity expert [Richard A. Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_A._Clarke) was a group member and stated on April 11, 2014, that NSA had no advance knowledge of [Heartbleed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heartbleed).[^o345]

### Illegally obtained evidence {#Illegally_obtained_evidence}

In August 2013 it was revealed that a 2005 IRS training document showed that NSA intelligence intercepts and wiretaps, both foreign and domestic, were being supplied to the [Drug Enforcement Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_Enforcement_Administration) (DEA) and [Internal Revenue Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Service) (IRS) and were illegally used to launch criminal investigations of US citizens. Law enforcement agents were directed to conceal how the investigations began and recreate a legal investigative trail by re-obtaining the same evidence by other means.[^o347][^o348]

### Obama administration {#Obama_administration}

In the months leading to April 2009, the NSA intercepted the communications of U.S. citizens, including a congressman, although the [Justice Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Justice) believed that the interception was unintentional. The Justice Department then took action to correct the issues and bring the program into compliance with existing laws.[^o350] United States Attorney General [Eric Holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Holder) resumed the program according to his understanding of the [Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Act) amendment of 2008, without explaining what had occurred.[^o351]

Polls conducted in June 2013 found divided results among Americans regarding NSA's secret data collection.[^o352] [Rasmussen Reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasmussen_Reports) found that 59% of Americans disapprove,[^o353] [Gallup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallup_%28company%29) found that 53% disapprove,[^o354] and [Pew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pew_Research_Center) found that 56% are in favor of NSA data collection.[^o355]

### Section 215 metadata collection {#Section_215_metadata_collection}

On April 25, 2013, the NSA obtained a court order requiring [Verizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verizon)'s Business Network Services to provide [metadata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metadata) on all calls in its system to the NSA "on an ongoing daily basis" for three months, as reported by *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* on June 6, 2013. This information includes "the numbers of both parties on a call ... location data, call duration, unique identifiers, and the time and duration of all calls" but not "\[t\]he contents of the conversation itself". The order relies on the so-called "business records" provision of the Patriot Act.[^o357][^o358]

In August 2013, following the Snowden leaks, new details about the NSA's data mining activity were revealed. Reportedly, the majority of emails into or out of the United States are captured at "selected communications links" and automatically analyzed for keywords or other "selectors". Emails that do not match are deleted.[^o359] The utility of such a massive metadata collection in preventing terrorist attacks is disputed. Many studies reveal the dragnet-like system to be ineffective. One such report, released by the [New America Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_America_Foundation) concluded that after an analysis of 225 terrorism cases, the NSA "had no discernible impact on preventing acts of terrorism."[^o360]

Defenders of the program said that while metadata alone cannot provide all the information necessary to prevent an attack, it assures the ability to "connect the dots"[^o361] between suspect foreign numbers and domestic numbers with a speed only the NSA's software is capable of. One benefit of this is quickly being able to determine the difference between suspicious activity and real threats.[^o362] As an example, NSA director General [Keith B. Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_B._Alexander) mentioned at the annual Cybersecurity Summit in 2013, that metadata analysis of domestic phone call records after the [Boston Marathon bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Marathon_bombing) helped determine that rumors of a follow-up attack in New York were baseless.[^o361] In addition to doubts about its effectiveness, many people argue that the collection of metadata is an unconstitutional invasion of privacy. , the collection process remained legal and grounded in the ruling from *[Smith v. Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_v._Maryland)* (1979). A prominent opponent of the data collection and its legality is [U.S. District Judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._District_Judge) [Richard J. Leon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_J._Leon), who issued a report in 2013[^o363] in which he stated: "I cannot imagine a more 'indiscriminate' and 'arbitrary invasion' than this systematic and high tech collection and retention of personal data on virtually every single citizen for purposes of querying and analyzing it without prior judicial approval...Surely, such a program infringes on 'that degree of privacy' that the founders enshrined in the [Fourth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution)".

As of May 7, 2015, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit ruled that the interpretation of Section 215 of the Patriot Act was wrong and that the NSA program that has been collecting Americans' phone records in bulk is illegal.[^o364] It stated that Section 215 cannot be interpreted to allow government to collect national phone data and, as a result, expired on June 1, 2015. This ruling "is the first time a higher-level court in the regular judicial system has reviewed the NSA phone records program."[^o365] The replacement law known as the [USA Freedom Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Freedom_Act), which will enable the NSA to continue to have bulk access to citizens' metadata but with the stipulation that the data will now be stored by the companies themselves.[^o365] This change will not have any effect on other Agency procedures—outside of metadata collection—which have purportedly challenged Americans' Fourth Amendment rights,[^o366] including [Upstream collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upstream_collection), a mass of techniques used by the Agency to collect and store American's data/communications directly from the [Internet backbone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_backbone).[^o367]

Under the Upstream collection program, the NSA paid telecommunications companies hundreds of millions of dollars in order to collect data from them.[^o368] While companies such as Google and Yahoo! claim that they do not provide "direct access" from their servers to the NSA unless under a court order,[^o369] the NSA had access to emails, phone calls, and cellular data users.[^o370] Under this new ruling, telecommunications companies maintain bulk user metadata on their servers for at least 18 months, to be provided upon request to the NSA.[^o365] This ruling made the mass storage of specific phone records at NSA datacenters illegal, but it did not rule on Section 215's constitutionality.[^o365]

### Fourth Amendment encroachment {#Fourth_Amendment_encroachment}

In a declassified document it was revealed that 17,835 phone lines were on an improperly permitted "alert list" from 2006 to 2009 in breach of compliance, which tagged these phone lines for daily monitoring.[^o372][^o373][^o374] Eleven percent of these monitored phone lines met the agency's legal standard for "reasonably articulable suspicion" (RAS).[^o372][^o375]

The NSA tracks the locations of hundreds of millions of cell phones per day, allowing it to map people's movements and relationships in detail.[^o376] The NSA has been reported to have access to all communications made via Google, Microsoft, Facebook, Yahoo, YouTube, [AOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AOL), Skype, Apple and Paltalk,[^o377] and collects hundreds of millions of contact lists from personal email and [instant messaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant_messaging) accounts each year.[^o378] It has also managed to weaken much of the encryption used on the Internet (by collaborating with, coercing, or otherwise infiltrating numerous technology companies to leave "backdoors" into their systems) so that the majority of encryption is inadvertently vulnerable to different forms of attack.[^o379][^o380]

Domestically, the NSA has been proven to collect and store metadata records of phone calls,[^o381] including over 120 million US [Verizon subscribers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAINWAY),[^o382] as well as intercept vast amounts of communications via the internet ([Upstream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upstream_collection)).[^o377] The government's legal standing had been to rely on a secret interpretation of the [Patriot Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_Act) whereby the entirety of US communications may be considered "relevant" to a terrorism investigation if it is expected that even a tiny minority may relate to terrorism.[^o383] The NSA also supplies foreign intercepts to the [DEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_Enforcement_Administration), [IRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_Revenue_Service) and other law enforcement agencies, who use these to initiate criminal investigations. Federal agents are then instructed to "recreate" the investigative trail via [parallel construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_construction).[^o384]

The NSA also spies on influential Muslim societies to obtain information that could be used to discredit them, such as their use of pornography. The targets, both domestic and abroad, are not suspected of any crime but hold religious or political views deemed "radical" by the NSA.[^o385] According to a report in *The Washington Post* in July 2014, relying on information provided by Snowden, 90% of those placed under surveillance in the U.S. are ordinary Americans and are not the intended targets. The newspaper said it had examined documents including emails, text messages, and online accounts that support the claim.[^o386]

### Congressional oversight {#Congressional_oversight}

*Image: Ron Wyden and James Clapper - 6 June 2013.webm.* Excerpt of James Clapper's testimony before the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence

The Intelligence Committees of the US House and Senate exercise primary oversight over the NSA; other members of Congress have been denied access to materials and information regarding the agency and its activities.[^o388] The [United States Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Foreign_Intelligence_Surveillance_Court), the secret court charged with regulating the NSA's activities is, according to its chief judge, incapable of investigating or verifying how often the NSA breaks even its own secret rules.[^o389] It has since been reported that the NSA violated its own rules on data access thousands of times a year, many of these violations involving large-scale data interceptions.[^o390] NSA officers have even used data intercepts to spy on love interests;[^o391] "most of the NSA violations were self-reported, and each instance resulted in administrative action of termination."[^o392]

The NSA has "generally disregarded the special rules for disseminating United States person information" by illegally sharing its intercepts with different law enforcement agencies.[^o393] A March 2009 FISA Court opinion, which the court released, states that protocols restricting data queries had been "so frequently and systemically violated that it can be fairly said that this critical element of the overall ... regime has never functioned effectively."[^o394][^o395] In 2011 the same court noted that the "volume and nature" of the NSA's bulk foreign Internet intercepts was "fundamentally different from what the court had been led to believe".[^o393] Email contact lists (including those of US citizens) are collected at numerous foreign locations to work around the illegality of doing so on US soil.[^o378]

Legal opinions on the NSA's bulk collection program have differed. In mid-December 2013, U.S. District Judge Richard Leon ruled that the "almost-Orwellian" program likely violates the Constitution, and wrote, "I cannot imagine a more 'indiscriminate' and 'arbitrary invasion' than this systematic and high-tech collection and retention of personal data on virtually every single citizen for purposes of querying and analyzing it without prior judicial approval. Surely, such a program infringes on 'that degree of privacy' that the Founders enshrined in the Fourth Amendment. Indeed, I have little doubt that the author of our Constitution, [James Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Madison), who cautioned us to beware 'the abridgment of the freedom of the people by gradual and silent encroachments by those in power,' would be aghast."[^o396]

Later that month, U.S. District Judge [William Pauley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Pauley_III) ruled that the NSA's collection of telephone records is legal and valuable in the fight against terrorism. In his opinion, he wrote, "a bulk telephony metadata collection program \[is\] a wide net that could find and isolate gossamer contacts among suspected terrorists in an ocean of seemingly disconnected data" and noted that a similar collection of data before 9/11 might have prevented the attack.[^o397]

### Official responses {#Official_responses}

At a March 2013 [Senate Intelligence Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Intelligence_Committee) hearing, Senator [Ron Wyden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Wyden) asked the Director of National Intelligence [James Clapper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clapper), "Does the NSA collect any type of data at all on millions or hundreds of millions of Americans?" Clapper replied "No, sir. ... Not wittingly. There are cases where they could inadvertently perhaps collect, but not wittingly."[^o399] This statement came under scrutiny months later, in June 2013, when details of the [PRISM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PRISM_%28surveillance_program%29) surveillance program were published, showing that "the NSA apparently can gain access to the servers of nine Internet companies for a wide range of digital data."[^o399] Wyden said that Clapper had failed to give a "straight answer" in his testimony. Clapper, in response to criticism, said, "I responded in what I thought was the most truthful, or least untruthful manner." Clapper added, "There are honest differences on the semantics of what—when someone says 'collection' to me, that has a specific meaning, which may have a different meaning to him."[^o399]

[Edward Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Snowden) additionally revealed the existence of [XKeyscore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XKeyscore), a top-secret surveillance program that allows the NSA to search vast databases of "the metadata as well as the content of emails and other internet activity, such as browser history," with the capability to search by "name, telephone number, IP address, keywords, the language in which the internet activity was conducted or the type of browser used."[^o400] XKeyscore "provides the technological capability, if not the legal authority, to target even US persons for extensive electronic surveillance without a warrant provided that some identifying information, such as their email or IP address, is known to the analyst."[^o400]

Regarding the necessity of these NSA programs, Alexander stated on June 27, 2013, that the NSA's bulk phone and Internet intercepts had been instrumental in preventing 54 terrorist "events", including 13 in the US, and in all but one of these cases had provided the initial tip to "unravel the threat stream".[^o401] On July 31 NSA Deputy Director John Inglis conceded to the Senate that these intercepts had not been vital in stopping any terrorist attacks, but were "close" to vital in identifying and convicting four San Diego men for sending US\$8,930 to [Al-Shabaab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Shabaab_%28militant_group%29), a militia that conducts terrorism in Somalia.[^o402][^o403][^o404] The U.S. government has aggressively sought to dismiss and challenge [Fourth Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_Amendment_to_the_United_States_Constitution) cases raised against it, and has granted retroactive immunity to ISPs and telecoms participating in domestic surveillance.[^o405][^o406]

The U.S. military has acknowledged blocking access to parts of *The Guardian* website for thousands of defense personnel across the country,[^o407][^o408] and blocking the entire *Guardian* website for personnel stationed throughout Afghanistan, the Middle East, and South Asia.[^o409] In October 2014, the United Nations report condemned mass surveillance programs carried out by the U.S. intelligence communities and other nations as violating multiple global treaties and conventions that guaranteed core privacy rights.[^o410]

### Responsibility for global ransomware attack {#Responsibility_for_global_ransomware_attack}

An exploit dubbed [EternalBlue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EternalBlue), created by the NSA, was used in the [WannaCry ransomware attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WannaCry_ransomware_attack) in May 2017.[^o412] The exploit had been leaked online by a hacking group, The Shadow Brokers, nearly a month before the attack. Several experts have pointed the finger at the NSA's non-disclosure of the underlying vulnerability, and their loss of control over the EternalBlue attack tool that exploited it. Edward Snowden said that if the NSA had "[privately disclosed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsible_disclosure) the flaw used to attack hospitals when they found it, not when they lost it, \[the attack\] might not have happened".[^o413] Wikipedia co-founder, [Jimmy Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jimmy_Wales), stated that he joined "with Microsoft and the other leaders of the industry in saying this is a huge screw-up by the government ... the moment the NSA found it, they should have notified Microsoft so they could quietly issue a [patch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patch_%28computing%29) and really chivvy people along, long before it became a huge problem."[^o414]

### Activities of previous employees {#Activities_of_previous_employees}

Former employee David Evenden, who had left the NSA to work for US defense contractor Cyperpoint at a position in the United Arab Emirates, was tasked with hacking UAE neighbor Qatar in 2015 to determine if they were funding terrorist group [Muslim Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Brotherhood). He quit the company after learning his team had hacked Qatari Sheikha [Moza bint Nasser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moza_bint_Nasser)'s email exchanges with [Michelle Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_Obama), just before she visited [Doha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doha).[^o416] Upon Evenden's return to the US, he reported his experiences to the [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI). The incident highlights a growing trend of former NSA employees and contractors leaving the agency to start up their firms, and then hiring out to countries like Turkey, Sudan, and even Russia, a country involved in [numerous cyberattacks against the US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberwarfare_by_Russia#United_States).[^o416]

### 2021 Denmark-NSA collaborative surveillance {#2021_Denmark-NSA_collaborative_surveillance}

In May 2021, it was reported that the [Danish Defence Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Defence_Intelligence_Service) collaborated with the NSA to wiretap on fellow EU members and leaders,[^o418][^o419] leading to wide backlash among EU countries and demands for explanation from Danish and American governments.[^o420]

### Buying data without a warrant {#Buying_data_without_a_warrant}

NSA director [Paul Nakasone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nakasone) disclosed in a letter to Representative [Ron Wyden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Wyden) that the NSA buys data without a warrant.[^o422][^o423]
