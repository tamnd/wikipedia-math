# Paradox

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paradox"}

A **paradox** is a [logically](wikipedia://en/46426065) self-contradictory statement or a statement that runs contrary to expectations.[^o2][^o3] It is a statement that, despite apparently valid reasoning from true or apparently true premises, leads to a seemingly self-contradictory or a logically unacceptable conclusion.[^o4][^o5] A paradox usually involves contradictory-yet-interrelated elements that exist simultaneously and persist over time.[^o6][^o7][^o8] They result in "persistent contradiction between interdependent elements" leading to a lasting "unity of opposites".[^o9]

:::

In [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), many paradoxes exist that are [invalid](wikipedia://en/21304742) arguments, yet are nevertheless valuable in promoting [critical thinking](wikipedia://en/276872),[^o10] while other paradoxes have revealed errors in definitions that were assumed to be rigorous, and have caused [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axioms) of mathematics and logic to be re-examined. One example is [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095), which questions whether a "list of all lists that do not contain themselves" would include itself and showed that attempts to found [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) on the identification of sets with [properties](wikipedia://en/508057) or [predicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29) were flawed.[^o11][^o12] Others, such as [Curry's paradox](wikipedia://en/154163), cannot be easily resolved by making foundational changes in a logical system.[^o13]

Examples outside logic include the [ship of Theseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_of_Theseus), a paradox that questions whether a ship repaired over time by replacing each and all of its wooden parts one at a time would remain the same ship.[^o14] Paradoxes can also take the form of images or other media. For example, [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) featured [perspective-based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspective_%28visual%29) paradoxes in many of his drawings, with walls that are regarded as floors from other points of view, and staircases that appear to climb endlessly.[^o15]

The term *paradox* is often used to describe a counterintuitive result, which may be termed a *veridical paradox*.

## Common elements {#Common_elements}

[Self-reference](wikipedia://en/28545), [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807) and [infinite regress](wikipedia://en/3004668) are core elements of many paradoxes.[^o17] Other common elements include [circular definitions](wikipedia://en/68038), and confusion or equivocation between different levels of [abstraction](wikipedia://en/2972).

### Self-reference {#Self-reference}

[Self-reference](wikipedia://en/28545) occurs when a [sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28linguistics%29), idea or [formula](wikipedia://en/404582) refers to itself. Although statements can be self referential without being paradoxical ("This statement is written in English" is a true and non-paradoxical self-referential statement), self-reference is a common element of paradoxes. One example occurs in the [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967), which is commonly formulated as the self-referential statement "This statement is false".[^o19] Another example occurs in the [barber paradox](wikipedia://en/46099), which poses the question of whether a [barber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barber) who shaves all and only those who do not shave themselves will shave himself. In this paradox, the barber is a self-referential concept.

### Contradiction {#Contradiction}

[Contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807), along with self-reference, is a core feature of many paradoxes.[^o17] The liar paradox, "This statement is false," exhibits contradiction because the statement cannot be false and true at the same time.[^o21] The barber paradox is contradictory because it implies that the barber shaves himself if and only if the barber does not shave himself.

As with self-reference, a statement can contain a contradiction without being a paradox. "This statement is written in French" is an example of a contradictory self-referential statement that is not a paradox and is instead false.[^o17]

### Vicious circularity, or infinite regress {#Vicious_circularity,_or_infinite_regress}

*Image: Liars paradox.svg.* Vicious circularity illustrated

Another core aspect of paradoxes is non-terminating [recursion](wikipedia://en/25407), in the form of [circular reasoning](wikipedia://en/313944) or [infinite regress](wikipedia://en/3004668).[^o17] When this recursion creates a metaphysical impossibility through contradiction, the regress or circularity is [vicious](wikipedia://en/3004668#Viciousness). Again, the liar paradox is an instructive example: "This statement is false"—if the statement is true, then the statement is false, thereby making the statement true, thereby making the statement false, and so on.[^o17][^o23]

The barber paradox also exemplifies vicious circularity: The barber shaves those who do not shave themselves, so if the barber does not shave himself, then he shaves himself, then he does not shave himself, and so on.

### Other elements {#Other_elements}

Other paradoxes involve false statements and [half-truths](wikipedia://en/6548700) or rely on hasty assumptions (A father and his son are in a car crash; the father is killed and the boy is rushed to the hospital. The doctor says, "I can't operate on this boy. He's my son." There is no contradiction; the doctor is the boy's mother.).

Paradoxes that are not based on a hidden error generally occur at the fringes of context or [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language), and require extending the context or language in order to lose their paradoxical quality. Paradoxes that arise from apparently intelligible uses of language are often of interest to [logicians](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher). "This sentence is false" is an example of the well-known [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967): it is a sentence that cannot be consistently interpreted as either true or false, because if it is known to be false, then it can be inferred that it must be true, and if it is known to be true, then it can be inferred that it must be false. [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095), which shows that the notion of *the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all those sets that do not contain themselves* leads to a contradiction, was instrumental in the development of modern logic and set theory.[^o11]

[Thought experiments](wikipedia://en/49535) can also yield interesting paradoxes. The [grandfather paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandfather_paradox), for example, would arise if a [time traveler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_travel) were to kill his own grandfather before his mother or father had been conceived, thereby preventing his own birth. This is a specific instance of the [butterfly effect](wikipedia://en/4024) – in that any interaction a time traveler has with the past would alter conditions such that divergent events "propagate" through the world over time, ultimately altering the circumstances in which the time travel initially takes place.

Often a seemingly paradoxical conclusion arises from an inconsistent or inherently contradictory definition of the initial premise. In the case of that apparent paradox of a time traveler killing his own grandfather, it is the inconsistency of defining the past to which he returns as being somehow different from the one that leads up to the future from which he begins his trip, but also insisting that he must have come to that past from the same future as the one that it leads up to.

## Quine's classification {#Quine's_classification}

[W. V. O. Quine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._O._Quine) (1962) distinguished between three classes of paradoxes:[^o26][^o27]

::: {#o29 .statement cue=named-heading name="Veridical paradox"}

### Veridical paradox {#Veridical_paradox}

A *veridical paradox* produces a result that appears counter to [intuition](wikipedia://en/154170), but is demonstrated to be true nonetheless:

- [Condorcet's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet's_paradox) demonstrates the surprising result that [majority rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_rule) can be self-contradictory, i.e. it is possible for a majority of voters to support some outcome other than the one chosen (regardless of the outcome itself).
- The [Monty Hall paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Hall_paradox) (or equivalently [three prisoners problem](wikipedia://en/4392266)) demonstrates that a decision that has an intuitive fifty–fifty chance can instead have a provably different probable outcome. Another veridical paradox with a concise mathematical proof is the [birthday paradox](wikipedia://en/73242).
- In 20th-century science, [Hilbert's paradox of the Grand Hotel](wikipedia://en/37797) is an example of a theory being taken to a logical but paradoxical end.

:::

::: {#o31 .statement cue=named-heading name="Falsidical paradox"}

### Falsidical paradox {#Falsidical_paradox}

A *falsidical paradox* establishes a result that appears false and actually is false, due to a [fallacy](wikipedia://en/53986) in the demonstration. Therefore, falsidical paradoxes can be classified as [fallacious arguments](wikipedia://en/53986).

:::

### Antinomy {#Antinomy}

An *[antinomy](wikipedia://en/3175)* is a paradox which reaches a self-contradictory result by properly applying accepted ways of reasoning. For example, the [Grelling–Nelson paradox](wikipedia://en/251277) points out genuine problems in our understanding of the ideas of truth and description.

Sometimes described since Quine's work, a *[dialetheia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialetheia)* is a paradox that is both true and false at the same time. It may be regarded as a fourth kind, or alternatively as a special case of antinomy. In logic, it is often assumed, following [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), that no *dialetheia* exist, but they are allowed in some [paraconsistent logics](wikipedia://en/421085).

::: {#o34 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Semantic paradox"}

## Ramsey's classification {#Ramsey's_classification}

[Frank Ramsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Ramsey_%28mathematician%29) drew a distinction between logical paradoxes and semantic paradoxes, with [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095) belonging to the former category, and the [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967) and Grelling's paradoxes to the latter.[^o35] Ramsey introduced the by-now standard distinction between logical and semantical contradictions. Logical contradictions involve mathematical or logical terms like *class* and *number*, and hence show that our logic or mathematics is problematic. Semantical contradictions involve, besides purely logical terms, notions like *thought*, *language*, and *symbolism*, which, according to Ramsey, are empirical (not formal) terms. Hence these contradictions are due to faulty ideas about thought or language, and they properly belong to [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology).[^o36]

:::

## In medicine {#In_medicine}

A [paradoxical reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradoxical_reaction) to a [drug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug) is the opposite of what one would expect, such as becoming agitated by a [sedative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedative) or sedated by a [stimulant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimulant). Some are common and are used regularly in medicine, such as the use of stimulants such as [Adderall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adderall) and [Ritalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritalin) in the treatment of [attention deficit hyperactivity disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention_deficit_hyperactivity_disorder) (also known as ADHD), while others are rare and can be dangerous as they are not expected, such as severe agitation from a [benzodiazepine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzodiazepine).[^o38]

The actions of [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody) on [antigens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antigen) can rarely take paradoxical turns in certain ways. One example is [antibody-dependent enhancement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody-dependent_enhancement) (immune enhancement) of a disease's virulence; another is the [hook effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hook_effect) (prozone effect), of which there are several types. However, neither of these problems is common, and overall, antibodies are crucial to health, as most of the time they do their protective job quite well.

In the [smoker's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoker's_paradox), cigarette smoking, despite its [proven harms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_effects_of_tobacco), has a surprising inverse correlation with the epidemiological incidence of certain diseases.

In the [negative outcome penalty paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_outcome_penalty_paradox), the use of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) in medical decision-making increases the risk of being penalized by juries whether the AI recommendations are followed or rejected.[^o39]

## As an aspect of reality {#As_an_aspect_of_reality}

[Carl Jung](wikipedia://en/42031) stated: "the paradox reflects a higher level of intellect and, by not forcibly representing the unknowable as known, gives a more faithful picture of the real state of affairs." [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Søren_Kierkegaard) posited that any true understanding of reality requires one to abandon rationalism and take a "leap of faith" into paradoxical thinking. Some have observed that the fundamental nature of reality is difficult to grasp or express in words because it is innately paradoxical and contradictory.[^o41]

## Teaching tool {#Teaching_tool}

Paradoxes can be used in management education.

Paradoxical questions, known as [koan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koan), are used as a teaching device in [Zen Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen_Buddhism).[^o44]
