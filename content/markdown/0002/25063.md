# Product of rings

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Product of rings"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **product of rings** or **direct product of rings** is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that is formed by the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of the underlying sets of several rings (possibly an infinity), equipped with [componentwise operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Componentwise_operation). It is a [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) in the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181).

:::

Since direct products are defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), one says colloquially that a ring is the product of some rings if it is isomorphic to the direct product of these rings. For example, the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) may be stated as: if $m$ and $n$ are [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556), the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z}$ is the product of $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.$

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

An important example is **Z**/*n***Z**, the [ring of integers modulo *n*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_integers_modulo_n). If *n* is written as a product of [prime powers](wikipedia://en/1630483) (see [Fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556)),

$$n=p_1^{n_1} p_2^{n_2}\cdots\ p_k^{n_k},$$

where the *p<sub>i</sub>* are distinct [primes](wikipedia://en/23666), then **Z**/*n***Z** is naturally [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) to the product

$$\mathbf{Z}/p_1^{n_1}\mathbf{Z} \ \times \ \mathbf{Z}/p_2^{n_2}\mathbf{Z} \ \times \ \cdots \ \times \ \mathbf{Z}/p_k^{n_k}\mathbf{Z}.$$

This follows from the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

If *R* = Π<sub>*i*∈*I*</sub> *R*<sub>*i*</sub> is a product of rings, then for every *i* in *I* we have a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) *p<sub>i</sub>* : *R* → *R<sub>i</sub>* which projects the product on the *i*th coordinate. The product *R* together with the projections *p<sub>i</sub>* has the following [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245):

if *S* is any ring and *f<sub>i</sub>* : *S* → *R<sub>i</sub>* is a ring homomorphism for every *i* in *I*, then there exists *precisely one* ring homomorphism *f* : *S* → *R* such that *p<sub>i</sub>* ∘ *f* = *f<sub>i</sub>* for every *i* in *I*.

This shows that the product of rings is an instance of [products in the sense of category theory](wikipedia://en/237868).

When *I* is finite, the underlying additive group of Π<sub>*i*∈*I*</sub> *R*<sub>*i*</sub> coincides with the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of the additive groups of the *R*<sub>*i*</sub>. In this case, some authors call *R* the "direct sum of the rings *R*<sub>*i*</sub>" and write ⊕<sub>*i*∈*I*</sub> *R*<sub>*i*</sub>, but this is incorrect from the point of view of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), since it is usually not a [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) in the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181) (with identity): for example, when two or more of the *R*<sub>*i*</sub> are non-[trivial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_ring), the inclusion map *R<sub>i</sub>* → *R* fails to map 1 to 1 and hence is not a ring homomorphism.

(A finite coproduct in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29) [algebras over a commutative ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_commutative_ring) is a [tensor product of algebras](wikipedia://en/372395). A coproduct in the category of algebras is a [free product of algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_product_of_algebras).)

Direct products are commutative and associative [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) natural isomorphism, meaning that it doesn't matter in which order one forms the direct product.

If *A<sub>i</sub>* is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of *R<sub>i</sub>* for each *i* in *I*, then *A* = Π<sub>*i*∈*I*</sub> *A<sub>i</sub>* is an ideal of *R*. If *I* is finite, then the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) is true, i.e., every ideal of *R* is of this form. However, if *I* is infinite and the rings *R<sub>i</sub>* are non-trivial, then the converse is false: the set of elements with all but finitely many nonzero coordinates forms an ideal which is not a direct product of ideals of the *R<sub>i</sub>*. The ideal *A* is a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) in *R* if all but one of the *A<sub>i</sub>* are equal to *R<sub>i</sub>* and the remaining *A<sub>i</sub>* is a prime ideal in *R<sub>i</sub>*. However, the converse is not true when *I* is infinite. For example, the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of the *R<sub>i</sub>* form an ideal not contained in any such *A*, but the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) gives that it is contained in some [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) which is [a fortiori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_fortiori) prime.

An element *x* in *R* is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) all of its components are units, i.e., if and only if *p*<sub>*i*</sub>(*x*) is a unit in *R<sub>i</sub>* for every *i* in *I*. The [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of *R* is the [product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of the groups of units of the *R<sub>i</sub>*.

A product of two or more non-trivial rings always has nonzero [zero divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_divisors): if *x* is an element of the product whose coordinates are all zero except *p*<sub>*i*</sub>(*x*) and *y* is an element of the product with all coordinates zero except *p*<sub>*j*</sub>(*y*) where *i* ≠ *j*, then *xy* = 0 in the product ring.
