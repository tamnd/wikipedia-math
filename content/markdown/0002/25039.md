# Principal ideal domain

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Principal ideal domain"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **principal ideal domain**, or **PID**, is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) (that is, a non-zero [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) without nonzero [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441)) in which every [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) is [principal](wikipedia://en/145126) (that is, is formed by the multiples of a single element). Some authors such as [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) refer to PIDs as **principal rings**.

:::

Principal ideal domains are mathematical objects that behave like the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), with respect to [divisibility](wikipedia://en/15462#Divisibility,_prime_and_irreducible_elements): any element of a PID has a unique factorization into [prime elements](wikipedia://en/60692) (so an analogue of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556) holds); any two elements of a PID have a [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) (although it may not be possible to find it using the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377)). If $x$ and $y$ are elements of a PID without common divisors, then every element of the PID can be written in the form $ax + by$, etc.

Principal ideal domains are [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129), they are [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209), they are [unique factorization domains](wikipedia://en/60546) and [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132). All [Euclidean domains](wikipedia://en/10376) and all [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) are principal ideal domains.

Some authors additionally require principal ideal domains to have [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) one, or equivalently, to possess ideals that are nonzero and proper, in order to specifically exclude [fields](wikipedia://en/10603),[^o2] while most other sources regard fields as trivial special cases of principal ideal domains.

## Examples {#Examples}

Examples include:

- $K$: any [field](wikipedia://en/10603), whose only ideals are $(0)$ and $K$,
- $\mathbb{Z}$: the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563),[^o5]
- $K[x]$: [rings of polynomials](wikipedia://en/373065) in one variable with coefficients in a field. (The converse is also true, i.e. if $A[x]$ is a PID then $A$ is a field.) Furthermore, a [ring of formal power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_formal_power_series) in one variable over a field is a PID since every ideal is of the form $(x^k)$,
- $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: the ring of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers),[^o6]
- $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ (where $\omega$ is a primitive [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506) of 1): the [Eisenstein integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisenstein_integers),
- Any [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633), for instance the ring of [$p$-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer) $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

::: {#o8 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

Examples of integral domains that are not PIDs:

- $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ is an example of a ring that is not a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546), since $4 = 2\cdot 2 = (1+\sqrt{-3})(1-\sqrt{-3}).$ Hence it is not a principal ideal domain because principal ideal domains are unique factorization domains. Also, $\langle 2, 1+\sqrt{-3} \rangle$ is an ideal that cannot be generated by a single element.
- $\mathbb{Z}[x]$: the ring of all polynomials with integer coefficients. It is not principal because $\langle 2, x \rangle$ is an ideal that cannot be generated by a single polynomial.
- $K[x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n],$ the [ring of polynomials in at least two variables](wikipedia://en/373065#Definition_%28multivariate_case%29) over a ring $K$ is not principal, since the ideal $\langle x_1, x_2 \rangle$ is not principal.
- Most [rings of algebraic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_algebraic_integers) are not principal ideal domains. This is one of the main motivations behind Dedekind's definition of [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132), which allows replacing [unique factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_factorization) of elements with unique factorization of ideals. In particular, many $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p],$ for the [primitive p-th root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) $\zeta_p,$ are not principal ideal domains.[^o9] The [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28number_theory%29) of a ring of algebraic integers gives a measure of "how far away" the ring is from being a principal ideal domain.

## Modules {#Modules}

The key result is the structure theorem: If *R* is a principal ideal domain, and *M* is a finitely generated *R*-module, then $M$ is a direct sum of cyclic modules, i.e., modules with one generator. The cyclic modules are isomorphic to $R/xR$ for some $x\in R$[^o11] (notice that $x$ may be equal to $0$, in which case $R/xR$ is $R$).

If *M* is a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) over a principal ideal domain *R*, then every submodule of *M* is again free.[^o12] This does not hold for modules over arbitrary rings, as the example $(2,X) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ of modules over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ shows.

## Properties {#Properties}

In a principal ideal domain, any two elements $a,b$ have a [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354), which may be obtained as a generator of the ideal $(a, b)$.

All [Euclidean domains](wikipedia://en/10376) are principal ideal domains, but the converse is not true. An example of a principal ideal domain that is not a Euclidean domain is the ring $\mathbb{Z}\bigl[\tfrac12\bigl(1+\sqrt{-19}~\!\bigr)\bigr]$,[^o15][^o16] this was proved by [Theodore Motzkin](wikipedia://en/13554055) and was the first case known.[^o17] In this domain no $q$ and $r$ exist, with $0 \le |r| < 4$, so that $\bigl(1+\sqrt{-19~\!}\bigr)=(4)q+r$, despite $1+\sqrt{-19}$ and $4$ having a greatest common divisor of $2$.

Every principal ideal domain is a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) (UFD).[^o18][^o19][^o20][^o21] The converse does not hold since for any UFD $K$, the ring $K[X, Y]$ of polynomials in 2 variables is a UFD but is not a PID. (To prove this look at the ideal generated by $\left\langle X,Y \right\rangle.$ It is not the whole ring since it contains no polynomials of degree 0, but it cannot be generated by any one single element.)

1. Every principal ideal domain is [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129).
1. In all unital rings, [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) are [prime](wikipedia://en/24928). In principal ideal domains a near converse holds: every nonzero prime ideal is maximal.
1. All principal ideal domains are [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209).

The previous three statements give the definition of a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132), and hence every principal ideal domain is a Dedekind domain.

Let *A* be an integral domain, the following are equivalent.

1. *A* is a PID.
1. Every prime ideal of *A* is principal.[^o22]
1. *A* is a Dedekind domain that is a UFD.
1. Every finitely generated ideal of *A* is principal (i.e., *A* is a [Bézout domain](wikipedia://en/3131407)) and *A* satisfies the [ascending chain condition on principal ideals](wikipedia://en/22243102).
1. *A* admits a [Dedekind–Hasse norm](wikipedia://en/22629651).[^o23]

Any [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_function) is a Dedekind-Hasse norm; thus, (5) shows that a Euclidean domain is a PID. (4) compares to:

- An integral domain is a UFD if and only if it is a [GCD domain](wikipedia://en/2932361) (i.e., a domain where every two elements have a greatest common divisor) satisfying the ascending chain condition on principal ideals.

An integral domain is a [Bézout domain](wikipedia://en/3131407) if and only if any two elements in it have a gcd *that is a linear combination of the two.* A Bézout domain is thus a GCD domain, and (4) gives yet another proof that a PID is a UFD.
