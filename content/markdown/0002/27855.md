# Separable space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Separable space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is called **separable** if it contains a [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29) subset; that is, there exists a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $( x_n )_{n=1}^{\infty}$ of elements of the space such that every nonempty [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of the space contains at least one element of the sequence.

:::

Like the other [axioms of countability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axioms_of_countability), separability is a "limitation on size", not necessarily in terms of [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) (though, in the presence of the [Hausdorff axiom](wikipedia://en/13637), this does turn out to be the case; see below) but in a more subtle topological sense. In particular, every [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) on a separable space whose image is a subset of a Hausdorff space is determined by its values on the countable dense subset.

Contrast separability with the related notion of [second countability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countability), which is in general stronger but equivalent on the class of [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable) spaces.

## First examples {#First_examples}

Any topological space that is itself [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) or [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite) is separable, for the whole space is a countable dense subset of itself. An important example of an uncountable separable space is the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), in which the [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_numbers) form a countable dense subset. Similarly the set of all length-$n$ [vectors](wikipedia://en/23218956) of rational numbers, $\boldsymbol{r}=(r_1,\ldots,r_n) \in \mathbb{Q}^n$, is a countable dense subset of the set of all length-$n$ vectors of real numbers, $\mathbb{R}^n$; so for every $n$, $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is separable.

A simple example of a space that is not separable is a [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) of uncountable cardinality.

Further examples are given below.

## Separability versus second countability {#Separability_versus_second_countability}

Any [second-countable space](wikipedia://en/901459) is separable: if $\{U_n\}$ is a countable base, choosing any $x_n \in U_n$ from the non-empty $U_n$ gives a countable dense subset. Conversely, a [metrizable space](wikipedia://en/19738) is separable if and only if it is second countable, which is the case if and only if it is [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480).

To further compare these two properties:

- An arbitrary [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of a second-countable space is second countable; subspaces of separable spaces need not be separable (see below).
- Any continuous image of a separable space is separable (Willard 1970, Th. 16.4a); even a [quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) of a second-countable space need not be second countable.
- A [product](wikipedia://en/24910) of at most continuum many separable spaces is separable (Willard 1970, 109). A countable product of second-countable spaces is second countable, but an uncountable product of second-countable spaces need not even be first countable.

We can construct an example of a separable topological space that is not second countable. Consider any uncountable set $X$, pick some $x_0 \in X$, and define the topology to be the collection of all sets that contain $x_0$ (or are empty). Then, the closure of ${x_0}$ is the whole space ($X$ is the smallest closed set containing $x_0$), but every set of the form $\{x_0, x\}$ is open. Therefore, the space is separable but there cannot have a countable base.

## Cardinality {#Cardinality}

The property of separability does not in and of itself give any limitations on the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of a topological space: any set endowed with the [trivial topology](wikipedia://en/382447) is separable, as well as second countable, [quasi-compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-compact), and [connected](wikipedia://en/6233). The "trouble" with the trivial topology is its poor separation properties: its [Kolmogorov quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_quotient) is the one-point space.

A [first-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-countable), separable Hausdorff space (in particular, a separable metric space) has at most the [continuum cardinality](wikipedia://en/1574901) $\mathfrak{c}$. In such a space, [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) is determined by limits of sequences and any convergent sequence has at most one limit, so there is a surjective map from the set of convergent sequences with values in the countable dense subset to the points of $X$.

A separable Hausdorff space has cardinality at most $2^\mathfrak{c}$, where $\mathfrak{c}$ is the cardinality of the continuum. For this closure is characterized in terms of [limits of filter bases](wikipedia://en/47516955): if $Y\subseteq X$ and $z\in X$, then $z\in\overline{Y}$ if and only if there exists a filter base $\mathcal{B}$ consisting of subsets of $Y$ that converges to $z$. The cardinality of the set $S(Y)$ of such filter bases is at most $2^{2^{|Y|}}$. Moreover, in a Hausdorff space, there is at most one limit to every filter base. Therefore, there is a surjection $S(Y) \rightarrow X$ when $\overline{Y}=X.$

The same arguments establish a more general result: suppose that a Hausdorff topological space $X$ contains a dense subset of cardinality $\kappa$. Then $X$ has cardinality at most $2^{2^{\kappa}}$ and cardinality at most $2^{\kappa}$ if it is first countable.

The product of at most continuum many separable spaces is a separable space (Willard 1970, 109). In particular the space $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}$ of all functions from the real line to itself, endowed with the product topology, is a separable Hausdorff space of cardinality $2^\mathfrak{c}$. More generally, if $\kappa$ is any infinite cardinal, then a product of at most $2^\kappa$ spaces with dense subsets of size at most $\kappa$ has itself a dense subset of size at most $\kappa$ ([Hewitt–Marczewski–Pondiczery theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hewitt–Marczewski–Pondiczery_theorem)).

## Constructive mathematics {#Constructive_mathematics}

Separability is especially important in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_constructivism), since many theorems that can be proved for nonseparable spaces have constructive proofs only for separable spaces. Such constructive proofs can be turned into [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) for use in numerical analysis, and they are the only sorts of proofs acceptable in constructive analysis. A famous example of a theorem of this sort is the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860).

## Further examples {#Further_examples}

### Separable spaces {#Separable_spaces}

- Every compact [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) (or metrizable space) is separable.
- Any topological space that is the union of a countable number of separable subspaces is separable. Together, these first two examples give a different proof that $n$-dimensional Euclidean space is separable.
- The space $C(K)$ of all continuous functions from a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subset $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ to the real line $\mathbb{R}$ is separable.
- The [Lebesgue spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) $L^{p}\left(X,\mu\right)$, over a measure space $\left\langle X,\mathcal{M},\mu\right\rangle$ whose σ-algebra is countably generated and whose measure is σ-finite, are separable for any $1\leq p<\infty$.[^o12]
- The space $C([0,1])$ of [continuous real-valued functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) $[0,1]$ with the metric of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652) is a separable space, since it follows from the [Weierstrass approximation theorem](wikipedia://en/28858) that the set $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ of polynomials in one variable with rational coefficients is a countable dense subset of $C([0,1])$. The [Banach–Mazur theorem](wikipedia://en/6583188) asserts that any separable [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) is isometrically isomorphic to a closed [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of $C([0,1])$.
- A [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) is separable if and only if it has a countable [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063). It follows that any separable, infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is isometric to the space $l^2$ of square-summable sequences.
- An example of a separable space that is not second-countable is the [Sorgenfrey line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorgenfrey_line) $\mathbb{S}$, the set of real numbers equipped with the [lower limit topology](wikipedia://en/310517).
- A [separable σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/&sigma;-algebra#Separable_&sigma;-algebras) is a σ-algebra $\mathcal{F}$ that is a separable space when considered as a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) with [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) $\rho(A,B) = \mu(A \triangle B)$ for $A,B \in \mathcal{F}$ and a given finite [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) $\mu$ (and with $\triangle$ being the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) operator).[^o13]

### Non-separable spaces {#Non-separable_spaces}

- The [first uncountable ordinal](wikipedia://en/20860540) $\omega_1$, equipped with its natural [order topology](wikipedia://en/213650), is not separable.
- The [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $l^\infty$ of all bounded real sequences, with the [supremum norm](wikipedia://en/349755), is not separable. The same holds for $L^\infty$.
- The [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) of [functions of bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) is not separable.

## Properties {#Properties}

- A [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of a separable space need not be separable (see the [Sorgenfrey plane](wikipedia://en/682853) and the [Moore plane](wikipedia://en/5516188)), but every *open* subspace of a separable space is separable (Willard 1970, Th 16.4b). Also every subspace of a separable [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is separable.
- In fact, every topological space is a subspace of a separable space of the same [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174). Only countably many points need to be added: the points of the countable dense subset of the separable space. A construction is given in (Sierpiński 1952, 49); this construction additionally embeds every Hausdorff space into a separable Hausdorff space.
- The set of all real-valued continuous functions on a separable space has a cardinality equal to $\mathfrak{c}$, the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901). This follows since such functions are determined by their values on dense subsets.
- From the above property, one can deduce the following: If *X* is a separable space having an uncountable closed discrete subspace, then *X* cannot be [normal](wikipedia://en/48629). This shows that the [Sorgenfrey plane](wikipedia://en/682853) is not normal.

### Embedding separable metric spaces {#Embedding_separable_metric_spaces}

- Every separable metric space is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to a subset of the [Hilbert cube](wikipedia://en/216389). This is established in the proof of the [Urysohn metrization theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urysohn_metrization_theorem).
- Every separable metric space is [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) to a subset of the (non-separable) [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) *l*<sup>∞</sup> of all bounded real sequences with the [supremum norm](wikipedia://en/349755); this is known as the Fréchet embedding. (Heinonen 2003)
- Every separable metric space is isometric to a subset of C(\[0,1\]), the separable Banach space of continuous functions \[0,1\] → **R**, with the [supremum norm](wikipedia://en/349755). This is due to [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646). (Heinonen 2003)
- Every separable metric space is isometric to a subset of the [Urysohn universal space](wikipedia://en/20983231).

*For nonseparable spaces*:

- A [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) of [density](wikipedia://en/23523594) equal to an infinite cardinal $\alpha$ is isometric to a subspace of $C([0,1]^{\alpha}, \mathbf{R})$, the space of real continuous functions on the product of $\alpha$ copies of the unit interval. (Kleiber Pervin 1969)
