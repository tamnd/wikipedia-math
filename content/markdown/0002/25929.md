# Regiomontanus

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Regiomontanus"}

**Johannes Müller von Königsberg** (6 June 1436 – 6 July 1476[^o6]), better known as **Regiomontanus** (), was a mathematician, [astrologer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrologer) and [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) of the [German Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Renaissance), active in [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866), [Buda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buda) and [Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg). His contributions were instrumental in the development of [Copernican heliocentrism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_heliocentrism) in the decades following his death.

:::

Regiomontanus wrote under the Latinized name of *Ioannes de Monteregio* (or *Monte Regio*; *Regio Monte*); the toponym *Regiomontanus* was first used by [Philipp Melanchthon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Melanchthon) in 1534. He is named after [Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg_in_Bayern) in [Lower Franconia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Franconia), not the larger [Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg) (modern [Kaliningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaliningrad)) in Prussia.

## Life {#Life}

*Image: Regiomontanus-Koenigsberg.JPG.* Plaque at Regiomontanus' birthplace

Although little is known of Regiomontanus' early life, it is believed that at eleven years of age, he became a student at the [University of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leipzig), [Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electorate_of_Saxony). In 1451 he continued his studies at [Alma Mater Rudolfina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna), the university in [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866), in the Duchy of Austria, where he became a pupil and friend of [Georg von Peuerbach](wikipedia://en/172394). In 1452 he was awarded his [bachelor's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree) (*baccalaureus*), and he was awarded his [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree) (*magister artium*) at the age of 21 in 1457.[^o4] He lectured in [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics) and ancient literature.

In 1460 the papal legate [Basilios Bessarion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilios_Bessarion) came to Vienna on a diplomatic mission. Being a humanist scholar with a great interest in the mathematical sciences, Bessarion sought out Peuerbach's company. [George of Trebizond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_of_Trebizond) who was Bessarion's philosophical rival had recently produced a new Latin translation of [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979)'s *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)* from the Greek, which Bessarion, correctly, regarded as inaccurate and badly translated, so he asked Peuerbach to produce a new one. Peuerbach's Greek was not good enough to do a translation but he knew the *Almagest* intimately so instead he started work on a modernised, improved abridgement of the work. Bessarion also invited Peuerbach to become part of his household and to accompany him back to Italy when his work in Vienna was finished. Peuerbach accepted the invitation on the condition that Regiomontanus could also accompany them. However Peuerbach fell ill in 1461 and died having completed only the first six books of his abridgement of the *Almagest*. On his death bed Peuerbach made Regiomontanus promise to finish the book and publish it.[^o5]

In 1461 Regiomontanus left Vienna with Bessarion and spent the next four years travelling around Northern Italy as a member of Bessarion's household, looking for and copying mathematical and astronomical manuscripts for Bessarion, who possessed the largest private library in Europe at the time. Regiomontanus also made the acquaintance of the leading Italian mathematicians of the age such as [Giovanni Bianchini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Bianchini) and [Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli](wikipedia://en/1295715) who had also been friends of Peuerbach during his prolonged stay in Italy more than twenty years earlier.[^o6]

In 1467, he went to work for [János Vitéz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/János_Vitéz_%28bishop%29), archbishop of [Esztergom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esztergom). There he calculated extensive astronomical tables and built astronomical instruments.[^o4] Next he went to [Buda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buda), and the court of [Matthias Corvinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthias_Corvinus) of Hungary, for whom he built an astrolabe, and where he collated Greek manuscripts for a handsome salary.[^o7] The [trigonometric tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_tables) that he created while living in Hungary, his *Tabulae directionum profectionumque* (printed [posthum.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posthumous_publication), 1490), were designed for astrology, including finding astrological houses.[^o8] The *Tabulae* also contained several [tangent tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_tables).[^o9]

In 1471 Regiomontanus moved to the Free City of [Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg), in [Franconia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franconia), then one of the Empire's important seats of learning, publication, commerce and art, where he worked with the humanist and merchant [Bernhard Walther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Walther).[^o7] Here he founded the world's first scientific printing press, and in 1472 he published the first printed astronomical textbook, the *Theoricae novae Planetarum* of his teacher Georg von Peurbach.[^o6]

Regiomontanus and Bernhard Walther observed the [Great Comet of 1472](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Comet_of_1472). Regiomontanus tried to estimate its distance from Earth, using the angle of parallax. According to David A. Seargeant:[^o10]

In agreement with the prevailing Aristotelian theory on comets as atmospheric phenomena, he estimated its distance to be at least 8,200 miles (13,120 km) and, from this, estimated the central condensation as 26, and the entire coma as 81 miles (41.6 and 129.6 km respectively) in diameter. These values, of course, fail by orders of magnitude, but he is to be commended for this attempt at determining the physical dimensions of the comet. The 1472 comet was visible from Christmas Day 1471 to 1 March 1472 (Julian Calendar), a total of 59 days.[^o11]

In 1475, Regiomontanus was called to Rome by [Pope Sixtus IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Sixtus_IV) on to work on the planned [calendar reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar#Background). Sixtus promised substantial rewards, including the title of [bishop of Regensburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Regensburg),[^o12][^o13] but it is unlikely that he was actually appointed to the role.[^o5]

On his way to Rome, stopping in Venice, he commissioned the publication of his *Calendarium* with [Erhard Ratdolt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erhard_Ratdolt) (printed in 1476).[^o14] Regiomontanus reached Rome, but he died there after only a few months, in his 41st year, on 6 July 1476. According to a rumor repeated by [Gassendi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Gassendi) in his Regiomontanus biography, he was poisoned by relatives of [George of Trebizond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_of_Trebizond) whom he had criticized in his writing; it is however considered more likely that he died from the plague.[^o6]

## Work {#Work}

*Image: Regiomontanus - De triangulis planis et sphaericis libri, per Henrichum Petri et Petrum Pernam - 4683051.tif.* *De triangulis planis et sphaericis libri*

*Image: Houghton Typ Inc 4366 - Regiomontanus 1.jpg.* Title page for *Qvesta opra da ogni parte e un libro doro*, 1476

During his time in Italy he completed Peuerbach's abridgement of *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*, *Epytoma in almagesti Ptolemei*. In 1464, he completed *De triangulis omnimodis* ("On Triangles of All Kinds"). *De triangulis omnimodis* was one of the first textbooks presenting the current state of [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) and included lists of questions for review of individual chapters. In it he wrote:

You who wish to study great and wonderful things, who wonder about the movement of the stars, must read these theorems about triangles. Knowing these ideas will open the door to all of astronomy and to certain geometric problems.

In 1465, he built a portable [sundial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundial) for [Pope Paul II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Paul_II).

In *Epytoma in almagesti Ptolemei*, he critiqued the translation of *Almagest* by [George of Trebizond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_of_Trebizond), pointing out inaccuracies. Later [Nicolaus Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus) would refer to this book as an influence on his own work.

A prolific author, Regiomontanus was internationally famous in his lifetime. Despite having completed only a quarter of what he had intended to write, he left a substantial body of work. [Nicolaus Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus)' teacher, [Domenico Maria Novara da Ferrara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domenico_Maria_Novara_da_Ferrara), referred to Regiomontanus as having been his own teacher. There is speculation that Regiomontanus had arrived at a theory of heliocentrism before he died; a manuscript shows particular attention to the [heliocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric) theory of the [Pythagorean](wikipedia://en/291170) [Aristarchus](wikipedia://en/74857), mention was also given to the motion of the earth in a letter to a friend.[^o16]

Much of the material on [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405) in Regiomontanus' *On Triangles* was taken directly from the twelfth-century work of [Jabir ibn Aflah](wikipedia://en/10228966) otherwise known as Geber, as noted in the sixteenth century by [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145).[^o17]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

[Simon Stevin](wikipedia://en/72586), in his book describing [decimal representation](wikipedia://en/3293594) of [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) ([De Thiende](wikipedia://en/36193619)), cites the trigonometric tables of Regiomontanus as suggestive of [positional notation](wikipedia://en/437052).[^o22]

Regiomontanus designed his own [astrological house system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological_houses#Regiomontanus), which became one of the most popular systems in Europe.[^o23]

In 1561, [Daniel Santbech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Santbech) compiled a collected edition of the works of Regiomontanus, *De triangulis planis et sphaericis libri quinque* (first published in 1533) and *Compositio tabularum sinum recto*, as well as Santbech's own *Problematum astronomicorum et geometricorum sectiones septem*. It was published in [Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel) by [Henrich Petri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrich_Petri) and [Petrus Perna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrus_Perna).

There is an image of him in Hartmann Schedel's 1493 *[Nuremberg Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Chronicle)*. He is holding an [astrolabe](wikipedia://en/73664). Yet, although there are thirteen illustrations of comets in the *Chronicle* (from 471 to 1472), they are stylized, rather than representing the actual objects.

The crater [Regiomontanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regiomontanus_%28crater%29) on the Moon is named after him.

An [observatory open to the public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_observatory) in Nuremberg, de:Regiomontanus-Sternwarte, is named after him.
