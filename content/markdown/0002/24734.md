# Product of group subsets

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Product of group subsets"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), one can define a **product of group subsets** in a natural way. If *S* and *T* are [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G*, then their product is the subset of *G* defined by

:::

$$ST = \{st : s \in S \text{ and } t\in T\}.$$

The subsets *S* and *T* need not be [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) for this product to be well defined. The [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) of this product [follows from](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Follows_from) that of the group product. The product of group subsets therefore defines a natural [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) structure on the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of *G*.

A lot more can be said in the case where *S* and *T* are subgroups. The product of two subgroups *S* and *T* of a group *G* is itself a subgroup of *G* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *ST* = *TS*.

## Product of subgroups {#Product_of_subgroups}

If *S* and *T* are subgroups of *G*, their product need not be a subgroup (for example, two distinct subgroups of order 2 in the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on 3 symbols). This product is sometimes called the *Frobenius product*.[^o3] In general, the product of two subgroups *S* and *T* is a subgroup if and only if *ST* = *TS*,[^o4] and the two subgroups are said to [permute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutable_subgroup). ([Walter Ledermann](wikipedia://en/33383638) has called this fact the *Product Theorem*,[^o5] but this name, just like "Frobenius product" is by no means standard.) In this case, *ST* is the group [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by *S* and *T*; i.e., *ST* = *TS* = ⟨*S* ∪ *T*⟩.

If either *S* or *T* is [normal](wikipedia://en/21918) then the condition *ST* = *TS* is satisfied and the product is a subgroup.[^o6][^o7] If both *S* and *T* are normal, then the product is normal as well.[^o6]

If *S* and *T* are finite subgroups of a group *G*, then *ST* is a subset of *G* of size *\|ST|* given by the *product formula*:

$$|ST| = \frac{|S||T|}{|S\cap T|}$$

Note that this applies even if neither *S* nor *T* is normal.

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Modular law"}

### Modular law {#Modular_law}

The following **modular law (for groups)** holds for any *Q* a subgroup of *S*, where *T* is any other arbitrary subgroup (and both *S* and *T* are subgroups of some group *G*):

*Q*(*S* ∩ *T*) = *S* ∩ (*QT*).

The two products that appear in this equality are not necessarily subgroups.

If *QT* is a subgroup (equivalently, as noted above, if *Q* and *T* permute) then *QT* = ⟨*Q* ∪ *T*⟩ = *Q* ∨ *T*; i.e., *QT* is the [join](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Join_%28mathematics%29) of *Q* and *T* in the [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) of *G*, and the modular law for such a pair may also be written as *Q* ∨ (*S* ∩ *T*) = *S* ∩ (*Q ∨ T*), which is the equation that defines a [modular lattice](wikipedia://en/1089311) if it holds for any three elements of the lattice with *Q* ≤ *S*. In particular, since normal subgroups permute with each other, they form a modular [sublattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublattice).

A group in which every subgroup permutes is called an [Iwasawa group](wikipedia://en/16355158). The subgroup lattice of an Iwasawa group is thus a modular lattice, so these groups are sometimes called *modular groups*[^o10] (although this latter term may have other meanings.)

The assumption in the modular law for groups (as formulated above) that *Q* is a subgroup of *S* is essential. If *Q* is *not* a subgroup of *S*, then the tentative, more general [distributive property](wikipedia://en/103118) that one may consider *S* ∩ (*QT*) = (*S* ∩ *Q*)(*S* ∩ *T*) is *false*.[^o11][^o12]

:::

### Product of subgroups with trivial intersection {#Product_of_subgroups_with_trivial_intersection}

In particular, if *S* and *T* intersect only in the identity, then every element of *ST* has a unique expression as a product *st* with *s* in *S* and *t* in *T*. If *S* and *T* also commute, then *ST* is a group, and is called a [Zappa–Szép product](wikipedia://en/7094111). Even further, if *S* or *T* is normal in *ST*, then *ST* coincides with the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of *S* and *T*. Finally, if both *S* and *T* are normal in *ST*, then *ST* coincides with the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of *S* and *T*.

If *S* and *T* are subgroups whose intersection is the trivial subgroup ([identity element](wikipedia://en/14962)) and additionally *ST* = *G*, then *S* is called a [complement](wikipedia://en/11436087) of *T* and vice versa.

By a (locally unambiguous) [abuse of terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abuse_of_terminology), two subgroups that intersect only on the (otherwise obligatory) identity are sometimes called [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359).[^o14]

### Product of subgroups with non-trivial intersection {#Product_of_subgroups_with_non-trivial_intersection}

A question that arises in the case of a non-trivial intersection between a normal subgroup *N* and a subgroup *K* is what is the structure of the quotient *NK*/*N*. Although one might be tempted to just "cancel out" *N* and say the answer is *K*, that is not correct because a homomorphism with kernel *N* will also "collapse" (map to 1) all elements of *K* that happen to be in *N*. Thus the correct answer is that *NK*/*N* is isomorphic with *K*/(*N*∩*K*). This fact is sometimes called the [second isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_isomorphism_theorem),[^o16] (although the numbering of these theorems sees some variation between authors); it has also been called the *diamond theorem* by [I. Martin Isaacs](wikipedia://en/42860250) because of the shape of the subgroup lattice involved,[^o17] and has also been called the *parallelogram rule* by [Paul Moritz Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Moritz_Cohn), who thus emphasized analogy with the [parallelogram rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram_rule) for vectors because in the resulting subgroup lattice the two sides assumed to represent the quotient groups (*SN*) / *N* and *S* / (*S* ∩ *N*) are "equal" in the sense of isomorphism.[^o18]

[Frattini's argument](wikipedia://en/6532631) guarantees the existence of a product of subgroups (giving rise to the whole group) in a case where the intersection is not necessarily trivial (and for this latter reason the two subgroups are not complements). More specifically, if *G* is a finite group with normal subgroup *N*, and if *P* is a [Sylow *p*-subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_p-subgroup) of *N*, then *G* = *N*<sub>*G*</sub>(*P*)*N*, where *N*<sub>*G*</sub>(*P*) denotes the [normalizer](wikipedia://en/145661) of *P* in *G*. (Note that the normalizer of *P* includes *P*, so the intersection between *N* and *N*<sub>*G*</sub>(*P*) is at least *P*.)

## Generalization to semigroups {#Generalization_to_semigroups}

In a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) S, the product of two subsets defines a structure of a semigroup on P(S), the power set of the semigroup S; furthermore P(S) is a [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468) with addition as union (of subsets) and multiplication as product of subsets.[^o20]
