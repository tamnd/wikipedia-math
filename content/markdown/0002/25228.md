# Q.E.D.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Q.E.D."}

**Q.E.D.** (also written as **QED**) is an [initialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initialism) of the [Latin phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_phrases_%28full%29) **quod erat demonstrandum**, meaning "that which was to be demonstrated". Literally, it states "what was to be shown".[^o2] Traditionally, the abbreviation is placed at the end of [mathematical proofs](wikipedia://en/82285#Ending_a_proof) and [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) [arguments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_argument) in print publications, to indicate that the proof or the argument is complete.

:::

## Etymology and early use {#Etymology_and_early_use}

The phrase *quod erat demonstrandum* is a translation into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_phrases) from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) ὅπερ ἔδει δεῖξαι (hoper edei deixai; abbreviated as *ΟΕΔ*). The meaning of the Latin phrase is "that \[thing\] which was to be demonstrated" (with *demonstrandum* in the [gerundive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerundive)).

The Greek phrase was used by many early Greek mathematicians, including [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)[^o4] and [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844).

The Latin phrase is attested in a 1501 Euclid translation of [Giorgio Valla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_Valla).[^o5] Its abbreviation *q.e.d.* is used once in 1598 by [Johannes Praetorius](wikipedia://en/14447712),[^o6] more in 1643 by [Anton Deusing](wikipedia://en/55737855),[^o7] extensively in 1655 by [Isaac Barrow](wikipedia://en/97805) in the form *Q.E.D.*,[^o8] and subsequently by many post-[Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) mathematicians and philosophers.[^o9]

## Modern philosophy {#Modern_philosophy}

*Image: Philippe van Lansberge 1604 QED.png.* Philippe van Lansberge's 1604 [*Triangulorum Geometriæ*](https://books.google.com/books?id=fg9KAAAAMAAJ&q=quod-erat-demonstrandum+date:0-1700) used quod erat demonstrandum to conclude some proofs; others ended with phrases such as sigillatim deinceps demonstrabitur, magnitudo demonstranda est, and other variants.[^o11]

During the European Renaissance, scholars often wrote in Latin, and phrases such as *Q.E.D.* were often used to conclude proofs.

*Image: Spinoza Ethica Pars1 Prop1.jpg.* [Spinoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baruch_Spinoza)'s original text of *[Ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_%28Spinoza%29)*, Part 1, *Q.E.D.* is used at the end of *Demonstratio* of *Propositio III* on the right hand page

Perhaps the most famous use of *Q.E.D.* in a philosophical argument is found in the *[Ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_%28Spinoza%29)* of [Baruch Spinoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baruch_Spinoza), [published posthumously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_works_published_posthumously) in 1677.[^o12] Written in Latin, it is considered by many to be Spinoza's *[magnum opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masterpiece)*. The style and system of the book are, as Spinoza says, "demonstrated in [geometrical](wikipedia://en/18973446) order", with [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) and definitions followed by [propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition_%28mathematics%29). For Spinoza, this is a considerable improvement over [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525)'s writing style in the *[Meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditations_on_First_Philosophy)*, which follows the form of a [diary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary).[^o13]

## Difference from Q.E.F. {#Difference_from_Q.E.F.}

There is another Latin phrase with a slightly different meaning, usually shortened similarly, but being less common in use. Quod erat faciendum, originating from the Greek geometers' closing ὅπερ ἔδει ποιῆσαι (grc), meaning "which had to be done".[^o15]

[Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) used the Greek equivalent of quod erat faciendum to close propositions that were not proofs of theorems, but constructions of geometric objects.[^o16] For example, Euclid's first proposition showing how to construct an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285), given one side, is concluded this way.[^o17]

## Equivalent forms {#Equivalent_forms}

There is no common formal English equivalent, although the end of a proof may be announced with a simple statement such as "thus it is proved", "this completes the proof", "as required", "as desired", "as expected", "hence proved", "ergo", "so correct", or other similar phrases.

## Typographical forms used symbolically {#Typographical_forms_used_symbolically}

Due to the paramount importance of [proofs in mathematics](wikipedia://en/82285), mathematicians since the time of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) have developed conventions to demarcate the beginning and end of proofs. In printed English language texts, the formal statements of [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977), [lemmas](wikipedia://en/18634), and propositions are set in italics by tradition. The beginning of a proof usually follows immediately thereafter, and is indicated by the word "proof" in boldface or italics. On the other hand, several symbolic conventions exist to indicate the end of a proof.

While some authors still use the classical abbreviation, Q.E.D., it is relatively uncommon in modern mathematical texts. [Paul Halmos](wikipedia://en/318598) claims to have pioneered the use of a solid black square (or rectangle) at the end of a proof as a Q.E.D. symbol,[^o20] a practice which has become standard, although not universal. Halmos noted that he adopted this use of a symbol from magazine [typography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typography) customs in which simple geometric shapes had been used to indicate the end of an article, so-called *end marks*.[^o21][^o22] This symbol was later called the *[tombstone](wikipedia://en/819251)*, the *Halmos symbol*, or even a *halmos* by mathematicians. Often the Halmos symbol is drawn on chalkboard to signal the end of a proof during a lecture, although this practice is not so common as its use in printed text.

The tombstone symbol appears in [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX) as the character $\blacksquare$ (filled square, \\blacksquare) and sometimes, as a $\square$ (hollow square, \\square or \\Box).[^o23] In the AMS Theorem Environment for [LaTeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LaTeX), the hollow square is the default end-of-proof symbol. [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) explicitly provides the "end of proof" character, U+220E (∎). Some authors use other Unicode symbols to note the end of a proof, including, ▮ (U+25AE, a black vertical rectangle), and ‣ (U+2023, a triangular bullet). Other authors have adopted two forward slashes (//, $//$) or four forward slashes (////, $////$).[^o24] In other cases, authors have elected to segregate proofs typographically—by displaying them as indented blocks.[^o25]
