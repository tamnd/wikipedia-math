# P-group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="P-group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), given a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*, a ***p*-group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) in which the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group_element) of every element is a [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28mathematics%29) of *p*. That is, for each element *g* of a *p*-group *G*, there exists a [nonnegative integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative_integer) *n* such that the product of *p<sup>n</sup>* copies of *g*, and not fewer, is equal to the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962). The orders of different elements may be different powers of *p*.

:::

[Abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) *p*-groups are also called ***p*-primary** or simply **primary**.

A [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is a *p*-group if and only if its [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) (the number of its elements) is a power of *p*. Given a finite group *G*, the [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993) guarantee the existence of a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of *G* of order *p<sup>n</sup>* for every [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) *p<sup>n</sup>* that divides the order of *G*.

Every finite *p*-group is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569).

The remainder of this article deals with finite *p*-groups. For an example of an infinite abelian *p*-group, see [Prüfer group](wikipedia://en/4885031), and for an example of an infinite [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) *p*-group, see [Tarski monster group](wikipedia://en/727196).

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Every *p*-group is [periodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_group) since by definition every element has [finite order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_order).

If *p* is prime and *G* is a group of order *p*<sup>*k*</sup>, then *G* has a normal subgroup of order *p*<sup>*m*</sup> for every 1 ≤ *m* ≤ *k*. This follows by induction, using [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/2838129) and the [Correspondence Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_theorem_%28group_theory%29) for groups. A proof sketch is as follows: because the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) *Z* of *G* is [non-trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) (see below), according to [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/2838129) *Z* has a subgroup *H* of order *p*. Being central in *G*, *H* is necessarily normal in *G*. We may now apply the inductive hypothesis to *G/H*, and the result follows from the Correspondence Theorem.

### Non-trivial center {#Non-trivial_center}

One of the first standard results using the [class equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_equation) is that the [center](wikipedia://en/7125) of a non-trivial finite *p*-group cannot be the trivial subgroup.[^o5]

This forms the basis for many inductive methods in *p*-groups.

For instance, the [normalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer) *N* of a [proper subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subgroup) *H* of a finite *p*-group *G* properly contains *H*, because for any [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) with *H* = *N*, the center *Z* is contained in *N*, and so also in *H*, but then there is a smaller example *H*/*Z* whose normalizer in *G*/*Z* is *N*/*Z* = *H*/*Z*, creating an infinite descent. As a corollary, every finite *p*-group is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569).

In another direction, every [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) *N* of a finite *p*-group intersects the center non-trivially as may be proved by considering the elements of *N* which are fixed when *G* acts on *N* by conjugation. Since every central subgroup is normal, it follows that every minimal normal subgroup of a finite *p*-group is central and has order *p*. Indeed, the [socle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socle_of_a_group) of a finite *p*-group is the subgroup of the center consisting of the central elements of order *p*.

If *G* is a *p*-group, then so is *G*/*Z*, and so it too has a non-trivial center. The preimage in *G* of the center of *G*/*Z* is called the [second center](wikipedia://en/7125#Higher_centers) and these groups begin the [upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series). Generalizing the earlier comments about the socle, a finite *p*-group with order *p<sup>n</sup>* contains normal subgroups of order *p<sup>i</sup>* with 0 ≤ *i* ≤ *n*, and any normal subgroup of order *p<sup>i</sup>* is contained in the *i*th center *Z*<sub>*i*</sub>. If a normal subgroup is not contained in *Z*<sub>*i*</sub>, then its intersection with *Z*<sub>*i*+1</sub> has size at least *p*<sup>*i*+1</sup>.

### Automorphisms {#Automorphisms}

The [automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_automorphism) groups of *p*-groups are well studied. Just as every finite *p*-group has a non-trivial center so that the [inner automorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_automorphism_group) is a proper quotient of the group, every finite *p*-group has a non-trivial [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466). Every automorphism of *G* induces an automorphism on *G*/Φ(*G*), where Φ(*G*) is the [Frattini subgroup](wikipedia://en/540707) of *G*. The quotient G/Φ(*G*) is an [elementary abelian group](wikipedia://en/4843202) and its [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) is a [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), so very well understood. The map from the automorphism group of *G* into this general linear group has been studied by [Burnside](wikipedia://en/251883), who showed that the kernel of this map is a *p*-group.

## Examples {#Examples}

*p*-groups of the same order are not necessarily [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828); for example, the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) *C*<sub>4</sub> and the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) *V*<sub>4</sub> are both 2-groups of order 4, but they are not isomorphic.

Nor need a *p*-group be [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974); the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) Dih<sub>4</sub> of order 8 is a non-abelian 2-group. However, every group of order *p*<sup>2</sup> is abelian.[^o9]

The dihedral groups are both very similar to and very dissimilar from the [quaternion groups](wikipedia://en/153130) and the [semidihedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semidihedral_group). Together the dihedral, semidihedral, and quaternion groups form the 2-groups of [maximal class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_class), that is those groups of order 2<sup>*n*+1</sup> and nilpotency class *n*.

### Iterated wreath products {#Iterated_wreath_products}

The iterated [wreath products](wikipedia://en/146625) of cyclic groups of order *p* are very important examples of *p*-groups. Denote the cyclic group of order *p* as *W*(1), and the wreath product of *W*(*n*) with *W*(1) as *W*(*n* + 1). Then *W*(*n*) is the Sylow *p*-subgroup of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) Sym(*p*<sup>*n*</sup>). Maximal *p*-subgroups of the general linear group GL(*n*,**Q**) are direct products of various *W*(*n*). It has order *p*<sup>*k*</sup> where *k* = (*p*<sup>*n*</sup> − 1)/(*p* − 1). It has nilpotency class *p*<sup>*n*−1</sup>, and its lower central series, upper central series, lower exponent-*p* central series, and upper exponent-*p* central series are equal. It is generated by its elements of order *p*, but its exponent is *p*<sup>*n*</sup>. The second such group, *W*(2), is also a *p*-group of maximal class, since it has order *p*<sup>*p*+1</sup> and nilpotency class *p*, but is not a [regular *p*-group](wikipedia://en/15442661). Since groups of order *p*<sup>*p*</sup> are always regular groups, it is also a minimal such example.

### Generalized dihedral groups {#Generalized_dihedral_groups}

When *p* = 2 and *n* = 2, *W*(*n*) is the dihedral group of order 8, so in some sense *W*(*n*) provides an analogue for the dihedral group for all primes *p* when *n* = 2. However, for higher *n* the analogy becomes strained. There is a different family of examples that more closely mimics the dihedral groups of order 2<sup>*n*</sup>, but that requires a bit more setup. Let ζ denote a primitive *p*th root of unity in the complex numbers, let **Z**\[ζ\] be the ring of [cyclotomic integers](wikipedia://en/460613) generated by it, and let *P* be the [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) generated by 1−ζ. Let *G* be a cyclic group of order *p* generated by an element *z*. Form the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) *E*(*p*) of **Z**\[ζ\] and *G* where *z* acts as multiplication by ζ. The powers *P*<sup>*n*</sup> are normal subgroups of *E*(*p*), and the example groups are *E*(*p*,*n*) = *E*(*p*)/*P*<sup>*n*</sup>. *E*(*p*,*n*) has order *p*<sup>*n*+1</sup> and nilpotency class *n*, so is a *p*-group of maximal class. When *p* = 2, *E*(2,*n*) is the dihedral group of order 2<sup>*n*</sup>. When *p* is odd, both *W*(2) and *E*(*p*,*p*) are irregular groups of maximal class and order *p*<sup>*p*+1</sup>, but are not isomorphic.

### Unitriangular matrix groups {#Unitriangular_matrix_groups}

The Sylow subgroups of [general linear groups](wikipedia://en/113564) are another fundamental family of examples. Let *V* be a vector space of dimension *n* with basis { *e*<sub>1</sub>, *e*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *e*<sub>*n*</sub> } and define *V*<sub>*i*</sub> to be the vector space generated by { *e*<sub>*i*</sub>, *e*<sub>*i*+1</sub>, ..., *e*<sub>*n*</sub> } for 1 ≤ *i* ≤ *n*, and define *V*<sub>*i*</sub> = 0 when *i* > *n*. For each 1 ≤ *m* ≤ *n*, the set of invertible linear transformations of *V* which take each *V*<sub>*i*</sub> to *V*<sub>*i*+*m*</sub> form a subgroup of Aut(*V*) denoted *U*<sub>*m*</sub>. If *V* is a vector space over **Z**/*p***Z**, then *U*<sub>1</sub> is a Sylow *p*-subgroup of Aut(*V*) = GL(*n*, *p*), and the terms of its [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series) are just the *U*<sub>*m*</sub>. In terms of matrices, *U*<sub>*m*</sub> are those upper triangular matrices with 1s one the diagonal and 0s on the first *m*−1 superdiagonals. The group *U*<sub>1</sub> has order *p*<sup>*n*·(*n*−1)/2</sup>, nilpotency class *n*, and exponent *p*<sup>*k*</sup> where *k* is the least integer at least as large as the base *p* [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of *n*.

:::

## Classification {#Classification}

The groups of order *p*<sup>*n*</sup> for 0 ≤ *n* ≤ 4 were classified early in the history of group theory,[^o14] and modern work has extended these classifications to groups whose order divides *p*<sup>7</sup>, though the sheer number of families of such groups grows so quickly that further classifications along these lines are judged difficult for the human mind to comprehend.[^o15] For example, [Marshall Hall Jr.](wikipedia://en/7758584) and James K. Senior classified groups of order 2<sup>*n*</sup> for *n* ≤ 6 in 1964.[^o16]

Rather than classify the groups by order, [Philip Hall](wikipedia://en/1655033) proposed using a notion of [isoclinism of groups](wikipedia://en/27114298) which gathered finite *p*-groups into families based on large quotient and subgroups.[^o17]

An entirely different method classifies finite *p*-groups by their **[coclass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coclass)**, that is, the difference between their [composition length](wikipedia://en/297493) and their [nilpotency class](wikipedia://en/144569). The so-called **[coclass conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coclass_conjectures)** described the set of all finite *p*-groups of fixed coclass as perturbations of finitely many [pro-p groups](wikipedia://en/1842075). The coclass conjectures were proven in the 1980s using techniques related to [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) and [powerful p-groups](wikipedia://en/1841291).[^o18] The final proofs of the **coclass theorems** are due to A. Shalev and independently to C. R. Leedham-Green, both in 1994. They admit a classification of finite *p*-groups in [directed coclass graphs](wikipedia://en/42823620#Multifurcation_and_coclass_graphs) consisting of only finitely many coclass trees whose (infinitely many) members are characterized by finitely many parametrized presentations.

Every group of order *p*<sup>5</sup> is [metabelian](wikipedia://en/1959273).[^o19]

### Up to *p*<sup>3</sup> {#Up_to_p3}

The trivial group is the only group of order one, and the cyclic group C<sub>*p*</sub> is the only group of order *p*. There are exactly two groups of order *p*<sup>2</sup>, both abelian, namely C<sub>*p*<sup>2</sup></sub> and C<sub>*p*</sub> × C<sub>*p*</sub>. For example, the cyclic group C<sub>4</sub> and the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928) *V*<sub>4</sub> which is C<sub>2</sub> × C<sub>2</sub> are both 2-groups of order 4.

There are three abelian groups of order *p*<sup>3</sup>, namely C<sub>*p*<sup>3</sup></sub>, C<sub>*p*<sup>2</sup></sub> × C<sub>*p*</sub>, and C<sub>*p*</sub> × C<sub>*p*</sub> × C<sub>*p*</sub>. There are also two non-abelian groups.

For *p* ≠ 2, one is a semi-direct product of C<sub>*p*</sub> × C<sub>*p*</sub> with C<sub>*p*</sub>, and the other is a semi-direct product of C<sub>*p*<sup>2</sup></sub> with C<sub>*p*</sub>. The first one can be described in other terms as group UT(3,*p*) of unitriangular matrices over finite field with *p* elements, also called the [Heisenberg group mod *p*](wikipedia://en/448518#Heisenberg_group_modulo_an_odd_prime_p).

For *p* = 2, both the semi-direct products mentioned above are isomorphic to the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) Dih<sub>4</sub> of order 8. The other non-abelian group of order 8 is the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) Q<sub>8</sub>.

## Prevalence {#Prevalence}

### Among groups {#Among_groups}

The [Higman–Sims asymptotic formula](wikipedia://en/59829761) states that the number of isomorphism classes of groups of order *p<sup>n</sup>* grows as $p^{\frac{2}{27}n^3+O(n^{8/3})}$, and these are dominated by the classes that are two-step nilpotent.[^o23] Because of this rapid growth, there is a [folklore](wikipedia://en/168905) conjecture asserting that almost all [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707) are 2-groups: the fraction of [isomorphism classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class) of 2-groups among isomorphism classes of groups of order at most *n* is thought to tend to 1 as *n* tends to infinity. For instance, of the 49 910 529 484 different groups of order at most 2000, 49, or just over 99%, are 2-groups of order 1024.[^o24]

### Within a group {#Within_a_group}

Every finite group whose order is divisible by *p* contains a subgroup which is a non-trivial *p*-group, namely a cyclic group of order *p* generated by an element of order *p* obtained from [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/2838129). In fact, it contains a *p*-group of maximal possible order: if $|G|=n=p^km$ where *p* does not divide *m,* then *G* has a subgroup *P* of order $p^k,$ called a Sylow *p*-subgroup. This subgroup need not be unique, but any subgroups of this order are conjugate, and any *p*-subgroup of *G* is contained in a Sylow *p*-subgroup. This and other properties are proved in the [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993).

## Application to structure of a group {#Application_to_structure_of_a_group}

*p*-groups are fundamental tools in understanding the structure of groups and in the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). *p*-groups arise both as subgroups and as quotient groups. As subgroups, for a given prime *p* one has the Sylow *p*-subgroups *P* (largest *p*-subgroup not unique but all conjugate) and the [*p*-core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-core) $O_p(G)$ (the unique largest *normal* *p*-subgroup), and various others. As quotients, the largest *p*-group quotient is the quotient of *G* by the [*p*-residual subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-residual_subgroup) $O^p(G).$ These groups are related (for different primes), possess important properties such as the [focal subgroup theorem](wikipedia://en/25971038), and allow one to determine many aspects of the structure of the group.

### Local control {#Local_control}

Much of the structure of a finite group is carried in the structure of its so-called **local subgroups**, the [normalizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer) of non-identity *p*-subgroups.[^o28]

The large [elementary abelian subgroups](wikipedia://en/4843202) of a finite group exert control over the group that was used in the proof of the [Feit–Thompson theorem](wikipedia://en/1656473). Certain [central extensions](wikipedia://en/345807#Central_extension) of elementary abelian groups called [extraspecial groups](wikipedia://en/6990619) help describe the structure of groups as acting on [symplectic vector spaces](wikipedia://en/292852).

[Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134) classified all groups whose Sylow 2-subgroups are the direct product of two cyclic groups of order 4, and [John Walter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Walter_%28mathematician%29), [Daniel Gorenstein](wikipedia://en/1569478), [Helmut Bender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Bender), [Michio Suzuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michio_Suzuki_%28mathematician%29), [George Glauberman](wikipedia://en/9744285), and others classified those simple groups whose Sylow 2-subgroups were abelian, dihedral, semidihedral, or quaternion.
