# Roger Bacon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Roger Bacon"}

**Roger Bacon** (;[^o5] Rogerus or *Rogerius Baconus, Baconis*, also *la Rogerus*; c. 1219/20), also known by the [scholastic accolade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastic_accolades) ***Doctor Mirabilis***, was a medieval English [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath), [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher), [scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientist), [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theologian) and [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscans) [friar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friar) who placed considerable emphasis on the study of [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature) through [empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism). Intertwining his Catholic faith with scientific thinking, Roger Bacon is considered one of the greatest polymaths of the [medieval period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Period).

:::

In the [early modern era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period), he was regarded as a [wizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wizard_%28paranormal%29) and particularly famed for the story of his [mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_robots) or [necromantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necromancy) [brazen head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazen_head). He is credited as one of the earliest European advocates of the modern [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method), along with his teacher [Robert Grosseteste](wikipedia://en/94721). Bacon applied the empirical method of [Ibn al-Haytham](wikipedia://en/1645) (Alhazen) to observations in texts attributed to [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308). Bacon discovered the importance of empirical testing when the results he obtained were different from those that would have been predicted by Aristotle.[^n1][^o7]

His linguistic work has been heralded for its early exposition of a [universal grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_grammar), and 21st-century re-evaluations emphasise that Bacon was essentially a medieval thinker, with much of his "experimental" knowledge obtained from books in the [scholastic tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism).[^n2] He was, however, partially responsible for a revision of the [medieval university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university) curriculum, which saw the addition of [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_optics) to the traditional *[quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium)*.[^n3]

Bacon's major work, the [Opus Majus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_Majus), was sent to [Pope Clement IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_IV) in Rome in 1267 upon the pope's request. Although [gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder) was [first invented and described in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_gunpowder), Bacon was the first in Europe to record its formula.

## Life {#Life}

Roger Bacon was born in [Ilchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilchester) in [Somerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerset,_England), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_England), in the early 13th century. His birth is sometimes narrowed down to 1210,[^n4] 1213 or 1214,[^n5] 1215[^n6] or 1220.[^o14] The only source for his birth date is a statement from his 1267 *Opus Tertium* that "forty years have passed since I first learned the *Alphabetum*".[^n7] The latest dates assume this referred to the [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet) itself, but elsewhere in the *Opus Tertium* it is clear that Bacon uses the term to refer to rudimentary studies, the [trivium](wikipedia://en/30666) or [quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium) that formed the [medieval curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university#Course_of_study).[^n8] His family appears to have been well off.[^n9]

Bacon studied at [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University). While [Robert Grosseteste](wikipedia://en/94721) had probably left shortly before Bacon's arrival, his work and legacy almost certainly influenced the young scholar[^n4] and it is possible Bacon subsequently visited him and [William of Sherwood](wikipedia://en/2884630) in [Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln,_England).[^n10] Bacon became a [Master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) at Oxford, lecturing on [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308). There is no evidence he was ever awarded a doctorate. (The title *Doctor Mirabilis* was a posthumous [scholastic accolade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastic_accolade).) A caustic cleric named Roger Bacon is recorded speaking before the king at Oxford in 1233.[^o20]

*Image: Roger Bacon Wellcome M0005408.jpg.* A diorama of Bacon presenting one of his works to the chancellors of [Paris University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_University)

In 1237 or at some point in the following decade, he accepted an invitation to teach at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris).[^n11] While there, he lectured on [Latin grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_grammar), [Aristotelian logic](wikipedia://en/321652), [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_arithmetic), [geometry](wikipedia://en/14220), and the mathematical aspects of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_astronomy) and [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_music).[^n12] His faculty colleagues included [Robert Kilwardby](wikipedia://en/153666), [Albertus Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus), and [Peter of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_of_Spain_%28logician%29),[^n13] who later became Pope as [Pope John XXI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_John_XXI).[^n14] The [Cornishman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_people) [Richard Rufus](wikipedia://en/4168253) was a scholarly opponent.[^n12] In 1247 or soon after, he left his position in Paris.[^n14]

*Image: Bacon\_1867.jpg.* A 19th-century engraving of Bacon observing the stars at [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University)

As a private scholar, his whereabouts for the next decade are uncertain[^n15] but he was likely in Oxford c. 1248–1251, where he met [Adam Marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Marsh), and in Paris in 1251.[^n12] He seems to have studied most of the known [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science_in_classical_antiquity) and [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world) works on [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_optics)[^n13] (then known as "perspective", *perspectiva*). A passage in the *Opus Tertium* states that at some point he took a two-year break from his studies.[^n7]

By the late 1250s, resentment against [the king](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England)'s preferential treatment of [his émigré Poitevin relatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lusignan) led to [a coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England#Revolution) and the imposition of the [Provisions of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisions_of_Oxford) and [Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisions_of_Westminster), instituting a [baronial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron) council and more frequent [parliaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_England). [Pope Urban IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_IV) absolved the king of his oath in 1261 and, after [initial abortive resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Lambeth), [Simon de Montfort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_de_Montfort,_6th&nbsp;Earl_of_Leicester) led a force, enlarged due to recent crop failures, that prosecuted the [Second Barons' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Barons'_War). Bacon's own family were considered royal partisans:[^n16] De Montfort's men seized their property and drove several members into exile.[^n17]

*Image: Roger Bacon in his observatory at Merton College, Oxford. Oi Wellcome M0001840.jpg.* [Ernest Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Board)'s portrayal of Bacon in his observatory at [Merton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College,_Oxford)

In 1256 or 1257, he became a [friar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friar) in the [Franciscan Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscans) in either Paris or Oxford, following the example of scholarly English Franciscans such as [Grosseteste](wikipedia://en/94721) and [Marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Marsh).[^n12] After 1260, Bacon's activities were restricted by a statute prohibiting the friars of his order from publishing books or pamphlets without prior approval.[^n18] He was likely kept at constant menial tasks to limit his time for contemplation[^n19] and came to view his treatment as an enforced absence from scholarly life.[^n12]

By the mid-1260s, he was undertaking a search for patrons who could secure permission and funding for his return to Oxford.[^n19] For a time, Bacon was finally able to get around his superiors' interference through his acquaintance with [Guy de Foulques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_IV), [bishop of Narbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Diocese_of_Narbonne), [cardinal of Sabina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Suburbicarian_Diocese_of_Sabina-Poggio_Mirteto), and the [papal legate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_legate) who negotiated between England's royal and baronial factions.[^n16]

In 1263 or 1264, a message garbled by Bacon's messenger, Raymond of Laon, led Guy to believe that Bacon had already completed a summary of the sciences. In fact, he had no money to research, let alone copy, such a work and attempts to secure financing from his family were thwarted by the Second Barons' War. However, in 1265, Guy was summoned to a conclave at [Perugia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perugia) that [elected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_conclave) him [Pope Clement IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_IV).[^n20] William Benecor, who had previously been the courier between [Henry III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_III_of_England) and the pope, now carried the correspondence between Bacon and Clement.[^n20] Clement's reply of 22 June 1266 commissioned "writings and remedies for current conditions", instructing Bacon not to violate any standing "prohibitions" of his order but to carry out his task in utmost secrecy.[^n20]

While faculties of the time were largely limited to addressing disputes on the known texts of Aristotle, Clement's patronage permitted Bacon to engage in a wide-ranging consideration of the state of knowledge in his era.[^n12] In 1267 or '68, Bacon sent the Pope his *[Opus Majus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_Majus)*, which presented his views on how to incorporate [Aristotelian logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_logic) and [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_science#Aristotle) into a new theology, supporting Grosseteste's text-based approach against the "sentence method" then fashionable.[^n12]

Bacon also sent his *Opus Minus*, *De Multiplicatione Specierum*,[^n21] *De Speculis Comburentibus*, an optical lens,[^n12] and possibly other works on [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy) and [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology).[^n21] The entire process has been called "one of the most remarkable single efforts of literary productivity", with Bacon composing referenced works of around a million words in about a year.[^n22]

Pope Clement died in 1268 and Bacon lost his protector. The [Condemnations of 1277](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condemnations_of_1210–1277) banned the teaching of certain philosophical doctrines, including deterministic astrology. Some time within the next two years, Bacon was apparently imprisoned or placed under [house arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_arrest). This was traditionally ascribed to [Franciscan Minister General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_General_%28Franciscan%29) [Jerome of Ascoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Nicholas_IV), probably acting on behalf of the many clergy, monks, and educators attacked by Bacon's 1271 *Compendium Studii Philosophiae*.[^n17]

Modern scholarship, however, notes that the first reference to Bacon's "imprisonment" dates from eighty years after his death on the charge of unspecified "suspected novelties"[^o49][^n23] and finds it less than credible.[^n24] Contemporary scholars who do accept Bacon's imprisonment typically associate it with Bacon's "attraction to contemporary prophesies",[^n25] his sympathies for "the radical 'poverty' wing of the Franciscans",[^n24] interest in certain [astrological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) doctrines,[^n26] or generally combative personality[^n23] rather than from "any scientific novelties which he may have proposed".[^n24]

Sometime after 1278, Bacon returned to the Franciscan House at Oxford, where he continued his studies[^n27] and is presumed to have spent most of the remainder of his life. His last dateable writing—the *Compendium Studii Theologiae*—was completed in 1292.[^n17] He seems to have died shortly afterwards and been buried at Oxford.[^n28][^o60]

## Work {#Work}

*Image: Roger Bacon Wellcome M0004484.jpg.* A manuscript illustration of Bacon presenting one of his works to the chancellor of the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris)

Medieval European philosophy often relied on [appeals to the authority](wikipedia://en/37568781) of [Church Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers) such as [St Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo), and on works by [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) and [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) only known at second hand or through Latin translations. By the 13th century, new works and better versions – in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) or in new Latin translations from the Arabic – began to trickle north from [Muslim Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus). In Roger Bacon's writings, he upholds Aristotle's calls for the collection of facts before deducing scientific truths, against the practices of his contemporaries, arguing that "thence cometh quiet to the mind".

Bacon also called for reform with regard to [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology). He argued that, rather than training to debate minor philosophical distinctions, theologians should focus their attention primarily on the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible) itself, learning the languages of its original sources thoroughly. He was fluent in several of these languages and was able to note and bemoan several corruptions of scripture, and of the works of the Greek philosophers that had been mistranslated or misinterpreted by scholars working in Latin. He also argued for the education of theologians in science ("[natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy)") and its addition to the [medieval curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university#Course_of_study).

### *Opus Majus* {#Opus_Majus}

*Image: Roger Bacon optics01.jpg.* [Optic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics) studies by Bacon

Bacon's 1267 *Greater Work*, the *[Opus Majus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_Majus)*, contains treatments of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy), and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), including theories on the positions and sizes of the [celestial bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_bodies). It is divided into seven sections: "The Four General Causes of Human Ignorance" (*Causae Erroris*), "The Affinity of Philosophy with Theology" (*Philosophiae cum Theologia Affinitas*), "On the Usefulness of Grammar" (*De Utilitate Grammaticae*),[^o63] "The Usefulness of Mathematics in Physics" (*Mathematicae in Physicis Utilitas*),[^o75] "On the Science of Perspective" (*De Scientia Perspectivae*), "On Experimental Knowledge" (*De Scientia Experimentali*), and "A Philosophy of Morality" (*Moralis Philosophia*).[^o64]

It was not intended as a complete work but as a "persuasive preamble" (*persuasio praeambula*), an enormous proposal for a reform of the [medieval university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university) curriculum and the establishment of a kind of library or encyclopedia, bringing in experts to compose a collection of definitive texts on these subjects.[^n29] The new subjects were to be "perspective" (i.e., [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics)), "astronomy" (inclusive of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) proper, [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), and the [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography) necessary to use them), "weights" (likely some treatment of [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) but this section of the *Opus Majus* has been lost), [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy), [agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture) (inclusive of [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany) and [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology)), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine), and "[experimental](wikipedia://en/59861) science", a [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science) that would guide the others.[^n29] The section on geography was allegedly originally ornamented with a [map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cartography) based on ancient and Arabic computations of longitude and latitude, but has since been lost. His (mistaken) arguments supporting the idea that dry land formed the larger proportion of the globe were apparently similar to those which later guided [Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus).[^o67]

In this work Bacon criticises his contemporaries [Alexander of Hales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_of_Hales) and [Albertus Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus), who were held in high repute despite having only acquired their knowledge of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) at second hand during their preaching careers.[^n30][^n31] Albert was received at Paris as an authority equal to Aristotle, [Avicenna](wikipedia://en/1130) and [Averroes](wikipedia://en/47836),[^n32] a situation Bacon decried: "never in the world \[had\] such monstrosity occurred before".[^n33]

In Part I of the *Opus Majus* Bacon recognises some philosophers as the *Sapientes*, or gifted few, and saw their knowledge in philosophy and theology as superior to the *vulgus philosophantium*, or common herd of philosophers. He held Islamic thinkers between 1210 and 1265 in especially high regard calling them "both philosophers and sacred writers" and defended the integration of philosophy from apostate philosopher of the Islamic world into Christian learning.[^n34]

#### Calendrical reform {#Calendrical_reform}

In Part IV of the *Opus Majus*, Bacon proposed a [calendrical reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar_reform) similar to the later [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar#Gregorian_reform) introduced in 1582 under [Pope Gregory XIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_XIII).[^o75] Drawing on [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_astronomy) and [medieval Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world) astronomy recently introduced to western Europe via Spain, Bacon continued the work of [Robert Grosseteste](wikipedia://en/94721) and criticised the then-current [Julian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_calendar) as "intolerable, horrible, and laughable".

It had become apparent that [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388) and [Sosigenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sosigenes_of_Alexandria)'s assumption of a year of 365¼ days was, over the course of centuries, too inexact. Bacon charged that this meant the [computation of Easter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computus) had shifted forward by 9 days since the [First Council of Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Council_of_Nicaea) in 325.[^o74] His proposal to drop one day every 125 years[^o75][^n35] and to cease the observance of fixed [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) and [solstices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstice)[^o74] was not acted upon following the death of [Pope Clement IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_IV) in 1268. The eventual [Gregorian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar) drops one day from the first three centuries in each set of 400 years.

#### Optics {#Optics}

*Image: Optics from Roger Bacon's De multiplicatone specierum.jpg.* Bacon's diagram of light being refracted by a spherical container of water

In Part V of the *[Opus Majus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_Majus)*, Bacon discusses [physiology of eyesight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_system) and the anatomy of the [eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_eye) and the [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_brain), considering [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light), distance, position, and size, direct and [reflected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28physics%29) vision, [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction), [mirrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror), and [lenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29).[^o64] His treatment was primarily oriented by the Latin translation of [Alhazen](wikipedia://en/1645)'s *[Book of Optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Optics)*. He also draws heavily on [Eugene of Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_of_Palermo)'s Latin translation of the Arabic translation of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claudius_Ptolemy)'s *[Optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics_%28Ptolemy%29)*; on [Robert Grosseteste](wikipedia://en/94721)'s work based on [Al-Kindi](wikipedia://en/272065)'s *[Optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics_%28Al-Kindi%29)*;[^n1][^o79] and, through Alhazen ([Ibn al-Haytham](wikipedia://en/1645)), on [Ibn Sahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Sahl_%28mathematician%29)'s work on [dioptrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioptrics).[^n36]

#### Gunpowder {#Gunpowder}

*Image: 137-ROGER BACON DISCOVERS GUNPOWDER.jpg.* "Roger Bacon discovers gunpowder", "whereby [Guy Fawkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Fawkes) was made possible",[^o82] an image from *[Bill Nye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Wilson_Nye)'s Comic History of England*[^o83]

A passage in the *Opus Majus* and another in the *Opus Tertium* are usually taken as the first European descriptions of a mixture containing the essential ingredients of [gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder). [Partington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._Partington) and others have come to the conclusion that Bacon most likely witnessed at least one demonstration of [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_dynasty) [firecrackers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firecracker), possibly obtained by Franciscans—including Bacon's friend [William of Rubruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Rubruck)—who visited the [Mongol Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongol_Empire) during this period.[^n37] The most telling passage reads:

> We have an example of these things (that act on the senses) in \[the sound and fire of\] that children's toy which is made in many \[diverse\] parts of the world; i.e. a device no bigger than one's thumb. From the violence of that salt called saltpetre \[together with sulphur and willow charcoal, combined into a powder\] so horrible a sound is made by the bursting of a thing so small, no more than a bit of parchment \[containing it\], that we find \[the ear assaulted by a noise\] exceeding the roar of strong thunder, and a flash brighter than the most brilliant lightning.[^n37]

At the beginning of the 20th century, [Henry William Lovett Hime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_William_Lovett_Hime) of the [Royal Artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Artillery) published the theory that Bacon's *Epistola* contained a [cryptogram](wikipedia://en/69794) giving a recipe for the gunpowder he witnessed.[^o86] The theory was criticised by [Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Thorndike) in a 1915 letter to *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_%28journal%29)*[^n38] and several books, a position joined by [Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._M._Pattison_Muir),[^n39] [John Maxson Stillman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maxson_Stillman),[^n39] [Steele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Steele_%28medievalist%29),[^n40] and [Sarton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Sarton).[^n41] [Needham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Needham) et al. concurred with these earlier critics that the additional passage did not originate with Bacon[^n37] and further showed that the proportions supposedly deciphered (a 7:5:5 ratio of [saltpetre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niter) to [charcoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcoal) to [sulphur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur)) as not even useful for firecrackers, burning slowly with a great deal of smoke and failing to ignite inside a gun barrel.[^n42] The ~41% [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) content is too low to have explosive properties.[^n43]

### *Secret of Secrets* {#Secret_of_Secrets}

Bacon attributed the *Secret of Secrets* (*Secretum Secretorum*) (Sirr al-ʿasrar), to [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), thinking that he had composed it for [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great). Bacon produced an edition of [Philip of Tripoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_of_Tripoli)'s Latin translation, complete with his own introduction and notes; and his writings of the 1260s and 1270s cite it far more than his contemporaries did. This led [Easton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewart_C._Easton)[^n45] and others, including [Robert Steele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Steele_%28medievalist%29),[^n46] to argue that the text spurred Bacon's own transformation into an experimentalist. (Bacon never described such a decisive impact himself.)[^n46] The dating of Bacon's edition of the *Secret of Secrets* is a key piece of evidence in the debate, with those arguing for a greater impact giving it an earlier date;[^n46] but it certainly influenced the elder Bacon's conception of the political aspects of his work in the sciences.[^n12]

### Alchemy {#Alchemy}

*Image: Roger Bacon conducting an alchemical experiment in a vaulted Wellcome V0025604.jpg.* A 19th-century etching of Bacon conducting an alchemical experiment

Bacon has been credited with a number of [alchemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy) texts.[^n47]

The *Letter on the Secret Workings of Art and Nature and on the Vanity of Magic* (*Epistola de Secretis Operibus Artis et Naturae et de Nullitate Magiae*),[^n48] also known as *On the Wonderful Powers of Art and Nature* (*De Mirabili Potestate Artis et Naturae*), a likely-forged letter to an unknown "William of Paris", dismisses practices such as [necromancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necromancy)[^n49] but contains most of the alchemical formulae attributed to Bacon,[^n47] including one for a [philosopher's stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher's_stone)[^n50] and another possibly for [gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder).[^n37] It also includes several passages about [hypothetical flying machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_flight) and [submarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_submarines), attributing their first use to [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great).[^n51] *On the Vanity of Magic* or *The Nullity of Magic* is a [debunking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debunking) of esoteric claims in Bacon's time, showing that they could be explained by natural phenomena.[^n52]

He wrote on the medicine of [Galen](wikipedia://en/12326), referring to the translations of [Avicenna](wikipedia://en/1130). He believed that the medicine of Galen belonged to an ancient tradition passed through [Chaldeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaldea), [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks) and [Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab).[^o111] Although he provided a negative image of [Hermes Trismegistus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus), his work was influenced by Hermetic thought.[^o112] Bacon's endorsement of Hermetic philosophy is evident, as his citations of the alchemical literature known as the Secretum Secretorum made several appearances in the Opus Majus. The Secretum Secretorum contains knowledge about the Hermetic [Emerald Tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald_Tablet), which was an integral component of alchemy, thus proving that Bacon's version of alchemy was much less secular, and much more spiritual than once interpreted. The importance of Hermetic philosophy in Bacon's work is also evident through his citations of classic Hermetic literature such as the Corpus Hermeticum. Bacon's citation of the Corpus Hermeticum, which consists of a dialogue between Hermes and the pagan deity [Asclepius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asclepius), proves that Bacon's ideas were much more in line with the spiritual aspects of alchemy rather than the scientific aspects. However, this is somewhat paradoxical as what Bacon was specifically trying to prove in the Opus Majus and subsequent works, was that spirituality and science were the same entity. Bacon believed that by using science, certain aspects of spirituality such as the attainment of "Sapientia" or "Divine Wisdom" could be logically explained using tangible evidence. Bacon's Opus Majus was first and foremost, a compendium of sciences which he believed would facilitate the first step towards "Sapientia". Bacon placed considerable emphasis on alchemy and even went so far as to state that alchemy was the most important science. The reason why Bacon kept the topic of alchemy vague for the most part, is due to the need for secrecy about esoteric topics in England at the time as well as his dedication to remaining in line with the alchemical tradition of speaking in symbols and metaphors.[^o113]

### Linguistics {#Linguistics}

Bacon's early linguistic and logical works are the *Overview of Grammar* (*[Summa Grammatica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Grammatica)*), *Summa de Sophismatibus et Distinctionibus*, and the *Summulae Dialectices* or *Summulae super Totam Logicam*.[^n12] These are mature but essentially conventional presentations of Oxford and Paris's terminist and pre-[modist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modistae) logic and grammar.[^n12] His later work in linguistics is much more idiosyncratic, using terminology and addressing questions unique in his era.[^o117]

In his *[Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatica_Graeca)* and *[Hebrew Grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatica_Hebraica)* (*Grammatica Graeca* and *Hebraica*), in his work "On the Usefulness of Grammar" (Book III of the *Opus Majus*), and in his *Compendium of the Study of Philosophy*,[^o117] Bacon stresses the need for scholars to know several languages.[^o118] Europe's vernacular languages are not ignored—he considers them useful for practical purposes such as [trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_trade), [proselytism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_proselytism), and [administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_in_the_High_Middle_Ages)—but Bacon is mostly interested in his era's [languages of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_science) and religion: [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language), [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Latin).[^o118]

Bacon is less interested in a full practical mastery of the other languages than on a theoretical understanding of their grammatical rules, ensuring that a Latin reader will not misunderstand passages' [original meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authorial_intent).[^o118] For this reason, his treatments of Greek and Hebrew grammar are not isolated works on their topic[^o118] but contrastive grammars treating the aspects which influenced Latin or which were required for properly understanding Latin texts.[^o119] He pointedly states, "I want to describe Greek grammar for the benefit of Latin speakers".[^n53] It is likely only this limited sense which was intended by Bacon's boast that he [could teach an interested pupil a new language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_education) within three days.[^o119]

Passages in the *Overview* and the Greek grammar have been taken as an early exposition of a [universal grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_grammar) underlying all [human](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human) [languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language).[^o120] The Greek grammar contains the tersest and most famous exposition:[^o120] Grammar is one and the same in all languages, substantially, though it may vary, accidentally, in each of them. However, Bacon's lack of interest in studying a literal [grammar](wikipedia://en/12569) underlying the languages known to him and his numerous works on linguistics and comparative linguistics has prompted [Hovdhaugen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eva_Hovdhaugen) to question the usual literal translation of Bacon's *grammatica* in such passages.[^o121] She notes the ambiguity in the Latin term, which could refer variously to the structure of language, to its description, and to the science underlying such descriptions: i.e., [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics).[^o121]

### Other works {#Other_works}

*Image: Roger Bacon Wellcome M0004130.jpg.* A portrait of Roger Bacon from a 15th-century edition of *De Retardatione*[^o123]

*Image: Roger Bacon page from book.jpg.* The first page of the letter from Bacon to [Clement IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_IV) introducing his *Opus Tertium*[^n54]

Bacon states that his *Lesser Work* (*Opus Minus*) and *Third Work* (*Opus Tertium*) were originally intended as summaries of the *Opus Majus* in case it was lost in transit.[^n29] [Easton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewart_C._Easton)'s review of the texts suggests that they became separate works over the course of the laborious process of creating a [fair copy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foul_papers) of the *Opus Majus*, whose half-million words were copied by hand and apparently greatly revised at least once.[^n22]

Other works by Bacon include his "Tract on the Multiplication of Species" (*Tractatus de Multiplicatione Specierum*),[^n55] "On Burning Lenses" (*De Speculis Comburentibus*), the *Communia Naturalium* and *Mathematica*, the "Compendium of the Study of Philosophy" and "of Theology" (*Compendium Studii Philosophiae* and *Theologiae*), and his *Computus*.[^n12] The "Compendium of the Study of Theology", presumably written in the last years of his life, was an anticlimax: adding nothing new, it is principally devoted to the concerns of the 1260s.

### Apocrypha {#Apocrypha}

*[The Mirror of Alchimy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mirror_of_Alchimy)* (*Speculum Alchemiae*), a short treatise on the origin and composition of metals, is traditionally credited to Bacon.[^o131] It espouses the Arabian theory of [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28element%29) and [sulphur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur) forming the other metals, with vague allusions to [transmutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy). [Stillman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maxson_Stillman) opined that "there is nothing in it that is characteristic of Roger Bacon's style or ideas, nor that distinguishes it from many unimportant alchemical lucubrations of anonymous writers of the thirteenth to the sixteenth centuries", and [Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._M._Pattison_Muir) and [Lippmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Oscar_von_Lippmann) also considered it a [pseudepigraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudepigrapha).[^n56]

The cryptic [Voynich manuscript](wikipedia://en/45845) has been attributed to Bacon by various sources, including by its first recorded owner,[^n57][^n58][^o135] but [historians of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science) [Lynn Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Thorndike) and [George Sarton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Sarton) dismissed these claims as unsupported,[^o136][^o137][^o138] and the [vellum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vellum) of the manuscript has since been dated to the 15th century.[^o139]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

*Image: Friar Bacon's Brazen Head.png.* A woodcut from [Robert Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Greene_%28dramatist%29)'s [play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friar_Bacon_and_Friar_Bungay) displaying the [brazen head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazen_head) pronouncing "Time is. Time was. Time is past."

*Image: Roger Bacons Study in Oxford.jpg.* "Friar Bacon's Study" in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford). By the late 18th century this study on [Folly Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folly_Bridge) had become a place of pilgrimage for scientists, but the building was pulled down in 1779 to allow for road widening.[^n59]

*Image: Roger Bacon Plaque.jpg.* The [Westgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westgate_Oxford) plaque at Oxford

Bacon was largely ignored by his contemporaries in favour of other scholars such as [Albertus Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus), [Bonaventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonaventure), and [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas),[^n9] although his works were studied by Bonaventure, [John Pecham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pecham), and [Peter of Limoges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_of_Limoges), through whom he may have influenced [Raymond Lull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Lull).[^n13] He was also partially responsible for the addition of [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_optics) (*perspectiva*) to the [medieval university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university) [curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university#curriculum).[^n3]

By the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period), the English considered him the epitome of a wise and subtle possessor of [forbidden knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_esotericism), a [Faust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faust)-like magician who had tricked the [devil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil_%28Christianity%29) and so was able to go to [heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven_%28Christianity%29). Of these legends, one of the most prominent was that he created a [talking brazen head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazen_head) which could answer any question. The story appears in the anonymous 16th-century account of *The Famous Historie of Fryer Bacon*, in which Bacon speaks with a demon but causes the head to speak by "the continuall fume of the six hottest Simples",[^o145] testing his theory that speech is caused by "an effusion of vapors".[^n60]

Around 1589, [Robert Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Greene_%28dramatist%29) adapted the story for the stage as *[The Honorable Historie of Frier Bacon and Frier Bongay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Honorable_Historie_of_Frier_Bacon_and_Frier_Bongay)*,[^n61][^n62][^n63] one of the most successful [Elizabethan comedies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Renaissance_theatre).[^n62] As late as the 1640s, [Thomas Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Browne) was still complaining that "Every ear is filled with the story of Frier Bacon, that made a brazen head to speak these words, *Time is*".[^o151] Greene's Bacon spent seven years creating a brass head that would speak "strange and uncouth aphorisms"[^o152] to enable him to encircle [Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) with a wall of brass that would make it impossible to conquer.

Unlike his source material, Greene does not cause his head to operate by natural forces but by "[nigromantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necromancy) charms" and "the enchanting forces of the [devil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil_%28Christianity%29)":[^o153] i.e., by entrapping a [dead spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost)[^n60] or [hobgoblin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobgoblin).[^o155] Bacon collapses, exhausted, just before his device comes to life and announces "Time is", "Time was", and "Time is Past"[^o156] before being destroyed in spectacular fashion: the [stage direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_direction) instructs that "*a lightening flasheth forth, and a hand appears that breaketh down the Head with a hammer*".[^o157]

A [necromantic head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazen_head) was ascribed to [Pope Sylvester&nbsp;II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Sylvester&nbsp;II) as early as the 1120s,[^o158] but [Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Browne) considered the legend to be a misunderstanding of a passage in [Peter the Good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrus_Bonus)'s c. 1335 *[Precious Pearl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrus_Bonus)* where the negligent alchemist misses the birth of his creation and loses it forever.[^o151] The story may also preserve the work by Bacon and his contemporaries to construct clockwork [armillary spheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere).[^n64] Bacon had praised a "self-activated working model of the heavens" as "the greatest of all things which have been devised".[^o160]

As early as the 16th century, [natural philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy) such as [Bruno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Bruno), [Dee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dee)[^n65] and [Francis Bacon](wikipedia://en/11319)[^n3] were attempting to rehabilitate Bacon's reputation and to portray him as a scientific pioneer who had avoided the petty bickering of his contemporaries to attempt a rational understanding of nature. By the 19th century, commenters following [Whewell](wikipedia://en/205488)[^n66][^n3] considered that "Bacon ... was not appreciated in his age because he was so completely in advance of it; he is a 16th- or 17th-century philosopher, whose lot has been by some accident cast in the 13th century".[^n9] His assertions in the *Opus Majus* that "theories supplied by reason should be verified by sensory data, aided by instruments, and corroborated by trustworthy witnesses"[^o166] were (and still are) considered "one of the first important formulations of the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) on record".[^n52]

This idea that Bacon was a modern experimental scientist reflected two views of the period: that the principal form of scientific activity is experimentation and that 13th-century Europe still represented the "[Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Ages_%28historiography%29)".[^n67] This view, which is still reflected in some 21st-century [popular science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_science) books, portrays Bacon as an advocate of modern experimental science who emerged as a solitary genius in an age hostile to his ideas.[^n68] Based on Bacon's apocrypha, he is also portrayed as a visionary who predicted the invention of the [submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine#History), [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_aviation), and [automobile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobile#History).[^n69] Consistent with this view of Bacon as a man ahead of his time, [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells)'s *[Outline of History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Outline_of_History)* attributes this prescient passage to him:

> Machines for navigating are possible without rowers, so that great ships suited to river or ocean, guided by one man, may be borne with greater speed than if they were full of men. Likewise, cars may be made so that without a draught animal they may be moved *cum impetu inaestimabili*, as we deem the scythed chariots to have been from which antiquity fought. And flying machines are possible, so that a man may sit in the middle turning some device by which artificial wings may beat the air in the manner of a flying bird.[^o171]

However, in the course of the 20th century, [Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl), [Heidegger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heidegger) and others emphasised the importance to the modern science of [Cartesian](wikipedia://en/25525) and [Galilean](wikipedia://en/29688374) projections of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) over sensory perceptions of nature; Heidegger, in particular, noted the lack of such an understanding in Bacon's works.[^n3] Although [Crombie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alistair_Cameron_Crombie),[^n70] [Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn)[^n71] and Matthias Schramm[^n72] continued to argue for Bacon's importance to the development of "qualitative" areas of modern science,[^n3] [Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Duhem),[^n73] [Thorndike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Thorndike),[^n74][^n75] [Carton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoul_Carton)[^n76] and [Koyré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Koyré)[^n77] emphasised the essentially medieval nature of Bacon's *scientia experimentalis*.[^n76][^n78]

Research also established that Bacon was probably not as isolated—and not as persecuted—as was once thought. Many medieval sources of and influences on Bacon's scientific activity have been identified.[^n79] In particular, Bacon often mentioned his debt to the work of [Robert Grosseteste](wikipedia://en/94721):[^n80] his work on optics and the calendar followed Grosseteste's lead,[^n81] as did his idea that [inductively-derived conclusions](wikipedia://en/393736) should be submitted for verification through experimental testing.[^n82]

Bacon noted of [William of Sherwood](wikipedia://en/2884630) that "nobody was greater in philosophy than he";[^n83][^n84] praised [Peter of Maricourt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrus_Peregrinus_de_Maricourt) (the author of "A Letter on Magnetism")[^o190] and [John of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_of_London) as "perfect" mathematicians; [Campanus of Novara](wikipedia://en/3926883) (the author of works on astronomy, astrology, and the calendar) and a Master Nicholas as "good";[^n85] and acknowledged the influence of [Adam Marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Marsh) and lesser figures. Some scholars have pushed back on his reputation as an isolated genius.[^n80]

As a result, the picture of Bacon has changed. Bacon is now seen as part of his age: a leading figure in the beginnings of the [medieval universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_university) at [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris#History) and [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) but one joined in the development of the philosophy of science by [Robert Grosseteste](wikipedia://en/94721), [William of Auvergne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Auvergne), [Henry of Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_of_Ghent), [Albert Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus), [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas), [John Duns Scotus](wikipedia://en/34293405), and [William of Ockham](wikipedia://en/33617).[^n86] [Lindberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_C._Lindberg) summarised:

> Bacon was not a modern, out of step with his age, or a harbinger of things to come, but a brilliant, combative, and somewhat eccentric [schoolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism) of the thirteenth century, endeavoring to take advantage of the new learning just becoming available while remaining true to traditional notions ... of the importance to be attached to philosophical knowledge".[^n87]

A recent review of the many visions of Bacon across the ages says contemporary scholarship still neglects one of the most important aspects of his life and thought: his commitment to the Franciscan order.

> His Opus majus was a plea for reform addressed to the supreme [spiritual head of the Christian faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope), written against a background of [apocalyptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apocalypse) expectation and informed by the driving concerns of the [friars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friar). It was designed to improve training for [missionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missionary) and to provide new skills to be employed in the defence of the Christian world against the enmity of non-Christians and of the [Antichrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antichrist). It cannot usefully be read solely in the context of the [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science) and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_philosophy).[^n88]

With regard to religion's influence on Bacon's philosophy, [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) noted, "To Roger Bacon, ... the schoolmen's conception of reasoning appeared only an obstacle to truth ... \[but\] Of all kinds of experience, the best, he thought, was interior illumination, which teaches many things about Nature which the external senses could never discover, such as the [transubstantiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transubstantiation) of bread."[^o196] Later scholars have therefore viewed him as a proto-protestant.[^o197]

In [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) lore, Bacon is credited as the namesake of [Folly Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folly_Bridge) for having been placed under house arrest nearby.[^n89] Although this is probably untrue,[^o199] it had formerly been known as "Friar Bacon's Bridge".[^o200] Bacon is also honoured at Oxford by a plaque affixed to the wall of the [Westgate shopping centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westgate_Oxford).[^n89]
