# Pseudoscience

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudoscience"}

*Image: Phrenological organs, 1887 Wellcome L0001965.jpg.* A typical 19th-century [phrenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrenology) chart: during the 1820s, phrenologists claimed the mind was located in areas of the brain, and were attacked for doubting that mind came from the nonmaterial soul. Their idea of reading "bumps" in the skull to predict personality traits was later discredited.[^o2][^o3] Phrenology was first termed a pseudoscience in 1843 and continues to be considered so.[^o18]

**Pseudoscience** consists of statements, [beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief), or practices that claim to be [scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) but are inherently incompatible with the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method). Pseudoscience is often characterized by contradictory, exaggerated or [unfalsifiable claims](wikipedia://en/11283); reliance on [confirmation bias](wikipedia://en/59160) rather than rigorous attempts at refutation; lack of openness to [evaluation by other experts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review); absence of systematic practices when developing [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis); and continued adherence long after the pseudoscientific hypotheses have been experimentally discredited.[^o4] It is not the same as [junk science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junk_science), which is spurious or [fraudulent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraud) scientific work.[^o5]

:::

The [demarcation between science and pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demarcation_problem) has [scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific), [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical), and [political](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political) implications.[^o151] Philosophers debate the nature of science and the general criteria for drawing the line between [scientific theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_theory) and pseudoscientific beliefs, but according to the *[Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Encyclopedia_of_Philosophy)*, "[creationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creationism), [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), [homeopathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeopathy), [Kirlian photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirlian_photography), [dowsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dowsing), [ufology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ufology), [ancient astronaut theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_astronauts), [Holocaust denialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust_denial), [Velikovskian catastrophism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Velikovsky), and [climate change denialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_denial) are pseudosciences."[^o6] There are implications for [health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care), the use of [expert testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expert_witness), and weighing [environmental policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_policy).[^o6] Recent empirical research has shown that individuals who indulge in pseudoscientific beliefs generally show lower evidential criteria, meaning they often require significantly less evidence before coming to conclusions. This can be coined as a 'jump-to-conclusions' bias that can increase the spread of pseudoscientific beliefs.[^o7] Addressing pseudoscience is part of [science education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_education) and developing scientific literacy.[^o154][^o8]

Pseudoscience can have dangerous effects. For example, pseudoscientific [anti-vaccine activism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-vaccine_activism) and promotion of homeopathic remedies as alternative disease treatments can result in people forgoing important medical treatments with demonstrable health benefits, leading to ill-health and deaths.[^o9][^o10][^o11] Furthermore, people who refuse legitimate medical treatments for contagious diseases may put others at risk. Pseudoscientific theories about [racial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism) and ethnic classifications have led to [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism) and [genocide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genocide).

The term *pseudoscience* is often considered [pejorative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pejorative), because it suggests something is being presented as science inaccurately or even deceptively. Therefore, practitioners and advocates of pseudoscience frequently dispute the characterization.[^o4][^o12]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word *pseudoscience* is derived from the Greek root *pseudo* meaning "false"[^o14][^o15] and the English word *science*, from the Latin word *scientia*, meaning "knowledge". Although the term has been in use since at least the late 18th century (e.g., in 1796 by [James Pettit Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Pettit_Andrews) in reference to [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy)[^o16][^n1]), the concept of pseudoscience as distinct from real or proper science seems to have become more widespread during the mid-19th century. Among the earliest uses of "pseudo-science" was in an 1844 article in the *Northern Journal of Medicine*, issue 387:

An earlier use of the term was in 1843 by the French physiologist [François Magendie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Magendie), that refers to [phrenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrenology) as "*a pseudo-science of the present day*".[^o18][^o19][^o20] During the 20th century, the word was used pejoratively to describe explanations of phenomena which were claimed to be scientific, but which were not in fact supported by reliable experimental evidence.

Dismissing the separate issue of intentional fraud – such as the [Fox sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_sisters)' "rappings" in the 1850s[^n2] – the pejorative label pseudoscience distinguishes the scientific '***us***', at one extreme, from the pseudo-scientific '***them***', at the other, and asserts that '***our***' beliefs, practices, theories, etc., by contrast with that of '***the others***', are scientific. There are four criteria: (a) the '***pseudoscientific'*** group asserts that its beliefs, practices, theories, etc., are '***scientific***'; (b) the '***pseudoscientific***' group claims that its allegedly established facts are justified true beliefs; (c) the '***pseudoscientific***' group asserts that its '***established facts***' have been justified by genuine, rigorous, scientific method; and (d) this assertion is false or deceptive: "it is not simply that subsequent evidence overturns established conclusions, but rather that ***the conclusions were never warranted in the first place***"

From time to time, however, the usage of the word occurred in a more formal, technical manner in response to a perceived threat to individual and institutional security in a social and cultural setting.[^o22]

## Relationship to science {#Relationship_to_science}

Pseudoscience is differentiated from science because, although it usually claims to be science, pseudoscience does not adhere to scientific standards, such as the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method), [falsifiability of claims](wikipedia://en/11283), and [Mertonian norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mertonian_norms).[^o24][^o25]

### Scientific method {#Scientific_method}

*Image: The Scientific Method (simple).png.* The scientific method is a continuous cycle of observation, questioning, hypothesis, experimentation, analysis and conclusion.

A number of basic principles are accepted by scientists as standards for determining whether a body of knowledge, method, or practice is scientific. Experimental results should be [reproducible](wikipedia://en/47651) and [verified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersubjective_verifiability) by other researchers.[^n3] These principles are intended to ensure experiments can be reproduced measurably given the same conditions, allowing further investigation to determine whether a [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) or [theory](wikipedia://en/30746) related to given [phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomena) is [valid](wikipedia://en/239140) and reliable. Standards require the scientific method to be applied throughout, and [bias](wikipedia://en/40786) to be controlled for or eliminated through [randomization](wikipedia://en/6033300), fair sampling procedures, [blinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_blind) of studies, and other methods. All gathered data, including the experimental or environmental conditions, are expected to be documented for scrutiny and made available for [peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review), allowing further experiments or studies to be conducted to confirm or falsify results. Statistical quantification of [significance](wikipedia://en/160995), [confidence](wikipedia://en/280911), and [error](wikipedia://en/277379)[^o28] are also important tools for the scientific method.

### Falsifiability {#Falsifiability}

During the mid-20th century, the philosopher [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) emphasized the criterion of [falsifiability](wikipedia://en/11283) to distinguish [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) from [non-science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-science).[^o30] [Statements](wikipedia://en/81094), [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis), or [theories](wikipedia://en/30746) have falsifiability or refutability if there is the inherent possibility that they can be proven [false](wikipedia://en/229823), that is, if it is possible to conceive of an observation or an argument that negates them. Popper used [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) and [psychoanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalysis) as examples of pseudoscience and Einstein's [theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/30001) as an example of science. He subdivided non-science into philosophical, mathematical, mythological, religious and metaphysical formulations on one hand, and pseudoscientific formulations on the other.[^n4]

Another example which shows the distinct need for a claim to be falsifiable was stated in [Carl Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan)'s book *[The Demon-Haunted World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Demon-Haunted_World)* when he discusses an invisible [dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon) that he has in his garage. The point is made that there is no physical test to refute the claim of the presence of this dragon. Whatever test one thinks can be devised, there is a reason why it does not apply to the invisible dragon, so one can never prove that the initial claim is wrong. Sagan concludes; "Now, what's the difference between an invisible, incorporeal, floating dragon who spits heatless fire and no dragon at all?". He states that "your inability to invalidate my hypothesis is not at all the same thing as proving it true",[^n5] once again explaining that even if such a claim were true, it would be outside the realm of [scientific inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science).

### Mertonian norms {#Mertonian_norms}

During 1942, [Robert K. Merton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_K._Merton) identified a set of five "norms" which characterize real science. If any of the norms were violated, Merton considered the enterprise to be non-science. His norms were:

- Originality: The tests and research done must present something new to the scientific community.
- Detachment: The scientists' reasons for practicing this science must be simply for the expansion of their knowledge. The scientists should not have personal reasons to expect certain results.
- Universality: No person should be able to more easily obtain the information of a test than another person. Social class, religion, ethnicity, or any other personal factors should not be factors in someone's ability to receive or perform a type of science.
- Skepticism: Scientific facts must not be based on faith. One should always question every case and argument and constantly check for errors or invalid claims.
- Public accessibility: Any scientific knowledge one obtains should be made available to everyone. The results of any research should be published and shared with the scientific community.[^o34]

### Refusal to acknowledge problems {#Refusal_to_acknowledge_problems}

In 1978, [Paul Thagard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Thagard) proposed that pseudoscience is primarily distinguishable from science when it is less progressive than alternative theories over a long period of time, and its proponents fail to acknowledge or address problems with the theory.[^n6] In 1983, [Mario Bunge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Bunge) suggested the categories of "belief fields" and "research fields" to help distinguish between pseudoscience and science, where the former is primarily personal and subjective and the latter involves a certain systematic method.[^n7] The 2018 book about [scientific skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_skepticism) by [Steven Novella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Novella), et al. *[The Skeptics' Guide to the Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Skeptics'_Guide_to_the_Universe_%28book%29)* lists hostility to criticism as one of the major features of pseudoscience.[^o38]

### Criticism of the term {#Criticism_of_the_term}

[Larry Laudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Laudan) has suggested that the word "pseudoscience" has no scientific meaning and is mostly used to describe human emotions: "If we would stand up and be counted on the side of reason, we ought to drop terms like 'pseudo-science' and 'unscientific' from our vocabulary; they are just hollow phrases which do only emotive work for us".[^o40] Likewise, [Richard McNally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_McNally) states, "The term 'pseudoscience' has become little more than an inflammatory buzzword for quickly dismissing one's opponents in media sound-bites" and "When therapeutic entrepreneurs make claims on behalf of their interventions, we should not waste our time trying to determine whether their interventions qualify as pseudoscientific. Rather, we should ask them: How do you know that your intervention works? What is your evidence?"[^o41]

### Alternative definition {#Alternative_definition}

For philosophers [Silvio Funtowicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silvio_Funtowicz) and [Jerome R. Ravetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_R._Ravetz) "pseudo-science may be defined as one where the uncertainty of its inputs must be suppressed, lest they render its outputs totally indeterminate". The definition, in the book *[Uncertainty and Quality in Science for Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_and_Quality_in_Science_for_Policy)*,[^o44] alludes to the loss of craft skills in handling quantitative information, and to the bad practice of achieving precision in prediction (inference) only at the expenses of ignoring uncertainty in the input which was used to formulate the prediction. This use of the term is common among practitioners of [post-normal science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-normal_science). Understood in this way, pseudoscience can be fought using good practices to assess uncertainty in quantitative information, such as [NUSAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NUSAP) and – in the case of mathematical modelling – [sensitivity auditing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_auditing).

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Venice ast sm.jpg.* The astrological signs of the zodiac

The history of pseudoscience is the study of pseudoscientific theories over time. A pseudoscience is a set of ideas that presents itself as science, while it does not meet the criteria to be properly called such.[^o46][^o47]

Distinguishing between proper science and pseudoscience is sometimes difficult.[^o48] One proposal for demarcation between the two is the falsification criterion, attributed most notably to the philosopher [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper).[^o49] In the [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science) and the [history of pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pseudoscience) it can be especially difficult to separate the two, because some sciences developed from pseudosciences. An example of this transformation is the science of [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), which traces its origins to the pseudoscientific or [pre-scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescientific) study of [alchemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy).

The vast diversity in pseudosciences further complicates the history of science. Some modern pseudosciences, such as [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) and [acupuncture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acupuncture), originated before the scientific era. Others developed as part of an ideology, such as [Lysenkoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysenkoism), or as a response to perceived threats to an ideology. Examples of this ideological process are [creation science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_science) and [intelligent design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_design), which were developed in response to the scientific theory of [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution).[^o50]

## Indicators of possible pseudoscience {#Indicators_of_possible_pseudoscience}

*Image: Rhustox.jpg.* Homeopathic preparation *Rhus toxicodendron*, derived from [poison ivy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicodendron_radicans)

A topic, practice, or body of knowledge might reasonably be termed pseudoscientific when it is presented as consistent with the norms of scientific research, but it demonstrably fails to meet these norms.[^o52][^n8]

### Use of vague, exaggerated or untestable claims {#Use_of_vague,_exaggerated_or_untestable_claims}

- Assertion of scientific claims that are vague rather than precise, and that lack specific measurements.[^n9]
- Assertion of a claim with little or no explanatory power.[^o140]
- Failure to make use of [operational definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operational_definition) (i.e., publicly accessible definitions of the variables, terms, or objects of interest so that persons other than the definer can measure or test them independently) (See also: [Reproducibility](wikipedia://en/47651)).
- Failure to make reasonable use of the principle of [parsimony](wikipedia://en/36797), i.e., failing to seek an explanation that requires the fewest possible additional assumptions when multiple viable explanations are possible (*See: [Occam's razor](wikipedia://en/36797)*).[^n10]
- Lack of boundary conditions: Most well-supported scientific theories possess well-articulated limitations under which the predicted phenomena do and do not apply.[^o79]
- Lack of effective [controls](wikipedia://en/1640288) in experimental design, such as the use of [placebos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placebos) and [double-blinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-blinding).
- Lack of understanding of basic and established principles of physics and engineering.[^o57]

### Improper collection of evidence {#Improper_collection_of_evidence}

- Assertions that do not allow the logical possibility that they can be shown to be false by observation or physical experiment (See also: [Falsifiability](wikipedia://en/11283)).[^o30][^o59]
- Assertion of claims that a theory predicts something that it has not been shown to predict.[^n11][^n9] Scientific claims that do not confer any predictive power are considered at best "conjectures", or at worst "pseudoscience" (e.g., *[ignoratio elenchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ignoratio_elenchi)*).[^o62]
- Assertion that claims which have not been proven false must therefore be true, and vice versa (*See: [Argument from ignorance](wikipedia://en/17699)*).[^o63]
- Over-reliance on testimonial, [anecdotal evidence](wikipedia://en/481925), or personal experience: This evidence may be useful for the context of discovery (i.e., hypothesis generation), but should not be used in the context of [justification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_justification) (e.g., [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing)).[^n12]
- Use of [myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth) and [religious texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_text) as if they were fact, or basing evidence on readings of such texts.[^o65]
- Use of concepts and scenarios from [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) as if they were fact. This technique appeals to the familiarity that many people already have with science fiction tropes through the popular media.[^o66]
- Presentation of data that seems to support claims while suppressing or refusing to consider data that conflict with those claims.[^n13] This is an example of [selection bias](wikipedia://en/394392) or [cherry picking](wikipedia://en/329277), a distortion of evidence or data that arises from the way that the data are collected. It is sometimes referred to as the selection effect.
- Repeating excessive or untested claims that have been previously published elsewhere, and promoting those claims as if they were facts; an accumulation of such uncritical secondary reports, which do not otherwise contribute their own empirical investigation, is called the [Woozle effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woozle_effect).[^o68]
- [Reversed burden of proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_of_absence): science places the burden of proof on those making a claim, not on the critic. "Pseudoscientific" arguments may neglect this principle and demand that [skeptics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeptic) demonstrate beyond a reasonable doubt that a claim (e.g., an assertion regarding the efficacy of a novel therapeutic technique) is false. It is essentially impossible to prove a universal negative, so this tactic incorrectly places the burden of proof on the skeptic rather than on the claimant.[^o69]
- Appeals to [holism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holism) as opposed to [reductionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductionism) to dismiss negative findings: proponents of pseudoscientific claims, especially in organic medicine, alternative medicine, naturopathy and mental health, often resort to the "mantra of holism" .[^n14]

### Lack of openness to testing by other experts {#Lack_of_openness_to_testing_by_other_experts}

- Evasion of peer review before publicizing results (termed "[science by press conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_by_press_conference)"):[^o69][^o72] Some proponents of ideas that contradict accepted scientific theories avoid subjecting their ideas to [peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review), sometimes on the grounds that peer review is biased towards established paradigms, and sometimes on the grounds that assertions cannot be evaluated adequately using standard scientific methods. By remaining insulated from the peer review process, these proponents forgo the opportunity of corrective feedback from informed colleagues.[^n14]
- Some agencies, institutions, and publications that fund scientific research require authors to [share data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_sharing_%28Science%29) so others can evaluate a paper independently. Failure to provide adequate information for other researchers to reproduce the claims contributes to a lack of openness.[^n15]
- Appealing to the need for secrecy or proprietary knowledge when an [independent review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_review) of data or methodology is requested.[^n15]
- Substantive debate on the evidence by knowledgeable proponents of all viewpoints is not encouraged.[^n16]

### Absence of progress {#Absence_of_progress}

- Failure to progress towards additional evidence of its claims.[^o59] [Terence Hines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence_Hines) has identified astrology as a subject that has changed very little in the past two millennia.[^n6][^o79]
- Lack of self-correction: scientific research programmes make mistakes, but they tend to reduce these errors over time.[^n17] By contrast, ideas may be regarded as pseudoscientific because they have remained unaltered despite contradictory evidence. The work *Scientists Confront Velikovsky* (1976) Cornell University, also delves into these features in some detail, as does the work of [Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn), e.g., *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) which also discusses some of the items on the list of characteristics of pseudoscience.
- Statistical significance of supporting experimental results does not improve over time and are usually close to the cutoff for statistical significance. Normally, experimental techniques improve or the experiments are repeated, and this gives ever stronger evidence. If statistical significance does not improve, this typically shows the experiments have just been repeated until a success occurs due to chance variations.

### Personalization of issues {#Personalization_of_issues}

- Tight social groups and [authoritarian personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authoritarian_personality), [suppression of dissent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suppression_of_dissent) and [groupthink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupthink) can enhance the adoption of beliefs that have no rational basis. In attempting to confirm their beliefs, the group tends to identify their critics as enemies.[^n18]
- Assertion of a conspiracy on the part of the mainstream scientific community, government, or educational facilities to suppress pseudoscientific information. People who make these accusations often compare themselves to [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374) and his persecution by the Roman Catholic Church; this comparison is commonly known as the [Galileo gambit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_gambit).[^o83]
- Attacking the motives, character, morality, or competence of critics, rather than their arguments (see *[ad hominem](wikipedia://en/2226)*)[^n18][^o85]

### Use of misleading language {#Use_of_misleading_language}

- Creating scientific-sounding terms to persuade non-experts to believe statements that may be false or meaningless: for example, a long-standing hoax refers to water by the rarely used formal name "[dihydrogen monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihydrogen_monoxide_parody)" and describes it as the main constituent in most [poisonous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poison) solutions to show how easily the general public can be misled.[^o87] This is closely related to the concept of [technobabble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technobabble) in science fiction.[^o88]
- Using established terms in idiosyncratic ways, thereby demonstrating unfamiliarity with mainstream work in the discipline.

## Prevalence of pseudoscientific beliefs {#Prevalence_of_pseudoscientific_beliefs}

### Countries {#Countries}

The [Ministry of AYUSH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_AYUSH) in the Government of India is purposed with developing education, research and propagation of indigenous alternative medicine systems in India. The ministry has faced significant criticism for funding systems that lack [biological plausibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_plausibility) and are either untested or conclusively proven as ineffective. Quality of research has been poor, and drugs have been launched without any rigorous pharmacological studies and meaningful [clinical trials on Ayurveda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_trials_on_Ayurveda) or other alternative healthcare systems.[^o91][^o92] There is no credible efficacy or scientific basis of any of these forms of treatment.[^o93]

In his book *The Demon-Haunted World*, Carl Sagan discusses the [government of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China) and the [Chinese Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Party)'s concern about Western pseudoscience developments and certain ancient Chinese practices in China. He sees pseudoscience occurring in the United States as part of a worldwide trend and suggests its causes, dangers, diagnosis and treatment may be universal.[^n19]

A large percentage of the United States population lacks scientific literacy, not adequately understanding scientific principles and [method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodology).[^o95] In the *Journal of College Science Teaching*, Art Hobson writes, "Pseudoscientific beliefs are surprisingly widespread in our culture even among public school science teachers and newspaper editors, and are closely related to scientific illiteracy."[^o96] However, a 10,000-student study in the same journal concluded there was no strong correlation between science knowledge and belief in pseudoscience.[^o97]

During 2006, the U.S. [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation) (NSF) issued an executive summary of a paper on science and engineering which briefly discussed the prevalence of pseudoscience in modern times. It said, "belief in pseudoscience is widespread" and, referencing a [Gallup Poll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallup_Poll),[^o98][^o99] stated that belief in the 10 commonly believed examples of paranormal phenomena listed in the poll were "pseudoscientific beliefs".[^o100] The items were "extrasensory perception (ESP), that [houses can be haunted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haunted_house), [ghosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost), [telepathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telepathy), [clairvoyance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clairvoyance), astrology, that people can [mentally communicate with the dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediumship), [witches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft), [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation), and [channelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channelling_%28mediumistic%29)".[^o100] Such beliefs in pseudoscience represent a lack of knowledge of how science works. The [scientific community](wikipedia://en/338705) may attempt to communicate information about science out of concern for the public's susceptibility to unproven claims.[^o100] The NSF stated that pseudoscientific beliefs in the U.S. became more widespread during the 1990s, peaked about 2001, and then decreased slightly since with pseudoscientific beliefs remaining common. According to the NSF report, there is a lack of knowledge of pseudoscientific issues in society and pseudoscientific practices are commonly followed.[^o101] Surveys indicate about a third of adult Americans consider astrology to be scientific.[^o102][^o103][^o104]

In Russia, in the late 20th and early 21st century, significant budgetary funds were spent on programs for the experimental study of "[torsion fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_field_%28pseudoscience%29)",[^o105] the extraction of energy from granite,[^o106] the study of "[cold nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_fusion)", and [astrological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) and [extrasensory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrasensory_perception) "research" by the [Ministry of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_%28Russia%29), the [Ministry of Emergency Situations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Emergency_Situations_%28Russia%29), the [Ministry of Internal Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Internal_Affairs_%28Russia%29), and the [State Duma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_Duma)[^o105] (see Military Unit 10003). In 2006, Deputy Chairman of the [Security Council of the Russian Federation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_Council_of_the_Russian_Federation) Nikolai Spassky published an article in [Rossiyskaya Gazeta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rossiyskaya_Gazeta), where among the priority areas for the development of the [Russian energy sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_in_Russia), the task of extracting [energy from a vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_energy) was in the first place.[^o107] The Clean Water project was adopted as a [United Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Russia) party project; in the version submitted to the government, the program budget for 2010–2017 exceeded \$14 billion.[^o108][^o107]

### Racism {#Racism}

There have been many connections between pseudoscientific writers and researchers and their anti-semitic, racist and [neo-Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Nazi) backgrounds. They often use pseudoscience to reinforce their beliefs. One of the most predominant pseudoscientific writers is [Frank Collin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Collin), a self-proclaimed Nazi who goes by Frank Joseph in his writings.[^o110] The majority of his works include the topics of [Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis), extraterrestrial encounters, and [Lemuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemuria_%28continent%29) as well as other ancient civilizations, often with [white supremacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacist) undertones. For example, he posited that European peoples migrated to North America before [Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus), and that all Native American civilizations were initiated by descendants of [white people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_people).[^o111]

The [alt-right](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alt-Right) using pseudoscience to base their ideologies on is not a new issue. The entire foundation of anti-Semitism is based on pseudoscience, or [scientific racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_racism). In an article from *[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek)* by Sander Gilman, Gilman describes the pseudoscience community's anti-Semitic views. "Jews as they appear in this world of pseudoscience are an invented group of ill, stupid or stupidly smart people who use science to their own nefarious ends. Other groups, too, are painted similarly in 'race science', as it used to call itself: African-Americans, the Irish, the Chinese and, well, any and all groups that you want to prove inferior to yourself".[^o112] Neo-Nazis and white supremacist often try to support their claims with studies that "prove" that their claims are more than just harmful stereotypes. For example [Bret Stephens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bret_Stephens) published a column in *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* where he claimed that [Ashkenazi Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews) had the highest [IQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IQ) among any ethnic group.[^o113] However, the scientific methodology and conclusions reached by the article Stephens cited has been called into question repeatedly since its publication. One article in *Nature*, titled "Racism in Science: The Taint That Lingers" notes that early-twentieth-century [eugenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenic) pseudoscience has been used to influence public policy, such as the [Immigration Act of 1924](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration_Act_of_1924) in the United States, which sought to prevent immigration from Asia and parts of Europe.[^o115]

## Explanations {#Explanations}

In a 1981 report Singer and Benassi wrote that pseudoscientific beliefs have their origin from at least four sources:[^o117]

- Common cognitive errors from [personal experience](wikipedia://en/481925)
- Erroneous sensationalistic mass media coverage
- Sociocultural factors
- Poor or erroneous [science education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_education)

A 1990 study by Eve and Dunn supported the findings of Singer and Benassi and found pseudoscientific belief being promoted by high school life science and biology teachers.[^o118]

### Psychology {#Psychology}

The psychology of pseudoscience attempts to explore and analyze pseudoscientific thinking by means of thorough clarification on making the distinction of what is considered scientific vs. pseudoscientific. The human proclivity for seeking confirmation rather than refutation ([confirmation bias](wikipedia://en/59160)),[^n20] the tendency to hold comforting beliefs, and the tendency to overgeneralize have been proposed as reasons for pseudoscientific thinking. According to Beyerstein, humans are prone to associations based on resemblances only, and often prone to misattribution in cause-effect thinking.[^o121]

[Michael Shermer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Shermer)'s theory of belief-dependent realism is driven by the idea that the brain is essentially a "belief engine" which scans data perceived by the senses and looks for patterns and meaning. There is also the tendency for the brain to create [cognitive biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias), as a result of inferences and assumptions made without logic and based on instinct – usually resulting in patterns in cognition. These tendencies of [patternicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apophenia) and agenticity are also driven "by a meta-bias called the [bias blind spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias_blind_spot), or the tendency to recognize the power of cognitive biases in other people but to be blind to their influence on our own beliefs".[^o122] Lindeman states that social motives (i.e., "to comprehend self and the world, to have a sense of control over outcomes, to belong, to find the world benevolent and to maintain one's self-esteem") are often "more easily" fulfilled by pseudoscience than by scientific information. Furthermore, pseudoscientific explanations are generally not analyzed rationally, but instead experientially. Operating within a different set of rules compared to rational thinking, experiential thinking regards an explanation as valid if the explanation is "personally functional, satisfying and sufficient", offering a description of the world that may be more personal than can be provided by science and reducing the amount of potential work involved in understanding complex events and outcomes.[^o123]

Anyone searching for psychological help that is based in science should seek a licensed therapist whose techniques are not based in pseudoscience. Hupp and Santa Maria provide a complete explanation of what that person should look for.[^o124]

### Education and scientific literacy {#Education_and_scientific_literacy}

There is a trend to believe in pseudoscience more than [scientific evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_evidence).[^o126] Some people believe the prevalence of pseudoscientific beliefs is due to widespread [scientific illiteracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_illiteracy).[^o127] Individuals lacking scientific literacy are more susceptible to wishful thinking, since they are likely to turn to immediate gratification powered by System 1, our default operating system which requires little to no effort. This system encourages one to [accept the conclusions they believe](wikipedia://en/59160), and reject the ones they do not. Further analysis of complex pseudoscientific phenomena require System 2, which follows rules, compares objects along multiple dimensions and weighs options. These two systems have several other differences which are further discussed in the [dual-process theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_process_theory).[^o128] The scientific and secular systems of morality and meaning are generally unsatisfying to most people. Humans are, by nature, a forward-minded species pursuing greater avenues of happiness and satisfaction, but we are all too frequently willing to grasp at unrealistic promises of a better life.[^o129]

Psychology has much to discuss about pseudoscience thinking, as it is the illusory perceptions of causality and effectiveness of numerous individuals that needs to be illuminated. Research suggests that illusionary thinking happens in most people when exposed to certain circumstances such as reading a book, an advertisement or the testimony of others are the basis of pseudoscience beliefs. It is assumed that illusions are not unusual, and given the right conditions, illusions are able to occur systematically even in normal emotional situations. One of the things pseudoscience believers quibble most about is that academic science usually treats them as fools. Minimizing these illusions in the real world is not simple.[^o130] To this aim, designing evidence-based educational programs can be effective to help people identify and reduce their own illusions.[^o130]

## Boundaries with science {#Boundaries_with_science}

### Classification {#Classification}

Philosophers classify types of [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge). In English, the word *[science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science)* is used to indicate specifically the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science) and related fields, which are called the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science).[^o133] Different philosophers of science may disagree on the exact limits – for example, is mathematics a [formal science](wikipedia://en/3694774) that is closer to the empirical ones, or is pure mathematics closer to the philosophical study of [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and therefore not a science?[^o134] – but all agree that all of the ideas that are not scientific are non-scientific. The large category of [non-science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-science) includes all matters outside the natural and social sciences, such as the study of [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion), [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art), and the [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities).[^o133] Dividing the category again, unscientific claims are a subset of the large category of non-scientific claims. This category specifically includes all matters that are directly opposed to good science.[^o133] Un-science includes both "bad science" (such as an error made in a good-faith attempt at learning something about the natural world) and pseudoscience.[^o133] Thus pseudoscience is a subset of un-science, and un-science, in turn, is subset of non-science.

Science is also distinguishable from revelation, theology, or spirituality in that it offers insight into the physical world obtained by empirical research and testing.[^n21][^o136] The most notable disputes concern the [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) of living organisms, the idea of common descent, the geologic history of the Earth, the formation of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), and the origin of the universe.[^o137] Systems of belief that derive from divine or inspired knowledge are not considered pseudoscience if they do not claim either to be scientific or to overturn well-established science. Moreover, some specific religious claims, such as [the power of intercessory prayer to heal the sick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studies_on_intercessory_prayer), although they may be based on untestable beliefs, can be tested by the scientific method.

Some statements and common beliefs of [popular science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_science) may not meet the criteria of science. "Pop" science may blur the divide between science and pseudoscience among the general public, and may also involve [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction).[^o138] Indeed, pop science is disseminated to, and can also easily emanate from, persons not accountable to scientific methodology and expert peer review.

If claims of a given field can be tested experimentally and standards are upheld, it is not pseudoscience, regardless of how odd, astonishing, or counterintuitive those claims are. If claims made are inconsistent with existing experimental results or established theory, but the method is sound, caution should be used, since science consists of testing hypotheses which may turn out to be false. In such a case, the work may be better described as ideas that are "not yet generally accepted". *[Protoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoscience)* is a term sometimes used to describe a hypothesis that has not yet been tested adequately by the scientific method, but which is otherwise consistent with existing science or which, where inconsistent, offers reasonable account of the inconsistency. It may also describe the transition from a body of practical knowledge into a scientific field.[^o30]

### Philosophy {#Philosophy}

Karl Popper stated it is insufficient to distinguish science from pseudoscience, or from [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) (such as the philosophical question of what [existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence) means), by the criterion of rigorous adherence to the [empirical method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism), which is essentially inductive, based on observation or experimentation.[^o140] He proposed a method to distinguish between genuine empirical, nonempirical or even pseudoempirical methods. The latter case was exemplified by astrology, which appeals to observation and experimentation. While it had [empirical evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_research) based on observation, on [horoscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horoscopes) and [biographies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biographies), it crucially failed to use acceptable scientific standards.[^o140] Popper proposed falsifiability as an important criterion in distinguishing science from pseudoscience.

To demonstrate this point, Popper[^o140] gave two cases of human behavior and typical explanations from [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud) and [Alfred Adler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Adler)'s theories: "that of a man who pushes a child into the water with the intention of drowning it; and that of a man who sacrifices his life in an attempt to save the child."[^o140] From Freud's perspective, the first man would have suffered from [psychological repression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_repression), probably originating from an [Oedipus complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus_complex), whereas the second man had attained [sublimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_%28psychology%29). From Adler's perspective, the first and second man suffered from feelings of [inferiority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferiority) and had to prove himself, which drove him to commit the crime or, in the second case, drove him to rescue the child. Popper was not able to find any counterexamples of human behavior in which the behavior could not be explained in the terms of Adler's or Freud's theory. Popper argued[^o140] it was that the observation always fitted or confirmed the theory which, rather than being its strength, was actually its weakness. In contrast, Popper[^o140] gave the example of Einstein's [gravitational theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_theory), which predicted "light must be attracted by heavy bodies (such as the Sun), precisely as material bodies were attracted."[^o140] Following from this, stars closer to the Sun would appear to have moved a small distance away from the Sun, and away from each other. This prediction was particularly striking to Popper because it involved considerable risk. The brightness of the Sun prevented this effect from being observed under normal circumstances, so photographs had to be taken during an eclipse and compared to photographs taken at night. Popper states, "If observation shows that the predicted effect is definitely absent, then the theory is simply refuted."[^o140] Popper summed up his criterion for the scientific status of a theory as depending on its falsifiability, refutability, or [testability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testability).

[Paul R. Thagard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_R._Thagard) used astrology as a case study to distinguish science from pseudoscience and proposed principles and criteria to delineate them.[^n22] First, astrology has not progressed in that it has not been updated nor added any explanatory power since [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979). Second, it has ignored outstanding problems such as the [precession of equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_precession_%28astronomy%29) in astronomy. Third, alternative theories of [personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_psychology) and behavior have grown progressively to encompass explanations of phenomena which astrology statically attributes to heavenly forces. Fourth, astrologers have remained uninterested in furthering the theory to deal with outstanding problems or in critically evaluating the theory in relation to other theories. Thagard intended this criterion to be extended to areas other than astrology. He believed it would delineate as pseudoscientific such practices as [witchcraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witchcraft) and [pyramidology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramidology), while leaving [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), [geoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoscience), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), and [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology) in the realm of science.[^n22]

In the [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science) and history of science, [Imre Lakatos](wikipedia://en/38455) stresses the social and political importance of the demarcation problem, the normative methodological problem of distinguishing between science and pseudoscience. His distinctive historical analysis of scientific methodology based on research programmes suggests: "scientists regard the successful theoretical prediction of stunning novel facts – such as the return of Halley's comet or the gravitational bending of light rays – as what demarcates good scientific theories from pseudo-scientific and degenerate theories, and in spite of all scientific theories being forever confronted by 'an ocean of counterexamples'".[^o151] Lakatos offers a "novel [fallibilist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallibilist) analysis of the development of Newton's celestial dynamics, \[his\] favourite historical example of his methodology" and argues in light of this historical turn, that his account answers for certain inadequacies in those of [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) and Thomas Kuhn.[^o151] "Nonetheless, Lakatos did recognize the force of Kuhn's historical criticism of Popper – all important theories have been surrounded by an 'ocean of anomalies', which on a falsificationist view would require the rejection of the theory outright...Lakatos sought to reconcile the [rationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism) of Popperian falsificationism with what seemed to be its own refutation by history".[^o143]

Many philosophers have tried to solve the problem of demarcation in the following terms: a statement constitutes knowledge if sufficiently many people believe it sufficiently strongly. But the history of thought shows us that many people were totally committed to absurd beliefs. If the strengths of beliefs were a hallmark of knowledge, we should have to rank some tales about demons, angels, devils, and of heaven and hell as knowledge. Scientists, on the other hand, are very sceptical even of their best theories. Newton's is the most powerful theory science has yet produced, but Newton himself never believed that bodies attract each other at a distance. So no degree of commitment to beliefs makes them knowledge. Indeed, the hallmark of scientific behaviour is a certain scepticism even towards one's most cherished theories. Blind commitment to a theory is not an intellectual virtue: it is an intellectual crime. Thus a statement may be pseudoscientific even if it is eminently 'plausible' and everybody believes in it, and it may be scientifically valuable even if it is unbelievable and nobody believes in it. A theory may even be of supreme scientific value even if no one understands it, let alone believes in it.[^o151]

The boundary between science and pseudoscience is disputed and difficult to determine analytically, even after more than a century of study by philosophers of science and [scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientist), and despite some basic agreements on the fundamentals of the scientific method.[^o52][^n23][^o145] The concept of pseudoscience rests on an understanding that the scientific method has been misrepresented or misapplied with respect to a given theory, but many philosophers of science maintain that different kinds of methods are held as appropriate across different fields and different eras of human history. According to Lakatos, the typical descriptive unit of great scientific achievements is not an isolated hypothesis but "a powerful problem-solving machinery, which, with the help of sophisticated mathematical techniques, digests anomalies and even turns them into positive evidence".[^o151]

To Popper, pseudoscience uses induction to generate theories, and only performs experiments to seek to verify them. To Popper, falsifiability is what determines the scientific status of a theory. Taking a historical approach, Kuhn observed that scientists did not follow Popper's rule, and might ignore falsifying data, unless overwhelming. To Kuhn, puzzle-solving within a paradigm is science. Lakatos attempted to resolve this debate, by suggesting history shows that science occurs in research programmes, competing according to how progressive they are. The leading idea of a programme could evolve, driven by its heuristic to make predictions that can be supported by evidence. Feyerabend claimed that Lakatos was selective in his examples, and the whole history of science shows there is no universal rule of scientific method, and imposing one on the scientific community impedes progress.[^o146] Laudan maintained that the demarcation between science and non-science was a pseudo-problem, preferring to focus on the more general distinction between reliable and unreliable knowledge.[^o147]

\[Feyerabend\] regards Lakatos's view as being closet anarchism disguised as methodological rationalism. Feyerabend's claim was not that standard methodological rules should never be obeyed, but rather that sometimes progress is made by abandoning them. In the absence of a generally accepted rule, there is a need for alternative methods of persuasion. According to Feyerabend, Galileo employed stylistic and rhetorical techniques to convince his reader, while he also wrote in Italian rather than Latin and directed his arguments to those already temperamentally inclined to accept them.[^o143]

## Politics, health, and education {#Politics,_health,_and_education}

### Political implications {#Political_implications}

The demarcation problem between science and pseudoscience brings up debate in the realms of science, [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) and [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics). [Imre Lakatos](wikipedia://en/38455), for instance, points out that the [Communist Party of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union) at one point declared that [Mendelian genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelian_genetics) was pseudoscientific and had its advocates, including well-established scientists such as [Nikolai Vavilov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Vavilov), sent to a [Gulag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulag) and that the "liberal Establishment of the West" denies freedom of speech to topics it regards as pseudoscience, particularly where they run up against social mores.[^o151]

Something becomes pseudoscientific when science cannot be separated from [ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology), scientists misrepresent scientific findings to promote or draw attention for publicity, when politicians, journalists and a nation's intellectual elite [distort the facts of science for short-term political gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politicization_of_science), or when powerful individuals of the public conflate causation and cofactors by clever wordplay. These ideas reduce the authority, value, integrity and independence of science in [society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society).[^o152]

### Health and education implications {#Health_and_education_implications}

Distinguishing science from pseudoscience has practical implications in the case of [health care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_care), expert testimony, [environmental policies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_policy), and [science education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_education). Treatments with a patina of scientific authority which have not actually been subjected to actual scientific testing may be ineffective, expensive and dangerous to patients and confuse health providers, insurers, government decision makers and the public as to what treatments are appropriate. Claims advanced by pseudoscience may result in government officials and educators making bad decisions in selecting curricula.

The extent to which students acquire a range of social and [cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive) thinking skills related to the proper usage of science and technology determines whether they are scientifically literate. Education in the sciences encounters new dimensions with the changing landscape of [science and technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science,_technology_and_society), a fast-changing culture and a knowledge-driven era. A reinvention of the school science curriculum is one that shapes students to contend with its changing influence on human welfare. Scientific literacy, which allows a person to distinguish science from pseudosciences such as astrology, is among the attributes that enable students to adapt to the changing world. Its characteristics are embedded in a curriculum where students are engaged in resolving problems, conducting investigations, or developing projects.[^o154]

[Alan J. Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_J._Friedman) mentions why most scientists avoid educating about pseudoscience, including that paying undue attention to pseudoscience could dignify it.[^o155]

On the other hand, [Robert L. Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_L._Park) emphasizes how pseudoscience can be a threat to society and considers that scientists have a responsibility to teach how to distinguish science from pseudoscience.[^o156]

Pseudosciences such as homeopathy, even if generally benign, are used by [charlatans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlatan). This poses a serious issue because it enables incompetent practitioners to administer health care. True-believing zealots may pose a more serious threat than typical con men because of their delusion to homeopathy's ideology. Irrational health care is not harmless and it is careless to create patient confidence in pseudomedicine.[^o157]

On 8 December 2016, journalist Michael V. LeVine pointed out the dangers posed by the *[Natural News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_News)* website: "Snake-oil salesmen have pushed false cures since the dawn of medicine, and now websites like *Natural News* flood social media with dangerous anti-pharmaceutical, anti-vaccination and anti-GMO pseudoscience that puts millions at risk of contracting preventable illnesses."[^o158]

The [anti-vaccine movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-vaccine_movement) has persuaded large numbers of parents not to vaccinate their children, citing pseudoscientific research that links [childhood vaccines with the onset of autism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaccines_and_autism).[^o159] These include the study by [Andrew Wakefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wakefield), which claimed that a combination of [gastrointestinal disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_disease) and [developmental regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_regression), which are often seen in children with [ASD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autism_spectrum), occurred within two weeks of receiving vaccines.[^o160][^o161] The study was eventually retracted by its publisher, and Wakefield was stripped of his license to practice medicine.[^o159]

[Alkaline water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_water) is water that has a pH of higher than 7, purported to host numerous health benefits, with no empirical backing. A practitioner known as [Robert O. Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_O._Young) who promoted alkaline water and an "[Alkaline diet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline_diet)" was sent to jail for 3 years in 2017 for practicing medicine without a license.[^o162]
