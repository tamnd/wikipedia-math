# Quadrilateral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quadrilateral"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), a **quadrilateral** is a four-sided [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621), having four [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) (sides) and four [corners](wikipedia://en/11308417) (vertices). The word is derived from the Latin words *quadri*, a variant of four, and *latus*, meaning "side". It is also called a **tetragon**, derived from Greek "tetra" meaning "four" and "gon" meaning "corner" or "angle", in analogy to other polygons (e.g. [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014)). Since "gon" means "angle", it is analogously called a **quadrangle**, or 4-angle. A quadrilateral with vertices $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ is sometimes denoted as $\square ABCD$.[^o3]

:::

Quadrilaterals are either [simple](wikipedia://en/1059530) (not self-intersecting), or [complex](wikipedia://en/1081538) (self-intersecting, or crossed). Simple quadrilaterals are either [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) or [concave](wikipedia://en/605156).

The [interior angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_and_external_angle) of a simple (and [planar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29)) quadrilateral *ABCD* add up to 360 [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294), that is[^o3]

$$\angle A+\angle B+\angle C+\angle D=360^{\circ}.$$

This is a special case of the *n*-gon interior angle sum formula: *S* = (*n* − 2) × 180° (here, n=4).[^o4]

All non-self-crossing quadrilaterals [tile the plane](wikipedia://en/321671), by repeated rotation around the midpoints of their edges.[^o5]

## Simple quadrilaterals {#Simple_quadrilaterals}

Any quadrilateral that is not self-intersecting is a simple quadrilateral.

### Convex quadrilateral {#Convex_quadrilateral}

*Image: Euler diagram of quadrilateral types.svg.* [Euler diagram](wikipedia://en/2427526) of some types of simple quadrilaterals. (UK) denotes British English and (US) denotes American English.

*Image: Symmetries\_of\_square.svg.* Convex quadrilaterals by symmetry, represented with a [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199)

In a convex quadrilateral all interior angles are less than 180°, and the two diagonals both lie inside the quadrilateral.

- Irregular quadrilateral: no sides are parallel.
- [Trapezium](wikipedia://en/201826) (UK) or [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/201826) (US): at least one pair of opposite sides are [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497). Trapezia (UK) and trapezoids (US) include parallelograms.
- [Isosceles trapezium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosceles_trapezium) (UK) or [isosceles trapezoid](wikipedia://en/511691) (US): one pair of opposite sides are parallel and the base [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) are equal in measure. Alternative definitions are a quadrilateral with an axis of symmetry bisecting one pair of opposite sides, or a trapezoid with diagonals of equal length.
- [Parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496): a quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides. Equivalent conditions are that opposite sides are of equal length; that opposite angles are equal; or that the diagonals bisect each other. Parallelograms include rhombi (including those rectangles called squares) and rhomboids (including those rectangles called oblongs). In other words, parallelograms include all rhombi and all rhomboids, and thus also include all rectangles.
- [Rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884), rhomb:[^o3] all four sides are of equal length (equilateral). An equivalent condition is that the diagonals perpendicularly bisect each other. Informally: "a pushed-over square" (but strictly including a square, too).
- [Rhomboid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhomboid): a parallelogram in which adjacent sides are of unequal lengths, and some angles are [oblique](wikipedia://en/1196#Types_of_angles) (equiv., having no right angles). Informally: "a pushed-over oblong". Not all references agree; some define a rhomboid as a parallelogram that is not a rhombus.[^o8]
- [Rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048): all four angles are right angles (equiangular). An equivalent condition is that the diagonals bisect each other, and are equal in length. Rectangles include squares and oblongs. Informally: "a box or oblong" (including a square).
- [Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) (regular quadrilateral): all four sides are of equal length (equilateral), and all four angles are right angles. An equivalent condition is that opposite sides are parallel (a square is a parallelogram), and that the diagonals perpendicularly bisect each other and are of equal length. A quadrilateral is a square if and only if it is both a rhombus and a rectangle (i.e., four equal sides and four equal angles).
- Oblong: longer than wide, or wider than long (i.e., a rectangle that is not a square).[^o9]
- [Kite](wikipedia://en/84840): two pairs of adjacent sides are of equal length. This implies that one diagonal divides the kite into [congruent triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruent_triangles), and so the angles between the two pairs of equal sides are equal in measure. It also implies that the diagonals are perpendicular. Kites include rhombi. It is a type of tangential quadrilateral.

*Image: Quadrilaterals.svg.*

- [Tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412): the four sides are tangents to an inscribed circle. A convex quadrilateral is tangential if and only if opposite sides have equal sums.
- [Tangential trapezoid](wikipedia://en/34853912): a trapezoid where the four sides are [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482) to an [inscribed circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_circle).
- [Cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131): the four vertices lie on a [circumscribed circle](wikipedia://en/161262). A convex quadrilateral is cyclic if and only if opposite angles sum to 180°.
- [Right kite](wikipedia://en/37263250): a kite with two opposite right angles. It is a cyclic kite, and thus a type of bicentric quadrilateral.
- [Harmonic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/61186948): a cyclic quadrilateral such that the products of the lengths of the opposing sides are equal.
- [Bicentric quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/23335118): it is both tangential and cyclic.
- [Orthodiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/30425383): the diagonals cross at [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956).
- [Equidiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/35012663): the diagonals are of equal length.
- Bisect-diagonal quadrilateral: one diagonal bisects the other into equal lengths. Every dart and kite is bisect-diagonal. When both diagonals bisect another, it's a parallelogram.
- [Ex-tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/32791638): the four extensions of the sides are tangent to an [excircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle).
- An *equilic quadrilateral* has two opposite equal sides that when extended, meet at 60°.
- A *Watt quadrilateral* is a quadrilateral with a pair of opposite sides of equal length.[^o10]
- A *quadric quadrilateral* is a convex quadrilateral whose four vertices all lie on the perimeter of a square.[^o11]
- A *diametric quadrilateral* is a cyclic quadrilateral having one of its sides as a diameter of the circumcircle.[^o12]
- A *Hjelmslev quadrilateral* is a quadrilateral with two right angles at opposite vertices.[^o13]

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Concave quadrilateral"}

### Concave quadrilaterals {#Concave_quadrilaterals}

In a concave quadrilateral, one interior angle is bigger than 180°, and one of the two diagonals lies outside the quadrilateral.

- A *dart* (or arrowhead) is a [concave](wikipedia://en/605156) quadrilateral with bilateral symmetry like a kite, but where one interior angle is reflex. See [Kite](wikipedia://en/84840).

:::

## Complex quadrilaterals {#Complex_quadrilaterals}

*Image: DU21 facets.png.* An antiparallelogram

A [self-intersecting](wikipedia://en/50055439) quadrilateral is called variously a **cross-quadrilateral**, **crossed quadrilateral**, **[butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly) quadrilateral** or **[bow-tie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow-tie) quadrilateral**. In a crossed quadrilateral, the four "interior" angles on either side of the crossing (two [acute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_angle) and two [reflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflex_angle), all on the left or all on the right as the figure is traced out) add up to 720°.[^o18]

- [Crossed trapezoid](wikipedia://en/511691#Self-intersections) (US) or trapezium (Commonwealth):[^o19] a crossed quadrilateral in which one pair of nonadjacent sides is parallel (like a [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/201826)).
- [Antiparallelogram](wikipedia://en/5817627): a crossed quadrilateral in which each pair of nonadjacent sides have equal lengths (like a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496)).
- [Crossed rectangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossed_rectangle): an antiparallelogram whose sides are two opposite sides and the two diagonals of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048), hence having one pair of parallel opposite sides.
- [Crossed square](wikipedia://en/659939#Crossed_square): a special case of a crossed rectangle where two of the sides intersect at right angles.

:::

## Special line segments {#Special_line_segments}

The two [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) of a convex quadrilateral are the [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) that connect opposite vertices.

The two **bimedians** of a convex quadrilateral are the line segments that connect the midpoints of opposite sides.[^o22] They intersect at the "vertex centroid" of the quadrilateral (see below).

The four **maltitudes** of a convex quadrilateral are the perpendiculars to a side—through the midpoint of the opposite side.[^o23]

:::

## Area of a convex quadrilateral {#Area_of_a_convex_quadrilateral}

There are various general formulas for the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) $K$ of a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* with sides $a = AB, b = BC, c = CD\text{ and } d = DA$.

::: {#o26 .statement cue=named-heading name="Trigonometric formulas"}

### Trigonometric formulas {#Trigonometric_formulas}

The area can be expressed in trigonometric terms as[^o27]

$$K = \tfrac12 pq \sin \theta,$$

where the lengths of the diagonals are $p$ and $q$ and the angle between them is $\theta$.[^o28] In the case of an orthodiagonal quadrilateral (e.g. rhombus, square, and kite), this formula reduces to $K=\tfrac{pq}{2}$ since $\theta$ is $90^{\circ}$.

The area can be also expressed in terms of bimedians as[^o31]

$$K = mn \sin \varphi,$$

where the lengths of the bimedians are $m$ and $n$ and the angle between them is $\varphi$.

[Bretschneider's formula](wikipedia://en/5615284)[^o29][^o27] expresses the area in terms of the sides and two opposite angles:

$$\begin{aligned} K &= \sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d) - \tfrac{1}{2} abcd \; [ 1 + \cos (A + C) ]} \\ &= \sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d) - abcd\, \cos^2 \tfrac12(A + C) } \end{aligned}$$

where the sides in sequence are $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, where $s$ is the semiperimeter, and $A$ and $C$ are two (in fact, any two) opposite angles. This reduces to [Brahmagupta's formula](wikipedia://en/194129) for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral—when $A + C = 180^{\circ}$.

Another area formula in terms of the sides and angles, with angle $C$ being between sides $b$ and $c$, and $A$ being between sides $a$ and $d$, is

$$K = \tfrac12 ad \sin{A} + \tfrac12 bc \sin{C}.$$

In the case of a cyclic quadrilateral, the latter formula becomes $K = \tfrac12(ad+bc)\sin{A}.$

In a parallelogram, where both pairs of opposite sides and angles are equal, this formula reduces to $K=ab \cdot \sin{A}.$

Alternatively, we can write the area in terms of the sides and the intersection angle $\theta$ of the diagonals, as long as $\theta$ is not $90^{\circ}$:[^o30]

$$K = \tfrac14 \left|\tan \theta\right| \cdot \left| a^2 + c^2 - b^2 - d^2 \right|.$$

In the case of a parallelogram, the latter formula becomes $K = \tfrac12 \left|\tan \theta\right| \cdot \left| a^2 - b^2 \right|.$

Another area formula including the sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ is[^o31]

$$K=\tfrac12 \sqrt{\bigl((a^2+c^2)-2x^2\bigr)\bigl((b^2+d^2)-2x^2\bigr)} \sin{\varphi}$$

where $x$ is the distance between the midpoints of the diagonals, and $\varphi$ is the angle between the [bimedians](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments).

The last trigonometric area formula including the sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ and the angle $\alpha$ (between $a$ and $b$) is:[^o32]

$$K=\tfrac12 ab \sin{\alpha}+\tfrac14 \sqrt{4c^2d^2-(c^2+d^2-a^2-b^2+2ab \cos{\alpha})^2} ,$$

which can also be used for the area of a concave quadrilateral (having the concave part opposite to angle $\alpha$), by just changing the first sign $+$ to $-$.

:::

::: {#o34 .statement cue=named-heading name="Non-trigonometric formulas"}

### Non-trigonometric formulas {#Non-trigonometric_formulas}

The following two formulas express the area in terms of the sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d$, the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617#Quadrilaterals) $s$, and the diagonals $p$, $q$:

$K = \sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d) - \tfrac{1}{4}(ac+bd+pq)(ac+bd-pq)},$ [^o35]

$K = \tfrac14 \sqrt{4p^2q^2 - \left( a^2 + c^2 - b^2 - d^2 \right)^2}.$ [^o36]

The first reduces to Brahmagupta's formula in the cyclic quadrilateral case, since then $pq = ac + bd$.

The area can also be expressed in terms of the bimedians $m$, $n$ and the diagonals $p$, $q$:

$K=\tfrac12 \sqrt{(m+n+p)(m+n-p)(m+n+q)(m+n-q)},$ [^o37]

$K=\tfrac12 \sqrt{p^2q^2-(m^2-n^2)^2}.$ [^o38]

In fact, any three of the four values $m$, $n$, $p$, and $q$ suffice for determination of the area, since in any quadrilateral the four values are related by $p^2+q^2=2(m^2+n^2).$[^o64] The corresponding expressions are:[^o39]

$$K=\tfrac12 \sqrt{[(m+n)^2-p^2]\cdot[p^2-(m-n)^2]},$$

if the lengths of two bimedians and one diagonal are given, and[^o39]

$$K=\tfrac14 \sqrt{[(p+q)^2-4m^2]\cdot[4m^2-(p-q)^2]},$$

if the lengths of two diagonals and one bimedian are given.

:::

::: {#o41 .statement cue=named-heading name="Vector formulas"}

### Vector formulas {#Vector_formulas}

The area of a quadrilateral $ABCD$ can be calculated using [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29). Let vectors $\mathbf{AC}$ and $\mathbf{BD}$ form the diagonals from $A$ to $C$ and from $B$ to $D$. The area of the quadrilateral is then

$$K = \tfrac12 |\mathbf{AC}\times\mathbf{BD}|,$$

which is half the magnitude of the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) of vectors $\mathbf{AC}$ and $\mathbf{BD}$. In two-dimensional Euclidean space, expressing vector $\mathbf{AC}$ as a [free vector in Cartesian space](wikipedia://en/32533#In_Cartesian_space) equal to $(\boldsymbol{x_{1},y_{1}})$ and $\mathbf{BD}$ as $(\boldsymbol{x_{2},y_{2}})$, this can be rewritten as:

$$K = \tfrac12 |x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1|.$$

:::

### Lengths of the diagonals {#Lengths_of_the_diagonals}

The lengths of the diagonals in a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* can be calculated using the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) on each triangle formed by one diagonal and two sides of the quadrilateral. Thus

$$p=\sqrt{a^2+b^2-2ab\cos{B}}=\sqrt{c^2+d^2-2cd\cos{D}}$$

and

$$q=\sqrt{a^2+d^2-2ad\cos{A}}=\sqrt{b^2+c^2-2bc\cos{C}}.$$

Other, more symmetric formulas for the lengths of the diagonals, are[^o54]

$$p=\sqrt{\frac{(ac+bd)(ad+bc)-2abcd(\cos{B}+\cos{D})}{ab+cd}}$$

and

$$q=\sqrt{\frac{(ab+cd)(ac+bd)-2abcd(\cos{A}+\cos{C})}{ad+bc}}.$$

::: {#o56 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Generalizations of the parallelogram law and Ptolemy's theorem"}

### Generalizations of the parallelogram law and Ptolemy's theorem {#Generalizations_of_the_parallelogram_law_and_Ptolemy's_theorem}

In any convex quadrilateral *ABCD*, the sum of the squares of the four sides is equal to the sum of the squares of the two diagonals plus four times the square of the line segment connecting the midpoints of the diagonals. Thus

$$a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 = p^2 + q^2 + 4x^2$$

where $x$ is the distance between the midpoints of the diagonals.[^o64] This is sometimes known as [Euler's quadrilateral theorem](wikipedia://en/53460920) and is a generalization of the [parallelogram law](wikipedia://en/362953).

The German mathematician [Carl Anton Bretschneider](wikipedia://en/30738616) derived in 1842 the following generalization of [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971), regarding the product of the diagonals in a convex quadrilateral[^o57]

$$p^2q^2=a^2c^2+b^2d^2-2abcd\cos{(A+C)}.$$

This relation can be considered to be a [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) for a quadrilateral. In a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131), where $A + C = 180^{\circ}$, it reduces to $pq = ac + bd$. Since $\cos\,(A + C) \ge -1$, it also gives a proof of Ptolemy's inequality.

:::

### Other metric relations {#Other_metric_relations}

If $X$ and $Y$ are the feet of the normals from $B$ and $D$ to the diagonal $AC = p$ in a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* with sides $a = AB$, $b = BC$, $c = CD$, $d = DA$, then[^o66]

$$XY=\frac{|a^2+c^2-b^2-d^2|}{2p}.$$

In a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* with sides $a = AB$, $b = BC$, $c = CD$, $d = DA$, and where the diagonals intersect at $E$,

$$efgh(a+c+b+d)(a+c-b-d) = (agh+cef+beh+dfg)(agh+cef-beh-dfg)$$

where $e = AE$, $f = BE$, $g = CE$, and $h = DE$.[^o59]

The shape and size of a convex quadrilateral are fully determined by the lengths of its sides in sequence and of one diagonal between two specified vertices. The two diagonals $p, q$ and the four side lengths $a, b, c, d$ of a quadrilateral are related[^o27] by the [Cayley-Menger](wikipedia://en/654387#Cayley.E2.80.93Menger_determinants) [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), as follows:

$$\det \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a^2 & p^2 & d^2 & 1 \\ a^2 & 0 & b^2 & q^2 & 1 \\ p^2 & b^2 & 0 & c^2 & 1 \\ d^2 & q^2 & c^2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0.$$

## Angle bisectors {#Angle_bisectors}

The internal [angle bisectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_bisector) of a convex quadrilateral either form a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131)[^o64] (that is, the four intersection points of adjacent angle bisectors are [concyclic](wikipedia://en/504404)) or they are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157). In the latter case the quadrilateral is a [tangential quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/9176412).

In quadrilateral *ABCD*, if the [angle bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Of_angles) of $A$ and $C$ meet on diagonal $BD$, then the angle bisectors of $B$ and $D$ meet on diagonal $AC$.[^o61]

## Bimedians {#Bimedians}

*Image: Varignon theorem convex.png.* The Varignon parallelogram *EFGH*

The [bimedians](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments) of a quadrilateral are the line segments connecting the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of the opposite sides. The intersection of the bimedians is the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the vertices of the quadrilateral.[^o27]

The midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral (convex, concave or crossed) are the vertices of a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) called the [Varignon parallelogram](wikipedia://en/17006564). It has the following properties:

- Each pair of opposite sides of the Varignon parallelogram are parallel to a diagonal in the original quadrilateral.
- A side of the Varignon parallelogram is half as long as the diagonal in the original quadrilateral it is parallel to.
- The area of the Varignon parallelogram equals half the area of the original quadrilateral. This is true in convex, concave and crossed quadrilaterals provided the area of the latter is defined to be the difference of the areas of the two triangles it is composed of.[^o63]
- The [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) of the Varignon parallelogram equals the sum of the diagonals of the original quadrilateral.
- The diagonals of the Varignon parallelogram are the bimedians of the original quadrilateral.

The two bimedians in a quadrilateral and the line segment joining the midpoints of the diagonals in that quadrilateral are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157) and are all bisected by their point of intersection.[^o64]

In a convex quadrilateral with sides $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d$, the length of the bimedian that connects the midpoints of the sides $a$ and $c$ is

$$m=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{-a^2+b^2-c^2+d^2+p^2+q^2}$$

where $p$ and $q$ are the length of the diagonals.[^o65] The length of the bimedian that connects the midpoints of the sides $b$ and $d$ is

$$n=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{a^2-b^2+c^2-d^2+p^2+q^2}.$$

Hence[^o64]

$$\displaystyle p^2+q^2=2(m^2+n^2).$$

This is also a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) to the [parallelogram law](wikipedia://en/362953) applied in the Varignon parallelogram.

The lengths of the bimedians can also be expressed in terms of two opposite sides and the distance $x$ between the midpoints of the diagonals. This is possible when using Euler's quadrilateral theorem in the above formulas. Whence[^o38]

$$m=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{2(b^2+d^2)-4x^2}$$

and

$$n=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{2(a^2+c^2)-4x^2}.$$

Note that the two opposite sides in these formulas are not the two that the bimedian connects.

In a convex quadrilateral, there is the following [dual](wikipedia://en/609737) connection between the bimedians and the diagonals:[^o66]

- The two bimedians have equal length [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the two diagonals are [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944).
- The two bimedians are perpendicular if and only if the two diagonals have equal length.

## Trigonometric identities {#Trigonometric_identities}

The four angles of a simple quadrilateral *ABCD* satisfy the following identities:[^o68]

$$\sin A + \sin B + \sin C + \sin D = 4\sin\tfrac12(A+B)\, \sin\tfrac12(A+C)\, \sin\tfrac12(A+D)$$

and

$$\frac{\tan A\,\tan{B} - \tan C\,\tan D}{\tan A\,\tan C - \tan B\,\tan D} = \frac{\tan(A+C)}{\tan(A+B)}.$$

Also,[^o69]

$$\frac{\tan A + \tan B + \tan C + \tan D}{\cot A + \cot B + \cot C + \cot D} = \tan{A}\tan{B}\tan{C}\tan{D}.$$

In the last two formulas, no angle is allowed to be a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956), since tan 90° is not defined.

Let $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ be the sides of a convex quadrilateral, $s$ is the semiperimeter, and $A$ and $C$ are opposite angles, then[^o70]

$$ad\sin^2\tfrac12 A + bc\cos^2\tfrac12C = (s-a)(s-d)$$

and

$$bc\sin^2\tfrac12 C + ad\cos^2\tfrac12 A = (s-b)(s-c).$$

We can use these identities to derive the [Bretschneider's Formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretschneider's_Formula).

## Inequalities {#Inequalities}

### Area {#Area}

If a convex quadrilateral has the consecutive sides *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* and the diagonals *p*, *q*, then its area *K* satisfies[^o73]

$K\le \tfrac{1}{4}(a+c)(b+d)$ with equality only for a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048).

$K\le \tfrac{1}{4}(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)$ with equality only for a [square](wikipedia://en/659939).

$K\le \tfrac{1}{4}(p^2+q^2)$ with equality only if the diagonals are perpendicular and equal.

$K\le \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(a^2+c^2)(b^2+d^2)}$ with equality only for a rectangle.[^o31]

From [Bretschneider's formula](wikipedia://en/5615284) it directly follows that the area of a quadrilateral satisfies

$$K \le \sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}$$

with equality [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the quadrilateral is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/194131) or degenerate such that one side is equal to the sum of the other three (it has collapsed into a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860), so the area is zero).

Also,

$$K \leq \sqrt{abcd},$$

with equality for a [bicentric quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/23335118) or a rectangle.

The area of any quadrilateral also satisfies the inequality[^o74]

$$\displaystyle K\le \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt[3]{(ab+cd)(ac+bd)(ad+bc)}.$$

Denoting the perimeter as *L*, we have[^o74]

$$K\le \tfrac{1}{16}L^2,$$

with equality only in the case of a square.

The area of a convex quadrilateral also satisfies

$$K \le \tfrac{1}{2}pq$$

for diagonal lengths *p* and *q*, with equality if and only if the diagonals are perpendicular.

Let *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* be the lengths of the sides of a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* with the area *K* and diagonals *AC = p*, *BD = q*. Then[^o75]

$K \leq \tfrac18(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2+p^2+q^2+pq-ac-bd)$ with equality only for a square.

Let *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* be the lengths of the sides of a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* with the area *K*, then the following inequality holds:[^o76]

$K \leq \frac{1}{3+\sqrt{3}}(ab+ac+ad+bc+bd+cd) - \frac{1}{2(1+\sqrt{3})^2}(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)$ with equality only for a square.

### Diagonals and bimedians {#Diagonals_and_bimedians}

A corollary to Euler's quadrilateral theorem is the inequality

$$a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 \ge p^2 + q^2$$

where equality holds if and only if the quadrilateral is a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496).

[Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) also generalized [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971), which is an equality in a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131), into an inequality for a convex quadrilateral. It states that

$$pq \le ac + bd$$

where there is equality [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the quadrilateral is cyclic.[^o64] This is often called [Ptolemy's inequality](wikipedia://en/7148302).

In any convex quadrilateral the bimedians *m, n* and the diagonals *p, q* are related by the inequality

$$pq \leq m^2+n^2,$$

with equality holding if and only if the diagonals are equal.[^o78] This follows directly from the quadrilateral identity $m^2+n^2=\tfrac{1}{2}(p^2+q^2).$

### Sides {#Sides}

The sides *a*, *b*, *c*, and *d* of any quadrilateral satisfy[^o80]

$$a^2+b^2+c^2 > \tfrac13 d^2$$

and[^o80]

$$a^4+b^4+c^4 \geq \tfrac1{27} d^4.$$

## Maximum and minimum properties {#Maximum_and_minimum_properties}

Among all quadrilaterals with a given [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636), the one with the largest area is the [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29). This is called the *[isoperimetric theorem](wikipedia://en/326182) for quadrilaterals*. It is a direct consequence of the area inequality[^o74]

$$K\le \tfrac{1}{16}L^2$$

where *K* is the area of a convex quadrilateral with perimeter *L*. Equality holds [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the quadrilateral is a square. The dual theorem states that of all quadrilaterals with a given area, the square has the shortest perimeter.

The quadrilateral with given side lengths that has the [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) area is the [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131).[^o94]

Of all convex quadrilaterals with given diagonals, the [orthodiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/30425383) has the largest area.[^o74] This is a direct consequence of the fact that the area of a convex quadrilateral satisfies

$$K=\tfrac{1}{2}pq\sin{\theta}\le \tfrac{1}{2}pq,$$

where *θ* is the angle between the diagonals *p* and *q*. Equality holds if and only if *θ* = 90°.

If *P* is an interior point in a convex quadrilateral *ABCD*, then

$$AP+BP+CP+DP\ge AC+BD.$$

From this inequality it follows that the point inside a quadrilateral that [minimizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) the sum of distances to the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) is the intersection of the diagonals. Hence that point is the [Fermat point](wikipedia://en/2666205) of a convex quadrilateral.[^o82]

## Remarkable points and lines in a convex quadrilateral {#Remarkable_points_and_lines_in_a_convex_quadrilateral}

The centre of a quadrilateral can be defined in several different ways. The "vertex centroid" comes from considering the quadrilateral as being empty but having equal masses at its vertices. The "side centroid" comes from considering the sides to have constant mass per unit length. The usual centre, called just [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) (centre of area) comes from considering the surface of the quadrilateral as having constant density. These three points are in general not all the same point.[^o85]

The "vertex centroid" is the intersection of the two [bimedians](wikipedia://en/25278#Special_line_segments).[^o86] As with any polygon, the *x* and *y* coordinates of the vertex centroid are the [arithmetic means](wikipedia://en/612) of the *x* and *y* coordinates of the vertices.

The "area centroid" of quadrilateral *ABCD* can be constructed in the following way. Let *G<sub>a</sub>*, *G<sub>b</sub>*, *G<sub>c</sub>*, *G<sub>d</sub>* be the centroids of triangles *BCD*, *ACD*, *ABD*, *ABC* respectively. Then the "area centroid" is the intersection of the lines *G<sub>a</sub>G<sub>c</sub>* and *G<sub>b</sub>G<sub>d</sub>*.[^o87]

In a general convex quadrilateral *ABCD*, there are no natural analogies to the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) and [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). But two such points can be constructed in the following way. Let *O<sub>a</sub>*, *O<sub>b</sub>*, *O<sub>c</sub>*, *O<sub>d</sub>* be the circumcenters of triangles *BCD*, *ACD*, *ABD*, *ABC* respectively; and denote by *H<sub>a</sub>*, *H<sub>b</sub>*, *H<sub>c</sub>*, *H<sub>d</sub>* the orthocenters in the same triangles. Then the intersection of the lines *O<sub>a</sub>O<sub>c</sub>* and *O<sub>b</sub>O<sub>d</sub>* is called the [quasicircumcenter](wikipedia://en/46449014), and the intersection of the lines *H<sub>a</sub>H<sub>c</sub>* and *H<sub>b</sub>H<sub>d</sub>* is called the *quasiorthocenter* of the convex quadrilateral.[^o87] These points can be used to define an [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) of a quadrilateral. In a convex quadrilateral, the quasiorthocenter *H*, the "area centroid" *G*, and the quasicircumcenter *O* are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear) in this order, and *HG* = 2*GO*.[^o87]

There can also be defined a *quasinine-point center* *E* as the intersection of the lines *E<sub>a</sub>E<sub>c</sub>* and *E<sub>b</sub>E<sub>d</sub>*, where *E<sub>a</sub>*, *E<sub>b</sub>*, *E<sub>c</sub>*, *E<sub>d</sub>* are the [nine-point centers](wikipedia://en/161243) of triangles *BCD*, *ACD*, *ABD*, *ABC* respectively. Then *E* is the [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979) of *OH*.[^o87]

Another remarkable line in a convex non-parallelogram quadrilateral is the [Newton line](wikipedia://en/35411198), which connects the midpoints of the diagonals, the segment connecting these points being bisected by the vertex centroid. One more interesting line (in some sense dual to the [Newton's](wikipedia://en/35411198) one) is the line connecting the point of intersection of diagonals with the vertex centroid. The line is remarkable by the fact that it contains the (area) centroid. The vertex centroid divides the segment connecting the intersection of diagonals and the (area) centroid in the ratio 3:1.[^o88]

For any quadrilateral *ABCD* with points *P* and *Q* the intersections of *AD* and *BC* and *AB* and *CD*, respectively, the circles *(PAB), (PCD), (QAD),* and *(QBC)* pass through a common point *M*, called a Miquel point.[^o89]

For a convex quadrilateral *ABCD* in which *E* is the point of intersection of the diagonals and *F* is the point of intersection of the extensions of sides *BC* and *AD*, let ω be a circle through *E* and *F* which meets *CB* internally at *M* and *DA* internally at *N*. Let *CA* meet ω again at *L* and let *DB* meet ω again at *K*. Then, applying [Pascal's theorem](wikipedia://en/699966) to the hexagons *EKNFML* and *EKMFNL* inscribed in ω, there holds: the straight lines *NK* and *ML* intersect at point *P* that is located on the side *AB*; the straight lines *NL* and *KM* intersect at point *Q* that is located on the side *CD*. Points *P* and *Q* are called "Pascal points" formed by circle ω on sides *AB* and *CD*. [^o90] [^o91] [^o92]

## Other properties of convex quadrilaterals {#Other_properties_of_convex_quadrilaterals}

- If exterior squares are drawn on all sides of a quadrilateral then the segments connecting the [centers](wikipedia://en/4850775#Symmetric_objects) of opposite squares are (a) equal in length, and (b) [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944). Thus these centers are the vertices of an [orthodiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/30425383). This is called [Van Aubel's theorem](wikipedia://en/3021425).
- For any simple quadrilateral with given edge lengths, there is a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) with the same edge lengths.[^o94]
- The four smaller triangles formed by the diagonals and sides of a convex quadrilateral have the property that the product of the areas of two opposite triangles equals the product of the areas of the other two triangles.[^o95]
- Let vectors $\mathbf{AC}$ and $\mathbf{BD}$ form the diagonals from *A* to *C* and from *B* to *D*. The angle $\theta$ at the intersection of the diagonals satisfies $$\cos \theta = \frac{b^2+d^2-a^2-c^2}{2pq},$$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf{AC}$ and $\mathbf{BD}$, and $p, q$ are the diagonals of the quadrilateral.[^o96]

## Skew quadrilaterals {#Skew_quadrilaterals}

*Image: Disphenoid tetrahedron.png.* The (red) side edges of [tetragonal disphenoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragonal_disphenoid) represent a regular zig-zag skew quadrilateral.

A generalization of ordinary quadrilaterals to non-planar figures is called a **skew quadrilateral**, a four-sided [skew polygon](wikipedia://en/6545461). Formulas to compute its dihedral angles from the edge lengths and the angle between two adjacent edges were derived for work on the properties of molecules such as [cyclobutane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclobutane) that contain a "puckered" ring of four atoms.[^o100] Historically the term **gauche quadrilateral** was also used to mean a skew quadrilateral.[^o101] A skew quadrilateral together with its diagonals form a (possibly non-regular) [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), and conversely every skew quadrilateral comes from a tetrahedron where a pair of opposite [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) is removed.
