# Subdivision surface

Subdiv SubD

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subdivision surface"}

In the field of [3D computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_computer_graphics), a **subdivision surface** (commonly shortened to **SubD surface** or **Subsurf**) is a curved [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_representation_of_surfaces) represented by the specification of a coarser [polygon mesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh) and produced by a [recursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive) algorithmic method. The curved surface, the underlying *inner mesh*,[^o2] can be calculated from the coarse mesh, known as the *control cage* or *outer mesh*, as the functional [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of an iterative process of subdividing each [polygonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygonal) [face](wikipedia://en/149926) into smaller faces that better approximate the final underlying curved surface. Less commonly, a simple algorithm is used to add geometry to a mesh by subdividing the faces into smaller ones without changing the overall shape or volume.

:::

The opposite operation is called *un-subdividing*, a type of mesh decimation that works on subdivided geometry.[^o3]

## Overview {#Overview}

*Image: Cube simple subdivisions (0-3).png.* Simple subdivision on a cube up to level 3

*Image: Tesselation pipeline.svg.* A [tessellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellation_%28computer_graphics%29) pipeline using a subdivision method

A subdivision surface algorithm is [recursive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive) in nature. The process starts with a base level polygonal mesh. A **refinement scheme** is then applied to this mesh. This process takes that mesh and subdivides it, creating new vertices and new faces. The positions of the new vertices in the mesh are computed based on the positions of nearby old vertices, edges, and/or faces. In many refinement schemes, the positions of old vertices are also altered (possibly based on the positions of new vertices).

This process produces a *denser* mesh than the original one, containing more polygonal faces (often by a factor of 4). This resulting mesh can be passed through the same refinement scheme again and again to produce more and more refined meshes. Each iteration is often called a subdivision *level*, starting at zero (before any refinement occurs).

The *limit* subdivision surface is the surface produced from this process being iteratively applied infinitely many times. In practical use however, this algorithm is only applied a limited number of times, typically no more than five.

Mathematically, the [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of an *extraordinary vertex* (non-4-[valent](wikipedia://en/1374448) node for quad refined meshes) of a subdivision surface is a [spline](wikipedia://en/457680) with a parametrically [singular point](wikipedia://en/4230456).[^o5]

## Refinement schemes {#Refinement_schemes}

Subdivision surface refinement schemes can be broadly classified into two categories: *interpolating* and *approximating*.

- Interpolating schemes are required to match the original position of vertices in the original mesh.
- Approximating schemes are not; they can and will adjust these positions as needed.

In general, approximating schemes have greater smoothness, but the user has less overall control of the outcome. This is analogous to [spline](wikipedia://en/457680) surfaces and curves, where [Bézier curves](wikipedia://en/4664) are required to interpolate certain control points, while [B-Splines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-Spline) are not (and are more approximate).

Subdivision surface schemes can also be categorized by the type of polygon that they operate on: some function best for quadrilaterals (quads), while others primarily operate on triangles (tris).

### Approximating schemes {#Approximating_schemes}

*Approximating* means that the limit surfaces approximate the initial meshes, and that after subdivision the newly generated control points are not in the limit surfaces. There are five approximating subdivision schemes:

- [Catmull and Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catmull-Clark_subdivision_surface) (1978), Quads – generalizes [bi-cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-cubic) [uniform](wikipedia://en/457680#Definition) [B-spline](wikipedia://en/21834) knot insertion. For arbitrary initial meshes, this scheme generates limit surfaces that are [C<sup>2</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) continuous everywhere except at extraordinary vertices where they are [C<sup>1</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) continuous (Peters and Reif 1998).[^o9]
- [Doo-Sabin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doo-Sabin_subdivision_surface) (1978), Quads – The second subdivision scheme was developed by Doo and Sabin, who successfully extended Chaikin's corner-cutting method (George Chaikin, 1974[^o10]) for curves to surfaces. They used the analytical expression of [bi-quadratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biquadratic) uniform B-spline surface to generate their subdivision procedure to produce [C<sup>1</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) limit surfaces with arbitrary topology for arbitrary initial meshes. An auxiliary point can improve the shape of Doo-Sabin subdivision.[^o11] After a subdivision, all vertices have *[valence](wikipedia://en/1374448)* 4.[^o12]
- [Loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loop_subdivision_surface) (1987), Triangles – Loop proposed his subdivision scheme based on a quartic [box-spline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box-spline) of six direction vectors to provide a rule to generate [C<sup>2</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) continuous limit surfaces everywhere except at extraordinary vertices where they are [C<sup>1</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) continuous (Zorin 1997).
- [Mid-Edge subdivision scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-Edge_subdivision_scheme) (1997–1999) – The mid-edge subdivision scheme was proposed independently by Peters-Reif (1997)[^o13] and Habib-Warren (1999).[^o14] The former used the mid-point of each edge to build the new mesh. The latter used a four-directional [box spline](wikipedia://en/35874454) to build the scheme. This scheme generates [C<sup>1</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) continuous limit surfaces on initial meshes with arbitrary topology. (Mid-Edge subdivision, which could be called "√2 subdivision" since two steps halve distances, could be considered the slowest.)
- [√3 subdivision scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/√3_subdivision_scheme) (2000), Triangles – This scheme was developed by Kobbelt[^o15] and offers several interesting features: it handles arbitrary triangular meshes, it is [C<sup>2</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) continuous everywhere except at extraordinary vertices where it is [C<sup>1</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_continuity) continuous and it offers a natural adaptive refinement when required. It exhibits at least two specificities: it is a *Dual* scheme for triangle meshes and it has a slower refinement rate than primal ones.

:::

### Interpolating schemes {#Interpolating_schemes}

After subdivision, the control points of the original mesh and the newly generated control points are interpolated on the limit surface. The earliest work was so-called "[butterfly scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly_scheme)" by Dyn, Levin and Gregory (1990), who extended the four-point interpolatory subdivision scheme for curves to a subdivision scheme for surfaces. Zorin, Schröder and Sweldens (1996) noticed that the butterfly scheme cannot generate smooth surfaces for irregular triangle meshes and thus modified this scheme. Kobbelt (1996) further generalized the four-point interpolatory subdivision scheme for curves to the tensor product subdivision scheme for surfaces. In 1991, Nasri proposed a scheme for interpolating Doo-Sabin;[^o17] while in 1993 Halstead, Kass, and DeRose proposed one for Catmull-Clark.[^o18]

- [Butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly_subdivision_surfaces) (1990), Triangles – named after the scheme's shape
- [Modified Butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midedge) (1996), Quads[^o19] – designed to overcome artifacts generated by irregular topology
- [Kobbelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobbelt) (1996), Quads – a variational subdivision method that tries to overcome uniform subdivision drawbacks

## Key developments {#Key_developments}

- 1978: Subdivision surfaces were described by [Edwin Catmull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Catmull) and Jim Clark (see [Catmull-Clark subdivision surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catmull-Clark_subdivision_surface)), and by Daniel Doo and Malcom Sabin (see [Doo-Sabin subdivision surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doo-Sabin_subdivision_surfaces)).
- 1995: [Ulrich Reif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulrich_Reif) solved subdivision surface behaviour near extraordinary vertices.[^o21]
- 1998: [Jos Stam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos_Stam) contributed a method for exact evaluation for Catmull-Clark subdivision surfaces under arbitrary parameter values.[^o22]
