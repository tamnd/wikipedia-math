# Dirac operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirac operator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), a **Dirac operator** is a first-order [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) that is a formal square root, or [half-iterate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-iterate), of a second-order differential operator such as a [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian). It was introduced in 1847 by [William Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817)[^o6] and in 1928 by [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742).[^o2] The question which concerned Dirac was to factorise formally the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706) of the [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space), to get an equation for the [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) which would be compatible with [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

In general, let $D$ be a first-order differential operator acting on a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) $V$ over a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) $M$. If

$$D^2=\Delta, \,$$

where $\Delta$ is the [(positive, or geometric) Laplacian](wikipedia://en/174706#Analytic_and_geometric_Laplacians) of $V$, then $D$ is called a **Dirac operator**.

Note that there are two different conventions as to how the Laplace operator is defined:

- the "analytic" Laplacian, which could be characterized in $\mathbb{R}^n$ as $$\Delta=\nabla^2=\sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\right)^2 {,}$$ which is [negative-definite](wikipedia://en/20437320), in the sense that $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\overline{\varphi(x)}\Delta\varphi(x)\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}|\nabla\varphi(x)|^2\,dx<0$ for any [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000) [compactly supported](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) function $\varphi(x)$ which is not identically zero, and
- the "geometric", [positive-definite](wikipedia://en/20437320) Laplacian defined by $$\Delta=-\nabla^2=-\sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\right)^2 {.}$$

:::

## History {#History}

[W.R. Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W.R._Hamilton) defined "the square root of the Laplacian" in 1847[^o6] in his series of articles about [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440):

> \<...> if we introduce a new characteristic of operation, $\triangleleft$, defined with relation to these three symbols $ijk,$ and to the known operation of partial differentiation, performed with respect to three independent but real variables $xyz,$ as follows:
>
> $$\triangleleft=\frac{i\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}+\frac{j\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}+\frac{k\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z};$$
>
> *this new characteristic* $\triangleleft$ *will have the negative of its symbolic square expressed by the following formula* :
>
> $$-\triangleleft^2=\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}y}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\right)^2;$$
>
> of which it is clear that the applications to analytical physics must be extensive in a high degree.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Example 1 {#Example_1}

$D = -i \partial_x$ is a Dirac operator on the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) over a line.

### Example 2 {#Example_2}

Consider a simple bundle of notable importance in physics: the configuration space of a particle with spin–$\tfrac{1}{2}$ confined to a plane, which is also the base manifold. It is represented by a wavefunction $\psi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{C}^2$

$$\psi(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix}\chi(x,y) \\ \eta(x,y)\end{bmatrix}$$

where $x$ and $y$ are the usual coordinate functions on $\mathbb{R}^2$. $\chi$ specifies the [probability amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude) for the particle to be in the spin-up state, and similarly for $\eta$. The so-called [spin-Dirac operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-Dirac_operator) can then be written

$$D=-i\sigma_x\partial_x-i\sigma_y\partial_y ,$$

where $\sigma_i$ are the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868). Note that the anticommutation relations for the Pauli matrices make the proof of the above defining property trivial. Those relations define the notion of a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305).

Solutions to the [Dirac equation](wikipedia://en/39407) for spinor fields are often called *harmonic spinors*.

### Example 3 {#Example_3}

Feynman's Dirac operator describes the propagation of a free [fermion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) in three dimensions and is elegantly written

$$D=\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\ \equiv \partial\!\!\!/,$$

using the [Feynman slash notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_slash_notation). In introductory textbooks to [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), this will appear in the form

$$D = c\vec\alpha \cdot (-i\hbar\nabla_x) + mc^2\beta$$

where $\vec\alpha = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3)$ are the off-diagonal [Dirac matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_matrices) $\alpha_i=\beta\gamma_i$, with $\beta=\gamma_0$ and the remaining constants are $c$ the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), $\hbar$ being the [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant), and $m$ the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of a fermion (for example, an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron)). It acts on a four-component wave function $\psi(x) \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^3, \mathbb{C}^4)$, the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) of smooth, square-integrable functions. It can be extended to a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408) on that domain. The square, in this case, is not the Laplacian, but instead $D^2=\Delta+m^2$ (after setting $\hbar=c=1.$)

### Example 4 {#Example_4}

Another Dirac operator arises in [Clifford analysis](wikipedia://en/22424754). In euclidean $n$-space this is

$$D=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}$$

where $\{ e_j \}_{j = 1}^{n}$ is an orthonormal basis for euclidean $n$-space, and $\mathbb{R}^n$ is considered to be embedded in a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305).

This is a special case of the [Atiyah–Singer–Dirac operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah–Singer–Dirac_operator) acting on sections of a [spinor bundle](wikipedia://en/348085).

### Example 5 {#Example_5}

For a [spin manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_manifold), *M*, the Atiyah–Singer–Dirac operator is locally defined as follows: For *x* ∈ *M* and *e<sub>1</sub>*(*x*), ..., *e<sub>j</sub>*(*x*) a local orthonormal basis for the tangent space of *M* at *x*, the Atiyah–Singer–Dirac operator is

$$D=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}(x)\tilde{\Gamma}_{e_{j}(x)} ,$$

where $\tilde{\Gamma}$ is the [spin connection](wikipedia://en/2915190), a lifting of the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) on *M* to the [spinor bundle](wikipedia://en/348085) over *M*. The square in this case is not the Laplacian, but instead $D^2=\Delta+R/4$ where $R$ is the [scalar curvature](wikipedia://en/285622) of the connection.[^o15]

### Example 6 {#Example_6}

On [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) $(M, g)$ of dimension $n=\dim(M)$ with [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) $\nabla$ and an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) $\{e_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n} {,}$ we can define [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) $d$ and [coderivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codifferential) $\delta$ as

$$d = e^{a}\wedge \nabla_{e_{a}}, \quad \delta = e^{a} \lrcorner \nabla_{e_{a}}.$$

Then we can define a Dirac-Kähler operator[^o17][^o18][^o19] $D$, as follows

$$D = e^{a}\nabla_{e_{a}}=d-\delta.$$

The operator acts on sections of [Clifford bundle](wikipedia://en/8687388) in general, and it can be restricted to spinor bundle, an ideal of Clifford bundle, only if the projection operator on the ideal is parallel.[^o17][^o18][^o19]

:::

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

In Clifford analysis, the operator *D* : *C*<sup>∞</sup>(**R**<sup>*k*</sup> ⊗ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, *S*) → *C*<sup>∞</sup>(**R**<sup>*k*</sup> ⊗ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, **C**<sup>*k*</sup> ⊗ *S*) acting on spinor valued functions defined by

$$f(x_1,\ldots,x_k)\mapsto \begin{pmatrix} \partial_{\underline{x_1}}f\\ \partial_{\underline{x_2}}f\\ \ldots\\ \partial_{\underline{x_k}}f\\ \end{pmatrix}$$

is sometimes called Dirac operator in *k* Clifford variables. In the notation, *S* is the space of spinors, $x_i=(x_{i1},x_{i2},\ldots,x_{in})$ are *n*-dimensional variables and $\partial_{\underline{x_i}}=\sum_j e_j\cdot \partial_{x_{ij}}$ is the Dirac operator in the *i*-th variable. This is a common generalization of the Dirac operator (*k* = 1) and the [Dolbeault operator](wikipedia://en/3189601) (*n* = 2, *k* arbitrary). It is an [invariant differential operator](wikipedia://en/6935363), invariant under the action of the group SL(*k*) × Spin(*n*). The [resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_resolution#Injective_resolutions) of *D* is known only in some special cases.
