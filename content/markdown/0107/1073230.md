# Eilenberg–MacLane space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eilenberg–MacLane space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), an **Eilenberg–MacLane space**[^o2] is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) with a single nontrivial [homotopy group](wikipedia://en/297506).

:::

Let *G* be a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) and *n* a positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). A [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) topological space *X* is called an Eilenberg–MacLane space of type $K(G,n)$, if it has *n*-th [homotopy group](wikipedia://en/297506) $\pi_n(X)$ [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to *G* and all other homotopy groups [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059). Assuming that *G* is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) in the case that $n > 1$, Eilenberg–MacLane spaces of type $K(G,n)$ always exist, and are all [weak homotopy equivalent](wikipedia://en/34615176). Thus, one may consider $K(G,n)$ as referring to a weak homotopy equivalence class of spaces. It is common to refer to any representative as "a $K(G,n)$" or as "a model of $K(G,n)$". Moreover, it is common to assume that this space is a [CW-complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CW-complex) (which is always possible via [CW approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CW_approximation)).

The name is derived from [Samuel Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501) and [Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574), who introduced such spaces in the late 1940s.

As such, an Eilenberg–MacLane space is a special kind of [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) that in [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) can be regarded as a building block for CW-complexes via [fibrations](wikipedia://en/316826) in a [Postnikov system](wikipedia://en/15708202). These spaces are important in many contexts in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), including computations of [homotopy groups of spheres](wikipedia://en/1104697), definition of [cohomology operations](wikipedia://en/1226719), and for having a strong connection to [singular cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060).

A generalised Eilenberg–MacLane space is a space which has the homotopy type of a [product](wikipedia://en/24910) of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces $\prod_{m}K(G_m,m)$.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) $S^1$ is a $K(\mathbb{Z},1)$.
- The infinite-dimensional [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) $\mathbb{CP}^{\infty}$ is a model of $K(\mathbb{Z},2)$.
- The infinite-dimensional [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) $\mathbb{RP}^{\infty}$ is a $K(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},1)$.
- The [wedge sum](wikipedia://en/684143) of *k* [unit circles](wikipedia://en/27072071) $\textstyle\bigvee_{i=1}^k S^1$ is a $K(F_k,1)$, where $F_k$ is the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) on *k* generators.
- The complement to any connected [knot](wikipedia://en/153008) or graph in a 3-dimensional sphere $S^3$ is of type $K(G,1)$; this is called the "[asphericity](wikipedia://en/7147287) of knots", and is a 1957 theorem of [Christos Papakyriakopoulos](wikipedia://en/1463636).[^o5]
- Any [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), connected, [non-positively curved](wikipedia://en/12989803) [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) *M* is a $K(\Gamma,1)$, where $\Gamma=\pi_1(M)$ is the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of *M*. This is a consequence of the [Cartan–Hadamard theorem](wikipedia://en/11939373).
- An infinite [lens space](wikipedia://en/885725) $L(\infty, q)$ given by the quotient of $S^\infty$ by the free action $(z \mapsto e^{2\pi i m/q}z)$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}/q$ is a $K(\mathbb{Z}/q,1)$. This can be shown using [covering space theory](wikipedia://en/250323#Deck_transformation) and the fact that the [infinite-dimensional sphere](wikipedia://en/80611932) is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414).[^o6] Note this includes $\mathbb{RP}^{\infty}$ as a $K(\mathbb{Z}/2,1)$.
- The [configuration space](wikipedia://en/54981574) of $n$ points in the plane is a $K(P_n,1)$, where $P_n$ is the [pure braid group](wikipedia://en/550138#Relation_with_symmetric_group_and_the_pure_braid_group) on $n$ strands.
- Correspondingly, the [$n$th unordered configuration space](wikipedia://en/54981574) of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is a $K(B_n,1)$, where $B_n$ denotes the [$n$-strand braid group](wikipedia://en/550138). [^o7]
- The [infinite symmetric product](wikipedia://en/31338286) $SP(S^n)$ of a [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) is a $K(\mathbb{Z},n)$. More generally $SP(M(G,n))$ is a $K(G,n)$ for all [Moore spaces](wikipedia://en/2139649) $M(G,n)$.

Some further elementary examples can be constructed from these by using the fact that the product $K(G,n) \times K(H,n)$ is $K(G\times H,n)$. For instance the [$n$-dimensional Torus](wikipedia://en/74800#n-dimensional_torus) $\mathbb{T}^n$ is a $K(\mathbb{Z}^n, 1)$.

:::

## Constructing Eilenberg–MacLane spaces {#Constructing_Eilenberg–MacLane_spaces}

For $n = 1$ and $G$ an arbitrary [group](wikipedia://en/19447) the construction of $K(G,1)$ is identical to that of the [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) of the group $G$. Note that if G has a torsion element, then every CW-complex of type K(G,1) has to be infinite-dimensional.

There are multiple techniques for constructing higher Eilenberg–MacLane spaces. One of which is to construct a [Moore space](wikipedia://en/2139649) $M(A,n)$ for an abelian group $A$: Take the [wedge](wikipedia://en/684143) of *n*-[spheres](wikipedia://en/27859), one for each generator of the group *A* and realise the relations between these generators by attaching *(n+1)*-cells via corresponding maps in $\pi_n(\bigvee S^n)$ of said wedge sum. Note that the lower homotopy groups $\pi_{i < n} (M(A,n))$ are already trivial by construction. Now iteratively kill all higher homotopy groups $\pi_{i > n} (M(A,n))$ by successively attaching cells of dimension greater than $n + 1$, and define $K(A,n)$ as [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) under inclusion of this iteration.

Another useful technique is to use the geometric realization of [simplicial abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_abelian_group).[^o9] This gives an explicit presentation of simplicial abelian groups which represent Eilenberg–MacLane spaces.

Another simplicial construction, in terms of [classifying spaces](wikipedia://en/923556) and [universal bundles](wikipedia://en/2179570), is given in [J. Peter May](wikipedia://en/14644098)'s book.[^o10]

Since taking the loop space lowers the homotopy groups by one slot, we have a canonical homotopy equivalence $K(G,n)\simeq\Omega K(G,n+1)$, hence there is a fibration sequence

$$K(G,n) \to * \to K(G,n+1).$$

Note that this is not a cofibration sequence ― the space $K(G,n+1)$ is not the homotopy cofiber of $K(G,n) \to *$.

This fibration sequence can be used to study the cohomology of $K(G,n+1)$ from $K(G,n)$ using the [Leray spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/5174983). This was exploited by [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) while he studied the homotopy groups of spheres using the [Postnikov system](wikipedia://en/15708202) and spectral sequences.

## Properties of Eilenberg–MacLane spaces {#Properties_of_Eilenberg–MacLane_spaces}

### Bijection between homotopy classes of maps and cohomology {#Bijection_between_homotopy_classes_of_maps_and_cohomology}

An important property of $K(G, n)$'s is that for any abelian group *G*, and any based CW-complex *X*, the set $[X, K(G,n)]$ of based homotopy classes of based maps from *X* to $K(G,n)$ is in natural bijection with the *n*-th [singular cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology) group $H^n(X, G)$ of the space *X*. Thus one says that the $K(G,n)s$ are [representing spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representing_space) for singular cohomology with coefficients in *G*. Since

$$\begin{array}{rcl} H^n(K(G,n),G) &=& \operatorname{Hom}(H_n(K(G,n);\mathbb{Z}), G) \\ &=& \operatorname{Hom}(\pi_n(K(G,n)), G) \\ &=& \operatorname{Hom}(G,G), \end{array}$$

there is a distinguished element $u \in H^n(K(G,n),G)$ corresponding to the identity. The above bijection is given by the pullback of that element $f \mapsto f^*u$. This is similar to the [Yoneda lemma](wikipedia://en/34359) of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869).

A constructive proof of this theorem can be found here,[^o13] another making use of the relation between [omega-spectra](wikipedia://en/1033697) and [generalized reduced cohomology theories](wikipedia://en/194060#Axioms_and_generalized_cohomology_theories) can be found here,[^o14] and the main idea is sketched later as well.

### Loop spaces and Omega spectra {#Loop_spaces_and_Omega_spectra}

The [loop space](wikipedia://en/2174332) of an Eilenberg–MacLane space is again an Eilenberg–MacLane space: $\Omega K(G,n) \cong K(G,n-1)$. Further there is an adjoint relation between the loop-space and the reduced suspension: $[\Sigma X, Y] = [X,\Omega Y]$, which gives $[X,K(G,n)] \cong [X,\Omega^2K(G,n+2)]$ the structure of an abelian group, where the operation is the concatenation of loops. This makes the bijection $[X, K(G,n)] \to H^n(X, G)$ mentioned above a group isomorphism.

Also this property implies that Eilenberg–MacLane spaces with various *n* form an [omega-spectrum](wikipedia://en/1033697), called an "Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum". This spectrum defines via $X \mapsto h^n(X):= [X, K(G,n)]$ a reduced cohomology theory on based CW-complexes and for any reduced cohomology theory $h^*$ on CW-complexes with $h^n(S^0) = 0$ for $n \neq 0$ there is a natural isomorphism $h^n(X) \cong \tilde{H}^n(X, h^0(S^0))$, where $\tilde{H^*}$ denotes reduced singular cohomology. Therefore these two cohomology theories coincide.

In a more general context, [Brown representability](wikipedia://en/1073746) says that every reduced cohomology theory on based CW-complexes comes from an [omega-spectrum](wikipedia://en/1033697).

### Relation with homology {#Relation_with_homology}

For a fixed abelian group $G$ there are maps on the [stable homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/2946655)

$$\pi_{q+n}^s(X \wedge K(G,n)) \cong \pi_{q+n+1}^s(X \wedge \Sigma K(G,n)) \to \pi_{q+n+1}^s(X \wedge K(G,n+1))$$

induced by the map $\Sigma K(G,n) \to K(G,n+1)$. Taking the direct limit over these maps, one can verify that this defines a reduced homology theory

$$h_q(X) = \varinjlim _{n} \pi_{q+n}^s(X \wedge K(G,n))$$

on CW complexes. Since $h_q(S^0) = \varinjlim \pi_{q+n}^s(K(G,n))$ vanishes for $q \neq 0$, $h_*$ agrees with reduced singular homology $\tilde{H}_*(\cdot,G)$ with coefficients in G on CW-complexes.

### Functoriality {#Functoriality}

It follows from the [universal coefficient theorem](wikipedia://en/996107) for cohomology that the Eilenberg MacLane space is a *quasi-functor* of the group; that is, for each positive integer $n$ if $a\colon G \to G'$ is any homomorphism of abelian groups, then there is a [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) set

$$K(a,n) = \{[f]: f\colon K(G,n) \to K(G',n), H_n(f) = a\},$$

satisfying $K(a \circ b,n) \supset K(a,n) \circ K(b,n) \text{ and } 1 \in K(1,n),$ where $[f]$ denotes the homotopy class of a continuous map $f$ and $S \circ T := \{s \circ t: s \in S, t \in T \}.$

### Relation with Postnikov/Whitehead towers {#Relation_with_Postnikov/Whitehead_towers}

Every connected CW-complex $X$ possesses a [Postnikov tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postnikov_tower), that is an inverse system of spaces:

$$\cdots \to X_3 \xrightarrow{p_3} X_2 \xrightarrow{p_2} X_1 \simeq K(\pi_1(X), 1)$$

such that for every $n$:

1. there are commuting maps $X \to X_n$, which induce isomorphism on $\pi_i$ for $i \leq n$ ,
1. $\pi_i(X_n) = 0$ for $i > n$,
1. the maps $X_n \xrightarrow{p_n} X_{n-1}$ are fibrations with fiber $K(\pi_n(X),n)$.

Dually there exists a [Whitehead tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehead_tower), which is a sequence of CW-complexes:

$$\cdots \to X_3 \to X_2 \to X_1 \to X$$

such that for every $n$:

1. the maps $X_n \to X$ induce isomorphism on $\pi_i$ for $i > n$,
1. $X_n$ is [n-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-connected_space),
1. the maps $X_n \to X_{n-1}$ are fibrations with fiber $K(\pi_n(X), n-1)$.

With help of [Serre spectral sequences](wikipedia://en/3085825) computations of higher [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) of spheres can be made. For instance $\pi_4(S^3)$ and $\pi_5(S^3)$ using a Whitehead tower of $S^3$ can be found here,[^o19] more generally those of $\pi_{n+i}(S^n) \ i \leq 3$ using a Postnikov systems can be found here. [^o20]

### Cohomology operations {#Cohomology_operations}

For fixed natural numbers *m,n* and abelian groups *G,H* exists a bijection between the set of all [cohomology operations](wikipedia://en/1226719) $\Theta :H^m(\cdot,G) \to H^n(\cdot,H)$ and $H^n(K(G,m),H)$ defined by $\Theta \mapsto \Theta(u)$, where $u \in H^m(K(G,m),G)$ is the fundamental class as defined in #Bijection between homotopy classes of maps and cohomology.[^o22]

As a result, cohomology operations cannot decrease the degree of the cohomology groups and degree preserving cohomology operations are corresponding to coefficient homomorphism $\operatorname{Hom}(G,H)$. This follows from the [Universal coefficient theorem for cohomology](wikipedia://en/996107) and the [(m-1)-connectedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-connected_space) of $K(G,m)$.

Some interesting examples for cohomology operations are [Steenrod Squares and Powers](wikipedia://en/2146220), when $G=H$ are [finite cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327). When studying those the importance of the cohomology of $K(\mathbb{Z} /p ,n)$ with coefficients in $\mathbb{Z} /p$ becomes apparent quickly;[^o23] some extensive tabeles of those groups can be found here. [^o24]

### Group (co)homology {#Group_(co)homology}

One can define the [group (co)homology](wikipedia://en/248635) of G with coefficients in the group A as the singular (co)homology of the Eilenberg–MacLane space $K(G,1)$ with coefficients in A.

### Further applications {#Further_applications}

The loop space construction described above is used in [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305) to obtain, for example, the [string group](wikipedia://en/28936135), the [fivebrane group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fivebrane_group) and so on, as the [Whitehead tower](wikipedia://en/15708202#Whitehead_tower) arising from the [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence)

$$0\to K(\mathbb{Z},2)\to \operatorname{String}(n)\to \operatorname{Spin}(n)\to 0$$

with $\operatorname{String}(n)$ the [string group](wikipedia://en/28936135), and $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231). The relevance of $K(\mathbb{Z},2)$ lies in the fact that there are the homotopy equivalences

$$K(\mathbb{Z},1) \simeq U(1) \simeq B\mathbb{Z}$$

for the [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) $B\mathbb{Z}$, and the fact $K(\mathbb{Z},2) \simeq BU(1)$. Notice that because the complex spin group is a group extension

$$0\to K(\mathbb{Z},1) \to \operatorname{Spin}^\mathbb{C}(n) \to \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to 0,$$

the String group can be thought of as a "higher" complex spin group extension, in the sense of [higher group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_group) since the space $K(\mathbb{Z},2)$ is an example of a higher group. It can be thought of the topological realization of the [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) $\mathbf{B}U(1)$ whose object is a single point and whose morphisms are the group $U(1)$. Because of these homotopical properties, the construction generalizes: any given space $K(\mathbb{Z},n)$ can be used to start a short exact sequence that kills the homotopy group $\pi_{n+1}$ in a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315).
