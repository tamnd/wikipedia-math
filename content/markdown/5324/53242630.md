# Penny graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Penny graph"}

*Image: Penny graph.jpg.* 11 pennies, forming a penny graph with 11 vertices and 19 edges

*Image: Sierpinski Pascal triangle.svg.* The [Hanoi graph](wikipedia://en/58619302) $H^5_3$ as a penny graph (the contact graph of the black disks)

In [geometric graph theory](wikipedia://en/3313527), a **penny graph** is a [contact graph](wikipedia://en/51513238) of [unit circles](wikipedia://en/27072071). It is formed from a collection of unit circles that do not cross each other, by creating a vertex for each circle and an edge for every pair of [tangent circles](wikipedia://en/19349153). The circles can be represented physically by [pennies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny), arranged without overlapping on a flat surface, with a vertex for each penny and an edge for each two pennies that touch.

:::

Penny graphs have also been called **unit coin graphs**, because they are the [coin graphs](wikipedia://en/16648043) formed from unit circles. If each vertex is represented by a point at the center of its circle, then two vertices will be adjacent [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) their distance is the minimum distance among all pairs of vertices. Therefore, penny graphs have also been called **minimum-distance graphs**, **smallest-distance graphs**, or **closest-pairs graphs**. Similarly, in a **mutual nearest neighbor graph** that links pairs of points in the plane that are each other's [nearest neighbors](wikipedia://en/12061759), each [connected component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) is a penny graph, although edges in different components may have different lengths.

Every penny graph is a [unit disk graph](wikipedia://en/3686677) and a [matchstick graph](wikipedia://en/25730128). Like [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) more generally, they obey the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949), but this theorem is easier to prove for penny graphs. Testing whether a graph is a penny graph, or finding its [maximum independent set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_independent_set), is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard); however, both [upper and lower bounds](wikipedia://en/42693) are known for the size of the maximum independent set, higher than the bounds that are possible for arbitrary planar graphs.

## Properties {#Properties}

### Number of edges {#Number_of_edges}

Every vertex in a penny graph has at most six neighboring vertices; here the number six is the [kissing number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kissing_number_problem) for circles in the plane. However, the pennies on the boundary of the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) have fewer neighbors. Counting more precisely this reduction in neighbors for boundary pennies leads to a precise bound on the number of edges in any penny graph: a penny graph with $n$ vertices has at most

$$\left\lfloor 3n - \sqrt{12n-3}\right\rfloor$$

edges. Some penny graphs, formed by arranging the pennies in a [triangular grid](wikipedia://en/2863858), have exactly this number of edges.

::: {#o5 .problem cue=unsolved}

What is the maximum number of edges in a triangle-free penny graph?

:::

By arranging the pennies in a [square grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_grid), or in the form of certain [squaregraphs](wikipedia://en/20741076), one can form [triangle-free](wikipedia://en/7667996) penny graphs whose number of edges is at least

$$\left\lfloor 2n-2\sqrt{n}\right\rfloor,$$

and in any triangle-free penny graph the number of edges is at most

$$2n-1.65\sqrt{n}.$$

Swanepoel conjectured that the $\left\lfloor 2n-2\sqrt{n}\right\rfloor$ bound is tight. Proving this, or finding a better bound, remains open.

### Coloring {#Coloring}

*Image: Penny graph 11 nodes.svg.* An optimal coloring of the 11-vertex penny graph shown above requires four colors

Every penny graph contains a vertex with at most three neighbors. For instance, such a vertex can be found at one of the corners of the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the circle centers. Therefore, penny graphs have [degeneracy](wikipedia://en/27545816) at most three. Based on this, one can prove that their [graph colorings](wikipedia://en/426743) require at most four colors, much more easily than the proof of the more general [four-color theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-color_theorem). However, despite their restricted structure, there exist penny graphs that do still require four colors.

*Image: Triangle-free penny graph.jpg.* A [triangle-free](wikipedia://en/7667996) penny graph with the property that all the pennies on the convex hull touch at least three other pennies

Analogously, the degeneracy of every triangle-free penny graph is at most two. Every such graph contains a vertex with at most two neighbors, even though it is not always possible to find this vertex on the convex hull. Based on this, one can prove that they require at most three colors, more easily than the proof of the more general [Grötzsch's theorem](wikipedia://en/24917736) that triangle-free planar graphs are 3-colorable.

### Independent sets {#Independent_sets}

A [maximum independent set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_independent_set) in a penny graph is a subset of the pennies, no two of which touch each other. Finding maximum independent sets is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) for arbitrary graphs, and remains [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) on penny graphs. It is an instance of the [maximum disjoint set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_disjoint_set) problem, in which one must find large subsets of non-overlapping regions of the plane. However, as with planar graphs more generally, [Baker's technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker's_technique) provides a [polynomial-time approximation scheme](wikipedia://en/666431) for this problem.

::: {#o8 .problem cue=unsolved}

What is the largest $c$ such that every $n$-vertex penny graph has an independent set of size $cn$?

:::

In 1983, [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) asked for the largest number $c$ such that every $n$-vertex penny graph has an independent set of at least $cn$ vertices. That is, if we place $n$ pennies on a flat surface, there should be a subset of $cn$ of the pennies that do not touch each other. By the four-color theorem, $c \ge 1/4$, and the improved bound $c \ge 8/31 \approx 0.258$ was proven by Swanepoel. In the other direction, Pach and Tóth proved that $c \le 5/16 = 0.3125$. As of 2013, these remained the best bounds known for this problem.

:::

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

Constructing a penny graph from the locations of its $n$ circles can be performed as an instance of the [closest pair of points problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closest_pair_of_points_problem), taking worst-case time $O(n \log n)$ or (with randomized time and with the use of the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function)) expected time $O(n)$. An alternative method with the same worst-case time is to construct the [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) or [nearest neighbor graph](wikipedia://en/12061759) of the circle centers (both of which contain the penny graph as a subgraph) and then test which edges correspond to circle tangencies.

However, if a graph is given without geometric positions for its vertices, then testing whether it can be represented as a penny graph is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard). It remains NP-hard even when the given graph is a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560). Similarly, testing whether a graph can be represented as a three-dimensional mutual nearest neighbor graph is also NP-hard.

It is possible to perform some computational tasks on directed penny graphs, such as testing whether one vertex can reach another, in [polynomial time](wikipedia://en/405944) and substantially less than linear space, given an input representing its circles in a form allowing basic computational tasks such as testing adjacency and finding intersections of the circles with axis-parallel lines.

## Related graph families {#Related_graph_families}

Penny graphs are a special case of the [coin graphs](wikipedia://en/16648043) (graphs that can be represented by tangencies of non-crossing circles of arbitrary radii). Because the coin graphs are the same as the [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314),[^n1] all penny graphs are planar. The penny graphs are also [unit disk graphs](wikipedia://en/3686677) (the [intersection graphs](wikipedia://en/7726759) of unit circles), [unit distance graphs](wikipedia://en/7158665) (graphs that can be drawn with all edges having equal lengths, allowing crossings), and [matchstick graphs](wikipedia://en/25730128) (graphs that can be drawn in the plane with equal-length straight edges and no edge crossings).
