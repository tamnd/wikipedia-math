# Implementation theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Implementation theory"}

Mechanism design **Implementation theory** is an area of research in [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924) concerned with whether a class of [mechanisms (or institutions) can be designed](wikipedia://en/689895) whose equilibrium outcomes implement a given set of [normative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normative) goals or [welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_economics) criteria.[^o2]

:::

There are two general types of implementation problems: the economic problem of [producing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_%28economics%29) and [allocating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resource_allocation) [public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_good_%28economics%29) and [private goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_good) and choosing over a finite set of alternatives.[^o3] In the case of producing and allocating public/private goods, [solution concepts](wikipedia://en/1601457) are focused on finding [dominant strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominant_Strategy).

In his paper "Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders", [William Vickrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Vickrey) showed that if preferences are restricted to the case of quasi-linear utility functions then the mechanism dominant strategy is dominant-strategy implementable.[^o4] "A [social choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_choice) rule is dominant strategy [incentive compatible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentive_compatible), or [strategy-proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategy_proof), if the associated [revelation mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation_mechanism) has the property that honestly reporting the truth is always a dominant strategy for each agent."[^o3] However, the payments to agents become large, sacrificing budget neutrality to incentive compatibility.

In a game where multiple agents are to report their preferences (or their type), it may be in the best interest of some agents to lie about their preferences. This may improve their [payoff](wikipedia://en/3736689), but it may not be seen as a fair outcome to other agents.[^o5]

Although largely theoretical, implementation theory may have profound implications on policy creation because some [social choice](wikipedia://en/1745670) rules may be impossible to implement under specific game conditions.[^o2]

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Implementability (mechanism design)"}

## Implementability {#Implementability}

In [mechanism design](wikipedia://en/689895), implementability is a property of a [social choice function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_choice_function). It means that there is an [incentive-compatible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incentive-compatible) mechanism that attains ("implements") this function. There are several degrees of implementability, corresponding to the different degrees of incentive-compatibility, including:

- A function is **dominant-strategy implementable** if it is attainable by a mechanism which is dominant-strategy-incentive-compatible (also called [strategyproof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategyproof)).
- A function is **Bayesian-Nash implementable** if it is attainable by a mechanism which is Bayesian-Nash-incentive-compatible.

See for a recent reference. In some textbooks, the entire field of mechanism design is called **implementation theory**.[^o8]
