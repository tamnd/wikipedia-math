# Grunwald–Winstein equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Grunwald–Winstein equation"}

In [physical organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_organic_chemistry), the **Grunwald–Winstein equation** is a [linear free energy relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_free_energy_relationship) between relative [rate constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_constant) and the [ionizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing) power of various [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent) systems, describing the effect of solvent as [nucleophile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleophile) on different substrates. The equation, which was developed by [Ernest Grunwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Grunwald) and [Saul Winstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul_Winstein) in 1948, could be written[^o2][^o3]

:::

$$\log \frac{k_{x,sol}}{k_{x,\ 80\%\ \ce{EtOH}}} = mY$$

where the $k_{x, \operatorname{sol}}$ and $k_{x, 80\% \operatorname{EtOH}}$ are the [solvolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvolysis) rate constants for a certain compound in different solvent systems and in the reference solvent, 80% aqueous [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol), respectively. The parameter $m$ is a parameter measuring the sensitivity of the solvolysis rate with respect to $Y$, the measure of ionizing power of the solvent.[^o4]

## Background {#Background}

*Image: Benzoicacid.png.* Dissociation of substituted benzoic acids.

The [Hammett equation](wikipedia://en/4986733) (Equation **1**) provides the relationship between the substituent on the benzene ring and the ionizing rate constant of the reaction. Hammett used the ionization of [benzoic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzoic_acid) as the standard reaction to define a set of substituent parameters σ<sub>X</sub>, and then to generate the ρ values, which represent ionizing abilities of different substrates. This relationship can be visualized through a Hammett plot.

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\log \frac{k_\ce{X}}{k_\ce{H}} = \rho\sigma_\ce{X} \tag{1}$$

:::

However, if the solvent of the reaction is changed, but not the structure of the substrate, the rate constant may change too. Following this idea, Grunwald and Winstein plotted the relative rate constant vs. the change of solvent system, and formulated this behavior in the Grunwald–Winstein equation. Since the equation has the same pattern as the Hammett equation but captures the change of the solvent system, it is considered as an extension of the [Hammett equation](wikipedia://en/4986733).

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Reference compound {#Reference_compound}

*Image: TBuCl.jpg.* S<sub>N</sub>1 mechanism of substitution reaction

The [substitution reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_reaction) of [tert-Butyl chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tert-Butyl_chloride) was chosen as reference reaction. The first step, ionizing step, is the [rate determining step](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_determining_step), SO stands for the nucleophilic solvent. The reference solvent is 80% Ethanol and 20% water by volume. Both of them can carry out the [nucleophilic attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleophilic_attack) on the carbocation.[^o10][^o11]

The [S<sub>N</sub>1 reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SN1_reaction) is performed through a stable [carbocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocation) intermediate, the more nucleophilic solvent can stabilize the [carbocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocation) better, thus the rate constant of the reaction could be larger. Since there’s no sharp line between the S<sub>N</sub>1 and [S<sub>N</sub>2 reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SN2_reaction), a reaction that goes through S<sub>N</sub>1 mechanism more is preferred to achieve a better linear relationship, hence *t*-BuCl was chosen.

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\log \frac{k_{t-\ce{BuCl}, sol}}{k_\ce{\mathit t-BuCl,\ 80\%\ EtOH}} = Y \tag{2}$$

:::

In equation (2), $k_{t-\operatorname{BuCl}, 80\% \operatorname{EtOH}}$ stands for the rate constant of *t*-BuCl reaction in 80% aqueous Ethanol, which is chosen as the reference. The variable $k_{t-\operatorname{BuCl}, \operatorname{sol}}$ stands for the rate constant of the same reaction in a different solvent system, such as ethanol-water, methanol-water, and [acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid)-[formic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formic_acid). Thus, *Y* reflects the ionizing power of different nucleophile solvents.

### *m* values {#m_values}

The equation parameter *m*, called the sensitivity factor of solvolysis, describes the compound’s ability to form the carbocation intermediate in given solvent system. It is the slope of the plot of log(k<sub>sol</sub>/k<sub>80%EtOH</sub>) vs Y values. Since the reference reaction has little solvent nucleophilic assistance, the reactions with *m* equal to 1 or larger than 1 have almost full ionized intermediates. If the compounds are not so sensitive to the ionizing ability of solvent, then the *m* values are smaller than 1. That is:

1. *m* ≥ 1, the reactions proceed through S<sub>N</sub>1 mechanism.
1. *m* \< 1, the reactions proceed through a mechanism between S<sub>N</sub>1 and S<sub>N</sub>2.

:::

## Disadvantages {#Disadvantages}

1. The Grunwald–Winstein equation cannot fit all data for different kinds of solvent mixtures. The combinations are limited to certain systems and only to nucleophilic solvents.
1. For many reactions and nucleophilic solvent systems, the relationships are not fully linear. This derives from the growing S<sub>N</sub>2 reaction character within the mechanism.
