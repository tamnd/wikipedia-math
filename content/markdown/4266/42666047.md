# Eisenstein triple

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eisenstein triple"}

Similar to a [Pythagorean triple](wikipedia://en/24172), an **Eisenstein triple** (named after [Gotthold Eisenstein](wikipedia://en/1452482)) is a set of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) which are the lengths of the sides of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) where one of the angles is 60 or 120 degrees. The relation of such triangles to the [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035) is analogous to the relation of Pythagorean triples to the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628).

:::

## Triangles with an angle of 60° {#Triangles_with_an_angle_of_60°}

*Image: Eisenstein triple.png.* An Eisenstein triple

Triangles with an angle of 60° are a special case of the [Law of Cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Cosines):[^o3][^o4][^o5]

$$c^2 = a^2 - ab + b^2.$$

When the lengths of the sides are integers, the values form a set known as an Eisenstein triple.[^o6]

Examples of Eisenstein triples include:[^o7]

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

| Side *a* | Side *b* | Side *c* |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 8 | 7 |
| 5 | 8 | 7 |
| 5 | 21 | 19 |
| 7 | 15 | 13 |
| 7 | 40 | 37 |
| 8 | 15 | 13 |
| 9 | 24 | 21 |

:::

## Triangles with an angle of 120° {#Triangles_with_an_angle_of_120°}

*Image: 120-degree-integer-triangle.svg.* Triangle with 120° angle and integer sides

A similar special case of the Law of Cosines relates the sides of a triangle with an angle of 120 degrees:

$$c^2 = a^2 + ab + b^2.$$

Examples of such triangles include:[^o10]

::: {#o11 .table cue=table}

| Side *a* | Side *b* | Side *c* |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 5 | 7 |
| 7 | 8 | 13 |
| 5 | 16 | 19 |

:::
