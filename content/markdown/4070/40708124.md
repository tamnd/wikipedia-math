# Legendre's three-square theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Legendre's three-square theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Legendre's three-square theorem** states that a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) can be represented as the sum of three squares of integers

:::

$$n = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$$

[if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $n$ is not of the form $n = 4^a(8b + 7)$ for nonnegative integers $a$ and $b$.

*Image: distances\_between\_double\_cube\_corners.svg.* Distances between [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a double [unit cube](wikipedia://en/407763) are [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208) of the first six [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) due to the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) (√7 is not possible due to Legendre's three-square theorem)

The first numbers that cannot be expressed as the sum of three squares (i.e. numbers that *can* be expressed as $n = 4^a(8b + 7)$) are

7, 15, 23, 28, 31, 39, 47, 55, 60, 63, 71 ... .

## History {#History}

[Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) gave a criterion for numbers of the form 8*a* + 1 and 8*a* + 3 to be sums of a square plus twice another square, but did not provide a proof.[^o4] N. Beguelin noticed in 1774[^o5] that every positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) which is neither of the form 8*n* + 7, nor of the form 4*n*, is the sum of three squares, but did not provide a satisfactory proof.[^o6] In 1796 [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) proved his [Eureka theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eureka_theorem) that every positive integer *n* is the sum of 3 [triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/213915); this is equivalent to the fact that 8*n* + 3 is a sum of three squares. In 1797 or 1798 [A.-M. Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) obtained the first proof of his 3 square theorem.[^o7] In 1813, [A. L. Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Louis_Cauchy) noted[^o8] that Legendre's theorem is equivalent to the statement in the introduction above. Previously, in 1801, Gauss had obtained a more general result,[^o9] containing Legendre's theorem of 1797–8 as a corollary. In particular, Gauss counted the number of solutions of the expression of an integer as a sum of three squares, and this is a generalisation of yet another result of Legendre,[^o10] whose proof is incomplete. This last fact appears to be the reason for later incorrect claims according to which Legendre's proof of the three-square theorem was defective and had to be completed by Gauss.[^o11]

With [Lagrange's four-square theorem](wikipedia://en/326395) and the [two-square theorem](wikipedia://en/1850216) of Girard, Fermat and Euler, the [Waring's problem](wikipedia://en/33569) for *k* = 2 is entirely solved.

## Proofs {#Proofs}

The "only if" of the theorem is simply because [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 8, every square is congruent to 0, 1 or 4. There are several proofs of the converse (besides Legendre's proof). One of them is due to [Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) (in 1850), and has become classical.[^o14] It requires three main lemmas:

- the [quadratic reciprocity](wikipedia://en/25272) law,
- [Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/101453), and
- the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of the trivial ternary [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478).

:::

## Relationship to the four-square theorem {#Relationship_to_the_four-square_theorem}

This theorem can be used to prove [Lagrange's four-square theorem](wikipedia://en/326395), which states that all natural numbers can be written as a sum of four squares. Gauss[^o17] pointed out that the four squares theorem follows easily from the fact that any positive integer that is 1 or 2 mod 4 is a sum of 3 squares, because any positive integer not divisible by 4 can be reduced to this form by subtracting 0 or 1 from it. However, proving the three-square theorem is considerably more difficult than a direct proof of the four-square theorem that does not use the three-square theorem. Indeed, the four-square theorem was proved earlier, in 1770.

:::
