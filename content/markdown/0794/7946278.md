# Co-premise

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Co-premise"}

A **co-premise** is a [premise](wikipedia://en/7993337) in [reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasoning) and [informal logic](wikipedia://en/14122398) which is not the main supporting reason for a contention or a [lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemma_%28logic%29), but is logically necessary to ensure the [validity](wikipedia://en/21304742) of an [argument](wikipedia://en/13018310). One premise by itself, or a group of co-premises can form a reason.

:::

## Logical structure {#Logical_structure}

Every significant term or phrase appearing in a premise of a [simple argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_argument), should also appear in the contention/conclusion or in a co-premise. But this by itself does not guarantee a valid argument, see the [fallacy of the undistributed middle](wikipedia://en/478089) for an example of this.

Sometimes a co-premise will not be explicitly stated. This type of argument is known as an 'enthymematic' argument, and the co-premise may be referred to as a 'hidden' or an 'unstated' co-premise and will often be subject to an [inference objection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference_objection). In this [argument map](wikipedia://en/6190251) of a simple argument the two reasons for the main contention are co-premises and not separate reasons for believing the contention to be true. They are both necessary to ensure that the argument as a whole retains logical validity.
