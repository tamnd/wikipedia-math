# Wiener index

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wiener index"}

In [chemical graph theory](wikipedia://en/14222708), the **Wiener index** (also **Wiener number**) introduced by [Harry Wiener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Wiener), is a [topological index](wikipedia://en/14221614) of a [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule), defined as the sum of the lengths of the [shortest paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path) between all pairs of vertices in the [chemical graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_graph) representing the non-[hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) atoms in the molecule.[^o2]

:::

The Wiener index can be used for the representation of [computer networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_networks) and enhancing lattice [hardware security](wikipedia://en/54213333).

## History {#History}

The Wiener index is named after [Harry Wiener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Wiener), who introduced it in 1947; at the time, Wiener called it the "path number".[^o4] It is the oldest topological index related to molecular branching.[^o5] Based on its success, many other topological indexes of chemical graphs, based on information in the distance matrix of the graph, have been developed subsequently to Wiener's work.[^o6]

The same quantity has also been studied in pure mathematics, under various names including the gross status,[^o7] the distance of a graph,[^o8] and the transmission.[^o9] The Wiener index is also closely related to the [closeness centrality](wikipedia://en/1462712) of a vertex in a graph, a quantity inversely proportional to the sum of all distances between the given vertex and all other vertices that has been frequently used in [sociometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociometry) and the theory of [social networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_network).[^o8]

## Example {#Example}

[Butane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butane) (C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>10</sub>) has two different [structural isomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_isomers): *n*-butane, with a linear structure of four carbon atoms, and [isobutane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isobutane), with a branched structure. The chemical graph for *n*-butane is a four-vertex [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190), and the chemical graph for isobutane is a tree with one central vertex connected to three leaves.

The *n*-butane molecule has three pairs of vertices at distance one from each other, two pairs at distance two, and one pair at distance three, so its Wiener index is

$$3\times 1 + 2\times 2 + 1\times 3 = 10.$$

The isobutane molecule has three pairs of vertices at distances one from each other (the three leaf-center pairs), and three pairs at distance two (the leaf-leaf pairs). Therefore, its Wiener index is

$$3\times 1 + 3\times 2 = 9.$$

These numbers are instances of formulas for special cases of the Wiener index: it is $(n^3-n)/6$ for any $n$-vertex path graph such as the graph of *n*-butane,[^o12] and $(n-1)^2$ for any $n$-vertex [star](wikipedia://en/19310129) such as the graph of isobutane.[^o13]

Thus, even though these two molecules have the same chemical formula, and the same numbers of carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds, their different structures give rise to different Wiener indices.

:::

## Relation to chemical properties {#Relation_to_chemical_properties}

Wiener showed that the Wiener index number is closely correlated with the [boiling points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling_point) of [alkane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkane) molecules.[^o4] Later work on [quantitative structure–activity relationships](wikipedia://en/826669) showed that it is also correlated with other quantities including the parameters of its [critical point](wikipedia://en/1951419),[^o15] the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density), [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension), and [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) of its liquid phase,[^o16] and the [van der Waals surface area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waals_surface_area) of the molecule.[^o17]

## Calculation in arbitrary graphs {#Calculation_in_arbitrary_graphs}

The Wiener index may be calculated directly using an algorithm for computing all pairwise distances in the graph. When the graph is unweighted (so the length of a path is just its number of edges), these distances may be calculated by repeating a [breadth-first search](wikipedia://en/97026) algorithm, once for each starting vertex.[^o27] The total time for this approach is O(*nm*), where *n* is the number of vertices in the graph and *m* is its number of edges.

For weighted graphs, one may instead use the [Floyd–Warshall algorithm](wikipedia://en/230401)[^o27][^o19][^o20] or [Johnson's algorithm](wikipedia://en/1284311),[^o21] with running time O(*n*<sup>3</sup>) or O(*nm* + *n*<sup>2</sup> log *n*) respectively. Alternative but less efficient algorithms based on repeated [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) have also been developed within the chemical informatics literature.[^o22][^o23]

## Calculation in special types of graph {#Calculation_in_special_types_of_graph}

When the underlying graph is a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) (as is true for instance for the alkanes originally studied by Wiener), the Wiener index may be calculated more efficiently. If the graph is partitioned into two subtrees by removing a single edge *e*, then its Wiener index is the sum of the Wiener indices of the two subtrees, together with a third term representing the paths that pass through *e*. This third term may be calculated in linear time by computing the sum of distances of all vertices from *e* within each subtree and multiplying the two sums.[^o25] This [divide and conquer algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divide_and_conquer_algorithm) can be generalized from trees to graphs of bounded [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086), and leads to near-linear-time algorithms for such graphs.[^o26]

An alternative method for calculating the Wiener index of a tree, by [Bojan Mohar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bojan_Mohar) and [Tomaž Pisanski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomaž_Pisanski), works by generalizing the problem to graphs with weighted vertices, where the weight of a path is the product of its length with the weights of its two endpoints. If *v* is a leaf vertex of the tree then the Wiener index of the tree may be calculated by merging *v* with its parent (adding their weights together), computing the index of the resulting smaller tree, and adding a simple correction term for the paths that pass through the edge from *v* to its parent. By repeatedly removing leaves in this way, the Wiener index may be calculated in linear time.[^o27]

For graphs that are constructed as [products](wikipedia://en/11492935) of simpler graphs, the Wiener index of the product graph can often be computed by a simple formula that combines the indices of its factors.[^o28] [Benzenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzenoid) (graphs formed by gluing regular hexagons edge-to-edge) can be embedded [isometrically](wikipedia://en/254777) into the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/2856852) of three trees, allowing their Wiener indices to be computed in linear time by using the product formula together with the linear time tree algorithm.[^o29]

The [double graph](wikipedia://en/81397754) of a graph $G$, denoted $\mathcal{D}[G]$, has a known index in relation to $G$ itself. For the number of vertices $n$ in $G$ and the Wiener index $W(G)$:[^o30]

$$W(\mathcal{D}[G]) = 4W(G) + 2n$$

## Inverse problem {#Inverse_problem}

(Gutman Yeh 1995) considered the problem of determining which numbers can be represented as the Wiener index of a graph.[^o33] They showed that all but two positive integers have such a representation; the two exceptions are the numbers 2 and 5, which are not the Wiener index of any graph. For graphs that must be bipartite, they found that again almost all integers can be represented, with a larger set of exceptions: none of the numbers in the set

{2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 11, 12, 13, 15, 17, 19, 33, 37, 39}

can be represented as the Wiener index of a bipartite graph.

Gutman and Yeh conjectured, but were unable to prove, a similar description of the numbers that can be represented as Wiener indices of trees, with a set of 49 exceptional values:

2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 19, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27, 30, 33, 34, 37, 38, 39, 41, 43, 45, 47, 51, 53, 55, 60, 61, 69, 73, 77, 78, 83, 85, 87, 89, 91, 99, 101, 106, 113, 147, 159

The conjecture was later proven by Wagner, Wang, and Yu.[^o34][^o35]
