# Chemical graph theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chemical graph theory"}

**Chemical graph theory** is the [topology](wikipedia://en/30275938) branch of [mathematical chemistry](wikipedia://en/1570072) which applies [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) to [mathematical modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_modelling) of chemical phenomena.[^o2] The pioneers of chemical graph theory are [Alexandru Balaban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandru_Balaban), [Ante Graovac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ante_Graovac), [Iván Gutman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iván_Gutman), [Haruo Hosoya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haruo_Hosoya), [Milan Randić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan_Randić) and [Nenad Trinajstić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nenad_Trinajstić)[^o3] (also [Harry Wiener](wikipedia://en/14223173) and others). In 1988, it was reported that several hundred researchers worked in this area, producing about 500 articles annually. A number of monographs have been written in the area, including the two-volume comprehensive text by Trinajstić, *Chemical Graph Theory*, that summarized the field up to mid-1980s.[^o4]

:::

The adherents of the theory maintain that the properties of a [chemical graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_graph) (i.e., a graph-theoretical representation of a [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule)) give valuable insights into the chemical phenomena. Others contend that graphs play only a fringe role in chemical research.[^o5] One variant of the theory is the representation of materials as infinite [Euclidean graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_graph), particularly crystals by [periodic graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_Graphs_%28Crystallography%29).
