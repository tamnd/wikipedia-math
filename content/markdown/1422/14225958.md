# Q-function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Q-function"}

*Image: Q-function.png.* A plot of the Q-function.

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Q-function** is the [tail distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793#Complementary_cumulative_distribution_function_%28tail_distribution%29) of the [standard normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462#Standard_normal_distribution).[^o2][^o3] In other words, $Q(x)$ is the probability that a normal (Gaussian) [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) will obtain a value larger than $x$ standard deviations. Equivalently, $Q(x)$ is the probability that a standard normal random variable takes a value larger than $x$.

:::

If $Y$ is a Gaussian random variable with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then $X = \frac{Y-\mu}{\sigma}$ is [standard normal](wikipedia://en/21462#Standard_normal_distribution) and

$$P(Y > y) = P(X > x) = Q(x)$$

where $x = \frac{y-\mu}{\sigma}$.

Other definitions of the *Q*-function, all of which are simple transformations of the normal [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793), are also used occasionally.[^o4]

Because of its relation to the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of the normal distribution, the *Q*-function can also be expressed in terms of the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111), which is an important function in applied mathematics and physics.

## Definition and basic properties {#Definition_and_basic_properties}

Formally, the *Q*-function is defined as

$$Q(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_x^\infty \exp\left(-\frac{u^2}{2}\right) \, du.$$

Thus,

$$Q(x) = 1 - Q(-x) = 1 - \Phi(x)\,\!,$$

where $\Phi(x)$ is the [cumulative distribution function of the standard normal Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution#Cumulative_distribution_function).

The *Q*-function can be expressed in terms of the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111), or the complementary error function, as[^o3]

$$\begin{aligned} Q(x) &=\frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{x/\sqrt{2}}^\infty \exp\left(-t^2\right) \, dt \right)\\ &= \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{erf} \left( \frac{x}{\sqrt{2}} \right) ~~\text{ -or-}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{erfc} \left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}} \right). \end{aligned}$$

An alternative form of the *Q*-function known as Craig's formula, after its discoverer, is expressed as:[^o6]

$$Q(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \exp \left( - \frac{x^2}{2 \sin^2 \theta} \right) d\theta.$$

The above proper integral form of Q-function, has been incorrectly credited to Craig. This form of Q-function was implied in earlier works by Wiesten[^o7] , and explicitly stated by Pawula, Rice and Roberts[^o8].

This expression is valid only for positive values of *x*, but it can be used in conjunction with *Q*(*x*) = 1 − *Q*(−*x*) to obtain *Q*(*x*) for negative values. This form is advantageous in that the range of integration is fixed and finite.

Craig's formula was later extended by Behnad (2020)[^o9] for the *Q*-function of the sum of two non-negative variables, as follows:

*Image: Q function complex plot plotted with Mathematica 13.1 ComplexPlot3D.svg.* the Q-function plotted in the complex plane

$$Q(x+y) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \exp \left( - \frac{x^2}{2 \sin^2 \theta} - \frac{y^2}{2 \cos^2 \theta} \right) d\theta, \quad x,y \geqslant 0 .$$

## Bounds and approximations {#Bounds_and_approximations}

- The *Q*-function is not an [elementary function](wikipedia://en/10412). However, it can be upper and lower bounded as,[^o11][^o12]

$$\left (\frac{x}{1+x^2} \right ) \phi(x) < Q(x) < \frac{\phi(x)}{x}, \qquad x>0,$$

where $\phi(x)$ is the density function of the standard normal distribution, and the bounds become increasingly tight for large *x*.

Using the [substitution](wikipedia://en/193748) *v* =*u*<sup>2</sup>/2, the upper bound is derived as follows:

$$Q(x) =\int_x^\infty\phi(u)\,du <\int_x^\infty\frac ux\phi(u)\,du =\int_{\frac{x^2}{2}}^\infty\frac{e^{-v}}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dv=-\biggl.\frac{e^{-v}}{x\sqrt{2\pi}}\biggr|_{\frac{x^2}{2}}^\infty=\frac{\phi(x)}{x}.$$

Similarly, using $\phi'(u) = - u \phi(u)$ and the [quotient rule](wikipedia://en/209128),

$$\left(1+\frac1{x^2}\right)Q(x) =\int_x^\infty \left(1+\frac1{x^2}\right)\phi(u)\,du >\int_x^\infty \left(1+\frac1{u^2}\right)\phi(u)\,du =-\biggl.\frac{\phi(u)}u\biggr|_x^\infty =\frac{\phi(x)}x.$$

Solving for *Q*(*x*) provides the lower bound.

The [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of the upper and lower bound gives a suitable approximation for $Q(x)$:

$$Q(x) \approx \frac{\phi(x)}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}, \qquad x \geq 0.$$

- Tighter bounds and approximations of $Q(x)$ can also be obtained by optimizing the following expression [^o12]

$$\tilde{Q}(x) = \frac{\phi(x)}{(1-a)x + a\sqrt{x^2 + b}}.$$

For $x \geq 0$, the best upper bound is given by $a = 0.344$ and $b = 5.334$ with maximum absolute relative error of 0.44%. Likewise, the best approximation is given by $a = 0.339$ and $b = 5.510$ with maximum absolute relative error of 0.27%. Finally, the best lower bound is given by $a = 1/\pi$ and $b = 2 \pi$ with maximum absolute relative error of 1.17%.

- The [Chernoff bound](wikipedia://en/741759) of the *Q*-function is

$$Q(x)\leq e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}, \qquad x>0$$

- Improved exponential bounds and a pure exponential approximation are [^o13]

$$Q(x)\leq \tfrac{1}{4}e^{-x^2}+\tfrac{1}{4}e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}} \leq \tfrac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}, \qquad x>0$$

$$Q(x)\approx \frac{1}{12}e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}+\frac{1}{4}e^{-\frac{2}{3} x^2}, \qquad x>0$$

- The above were generalized by Tanash & Riihonen (2020),[^o14] who showed that $Q(x)$ can be accurately approximated or bounded by

$$\tilde{Q}(x) = \sum_{n=1}^N a_n e^{-b_n x^2}.$$

In particular, they presented a systematic methodology to solve the numerical coefficients $\{(a_n,b_n)\}_{n=1}^N$ that yield a [minimax](wikipedia://en/4882595) approximation or bound: $Q(x) \approx \tilde{Q}(x)$, $Q(x) \leq \tilde{Q}(x)$, or $Q(x) \geq \tilde{Q}(x)$ for $x\geq0$. With the example coefficients tabulated in the paper for $N = 20$, the relative and absolute approximation errors are less than $2.831 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and $1.416 \cdot 10^{-6}$, respectively. The coefficients $\{(a_n,b_n)\}_{n=1}^N$ for many variations of the exponential approximations and bounds up to $N = 25$ have been released to open access as a comprehensive dataset.[^o15]

- Another approximation of $Q(x)$ for $x \in [0,\infty)$ is given by [Karagiannidis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Karagiannidis) & Lioumpas (2007)[^o16] who showed for the appropriate choice of parameters $\{A, B\}$ that

$$f(x; A, B) = \frac{\left(1 - e^{-Ax}\right)e^{-x^2}}{B\sqrt{\pi} x} \approx \operatorname{erfc} \left(x\right).$$

The absolute error between $f(x; A, B)$ and $\operatorname{erfc}(x)$ over the range $[0, R]$ is minimized by evaluating

$$\{A, B\} = \underset{\{A,B\}}{\arg \min} \frac{1}{R} \int_0^R | f(x; A, B) - \operatorname{erfc}(x) |dx.$$

Using $R = 20$ and numerically integrating, they found the minimum error occurred when $\{A, B\} = \{1.98, 1.135\},$ which gave a good approximation for $\forall x \ge 0.$

Substituting these values and using the relationship between $Q(x)$ and $\operatorname{erfc}(x)$ from above gives

$$Q(x)\approx\frac{\left( 1-e^{\frac{-1.98x} {\sqrt{2}}}\right) e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{1.135\sqrt{2\pi}x}, x \ge 0.$$

Alternative coefficients are also available for the above 'Karagiannidis–Lioumpas approximation' for tailoring accuracy for a specific application or transforming it into a tight bound.[^o17]

- A tighter and more tractable approximation of $Q(x)$ for positive arguments $x \in [0,\infty)$ is given by López-Benítez & Casadevall (2011)[^o18] based on a second-order exponential function:

$$Q(x) \approx e^{-ax^2-bx-c}, \qquad x \ge 0.$$

The fitting coefficients $(a,b,c)$ can be optimized over any desired range of arguments in order to minimize the sum of square errors ($a = 0.3842$, $b = 0.7640$, $c = 0.6964$ for $x \in [0,20]$) or minimize the maximum absolute error ($a = 0.4920$, $b = 0.2887$, $c = 1.1893$ for $x \in [0,20]$). This approximation offers some benefits such as a good trade-off between accuracy and analytical tractability (for example, the extension to any arbitrary power of $Q(x)$ is trivial and does not alter the algebraic form of the approximation).

- A pair of tight lower and upper bounds on the Gaussian Q-function for positive arguments $x \in [0, \infty)$ was introduced by Abreu (2012)[^o19] based on a simple [algebraic expression](wikipedia://en/4283745) with only two exponential terms:

$$Q(x) \geq \frac{1}{12} e^{-x^2} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi} (x + 1)} e^{-x^2 / 2}, \qquad x \geq 0,$$

$$Q(x) \leq \frac{1}{50} e^{-x^2} + \frac{1}{2 (x + 1)} e^{-x^2 / 2}, \qquad x \geq 0.$$

These bounds are derived from a unified form $Q_{\mathrm{B}}(x; a, b) = \frac{\exp(-x^2)}{a} + \frac{\exp(-x^2 / 2)}{b (x + 1)}$, where the parameters $a$ and $b$ are chosen to satisfy specific conditions ensuring the lower ($a_{\mathrm{L}} = 12$, $b_{\mathrm{L}} = \sqrt{2\pi}$) and upper ($a_{\mathrm{U}} = 50$, $b_{\mathrm{U}} = 2$) bounding properties. The resulting expressions are notable for their simplicity and tightness, offering a favorable trade-off between accuracy and mathematical tractability. These bounds are particularly useful in theoretical analysis, such as in communication theory over fading channels. Additionally, they can be extended to bound $Q^n(x)$ for positive integers $n$ using the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677), maintaining their simplicity and effectiveness.

## Inverse *Q* {#Inverse_Q}

The inverse *Q*-function can be related to the [inverse error functions](wikipedia://en/177111#Inverse_functions):

$$Q^{-1}(y) = \sqrt{2}\ \mathrm{erf}^{-1}(1-2y) = \sqrt{2}\ \mathrm{erfc}^{-1}(2y)$$

The function $Q^{-1}(y)$ finds application in digital communications. It is usually expressed in [dB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibel#Field_quantities_and_root-power_quantities) and generally called **Q-factor**:

$$\mathrm{Q\text{-}factor} = 20 \log_{10}\!\left(Q^{-1}(y)\right)\!~\mathrm{dB}$$

where *y* is the bit-error rate (BER) of the digitally modulated signal under analysis. For instance, for [quadrature phase-shift keying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_phase-shift_keying) (QPSK) in [additive white Gaussian noise](wikipedia://en/202097), the Q-factor defined above coincides with the value in dB of the [signal to noise ratio](wikipedia://en/41706#Decibels) that yields a [bit error rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_error_rate) equal to *y*.

## Generalization to high dimensions {#Generalization_to_high_dimensions}

The *Q*-function can be generalized to higher dimensions:[^o27]

$$Q(\mathbf{x})= \mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\geq \mathbf{x}),$$

where $\mathbf{X}\sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\, \Sigma)$ follows the [multivariate normal distribution](wikipedia://en/50347) with covariance $\Sigma$ and the threshold is of the form $\mathbf{x}=\gamma\Sigma\mathbf{l}^*$ for some positive vector $\mathbf{l}^*>\mathbf{0}$ and positive constant $\gamma>0$. As in the one dimensional case, there is no simple analytical formula for the *Q*-function. Nevertheless, the *Q*-function can be [approximated arbitrarily well](http://www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/53796) as $\gamma$ becomes larger and larger.[^o28][^o29]
