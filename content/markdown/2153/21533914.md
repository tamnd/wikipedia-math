# Regular map (graph theory)

*Image: Hexagonal Hosohedron.svg.* The hexagonal [hosohedron](wikipedia://en/2316192), a regular map on the sphere with two vertices, six edges, six faces, and 24 flags.

*Image: Regmap-6340.png.* The regular map {6,3}<sub>*4*,*0*</sub> on the torus with 16 faces, 32 vertices and 48 edges.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular map"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **regular map** is a symmetric [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) of a closed [surface](wikipedia://en/27865). More precisely, a regular map is a [decomposition](wikipedia://en/1227329) of a two-dimensional [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) (such as a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), or [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325)) into topological disks such that every [flag](wikipedia://en/9352497) (an incident vertex-edge-face triple) can be transformed into any other flag by a [symmetry](wikipedia://en/56661772) of the decomposition. Regular maps are, in a sense, topological generalizations of [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905). The theory of maps and their classification is related to the theory of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), and [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316). Regular maps are classified according to either: the [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) and [orientability](wikipedia://en/187446) of the supporting surface, the [underlying graph](wikipedia://en/8149170), or the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Regular maps are typically defined and studied in three ways: topologically, group-theoretically, and graph-theoretically.

### Topological approach {#Topological_approach}

Topologically, a map is a [2-cell decomposition](wikipedia://en/25884400) of a compact connected 2-manifold.[^o4]

The genus g, of a map M is given by [Euler's relation](wikipedia://en/148420) $\chi (M) = |V| - |E| +|F|$ which is equal to $2 -2g$ if the map is orientable, and $2 - g$ if the map is non-orientable. It is a crucial fact that there is a finite (non-zero) number of regular maps for every orientable genus except the torus.

### Group-theoretical approach {#Group-theoretical_approach}

Group-theoretically, the permutation representation of a regular map *M* is a transitive [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) *C*, on a set $\Omega$ of [flags](wikipedia://en/9352497), generated by three fixed-point free involutions *r*<sub>0</sub>, *r*<sub>1</sub>, *r*<sub>2</sub> satisfying (r<sub>0</sub>r<sub>2</sub>)<sup>2</sup>= I. In this definition the faces are the orbits of *F* = *\<*r<sub>0</sub>, *r*<sub>1</sub>>, edges are the orbits of *E* = \<*r*<sub>0</sub>, *r*<sub>2</sub>>, and vertices are the orbits of *V* = \<*r*<sub>1</sub>, *r*<sub>2</sub>>. More abstractly, the automorphism group of any regular map is the non-degenerate, homomorphic image of a \<2,m,n>-[triangle group](wikipedia://en/2069342).

### Graph-theoretical approach {#Graph-theoretical_approach}

Graph-theoretically, a map is a cubic graph $\Gamma$ with edges coloured blue, yellow, red such that: $\Gamma$ is connected, every vertex is incident to one edge of each colour, and cycles of edges not coloured yellow have length 4. Note that $\Gamma$ is the *flag graph* or [graph-encoded map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph-encoded_map) (GEM) of the map, defined on the vertex set of flags $\Omega$ and is not the skeleton G = (V,E) of the map. In general, |$\Omega$| = 4|E|.

A map M is regular if Aut(M) [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) [regularly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_action) on the flags. Aut(*M*) of a regular map is transitive on the vertices, edges, and faces of *M*. A map *M* is said to be reflexible iff Aut(*M*) is regular and contains an automorphism $\phi$ that fixes both a vertex *v* and a face *f*, but reverses the order of the edges. A map which is regular but not reflexible is said to be [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160).

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Hemicube.svg.* The hemicube, a regular map.

- The [great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441) is a regular map with pentagonal faces in the orientable surface of genus 4.
- The [hemicube](wikipedia://en/10431815) is a regular map of type {4,3} in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350).
- The [hemi-dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/10393833) is a regular map produced by pentagonal embedding of the Petersen graph in the projective plane.
- The p-[hosohedron](wikipedia://en/2316192) is a regular map of type {2,p}.
- The [Dyck map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyck_map) is a regular map of 12 octagons on a genus-3 surface. Its underlying graph, the [Dyck graph](wikipedia://en/24041180), can also form a regular map of 16 hexagons in a torus.

Regular maps exist as torohedral polyhedra as finite portions of Euclidean tilings, wrapped onto the surface of a [duocylinder](wikipedia://en/2114445) as a [flat torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_torus). These are labeled {4,4}<sub>*b*,*c*</sub> for those related to the [square tiling](wikipedia://en/2863809), {4,4}.[^o14] {3,6}<sub>*b*,*c*</sub> are related to the [triangular tiling](wikipedia://en/2863858), {3,6}, and {6,3}<sub>*b*,*c*</sub> related to the [hexagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/2863719), {6,3}. *b* and *c* are [whole numbers](wikipedia://en/21474).[^o15] There are 2 special cases (*b*,0) and (*b*,*b*) with reflective symmetry, while the general cases exist in chiral pairs (*b*,*c*) and (*c*,*b*).

Regular maps of the form {4,4}<sub>*m*,0</sub> can be represented as the finite [regular skew polyhedron](wikipedia://en/19193037) {4,4 | *m*}, seen as the square faces of a *m*×*m* [duoprism](wikipedia://en/2114155) in 4-dimensions.

Here's an example {4,4}<sub>8,0</sub> mapped from a plane as a [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) to a cylinder section to a torus. The projection from a cylinder to a torus distorts the geometry in 3 dimensions, but can be done without distortion in 4-dimensions.

In generally regular toroidal polyhedra {*p*,*q*}<sub>*b*,*c*</sub> can be defined if either *p* or *q* are even, although only euclidean ones above can exist as toroidal polyhedra in 4-dimensions. In {2*p*,*q*}, the paths (*b*,*c*) can be defined as stepping face-edge-face in straight lines, while the dual {*p*,2*q*} forms will see the paths (*b*,*c*) as stepping vertex-edge-vertex in straight lines.
