# Specker sequence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Specker sequence"}

*Image: SuiteSpecker.svg.* A Specker sequence. The *n*<sup>th</sup> digit of x<sub>*k*</sub> is **4** if *n* ≤ *k* and the *n*th [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403) in a computable [Gödel numbering](wikipedia://en/207790) halts on input *n* after *k* steps; otherwise it is **3**.

In [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414), a **Specker sequence** is a [computable](wikipedia://en/1139338), [monotonically increasing](wikipedia://en/27838#Increasing_and_decreasing), [bounded](wikipedia://en/27838#Bounded) [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) whose [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) is not a [computable real number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computable_real_number). The first example of such a sequence was constructed by [Ernst Specker](wikipedia://en/22209158).[^n1]

:::

The existence of Specker sequences has consequences for [computable analysis](wikipedia://en/21535996). The fact that such sequences exist means that the collection of all computable real numbers does not satisfy the [least upper bound principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound_principle) of real analysis, even when considering only computable sequences. A common way to resolve this difficulty is to consider only sequences that are accompanied by a [modulus of convergence](wikipedia://en/21536099); no Specker sequence has a computable modulus of convergence. More generally, a Specker sequence is called a *recursive counterexample* to the least upper bound principle, i.e. a construction that shows that this theorem is false when restricted to computable reals.

The least upper bound principle has also been analyzed in the program of [reverse mathematics](wikipedia://en/326365), where the exact strength of this principle has been determined. In the terminology of that program, the least upper bound principle is equivalent to ACA<sub>0</sub> over RCA<sub>0</sub>. In fact, the proof of the forward implication, i.e. that the least upper bound principle implies ACA<sub>0</sub>, is readily obtained from the textbook proof (see Simpson, 1999) of the non-computability of the supremum in the least upper bound principle.

## Construction 1 {#Construction_1}

One way to construct a Specker sequence is as follows:[^n2] let *A* be any [recursively enumerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable) set of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) that is not [decidable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decidable_set), and let (*a*<sub>*i*</sub>) be a computable enumeration of *A* without repetition. Define a sequence (*q*<sub>*n*</sub>) of rational numbers with the rule

$$q_n = \sum_{i = 0}^n 2^{-a_i - 1}.$$

It is clear that each *q*<sub>*n*</sub> is nonnegative and rational, and that the sequence *q*<sub>*n*</sub> is strictly increasing. Moreover, because *a*<sub>*i*</sub> has no repetition, it is possible to estimate each *q*<sub>*n*</sub> against the series

$$\sum_{i = 0}^\infty 2^{-i-1} = 1.$$

Thus the sequence (*q*<sub>*n*</sub>) is bounded above by 1. Classically, this means that *q*<sub>*n*</sub> has a supremum *x*.

It can be shown that $x$ is not a [computable real number](wikipedia://en/6206). Suppose, to the contrary, that $x$ were computable. Then the sequence $(q_i)$ would have a computable [modulus of convergence](wikipedia://en/21536099): there would be a computable function $r(n)$ such that

$$|q_j-q_i|<\frac{1}{n}$$

for all $i,j>r(n)$. Such a function can be obtained by approximating $x$ and comparing it with successive terms of $(q_i)$. Since each new term in the enumeration changes a single digit in the binary expansion, a sufficiently accurate approximation to $x$ determines a stage after which the relevant initial segment can no longer change.

If such a function $r$ were computable, it would provide a [decision procedure](wikipedia://en/8336) for $A$. Given $k$, compute $r(2^{k+1})$. If $k$ were later enumerated into $A$, the corresponding term would increase the sequence by $2^{-k-1}$. This cannot occur after all terms with indices greater than $r(2^{k+1})$ are within $2^{-k-1}$ of one another. Therefore, if $k$ belongs to $A$, it must appear among the first $r(2^{k+1})$ terms of the enumeration. These finitely many terms can be checked effectively, yielding a decision procedure for $A$. This contradicts the assumption that $A$ is not [decidable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decidable_set), so $x$ is not computable.

## Construction 2 {#Construction_2}

Another construction of a Specker sequence (*x<sub>m</sub>*) can be given using an [enumeration](wikipedia://en/212115) of [Turing machines](wikipedia://en/30403). Let {*n*}(*n*) denote the action of the *n*-th Turing machine on the number *n*. Then let the *n*-th [decimal digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_digit) of *x<sub>m</sub>* be 4 if *m* > *n* and the computation {*n*}(*n*) halts within *m* − *n* steps. If the computation has not finished after *m* − *n* steps (or if *m* \< *n*), the digit is 3. (See the figure above.)

- Each *x<sub>m</sub>* is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024), since it has a [periodic decimal expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_expansion): after the first *m* decimal places, all digits are 3.
- The sequence is computable, since to produce *x<sub>m+1</sub>* after having calculated *x<sub>m</sub>*, one only has to simulate *m* + 1 Turing machines running for one step each.
- The sequence is increasing, because when passing from *x<sub>m</sub>* to *x<sub>m+1</sub>*, digits can only change from 3 to 4.
- The sequence is bounded, because it never exceeds 0.4444444... = 4/9.

The limit of this sequence is not a [computable real number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computable_real_number), since its decimal expansion contains a 4 in its *n*-th decimal place if the computation {*n*}(*n*) halts, and a 3 otherwise. Determining the digits of the limit would therefore solve the [Halting problem](wikipedia://en/21391870), which is impossible.
