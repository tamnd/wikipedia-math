# Rips machine

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rips machine"}

In [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), the **Rips machine** is a method of studying the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) on [**R**-trees](wikipedia://en/371914). It was introduced in unpublished work of [Eliyahu Rips](wikipedia://en/673693) in about 1991.

:::

An **R**-tree is a uniquely [arcwise-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcwise-connected) [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) in which every arc is isometric to some real interval. Rips proved the conjecture of Morgan and Shalen[^o2] that any [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) acting freely on an **R**-tree is a [free product](wikipedia://en/360036) of free abelian and surface groups.[^o3]

## Actions of surface groups on R-trees {#Actions_of_surface_groups_on_R-trees}

By [Bass–Serre theory](wikipedia://en/18673708), a group acting freely on a simplicial tree is free. This is no longer true for **R**-trees, as Morgan and Shalen showed that the fundamental groups of surfaces of [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) less than −1 also act freely on a **R**-trees.[^o2] They proved that the fundamental group of a connected closed surface S acts freely on an R-tree if and only if S is not one of the 3 nonorientable surfaces of Euler characteristic ≥−1.

::: {#o6 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The Rips machine assigns to a stable isometric action of a finitely generated group *G* a certain "normal form" approximation of that action by a stable action of *G* on a simplicial tree and hence a splitting of *G* in the sense of Bass–Serre theory. Group actions on [real trees](wikipedia://en/371914) arise naturally in several contexts in [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732): for example as boundary points of the [Teichmüller space](wikipedia://en/1835661)[^o7] (every point in the Thurston boundary of the Teichmüller space is represented by a measured geodesic lamination on the surface; this lamination lifts to the universal cover of the surface and a naturally dual object to that lift is an $\mathbb R$-tree endowed with an isometric action of the fundamental group of the surface), as [Gromov-Hausdorff limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gromov-Hausdorff_convergence) of, appropriately rescaled, [Kleinian group](wikipedia://en/1362652) actions,[^o8][^o9] and so on. The use of $\mathbb R$-trees machinery provides substantial shortcuts in modern proofs of Thurston's [hyperbolization theorem](wikipedia://en/9155837) for [Haken 3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/236567).[^o9][^o10] Similarly, $\mathbb R$-trees play a key role in the study of [Culler](wikipedia://en/29258614)-[Vogtmann](wikipedia://en/20455422)'s Outer space[^o11][^o12] as well as in other areas of [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164); for example, [asymptotic cones](wikipedia://en/18609360#Asymptotic_cones) of groups often have a tree-like structure and give rise to group actions on [real trees](wikipedia://en/371914).[^o13][^o14] The use of $\mathbb R$-trees, together with Bass–Serre theory, is a key tool in the work of Sela on solving the isomorphism problem for (torsion-free) [word-hyperbolic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group), Sela's version of the JSJ-decomposition theory and the work of Sela on the Tarski Conjecture for free groups and the theory of [limit groups](wikipedia://en/78938535).[^o15][^o16]
