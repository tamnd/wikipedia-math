# Detrended fluctuation analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Detrended fluctuation analysis"}

In [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes), [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295) and [time series analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series_analysis), **detrended fluctuation analysis** (**DFA**) is a method for determining the statistical [self-affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-affinity) of a signal. It is useful for analysing [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) that appear to be [long-memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long-memory) processes (diverging [correlation time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_time), e.g. power-law decaying [autocorrelation function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocorrelation_function)) or [1/f noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1/f_noise).

:::

The obtained exponent is similar to the [Hurst exponent](wikipedia://en/7847320), except that DFA may also be applied to signals whose underlying statistics (such as mean and variance) or dynamics are [non-stationary](wikipedia://en/329898) (changing with time). It is related to measures based upon spectral techniques such as [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) and [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247).

[Peng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chung-Kang_Peng) et al. introduced DFA in 1994 in a paper that has been cited over 3,000 times as of 2022[^o2] and represents an extension of the (ordinary) [fluctuation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluctuation_analysis) (FA), which is affected by non-stationarities.

Systematic studies of the advantages and limitations of the DFA method were performed by PCh Ivanov et al. in a series of papers focusing on the effects of different types of nonstationarities in real-world signals: (1) types of trends;[^o3] (2) random outliers/spikes, noisy segments, signals composed of parts with different correlation;[^o4] (3) nonlinear filters;[^o5] (4) [missing data](wikipedia://en/7859676);[^o6] (5) signal coarse-graining procedures[^o7] and comparing DFA performance with [moving average](wikipedia://en/990809) techniques[^o8] (cumulative citations > 4,000). Datasets[^o9] generated to test DFA are available on PhysioNet.[^o10]

### Algorithm {#Algorithm}

Given: a [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) $x_1, x_2, ..., x_N$.

Compute its average value $\langle x\rangle = \frac 1N \sum_{t=1}^N x_t$.

Sum it into a process $X_t=\sum_{i=1}^t (x_i-\langle x\rangle)$. This is the **cumulative sum**, or **profile**, of the original time series. For example, the profile of an [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d.) [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182) is a standard [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451).

Select a set $T = \{n_1, ..., n_k\}$ of integers, such that $n_1 < n_2 < \cdots < n_k$, the smallest $n_1 \approx 4$, the largest $n_k \approx N/4<ref></ref>$, and the sequence is roughly distributed evenly in log-scale: $\log(n_2) - \log(n_1) \approx \log(n_3) - \log(n_2) \approx \cdots$. In other words, it is approximately a [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670).[^o15]

For each $n \in T$, divide the sequence $X_t$ into consecutive segments of length $n$. Within each segment, compute the [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) straight-line fit (the **local trend**). Let $Y_{1,n}, Y_{2,n}, ..., Y_{N,n}$ be the resulting piecewise-linear fit.

Compute the [root-mean-square deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root-mean-square_deviation) from the local trend (**local fluctuation**):

$$F( n, i) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t = in+1}^{in+n} \left( X_t - Y_{t, n} \right)^2}.$$

And their root-mean-square is the total fluctuation:

$$F( n ) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N/n}\sum_{i = 1}^{N/n} F(n, i)^2}.$$

(If $N$ is not divisible by $n$, then one can either discard the remainder of the sequence, or repeat the procedure on the reversed sequence, then take their root-mean-square.[^o16])

Make the [log-log plot](wikipedia://en/1659215) $\log n - \log F(n)$.[^o17][^o18]

:::

### Interpretation {#Interpretation}

A straight line of slope $\alpha$ on the log-log plot indicates a statistical [self-affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-affinity) of form $F(n) \propto n^{\alpha}$. Since $F(n)$ monotonically increases with $n$, we always have $\alpha > 0$.

The scaling exponent $\alpha$ is a generalization of the [Hurst exponent](wikipedia://en/7847320), with the precise value giving information about the series self-correlations:

- $\alpha<1/2$: anti-correlated
- $\alpha \simeq 1/2$: uncorrelated, [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182)
- $\alpha>1/2$: correlated
- $\alpha\simeq 1$: 1/f-noise, [pink noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_noise)
- $\alpha>1$: non-stationary, unbounded
- $\alpha\simeq 3/2$: [Brownian noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_noise)

Because the expected displacement in an [uncorrelated random walk](wikipedia://en/235451#Correlated_steps) of length N grows like $\sqrt{N}$, an exponent of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ would correspond to uncorrelated white noise. When the exponent is between 0 and 1, the result is [fractional Gaussian noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_Gaussian_noise).

### Pitfalls in interpretation {#Pitfalls_in_interpretation}

Though the DFA algorithm always produces a positive number $\alpha$ for any time series, it does not necessarily imply that the time series is self-similar. [Self-similarity](wikipedia://en/28782) requires the log-log graph to be sufficiently linear over a wide range of $n$. Furthermore, a combination of techniques including [maximum likelihood estimation](wikipedia://en/140806) (MLE), rather than least-squares has been shown to better approximate the scaling, or power-law, exponent.[^o21]

Also, there are many scaling exponent-like quantities that can be measured for a self-similar time series, including the divider dimension and [Hurst exponent](wikipedia://en/7847320). Therefore, the DFA scaling exponent $\alpha$ is not a [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907), and does not have certain desirable properties that the [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) has, though in certain special cases it is related to the [box-counting dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box-counting_dimension) for the graph of a time series.

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Generalization to polynomial trends (higher order DFA) {#Generalization_to_polynomial_trends_(higher_order_DFA)}

The standard DFA algorithm given above removes a linear trend in each segment. If we remove a degree-n polynomial trend in each segment, it is called DFAn, or higher order DFA.[^o25]

Since $X_t$ is a cumulative sum of $x_t-\langle x\rangle$, a linear trend in $X_t$ is a constant trend in $x_t-\langle x\rangle$, which is a constant trend in $x_t$ (visible as short sections of "flat plateaus"). In this regard, DFA1 removes the mean from segments of the time series $x_t$ before quantifying the fluctuation.

Similarly, a degree n trend in $X_t$ is a degree (n-1) trend in $x_t$. For example, DFA1 removes linear trends from segments of the time series $x_t$ before quantifying the fluctuation, DFA1 removes parabolic trends from $x_t$, and so on.

The Hurst [R/S analysis](wikipedia://en/10625394) removes constant trends in the original sequence and thus, in its detrending it is equivalent to DFA1.

::: {#o27 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="MF-DFA"}

### Generalization to different moments (multifractal DFA) {#Generalization_to_different_moments_(multifractal_DFA)}

DFA can be generalized by computing

$$F_q( n ) = \left(\frac{1}{N/n}\sum_{i = 1}^{N/n} F(n, i)^q\right)^{1/q}$$

then making the log-log plot of $\log n - \log F_q(n)$, If there is a strong linearity in the plot of $\log n - \log F_q(n)$, then that slope is $\alpha(q)$.[^o28] DFA is the special case where $q=2$.

Multifractal systems scale as a function $F_q(n) \propto n^{\alpha(q)}$. Essentially, the scaling exponents need not be independent of the scale of the system. In particular, DFA measures the scaling-behavior of the second moment-fluctuations.

Kantelhardt et al. intended this scaling exponent as a generalization of the classical Hurst exponent. The classical Hurst exponent corresponds to $H=\alpha(2)$ for stationary cases, and $H=\alpha(2)-1$ for nonstationary cases.[^o28][^o29][^o45]

:::

::: {#o31 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The DFA method has been applied to many systems, e.g. DNA sequences;[^o32][^o33] heartbeat dynamics in sleep and wake,[^o34] sleep stages,[^o35][^o36] rest and exercise,[^o37][^o38] and across circadian phases;[^o39][^o40] locomotor gate and wrist dynamics,[^o41][^o42][^o43][^o44] neuronal oscillations,[^o45] speech pathology detection,[^o46] and animal behavior pattern analysis.[^o47][^o48]

:::

## Relations to other methods, for specific types of signal {#Relations_to_other_methods,_for_specific_types_of_signal}

::: {#o51 .statement cue=named-heading name="For signals with power-law-decaying autocorrelation"}

### For signals with power-law-decaying autocorrelation {#For_signals_with_power-law-decaying_autocorrelation}

In the case of power-law decaying auto-correlations, the [correlation function](wikipedia://en/59500109) decays with an exponent $\gamma$: $C(L)\sim L^{-\gamma}\!\$. In addition the [power spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_spectrum) decays as $P(f)\sim f^{-\beta}\!\$. The three exponents are related by:[^o32]

- $$\gamma=2-2\alpha$$
- $\beta=2\alpha-1$ and
- $$\gamma=1-\beta.$$

The relations can be derived using the [Wiener–Khinchin theorem](wikipedia://en/3350111). The relation of DFA to the power spectrum method has been well studied.[^o52]

Thus, $\alpha$ is tied to the slope of the power spectrum $\beta$ and is used to describe the [color of noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_of_noise) by this relationship: $\alpha = (\beta+1)/2$.

:::

### For fractional Gaussian noise {#For_fractional_Gaussian_noise}

For [fractional Gaussian noise](wikipedia://en/1607785) (FGN), we have $\beta \in [-1,1]$, and thus $\alpha \in [0,1]$, and $\beta = 2H-1$, where $H$ is the [Hurst exponent](wikipedia://en/7847320). $\alpha$ for FGN is equal to $H$.[^o54]

### For fractional Brownian motion {#For_fractional_Brownian_motion}

For [fractional Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/1607785) (FBM), we have $\beta \in [1,3]$, and thus $\alpha \in [1,2]$, and $\beta = 2H+1$, where $H$ is the [Hurst exponent](wikipedia://en/7847320). $\alpha$ for FBM is equal to $H+1$.[^o29] In this context, FBM is the cumulative sum or the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of FGN, thus, the exponents of their power spectra differ by 2.
