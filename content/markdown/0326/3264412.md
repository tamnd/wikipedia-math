# Pseudo-arc

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudo-arc"}

In [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649), the **pseudo-arc** is the simplest nondegenerate [hereditarily](wikipedia://en/13552465#In_topology) [indecomposable continuum](wikipedia://en/3261244). The pseudo-arc is an arc-like [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325) continuum, and played a central role in the classification of homogeneous planar continua. [R. H. Bing](wikipedia://en/967353) proved that, in a certain well-defined sense, most continua in $\mathbb{R}^n,$ $n \ge 2$, are [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to the pseudo-arc.

:::

## History {#History}

In 1920, [Bronisław Knaster](wikipedia://en/1003125) and [Kazimierz Kuratowski](wikipedia://en/46075) asked whether a nondegenerate homogeneous continuum in the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ must be a [Jordan curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve). In 1921, [Stefan Mazurkiewicz](wikipedia://en/629203) asked whether a nondegenerate continuum in $\mathbb{R}^2$ that is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to each of its nondegenerate subcontinua must be an arc. In 1922, Knaster discovered the first example of a hereditarily indecomposable continuum $K$, later named the pseudo-arc, giving a negative answer to a Mazurkiewicz question. In 1948, [R. H. Bing](wikipedia://en/967353) proved that Knaster's continuum is homogeneous, i.e. for any two of its points there is a homeomorphism taking one to the other. Yet also in 1948, [Edwin Moise](wikipedia://en/19236494) showed that Knaster's continuum is homeomorphic to each of its non-degenerate subcontinua. Due to its resemblance to the fundamental property of the arc, namely, being homeomorphic to all its nondegenerate subcontinua, Moise called his example $M$ a **pseudo-arc**. Bing's construction is a modification of Moise's construction of $M$, which he had first heard described in a lecture. In 1951, Bing proved that all hereditarily indecomposable arc-like continua are homeomorphic — this implies that Knaster's $K$, Moise's $M$, and Bing's $B$ are all homeomorphic. Bing also proved that the pseudo-arc is typical among the continua in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of dimension at least 2 or an infinite-dimensional separable [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). Bing and [F. Burton Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Burton_Jones) constructed a decomposable planar continuum that admits an [open map](wikipedia://en/372090) onto the circle, with each point preimage homeomorphic to the pseudo-arc, called the circle of pseudo-arcs. Bing and Jones also showed that it is homogeneous. In 2016 Logan Hoehn and Lex Oversteegen classified all planar homogeneous continua, up to a homeomorphism, as the circle, pseudo-arc and circle of pseudo-arcs. A continuum is called "hereditarily equivalent" if it is homeomorphic to each of its non-degenerate sub-continua. In 2019 Hoehn and Oversteegen showed that the single point, the arc, and the pseudo-arc are topologically the only hereditarily equivalent planar continua, thus providing a complete solution to the planar case of Mazurkiewicz's problem from 1921.

## Construction {#Construction}

The following construction of the pseudo-arc follows (Lewis 1999).

### Chains {#Chains}

At the heart of the definition of the pseudo-arc is the concept of a *chain*, which is defined as follows:

A **chain** is a [finite collection](wikipedia://en/11742) of [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) $\mathcal{C}=\{C_1,C_2,\ldots,C_n\}$ in a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) such that $C_i\cap C_j\ne\emptyset$ if and only if $|i-j|\le1.$ The [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) of a chain are called its **links**, and a chain is called an **$\varepsilon$-chain** if each of its links has [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) less than $\varepsilon$.

While being the simplest of the type of spaces listed above, the pseudo-arc is actually very complex. The concept of a chain being *crooked* (defined below) is what endows the pseudo-arc with its complexity. Informally, it requires a chain to follow a certain [recursive](wikipedia://en/25407) zig-zag pattern in another chain. To 'move' from the $m$-th link of the larger chain to the $n$-th, the smaller chain must first move in a crooked manner from the $m$-th link to the $(n - 1)$-th link, then in a crooked manner to the $(m + 1)$-th link, and then finally to the $n$-th link.

More formally:

Let $\mathcal{C}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ be chains such that

1. each link of $\mathcal{D}$ is a subset of a link of $\mathcal{C}$, and
1. for any indices $i, j, m, n$ with $D_i\cap C_m\ne\emptyset$, $D_j\cap C_n\ne\emptyset$, and $m<n-2$, there exist indices $k$ and $\ell$ with $i<k<\ell<j$ (or $i>k>\ell>j$) and $D_k\subseteq C_{n-1}$ and $D_\ell\subseteq C_{m+1}.$

Then $\mathcal{D}$ is **crooked** in $\mathcal{C}.$

### Pseudo-arc {#Pseudo-arc}

For any collection $C$ of sets, let $C*$ denote the union of all of the elements of $C$. That is, let

$$C^*=\bigcup_{S\in C}S.$$

The *pseudo-arc* is defined as follows:

Let $p, q$ be distinct points in the plane and $\left\{\mathcal{C}^{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of chains in the plane such that for each $i$,

1. the first link of $\mathcal{C}^i$ contains $p$ and the last link contains $q$,
1. the chain $\mathcal{C}^i$ is a $1/2^i$-chain,
1. the closure of each link of $\mathcal{C}^{i+1}$ is a subset of some link of $\mathcal{C}^i$, and
1. the chain $\mathcal{C}^{i+1}$ is crooked in $\mathcal{C}^i$.

Let

$$P=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\mathcal{C}^i\right)^{*}.$$

Then $P$ is a **pseudo-arc**.
