# Ibn Mu'adh al-Jayyani

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ibn Mu'adh al-Jayyani"}

**Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn Muʿādh al-Jayyānī**[^o3] (أبو عبد الله محمد بن معاذ الجياني; 989, [Cordova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Córdoba,_Spain), [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) – 1079, [Jaén](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaén,_Spain), Al-Andalus) was an [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs) [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematics), [Islamic scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_scholar), and [Qadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qadi) from Al-Andalus (in present-day Spain).[^n1] Al-Jayyānī wrote important commentaries on [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)* and he wrote the first known treatise on [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Little is known about his life. Confusion exists over the identity of *al-Jayyānī* of the same name mentioned by [ibn Bashkuwal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Bashkuwal) (died 1183), [Qur'anic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qur'anic) scholar, Arabic [Philologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philologist), and expert in [inheritance laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_inheritance) (farāʾiḍī). It is unknown whether they are the same person.[^n2] There is some evidence that he lived in [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo) from 1012/13 to 1016/17.[^o7]

## Works {#Works}

Al-Jayyānī wrote *The book of unknown arcs of a sphere*, which is considered "the first treatise on [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405)", although spherical trigonometry in its ancient Hellenistic form was dealt with by earlier mathematicians such as [Menelaus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/46301), whose treatise the *Spherics* included [Menelaus' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus'_theorem),[^o10] still a basic tool for solving spherical geometry problems in Al-Jayyānī's time. However, [E. S. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Stewart_Kennedy) points out that while it was possible in pre-Islamic mathematics to compute the magnitudes of a spherical figure, in principle, by use of the table of chords and Menelaus' theorem, the application of the theorem to spherical problems was very difficult in practice.[^o11] Al-Jayyānī's work on spherical trigonometry "contains formulae for [right-handed triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_right_triangles), the general [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222), and the solution of a [spherical triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle) by means of the [polar triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_triangle)." This treatise later had a "strong influence on European mathematics", and his "definition of [ratios](wikipedia://en/87837) as numbers" and "method of solving a spherical triangle when all sides are unknown" are likely to have influenced [Regiomontanus](wikipedia://en/25929).[^o9]

There is strong consensus in attributing to him the authorship of a manuscript about the duration of twilight, and using that to measure the thickness of the atmosphere.[^o12] The short treatise *On twilight* was incorrectly attributed to [Ibn al-Haytham](wikipedia://en/1645) for several centuries, due to a reference made by [Gerard of Cremona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_of_Cremona), who translated it into Latin. The first printed edition of Cremona's translation (*Allacen arabis uetustissimi liber de crepusculis, Gerardo Cremonensi interprete*) was made in 1542, by the Portuguese mathematician and cosmographer [Pedro Nunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Nunes).[^o13]
