# David Kolb

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="David Kolb"}

**David Alan Kolb** (born April 15, 1939) is an American philosopher and the [Charles A. Dana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_A._Dana_%28philanthropist%29) Professor Emeritus of Philosophy at [Bates College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bates_College) in [Maine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maine).

:::

## Life and career {#Life_and_career}

Kolb received a B.A. from [Fordham University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fordham_University) in 1963 and an M.A. in 1965. He later received a M.Phil. from [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) in 1970 and a Ph.D. in 1972. Kolb's dissertation was titled "*Conceptual Pluralism and Rationality*."[^o5] Most of Kolb's writing deals with "what it means to live with historical connections and traditions at a time when we can no longer be totally defined by that history." Professor Kolb taught at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) before moving to Bates in 1977 and teaching there until 2005, when he took emeritus status.

## Electronic literature {#Electronic_literature}

Kolb was also an early experimenter in hypertext and electronic literature. His work, *Socrates in the Labyrinth: Hypertext, Argument, Philosophy*, 1994 from [Eastgate Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastgate_Systems) is a philosophical work in five files (title, Habermas Pyramid, Earth Orbit, Cleavings, and Aristotle’s Argument).[^o7] The work was done in [Storyspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storyspace), a hypertextual writing program. A traversal of the work with documentation and scholarship about it is archived by The NEXT Museum.[^o8]

A second work, *Caged Texts,* was originally intended to accompany this main work, but remained unpublished until 2023, when it was resurrected in [The NEXT Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_NEXT_Museum) and featured in [The Digital Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Digital_Review).[^o9] *Caged Texts* experiments with random elements as a homage to [John Cage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cage)'s experimentation with random content. As [Dene Grigar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dene_Grigar) notes, this reimagined web version maintains the original random elements within the hypertext structure and takes advantage of web elements to also randomize the interface for a further representation of this experimental approach.[^o10]

*Hypertext as Resistance* has colored links to guide the essay as a response to a polemic by [Sanford Kwinter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanford_Kwinter) in the architectural theory journal *Assemblage*. This work is now reconstructed in The NEXT Museum.[^o11]
