# WAIFW matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="WAIFW matrix"}

In [infectious disease modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_modelling_in_epidemiology), a **who acquires infection from whom (WAIFW) matrix** is a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) that describes the rate of transmission of infection between different groups in a population, such as people of different ages.[^o2] Used with an [SIR model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compartmental_models_in_epidemiology), the entries of the WAIFW matrix can be used to calculate the [basic reproduction number](wikipedia://en/917273) using the [next generation operator](wikipedia://en/39592843) approach.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The $2 \times 2$ WAIFW matrix for two groups is expressed as $\begin{bmatrix} \beta_{11} & \beta_{12} \\ \beta_{21} & \beta_{22} \end{bmatrix}$ where $\beta_{ij}$ is the transmission coefficient from an infected member of group $i$ and a susceptible member of group $j$. Usually specific mixing patterns are assumed.

### Assortative mixing {#Assortative_mixing}

Assortative mixing occurs when those with certain characteristics are more likely to mix with others with whom they share those characteristics. It could be given by $\begin{bmatrix} \beta & 0 \\ 0 & \beta \end{bmatrix}$[^o3] or the general $2 \times 2$ WAIFW matrix so long as $\beta_{11}, \beta_{22} > \beta_{12}, \beta_{21}$. Disassortative mixing is instead when $\beta_{11}, \beta_{22} < \beta_{12}, \beta_{21}$.

### Homogenous mixing {#Homogenous_mixing}

Homogenous mixing, which is also dubbed random mixing, is given by $\begin{bmatrix} \beta & \beta \\ \beta & \beta \end{bmatrix}$.[^o8] Transmission is assumed equally likely regardless of group characteristics when a homogenous mixing WAIFW matrix is used. Whereas for heterogenous mixing, transmission rates depend on group characteristics.

### Asymmetric mixing {#Asymmetric_mixing}

It need not be the case that $\beta_{ij} = \beta_{ji}$. Examples of asymmetric WAIFW matrices are[^o10]

$$\begin{bmatrix} \beta_1 & \beta_2 \\ \beta_1 & \beta_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \beta_1 & \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 & \beta_2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Social contact hypothesis"}

## Social contact hypothesis {#Social_contact_hypothesis}

The social contact hypothesis was proposed by Jacco Wallinga, Peter Teunis, and Mirjam Kretzschmar in 2006. The hypothesis states that transmission rates are proportional to contact rates, $\beta_{ij} \propto c_{ij}$ and allows for social contact data to be used in place of WAIFW matrices.[^o13]
