# Duocylinder

*Image: Duocylinder ridge animated.gif.* [Stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) of the duocylinder's [ridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_%28geometry%29) (see below), as a [flat torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_torus). The ridge is rotating about the $xw$-plane.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Duocylinder"}

The **duocylinder**, also called the **double cylinder** or the **bidisc**, is a geometric object embedded in 4-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), defined as the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two [disks](wikipedia://en/329542) of respective radii *r*<sub>1</sub> and *r*<sub>2</sub>:$D = \left\{ (x,y,z,w) \, \left| \, x^2+y^2\leq r_1^2,\ z^2+w^2\leq r_2^2 \right. \right\}$

:::

It is similar to a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) in 3-space, which is the Cartesian product of a disk with a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860). But unlike the cylinder, both [hypersurfaces](wikipedia://en/525101) (of a [regular](wikipedia://en/333306) duocylinder) are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330).

Its [polar dual](wikipedia://en/19451201) is a [duospindle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duospindle), constructed as the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of two circles, one in the $xy$-plane and the other in the $zw$-plane.

## Geometry {#Geometry}

### Bounding 3-manifolds {#Bounding_3-manifolds}

The duocylinder is bounded by two mutually [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) 3-[manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) with [torus](wikipedia://en/74800)-like [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865), respectively described by the formulae:

$$x^2 + y^2 = r_1^2, z^2 + w^2 \leq r_2^2$$

and

$$z^2 + w^2 = r_2^2, x^2 + y^2 \leq r_1^2$$

The duocylinder is so called because these two bounding 3-manifolds may be thought of as 3-dimensional [cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) 'bent around' in 4-dimensional space such that they form closed loops in the $xy$- and $zw$-[planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29). The duocylinder has [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) in both of these planes, and as such can be used to understand [double rotations](wikipedia://en/2307854) by unwrapping the duocylinder's surface into its two cylindrical cells - rotation through one of the planes of symmetry translates one cylinder while rotating the other, and so in a double rotation, both cylinders rotate and translate.

A regular duocylinder consists of two congruent cells, one square flat torus face (the ridge), zero edges, and zero vertices.

### The ridge {#The_ridge}

The *ridge* of the duocylinder is the 2-manifold that is the boundary between the two bounding (solid) torus cells. It is in the shape of a [Clifford torus](wikipedia://en/7950934), which is the Cartesian product of two circles. Intuitively, it may be constructed as follows: Roll a 2-dimensional [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) into a cylinder, so that its top and bottom edges meet. Then roll the cylinder in the plane perpendicular to the 3-dimensional hyperplane that the cylinder lies in, so that its two circular ends meet.

The resulting shape is topologically equivalent to a Euclidean 2-[torus](wikipedia://en/74800) (a doughnut shape). However, unlike the latter, all parts of its surface are identically deformed. On the (2D surface, embedded in 3D) doughnut, the surface around the 'doughnut hole' is deformed with negative curvature (like a saddle) while the surface outside is deformed with positive curvature (like a sphere).

The ridge of the duocylinder may be thought of as the actual global shape of the screens of [video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_games) such as [Asteroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroids_%28video_game%29), where going off the edge of one side of the screen leads to the other side. It cannot be embedded without distortion in 3-dimensional space, because it requires two degrees of freedom ("directions") in addition to its inherent 2-dimensional surface in order for both pairs of edges to be joined.

The duocylinder can be constructed from the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) by "slicing" off the bulge of the 3-sphere on either side of the ridge. The analog of this on the 2-sphere is to draw minor latitude circles at ±45 degrees and slicing off the bulge between them, leaving a cylindrical wall, and slicing off the tops, leaving flat tops. This operation is equivalent to removing select vertices/pyramids from [polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopes), but since the 3-sphere is smooth/regular you have to generalize the operation.

The [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046) between the two 3-dimensional hypersurfaces on either side of the ridge is 90 degrees.

### Projections {#Projections}

Parallel projections of the duocylinder into 3-dimensional space and its cross-sections with 3-dimensional space both form cylinders. Perspective projections of the duocylinder form [torus](wikipedia://en/74800)-like shapes with the 'doughnut hole' filled in.

### Relation to other shapes {#Relation_to_other_shapes}

The duocylinder is the limiting shape of [duoprisms](wikipedia://en/2114155) as the number of sides in the constituent polygonal prisms approaches infinity. The duoprisms therefore serve as good [polytopic](wikipedia://en/23471) approximations of the duocylinder.

In 3-space, a cylinder can be considered intermediate between a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) and a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). In 4-space there are three Cartesian products that in the same sense are intermediate between the [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112) (1-[ball](wikipedia://en/160556) × 1-ball × 1-ball × 1-ball) and the [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) (4-[ball](wikipedia://en/160556)). They are:

- the [cubinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubinder) (2-ball × 1-ball × 1-ball), whose surface consists of four cylindrical cells and one square torus.
- the [spherinder](wikipedia://en/39661691) (3-ball × 1-ball), whose surface consists of three cells—two spheres, and the region in between.
- the duocylinder (2-ball × 2-ball), whose surface consists of two toroidal cells.

The duocylinder is the only one of the above three that is regular. These constructions correspond to the five [partitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29) of 4, the number of dimensions.
