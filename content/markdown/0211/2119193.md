# Topological manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Topological manifold"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **topological manifold** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) that locally resembles [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) *n*-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics%29) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). Topological manifolds are an important class of topological spaces, with applications throughout mathematics. All [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) are topological manifolds by definition, so the qualifier "topological" emphasizes the lack of additional structure. E.g. [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219) are topological manifolds equipped with a [differential structure](wikipedia://en/725272). Every manifold has an "underlying" topological manifold, obtained by simply "forgetting" the added structure.[^o2] However, not every topological manifold can be endowed with a particular additional structure. For example, the [E8 manifold](wikipedia://en/6091657) is a topological manifold which cannot be endowed with a differentiable structure.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is called **locally Euclidean** if there is a non-negative [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n* such that every point in *X* has a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29) which is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of [real *n*-space](wikipedia://en/1482083) **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.[^o5]

A **topological manifold** is a locally Euclidean [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637). It is common to place additional requirements on topological manifolds. In particular, many authors define them to be [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact)[^o6] or [second-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-countable).[^o5]

In the remainder of this article a *manifold* will mean a topological manifold. An *n-manifold* will mean a topological manifold such that every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### *n*-manifolds {#n-manifolds}

- The [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is an *n*-manifold.
- Any [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) is a 0-dimensional manifold.
- A [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) 1-manifold.
- A [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) and a [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412) are compact 2-manifolds (or [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865)).
- The [*n*-dimensional sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) *S*<sup>*n*</sup> is a compact *n*-manifold.
- The [*n*-dimensional torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-torus) **T**<sup>*n*</sup> (the product of *n* circles) is a compact *n*-manifold.

### Projective manifolds {#Projective_manifolds}

- [Projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135) over the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), [complexes](wikipedia://en/5826), or [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) are compact manifolds.
  - [Real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a *n*-dimensional manifold.
  - [Complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a 2*n*-dimensional manifold.
  - [Quaternionic projective space](wikipedia://en/2808931) **HP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a 4*n*-dimensional manifold.
- Manifolds related to projective space include [Grassmannians](wikipedia://en/373810), [flag manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_manifold), and [Stiefel manifolds](wikipedia://en/1392495).

### Other manifolds {#Other_manifolds}

- [Differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219) are a class of topological manifolds equipped with a [differential structure](wikipedia://en/725272).
- [Lens spaces](wikipedia://en/885725) are a class of differentiable manifolds that are [quotients](wikipedia://en/237213) of odd-dimensional spheres.
- [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) are a class of differentiable manifolds equipped with a compatible [group](wikipedia://en/19447) structure.
- The [E8 manifold](wikipedia://en/6091657) is a topological manifold which cannot be given a differentiable structure.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The property of being locally Euclidean is preserved by [local homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/248582). That is, if *X* is locally Euclidean of dimension *n* and *f* : *Y* → *X* is a local homeomorphism, then *Y* is locally Euclidean of dimension *n*. In particular, being locally Euclidean is a [topological property](wikipedia://en/1337282).

Manifolds inherit many of the local properties of Euclidean space. In particular, they are [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632), [locally connected](wikipedia://en/6959754), [first countable](wikipedia://en/904940), [locally contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_contractible_space), and [locally metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_metrizable_space). Being locally compact Hausdorff spaces, manifolds are necessarily [Tychonoff spaces](wikipedia://en/30554).

Adding the Hausdorff condition can make several properties become equivalent for a manifold. As an example, we can show that for a Hausdorff manifold, the notions of [σ-compactness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Σ-compact) and second-countability are the same. Indeed, a [Hausdorff manifold](wikipedia://en/13637) is a locally compact Hausdorff space, hence it is (completely) regular.[^o14] Assume such a space X is σ-compact. Then it is Lindelöf, and because Lindelöf + regular implies paracompact, X is metrizable. But in a metrizable space, second-countability coincides with being Lindelöf, so X is second-countable. Conversely, if X is a Hausdorff second-countable manifold, it must be σ-compact.[^o15]

A manifold need not be connected, but every manifold *M* is a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/1564380) of connected manifolds. These are just the [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) of *M*, which are [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) since manifolds are locally-connected. Being locally path connected, a manifold is path-connected [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is connected. It follows that the path-components are the same as the components.

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="The Hausdorff axiom"}

### The Hausdorff axiom {#The_Hausdorff_axiom}

The Hausdorff property is not a local one; so even though Euclidean space is Hausdorff, a locally Euclidean space need [not be](wikipedia://en/14194283). It is true, however, that every locally Euclidean space is [T<sub>1</sub>](wikipedia://en/148721).

An example of a non-Hausdorff locally Euclidean space is the [line with two origins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_with_two_origins). This space is created by replacing the origin of the real line with *two* points, an open neighborhood of either of which includes all nonzero numbers in some open interval centered at zero. This space is not Hausdorff because the two origins cannot be separated.

:::

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Compactness and countability axioms"}

### Compactness and countability axioms {#Compactness_and_countability_axioms}

A manifold is [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738) if and only if it is [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact). The [long line](wikipedia://en/338403) is an example a [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) 1-dimensional topological manifold that is not metrizable nor paracompact. Since metrizability is such a desirable property for a topological space, it is common to add paracompactness to the definition of a manifold. In any case, non-paracompact manifolds are generally regarded as [pathological](wikipedia://en/372742). Paracompact manifolds have all the topological properties of metric spaces. In particular, they are [perfectly normal Hausdorff spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfectly_normal_Hausdorff_space).

Manifolds are also commonly required to be [second-countable](wikipedia://en/901459). This is precisely the condition required to ensure that the manifold [embeds](wikipedia://en/52119) in some finite-dimensional Euclidean space. For any manifold the properties of being second-countable, [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480), and [σ-compact](wikipedia://en/346681) are all equivalent.

Every second-countable manifold is paracompact, but not vice versa. However, the converse is nearly true: a paracompact manifold is second-countable if and only if it has a [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) number of [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29). In particular, a connected manifold is paracompact if and only if it is second-countable. Every second-countable manifold is [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) and paracompact. Moreover, if a manifold is separable and paracompact then it is also second-countable.

Every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) manifold is second-countable and paracompact.

:::

### Dimensionality {#Dimensionality}

By [invariance of domain](wikipedia://en/210731), a non-empty *n*-manifold cannot be an *m*-manifold for *n* ≠ *m*.[^o21] The dimension of a non-empty *n*-manifold is *n*. Being an *n*-manifold is a [topological property](wikipedia://en/1337282), meaning that any topological space homeomorphic to an *n*-manifold is also an *n*-manifold.[^o42]

:::

## Coordinate charts {#Coordinate_charts}

By definition, every point of a locally Euclidean space has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb R^n$. Such neighborhoods are called *Euclidean neighborhoods*. It follows from [invariance of domain](wikipedia://en/210731) that Euclidean neighborhoods are always open sets. One can always find Euclidean neighborhoods that are homeomorphic to "nice" open sets in $\mathbb R^n$. Indeed, a space *M* is locally Euclidean if and only if either of the following equivalent conditions holds:

- every point of *M* has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an [open ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) in $\mathbb R^n$.
- every point of *M* has a neighborhood homeomorphic to $\mathbb R^n$ itself.

A Euclidean neighborhood homeomorphic to an open ball in $\mathbb R^n$ is called a *Euclidean ball*. Euclidean balls form a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) for the topology of a locally Euclidean space.

For any Euclidean neighborhood *U*, a homeomorphism $\phi : U \rightarrow \phi \left ( U \right ) \subset \mathbb R^n$ is called a **coordinate chart** on *U* (although the word *chart* is frequently used to refer to the domain or range of such a map). A space *M* is locally Euclidean if and only if it can be [covered](wikipedia://en/317552) by Euclidean neighborhoods. A set of Euclidean neighborhoods that cover *M*, together with their coordinate charts, is called an [atlas](wikipedia://en/47436) on *M*. (The terminology comes from an analogy with [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography) whereby a spherical [globe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe) can be described by an [atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas) of flat maps or charts).

Given two charts $\phi$ and $\psi$ with overlapping domains *U* and *V*, there is a *transition function*

$$\psi\phi^{-1} : \phi \left ( U \cap V \right ) \rightarrow \psi \left ( U \cap V \right )$$

Such a map is a homeomorphism between open subsets of $\mathbb R^n$. That is, coordinate charts agree on overlaps up to homeomorphism. Different types of manifolds can be defined by placing restrictions on types of transition maps allowed. For example, for [differentiable manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_manifolds) the transition maps are required to be [smooth](wikipedia://en/8564).

:::

## Classification of manifolds {#Classification_of_manifolds}

### Discrete spaces (0-Manifold) {#Discrete_spaces_(0-Manifold)}

A 0-manifold is just a [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061). A discrete space is second-countable if and only if it is [countable](wikipedia://en/6026).[^o42]

### Curves (1-Manifold) {#Curves_(1-Manifold)}

Every nonempty, paracompact, connected 1-manifold is homeomorphic either to **R** or the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220).[^o42]

### Surfaces (2-Manifold) {#Surfaces_(2-Manifold)}

*Image: Sphere wireframe.svg.* The [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) is a 2-manifold.

Every nonempty, compact, connected 2-manifold (or [surface](wikipedia://en/27865)) is homeomorphic to the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), a [connected sum](wikipedia://en/490134) of [tori](wikipedia://en/74800), or a connected sum of [projective planes](wikipedia://en/411325).[^o28]

### Volumes (3-Manifold) {#Volumes_(3-Manifold)}

A classification of 3-manifolds results from [Thurston's geometrization conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thurston's_geometrization_conjecture), proven by [Grigori Perelman](wikipedia://en/225266) in 2003. More specifically, Perelman's results provide an algorithm for deciding if two three-manifolds are homeomorphic to each other.[^o30]

### General *n*-manifold {#General_n-manifold}

The full classification of *n*-manifolds for *n* greater than three is known to be impossible; it is at least as hard as the [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563) in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), which is known to be [algorithmically undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055).[^o32]

In fact, there is no [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for deciding whether a given manifold is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected). There is, however, a classification of simply connected manifolds of dimension ≥ 5.[^o33][^o34]

::: {#o36 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Surface with boundary"}

### Manifolds with boundary {#Manifolds_with_boundary}

A slightly more general concept is sometimes useful. A **topological manifold with boundary** is a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) in which every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open subset of Euclidean [half-space](wikipedia://en/647297) (for a fixed *n*):

$$\mathbb R^n_{+} = \{(x_1,\ldots,x_n) \in \mathbb R^n : x_n \ge 0\}.$$

Every topological manifold is a topological manifold with boundary, but not vice versa.[^o42]

:::

## Constructions {#Constructions}

There are several methods of creating manifolds from other manifolds.

### Product manifolds {#Product_manifolds}

If *M* is an *m*-manifold and *N* is an *n*-manifold, the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) *M*×*N* is a (*m*+*n*)-manifold when given the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910).[^o40]

### Disjoint union {#Disjoint_union}

The [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/1564380) of a countable family of *n*-manifolds is a *n*-manifold (the pieces must all have the same dimension).[^o42]

### Connected sum {#Connected_sum}

The [connected sum](wikipedia://en/490134) of two *n*-manifolds is defined by removing an open ball from each manifold and taking the [quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) of the disjoint union of the resulting manifolds with boundary, with the quotient taken with regards to a homeomorphism between the boundary spheres of the removed balls. This results in another *n*-manifold.[^o42]

### Submanifold {#Submanifold}

Any open subset of an *n*-manifold is an *n*-manifold with the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926).[^o40]

:::
