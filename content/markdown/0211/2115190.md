# Meyer's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Meyer's theorem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Meyer's theorem** on [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) states that an [indefinite quadratic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_quadratic_form) $Q$ in five or more variables over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) nontrivially represents zero. In other words, if the equation $Q(x) = 0$ has a non-zero [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) solution, then it has a non-zero rational solution. By clearing the denominators, an [integral](wikipedia://en/14563) solution $x$ may also be found.

:::

Meyer's theorem is usually deduced from the [Hasse–Minkowski theorem](wikipedia://en/1109126) (which was [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) later) and the following statement:

A rational quadratic form in five or more variables represents zero over the field $\mathbb{Q}_{p}$ of [*$p$*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) for all $p$.

Meyer's theorem is the best possible with respect to the number of variables: there are indefinite rational quadratic forms $Q$ in four variables which do not represent zero. One family of examples is given by

$$Q(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}) = x^{2}_{1} + x^{2}_{2} - px^{2}_{3} - px^{2}_{4},$$

where *$p$* is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) that is congruent to 3 [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 4. This can be proved by the method of [infinite descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_descent) using the fact that, if the sum of two [perfect squares](wikipedia://en/213919) is divisible by such a *$p$*, then each summand is divisible by *$p$*.
