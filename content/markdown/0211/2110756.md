# Phase plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Phase plane"}

In [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), in particular the context of [nonlinear system analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_systems), a **phase plane** is a visual display of certain characteristics of certain kinds of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309); a coordinate plane with axes being the values of the two state variables, say (*x*, *y*), or (*q*, *p*) etc. (any pair of variables). It is a [two-dimensional](wikipedia://en/19469852) case of the general *n*-dimensional [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).

:::

The **phase plane method** refers to graphically determining the existence of [limit cycles](wikipedia://en/452950) in the solutions of the differential equation.

The solutions to the differential equation are a family of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427). Graphically, this can be plotted in the phase plane like a two-dimensional [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641). Vectors representing the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of the points with respect to a parameter (say time *t*), that is (*dx*/*dt*, *dy*/*dt*), at representative points are drawn. With enough of these arrows in place the system behaviour over the regions of plane in analysis can be visualized and [limit cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_cycles) can be easily identified.

The entire field is the *[phase portrait](wikipedia://en/4519252)*, a particular path taken along a flow line (i.e. a path always tangent to the vectors) is a *phase path*. The flows in the vector field indicate the time-evolution of the system the differential equation describes.

In this way, phase planes are useful in visualizing the behaviour of [physical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system); in particular, of oscillatory systems such as [predator-prey models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predator-prey_model) (see [Lotka–Volterra equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka–Volterra_equation)). In these models the phase paths can "spiral in" towards zero, "spiral out" towards infinity, or reach neutrally stable situations called centres where the path traced out can be either circular, elliptical, or ovoid, or some variant thereof. This is useful in determining if the dynamics are stable or not.[^o2]

Other examples of oscillatory systems are certain chemical reactions with multiple steps, some of which involve dynamic equilibria rather than reactions that go to completion. In such cases one can model the rise and fall of reactant and product concentration (or mass, or amount of substance) with the correct differential equations and a good understanding of [chemical kinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_kinetics).[^o3]

## Example of a linear system {#Example_of_a_linear_system}

A two-dimensional [system](wikipedia://en/63871781) of [linear differential equations](wikipedia://en/379868) can be written in the form:[^o2]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{dx}{dt} & = Ax + By \\ \frac{dy}{dt} & = Cx + Dy \end{aligned}$$

which can be organized into a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) equation:

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{d}{dt} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ \end{bmatrix} \\ & \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}. \end{aligned}$$

where **A** is the 2 × 2 [coefficient matrix](wikipedia://en/4636611) above, and **v** = (*x*, *y*) is a [coordinate vector](wikipedia://en/879358) of two [independent variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable).

Such systems may be solved analytically, for this case by integrating:[^o5]

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{Cx+Dy}{Ax+By}$$

although the solutions are [implicit functions](wikipedia://en/250896) in *x* and *y*, and are difficult to interpret.[^o2]

### Solving using eigenvalues {#Solving_using_eigenvalues}

More commonly they are solved with the coefficients of the right hand side written in matrix form using [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) *λ*, given by the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468):

$$\det \left(\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \\ \end{bmatrix}- \lambda \mathbf{I}\right) = 0$$

and [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvectors):

$$\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \\ \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}$$

The eigenvalues represent the powers of the exponential components and the eigenvectors are coefficients. If the solutions are written in algebraic form, they express the fundamental multiplicative factor of the exponential term. Due to the nonuniqueness of eigenvectors, every solution arrived at in this way has undetermined constants *c*<sub>1</sub>, *c*<sub>2</sub>, …, *c<sub>n</sub>*.

The general solution is:

$$x = \begin{bmatrix} k_{1} \\ k_{2} \end{bmatrix} c_{1}e^{\lambda_1 t} + \begin{bmatrix} k_{3} \\ k_{4} \end{bmatrix} c_{2}e^{\lambda_2 t}.$$

where *λ*<sub>1</sub> and *λ*<sub>2</sub> are the eigenvalues, and (*k*<sub>1</sub>, *k*<sub>2</sub>), (*k*<sub>3</sub>, *k*<sub>4</sub>) are the basic eigenvectors. The constants *c*<sub>1</sub> and *c*<sub>2</sub> account for the nonuniqueness of eigenvectors and are not solvable unless an initial condition is given for the system.

The above determinant leads to the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268):

$$\lambda^2 - (A+D)\lambda + (AD-BC) = 0$$

which is just a [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) of the form:

$$\lambda^2 - p\lambda + q=0$$

where

$$p = A+D = \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}) \,,$$

("tr" denotes [trace](wikipedia://en/43270)) and

$$q=AD-BC=\det(\mathbf{A})\,.$$

The explicit solution of the eigenvalues are then given by the [quadratic formula](wikipedia://en/59217):

$$\lambda = \frac{1}{2}(p\pm \sqrt{\Delta})\,$$

where

$$\Delta=p^2-4q \,.$$

### Eigenvectors and nodes {#Eigenvectors_and_nodes}

The eigenvectors and nodes determine the profile of the phase paths, providing a pictorial interpretation of the solution to the dynamical system, as shown next.

*Image: Phase plane nodes.svg.* Classification of equilibrium points of a [linear](wikipedia://en/379868) [autonomous system](wikipedia://en/312398).[^o2] These profiles also arise for non-linear autonomous systems in linearized approximations.

The phase plane is then first set-up by drawing straight lines representing the two eigenvectors (which represent stable situations where the system either converges towards those lines or diverges away from them). Then the phase plane is plotted by using full lines instead of direction field dashes. The signs of the eigenvalues indicate the phase plane's behaviour:

- If the signs are opposite, the intersection of the eigenvectors is a [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249).
- If the signs are both positive, the eigenvectors represent stable situations that the system diverges away from, and the intersection is an **unstable [node](wikipedia://en/13678647)**.
- If the signs are both negative, the eigenvectors represent stable situations that the system converges towards, and the intersection is a **stable node**.

The above can be visualized by recalling the behaviour of exponential terms in differential equation solutions.

### Repeated eigenvalues {#Repeated_eigenvalues}

This example covers only the case for real, separate eigenvalues. Real, repeated eigenvalues require solving the coefficient matrix with an unknown vector and the first eigenvector to generate the second solution of a two-by-two system. However, if the matrix is symmetric, it is possible to use the orthogonal eigenvector to generate the second solution.

### Complex eigenvalues {#Complex_eigenvalues}

Complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors generate solutions in the form of [sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) and [cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) as well as exponentials. One of the simplicities in this situation is that only one of the eigenvalues and one of the eigenvectors is needed to generate the full solution set for the system.
