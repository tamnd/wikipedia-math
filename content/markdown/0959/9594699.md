# Risk difference

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Risk difference"}

*Image: Illustration of risk reduction.svg.* The adverse outcome (dark) risk difference between the group exposed to the treatment (left) and the group unexposed to the treatment (right) is −0.25 (RD = −0.25, ARR = 0.25).

The **risk difference** (RD), **excess risk**, or **attributable risk**[^o2] is the difference between the [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk) of an outcome in the exposed group and the unexposed group. It is computed as $I_e - I_u$, where $I_e$ is the [incidence](wikipedia://en/167008) in the exposed group, and $I_u$ is the incidence in the unexposed group. If the risk of an outcome is increased by the exposure, the term **absolute risk increase** (ARI) is used, and computed as $I_e - I_u$. Equivalently, if the risk of an outcome is decreased by the exposure, the term **absolute risk reduction** (ARR) is used, and computed as $I_u - I_e$.[^o3][^o4]

:::

The inverse of the absolute risk reduction is the [number needed to treat](wikipedia://en/1964571), and the inverse of the absolute risk increase is the [number needed to harm](wikipedia://en/2622311).[^o3]

## Usage in reporting {#Usage_in_reporting}

It is recommended to use absolute measurements, such as risk difference, alongside the relative measurements, when presenting the results of [randomized controlled trials](wikipedia://en/163180).[^o6] Their utility can be illustrated by the following example of a hypothetical drug which reduces the risk of [colon cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_cancer) from 1 case in 5000 to 1 case in 10,000 over one year. The [relative risk reduction](wikipedia://en/9795530) is 0.5 (50%), while the absolute risk reduction is 0.0001 (0.01%). The absolute risk reduction reflects the low probability of getting colon cancer in the first place, while reporting only relative risk reduction, would run into risk of readers exaggerating the effectiveness of the drug.[^o7]

Authors such as [Ben Goldacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Goldacre) believe that the risk difference is best presented as a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) - drug reduces 2 cases of colon cancer to 1 case if you treat 10,000 people. Natural numbers, which are used in the [number needed to treat](wikipedia://en/1964571) approach, are easily understood by non-experts.[^o8]

## Inference {#Inference}

Risk difference can be estimated from a 2x2 [contingency table](wikipedia://en/935515):

::: {#o10 .table cue=table}

|  | Group |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | Experimental (E) | [Control](wikipedia://en/1640288) (C) |
| Events (E) | EE | CE |
| Non-events (N) | EN | CN |

:::

The [point estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_estimate) of the risk difference is

$$RD = \frac{EE}{EE + EN} - \frac{CE}{CE + CN}.$$

The [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) of RD is approximately [normal](wikipedia://en/21462), with [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520)

$$SE(RD) = \sqrt{\frac{EE\cdot EN}{(EE + EN)^3} + \frac{CE\cdot CN}{(CE + CN)^3}}.$$

The $1 - \alpha$ [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) for the RD is then

$$CI_{1 - \alpha}(RD) = RD\pm SE(RD)\cdot z_\alpha,$$

where $z_\alpha$ is the [standard score](wikipedia://en/221722) for the chosen level of [significance](wikipedia://en/160995)[^o4]

## Bayesian interpretation {#Bayesian_interpretation}

We could assume a disease noted by $D$, and no disease noted by $\neg D$, exposure noted by $E$, and no exposure noted by $\neg E$. The risk difference can be written as

$$RD = P(D\mid E)-P(D\mid \neg E).$$
