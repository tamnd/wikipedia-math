# Folkman's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Folkman's theorem"}

**Folkman's theorem** is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more particularly in [arithmetic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/18243381) and [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175). According to this theorem, whenever the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) are [partitioned](wikipedia://en/340240) into finitely many subsets, there exist arbitrarily large sets of numbers all of whose sums belong to the same subset of the partition.[^o2] The theorem had been discovered and proved independently by several mathematicians,[^o3][^o4] before it was named "Folkman's theorem", as a memorial to [Jon&nbsp;Folkman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon&nbsp;Folkman), by [Graham](wikipedia://en/240694), [Rothschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Lee_Rothschild), and [Spencer](wikipedia://en/4244250).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

Let **N** be the set {1, 2, 3, ...} of positive integers, and suppose that **N** is partitioned into *k* different subsets *N*<sub>1</sub>, *N*<sub>2</sub>, ... *N*<sub>*k*</sub>, where *k* is any positive integer. Then Folkman's theorem states that, for every positive integer *m*, there exists a set *S*<sub>*m*</sub> and an index *i*<sub>*m*</sub> such that *S*<sub>*m*</sub> has *m* elements and such that every sum of a nonempty subset of *S*<sub>*m*</sub> belongs to *N*<sub>*i*<sub>*m*</sub></sub>.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Relation to Rado's theorem and Schur's theorem"}

## Relation to Rado's theorem and Schur's theorem {#Relation_to_Rado's_theorem_and_Schur's_theorem}

[Schur's theorem](wikipedia://en/1522286) in Ramsey theory states that, for every positive integer *k*, there exists a positive integer *S*, such that for every partition of the integers $\{1,\ldots, S \}$ into *k* parts, one of the parts contains integers *x*, *y* and *z* with $x + y = z$. That is, it is the special case *m* = 2 of Folkman's theorem.

[Rado's theorem](wikipedia://en/1706360) in Ramsey theory concerns a similar problem statement in which the integers are partitioned into finitely many subsets; the theorem characterizes the integer matrices **A** with the property that the [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) **A** *x* = 0 can be guaranteed to have a solution in which every coordinate of the solution vector *x* belongs to the same subset of the partition. A system of equations is said to be *regular* whenever it satisfies the conditions of Rado's theorem; Folkman's theorem is equivalent to the regularity of the system of equations

$$x_T = \sum_{i\in T}x_{\{i\}},$$

where *T* ranges over each nonempty subset of the set {1, 2, ..., *m*}.[^o2]

:::

## Multiplication versus addition {#Multiplication_versus_addition}

It is possible to replace addition by multiplication in Folkman's theorem: if the natural numbers are finitely partitioned, there exist arbitrarily large sets *S* such that all products of nonempty subsets of *S* belong to a single partition set. Indeed, if one restricts *S* to consist only of [powers of two](wikipedia://en/376948), then this result follows immediately from the additive version of Folkman's theorem. However, it is open whether there exist arbitrarily large sets such that all sums and all products of nonempty subsets belong to a single partition set. The first example of nonlinearity in Ramsey Theory which does not consist of monomials was given, independently, by Furstenberg and Sarkozy in 1977, with the family {*x*, *x* + *y*<sup>2</sup>}, result which was further improved by Bergelson in 1987. In 2016, J. Moreira proved there exists a set of the form {*x*, *x* + *y*, *xy*} contained in an element of the partition[^o10] However it is not even known whether there must necessarily exist a set of the form {*x*, *y*, *x* + *y*, *xy*} for which all four elements belong to the same partition set.[^o2]

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Canonical Folkman Theorem"}

## Canonical Folkman Theorem {#Canonical_Folkman_Theorem}

Let $FS(\{x_i\}_{i = 1}^n)$ denote the set of all finite sums of elements of $\{x_i\}_{i = 1}^n$. Let $C$ be a (possibly infinite) coloring of the positive integers, and let $n$ be an arbitrary positive integer. There exists $\{x_i\}_{i = 1}^n$ such that at least one of the following 3 conditions holds.

\1) $FS(\{x_i\}_{i = 1}^n)$ is a monochromatic set.

\2) $FS(\{x_i\}_{i = 1}^n)$ is a rainbow set.

\3) For any $B \subseteq [1,n]$, the color of $\sum_{i \in B}x_i$ is determined solely by $\min(B)$.

:::

## Previous results {#Previous_results}

Variants of Folkman's theorem had been proved by [Richard&nbsp;Rado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard&nbsp;Rado) and by J. H. Sanders.[^o3][^o4][^o2] Folkman's theorem was named in memory of [Jon Folkman](wikipedia://en/29238744) by [Ronald Graham](wikipedia://en/240694), [Bruce Lee Rothschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Lee_Rothschild), and [Joel Spencer](wikipedia://en/4244250), in their book on [Ramsey&nbsp;theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey&nbsp;theory).[^o2]
