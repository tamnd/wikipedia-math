# Feret diameter

*Image: Feret diameter.png.* Illustration of horizontal and vertical Feret diameters of a particle, Fh and Fv, respectively.

*Image: DigitalCaliperEuro.jpg.* The diameter of an object measured with a [caliper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliper) is sometimes called the caliper diameter; it is the same as Feret diameter.

*Image: Diamètre de feret.jpg.* Feret diameter applied to a projection of a 3D object.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Feret diameter"}

The **Feret diameter** or **Feret's diameter** is a measure of an object's size along a specified direction. In general, it can be defined as the distance between the two parallel planes restricting the object perpendicular to that direction. It is therefore also called the **caliper diameter**, referring to the measurement of the object size with a [caliper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliper). This measure is used in the analysis of [particle sizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_size_%28grain_size%29), for example in [microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopy), where it is applied to [projections](wikipedia://en/313741) of a three-dimensional (3D) object on a 2D plane. In such cases, the Feret diameter is defined as the distance between two parallel tangential *lines* rather than *planes*.[^o6][^o7]

:::

## Mathematical properties {#Mathematical_properties}

From [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/8317687) it follows that for a 2D [convex body](wikipedia://en/8652020), the Feret diameter averaged over all directions (〈F〉) is equal to the ratio of the object [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) (P) and [pi](wikipedia://en/23601), i.e.,〈F〉= P/$\pi$. There is no such relation between〈F〉and P for a concave object.[^o6][^o7]

::: {#o4 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Feret diameter is used in the analysis of particle size and its distribution, e.g. in a powder or a polycrystalline solid; alternative measures include [Martin diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_diameter), [Krumbein diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krumbein_diameter) and [Heywood diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heywood_diameter).[^o8] The term first became common in scientific literature in the 1970s[^o9] and can be traced to L.R. Feret (after whom the diameter is named) in the 1930s. [^o10]

It is also used in biology as a method to analyze the size of cells in tissue sections.
