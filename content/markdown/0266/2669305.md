# Semicubical parabola

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semicubical parabola"}

*Image: Neil-parab-3.svg.* Semicubical parabola for various $a$

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **cuspidal cubic** or **semicubical parabola** is an [algebraic plane curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_plane_curve) that has an [implicit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation) of the form $y^2 - a^2 x^3 = 0$ (with $a \ne 0$) in some [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706).

:::

Solving for $y$ leads to the [explicit form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation)

$$y = \pm a x^{\frac{3}{2}},$$

which implies that every [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) point satisfies $x \ge 0$. The exponent explains the term *semicubical parabola*. (A [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) can be described by the equation $y = ax^{2}$.)

Solving the implicit equation for $x$ yields a second explicit form

$$x = \left(\frac{y}{a}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}.$$

The [parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108)

$$x = t^2, \quad y = a t^3$$

can also be deduced from the implicit equation by putting $t = \tfrac{y}{ax}.$[^o2]

The semicubical parabolas have a [cuspidal singularity](wikipedia://en/6851758); hence the name of *cuspidal cubic*.

The arc length of the curve was calculated by the English mathematician [William Neile](wikipedia://en/21605403) and published in 1657 (see section History).[^o3]

## Properties of semicubical parabolas {#Properties_of_semicubical_parabolas}

### Similarity {#Similarity}

Any semicubical parabola $(t^2,at^3)$ is **[similar](wikipedia://en/39377)** to the *semicubical unit parabola* $(u^2,u^3)$.

*Proof:* The similarity $(x,y) \rightarrow (a^2x,a^2y)$ (uniform scaling) maps the semicubical parabola $(t^2,at^3)$ onto the curve $((at)^2,(at)^3) = (u^2,u^3)$ with $u=at$.

### Singularity {#Singularity}

The parametric representation $(t^2,at^3)$ is *[regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_curve) except* at point $(0,0)$. At point $(0,0)$ the curve has a *[singularity](wikipedia://en/54240)* (cusp). The *proof* follows from the tangent vector $(2t,3t^2)$. Only for $t=0$ this vector has zero length.

*Image: Neil-parab-t.svg.* Tangent at a semicubical parabola

### Tangents {#Tangents}

Differentiating the *semicubical unit parabola* $y = \pm x^{3/2}$ one gets at point $(x_0,y_0)$ of the *upper* branch the equation of the tangent:

$$y = \frac{\sqrt{x_0}}{2}\left(3x-x_0\right) .$$

This tangent intersects the *lower* branch at exactly one further point with coordinates [^o8]

$$\left(\frac{x_0}{4}, -\frac{y_0}{8}\right) .$$

(Proving this statement one should use the fact, that the tangent meets the curve at $(x_0,y_0)$ twice.)

### Arclength {#Arclength}

Determining the [arclength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arclength) of a curve $(x(t),y(t))$ one has to solve the integral $\int \sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2} \;dt .$ For the semicubical parabola $(t^2,at^3), \; 0\le t\le b ,$ one gets

$$\int^b_0\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2} \;dt = \int^b_0 t\sqrt{4+9a^2t^2}\; dt = \cdots = \left[\frac{1}{27a^2} \left(4 + 9a^2t^2\right)^\frac{3}{2}\right]^b_0\; .$$

(The integral can be solved by the [substitution](wikipedia://en/193748) $u=4+9a^2t^2$.)

*Example:* For $a = 1$ (semicubical unit parabola) and $b = 2,$ which means the length of the arc between the origin and point (4,8), one gets the arc length 9.073.

### Evolute of the unit parabola {#Evolute_of_the_unit_parabola}

The [evolute of the *parabola*](wikipedia://en/842387#Evolute_of_a_parabola) $(t^2,t)$ is a semicubical parabola shifted by 1/2 along the *x*-axis: $\left(\frac{1}{2}+t^2,\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}^3}t^3\right).$

### Polar coordinates {#Polar_coordinates}

In order to get the representation of the semicubical parabola $(t^2,at^3)$ in polar coordinates, one determines the intersection point of the line $y=mx$ with the curve. For $m\ne 0$ there is one point different from the origin: $\left(\frac{m^2}{a^2}, \frac{m^3}{a^2}\right).$ This point has distance $\frac{m^2}{a^2}\sqrt{1+m^2}$ from the origin. With $m=\tan \varphi$ and $\sec^2 \varphi = 1 + \tan^2\varphi$ ( see [List of identities](wikipedia://en/25804104#Pythagorean_identities)) one gets [^o12]

$$r=\left(\frac{\tan\varphi}{ a}\right)^2 \sec \varphi \; ,\quad -\frac{\pi}{2} < \varphi < \frac{\pi}{2} .$$

*Image: Neil-parab-p.svg.* Relation between a semicubical parabola and a *cubic* function (green)

### Relation between a semicubical parabola and a cubic function {#Relation_between_a_semicubical_parabola_and_a_cubic_function}

Mapping the semicubical parabola $(t^2,t^3)$ by the [projective map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_map) $(x,y) \rightarrow \left(\frac{x}{y}, \frac{1}{y}\right)$ ([involutory](wikipedia://en/293450) [perspectivity](wikipedia://en/14909067) with axis $y=1$ and center $(0,-1)$) yields $\left(\frac{1}{t}, \frac{1}{t^3}\right),$ hence the *cubic function* $y=x^3.$ The cusp (origin) of the semicubical parabola is exchanged with the [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) of the y-axis.

This property can be derived, too, if one represents the semicubical parabola by *[homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316)*: In equation **(A)** the replacement $x=\tfrac{x_1}{x_3}, \; y=\tfrac{x_2}{x_3}$ (the line at infinity has equation $x_3=0$.) and the multiplication by $x_3^3$ is performed. One gets the equation of the curve

- in *homogeneous coordinates*: $x_3 x_2^2 - x_1^3 = 0 .$

Choosing line $x_{\color{red}2}=0$ as line at infinity and introducing $x=\tfrac{x_1}{x_2}, \; y=\tfrac{x_3}{x_2}$ yields the (affine) curve $y=x^3.$

### Isochrone curve {#Isochrone_curve}

An additional defining property of the semicubical parabola is that it is an *[isochrone curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isochrone_curve)*, meaning that a particle following its path while being pulled down by gravity travels equal vertical intervals in equal time periods. In this way it is related to the *tautochrone curve*, for which particles at different starting points always take equal time to reach the bottom, and the [brachistochrone curve](wikipedia://en/171879), the curve that minimizes the time it takes for a falling particle to travel from its start to its end.

## History {#History}

The semicubical parabola was discovered in 1657 by [William Neile](wikipedia://en/21605403) who computed its [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401). Although the lengths of some other non-algebraic curves including the [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373) and [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638) had already been computed (that is, those curves had been *rectified*), the semicubical parabola was the first [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) (excluding the [line](wikipedia://en/946975) and [circle](wikipedia://en/6220)) to be rectified.[^o2]

The length of the semicubical parabola was computed by [van Heuraet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Heuraet) in 1659.[^o16]
