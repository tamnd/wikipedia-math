# Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition** is a partition of the vertices of a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) into subsets, with the property that two adjacent vertices belong to the same subset [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are paired with each other in a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) of the graph. It is named after A. L. Dulmage and [Nathan Mendelsohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Mendelsohn), who published it in 1958.[^o2] A generalization to any graph is the [Edmonds–Gallai decomposition](wikipedia://en/59747277), using the [Blossom algorithm](wikipedia://en/24277294).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

The Dulmage-Mendelshon decomposition can be constructed as follows.[^o5] (it is attributed to [^o6] who in turn attribute it to [^o7]).

Let *G* be a bipartite graph, *M* a [maximum-cardinality matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_cardinality_matching) in *G*, and *V*<sub>0</sub> the set of vertices of *G* unmatched by *M* (the "free vertices"). Then *G* can be partitioned into three parts:

*Image: Even-odd-decomposition.svg.* The E-O-U decomposition

- *E* - the *even* vertices - the vertices reachable from *V*<sub>0</sub> by an *M*-alternating path of even length.
- *O* - the *odd* vertices - the vertices reachable from *V*<sub>0</sub> by an *M*-alternating path of odd length.
- *U* - the *unreachable* vertices - the vertices unreachable from *V*<sub>0</sub> by an *M*-alternating path.

An illustration is shown on the left. The bold lines are the edges of *M*. The weak lines are other edges of *G*. The red dots are the vertices of *V*<sub>0</sub>. Note that *V*<sub>0</sub> is contained in *E*, since it is reachable from *V*<sub>0</sub> by a path of length 0.

Based on this decomposition, the edges in G can be partitioned into six parts according to their endpoints: *E-U, E-E, O-O, O-U, E-O, U-U*. This decomposition has the following properties: [^o6]

1. The sets *E*, *O*, *U* are pairwise-disjoint. *Proof*: *U* is disjoint from *E* and *O* by definition. To prove that *E* and *O* are disjoint, suppose that some vertex *v* has both an even-length alternating path to an unmatched vertex *u<sub>1</sub>*, and an odd-length alternating path to an unmatched vertex *u<sub>2</sub>*. Then, concatenating these two paths yields an augmenting path from *u<sub>1</sub>* through *v* to u<sub>2</sub>. But this contradicts the assumption that *M* is a maximum-cardinality matching.
1. The sets *E*, *O*, *U* do not depend on the maximum-cardinality matching *M* (i.e., any maximum-cardinality matching defines exactly the same decomposition).
1. G contains only *O-O, O-U, E-O* and *U-U* edges.
1. Any maximum-cardinality matching in *G* contains only *E-O* and *U-U* edges.
1. Any maximum-cardinality matching in *G* saturates all vertices in *O* and all vertices in *U*.
1. The size of a maximum-cardinality matching in G is |*O*| + |*U*| / 2.
1. If G has a perfect matching, then all vertices of *G* are in *U*.

:::

## Alternative definition {#Alternative_definition}

### The coarse decomposition {#The_coarse_decomposition}

Let *G* = (*X+Y*,*E*) be a bipartite graph, and let *D* be the set of vertices in *G* that are not matched in at least one [maximum matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_matching) of *G*. Then *D* is necessarily an [independent set](wikipedia://en/524501). So *G* can be partitioned into three parts:

1. The vertices in *D* ∩ *X* and their neighbors;
1. The vertices in *D* ∩ Y and their neighbors;
1. The remaining vertices.

Every maximum matching in *G* consists of matchings in the first and second part that match all neighbors of *D*, together with a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) of the remaining vertices. If *G* has a perfect matching, then the third set contains *all* vertices of G.

### The fine decomposition {#The_fine_decomposition}

The third set of vertices in the coarse decomposition (or all vertices in a graph with a perfect matching) may additionally be partitioned into subsets by the following steps:

- Find a perfect matching of *G*.
- Form a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) *H* whose vertices are the matched edges in *G*. For each unmatched edge (*x,y*) in *G*, add a directed edge in *H* from the matched edge of *x* to the matched edge of *y*.
- Find the [strongly connected components](wikipedia://en/684680) of the resulting graph.
- For each component of *H*, form a subset of the Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition consisting of the vertices in *G* that are endpoints of edges in the component.

To see that this subdivision into subsets characterizes the edges that belong to perfect matchings, suppose that two vertices *x* and *y* in *G* belong to the same subset of the decomposition, but are not already matched by the initial perfect matching. Then there exists a strongly connected component in *H* containing edge *x,y*. This edge must belong to a [simple cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_cycle) in *H* (by the definition of strong connectivity) which necessarily corresponds to an alternating cycle in *G* (a cycle whose edges alternate between matched and unmatched edges). This alternating cycle may be used to modify the initial perfect matching to produce a new matching containing edge *x,y*.

An edge *x,y* of the graph *G* belongs to all perfect matchings of *G*, if and only if *x* and *y* are the only members of their set in the decomposition. Such an edge exists if and only if the [matching preclusion](wikipedia://en/27970912) number of the graph is one.

:::

## Core {#Core}

As another component of the Dulmage–Mendelsohn decomposition, Dulmage and Mendelsohn defined the *core* of a graph to be the union of its maximum matchings.[^o13] However, this concept should be distinguished from the [core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_%28graph_theory%29) in the sense of [graph homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/676328), and from the [*k*-core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-core) formed by the removal of low-degree vertices.

::: {#o15 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

This decomposition has been used to partition meshes in [finite element analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element_analysis), and to determine specified, underspecified and overspecified equations in systems of nonlinear equations. It was also used for an algorithm for [rank-maximal matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank-maximal_matching).

:::

## Asymmetric variant {#Asymmetric_variant}

In [^o17] there is a different decomposition of a bipartite graph, which is asymmetric - it distinguishes between vertices in one side of the graph and the vertices on the other side. It can be used to find a maximum-cardinality [envy-free matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_matching) in an unweighted bipartite graph, as well as a minimum-cost maximum-cardinality matching in a weighted bipartite graph.[^o17]
