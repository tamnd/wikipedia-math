# Zero-product property

*Image: Zero product property.svg.* A zero product [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) with three solutions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zero-product property"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the **zero-product property** states that the product of two [nonzero elements](wikipedia://en/3476379) is nonzero. In other words, $\text{if }ab=0,\text{ then }a=0\text{ or }b=0.$

:::

This property is also known as the **rule of zero product**, the **null factor law**, the **multiplication property of zero**, the **nonexistence of nonzero [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441)**, or one of the two **zero-factor properties**.[^o2] All of the [number systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_system) studied in [elementary mathematics](wikipedia://en/1775329) — the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $\mathbb{Z}$, the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\mathbb{Q}$, the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$, and the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$ — satisfy the zero-product property. In general, a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) which satisfies the zero-product property is called a [domain](wikipedia://en/487627).

## Algebraic context {#Algebraic_context}

Suppose $A$ is an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364). We might ask, does $A$ have the zero-product property? In order for this question to have meaning, $A$ must have both additive structure and multiplicative structure.[^o4] Usually one assumes that $A$ is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), though it could be something else, e.g. the set of nonnegative integers $\{ 0, 1, 2, \ldots \}$ with ordinary addition and multiplication, which is only a (commutative) [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468).

Note that if $A$ satisfies the zero-product property, and if $B$ is a subset of $A$, then $B$ also satisfies the zero product property: if $a$ and $b$ are elements of $B$ such that $ab = 0$, then either $a = 0$ or $b = 0$ because $a$ and $b$ can also be considered as elements of $A$.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- A ring in which the zero-product property holds is called a [domain](wikipedia://en/487627). A [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) domain is called an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462). Every [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and every subring of a field are integral domains. Similarly, every subring of a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) is a domain and satisfies the zero-product property.
- If $p$ is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), then the ring of [integers modulo $p$](wikipedia://en/20087) has the zero-product property (in fact, it is a field).
- The [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers) are an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) because they are a subring of the complex numbers.
- The zero-product property holds in the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), since the quaternions form a division ring.
- The set of nonnegative integers $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ satisfies the zero-product property, as being a subset of the [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers), which form an integral domain.

:::

## Non-examples {#Non-examples}

- Let $\mathbb{Z}_n$ denote the ring of [integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/20087). Then $\mathbb{Z}_6$ does not satisfy the zero product property: 2 and 3 are nonzero elements, yet $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 0 \pmod{6}$.
- In general, if $n$ is a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289), then $\mathbb{Z}_n$ does not satisfy the zero-product property. Namely, if $n = qm$ where $0 < q,m < n$, then $m$ and $q$ are nonzero modulo $n$, yet $qm \equiv 0 \pmod{n}$.
- The ring of 2×2 [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) entries does not satisfy the zero-product property: if $$M = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$$ and $N = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},$ then $$MN = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix} = 0,$$ yet neither $M$ nor $N$ is zero.
- The ring of all [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) $f: [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$, from the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) to the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), has nontrivial zero divisors: there are pairs of functions which are not identically equal to zero yet whose product is the zero function. In fact, it is not hard to construct, for any *n* ≥ 2, functions $f_1,\ldots,f_n$, none of which is identically zero, such that $f_i \, f_j$ is identically zero whenever $i \neq j$.
- The same is true even if we consider only continuous functions, or only even infinitely [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function). On the other hand, [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) have the zero-product property.

:::

## Application to finding roots of polynomials {#Application_to_finding_roots_of_polynomials}

Suppose $P$ and $Q$ are univariate polynomials with real coefficients, and $x$ is a real number such that $P(x)Q(x) = 0$. (Actually, we may allow the coefficients and $x$ to come from any integral domain.) By the zero-product property, it follows that either $P(x) = 0$ or $Q(x) = 0$. In other words, the roots of $PQ$ are precisely the roots of $P$ together with the roots of $Q$.

Thus, one can use [factorization](wikipedia://en/3776351) to find the roots of a polynomial. For example, the polynomial $x^3 - 2x^2 - 5x + 6$ factorizes as $(x-3)(x-1)(x+2)$; hence, its roots are precisely 3, 1, and −2.

In general, suppose $R$ is an integral domain and $f$ is a [monic](wikipedia://en/314730) univariate polynomial of degree $d \geq 1$ with coefficients in $R$. Suppose also that $f$ has $d$ distinct roots $r_1,\ldots,r_d \in R$. It follows (but we do not prove here) that $f$ factorizes as $f(x) = (x-r_1) \cdots (x-r_d)$. By the zero-product property, it follows that $r_1,\ldots,r_d$ are the *only* roots of $f$: any root of $f$ must be a root of $(x-r_i)$ for some $i$. In particular, $f$ has at most $d$ distinct roots.

If however $R$ is not an integral domain, then the conclusion need not hold. For example, the cubic polynomial $x^3 + 3x^2 + 2x$ has six roots in $\mathbb{Z}_6$ (though it has only three roots in $\mathbb{Z}$).

## More properties {#More_properties}

In a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) or, more generally, in a [rng](wikipedia://en/3405004) (ring without [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity)) $R$, the multiplication has the (left) [cancellation property](wikipedia://en/490646) if, for every nonzero element $a\in R$ one has

$$ab=ac \implies b=c$$

for every $b\in R$ and $c\in R$. The [distributive property](wikipedia://en/103118) $a(b-c)=ab-ac$ implies that a ring or rng has the zero product property [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its multiplication has the left cancellation.

If $R$ is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) or a commutative rng with the zero-product property, one can define *fractions* similarly as for the [definition of rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024#Formal_construction). These fractions form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) called the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of $R$.

It follows that *a commutative ring or rng has the zero-product property is and only if it is a subring or sub-rng of a field.*
