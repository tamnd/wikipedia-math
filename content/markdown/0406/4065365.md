# Carlson's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carlson's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), **Carlson's theorem** is a [uniqueness theorem](wikipedia://en/29759120) which was discovered by [Fritz David Carlson](wikipedia://en/5094208). Informally, it states that two different [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) which do not grow very fast at infinity can not coincide at the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). The theorem may be obtained from the [Phragmén–Lindelöf theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phragmén–Lindelöf_theorem), which is itself an extension of the [maximum-modulus theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum-modulus_theorem).

:::

Carlson's theorem is typically invoked to defend the uniqueness of a [Newton series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_series) expansion. Carlson's theorem has generalized analogues for other expansions.

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Assume that $f$ satisfies the following three conditions. The first two conditions bound the growth of $f$ at infinity, whereas the third one states that $f$ vanishes on the non-negative integers.

1. $f(z)$ is an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) of [exponential type](wikipedia://en/4081509), meaning that $$|f(z)| \leq C e^{\tau|z|}, \quad z \in \mathbb{C}$$ for some [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) values $C$, $\tau$.
1. There exists $c < \pi$ such that $$|f(iy)| \leq C e^{c|y|}, \quad y \in \mathbb{R}$$
1. $f(n) = 0$ for every non-negative integer $n$.

Then $f$ is identically zero.

:::

## Sharpness {#Sharpness}

### First condition {#First_condition}

The first condition may be relaxed: it is enough to assume that $f$ is analytic in $\operatorname{Re} z > 0$, [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) in $\operatorname{Re} z \ge 0$, and satisfies

$$|f(z)| \leq C e^{\tau|z|}, \quad \operatorname{Re} z > 0$$

for some real values $C$, $\tau$.

### Second condition {#Second_condition}

To see that the second condition is sharp, consider the function $f(z) = \sin(\pi z)$. It vanishes on the integers; however, it grows exponentially on the [imaginary axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_axis) with a growth rate of $c = \pi$, and indeed it is not identically zero.

### Third condition {#Third_condition}

A result due to (Rubel 1956) relaxes the condition that $f$ vanish on the integers. Namely, Rubel showed that the conclusion of the theorem remains valid if $f$ vanishes on a subset $A \subset \{0, 1, 2, ...\}$ of [upper density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_density) 1, meaning that

[$\limsup_{n \to \infty}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limsup) $\frac{\left| A \cap \{0,1,\ldots,n-1\} \right|}{n} = 1.$

This condition is sharp, meaning that the theorem fails for sets $A$ of upper density smaller than 1.

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Suppose $f(z)$ is a function that possesses all finite [forward differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference) $\Delta^n f(0)$. Consider then the [Newton series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_series)

$$g(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty {z \choose n} \, \Delta^n f(0)$$

where ${z \choose n}$ is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) and $\Delta^n f(0)$ is the $n$-th forward difference. By construction, one then has that $f(k) = g(k)$ for all non-negative integers $k$, so that the difference $h(k) = f(k) - g(k) = 0$. This is one of the conditions of Carlson's theorem; if $h$ obeys the others, then $h$ is identically zero, and the finite differences for $f$ uniquely determine its Newton series. That is, if a Newton series for $f$ exists, and the difference satisfies the Carlson conditions, then $f$ is unique.

:::
