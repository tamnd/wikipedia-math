# Itô diffusion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Itô diffusion"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) – specifically, in [stochastic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) – an **Itô diffusion** is a solution to a specific type of [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454). That equation is similar to the [Langevin equation](wikipedia://en/166890) used in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) to describe the [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) of a particle subjected to a potential in a [viscous](wikipedia://en/18963754) fluid. Itô diffusions are named after the [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Kiyosi Itô](wikipedia://en/1329240).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

*Image: Wiener process 3d.png.* This [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984) (Brownian motion) in three-dimensional space (one sample path shown) is an example of an Itô diffusion.

A (**time-homogeneous**) **Itô diffusion** in *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\boldsymbol{\textbf{R}}^n$ is a [process](wikipedia://en/47895) *X* : \[0, +∞) × Ω → **R**<sup>*n*</sup> defined on a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) (Ω, Σ, **P**) and satisfying a stochastic differential equation of the form

$$\mathrm{d} X_{t} = b(X_t) \, \mathrm{d} t + \sigma (X_{t}) \, \mathrm{d} B_{t},$$

where *B* is an *m*-dimensional [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) and *b* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R**<sup>*n*</sup> and σ : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R**<sup>*n*×*m*</sup> satisfy the usual [Lipschitz continuity](wikipedia://en/51731) condition

$$| b(x) - b(y) | + | \sigma (x) - \sigma (y) | \leq C | x - y |$$

for some constant *C* and all *x*, *y* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>; this condition ensures the existence of a unique [strong solution](wikipedia://en/1361454#Use_in_probability_and_mathematical_finance) *X* to the stochastic differential equation given above. The [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) *b* is known as the **[drift](wikipedia://en/2486019) coefficient** of *X*; the [matrix field](wikipedia://en/256738) σ is known as the **diffusion coefficient** of *X*. *b* and σ do not depend upon time; if they were, *X* would be referred to only as an *[Itô process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itô_process)*, not a diffusion. Itô diffusions have a number of nice properties, which include

- [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_continuous_process) and [Feller continuity](wikipedia://en/12429079);
- the [Markov property](wikipedia://en/306422);
- the [strong Markov property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_Markov_property);
- the existence of an [infinitesimal generator](wikipedia://en/12193437);
- the existence of a characteristic operator;
- [Dynkin's formula](wikipedia://en/12130843).

In particular, an Itô diffusion is a continuous, strongly Markovian process such that the domain of its characteristic operator includes all [twice-continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) functions, so it is a *diffusion* in the sense defined by Dynkin (1965).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Continuity"}

## Continuity {#Continuity}

### Sample continuity {#Sample_continuity}

An Itô diffusion *X* is a [sample continuous process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_continuous_process), i.e., for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) realisations *B<sub>t</sub>*(ω) of the noise, *X<sub>t</sub>*(ω) is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) of the time parameter, *t*. More accurately, there is a "continuous version" of *X*, a continuous process *Y* so that

$$\mathbf{P} [ X_t = Y_t] = 1 \mbox{ for all } t.$$

This follows from the standard existence and uniqueness theory for strong solutions of stochastic differential equations.

### Feller continuity {#Feller_continuity}

In addition to being (sample) continuous, an Itô diffusion *X* satisfies the stronger requirement to be a [Feller-continuous process](wikipedia://en/12429079).

For a point *x* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, let **P**<sup>*x*</sup> denote the law of *X* given initial datum *X*<sub>0</sub> = *x*, and let **E**<sup>*x*</sup> denote [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653) with respect to **P**<sup>*x*</sup>.

Let *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** be a [Borel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra)-[measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) that is [bounded below](wikipedia://en/311509) and define, for fixed *t* ≥ 0, *u* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** by

$$u(x) = \mathbf{E}^{x}[ f(X_t) ].$$

- [Lower semi-continuity](wikipedia://en/57824): if *f* is lower semi-continuous, then *u* is lower semi-continuous.
- Feller continuity: if *f* is bounded and continuous, then *u* is continuous.

The behaviour of the function *u* above when the time *t* is varied is addressed by the Kolmogorov backward equation, the Fokker–Planck equation, etc. (See below.)

:::

## The Markov property {#The_Markov_property}

### The Markov property {#The_Markov_property}

An Itô diffusion *X* has the important property of being *Markovian*: the future behaviour of *X*, given what has happened up to some time *t*, is the same as if the process had been started at the position *X<sub>t</sub>* at time 0. The precise mathematical formulation of this statement requires some additional notation:

Let Σ<sub>∗</sub> denote the [natural](wikipedia://en/5864648) [filtration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtration_%28abstract_algebra%29) of (Ω, Σ) generated by the Brownian motion *B*: for *t* ≥ 0,

$$\Sigma_{t} = \Sigma_{t}^{B} = \sigma \left \{ B_{s}^{-1} (A) \subseteq \Omega \ : \ 0 \leq s \leq t, A \subseteq \mathbf{R}^{n} \mbox{ Borel} \right\}.$$

It is easy to show that *X* is [adapted](wikipedia://en/6583420) to Σ<sub>∗</sub> (i.e. each *X<sub>t</sub>* is Σ<sub>*t*</sub>-measurable), so the natural filtration *F*<sub>∗</sub> = *F*<sub>∗</sub><sup>*X*</sup> of (Ω, Σ) generated by *X* has *F<sub>t</sub>* ⊆ Σ<sub>*t*</sub> for each *t* ≥ 0.

Let *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** be a bounded, Borel-measurable function. Then, for all *t* and *h* ≥ 0, the [conditional expectation](wikipedia://en/435099) conditioned on the [σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) Σ<sub>*t*</sub> and the expectation of the process "restarted" from *X<sub>t</sub>* satisfy the **Markov property**:

$$\mathbf{E}^{x} \big[ f(X_{t+h}) \big| \Sigma_{t} \big] (\omega) = \mathbf{E}^{X_{t} (\omega)}[ f(X_{h})].$$

In fact, *X* is also a Markov process with respect to the filtration *F*<sub>∗</sub>, as the following shows:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{E}^{x} \left [ f(X_{t+h}) \big| F_{t} \right ] &= \mathbf{E}^{x} \left [ \mathbf{E}^{x} \left [ f(X_{t+h}) \big| \Sigma_{t} \right] \big| F_{t} \right] \\ &= \mathbf{E}^{x} \left [ \mathbf{E}^{X_{t}} \left [ f(X_{h}) \right] \big| F_{t} \right] \\ &= \mathbf{E}^{X_{t}} \left [ f(X_{h}) \right ]. \end{aligned}$$

### The strong Markov property {#The_strong_Markov_property}

The strong Markov property is a generalization of the Markov property above in which *t* is replaced by a suitable random time τ : Ω → \[0, +∞\] known as a [stopping time](wikipedia://en/1202193). So, for example, rather than "restarting" the process *X* at time *t* = 1, one could "restart" whenever *X* first reaches some specified point *p* of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.

As before, let *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** be a bounded, Borel-measurable function. Let τ be a stopping time with respect to the filtration Σ<sub>∗</sub> with τ \< +∞ [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908). Then, for all *h* ≥ 0,

$$\mathbf{E}^{x} \big[ f(X_{\tau+h}) \big| \Sigma_{\tau} \big] = \mathbf{E}^{X_{\tau}} \big[ f(X_{h}) \big].$$

## The generator {#The_generator}

::: {#o12 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

Associated to each Itô diffusion, there is a second-order [partial differential operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) known as the *generator* of the diffusion. The generator is very useful in many applications and encodes a great deal of information about the process *X*. Formally, the **infinitesimal generator** of an Itô diffusion *X* is the operator *A*, which is defined to act on suitable functions *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** by

$$A f (x) = \lim_{t \downarrow 0} \frac{\mathbf{E}^{x} [f(X_{t})] - f(x)}{t}.$$

The set of all functions *f* for which this limit exists at a point *x* is denoted *D<sub>A</sub>*(*x*), while *D<sub>A</sub>* denotes the set of all *f* for which the limit exists for all *x* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. One can show that any [compactly-supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) *C*<sup>2</sup> (twice differentiable with continuous second derivative) function *f* lies in *D<sub>A</sub>* and that

$$Af(x) = \sum_{i} b_{i} (x) \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} (x) + \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j} \left( \sigma (x) \sigma (x)^{\top} \right)_{i, j} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial x_i \, \partial x_{j}} (x),$$

or, in terms of the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) and [scalar](wikipedia://en/157093) and [Frobenius](wikipedia://en/52203645) [inner products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product),

$$A f (x) = b(x) \cdot \nabla_{x} f(x) + \tfrac1{2} \left( \sigma(x) \sigma(x)^{\top} \right ) : \nabla_{x} \nabla_{x} f(x).$$

:::

::: {#o14 .example cue=heading name="An example"}

### An example {#An_example}

The generator *A* for standard *n*-dimensional Brownian motion *B*, which satisfies the stochastic differential equation d*X<sub>t</sub>* = d*B<sub>t</sub>*, is given by

$$A f (x) = \tfrac1{2} \sum_{i, j} \delta_{ij} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial x_{i} \, \partial x_{j}} (x) = \tfrac1{2} \sum_{i} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial x_{i}^{2}} (x),$$

i.e., *A* = Δ/2, where Δ denotes the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706).

:::

### The Kolmogorov and Fokker–Planck equations {#The_Kolmogorov_and_Fokker–Planck_equations}

The generator is used in the formulation of Kolmogorov's backward equation. Intuitively, this equation tells us how the expected value of any suitably smooth statistic of *X* evolves in time: it must solve a certain [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) in which time *t* and the initial position *x* are the independent variables. More precisely, if *f* ∈ *C*<sup>2</sup>(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>; **R**) has compact support and *u* : \[0, +∞) × **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is defined by

$$u(t, x) = \mathbf{E}^{x} [ f(X_t)],$$

then *u*(*t*, *x*) is differentiable with respect to *t*, *u*(*t*, ·) ∈ *D<sub>A</sub>* for all *t*, and *u* satisfies the following [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564), known as **Kolmogorov's backward equation**:

$$\begin{cases} \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t, x) = A u (t, x), & t > 0, x \in \mathbf{R}^{n}; \\ u(0, x) = f(x), & x \in \mathbf{R}^{n}. \end{cases}$$

The Fokker–Planck equation (also known as *Kolmogorov's forward equation*) is in some sense the "[adjoint](wikipedia://en/644032)" to the backward equation, and tells us how the [probability density functions](wikipedia://en/43487) of *X<sub>t</sub>* evolve with time *t*. Let ρ(*t*, ·) be the density of *X<sub>t</sub>* with respect to [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, i.e., for any Borel-measurable set *S* ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>,

$$\mathbf{P} \left [ X_t \in S \right ] = \int_{S} \rho(t, x) \, \mathrm{d} x.$$

Let *A*<sup>∗</sup> denote the [Hermitian adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) of *A* (with respect to the [*L*<sup>2</sup>](wikipedia://en/45194) [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product)). Then, given that the initial position *X*<sub>0</sub> has a prescribed density ρ<sub>0</sub>, ρ(*t*, *x*) is differentiable with respect to *t*, ρ(*t*, ·) ∈ *D<sub>A</sub>*<sub>\*</sub> for all *t*, and ρ satisfies the following partial differential equation, known as the **Fokker–Planck equation**:

$$\begin{cases} \dfrac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}(t, x) = A^{*} \rho (t, x), & t > 0, x \in \mathbf{R}^{n}; \\ \rho(0, x) = \rho_{0} (x), & x \in \mathbf{R}^{n}. \end{cases}$$

::: {#o17 .statement cue=named-heading name="The Feynman–Kac formula"}

### The Feynman–Kac formula {#The_Feynman–Kac_formula}

The Feynman–Kac formula is a useful generalization of Kolmogorov's backward equation. Again, *f* is in *C*<sup>2</sup>(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>; **R**) and has compact support, and *q* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is taken to be a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) that is bounded below. Define a function *v* : \[0, +∞) × **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** by

$$v(t, x) = \mathbf{E}^{x} \left[ \exp \left( - \int_{0}^{t} q(X_{s}) \, \mathrm{d} s \right) f(X_{t}) \right].$$

The **Feynman–Kac formula** states that *v* satisfies the partial differential equation

$$\begin{cases} \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial t}(t, x) = A v (t, x) - q(x) v(t, x), & t > 0, x \in \mathbf{R}^{n}; \\ v(0, x) = f(x), & x \in \mathbf{R}^{n}. \end{cases}$$

Moreover, if *w* : \[0, +∞) × **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is *C*<sup>1</sup> in time, *C*<sup>2</sup> in space, bounded on *K* × **R**<sup>*n*</sup> for all compact *K*, and satisfies the above partial differential equation, then *w* must be *v* as defined above.

Kolmogorov's backward equation is the special case of the Feynman–Kac formula in which *q*(*x*) = 0 for all *x* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.

:::

## The characteristic operator {#The_characteristic_operator}

::: {#o20 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

The [characteristic operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_operator) of an Itô diffusion *X* is a partial differential operator closely related to the generator, but somewhat more general. It is more suited to certain problems, for example in the solution of the [Dirichlet problem](wikipedia://en/571109).

The **characteristic operator** $\mathcal{A}$ of an Itô diffusion *X* is defined by

$$\mathcal{A} f (x) = \lim_{U \downarrow x} \frac{\mathbf{E}^{x} \left [ f(X_{\tau_{U}}) \right ] - f(x)}{\mathbf{E}^{x} [\tau_{U}]},$$

where the sets *U* form a sequence of [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) *U<sub>k</sub>* that decrease to the point *x* in the sense that

$$U_{k + 1} \subseteq U_{k} \mbox{ and } \bigcap_{k = 1}^{\infty} U_{k} = \{ x \},$$

and

$$\tau_{U} = \inf \{ t \geq 0 \ : \ X_{t} \not \in U \}$$

is the first exit time from *U* for *X*. $D_{\mathcal{A}}$ denotes the set of all *f* for which this limit exists for all *x* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> and all sequences {*U<sub>k</sub>*}. If **E**<sup>*x*</sup>\[τ<sub>*U*</sub>\] = +∞ for all open sets *U* containing *x*, define

$$\mathcal{A} f (x) = 0.$$

:::

### Relationship with the generator {#Relationship_with_the_generator}

The characteristic operator and infinitesimal generator are very closely related, and even agree for a large class of functions. One can show that

$$D_{A} \subseteq D_{\mathcal{A}}$$

and that

$$A f = \mathcal{A} f \mbox{ for all } f \in D_{A}.$$

In particular, the generator and characteristic operator agree for all *C*<sup>2</sup> functions *f*, in which case

$$\mathcal{A} f(x) = \sum_i b_i (x) \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}} (x) + \tfrac1{2} \sum_{i, j} \left( \sigma (x) \sigma (x)^{\top} \right)_{i, j} \frac{\partial^{2} f}{\partial x_{i} \, \partial x_{j}} (x).$$

### Application: Brownian motion on a Riemannian manifold {#Application:_Brownian_motion_on_a_Riemannian_manifold}

*Image: BMonSphere.jpg.* The characteristic operator of a Brownian motion is $\tfrac{1}{2}$ times the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Here it is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a 2-sphere.

Above, the generator (and hence characteristic operator) of Brownian motion on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> was calculated to be $\tfrac{1}{2}$Δ, where Δ denotes the Laplace operator. The characteristic operator is useful in defining Brownian motion on an *m*-dimensional [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) (*M*, *g*): a **Brownian motion on** *M* is defined to be a diffusion on *M* whose characteristic operator $\mathcal{A}$ in local coordinates *x<sub>i</sub>*, 1 ≤ *i* ≤ *m*, is given by $\tfrac{1}{2}$Δ<sub>LB</sub>, where Δ<sub>LB</sub> is the [Laplace-Beltrami operator](wikipedia://en/174706#Laplace–Beltrami_operator) given in local coordinates by

$$\Delta_{\mathrm{LB}} = \frac1{\sqrt{\det(g)}} \sum_{i = 1}^{m} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}} \left( \sqrt{\det(g)} \sum_{j = 1}^{m} g^{ij} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}} \right),$$

where \[*g<sup>ij</sup>*\] = \[*g<sub>ij</sub>*\]<sup>−1</sup> in the sense of [the inverse of a square matrix](wikipedia://en/217122).

## The resolvent operator {#The_resolvent_operator}

In general, the generator *A* of an Itô diffusion *X* is not a [bounded operator](wikipedia://en/455961). However, if a positive multiple of the identity operator **I** is subtracted from *A* then the resulting operator is invertible. The inverse of this operator can be expressed in terms of *X* itself using the [resolvent](wikipedia://en/6891551#Resolvent) operator.

For α > 0, the **resolvent operator** *R*<sub>α</sub>, acting on bounded, continuous functions *g* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R**, is defined by

$$R_{\alpha} g (x) = \mathbf{E}^{x} \left[ \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{- \alpha t} g(X_{t}) \, \mathrm{d} t \right].$$

It can be shown, using the Feller continuity of the diffusion *X*, that *R*<sub>α</sub>*g* is itself a bounded, continuous function. Also, *R*<sub>α</sub> and α**I** − *A* are mutually inverse operators:

- if *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is *C*<sup>2</sup> with compact support, then, for all α > 0,

$$R_{\alpha} (\alpha \mathbf{I} - A) f = f;$$

- if *g* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is bounded and continuous, then *R*<sub>α</sub>*g* lies in *D<sub>A</sub>* and, for all α > 0,

$$(\alpha \mathbf{I} - A) R_{\alpha} g = g.$$

## Invariant measures {#Invariant_measures}

Sometimes it is necessary to find an [invariant measure](wikipedia://en/7635201) for an Itô diffusion *X*, i.e. a measure on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> that does not change under the "flow" of *X*: i.e., if *X*<sub>0</sub> is distributed according to such an invariant measure μ<sub>∞</sub>, then *X<sub>t</sub>* is also distributed according to μ<sub>∞</sub> for any *t* ≥ 0. The Fokker–Planck equation offers a way to find such a measure, at least if it has a probability density function ρ<sub>∞</sub>: if *X*<sub>0</sub> is indeed distributed according to an invariant measure μ<sub>∞</sub> with density ρ<sub>∞</sub>, then the density ρ(*t*, ·) of *X<sub>t</sub>* does not change with *t*, so ρ(*t*, ·) = ρ<sub>∞</sub>, and so ρ<sub>∞</sub> must solve the (time-independent) partial differential equation

$$A^{*} \rho_{\infty} (x) = 0, \quad x \in \mathbf{R}^{n}.$$

This illustrates one of the connections between stochastic analysis and the study of partial differential equations. Conversely, a given second-order linear partial differential equation of the form Λ*f* = 0 may be hard to solve directly, but if Λ = *A*<sup>∗</sup> for some Itô diffusion *X*, and an invariant measure for *X* is easy to compute, then that measure's density provides a solution to the partial differential equation.

### Invariant measures for gradient flows {#Invariant_measures_for_gradient_flows}

An invariant measure is comparatively easy to compute when the process *X* is a stochastic gradient flow of the form

$$\mathrm{d} X_{t} = - \nabla \Psi (X_{t}) \, \mathrm{d} t + \sqrt{2 \beta^{-1}} \, \mathrm{d} B_{t},$$

where β > 0 plays the role of an [inverse temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_temperature) and Ψ : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is a scalar potential satisfying suitable smoothness and growth conditions. In this case, the Fokker–Planck equation has a unique stationary solution ρ<sub>∞</sub> (i.e. *X* has a unique invariant measure μ<sub>∞</sub> with density ρ<sub>∞</sub>) and it is given by the [Gibbs distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_distribution):

$$\rho_{\infty} (x) = Z^{-1} \exp ( - \beta \Psi (x) ),$$

where the [partition function](wikipedia://en/312252) *Z* is given by

$$Z = \int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}} \exp ( - \beta \Psi (x) ) \, \mathrm{d} x.$$

Moreover, the density ρ<sub>∞</sub> satisfies a [variational principle](wikipedia://en/491097): it minimizes over all probability densities ρ on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> the [free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy) functional *F* given by

$$F[\rho] = E[\rho] + \frac1{\beta} S[\rho],$$

where

$$E[\rho] = \int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}} \Psi(x) \rho(x) \, \mathrm{d} x$$

plays the role of an energy functional, and

$$S[\rho] = \int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}} \rho(x) \log \rho(x) \, \mathrm{d} x$$

is the negative of the Gibbs-Boltzmann entropy functional. Even when the potential Ψ is not well-behaved enough for the partition function *Z* and the Gibbs measure μ<sub>∞</sub> to be defined, the free energy *F*\[ρ(*t*, ·)\] still makes sense for each time *t* ≥ 0, provided that the initial condition has *F*\[ρ(0, ·)\] \< +∞. The free energy functional *F* is, in fact, a [Lyapunov function](wikipedia://en/352267) for the Fokker–Planck equation: *F*\[ρ(*t*, ·)\] must decrease as *t* increases. Thus, *F* is an [*H*-function](wikipedia://en/424440) for the *X*-dynamics.

::: {#o27 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Consider the [Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornstein-Uhlenbeck_process) *X* on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> satisfying the stochastic differential equation

$$\mathrm{d} X_{t} = - \kappa ( X_{t} - m) \, \mathrm{d} t + \sqrt{2 \beta^{-1}} \, \mathrm{d} B_{t},$$

where *m* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> and β, κ > 0 are given constants. In this case, the potential Ψ is given by

$$\Psi(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} \kappa |x - m|^2,$$

and so the invariant measure for *X* is a [Gaussian measure](wikipedia://en/6572909) with density ρ<sub>∞</sub> given by

$$\rho_{\infty} (x) = \left( \frac{\beta \kappa}{2 \pi} \right)^{\frac{n}{2}} \exp \left( - \frac{\beta \kappa | x - m |^{2}}{2} \right).$$

Heuristically, for large *t*, *X<sub>t</sub>* is approximately [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) with mean *m* and variance (βκ)<sup>−1</sup>. The expression for the variance may be interpreted as follows: large values of κ mean that the potential well Ψ has "very steep sides", so *X<sub>t</sub>* is unlikely to move far from the minimum of Ψ at *m*; similarly, large values of β mean that the system is quite "cold" with little noise, so, again, *X<sub>t</sub>* is unlikely to move far away from *m*.

:::

## The martingale property {#The_martingale_property}

In general, an Itô diffusion *X* is not a [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536). However, for any *f* ∈ *C*<sup>2</sup>(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>; **R**) with compact support, the process *M* : \[0, +∞) × Ω → **R** defined by

$$M_{t} = f(X_{t}) - \int_{0}^{t} A f(X_{s}) \, \mathrm{d} s,$$

where *A* is the generator of *X*, is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration *F*<sub>∗</sub> of (Ω, Σ) by *X*. The proof is quite simple: it follows from the usual expression of the action of the generator on smooth enough functions *f* and [Itô's lemma](wikipedia://en/203962) (the stochastic [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113)) that

$$f(X_{t}) = f(x) + \int_{0}^{t} A f(X_{s}) \, \mathrm{d} s + \int_{0}^{t} \nabla f(X_{s})^{\top} \sigma(X_{s}) \, \mathrm{d} B_{s}.$$

Since Itô integrals are martingales with respect to the natural filtration Σ<sub>∗</sub> of (Ω, Σ) by *B*, for *t* > *s*,

$$\mathbf{E}^{x} \big[ M_{t} \big| \Sigma_{s} \big] = M_{s}.$$

Hence, as required,

$$\mathbf{E}^{x}[M_t | F_s] = \mathbf{E}^{x} \left[ \mathbf{E}^{x} \big[ M_{t} \big| \Sigma_{s} \big] \big| F_{s} \right] = \mathbf{E}^{x} \big[ M_{s} \big| F_{s} \big] = M_{s},$$

since *M<sub>s</sub>* is *F<sub>s</sub>*-measurable.

::: {#o30 .statement cue=named-heading name="Dynkin's formula"}

## Dynkin's formula {#Dynkin's_formula}

Dynkin's formula, named after [Eugene Dynkin](wikipedia://en/1351540), gives the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of any suitably smooth statistic of an Itô diffusion *X* (with generator *A*) at a stopping time. Precisely, if τ is a stopping time with **E**<sup>*x*</sup>\[τ\] \< +∞, and *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is *C*<sup>2</sup> with compact support, then

$$\mathbf{E}^{x} [f(X_{\tau})] = f(x) + \mathbf{E}^{x} \left[ \int_{0}^{\tau} A f (X_{s}) \, \mathrm{d} s \right].$$

Dynkin's formula can be used to calculate many useful statistics of stopping times. For example, canonical Brownian motion on the real line starting at 0 exits the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) (−*R*, +*R*) at a random time τ<sub>*R*</sub> with expected value

$$\mathbf{E}^{0} [\tau_{R}] = R^{2}.$$

Dynkin's formula provides information about the behaviour of *X* at a fairly general stopping time. For more information on the distribution of *X* at a [hitting time](wikipedia://en/7059985), one can study the *harmonic measure* of the process.

:::

## Associated measures {#Associated_measures}

### The harmonic measure {#The_harmonic_measure}

In many situations, it is sufficient to know when an Itô diffusion *X* will first leave a [measurable set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable_set) *H* ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. That is, one wishes to study the [first exit time](wikipedia://en/7059985)

$$\tau_{H} (\omega) = \inf \{ t \geq 0 | X_{t} \not \in H \}.$$

Sometimes, however, one also wishes to know the distribution of the points at which *X* exits the set. For example, canonical Brownian motion *B* on the real line starting at 0 exits the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) (−1, 1) at −1 with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and at 1 with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so *B*<sub>τ<sub>(−1, 1)</sub></sub> is [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28discrete%29) on the set {−1, 1}.

In general, if *G* is [compactly embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactly_embedded) within **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, then the **harmonic measure** (or **hitting distribution**) of *X* on the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) ∂*G* of *G* is the measure μ<sub>*G*</sub><sup>*x*</sup> defined by

$$\mu_{G}^{x} (F) = \mathbf{P}^{x} \left [ X_{\tau_{G}} \in F \right ]$$

for *x* ∈ *G* and *F* ⊆ ∂*G*.

Returning to the earlier example of Brownian motion, one can show that if *B* is a Brownian motion in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> starting at *x* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> and *D* ⊂ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is an [open ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) centred on *x*, then the harmonic measure of *B* on ∂*D* is [invariant](wikipedia://en/7635201) under all [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) of *D* about *x* and coincides with the normalized [surface measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_measure) on ∂*D*.

The harmonic measure satisfies an interesting **mean value property**: if *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is any bounded, Borel-measurable function and φ is given by

$$\varphi (x) = \mathbf{E}^{x} \left [ f(X_{\tau_{H}}) \right],$$

then, for all Borel sets *G* ⊂⊂ *H* and all *x* ∈ *G*,

$$\varphi (x) = \int_{\partial G} \varphi (y) \, \mathrm{d} \mu_{G}^{x} (y).$$

The mean value property is very useful in the [solution of partial differential equations using stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/12195613).

::: {#o34 .statement cue=named-heading name="The Green measure and Green formula"}

### The Green measure and Green formula {#The_Green_measure_and_Green_formula}

Let *A* be a partial differential operator on a domain *D* ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> and let *X* be an Itô diffusion with *A* as its generator. Intuitively, the Green measure of a Borel set *H* is the expected length of time that *X* stays in *H* before it leaves the domain *D*. That is, the **Green measure** of *X* with respect to *D* at *x*, denoted *G*(*x*, ·), is defined for Borel sets *H* ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup> by

$$G(x, H) = \mathbf{E}^{x} \left[ \int_{0}^{\tau_{D}} \chi_{H} (X_{s}) \, \mathrm{d} s \right],$$

or for bounded, continuous functions *f* : *D* → **R** by

$$\int_{D} f(y) \, G(x, \mathrm{d} y) = \mathbf{E}^{x} \left[ \int_{0}^{\tau_{D}} f(X_{s}) \, \mathrm{d} s \right].$$

The name "Green measure" comes from the fact that if *X* is Brownian motion, then

$$G(x, H) = \int_{H} G(x, y) \, \mathrm{d} y,$$

where *G*(*x*, *y*) is [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) for the operator $\tfrac{1}{2}$Δ on the domain *D*.

Suppose that **E**<sup>*x*</sup>\[τ<sub>*D*</sub>\] \< +∞ for all *x* ∈ *D*. Then the **Green formula** holds for all *f* ∈ *C*<sup>2</sup>(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>; **R**) with compact support:

$$f(x) = \mathbf{E}^{x} \left[ f \left( X_{\tau_{D}} \right) \right] - \int_{D} A f (y) \, G(x, \mathrm{d} y).$$

In particular, if the support of *f* is [compactly embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactly_embedded) in *D*,

$$f(x) = - \int_{D} A f (y) \, G(x, \mathrm{d} y).$$

:::
