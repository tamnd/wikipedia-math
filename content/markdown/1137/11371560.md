# Expectation value (quantum mechanics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Expectation value"}

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the **expectation value** is the probabilistic [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of the result (measurement) of an experiment. It can be thought of as an average of all the possible outcomes of a measurement as weighted by their likelihood, and as such it is not the *most* probable value of a measurement; indeed the expectation value may have [zero probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_probability) of occurring (e.g. measurements which can only yield integer values may have a non-integer mean), like the expected value from statistics. It is a fundamental concept in all areas of [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics).

:::

## Operational definition {#Operational_definition}

Consider an [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28physics%29) $A$. The expectation value is then $\langle A \rangle = \langle \psi | A | \psi \rangle$ in [Dirac notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bra_ket_notation) with $|\psi \rangle$ a [normalized](wikipedia://en/2978513) state vector.

## Formalism in quantum mechanics {#Formalism_in_quantum_mechanics}

In quantum theory, an experimental setup is described by the [observable](wikipedia://en/294248) $A$ to be measured, and the [state](wikipedia://en/30876419) $\sigma$ of the system. The expectation value of $A$ in the state $\sigma$ is denoted as $\langle A \rangle_\sigma$.

Mathematically, $A$ is a [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) operator on a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). In the most commonly used case in quantum mechanics, $\sigma$ is a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state), described by a normalized vector $\psi$ in the Hilbert space. The expectation value of $A$ in the state $\psi$ is defined as

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\langle A \rangle_\psi = \langle \psi | A | \psi \rangle . \tag{1}$$

:::

If [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28physics%29) is considered, either the vector $\psi$ or the operator $A$ is taken to be time-dependent, depending on whether the [Schrödinger picture](wikipedia://en/419200) or [Heisenberg picture](wikipedia://en/306706) is used. The evolution of the expectation value does not depend on this choice, however.

If $A$ has a complete set of [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) $\phi_j$, with [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) $a_j$ so that $A = \sum_j a_j | \phi_j\rangle \langle \phi_j |,$ then ((1)) can be expressed as[^o5]

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\langle A \rangle_\psi = \sum_j a_j |\langle \psi | \phi_j \rangle|^2 . \tag{2}$$

:::

This expression is similar to the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612), and illustrates the physical meaning of the mathematical formalism: The eigenvalues $a_j$ are the possible outcomes of the experiment, and their corresponding coefficient $|\langle \psi | \phi_j \rangle|^2$ is the probability that this outcome will occur; it is often called the *transition probability*.

A particularly simple case arises when $A$ is a [projection](wikipedia://en/519182), and thus has only the eigenvalues 0 and 1. This physically corresponds to a "yes-no" type of experiment. In this case, the expectation value is the probability that the experiment results in "1", and it can be computed as

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\langle A \rangle_\psi = \| A | \psi \rangle \|^2 . \tag{3}$$

:::

In quantum theory, it is also possible for an operator to have a non-discrete spectrum, such as the [position operator](wikipedia://en/4838571) $X$ in quantum mechanics. This operator has a completely [continuous spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_spectrum), with eigenvalues and eigenvectors depending on a continuous parameter, $x$. Specifically, the operator $X$ acts on a spatial vector $| x \rangle$ as $X | x \rangle = x |x\rangle$.[^o8] In this case, the vector $\psi$ can be written as a [complex-valued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) function $\psi(x)$ on the spectrum of $X$ (usually the real line). This is formally achieved by projecting the state vector $| \psi \rangle$ onto the eigenvalues of the operator, as in the discrete case $\psi(x) \equiv \langle x | \psi \rangle$. It happens that the eigenvectors of the position operator form a complete basis for the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of states, and therefore obey a [completeness relation in quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completeness_relation_in_quantum_mechanics):

$$\int |x \rangle \langle x| \, dx \equiv \mathbb{I}$$

The above may be used to derive the common, integral expression for the expected value ((4)), by inserting identities into the vector expression of expected value, then expanding in the position basis:

$$\begin{aligned} \langle X \rangle_{\psi} &= \langle \psi | X | \psi \rangle = \langle \psi |\mathbb{I} X \mathbb{I}| \psi \rangle \\ &= \iint \langle \psi | x \rangle \langle x | X | x' \rangle \langle x' | \psi \rangle dx\ dx' \\ &= \iint \langle x | \psi \rangle^* x' \langle x | x' \rangle \langle x' | \psi \rangle dx\ dx' \\ &= \iint \langle x | \psi \rangle^* x' \delta(x - x') \langle x' | \psi \rangle dx\ dx' \\ &= \int \psi(x)^* x \psi(x) dx = \int x \psi(x)^* \psi(x) dx = \int x |\psi(x)|^2 dx \end{aligned}$$

Where the [orthonormality relation](wikipedia://en/194743) of the position basis vectors $\langle x | x' \rangle = \delta(x - x')$, reduces the double integral to a single integral. The last line uses the [modulus of a complex valued function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulus_of_complex_number) to replace $\psi^*\psi$ with $|\psi|^2$, which is a common substitution in quantum-mechanical integrals.

The expectation value may then be stated, where $x$ is unbounded, as the formula

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\langle X \rangle_\psi = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \, x \, |\psi(x)|^2 \, dx . \tag{4}$$

:::

A similar formula holds for the [momentum operator](wikipedia://en/2288549), in systems where it has continuous spectrum.

All the above formulas are valid for pure states $\sigma$ only. Prominently in [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) and [quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_optics), also *mixed states* are of importance; these are described by a positive [trace-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace-class) operator $\rho = \sum_i p_i | \psi_i \rangle \langle \psi_i |$, the *statistical operator* or *[density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844)*. The expectation value then can be obtained as

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\langle A \rangle_\rho = \operatorname{Trace} (\rho A) = \sum_i p_i \langle \psi_i | A | \psi_i \rangle = \sum_i p_i \langle A \rangle_{\psi_i} . \tag{5}$$

:::

## General formulation {#General_formulation}

In general, quantum states $\sigma$ are described by positive normalized [linear functionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on the set of observables, mathematically often taken to be a [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184). The expectation value of an observable $A$ is then given by

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\langle A \rangle_\sigma = \sigma(A) . \tag{6}$$

:::

If the algebra of observables acts irreducibly on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), and if $\sigma$ is a *normal functional*, that is, it is continuous in the [ultraweak topology](wikipedia://en/30876937), then it can be written as

$$\sigma (\cdot) = \operatorname{Tr} (\rho \; \cdot)$$

with a positive [trace-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace-class) operator $\rho$ of trace 1. This gives formula ((5)) above. In the case of a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state), $\rho= |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ is a [projection](wikipedia://en/519182) onto a unit vector $\psi$. Then $\sigma = \langle \psi |\cdot \; \psi\rangle$, which gives formula ((1)) above.

$A$ is assumed to be a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408). In the general case, its spectrum will neither be entirely discrete nor entirely continuous. Still, one can write $A$ in a [spectral decomposition](wikipedia://en/123495),

$$A = \int a \, dP(a)$$

with a [projection-valued measure](wikipedia://en/1046155) $P$. For the expectation value of $A$ in a pure state $\sigma = \langle \psi | \cdot \, \psi \rangle$, this means

$$\langle A \rangle_\sigma = \int a \; d \langle \psi | P(a) \psi\rangle ,$$

which may be seen as a common generalization of formulas ((2)) and ((4)) above.

In non-relativistic theories of finitely many particles (quantum mechanics, in the strict sense), the states considered are generally normal. However, in other areas of quantum theory, also non-normal states are in use: They appear, for example. in the form of [KMS states](wikipedia://en/2834214) in [quantum statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/712450) of infinitely extended media,[^o13] and as charged states in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267).[^o14] In these cases, the expectation value is determined only by the more general formula ((6)).

## Example in configuration space {#Example_in_configuration_space}

As an example, consider a quantum mechanical particle in one spatial dimension, in the [configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596) representation. Here the Hilbert space is $\mathcal{H} = L^2(\mathbb{R})$, the space of square-integrable functions on the real line. Vectors $\psi\in\mathcal{H}$ are represented by functions $\psi(x)$, called [wave functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_functions). The scalar product is given by $\langle \psi_1 | \psi_2 \rangle = \int \psi_1^\ast (x) \psi_2(x) \, dx$. The wave functions have a direct interpretation as a probability distribution:

$$\rho(x) dx = \psi^*(x)\psi(x) dx$$

gives the probability of finding the particle in an infinitesimal interval of length $dx$ about some point $x$.

As an observable, consider the position operator $Q$, which acts on wavefunctions $\psi$ by

$$(Q \psi) (x) = x \psi(x) .$$

The expectation value, or mean value of measurements, of $Q$ performed on a very large number of *identical* independent systems will be given by

$$\langle Q \rangle_\psi = \langle \psi | Q | \psi \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^\ast(x) \, x \, \psi(x) \, dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, \rho(x) \, dx .$$

The expectation value only exists if the integral converges, which is not the case for all vectors $\psi$. This is because the position operator is [unbounded](wikipedia://en/1422584), and $\psi$ has to be chosen from its [domain of definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_of_definition).

In general, the expectation of any observable can be calculated by replacing $Q$ with the appropriate operator. For example, to calculate the average momentum, one uses the momentum operator *in [configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596)*, $\mathbf{p} = -i \hbar \, \frac{d}{dx}$. Explicitly, its expectation value is

$$\langle \mathbf{p} \rangle_\psi = -i\hbar \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^\ast(x) \, \frac{d\psi(x)}{dx} \, dx.$$

Not all operators in general provide a measurable value. An operator that has a pure real expectation value is called an [observable](wikipedia://en/294248) and its value can be directly measured in experiment.
