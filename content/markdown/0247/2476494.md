::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Equiangular polygon"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), an **equiangular polygon** is a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) whose vertex angles are equal. If the lengths of the sides are also equal (that is, if it is also [equilateral](wikipedia://en/214547)) then it is a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306). [Isogonal polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isogonal_polygon) are equiangular polygons which alternate two edge lengths.

:::

For clarity, a **planar equiangular polygon** can be called *direct* or *indirect*. A **direct equiangular polygon** has all angles turning in the same direction in a plane and can include multiple [turns](wikipedia://en/855329). **Convex equiangular polygons** are always direct. An **indirect equiangular polygon** can include angles turning right or left in any combination. A **[skew](wikipedia://en/6545461) equiangular polygon** may be [isogonal](wikipedia://en/581560), but can't be considered direct since it is nonplanar.

A [spirolateral](wikipedia://en/67378893) *n*<sub>θ</sub> is a special case of an *equiangular polygon* with a set of *n* integer edge lengths repeating sequence until returning to the start, with vertex internal angles θ.

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

An *equiangular polygon* can be constructed from a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) or [regular star polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_star_polygon) where edges are extended as infinite [lines](wikipedia://en/946975). Each edges can be independently moved perpendicular to the line's direction. Vertices represent the intersection point between pairs of neighboring line. Each moved line adjusts its edge-length and the lengths of its two neighboring edges.[^o7] If edges are reduced to zero length, the polygon becomes degenerate, or if reduced to *negative* lengths, this will reverse the internal and external angles.

For an even-sided direct equiangular polygon, with internal angles θ°, moving alternate edges can invert all vertices into [supplementary angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_angle), 180-θ°. Odd-sided direct equiangular polygons can only be partially inverted, leaving a mixture of supplementary angles.

Every equiangular polygon can be adjusted in proportions by this construction and still preserve equiangular status.

## Equiangular polygon theorem {#Equiangular_polygon_theorem}

For a *convex equiangular* *p*-gon, each [internal angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_angle) is 180(1−2/*p*)°; this is the *equiangular polygon theorem*.

For a direct equiangular *p*/*q* [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303), [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_%28geometry%29) *q*, each internal angle is 180(1−2*q*/*p*)°, with $1 < 2q < p$. For $w = \gcd(p,q) > 1$, this represents a *w*-wound $\tfrac{p/w}{q/w}$ star polygon, which is degenerate for the regular case.

A concave *indirect equiangular* $(p_{r}+p_{l})$-gon, with $p_{r}$ right turn vertices and $p_{l}$ left turn vertices, will have internal angles of $180(1-2/\lvert p_{r}-p_{l}\rvert))^{\circ}$, regardless of their sequence. An *indirect star equiangular* $(p_{r}+p_{l})$-gon, with $p_{r}$ right turn vertices and $p_{l}$ left turn vertices and *q* total [turns](wikipedia://en/855329), will have internal angles of $180(1-2q/\lvert p_{r}-p_{l}\rvert))^{\circ}$, regardless of their sequence. An equiangular polygon with the same number of right and left turns has zero total turns, and has no constraints on its angles.

:::

## Notation {#Notation}

Every direct equiangular *p*-gon can be given a notation $\langle p\rangle$ or $\langle p/q\rangle$, like [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) {*p*} and [regular star polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_star_polygon) {*p*/*q*}, containing *p* vertices, and stars having [density](wikipedia://en/34257549#Polygons) *q*.

Convex equiangular *p*-gons $\langle p\rangle$ have internal angles 180(1−2/*p*)°, while direct star equiangular polygons, $\langle p/q\rangle$, have internal angles 180(1−2*q*/*p*)°.

A concave indirect equiangular *p*-gon can be given the notation $\langle p-2c\rangle$, with *c* counter-turn vertices. For example, $\langle 6-2\rangle$ is a hexagon with 90° internal angles of the difference, $\langle 4\rangle$, 1 counter-turned vertex. A multiturn indirect equilateral *p*-gon can be given the notation $\langle {}^{p-2c}/_{q}\rangle$ with *c* counter turn vertices, and *q* total [turns](wikipedia://en/855329). An equiangular polygon \<*p*−*p*> is a *p*-gon with undefined internal angles $\theta$, but can be expressed explicitly as $\langle p-p\rangle$<sub>*θ*</sub>.

:::

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

[Viviani's theorem](wikipedia://en/10460511) holds for equiangular polygons:[^o15]

*The sum of distances from an interior point to the sides of an equiangular polygon does not depend on the location of the point, and is that polygon's invariant.*

A [cyclic polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon) is equiangular if and only if the alternate sides are equal (that is, sides 1, 3, 5, ... are equal and sides 2, 4, ... are equal). Thus if *n* is odd, a cyclic polygon is equiangular if and only if it is regular.[^o16]

For prime *p*, every integer-sided equiangular *p*-gon is regular. Moreover, every integer-sided equiangular *p*<sup>*k*</sup>-gon has *p*-fold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096).[^o17]

An ordered set of side lengths $(a_1, \dots , a_n)$ gives rise to an equiangular *n*-gon if and only if either of two equivalent conditions holds for the polynomial $a_1+a_2x+\cdots + a_{n-1}x^{n-2}+a_nx^{n-1}:$ it equals zero at the complex value $e^{2\pi i/n};$ it is divisible by $x^2-2x \cos (2\pi /n)+1.$[^o18]

## Direct equiangular polygons by sides {#Direct_equiangular_polygons_by_sides}

Direct equiangular polygons can be regular, isogonal, or lower symmetries. Examples for \<*p*/*q*> are grouped into sections by *p* and subgrouped by density *q*.

### Equiangular triangles {#Equiangular_triangles}

**Equiangular triangles** must be convex and have 60° internal angles. It is an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) and a [regular triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_triangle), $\langle 3\rangle$={3}. The only degree of freedom is edge-length.

::: {#o22 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Equiangular quadrilaterals"}

### Equiangular quadrilaterals {#Equiangular_quadrilaterals}

*Image: Rectangle-2x3.svg.* A rectangle dissected into a 2×3 array of squares[^o23]

Direct **equiangular quadrilaterals** have 90° internal angles. The only equiangular quadrilaterals are [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048), $\langle 4\rangle$, and [squares](wikipedia://en/659939), {4}.

An equiangular quadrilateral with integer side lengths may be tiled by [unit squares](wikipedia://en/285301).[^o23]

:::
