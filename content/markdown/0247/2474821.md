# Goodness of fit

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Goodness of fit"}

The **goodness of fit** of a [statistical model](wikipedia://en/27576) describes how well it fits a set of observations. Measures of goodness of fit typically summarize the discrepancy between observed values and the values expected under the model in question. Such measures can be used in [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing), e.g. to [test for normality](wikipedia://en/8617378) of [residuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics), to test whether two samples are drawn from identical distributions (see [Kolmogorov–Smirnov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov–Smirnov) test), or whether outcome frequencies follow a specified distribution (see [Pearson's chi-square test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson's_chi-square_test)). In the [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634), one of the components into which the variance is partitioned may be a [lack-of-fit sum of squares](wikipedia://en/18883979).

:::

## Categorical data {#Categorical_data}

The following are examples that arise in the context of [categorical data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_data).

### Pearson's chi-square test {#Pearson's_chi-square_test}

[Pearson's chi-square test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson's_chi-square_test) uses a measure of goodness of fit which is the sum of differences between observed and [expected outcome](wikipedia://en/9653) frequencies (that is, counts of observations), each squared and divided by the expectation:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n {\frac{(O_i - E_i)}{E_i}^2}$$

where:

- *O<sub>i</sub>* = an observed count for bin *i*
- *E<sub>i</sub>* = an expected count for bin *i*, asserted by the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis).

The expected frequency is calculated by:

$$E_i \, = \, \bigg( F(Y_u) \, - \, F(Y_l) \bigg) \, N$$

where:

- *F* = the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) for the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) being tested.
- *Y<sub>u</sub>* = the upper limit for bin *i*,
- *Y<sub>l</sub>* = the lower limit for bin *i*, and
- *N* = the sample size

The resulting value can be compared with a [chi-square distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-square_distribution) to determine the goodness of fit. The chi-square distribution has (*k* − *c*) [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606), where *k* is the number of non-empty bins and *c* is the number of estimated parameters (including location and scale parameters and shape parameters) for the distribution plus one. For example, for a 3-parameter [Weibull distribution](wikipedia://en/206948), *c* = 4.

#### Binomial case {#Binomial_case}

A binomial experiment is a sequence of independent trials in which the trials can result in one of two outcomes, success or failure. There are *n* trials each with probability of success, denoted by *p*. Provided that *np*<sub>*i*</sub> ≫ 1 for every *i* (where *i* = 1, 2, ..., *k*), then

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} {\frac{(N_i - np_i)^2}{np_i}} = \sum_{\mathrm{all\ bins}}^{} {\frac{(\mathrm{O} - \mathrm{E})^2}{\mathrm{E}}}.$$

This has approximately a chi-square distribution with *k* − 1 degrees of freedom. The fact that there are *k* − 1 degrees of freedom is a consequence of the restriction $\sum N_i=n$. We know there are *k* observed bin counts, however, once any *k* − 1 are known, the remaining one is uniquely determined. Basically, one can say, there are only *k* − 1 freely determined bin counts, thus *k* − 1 degrees of freedom.

### *G*-test {#G-test}

[*G*-tests](wikipedia://en/914820) are [likelihood-ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood_ratio_test) tests of [statistical significance](wikipedia://en/160995) that are increasingly being used in situations where Pearson's chi-square tests were previously recommended.[^o14]

The general formula for *G* is

$$G = 2\sum_{i} {O_{i} \cdot \ln\left(\frac{O_i}{E_i}\right)},$$

where $O_i$ and $E_i$ are the same as for the chi-square test, $\ln$ denotes the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), and the sum is taken over all non-empty bins. Furthermore, the total observed count should be equal to the total expected count:

$$\sum_i O_i = \sum_i E_i = N$$

where $N$ is the total number of observations.

*G*-tests have been recommended at least since the 1981 edition of the popular statistics textbook by [Robert R. Sokal](wikipedia://en/15415012) and [F. James Rohlf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._James_Rohlf).[^o15]
