# Direction cosine

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Direction cosine"}

In [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), the **direction cosines** (or **directional cosines**) of a [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) are the [cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) of the angles between the vector and the three positive coordinate axes. Equivalently, they are the contributions of each component of the [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) to a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) in that [direction](wikipedia://en/54516626).

:::

## Three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates {#Three-dimensional_Cartesian_coordinates}

*Image: Direction cosine vector.svg.* Vector $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$

*Image: Direction cosine unit vector.svg.* Direction cosines and direction angles for the unit vector $\tfrac{\mathbf v}{| \mathbf v|}$

If $\mathbf{v}$ is a [Euclidean vector](wikipedia://en/32533) in [three-dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), $\mathbb{R}^3,$

$$\mathbf v = v_x \mathbf e_x + v_y \mathbf e_y + v_z \mathbf e_z,$$

where $\mathbf{e}_{x}, \mathbf{e}_{y}, \mathbf{e}_{z}$ are the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) in Cartesian notation, then the direction cosines are

$$\begin{alignedat}{2} \alpha &{}= \cos a = \frac{\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf e_x}{\Vert\mathbf v\Vert } &&{}= \frac{v_x}{\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2}},\\ \beta &{}= \cos b = \frac{\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf e_y}{\Vert\mathbf v\Vert } &&{}= \frac{v_y}{\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2}},\\ \gamma &{}= \cos c = \frac{\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf e_z}{\Vert\mathbf v\Vert } &&{}= \frac{v_z}{\sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2}}. \end{alignedat}$$

It follows that by squaring each equation and adding the results

$$\cos^2 a + \cos^2 b + \cos^2 c = \alpha^{2} + \beta^{2} + \gamma^{2} = 1.$$

Here $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are the direction cosines and the Cartesian coordinates of the [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) $\tfrac{\mathbf v}{| \mathbf v|},$ and $a, b, c$ are the direction angles of the vector $\mathbf{v}$.

The direction angles $a, b, c$ are [acute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_angle) or [obtuse angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_angle), i.e., $0 \le a \le \pi$, $0 \le b \le \pi$ and $0 \le c \le \pi$, and they denote the angles formed between $\mathbf{v}$ and the unit basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_{x}, \mathbf{e}_{y}, \mathbf{e}_{z}$.

## General meaning {#General_meaning}

More generally, **direction cosine** refers to the cosine of the angle between any two [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533). They are useful for forming [direction cosine matrices](wikipedia://en/32533#Conversion_between_multiple_Cartesian_bases) that express one set of [orthonormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal) [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vectors) in terms of another set, or for expressing a known [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) in a different basis. Simply put, direction cosines provide an easy method of representing the direction of a vector in a Cartesian coordinate system.

::: {#o5 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

### Determining angles between two vectors {#Determining_angles_between_two_vectors}

Let $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ have direction cosines $(\alpha_{u}, \beta_{u}, \gamma_{u})$ and $(\alpha_{v}, \beta_{v}, \gamma_{v})$, respectively, having an angle $\theta$ between them. Their unit vectors are

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{\hat u} &= \frac{u_x}{ \rVert \mathbf{u} \lVert } \mathbf e_x + \frac{u_y}{ \rVert \mathbf{u} \lVert } \mathbf e_y + \frac{u_z}{ \rVert \mathbf{u} \lVert } \mathbf e_z = \alpha_u \mathbf e_x + \beta_u \mathbf e_y + \gamma_u \mathbf e_z \\ \mathbf{\hat v} &= \frac{v_x}{ \rVert \mathbf{v} \lVert } \mathbf e_x + \frac{v_y}{ \rVert \mathbf{v} \lVert } \mathbf e_y + \frac{v_z}{ \rVert \mathbf{v} \lVert } \mathbf e_z = \alpha_v \mathbf e_x + \beta_v \mathbf e_y + \gamma_v \mathbf e_z \\ \end{aligned}$$

respectively.

Taking the [scalar product](wikipedia://en/157093#Geometric_definition) of these two unit vectors yield,

$$\mathbf{\hat u \cdot \hat v} = \alpha_u\alpha_v + \beta_u\beta_v + \gamma_u\gamma_v.$$

The geometric interpretation of the scalar product of these two unit vectors is equivalent to the [projection](wikipedia://en/1508507) of one vector onto another; linking the two definitions we find the following.

$$\alpha_u\alpha_v + \beta_u\beta_v + \gamma_u\gamma_v = \cos \theta$$

There exist two choices for $\theta$ (because cosine is [even](wikipedia://en/592151)); one is acute, another is the obtuse angle between them. The convention is to choose the acute, so we take the absolute value of the scalar product.

$$\theta = \arccos \left(\left|\alpha_u\alpha_v + \beta_u\beta_v + \gamma_u\gamma_v\right|\right).$$
