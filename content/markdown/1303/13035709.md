# Hellinger distance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hellinger distance"}

In [probability](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/888711), the **Hellinger distance** (closely related to, although different from, the [Bhattacharyya distance](wikipedia://en/831261)) is used to quantify the similarity between two [probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distributions). It is a type of [*f*-divergence](wikipedia://en/15224289). The Hellinger distance is defined in terms of the [Hellinger integral](wikipedia://en/33389115), which was introduced by [Ernst Hellinger](wikipedia://en/12415098) in 1909.[^o3]

:::

It is sometimes called the Jeffreys distance.[^o4][^o5]

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Measure theory {#Measure_theory}

To define the Hellinger distance in terms of [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), let $P$ and $Q$ denote two [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) on a measure space $\mathcal{X}$ that are [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670) with respect to an auxiliary measure $\lambda$. Such a measure always exists, e.g $\lambda = (P + Q)$. The square of the Hellinger distance between $P$ and $Q$ is defined as the quantity

$$H^2(P,Q) = \frac{1}{2}\displaystyle \int_{\mathcal{X}} \left(\sqrt{p(x)} - \sqrt{q(x)}\right)^2 \lambda(dx).$$

Here, $P(dx) = p(x)\lambda(dx)$ and $Q(dx) = q(x) \lambda(dx)$, i.e. $p$ and $q$ are the [Radon–Nikodym derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) of *P* and *Q* respectively with respect to $\lambda$. This definition does not depend on $\lambda$, i.e. the Hellinger distance between *P* and *Q* does not change if $\lambda$ is replaced with a different probability measure with respect to which both *P* and *Q* are absolutely continuous. For compactness, the above formula is often written as

$$H^2(P,Q) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}} \left(\sqrt{P(dx)} - \sqrt{Q(dx)}\right)^2.$$

### Probability theory using Lebesgue measure {#Probability_theory_using_Lebesgue_measure}

To define the Hellinger distance in terms of elementary probability theory, we take λ to be the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), so that *dP* / *dλ* and *dQ* / *d*λ are simply [probability density functions](wikipedia://en/43487). If we denote the densities as *f* and *g*, respectively, the squared Hellinger distance can be expressed as a standard calculus integral

$$H^2(f,g) =\frac{1}{2}\int \left(\sqrt{f(x)} - \sqrt{g(x)}\right)^2 \, dx = 1 - \int \sqrt{f(x) g(x)} \, dx,$$

where the second form can be obtained by expanding the square and using the fact that the integral of a probability density over its domain equals 1.

The Hellinger distance *H*(*P*, *Q*) satisfies the property (derivable from the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128#L2))

$$0\le H(P,Q) \le 1.$$

### Discrete distributions {#Discrete_distributions}

For two discrete probability distributions $P=(p_1, \ldots, p_k)$ and $Q=(q_1, \ldots, q_k)$, their Hellinger distance is defined as

$$H(P, Q) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k (\sqrt{p_i} - \sqrt{q_i})^2},$$

which is directly related to the [Euclidean norm](wikipedia://en/53932) of the difference of the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) vectors, i.e.

$$H(P, Q) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \; \bigl\|\sqrt{P} - \sqrt{Q} \bigr\|_2 .$$

::: {#o11 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof"}

Also, $1 - H^2(P,Q) = \sum_{i=1}^k \sqrt{p_i q_i}.$

$$\begin{aligned} 1 - H^2(P,Q) &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^k (p_i + q_i) - (\sqrt{p_i} - \sqrt{q_i})^2\\ &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^k p_i + q_i - (p_i + q_i - 2\sqrt{p_i q_i})\\ &= \sum_{i=1}^k \sqrt{p_i q_i} \end{aligned}$$

:::

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The Hellinger distance forms a [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) on the [space](wikipedia://en/340630) of probability distributions over a given [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325).

The maximum distance 1 is achieved when *P* assigns probability zero to every set to which *Q* assigns a positive probability, and vice versa.

Sometimes the factor $1/\sqrt{2}$ in front of the integral is omitted, in which case the Hellinger distance ranges from zero to the square root of two.

The Hellinger distance is related to the [Bhattacharyya coefficient](wikipedia://en/831261) $BC(P,Q)$ as it can be defined as

$$H(P,Q) = \sqrt{1 - BC(P,Q)}.$$

Hellinger distances are used in the theory of [sequential](wikipedia://en/3509530) and [asymptotic statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_statistics).[^o14][^o15]

The squared Hellinger distance between two [normal distributions](wikipedia://en/21462) $P \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ and $Q \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$ is:

$$H^2(P, Q) = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}} \, e^{-\frac{1}{4}\frac{(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}}.$$

The squared Hellinger distance between two [multivariate normal distributions](wikipedia://en/50347) $P \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\Sigma_1)$ and $Q \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\Sigma_2)$ is [^o16]

$$H^2(P, Q) = 1 - \frac{ \det (\Sigma_1)^{1/4} \det (\Sigma_2) ^{1/4}} { \det \left( \frac{\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2}{2}\right)^{1/2} } \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{8}(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^T \left(\frac{\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2}{2}\right)^{-1} (\mu_1 - \mu_2) \right\}$$

The squared Hellinger distance between two [exponential distributions](wikipedia://en/45906) $P \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\alpha)$ and $Q \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\beta)$ is:

$$H^2(P, Q) = 1 - \frac{2 \sqrt{\alpha \beta}}{\alpha + \beta}.$$

The squared Hellinger distance between two [Weibull distributions](wikipedia://en/206948) $P \sim \mathrm{W}(k,\alpha)$ and $Q \sim \mathrm{W}(k,\beta)$ (where $k$ is a common [shape parameter](wikipedia://en/9210865) and $\alpha\, , \beta$ are the scale parameters respectively):

$$H^2(P, Q) = 1 - \frac{2 (\alpha \beta)^{k/2}}{\alpha^k + \beta^k}.$$

The squared Hellinger distance between two [Poisson distributions](wikipedia://en/23009144) with rate parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$, so that $P \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\alpha)$ and $Q \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\beta)$, is:

$$H^2(P,Q) = 1-e^{-\frac{1}{2} (\sqrt{\alpha} - \sqrt{\beta})^2}.$$

The squared Hellinger distance between two [beta distributions](wikipedia://en/207074) $P \sim \text{Beta}(a_1,b_1)$ and $Q \sim \text{Beta}(a_2, b_2)$ is:

$$H^2(P,Q) = 1 - \frac{B\left(\frac{a_1 + a_2}{2}, \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{B(a_1, b_1) B(a_2, b_2)}}$$

where $B$ is the [beta function](wikipedia://en/245660).

The squared Hellinger distance between two [gamma distributions](wikipedia://en/207079) $P \sim \text{Gamma}(a_1,b_1)$ and $Q \sim \text{Gamma}(a_2, b_2)$ is:

$$H^2(P,Q) = 1 - \Gamma\left({\scriptstyle\frac{a_1 + a_2}{2}}\right)\left(\frac{b_1+b_2}{2}\right)^{-(a_1+a_2)/2}\sqrt{\frac{b_1^{a_1}b_2^{a_2}}{\Gamma(a_1)\Gamma(a_2)}}$$

where $\Gamma$ is the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316).

:::

## Connection with total variation distance {#Connection_with_total_variation_distance}

The Hellinger distance $H(P,Q)$ and the [total variation distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_variation_distance) (or statistical distance) $\delta(P,Q)$ are related as follows:[^o18]

$$H^2(P,Q) \leq \delta(P,Q) \leq \sqrt{2}H(P,Q)\,.$$

The constants in this inequality may change depending on which [renormalization](wikipedia://en/291453) you choose ($1/2$ or $1/\sqrt{2}$).

These inequalities follow immediately from the inequalities between the [1-norm](wikipedia://en/45194#The_p-norm_in_finite_dimensions) and the [2-norm](wikipedia://en/45194#The_p-norm_in_finite_dimensions).
