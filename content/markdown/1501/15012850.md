# Leonardo number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Leonardo number"}

The **Leonardo numbers** are a sequence of numbers given by the recurrence:

:::

$$L(n) = \begin{cases} 1 & \mbox{if } n = 0 \\ 1 & \mbox{if } n = 1 \\ L(n - 1) + L(n - 2) + 1 & \mbox{if } n > 1 \\ \end{cases}$$

[Edsger W. Dijkstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edsger_W._Dijkstra)[^o2] used them as an integral part of his [smoothsort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoothsort) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775), and also analyzed them in some detail.[^o4][^o5]

A **Leonardo prime** is a **Leonardo number** that is also [prime](wikipedia://en/23666).

## Name {#Name}

The term "Leonardo number" was coined by Dijkstra[^o7], and the derivation is not given explicitly. Given the close relationship to the famous sequence credited to [Leonardo Fibonacci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_Fibonacci), he may have considered the subject trivial. There is no known nor likely connection to [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), the most common subject of that [mononym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mononym).

## Modulo cycles {#Modulo_cycles}

The Leonardo numbers form a cycle in any modulo $n \geq 2$. An easy way to see it is:

- If a pair of numbers modulo $n$ appears twice in the sequence, then there is a cycle.
- If we assume the main statement is false, using the previous statement, then it would imply there are an infinite number of distinct pairs of numbers between $0$ and $n-1$, which is false since there are $n^2$ such pairs.

The cycles for $n \leq 8$ are:

::: {#o10 .table cue=table}

| Modulo | Cycle | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 1 |
| 3 | 1,1,0,2,0,0,1,2 | 8 |
| 4 | 1,1,3 | 3 |
| 5 | 1,1,3,0,4,0,0,1,2,4,2,2,0,3,4,3,3,2,1,4 | 20 |
| 6 | 1,1,3,5,3,3,1,5 | 8 |
| 7 | 1,1,3,5,2,1,4,6,4,4,2,0,3,4,1,6 | 16 |
| 8 | 1,1,3,5,1,7 | 6 |

:::

The cycle always end on the pair $(1,n-1)$, as it is the only pair which can precede the pair $(1,1)$.

## Expressions {#Expressions}

- The following equation applies:

$$L(n)=2L(n-1)-L(n-3)$$

::: {#o12 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof.**

$$L(n)=L(n-1)+L(n-2)+1=L(n-1)+L(n-2)+1+L(n-3)-L(n-3)=2L(n-1)-L(n-3)$$

:::

## Relation to Fibonacci numbers {#Relation_to_Fibonacci_numbers}

The Leonardo numbers are related to the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number) by the relation $L(n) = 2 F(n+1) - 1, n \ge 0$.

From this relation it is straightforward to derive a [closed-form expression](wikipedia://en/585143) for the Leonardo numbers, analogous to Binet's formula for the Fibonacci numbers:

$$L(n) = 2 \frac{\varphi^{n+1} - \psi^{n+1}}{\varphi - \psi}- 1 = \frac{2}{\sqrt 5} \left(\varphi^{n+1} - \psi^{n+1}\right) - 1 = 2F(n+1) - 1$$

where the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) $\varphi = \left(1 + \sqrt 5\right)/2$ and $\psi = \left(1 - \sqrt 5\right)/2$ are the roots of the [quadratic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) $x^2 - x - 1 = 0$.

## Leonardo polynomials {#Leonardo_polynomials}

The Leonardo polynomials $L_{n}(x)$ is defined by [^o15]

$L_{n+2}(x)=xL_{n+1}(x)+L_{n}(x)+x$ with $L_{0}(x) = 1, L_{1}(x)=2x-1.$

Equivalently, in homogeneous form, the Leonardo polynomials can be written as

$$L_{n+3}(x)= (x+1)L_{n+2}(x)-(x-1)L_{n+1}(x)-L_{n}(x):$$

where $L_{0}(x)=1, L_{1}(x)=2x-1$ and $L_{2}(x)=2x^2+1.$

## Examples of Leonardo polynomials {#Examples_of_Leonardo_polynomials}

$$L_{0}(x)=1 \,$$

$$L_{1}(x)=2x-1\,$$

$$L_{2}(x)=2x^2+1\,$$

$$L_{3}(x)=2x^3+4x-1\,$$

$$L_{4}(x)=2x^4 + 6x^2 + 1 \,$$

$$L_{5}(x)=2x^5+8x^3+6x-1 \,$$

$$L_{6}(x)=2x^6+10x^4+12x^2+1\,$$

$$L_{7}(x)=2x^7+12x^5+20x^3+8x-1 \,$$

$$L_{8}(x)=2 x^8+14 x^6+30 x^4+20 x^2+1 \,$$

$$L_{9}(x)=2 x^9+16 x^7+42 x^5 + 40x^3 + 10x-1 \,$$

$$L_{10}(x)=2 x^{10}+18 x^8+56 x^6+70 x^4+30 x^2+1\,$$

$$L_{11}(x)=2 x^{11}+20 x^9+72 x^7+112 x^5+70 x^3+12x-1 \,$$

Substituting $x=1$ in the above polynomials gives the Leonardo numbers and setting $x=k$ gives the $k$-Leonardo numbers.[^o17]
