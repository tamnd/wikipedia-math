# Fixed-point property

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fixed-point property"}

A [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) object $X$ has the **fixed-point property** if every suitably well-behaved [mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mapping_%28mathematics%29) from $X$ to itself has a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738). The term is most commonly used to describe [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) on which every [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28mathematics%29) mapping has a fixed point. But another use is in [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), where a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) $P$ is said to have the fixed point property if every [increasing function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Increasing_function) on $P$ has a fixed point.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $A$ be an object in the [concrete category](wikipedia://en/167001) $\mathbf{C}$. Then $A$ has the *fixed-point property* if every [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) (i.e., every [function](wikipedia://en/185427)) $f: A \to A$ has a fixed point.

The most common usage is when $\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{Top}$ is the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051). Then a topological space $X$ has the fixed-point property if every continuous map $f: X \to X$ has a fixed point.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Singletons {#Singletons}

In the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), the objects with the fixed-point property are precisely the [singletons](wikipedia://en/381798).

### The closed interval {#The_closed_interval}

The [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) $[0, 1]$ has the fixed point property: Let $f : [0, 1] \to [0, 1]$ be a continuous mapping. If $f(0) = 0$ or $f(1) = 1$, then our mapping has a fixed point at 0 or 1. If not, then $f(0) > 0$ and $f(1) - 1 < 0$. Thus the function $f(g) = f(x) - x$ is a continuous real valued function which is positive at $x = 0$ and negative at $x = 1$. By the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884), there is some point $x_0$ with $g(x_0) = 0$, which is to say that $f(x) - x = 0$, and so $x_0$ is a fixed point.

The [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) does *not* have the fixed-point property. The mapping $f(x) = x^2$ has no fixed point on the interval $(0, 1)$.

### The closed disc {#The_closed_disc}

The closed interval is a special case of the [closed disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_disc), which in any finite dimension has the fixed-point property by the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101).

:::

## Topology {#Topology}

A [retract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) $A$ of a space $X$ with the fixed-point property also has the fixed-point property. This is because if $r: X \to A$ is a retraction and $f: A \to A$ is any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), then the composition $i \circ f \circ r: X \to X$ (where $i: A \to X$ is inclusion) has a fixed point. That is, there is $x \in A$ such that $f \circ r(x) = x$. Since $x \in A$ we have that $r(x) = x$ and therefore $f(x) = x.$

A topological space has the fixed-point property [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069) is [universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_map).

A [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_%28topology%29) of spaces with the fixed-point property in general fails to have the fixed-point property even if one of the spaces is the closed real interval.

The FPP is a [topological invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant), i.e. is preserved by any [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660). The FPP is also preserved by any [retraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract).

According to the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101), every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) has the FPP. More generally, according to the [Schauder-Tychonoff fixed point theorem](wikipedia://en/638200) every [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) subset of a [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844) has the FPP. Compactness alone does not imply the FPP and convexity is not even a topological property so it makes sense to ask how to topologically characterize the FPP. In 1932 [Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk) asked whether compactness together with [contractibility](wikipedia://en/1752414) could be a sufficient condition for the FPP to hold. The problem was open for 20 years until the conjecture was disproved by Kinoshita who found an example of a compact contractible space without the FPP.[^o10]
