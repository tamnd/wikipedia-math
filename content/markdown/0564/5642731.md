# Information theory and measure theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Information theory and measure theory"}

This article discusses how [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773) (a branch of mathematics studying the transmission, processing and storage of [information](wikipedia://en/18985062)) is related to [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) (a branch of mathematics related to [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) and [probability](wikipedia://en/22934)).

:::

## Measures in information theory {#Measures_in_information_theory}

Many of the concepts in information theory have separate definitions and formulas for [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_probability_distribution) and [discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_probability_distribution) cases. For example, [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) $\mathrm{H}(X)$ is usually defined for discrete random variables, whereas for continuous random variables the related concept of [differential entropy](wikipedia://en/3504168), written $h(X)$, is used (see Cover and Thomas, 2006, chapter 8). Both these concepts are mathematical [expectations](wikipedia://en/9653), but the expectation is defined with an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) for the continuous case, and a sum for the discrete case.

These separate definitions can be more closely related in terms of [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory). For discrete random variables, probability mass functions can be considered density functions with respect to the counting measure. Thinking of both the integral and the sum as integration on a measure space allows for a unified treatment.

Consider the formula for the differential entropy of a continuous [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ with range $\mathbb{R}$ and [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) $f(x)$:

$$h(X) = -\int_\mathbb{R} f(x) \log f(x) \,dx.$$

This can usually be interpreted as the following [Riemann–Stieltjes integral](wikipedia://en/330491):

$$h(X) = -\int_\mathbb{R} f(x) \log f(x) \,d\mu(x),$$

where $\mu$ is the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198).

If instead, $X$ is discrete, with range $\Omega$ a finite set, $f$ is a probability mass function on $\Omega$, and $\nu$ is the [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786) on $\Omega$, we can write:

$$\mathrm{H}(X) = -\sum_{x\in \Omega} f(x) \log f(x) = -\int_\Omega f(x) \log f(x) \,d\nu(x).$$

The integral expression, and the general concept, are identical in the continuous case; the only difference is the measure used. In both cases the probability density function $f$ is the [Radon–Nikodym derivative](wikipedia://en/338746) of the [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) with respect to the measure against which the integral is taken.

If $P$ is the probability measure induced by $X$, then the integral can also be taken directly with respect to $P$:

$$h(X) = -\int_{\Omega} \log \frac{\mathrm d P}{\mathrm d\mu} \,dP,$$

If instead of the underlying measure μ we take another probability measure $Q$, we are led to the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052): let $P$ and $Q$ be probability measures over the same space. Then if $P$ is [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670) with respect to $Q$, written $P \ll Q,$ the [Radon–Nikodym derivative](wikipedia://en/338746) $\frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dQ}$ exists and the Kullback–Leibler divergence can be expressed in its full generality:

$$D_\operatorname{KL}(P \| Q) = \int_{\operatorname{supp}P} \frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dQ} \log \frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dQ} \,d Q = \int_{\operatorname{supp}P} \log \frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dQ} \,d P,$$

where the integral runs over the [support](wikipedia://en/381013) of $P.$ Note that we have dropped the negative sign: the Kullback–Leibler divergence is always non-negative due to [Gibbs' inequality](wikipedia://en/2035678).

## Entropy as a "measure" {#Entropy_as_a_"measure"}

*Image: Entropy-mutual-information-relative-entropy-relation-diagram.svg.* [Venn diagram](wikipedia://en/61701) for various information measures associated with correlated variables *X* and *Y*. The area contained by both circles is the joint entropy *H*(*X*,*Y*). The circle on the left (red and cyan) is the individual entropy *H*(*X*), with the red being the conditional entropy *H*(*X*$|$*Y*). The circle on the right (blue and cyan) is *H*(*Y*), with the blue being *H*(*Y*$|$*X*). The cyan is the mutual information *I*(*X*;*Y*).

*Image: VennInfo3Var.svg.* [Venn diagram](wikipedia://en/61701) of information theoretic measures for three variables *x*, *y*, and *z*. Each circle represents an individual [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445): *H*(*x*) is the lower left circle, *H*(*y*) the lower right, and *H*(*z*) is the upper circle. The intersections of any two circles represents the [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282) for the two associated variables (e.g. *I*(*x*;*z*) is yellow and gray). The union of any two circles is the [joint entropy](wikipedia://en/910967) for the two associated variables (e.g. *H*(*x*,*y*) is everything but green). The joint entropy *H*(*x*,*y*,*z*) of all three variables is the union of all three circles. It is partitioned into 7 pieces, red, blue, and green being the [conditional entropies](wikipedia://en/908548) *H*(*x*$|$*y*,*z*), *H*(*y*$|$*x*,*z*), *H*(*z*$|$*x*,*y*) respectively, yellow, magenta and cyan being the [conditional mutual informations](wikipedia://en/18867510) *I*(*x*;*z*$|$*y*), *I*(*y*;*z*$|$*x*) and *I*(*x*;*y*$|$*z*) respectively, and gray being the [multivariate mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_mutual_information) *I*(*x*;*y*;*z*). The multivariate mutual information is the only one of all that may be negative.

There is an analogy between [Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Elwood_Shannon)'s basic "[measures](wikipedia://en/6101309)" of the [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy) content of random variables and a [measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) over sets. Namely the [joint entropy](wikipedia://en/910967), [conditional entropy](wikipedia://en/908548), and [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282) can be considered as the measure of a [set union](wikipedia://en/47949), [set difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_difference), and [set intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429), respectively (Reza pp. 106–108).

If we associate the existence of abstract [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) $\tilde X$ and $\tilde Y$ to arbitrary [discrete](wikipedia://en/8492) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* and *Y*, somehow representing the [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy) borne by *X* and *Y*, respectively, such that:

- $\mu(\tilde X \cap \tilde Y) = 0$ whenever *X* and *Y* are unconditionally [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence), and
- $\tilde X = \tilde Y$ whenever *X* and *Y* are such that either one is completely determined by the other (i.e. by a bijection);

where $\mu$ is a [signed measure](wikipedia://en/2985811) over these sets, and we set:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{H}(X) & = \mu(\tilde X), \\ \mathrm{H}(Y) & = \mu(\tilde Y), \\ \mathrm{H}(X,Y) & = \mu(\tilde X \cup \tilde Y), \\ \mathrm{H}(X\mid Y) & = \mu(\tilde X \setminus \tilde Y), \\ \operatorname{I}(X;Y) & = \mu(\tilde X \cap \tilde Y); \end{aligned}$$

we find that [Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Elwood_Shannon)'s "measure" of information content satisfies all the postulates and basic properties of a formal [signed measure](wikipedia://en/2985811) over sets, as commonly illustrated in an **[information diagram](wikipedia://en/18372173)**. This allows the sum of two measures to be written:

$$\mu(A)+\mu(B)=\mu(A\cup B)+\mu(A\cap B)$$

and the analog of [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569) ($\mu(A)+\mu(B\setminus A)=\mu(B)+\mu(A\setminus B)$) allows the difference of two measures to be written:

$$\mu(A)-\mu(B)=\mu(A\setminus B)-\mu(B\setminus A)$$

This can be a handy [mnemonic device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemonic_device) in some situations, e.g.

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{H}(X,Y) & =\mathrm{H}(X)+\mathrm{H}(Y\mid X) & \mu(\tilde X \cup \tilde Y) & =\mu(\tilde X)+\mu(\tilde Y \setminus \tilde X) \\ \operatorname{I}(X;Y) & =\mathrm{H}(X)-\mathrm{H}(X\mid Y) & \mu(\tilde X \cap \tilde Y) & =\mu(\tilde X)-\mu(\tilde X \setminus \tilde Y) \end{aligned}$$

Note that measures (expectation values of the logarithm) of true probabilities are called "entropy" and generally represented by the letter *H*, while other measures are often referred to as "information" or "correlation" and generally represented by the letter *I*. For notational simplicity, the letter *I* is sometimes used for all measures.

## Multivariate mutual information {#Multivariate_mutual_information}

Certain extensions to the definitions of Shannon's basic measures of information are necessary to deal with the [σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) generated by the sets that would be associated to three or more arbitrary random variables. (See Reza pp. 106–108 for an informal but rather complete discussion.) Namely $\mathrm{H}(X,Y,Z,\cdots)$ needs to be defined in the obvious way as the entropy of a joint distribution, and a multivariate [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282) $\operatorname{I}(X;Y;Z;\cdots)$ defined in a suitable manner so that we can set:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{H}(X,Y,Z,\cdots) & = \mu(\tilde X \cup \tilde Y \cup \tilde Z \cup \cdots), \\ \operatorname{I}(X;Y;Z;\cdots) & = \mu(\tilde X \cap \tilde Y \cap \tilde Z \cap \cdots); \end{aligned}$$

in order to define the (signed) measure over the whole σ-algebra. There is no single universally accepted definition for the multivariate mutual information, but the one that corresponds here to the measure of a set intersection is due to Fano (1966: p. 57-59). The definition is recursive. As a base case the mutual information of a single random variable is defined to be its entropy: $\operatorname{I}(X)=\mathrm{H}(X)$. Then for $n\geq 2$ we set

$$\operatorname{I}(X_1; \cdots; X_n) = \operatorname{I}(X_1; \cdots; X_{n-1}) - \operatorname{I}(X_1; \cdots; X_{n-1}\mid X_n),$$

where the [conditional mutual information](wikipedia://en/18867510) is defined as

$$\operatorname{I}(X_1; \cdots; X_{n-1}\mid X_n) = \mathbb E_{X_n}\big(\operatorname{I}(X_1; \cdots; X_{n-1})\mid X_n\big).$$

The first step in the recursion yields Shannon's definition $\operatorname{I}(X_1;X_2)=\mathrm{H}(X_1)-\mathrm{H}(X_1\mid X_2).$ The multivariate mutual information (same as [interaction information](wikipedia://en/6964629) but for a change in sign) of three or more random variables can be negative as well as positive: Let *X* and *Y* be two independent fair coin flips, and let *Z* be their [exclusive or](wikipedia://en/105979). Then $\operatorname{I}(X;Y;Z) = - 1$ bit.

Many other variations are possible for three or more random variables: for example, $\operatorname{I}(X,Y;Z)$ is the mutual information of the joint distribution of *X* and *Y* relative to *Z*, and can be interpreted as $\mu((\tilde X \cup \tilde Y) \cap \tilde Z).$ Many more complicated expressions can be built this way, and still have meaning, e.g. $\operatorname{I}(X,Y;Z\mid W),$ or $\mathrm{H}(X,Z\mid W,Y).$
