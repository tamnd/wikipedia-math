# Substructure search

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Substructure search"}

*Image: Lenalidomide substructures.svg.* The drug [lenalidomide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenalidomide) contains substructures [isoindoline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isoindoline) (red) and [glutarimide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glutarimide) (blue)

**Substructure search** (**SSS**) is a method to retrieve from a [database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_database) only those [chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) matching a pattern of atoms and bonds which a user specifies. It is an application of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), specifically [subgraph matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgraph_matching) in which the query is a [hydrogen-depleted molecular graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen-depleted_molecular_graph). The mathematical foundations for the method were laid in the 1870s, when it was suggested that [chemical structure drawings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule_editor) were equivalent to graphs with atoms as vertices and bonds as edges. SSS is now a standard part of [cheminformatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheminformatics) and is widely used by pharmaceutical chemists in [drug discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_discovery).

:::

There are many commercial systems that provide SSS, typically having a [graphical user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_user_interface) and chemical drawing software. Large publicly available databases like [PubChem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PubChem) and [ChemSpider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChemSpider) can be searched this way, as can [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia)'s articles describing individual chemicals.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Substructure search is used to retrieve from a [database of chemicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_database) those which contain the pattern of atoms and bonds specified by a user. It is implemented using a specialist type of [query language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Query_language) and in real-world applications the search may be further constrained using [logical operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_operator) on additional data held in the database. Thus "return all [carboxylic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxylic_acids) where a sample of >1 g is available".[^o4][^o5] One definition of "substructure" was provided in 2008: "given two chemical structures A and B, if structure A is fully contained in structure B, then A is a substructure of B, while B is a superstructure of A."[^o6] **molecular graph**: The graph with differently labelled (coloured) vertices (chromatic graph) which represent different kinds of atoms and differently labelled (coloured) edges related to different types of bonds. Within the topological electron distribution theory, a complete network of the bond paths for a given nuclear configuration.[^o7] In this definition, the word "structure" is not synonymous with "[compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound)". If it were, the structure for [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol), CH3CH2OH would not be a substructure of [propanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propanol), CH3CH2CH2OH, since the terminal CH<sub>3</sub> of ethanol is not *fully contained* at the propanol chain two atoms away from the OH group. Instead the query structure is, formally, a [hydrogen-depleted molecular graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen-depleted_molecular_graph). The search is thus for substances which contain three atoms and two single bonds connected as C–C–O. Propanol is a "hit", as is [diethyl ether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diethyl_ether), with C–C–O–C–C. If a user wished to limit the hits to [alcohols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohols), then the query structure would have to be drawn with an "explicit hydrogen", as C–C–O–H and ether would no longer match.[^o4] In mathematical terms, finding substructures is an application of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), specifically [subgraph matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgraph_matching).[^o8]

### Examples {#Examples}

Standard conventions used when chemists draw [chemical structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_structure)[^o11] need to be considered when implementing substructure search. Historically, the representation of [tautomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautomer)[^o12] forms and [stereochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereochemistry)[^o13] has posed difficulties. This can be illustrated using [histidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histidine).[^o14]

*Image: Histidine isomers and tautomers.svg.*

The top row shows the standard two-dimensional chemical drawing for (S)-histidine (the natural isomer of this [amino acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid)), its [enantiomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enantiomer) (R)-histidine and a drawing which conventionally indicates the [racemic mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racemic_mixture) of equal amounts of the R and S forms.[^o15] The bottom row shows the same three compounds with the [imidazole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imidazole) ring drawn in its alternative tautomer form. For histidine, it has been experimentally determined by <sup>15</sup>N NMR spectroscopy that the 1-H tautomer is preferred over the 3-H form in samples.[^o16] Choice of representation for storage in a database can influence substucture searches. All six drawings are hits for a propanol substructure C–C–C–O, as shown in red. However, only the top row would, apparently, be a hit for the blue substructure of 1-H imidazole-4-methyl, as this is not *fully contained* in the other three compounds. In fact, each vertical pair is the same chemical substance: tautomers in general cannot be isolated as separate samples.[^o12] In modern databases, substances are held in a single [canonical form](wikipedia://en/515096), with checks made for uniqueness. The [InChIKey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Chemical_Identifier#InChIKey) provides one way to do this.[^o14] (S)-Histidine's standard key is HNDVDQJCIGZPNO-YFKPBYRVSA-N,[^o17] (R)-histidine's key is HNDVDQJCIGZPNO-RXMQYKEDSA-N[^o18] and (RS)-histidine's is HNDVDQJCIGZPNO-UHFFFAOYSA-N.[^o19] The first block of 14 letters is identical for all these substances, as it encodes the molecular graph.[^o14]

## Query interfaces and search algorithms {#Query_interfaces_and_search_algorithms}

Most substructure search systems present the user with a [graphical user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_user_interface) with a [chemical structure drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule_editor) component. Query structures may contain bonding patterns such as "single/aromatic" or "any" to provide flexibility. Similarly, the vertices which in an actual compound would be a specific atom may be replaced with an atom list in the query. [*Cis*–*trans* isomerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cis–trans_isomerism) at [double bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bond) is catered for by giving a choice of retrieving only the [E form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E–Z_notation), the [Z form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E–Z_notation), or both.[^o4][^o21]

The algorithms for searching are computationally intensive, often of [O](wikipedia://en/44578) (*n*<sup>3</sup>) or O (*n*<sup>4</sup>) time complexity (where *n* is the number of atoms involved) but the problem is known to be [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).[^o22] Speedups are achieved using fragment screening as a first step. This pre-computation typically involves creation of [bitstrings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitstring) representing presence or absence of molecular fragments. Target compounds that do not possess the fragments present in the query cannot be hits and are eliminated.[^o23][^o24] Atom-by-atom-searching, in which a mapping of the query's atoms and bonds with the target molecule is sought, is usually done with a variant of the Ullman algorithm.[^o8][^o25]

::: {#o27 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Implementations"}

## Implementations {#Implementations}

, substructure search is a standard feature in [chemical databases accessible via the web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chemical_databases). Large databases such as [PubChem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PubChem),[^o28][^o21] maintained by the [National Center for Biotechnology Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Center_for_Biotechnology_Information) and [ChemSpider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChemSpider),[^o29] maintained by the [Royal Society of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Chemistry) have graphical interfaces for search. The [Chemical Abstracts Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Abstracts_Service), a division of the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society), provides tools to search the chemical literature and [Reaxys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaxys) supplied by [Elsevier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elsevier) covers both chemicals and reaction information, including that originally held in the [Beilstein database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beilstein_database).[^o30] [PATENTSCOPE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patentscope) maintained by the [World Intellectual Property Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Intellectual_Property_Organization) makes chemical patents accessible by substructure[^o31] and Wikipedia's articles describing individual chemicals can also be searched that way.[^o32]

Suppliers of chemicals as synthesis intermediates or for [high-throughput screening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-throughput_screening) routinely provide search interfaces. Currently, the largest database that can be freely searched by the public is the [ZINC database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZINC_database), which is claimed to contain over 37 billion commercially available molecules.[^o33][^o34]

## History {#History}

*Image: Historic Benzene Formulae Kekulé (original).png.* Kekulé structure of benzene, 1872

The idea that chemical structures as depicted using drawings of the type introduced by [Kekulé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Kekulé) were related to what is now called [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) was suggested by the mathematician [J. J. Sylvester](wikipedia://en/234861) in 1878. He was the first to use the word "graph" in the sense of a [network](wikipedia://en/766409).[^o36][^o37] [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) had already, in 1874, considered how to enumerate chemical [isomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomers), in what was an early approach to [molecular graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_graph), where atoms are at [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) and bonds correspond to [edges](wikipedia://en/325802#edge).[^o38][^o39] **structural formula**: A formula which gives information about the way the atoms in a molecule are connected and arranged in space.[^o40] In the 20th century, chemists developed standard ways to show [structural formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_formula), especially for individual [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compounds) that were increasingly being synthesized and tested as potential drugs or agrochemicals,[^o41][^o11] By the 1950s, as the number of compounds made and tested grew, the first attempts to create [chemical databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_databases) were made and the sub-discipline of [cheminformatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheminformatics) was established.[^o42] As stated in 2012, "searching for substructures in molecules belongs to the most elementary tasks in cheminformatics and is nowadays part of virtually every cheminformatics software".[^o43]

*Image: Markush structure.svg.* Example of a [Markush structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markush_structure)

The first suggested use for substructure search was in 1957, to reduce the workload of [patent examiners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent_examiners). They have to search published literature to decide whether an invention is novel, which for [chemical patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_patents) often means finding known examples within the generic claims of a Markush structure.[^o44][^o42] Before this could become a reality, a number of developments were required. Importantly, the existing literature had to be made searchable and a way to input a [chemical structure query](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule_editor) and return the matching results had to devised. These requirements had been partially met as early as 1881 when [Friedrich Konrad Beilstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Konrad_Beilstein) introduced the *[Handbuch der organischen Chemie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beilstein_database)* (*Handbook of Organic Chemistry*) which carefully classified known chemicals in a very systematic manner so that all examples containing a given [heterocycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterocycle) would be located together.[^o45][^o46]

In 1907, the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society) set up the [Chemical Abstracts Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Abstracts_Service) (CAS). This weekly subscription service included a printed publication with summaries of articles in thousands of scholarly journals and claims in worldwide patents. This had a chemical substance index that, in principle, allowed searching by chemical name or formula.[^o47] However, it was only when the CAS records had been fully converted into [machine-readable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine-readable) form and the internet was available to connect its database to end-users that comprehensive searching became possible. CAS provided various specialist search services from the 1980s but it was not until 2008 that its "SciFinder" system became available via [the web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_web).[^o48]

By the 1960s, companies synthesizing and testing new chemicals made significant progress in creating in-house databases. [Imperial Chemical Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Chemical_Industries) stored chemical structures encoded as text [strings](wikipedia://en/27701), using [Wiswesser line notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiswesser_line_notation). Its associated CROSSBOW software allowed substructure search using key-based searches followed by more processor-intensive atom-by-atom search.[^o49][^o50] It was recognised that research chemists wanted not only to search company collections for existing inventory but also to search third-party databases supplied by vendors of small-molecule intermediates. The latter application evolved as a collaboration involving six companies with pharmaceutical interests and their commercial suppliers.[^o51][^o14]

By the 1980s, other line notations were used for commercially available substructure search systems. [SMILES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMILES) encoding, together with its [SMARTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMILES_arbitrary_target_specification) query language,[^o52] and [SYBYL line notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SYBYL_line_notation)[^o14][^o53] are examples.[^o54] A comprehensive survey of then-available chemical information systems was produced for [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA) in 1985.[^o55]

The need to combine chemistry search with biological data produced by screening compounds at ever-larger scales led to implementation of systems such as MACCS.[^o55][^o56] This commercial system from [MDL Information Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MDL_Information_Systems) made use of an algorithm specifically designed for storage and search within groups of chemicals that differed only in their stereochemistry.[^o57] A review of the many systems available by the mid-1980s pointed out that "most in-house developed systems have been replaced with commercially available standardised software for managing chemical structure databases."[^o58] The [MDL Molfile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MDL_Molfile) is now an [open file format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_file_format) for storing single-molecule data in the form of a connection table.[^o59][^o14]

By the 2000s, [personal computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computers) had become powerful enough that storage and search of chemistry within [office software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_software) such as [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel) was possible.[^o60]

Subsequent developments involved the use of new techniques to allow efficient searches over very large databases and, importantly, the use of a standardised [International Chemical Identifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Chemical_Identifier), a type of line notation, to uniquely define a chemical substance.[^o14][^o33][^o61][^o62]
