# History of information theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="History of information theory"}

The study of **[information theory](wikipedia://en/14773)** began with the publication of [Claude E. Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_E._Shannon)'s classic paper "[A Mathematical Theory of Communication](wikipedia://en/828061)" in the *[Bell System Technical Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_System_Technical_Journal)* in July and October 1948, although Shannon had substantially completed the paper at Bell Labs by the end of 1944,

:::

Shannon introduced the qualitative and quantitative model of communication as a statistical process, opening with the assertion that

"The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point, either exactly or approximately, a message selected at another point."

With it came the ideas of

- the [information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy) and [redundancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redundancy_%28information_theory%29) of a source, and its relevance through the [source coding theorem](wikipedia://en/1208872);
- the [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282), and the [channel capacity](wikipedia://en/231204) of a noisy channel, including the promise of perfect loss-free communication given by the [noisy-channel coding theorem](wikipedia://en/3474289);
- the practical result of the [Shannon–Hartley law](wikipedia://en/71085) for the channel capacity of a Gaussian channel; and of course
- the [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) - a new way of seeing the most fundamental unit of information.

## Before 1948 {#Before_1948}

### Early telecommunications {#Early_telecommunications}

Some of the oldest methods of [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunication) implicitly used some of the ideas that were later formalized by Shannon in the development of information theory. In [telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphy), starting in the 1830s, [Morse code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_code) considered the frequency of [more common letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_frequencies). Thus the letter "E", which is expressed as one "dot", is transmitted more quickly than less common letters like "J", which is expressed by one "dot" followed by three "dashes". The idea of encoding information in this manner is the cornerstone of [lossless data compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossless_data_compression). A hundred years later, [frequency modulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_modulation) illustrated that [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_%28signal_processing%29) can be considered merely another degree of freedom. The [vocoder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vocoder), now largely looked at as an audio engineering curiosity, was originally designed in 1939 to use less bandwidth than that of an original message, in much the same way that [mobile phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_phone) now trade off voice quality with bandwidth.

### Quantitative ideas of information {#Quantitative_ideas_of_information}

The most direct antecedents of Shannon's work were two papers published in the 1920s by [Harry Nyquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Nyquist) and [Ralph Hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Hartley), who were both still research leaders at Bell Labs when Shannon arrived in the early 1940s.

Nyquist's 1924 paper, "Certain Factors Affecting Telegraph Speed", is mostly concerned with some detailed engineering aspects of telegraph signals. But a more theoretical section discusses quantifying "intelligence" and the "line speed" at which it can be transmitted by a communication system, giving the relation

$$W = K \log m \,$$

where *W* is the speed of transmission of intelligence, *m* is the number of different voltage levels to choose from at each time step, and *K* is a constant.[^o5]

Hartley's 1928 paper, called simply "Transmission of Information", went further by using the word *information* (in a technical sense), and making explicitly clear that information in this context was a measurable quantity, reflecting only the receiver's ability to distinguish that one sequence of symbols had been intended by the sender rather than any other—quite regardless of any associated meaning or other psychological or semantic aspect the symbols might represent. This amount of information he quantified as

$$H = \log S^n \,$$

where *S* was the number of possible symbols, and *n* the number of symbols in a transmission. The natural unit of information was therefore the decimal digit, much later renamed the [hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_%28unit%29) in his honour as a unit or scale or measure of information. The [Hartley information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_information), *H*<sub>0</sub>, is still used as a quantity for the logarithm of the total number of possibilities.[^o6]

A similar unit of log<sub>10</sub> probability, the *ban*, and its derived unit the [deciban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_%28unit%29) (one tenth of a ban), were introduced by [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) in 1940 as part of the statistical analysis of the breaking of the German second world war [Enigma](wikipedia://en/872175) cyphers. The *decibannage* represented the reduction in (the logarithm of) the total number of possibilities (similar to the change in the Hartley information); and also the [log-likelihood ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-likelihood_ratio) (or change in the weight of evidence) that could be inferred for one hypothesis over another from a set of observations. The expected change in the weight of evidence is equivalent to what was later called the Kullback [discrimination information](wikipedia://en/1115052#Discrimination_information).

But underlying this notion was still the idea of equal a-priori probabilities, rather than the information content of events of unequal probability; nor yet any underlying picture of questions regarding the communication of such varied outcomes.

In a 1939 letter to [Vannevar Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vannevar_Bush), Shannon had already outlined some of his initial ideas of information theory.[^o7]

### Entropy in statistical mechanics {#Entropy_in_statistical_mechanics}

One area where unequal probabilities were indeed well known was statistical mechanics, where [Ludwig Boltzmann](wikipedia://en/544255) had, in the context of his [H-theorem](wikipedia://en/424440) of 1872, first introduced the quantity

$$H = - \sum f_i \log f_i$$

as a measure of the breadth of the spread of states available to a single particle in a gas of like particles, where *f* represented the relative [frequency distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_distribution) of each possible state. Boltzmann argued mathematically that the effect of collisions between the particles would cause the *H*-function to inevitably increase from any initial configuration until equilibrium was reached; and further identified it as an underlying microscopic rationale for the macroscopic [thermodynamic entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_%28classical_thermodynamics%29) of [Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clausius).

Boltzmann's definition was soon reworked by the American mathematical physicist [J. Willard Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Willard_Gibbs) into a general formula for statistical-mechanical entropy, no longer requiring identical and non-interacting particles, but instead based on the probability distribution *p<sub>i</sub>* for the complete microstate *i* of the total system:

$$S = -k_\text{B} \sum p_i \ln p_i \,$$

This (Gibbs) entropy, from statistical mechanics, can be found to directly correspond to the Clausius's classical thermodynamic [definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_%28classical_thermodynamics%29).

Shannon himself was apparently not particularly aware of the [close similarity](wikipedia://en/3325140) between his new measure and earlier work in thermodynamics, but [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) was. It is said that, when Shannon was deciding what to call his new measure and fearing the term 'information' was already over-used, von Neumann told him firmly: "You should call it entropy, for two reasons. In the first place your uncertainty function has been used in statistical mechanics under that name, so it already has a name. In the second place, and more important, no one really knows what entropy really is, so in a debate you will always have the advantage."

(Connections between information-theoretic entropy and thermodynamic entropy, including the important contributions by [Rolf Landauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Landauer) in the 1960s, are explored further in the article *[Entropy in thermodynamics and information theory](wikipedia://en/3325140)*).

## Development since 1948 {#Development_since_1948}

The publication of Shannon's 1948 paper, "[A Mathematical Theory of Communication](wikipedia://en/828061)", in the *Bell System Technical Journal* was the founding of information theory as we know it today. Many developments and applications of the theory have taken place since then, which have made many modern devices for [data communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_communication) and storage such as [CD-ROMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD-ROM) and [mobile phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_phone) possible.

Notable later developments are listed in a [timeline of information theory](wikipedia://en/3475938), including:

- The 1951, invention of [Huffman encoding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huffman_encoding), a method of finding optimal [prefix codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_codes) for [lossless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossless) [data compression](wikipedia://en/8013).
- [Irving S. Reed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_S._Reed) and [David E. Muller](wikipedia://en/35667779) proposing [Reed–Muller codes](wikipedia://en/2032752) in 1954.
- The 1960 proposal of [Reed–Solomon codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed–Solomon_code).
- In 1966, [Fumitada Itakura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumitada_Itakura) ([Nagoya University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya_University)) and Shuzo Saito ([Nippon Telegraph and Telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippon_Telegraph_and_Telephone)) develop [linear predictive coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_predictive_coding) (LPC), a form of [speech coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_coding).[^o10]
- In 1968, [Elwyn Berlekamp](wikipedia://en/655893) invents the [Berlekamp–Massey algorithm](wikipedia://en/514832); its application to decoding BCH and Reed–Solomon codes is pointed out by [James L. Massey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lee_Massey) the following year.
- In 1972, [Nasir Ahmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._Ahmed) proposes the [discrete cosine transform](wikipedia://en/59962) (DCT).[^o11] It later becomes the most widely used [lossy compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossy_compression) algorithm, and the basis for [digital media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_media) compression standards from 1988 onwards, including [H.26x](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.26x) (since [H.261](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.261)) and [MPEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MPEG) [video coding standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_coding_standards),[^o12] [JPEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG) [image compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_compression),[^o13] [MP3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MP3) [audio compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_compression_%28data%29),[^o14] and [Advanced Audio Coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Audio_Coding) (AAC).[^o15]
- In 1976, [Gottfried Ungerboeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Ungerboeck) gives the first paper on [trellis modulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trellis_modulation); a more detailed exposition in 1982 leads to a raising of analogue modem [POTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_old_telephone_service) speeds from 9.6 kbit/s to 33.6 kbit/s
- In 1977, [Abraham Lempel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lempel) and [Jacob Ziv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Ziv) develop Lempel–Ziv compression ([LZ77](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LZ77))
- In the early 1980s, Renuka P. Jindal at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) improves the noise performance of [metal–oxide–semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal–oxide–semiconductor) (MOS) devices, resolving issues that limited their receiver sensitivity and [data rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_rates). This leads to the wide adoption of MOS technology in [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser) lightwave systems and [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless) terminal applications, enabling [Edholm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edholm's_law).[^o16]
- In 1989, [Phil Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Katz) publishes [the `.zip` format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZIP_%28file_format%29) including [DEFLATE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEFLATE) (LZ77 + Huffman coding); later to become the most widely used archive container.
- In 1995, [Benjamin Schumacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Schumacher) coins the term [qubit](wikipedia://en/25284) and proves the quantum noiseless coding theorem.
