# Arthur Hobbs (mathematician)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arthur Hobbs"}

**Arthur Marmaduke Hobbs** (19 June 1940 – 25 October 2020)[^o2] was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) specializing in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). He spent his teaching career at [Texas A&M University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_A&M_University).

:::

## Early and personal life {#Early_and_personal_life}

Arthur Hobbs was born on June 19, 1940, in Washington, D.C. He was the eldest child of his family, having two younger brothers. His father was an engineer and later became an attorney. The family moved in 1941 to Pennsylvania, and again after World War II to [South Bend, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Bend,_Indiana), where Arthur Hobbs grew up. He married his wife Barbara in 1964. They were married for 50 years until she died in 2014. They had two daughters, six grandchildren, and one great-granddaughter.[^o4]

## Education and early career {#Education_and_early_career}

After graduating in 1958 from [John Adams High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Adams_High_School_%28South_Bend,_Indiana%29), Hobbs studied mathematics at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan), graduating in 1962. He then served in the US Army in Washington, D.C., for approximately two years, and then from 1965 to 1968 worked for the [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards).

He received his Ph.D. from the [University of Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Waterloo) in Ontario, Canada, in 1971. His research focused on [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/244437) cycles, particularly concentrating in squares and higher powers of graphs, and his thesis adviser was the graph theorist [William Thomas Tutte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomas_Tutte).

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

After receiving his Ph.D., Hobbs began teaching as a mathematics professor at [Texas A&M University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_A&M_University) in 1971, where he worked until his retirement in 2008. He was the faculty senator for twelve years, and also taught various mathematics courses including, but not limited to [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492), [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). ## Research {#Research}

Hobbs' research before entering graduate school was on thickness of graphs. Later, in graduate school and for ten years following, he concentrated on Hamiltonian cycles, particularly in squares and higher powers of graphs. He then spent a couple of years working on the Gyarfas and Lehel conjecture that any family of trees T1; T2; : : : Tn, with 1; 2; : : : ; *n* vertices respectively, can be packed in an edge-disjoint manner into the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on *n* vertices. This conjecture is still open. Hobbs also worked with packings of graphs with trees and coverings by trees, which he worked on with several co-authors, including [Paul A. Catlin](wikipedia://en/37440786), Jerrold W. Grossman, Lavanya Kannan, and Hong-Jian Lai.

They defined the fractional [arboricity](wikipedia://en/2476311) of a graph as

$$\gamma(G) = \max_{H \subseteq G} \left({{|E(H)}|\over{|V(H)| - \omega(H)}}\right),$$

where *ω*(*H* is the number of components of H and the maximum is taken over all subgraphs H for which the denominator is not zero. They also defined the [strength of a graph](wikipedia://en/23093011) as

$$\eta(G) = \min_{S \subseteq E(G)} \left( {|S|\over{\omega(G-S)-\omega(G)}}\right),$$

where the maximum is taken over all subsets *S* of *E*(*G*) for which the denominator is not zero. Additionally, they characterized uniformly dense graphs, and have found several classes of uniformly dense graphs and several ways of constructing such graphs.

Hobbs had also done research in [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) theory.
