# Baum–Connes conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Baum–Connes conjecture"}

*Image: Baum\_and\_Connes.jpg.* Photo of Baum and Connes

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [operator K-theory](wikipedia://en/13314265), the **Baum–Connes conjecture** suggests a link between the [K-theory](wikipedia://en/13314265) of the [reduced C\*-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_C*-algebra) of a [group](wikipedia://en/41890) and the [K-homology](wikipedia://en/3021282) of the [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) of [proper actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_action) of that group. The conjecture sets up a correspondence between different areas of mathematics, with the K-homology of the classifying space being related to geometry, [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) theory, and [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), while the K-theory of the group's reduced C\*-algebra is a purely analytical object.

:::

The conjecture, if true, would have some older famous conjectures as consequences. For instance, the surjectivity part implies the [Kadison–Kaplansky conjecture for discrete torsion-free groups](wikipedia://en/4025490#Group_rings), and the injectivity is closely related to the [Novikov conjecture](wikipedia://en/3035194).

The conjecture is also closely related to [index theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_theory), as the [assembly map](wikipedia://en/21311179) $\mu$ is a sort of index, and it plays a major role in [Alain Connes](wikipedia://en/340)' [noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917) program.

The origins of the conjecture go back to [Fredholm theory](wikipedia://en/4355120), the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](wikipedia://en/324752) and the interplay of geometry with operator K-theory as expressed in the works of Brown, Douglas and Fillmore, among many other motivating subjects.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Let Γ be a [second countable](wikipedia://en/901459) [locally compact group](wikipedia://en/1651204) (for instance a countable [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536)). One can define a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891)

$$\mu: RK^\Gamma_*(\underline{E\Gamma}) \to K_*(C^*_r(\Gamma)),$$

called the **assembly map**, from the equivariant K-homology with $\Gamma$-compact supports of the classifying space of proper actions $\underline{E\Gamma}$ to the K-theory of the [reduced C\*-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_C*-algebra) of Γ. The subscript index <sub>\*</sub> can be 0 or 1.

[Paul Baum](wikipedia://en/35077006) and [Alain Connes](wikipedia://en/340) introduced the following conjecture (1982) about this morphism:

**Baum-Connes Conjecture.** The assembly map $\mu$ is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828).

As the left hand side tends to be more easily accessible than the right hand side, because there are hardly any general structure theorems of the $C^*$-algebra, one usually views the conjecture as an "explanation" of the right hand side.

The original formulation of the conjecture was somewhat different, as the notion of equivariant K-homology was not yet common in 1982.

In case $\Gamma$ is discrete and torsion-free, the left hand side reduces to the non-equivariant K-homology with compact supports of the ordinary classifying space $B\Gamma$ of $\Gamma$.

There is also more general form of the conjecture, known as Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients, where both sides have coefficients in the form of a $C^*$-algebra $A$ on which $\Gamma$ acts by $C^*$-automorphisms. It says in [KK-language](wikipedia://en/13314087) that the assembly map

$$\mu_{A,\Gamma}: RKK^\Gamma_*(\underline{E\Gamma},A) \to K_*(A\rtimes_\lambda \Gamma),$$

is an isomorphism, containing the case without coefficients as the case $A=\mathbb{C}.$

However, counterexamples to the conjecture with coefficients were found in 2002 by [Nigel Higson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Higson), [Vincent Lafforgue](wikipedia://en/27229287) and [Georges Skandalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Skandalis). However, the conjecture with coefficients remains an active area of research, since it is, not unlike the classical conjecture, often seen as a statement concerning particular groups or class of groups.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Let $\Gamma$ be the integers $\mathbb{Z}$. Then the left hand side is the [K-homology](wikipedia://en/3021282) of $B\mathbb{Z}$ which is the circle. The $C^*$-algebra of the integers is by the commutative Gelfand–Naimark transform, which reduces to the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) in this case, isomorphic to the algebra of continuous functions on the circle. So the right hand side is the topological K-theory of the circle. One can then show that the assembly map is [KK-theoretic](wikipedia://en/13314087) [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732) as defined by [Gennadi Kasparov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gennadi_Kasparov), which is an isomorphism.

:::

## Results {#Results}

The conjecture without coefficients is still open, although the field has received great attention since 1982.

The conjecture is proved for the following classes of groups:

- Discrete subgroups of [$SO(n,1)$](wikipedia://en/293609) and $SU(n,1)$.
- Groups with the [Haagerup property](wikipedia://en/15970956), sometimes called [a-T-menable groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-T-menability). These are groups that admit an isometric action on an affine Hilbert space $H$ which is proper in the sense that $\lim_{n\to\infty} g_n\xi\to\infty$ for all $\xi\in H$ and all sequences of group elements $g_n$ with $\lim_{n\to\infty}g_n\to\infty$. Examples of a-T-menable groups are [amenable groups](wikipedia://en/657430), [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004), groups acting properly on [trees](wikipedia://en/48560), and groups acting properly on simply connected [$CAT(0)$](wikipedia://en/12941912) cubical complexes.
- Groups that admit a [finite presentation](wikipedia://en/99494) with only one relation.
- Discrete cocompact subgroups of real Lie groups of real rank 1.
- Cocompact lattices in $SL(3,\mathbb{R}), SL(3,\mathbb{C})$ or $SL(3,\mathbb{Q}_p)$. It was a long-standing problem since the first days of the conjecture to expose a single infinite [property T-group](wikipedia://en/1362724) that satisfies it. However, such a group was given by V. Lafforgue in 1998 as he showed that cocompact lattices in $SL(3,\mathbb{R})$ have the property of rapid decay and thus satisfy the conjecture.
- [Gromov hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/3172817) and their subgroups.
- Among non-discrete groups, the conjecture has been shown in 2003 by J. Chabert, S. Echterhoff and R. Nest for the vast class of all almost connected groups (i. e. groups having a cocompact connected component), and all groups of $k$-rational points of a [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885) over a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) $k$ of characteristic zero (e.g. $k = \mathbb{Q}_p$). For the important subclass of real reductive groups, the conjecture had already been shown in 1987 by [Antony Wassermann](wikipedia://en/57866112).[^o8]

Injectivity is known for a much larger class of groups thanks to the Dirac-dual-Dirac method. This goes back to ideas of [Michael Atiyah](wikipedia://en/20698) and was developed in great generality by [Gennadi Kasparov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gennadi_Kasparov) in 1987. Injectivity is known for the following classes:

- Discrete subgroups of connected [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) or virtually connected Lie groups.
- Discrete subgroups of [p-adic groups](wikipedia://en/51423).
- Bolic groups (a certain generalization of hyperbolic groups).
- Groups which admit an amenable action on some compact space.

The simplest example of a group for which it is not known whether it satisfies the conjecture is $SL_3(\mathbb{Z})$.
