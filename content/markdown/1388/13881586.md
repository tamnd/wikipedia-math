# Semimodular lattice

generalizations of modularity in terms of the atomic covering relation

*Image: Centred hexagon lattice D2.svg.* The centred hexagon lattice *S*<sub>7</sub>, also known as *D*<sub>2</sub>, is semimodular but not modular.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semimodular lattice"}

In the branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) known as [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), a **semimodular lattice**, is a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) that satisfies the following condition:

:::

**Semimodular law**

*a* ∧ *b* \<: *a* implies *b* \<: *a* ∨ *b*.

The notation *a* \<: *b* means that *b* [covers](wikipedia://en/14835049) *a*, i.e. *a* \< *b* and there is no element *c* such that *a* \< *c* \< *b*.

An [atomistic](wikipedia://en/998824) semimodular [bounded lattice](wikipedia://en/310878) is called a [matroid lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matroid_lattice) because such lattices are equivalent to (simple) [matroids](wikipedia://en/244321). An atomistic semimodular bounded lattice of finite length is called a [geometric lattice](wikipedia://en/17243191) and corresponds to a matroid of finite rank.[^o2]

Semimodular lattices are also known as upper semimodular lattices; the [dual](wikipedia://en/600618) notion is that of a **lower semimodular lattice**. A finite lattice is [modular](wikipedia://en/1089311) if and only if it is both upper and lower semimodular.

A finite lattice, or more generally a lattice satisfying the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189) or the descending chain condition, is semimodular if and only if it is [M-symmetric](wikipedia://en/1089311#Modular_pairs_and_related_notions). Some authors refer to M-symmetric lattices as semimodular lattices.[^o3]

A semimodular lattice is one kind of [algebraic](wikipedia://en/680261) lattice.

## Birkhoff's condition {#Birkhoff's_condition}

A lattice is sometimes called **weakly semimodular** if it satisfies the following condition due to [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916):

**Birkhoff's condition**

If *a* ∧ *b* \<: *a* and *a* ∧ *b* \<: *b*,

then *a* \<: *a* ∨ *b* and *b* \<: *a* ∨ *b*.

Every semimodular lattice is weakly semimodular. The converse is true for lattices of finite length, and more generally for upper continuous (meets distribute over joins of chains) [relatively atomic](wikipedia://en/998824) lattices.

## Mac Lane's condition {#Mac_Lane's_condition}

The following two conditions are equivalent to each other for all lattices. They were found by [Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574), who was looking for a condition that is equivalent to semimodularity for finite lattices, but does not involve the covering relation.

**Mac Lane's condition 1**

For any *a, b, c* such that *b* ∧ *c* \< *a* \< *c* \< *b* ∨ *a*,

there is an element *d* such that *b* ∧ *c* \< *d* ≤ *b* and *a* = (*a* ∨ *d*) ∧ *c*.

**Mac Lane's condition 2**

For any *a, b, c* such that *b* ∧ *c* \< *a* \< *c* \< *b* ∨ *c*,

there is an element *d* such that *b* ∧ *c* \< *d* ≤ *b* and *a* = (*a* ∨ *d*) ∧ *c*.

To see the equivalence of these two conditions, write condition 1 as *P* → *R* and condition 2 as *Q* → *R* where *R* is the common second line of each. By *a* \< *c* and monotonicity of *b* ∨ *a* in *a*, *P* → *Q* and therefore (*Q* → *R*) → (*P* → *R*), i.e. 2 → 1. For the other direction, 1 → 2, to be false would require *Q* to hold while *P* and *R* are both false. We would then have *a* \< *c* \< *b* ∨ *c* whence *c* \< *b* so *c* \< *b* ∨ *a*, making *P* true, contradiction. This second direction is only of interest when *c* \< *b* ∨ *a* is more work to prove than *c* \< *b* ∨ *c*, otherwise condition 1 would be a sufficient test.

Every lattice satisfying Mac Lane's condition is semimodular. The converse is true for lattices of finite length, and more generally for [relatively atomic](wikipedia://en/998824) lattices. Moreover, every upper continuous lattice satisfying Mac Lane's condition is M-symmetric.
