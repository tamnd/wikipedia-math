# Stokes drift

*Image: Driftwood Expanse, Northern Washington Coast.png.* An expanse of [driftwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driftwood) along the northern [coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coast) of [Washington state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_state). Stokes drift – besides e.g. [Ekman drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekman_drift) and [geostrophic currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostrophic_current) – is one of the relevant processes in the transport of [marine debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_debris).[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Stokes drift"}

For a pure [wave](wikipedia://en/33516) [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29) in [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), the **Stokes drift velocity** is the [average](wikipedia://en/60167) [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) when following a specific [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) parcel as it travels with the [fluid flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_flow). For instance, a particle floating at the [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) of [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves), experiences a net Stokes drift velocity in the direction of [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation).

:::

More generally, the Stokes drift velocity is the difference between the [average](wikipedia://en/60167) [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_and_Eulerian_coordinates) [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) of a fluid parcel, and the average [Eulerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_and_Eulerian_coordinates) [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) of the [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) at a fixed position. This [nonlinear](wikipedia://en/146103) phenomenon is named after [George Gabriel Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gabriel_Stokes), who derived expressions for this drift in his 1847 study of [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves).

The **Stokes drift** is the difference in end positions, after a predefined amount of time (usually one [wave period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_period)), as derived from a description in the [Lagrangian and Eulerian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_and_Eulerian_coordinates). The end position in the [Lagrangian description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_and_Eulerian_coordinates) is obtained by following a specific fluid parcel during the time interval. The corresponding end position in the [Eulerian description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_and_Eulerian_coordinates) is obtained by integrating the [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) at a fixed position—equal to the initial position in the Lagrangian description—during the same time interval.

The Stokes drift velocity equals the Stokes drift divided by the considered time interval. Often, the Stokes drift velocity is loosely referred to as Stokes drift. Stokes drift may occur in all instances of oscillatory flow which are [inhomogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inhomogeneous) in space. For instance in [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves), [tides](wikipedia://en/30718) and [atmospheric waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_waves).

In the [Lagrangian description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_and_Eulerian_coordinates), fluid parcels may drift far from their initial positions. As a result, the unambiguous definition of an [average](wikipedia://en/60167) Lagrangian velocity and Stokes drift velocity, which can be attributed to a certain fixed position, is by no means a trivial task. However, such an unambiguous description is provided by the *[Generalized Lagrangian Mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Lagrangian_Mean)* (GLM) theory of Andrews and McIntyre in 1978.[^o3]

The Stokes drift is important for the [mass transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_transfer) of various kinds of material and organisms by oscillatory flows. It plays a crucial role in the generation of [Langmuir circulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langmuir_circulation).[^o4] For nonlinear and [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848) water waves, accurate results on the Stokes drift have been computed and tabulated.[^o5]

## Mathematical description {#Mathematical_description}

The [Lagrangian motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_and_Eulerian_coordinates) of a fluid parcel with [position vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_vector) *x = **ξ**(**α**, t)* in the Eulerian coordinates is given by[^o7]

$$\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}} = \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\xi}}{\partial t} = \mathbf{u}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, t), t\big),$$

where

∂***ξ***/∂*t* is the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) of ***ξ***(***α***, *t*) with respect to *t*,

***ξ***(***α***, *t*) is the Lagrangian position vector of a fluid parcel,

**u**(**x**, *t*) is the Eulerian [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903),

**x** is the position vector in the [Eulerian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/5289693),

***α*** is the position vector in the [Lagrangian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/5289693),

*t* is [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time).

Often, the Lagrangian coordinates ***α*** are chosen to coincide with the Eulerian coordinates **x** at the initial time *t* = *t*<sub>0</sub>:[^o7]

$$\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, t_0) = \boldsymbol{\alpha}.$$

If the [average](wikipedia://en/60167) value of a quantity is denoted by an overbar, then the average Eulerian velocity vector **ū**<sub>E</sub> and average Lagrangian velocity vector **ū**<sub>L</sub> are

$$\begin{aligned} \bar\mathbf{u}_\text{E} &= \overline{\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t)}, \\ \bar\mathbf{u}_\text{L} &= \overline{\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, t)} = \overline{\left(\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, t)}{\partial t}\right)} = \overline{\boldsymbol{u}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}, t), t\big)}. \end{aligned}$$

Different definitions of the [average](wikipedia://en/60167) may be used, depending on the subject of study (see [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986#Ergodic_theorems)):

- [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) average,
- [space](wikipedia://en/27667) average,
- [ensemble average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ensemble_average),
- [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) average.

The Stokes drift velocity **ū**<sub>S</sub> is defined as the difference between the average Eulerian velocity and the average Lagrangian velocity:[^o8]

$$\bar\mathbf{u}_\text{S} = \bar\mathbf{u}_\text{L} - \bar\mathbf{u}_\text{E}.$$

In many situations, the [mapping](wikipedia://en/516931) of average quantities from some Eulerian position **x** to a corresponding Lagrangian position ***α*** forms a problem. Since a fluid parcel with label ***α*** traverses along a [path](wikipedia://en/1189553) of many different Eulerian positions **x**, it is not possible to assign ***α*** to a unique **x**. A mathematically sound basis for an unambiguous mapping between average Lagrangian and Eulerian quantities is provided by the theory of the [generalized Lagrangian mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Lagrangian_mean) (GLM) by Andrews and McIntyre (1978).

## Example: A one-dimensional compressible flow {#Example:_A_one-dimensional_compressible_flow}

For the Eulerian velocity as a monochromatic wave of any nature in a continuous medium: $u = \hat{u} \sin(kx - \omega t),$ one readily obtains by the [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/102140) – with $k\hat{u}/\omega$ as a small parameter – for the particle position $x = \xi(\xi_0, t)$:

$$\dot\xi = u(\xi, t) = \hat{u} \sin(k\xi - \omega t),$$

$$\xi(\xi_0, t) \approx \xi_0 + \frac{\hat{u}}{\omega} \cos(k\xi_0 - \omega t) - \frac14 \frac{k\hat{u}^2}{\omega^2} \sin 2(k\xi_0 - \omega t) + \frac12 \frac{k\hat{u}^2}{\omega} t.$$

Here the last term describes the Stokes drift velocity $\tfrac12 k\hat{u}^2/\omega.$[^o10]

## Example: Deep water waves {#Example:_Deep_water_waves}

*Image: Vitesses derive.png.* Stokes drift under periodic waves in deep water, for a [period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_%28physics%29) *T* = 5 s and a mean water depth of 25 m. *Left*: instantaneous horizontal [flow velocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity). *Right*: [average](wikipedia://en/60167) flow velocities. Black solid line: average Eulerian velocity; red dashed line: average Lagrangian velocity, as derived from the *[Generalized Lagrangian Mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Lagrangian_Mean)* (GLM).

The Stokes drift was formulated for [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves) by [George Gabriel Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gabriel_Stokes) in 1847. For simplicity, the case of [infinitely](wikipedia://en/20110824) deep water is considered, with [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) of a [sinusoidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinusoidal) wave on the [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) of a fluid layer:[^o12]

$$\eta = a \cos(kx - \omega t),$$

where

*η* is the [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation) of the [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) in the *z* direction (meters),

*a* is the wave [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) (meters),

*k* is the [wave number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_number): *k* = 2*π*/*λ* ([radians](wikipedia://en/26003) per meter),

*ω* is the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency): *ω* = 2*π*/*T* ([radians](wikipedia://en/26003) per [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second)),

*x* is the horizontal [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) and the wave propagation direction (meters),

*z* is the vertical [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate), with the positive *z* direction pointing out of the fluid layer (meters),

*λ* is the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) (meters),

*T* is the [wave period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_period) ([seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second)).

As derived below, the horizontal component *ū*<sub>S</sub>(*z*) of the Stokes drift velocity for deep-water waves is approximately:[^o13]

$$\bar{u}_\text{S} \approx \omega k a^2 \text{e}^{2kz} = \frac{4\pi^2 a^2}{\lambda T} \text{e}^{4\pi z / \lambda}.$$

As can be seen, the Stokes drift velocity *ū*<sub>S</sub> is a nonlinear quantity in terms of the wave [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) *a*. Further, the Stokes drift velocity decays exponentially with depth: at a depth of a quarter wavelength, *z* = −*λ*/4, it is about 4% of its value at the mean [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface), *z* = 0.

### Derivation {#Derivation}

It is assumed that the waves are of [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) and the [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) oscillates around the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) level *z* = 0. The waves propagate under the action of gravity, with a constant [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) by [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) (pointing downward in the negative *z* direction). Further the fluid is assumed to be [inviscid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inviscid)[^o15] and [incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477), with a constant [mass density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_density). The fluid [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) is [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational). At infinite depth, the fluid is taken to be at [rest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_%28physics%29).

Now the [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) may be represented by a [velocity potential](wikipedia://en/2448955) *φ*, satisfying the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation) and[^o12]

$$\varphi = \frac{\omega}{k} a \text{e}^{kz} \sin(kx - \omega t).$$

In order to have [non-trivial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-trivial) solutions for this [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) problem, the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) and [wave period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_period) may not be chosen arbitrarily, but must satisfy the deep-water [dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650) relation:[^o16]

$$\omega^2 = gk$$

with *g* the [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) by [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) in (m/s<sup>2</sup>). Within the framework of [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) theory, the horizontal and vertical components, *ξ<sub>x</sub>* and *ξ<sub>z</sub>* respectively, of the Lagrangian position ***ξ*** are[^o13]

$$\begin{aligned} \xi_x &= x + \int \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial x}\, \text{d}t = x - a \text{e}^{kz} \sin(kx - \omega t), \\ \xi_z &= z + \int \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial z}\, \text{d}t = z + a \text{e}^{kz} \cos(kx - \omega t). \end{aligned}$$

The horizontal component *ū*<sub>S</sub> of the Stokes drift velocity is estimated by using a [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) around **x** of the Eulerian horizontal velocity component *u<sub>x</sub>* = ∂*ξ<sub>x</sub>* / ∂*t* at the position ***ξ***:[^o7]

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{u}_\text{S} &= \overline{u_x(\boldsymbol{\xi}, t)} - \overline{u_x(\mathbf{x}, t)} \\ &= \overline{\left[ u_x(\mathbf{x}, t) + (\xi_x - x) \frac{\partial u_x(\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial x} + (\xi_z - z) \frac{\partial u_x(\mathbf{x}, t)}{\partial z} + \cdots \right]} - \overline{u_x(\mathbf{x} ,t)} \\ &\approx \overline{(\xi_x - x) \frac{\partial^2 \xi_x}{\partial x\, \partial t}} + \overline{(\xi_z - z) \frac{\partial^2 \xi_x}{\partial z\, \partial t}} \\ &= \overline{\left[-a \text{e}^{kz} \sin(kx - \omega t)\right] \left[-\omega ka \text{e}^{kz} \sin(kx - \omega t)\right]} \\ &+ \overline{\left[a \text{e}^{kz} \cos(kx - \omega t)\right] \left[\omega ka \text{e}^{kz} \cos(kx - \omega t)\right] } \\ &= \overline{\omega ka^2 \text{e}^{2kz} \left[\sin^2(kx - \omega t) + \cos^2(kx - \omega t)\right]} \\ &= \omega ka^2 \text{e}^{2kz}. \end{aligned}$$
