# Richards equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Richards equation"}

The **Richards equation** represents the movement of water in [unsaturated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vadose_zone) soils, and is attributed to [Lorenzo A. Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenzo_A._Richards) who published the equation in 1931.[^o2] It is a [quasilinear](wikipedia://en/1424309) [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564); its analytical solution is often limited to specific initial and boundary conditions.[^o3] Proof of the [existence](wikipedia://en/251177) and [uniqueness](wikipedia://en/29759120) of solution was given only in 1983 by [Alt](wikipedia://en/56765498) and [Luckhaus](wikipedia://en/31696418).[^o4] The equation is based on Darcy-Buckingham law[^o2] representing flow in porous media under variably saturated conditions, which is stated as

:::

$$\vec{q}=-\mathbf{K}(\theta) (\nabla h + \nabla z),$$

where

$\vec{q}$ is the [volumetric flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volumetric_flux) \[L/T\];

$\theta$ is the [volumetric water content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_content) \[1\];

$h$ is the liquid [pressure head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_head), which is negative for unsaturated porous media \[L\];

$\mathbf{K}(h)$ is the unsaturated [hydraulic conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_conductivity) \[L/T\];

$\nabla z$ is the geodetic head gradient, which is assumed as $\nabla z = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix} \right)$ for three-dimensional problems.

Richards equation is derived by first considering the law of [mass conservation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_conservation) for an incompressible [porous medium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porous_medium) and constant liquid density, expressed as

$$\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \vec{q} + S = 0,$$

where

$S$ is the source/sink term \[T$^{-1}$\], typically root water uptake.[^o5] Then substituting the fluxes by the Darcy-Buckingham law the following mixed-form Richards equation is obtained:

$$\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{K}(h) (\nabla h + \nabla z) - S.$$

For modeling of one-dimensional infiltration this [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) form reduces to

$$\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial t}= \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \mathbf{K}(\theta) \left (\frac{\partial h}{\partial z} + 1 \right) \right) - S.$$

Although attributed to L. A. Richards, the equation was originally introduced 9 years earlier by [Lewis Fry Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Fry_Richardson) in 1922.[^o6][^o7]

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Formulations"}

## Formulations {#Formulations}

The Richards equation appears in many articles in the environmental literature because it describes the flow in the [vadose zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vadose_zone) between the atmosphere and the [aquifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquifer). It also appears in pure mathematical journals because it has non-trivial solutions. The above-given mixed formulation involves two unknown variables: $\theta$ and $h$. This can be easily resolved by considering constitutive relation $\theta(h)$, which is known as the [water retention curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_retention_curve). Applying the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113), the Richards equation may be reformulated as either $h$-form (head based) or $\theta$-form (saturation based) Richards equation.

### Head-based {#Head-based}

By applying the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) on temporal derivative leads to

$$\frac{\partial \theta(h)}{\partial t} = \frac{\textrm{d} \theta}{\textrm{d} h} \frac{\partial h}{\partial t},$$

where $\frac{\textrm{d} \theta}{\textrm{d} h}$ is known as the [retention water capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retention_water_capacity) $C(h)$. The equation is then stated as

$$C(h)\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}= \nabla \cdot \left( \mathbf{K}(h) \nabla h + \nabla z\right) - S.$$

The head-based Richards equation is prone to the following computational issue: the discretized temporal derivative using the implicit [Rothe](wikipedia://en/67679736) method yields the following approximation:

$$\frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta t} \approx C(h) \frac{\Delta h}{\Delta t}, \quad \mbox{and so} \quad \frac{\Delta \theta}{\Delta t} - C(h) \frac{\Delta h}{\Delta t} = \varepsilon .$$

This approximation produces an error $\varepsilon$ that affects the mass conservation of the numerical solution, and so special strategies for temporal derivatives treatment are necessary.[^o11]

### Saturation-based {#Saturation-based}

By applying the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) on the spatial derivative leads to

$$\mathbf{K}(h) \nabla h = \mathbf{K}(h) \frac{\textrm{d}h}{\textrm{d} \theta} \nabla \theta,$$

where $\mathbf{K}(h) \frac{\textrm{d}h}{\textrm{d} \theta}$, which could be further formulated as $\frac{\mathbf{K}(\theta)}{C(\theta)}$, is known as the [soil water diffusivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_water_diffusivity) $\mathbf{D}(\theta)$. The equation is then stated as

$$\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial t}= \nabla \cdot \mathbf{D}(\theta) \nabla \theta - S.$$

The saturation-based Richards equation is prone to the following computational issues. Since the limits $\lim_{\theta \to \theta_s} ||\mathbf{D}(\theta)|| = \infty$ and $\lim_{\theta \to \theta_r}||\mathbf{D}(\theta)|| = \infty$, where $\theta_s$ is the saturated (maximal) water content and $\theta_r$ is the residual (minimal) water content a successful numerical solution is restricted just for ranges of water content satisfactory below the full saturation (the saturation should be even lower than [air entry value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_retention_curve)) as well as satisfactory above the residual water content.[^o13]

:::

## Parametrization {#Parametrization}

The Richards equation in any of its forms involves soil hydraulic properties, which is a set of five parameters representing [soil type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_type). The soil hydraulic properties typically consist of [water retention curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_retention_curve) parameters by van Genuchten:[^o15] ($\alpha, \, n, \,m, \, \theta_s, \theta_r$), where $\alpha$ is the inverse of air entry value \[L<sup>−1</sup>\], $n$ is the pore size distribution parameter \[-\], and $m$ is usually assumed as $m= 1-\frac{1}{n}$. Further the saturated hydraulic conductivity $\mathbf{K}_s$ (which is for non [isotropic](wikipedia://en/14865) environment a [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) of second order) should also be provided. Identification of these parameters is often non-trivial and was a subject of numerous publications over several decades.[^o16][^o17][^o18][^o19][^o20][^o21]

## Limitations {#Limitations}

The numerical solution of the Richards equation is one of the most challenging problems in earth science.[^o23] Richards' equation has been criticized for being computationally expensive and unpredictable [^o24][^o25] because there is no guarantee that a solver will converge for a particular set of soil constitutive relations. Advanced computational and software solutions are required here to over-come this obstacle. The method has also been criticized for over-emphasizing the role of capillarity,[^o26] and for being in some ways 'overly simplistic' [^o27] In one dimensional simulations of rainfall infiltration into dry soils, fine spatial [discretization](wikipedia://en/330017) less than one cm is required near the land surface,[^o28] which is due to the small size of the [representative elementary volume](wikipedia://en/11913417) for [multiphase flow](wikipedia://en/5587769) in porous media. In three-dimensional applications the numerical solution of the Richards equation is subject to [aspect ratio](wikipedia://en/1021) constraints where the ratio of horizontal to vertical resolution in the solution domain should be less than about 7.
