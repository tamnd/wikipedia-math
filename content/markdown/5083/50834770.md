# Crossing number inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Crossing number inequality"}

In the mathematics of [graph drawing](wikipedia://en/325813), the **crossing number inequality** or **crossing lemma** gives a [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) on the [minimum number of edge crossings](wikipedia://en/18298594) in a plane drawing of a given [graph](wikipedia://en/325806), as a function of the number of [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) and [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) of the graph. It states that, for graphs where the number $e$ of edges is sufficiently larger than the number $n$ of vertices, the crossing number is at least [proportional](wikipedia://en/81863) to $e^{3}/n^{2}$.

:::

It has applications in [VLSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLSI) design and [combinatorial geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_geometry), and was discovered independently by [Ajtai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miklós_Ajtai), [Chvátal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Václav_Chvátal), [Newborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monty_Newborn), and [Szemerédi](wikipedia://en/663489)[^o2] and by [Leighton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Thomson_Leighton).[^o3]

## Statement and history {#Statement_and_history}

The crossing number inequality states that, for an undirected [simple graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) $G$ with $n$ vertices and $e$ edges such that $e > 7n$, the [crossing number](wikipedia://en/18298594) $\operatorname{cr}(G)$ obeys the [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489)

$$\operatorname{cr}(G) \geq \frac{e^3}{29 n^2}.$$

The constant $29$ is the best known to date, and is due to Ackerman.[^o5] For earlier results with weaker constants see (Pach Tóth 1997) and (Pach Radoičić Tardos Tóth 2006).[^o8][^o9] The constant $7$ can be lowered to $4$, but at the expense of replacing $29$ with the worse constant of $64$.

It is important to distinguish between the crossing number $\operatorname{cr}(G)$ and the pairwise crossing number $\operatorname{pair}-\operatorname{cr}(G)$. As noted by (Pach Tóth 2000), the pairwise crossing number $\text{pair-cr}(G) \leq \text{cr}(G)$ refers to the minimum number of pairs of edges that each determine one crossing, whereas the crossing number simply refers to the minimum number of crossings. (This distinction is necessary since some authors assume that in a proper drawing no two edges cross more than once.)[^o11]

::: {#o13 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The motivation of Leighton in studying crossing numbers was for applications to [VLSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLSI) design in theoretical computer science.[^o3]

Later, (Székely 1997) realized that this inequality yielded very simple proofs of some important theorems in [incidence geometry](wikipedia://en/387224). For instance, the [Szemerédi–Trotter theorem](wikipedia://en/1051627), an [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) on the number of incidences that are possible between given numbers of points and lines in the plane, follows by constructing a graph whose vertices are the points and whose edges are the segments of lines between incident points. If there were more incidences than the Szemerédi–Trotter bound, this graph would necessarily have more crossings than the total number of pairs of lines, an impossibility. The inequality can also be used to prove [Beck's theorem](wikipedia://en/2082759), that if a finite point set does not have a linear number of collinear points, then it determines a quadratic number of distinct lines.[^o15] Similarly, [Tamal Dey](wikipedia://en/59294216) used it to prove upper bounds on [geometric *k*-sets](wikipedia://en/8075001).[^o16]

:::

## Proof {#Proof}

We first give a preliminary estimate: for any graph $G$ with $n$ vertices and $e$ edges, we have

$$\operatorname{cr}(G) \geq e - 3n.$$

To prove this, consider a diagram of $G$ which has exactly $\operatorname{cr}(G)$ crossings. Each of these crossings can be removed by removing an edge from $G$. Thus we can find a graph with at least $e - \operatorname{cr}(G)$ edges and $n$ vertices with no crossings, and is thus a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314). But from [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/24314#Euler's_formula) we must then have $e - \operatorname{cr}(G) \le 3n$, and the claim follows. (In fact we have $e - \operatorname{cr}(G) \le 3n - 6$ for $n \ge 3$).

To obtain the actual crossing number inequality, we now use a [probabilistic argument](wikipedia://en/173525). We let $0 < p < 1$ be a [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) parameter to be chosen later, and construct a [random](wikipedia://en/358477) [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) $H$ of $G$ by allowing each vertex of $G$ to lie in $H$ independently with probability $p$ (and edges of $G$ then lie in $H$ if and only if their two endpoints were chosen to lie in $H$). Let $e_{H}, n_{H}$ and $\operatorname{cr}_{H}$ denote the number of edges, vertices and crossings of $H$, respectively. Since $H$ is a subgraph of $G$, the diagram of $G$ contains a diagram of $H$. By the preliminary crossing number inequality, we have

$$\operatorname{cr}_H \geq e_H - 3n_H.$$

Taking [expectations](wikipedia://en/9653) we obtain

$$\mathbf{E}[\operatorname{cr}_H] \geq \mathbf{E}[e_H] - 3 \mathbf{E}[n_H].$$

Since each of the $n$ vertices in $G$ had a probability $p$ of being in $H$, we have $\mathbf{E}[n_{H}] = pn$. Similarly, each of the edges in $G$ has a probability $p^{2}$ of remaining in $H$ since both endpoints need to stay in $H$, therefore $\mathbf{E}[e_{H}] = p^{2}e$. Finally, every crossing in the diagram of $G$ has a probability $p^{4}$ of remaining in $H$, since every crossing involves four vertices. To see this consider a diagram of $G$ with $\operatorname{cr}(G)$ crossings. We may assume that any two edges in this diagram with a common vertex are disjoint, otherwise we could interchange the intersecting parts of the two edges and reduce the crossing number by one. Thus every crossing in this diagram involves four distinct vertices of $G$. The diagram we have got may be not optimal in terms of number of crossings, but it obviously has at least $\operatorname{cr}_{H}$ crossings. Therefore,

$$p^4 \operatorname{cr}(G) \geq \mathbf{E} [ \operatorname{cr}_H ]$$

and we have

$$p^4 \operatorname{cr}(G) \geq p^2 e - 3 p n.$$

Now if we set $p = 4n/e < 1$ (since we assumed that $e > 4n$), we obtain after some algebra

$$\operatorname{cr}(G) \geq \frac{e^3}{64 n^2}.$$

A slight refinement of this argument allows one to replace $64$ by $33.75$ for $e > 7.5n$.[^o5]

:::

## Variations {#Variations}

For graphs with [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) larger than $2r$ and $e \ge 4n$, (Pach Spencer Tóth 2000) demonstrated an improvement of this inequality to[^o22]

$$\operatorname{cr}(G) \geq c_r\frac{e^{r+2}}{n^{r+1}}.$$

[Adiprasito](wikipedia://en/47874333) showed a generalization to higher dimensions:[^o23] If $\Delta$ is a simplicial complex that is mapped piecewise-linearly to $\mathbf{R}^{2d}$, and it has $f_d(\Delta)\ \ d$-dimensional faces and $f_{d-1}(\Delta)\ \ (d-1)$-dimensional faces such that $f_d(\Delta)> (d+3)f_{d-1}(\Delta)$, then the number of pairwise intersecting $d$-dimensional faces is at least

$$\frac{f_d^{d+2}(\Delta)}{(d+3)^{d+2}f_{d-1}^{d+1}(\Delta)}.$$

:::
