# Riemann–Lebesgue lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Riemann–Lebesgue lemma"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Riemann–Lebesgue lemma**, named after [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) and [Henri Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/49514), states that the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) or [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) of an [*L*<sup>1</sup> function](wikipedia://en/45194) [vanishes at infinity](wikipedia://en/461986). It is of importance in [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) and [asymptotic analysis](wikipedia://en/641995).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $f\in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$ be an integrable function, i.e. $f\colon\mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ is a [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) such that

$$\|f\|_{L^1} = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} |f(x)| \mathrm{d}x < \infty,$$

and let $\hat{f}$ be the Fourier transform of $f$, i.e.

$$\hat{f}\colon\mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{C}, \ \xi\mapsto \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}\mathrm{d}x.$$

Then $\hat{f}$ vanishes at infinity: $|\hat{f}(\xi)| \to 0$ as $|\xi| \to\infty$.

Because the Fourier transform of an integrable function is continuous, the Fourier transform $\hat{f}$ is a continuous function vanishing at infinity. If $C_0(\mathbb{R}^n)$ denotes the vector space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity, the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma may be formulated as follows: The Fourier transformation maps $L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$ to $C_0(\mathbb{R}^n)$.

::: {#o5 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

We will focus on the one-dimensional case $n=1$, the proof in higher dimensions is similar. First, suppose that $f$ is continuous and [compactly supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactly_supported). For $\xi \neq 0$, the substitution $\textstyle x\to x+\frac{\pi}{\xi}$ leads to

$$\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x) \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}x\xi}\mathrm{d}x = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f\left(x+\frac{\pi}{\xi}\right) \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}x\xi} \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi} \mathrm{d}x = -\int_{\mathbb{R}} f\left(x+\frac{\pi}{\xi}\right) \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}x\xi} \mathrm{d}x.$$

This gives a second formula for $\hat{f}(\xi)$. Taking the mean of both formulas, we arrive at the following estimate:

$$|\hat{f}(\xi)|\le \frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}} \left|f(x)-f\left(x+\frac{\pi}{\xi}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}x.$$

When $f$ is continuous, $\left|f(x)-f\left(x+\tfrac{\pi}{\xi}\right)\right|$ converges to $0$ as $|\xi| \to \infty$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Thus, $|\hat{f}(\xi)|$ converges to 0 as $|\xi| \to \infty$ due to the [dominated convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/351853).

Now, if $f$ is an arbitrary integrable function, it may be approximated in the $L^1$ norm by a compactly supported continuous function. For $\varepsilon > 0$, pick a compactly supported continuous function $g$ such that $\|f-g\|_{L^1} \leq \varepsilon$. Then

$$\limsup_{\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty} |\hat{f}(\xi)| \leq \limsup_{\xi\to\pm\infty} \left|\int (f(x)-g(x))\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}x\xi} \, \mathrm{d}x\right| + \limsup_{\xi\rightarrow\pm\infty} \left|\int g(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}x\xi} \, \mathrm{d}x\right| \leq \varepsilon + 0 = \varepsilon.$$

Since $g$ is continuous, we have $\hat{g}\to 0$, because of RL lemma shown above for continuous functions. Because this holds for any $\varepsilon > 0$, it follows that $|\hat{f}(\xi)| \to 0$ as $|\xi| \to\infty$.

:::

## Other versions {#Other_versions}

The Riemann–Lebesgue lemma holds in a variety of other situations.

- If $f \in L^1[0,\infty)$, then the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma also holds for the Laplace transform of $f$, that is,

$$\int_0^\infty f(t) \mathrm{e}^{-tz} \mathrm{d}t \to 0$$

as $|z| \to \infty$ within the half-plane $\mathrm{Re}(z) \geq 0$.

- A version holds for [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) as well: if $f$ is an integrable function on a bounded interval, then the [Fourier coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_coefficient) $\hat{f}_k$ of $f$ tend to 0 as $k \to \pm \infty$. This follows by extending $f$ by zero outside the interval, and then applying the version of the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma on the entire real line.
- However, the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma does not hold for arbitrary distributions. For example, the Dirac delta function distribution formally has a finite integral over the real line, but its Fourier transform is a constant and does not vanish at infinity.

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The Riemann–Lebesgue lemma can be used to prove the validity of asymptotic approximations for integrals. Rigorous treatments of the [method of steepest descent](wikipedia://en/27082137) and the [method of stationary phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_stationary_phase), amongst others, are based on the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma.
