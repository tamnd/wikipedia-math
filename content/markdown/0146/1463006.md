# Examples of vector spaces

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Examples of vector spaces"}

This page lists some **examples of vector spaces**. See [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) for the definitions of terms used on this page. See also: [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267), [basis](wikipedia://en/18420).

:::

*Notation*. Let *F* denote an arbitrary [field](wikipedia://en/10603) such as the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) **R** or the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) **C**.

## Trivial or zero vector space {#Trivial_or_zero_vector_space}

The simplest example of a vector space is the trivial one: {0}, which contains only the zero vector (see the third axiom in the [Vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) article). Both vector addition and scalar multiplication are trivial. A [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for this vector space is the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566), so that {0} is the 0-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) vector space over *F*. Every vector space over *F* contains a [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to this one.

The zero vector space is conceptually different from the [null space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_space) of a linear operator *L*, which is the [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915) of *L*. (Incidentally, the null space of *L* is a zero space if and only if *L* is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective).)

## Field {#Field}

The next simplest example is the field *F* itself. Vector addition is just field addition, and scalar multiplication is just field multiplication. This property can be used to prove that a field is a vector space. Any non-zero element of *F* serves as a basis so *F* is a 1-dimensional vector space over itself.

The field is a rather special vector space; in fact it is the simplest example of a **[commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/191788)** over *F*. Also, *F* has just two [subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357): {0} and *F* itself.

## Coordinate space {#Coordinate_space}

*Image: Line equation qtl4.svg.* Planar [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) uses the coordinate space **R**<sup>2</sup>. *Depicted:* description of a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) as the [solution set](wikipedia://en/537048#Solution_sets) in $\vec x$ of the vector equation $\vec x \cdot \vec n = d$.

A basic example of a vector space is the following. For any [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n*, the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all *n*-tuples of elements of *F* forms an *n*-dimensional vector space over *F* sometimes called *[coordinate space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_space)* and denoted *F*<sup>*n*</sup>. An element of *F*<sup>*n*</sup> is written

$$x = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$$

where each *x*<sub>*i*</sub> is an element of *F*. The operations on *F*<sup>*n*</sup> are defined by

$$x + y = (x_1 + y_1, x_2 + y_2, \ldots, x_n + y_n)$$

$$\alpha x = (\alpha x_1, \alpha x_2, \ldots, \alpha x_n)$$

$$0 = (0, 0, \ldots, 0)$$

$$-x = (-x_1, -x_2, \ldots, -x_n)$$

Commonly, *F* is the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), in which case we obtain [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. The field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) gives [complex coordinate space](wikipedia://en/39125683) **C**<sup>*n*</sup>. The *a + bi* form of a complex number shows that **C** itself is a two-dimensional real vector space with coordinates (*a*,*b*). Similarly, the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) and the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) are respectively four- and eight-dimensional real vector spaces, and **C**<sup>*n*</sup> is a *2n*-dimensional real vector space.

The vector space *F*<sup>*n*</sup> has a [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005):

$$e_1 = (1, 0, \ldots, 0)$$

$$e_2 = (0, 1, \ldots, 0)$$

$$\vdots$$

$$e_n = (0, 0, \ldots, 1)$$

where 1 denotes the multiplicative identity in *F*.

## Infinite coordinate space {#Infinite_coordinate_space}

Let *F*<sup>∞</sup> denote the space of [infinite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence) of elements from *F* such that only *finitely* many elements are nonzero. That is, if we write an element of *F*<sup>∞</sup> as

$$x = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots)$$

then only a finite number of the *x*<sub>*i*</sub> are nonzero (i.e., the coordinates become all zero after a certain point). Addition and scalar multiplication are given as in finite coordinate space. The dimensionality of *F*<sup>∞</sup> is [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite). A standard basis consists of the vectors *e*<sub>*i*</sub> which contain a 1 in the *i*-th slot and zeros elsewhere. This vector space is the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) (or [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899)) of countably many copies of the vector space *F*.

Note the role of the finiteness condition here. One could consider arbitrary sequences of elements in *F*, which also constitute a vector space with the same operations, often denoted by *F*<sup>**N**</sup> - see [below](wikipedia://en/1463006#Function_spaces). *F*<sup>**N**</sup> is the *[product](wikipedia://en/237868)* of countably many copies of *F*.

By [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442), *F*<sup>**N**</sup> has a basis (there is no obvious basis). There are [uncountably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountably_infinite) elements in the basis. Since the dimensions are different, *F*<sup>**N**</sup> is *not* isomorphic to *F*<sup>∞</sup>. It is worth noting that *F*<sup>**N**</sup> is (isomorphic to) the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of *F*<sup>∞</sup>, because a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) *T* from *F*<sup>∞</sup> to *F* is determined uniquely by its values *T*(*e<sub>i</sub>*) on the basis elements of *F*<sup>∞</sup>, and these values can be arbitrary. Thus one sees that a vector space need not be isomorphic to its double dual if it is infinite dimensional, in contrast to the finite dimensional case.

## Product of vector spaces {#Product_of_vector_spaces}

Starting from *n* vector spaces, or a countably infinite collection of them, each with the same field, we can define the product space like above.

## Matrices {#Matrices}

Let *F*<sup>*m*×*n*</sup> denote the set of *m*×*n* [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with entries in *F*. Then *F*<sup>*m*×*n*</sup> is a vector space over *F*. Vector addition is just matrix addition and scalar multiplication is defined in the obvious way (by multiplying each entry by the same scalar). The zero vector is just the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888). The [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of *F*<sup>*m*×*n*</sup> is *mn*. One possible choice of basis is the matrices with a single entry equal to 1 and all other entries 0.

When *m* = *n* the matrix is [square](wikipedia://en/166022) and [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) of two such matrices produces a third. This vector space of dimension *n*<sup>2</sup> forms an [algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788).

## Polynomial vector spaces {#Polynomial_vector_spaces}

### One variable {#One_variable}

The set of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) with coefficients in *F* is a vector space over *F*, denoted *F*\[*x*\]. Vector addition and scalar multiplication are defined in the obvious manner. If the [degree of the polynomials](wikipedia://en/5930652) is unrestricted then the dimension of *F*\[*x*\] is [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite). If instead one restricts to polynomials with degree less than or equal to *n*, then we have a vector space with dimension *n* + 1.

One possible basis for *F*\[*x*\] is a [monomial basis](wikipedia://en/976666): the coordinates of a polynomial with respect to this basis are its [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), and the map sending a polynomial to the sequence of its coefficients is a [linear isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isomorphism) from *F*\[*x*\] to the infinite coordinate space *F*<sup>∞</sup>.

The vector space of polynomials with real coefficients and degree less than or equal to *n* is often denoted by *P*<sub>*n*</sub>.

### Several variables {#Several_variables}

The set of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in several variables with coefficients in *F* is vector space over *F* denoted *F*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*r*</sub>\]. Here *r* is the number of variables.

:::

## Function spaces {#Function_spaces}

*See main article at [Function space](wikipedia://en/340630), especially the functional analysis section.*

Let *X* be a non-empty arbitrary set and *V* an arbitrary vector space over *F*. The space of all [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) from *X* to *V* is a vector space over *F* under [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) addition and multiplication. That is, let *f* : *X* → *V* and *g* : *X* → *V* denote two functions, and let *α* in *F*. We define

$$(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$$

$$(\alpha f)(x) = \alpha f(x)$$

where the operations on the right hand side are those in *V*. The zero vector is given by the constant function sending everything to the zero vector in *V*. The space of all functions from *X* to *V* is commonly denoted *V*<sup>*X*</sup>.

If *X* is finite and *V* is finite-dimensional then *V*<sup>*X*</sup> has dimension |*X*|(dim *V*), otherwise the space is infinite-dimensional (uncountably so if *X* is infinite).

Many of the vector spaces that arise in mathematics are subspaces of some function space. We give some further examples.

### Generalized coordinate space {#Generalized_coordinate_space}

Let *X* be an arbitrary set. Consider the space of all functions from *X* to *F* which vanish on all but a finite number of points in *X*. This space is a vector subspace of *F*<sup>*X*</sup>, the space of all possible functions from *X* to *F*. To see this, note that the union of two finite sets is finite, so that the sum of two functions in this space will still vanish outside a finite set.

The space described above is commonly denoted (*F*<sup>*X*</sup>)<sub>0</sub> and is called *generalized coordinate space* for the following reason. If *X* is the set of numbers between 1 and *n* then this space is easily seen to be equivalent to the coordinate space *F*<sup>*n*</sup>. Likewise, if *X* is the set of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), **N**, then this space is just *F*<sup>∞</sup>.

A canonical basis for (*F*<sup>*X*</sup>)<sub>0</sub> is the set of functions {δ<sub>*x*</sub> | *x* ∈ *X*} defined by

$$\delta_x(y) = \begin{cases}1 \quad x = y \\ 0 \quad x \neq y\end{cases}$$

The dimension of (*F*<sup>*X*</sup>)<sub>0</sub> is therefore equal to the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of *X*. In this manner we can construct a vector space of any dimension over any field. Furthermore, *every vector space is isomorphic to one of this form*. Any choice of basis determines an isomorphism by sending the basis onto the canonical one for (*F*<sup>*X*</sup>)<sub>0</sub>.

Generalized coordinate space may also be understood as the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of |*X*| copies of *F* (i.e. one for each point in *X*):

$$(\mathbf F^X)_0 = \bigoplus_{x\in X}\mathbf F.$$

The finiteness condition is built into the definition of the direct sum. Contrast this with the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of |*X*| copies of *F* which would give the full function space *F*<sup>*X*</sup>.

### Linear maps {#Linear_maps}

An important example arising in the context of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) itself is the vector space of [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102). Let *L*(*V*,*W*) denote the set of all linear maps from *V* to *W* (both of which are vector spaces over *F*). Then *L*(*V*,*W*) is a subspace of *W*<sup>*V*</sup> since it is closed under addition and scalar multiplication.

Note that L(*F*<sup>*n*</sup>,*F*<sup>*m*</sup>) can be identified with the space of matrices *F*<sup>*m*×*n*</sup> in a natural way. In fact, by choosing appropriate bases for finite-dimensional spaces V and W, L(V,W) can also be identified with *F*<sup>*m*×*n*</sup>. This identification normally depends on the choice of basis.

### Continuous functions {#Continuous_functions}

If *X* is some [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), such as the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) \[0,1\], we can consider the space of all [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) from *X* to **R**. This is a vector subspace of **R**<sup>*X*</sup> since the sum of any two continuous functions is continuous and scalar multiplication is continuous.

### Differential equations {#Differential_equations}

The subset of the space of all functions from **R** to **R** consisting of (sufficiently differentiable) functions that satisfy a certain [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) is a subspace of **R**<sup>**R**</sup> if the equation is linear. This is because [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) is a linear operation, i.e., (*a* *f* + *b* *g*)′ = *a* *f*hair′ + *b* *g*′, where ′ is the differentiation operator.

## Field extensions {#Field_extensions}

Suppose *K* is a [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634) of *F* (cf. [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634)). Then *F* can be regarded as a vector space over *K* by restricting scalar multiplication to elements in *K* (vector addition is defined as normal). The dimension of this vector space, if it exists, is called the *degree* of the extension. For example, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) **C** form a two-dimensional vector space over the real numbers **R**. Likewise, the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) **R** form a vector space over the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q** which has (uncountably) infinite dimension, if a Hamel basis exists.

If *V* is a vector space over *F* it may also be regarded as vector space over *K*. The dimensions are related by the formula

dim<sub>*K*</sub>*V* = (dim<sub>*F*</sub>*V*)(dim<sub>*K*</sub>*F*)

For example, **C**<sup>*n*</sup>, regarded as a vector space over the reals, has dimension 2*n*.

## Finite vector spaces {#Finite_vector_spaces}

Apart from the trivial case of a zero-dimensional space over any field, a vector space over a field *F* has a finite number of elements if and only if *F* is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) and the vector space has a finite dimension. Thus we have *F*<sub>*q*</sub>, the unique finite field (up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)) with *q* elements. Here *q* must be a power of a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) (*q* = *p*<sup>*m*</sup> with *p* prime). Then any *n*-dimensional vector space *V* over *F*<sub>*q*</sub> will have *q*<sup>*n*</sup> elements. Note that the number of elements in *V* is also the power of a prime (because a power of a prime power is again a prime power). The primary example of such a space is the coordinate space (*F*<sub>*q*</sub>)<sup>*n*</sup>.

These vector spaces are of critical importance in the [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707), [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), and [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432).
