# Lindelöf hypothesis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lindelöf hypothesis"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Lindelöf hypothesis** is a [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) by Finnish mathematician [Ernst Leonard Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/960668)[^o2] about the rate of growth of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) on the critical line. This hypothesis is implied by the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125). It says that for any *ε* > 0,

$$\zeta\!\left(\frac{1}{2} + it\right)\! = O(t^\varepsilon)$$

as *t* tends to infinity (see [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578)). Since *ε* can be replaced by a smaller value, the conjecture can be restated as follows: for any positive *ε*,

$$\zeta\!\left(\frac{1}{2} + it\right)\! = o(t^\varepsilon).$$

:::

## The μ function {#The_μ_function}

If σ is [real](wikipedia://en/20646438), then *μ*(σ) is defined to be the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all real numbers *a* such that ζ(σ + *iT* ) = O(*T*<sup>*a*</sup>). It is trivial to check that *μ*(σ) = 0 for σ > 1, and the [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377) of the zeta function implies that *μ*(σ) = *μ*(1 − σ) − σ + 1/2. The [Phragmén–Lindelöf theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phragmén–Lindelöf_theorem) implies that *μ* is a [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568). The Lindelöf hypothesis states *μ*(1/2) = 0, which together with the above properties of *μ* implies that *μ*(σ) is 0 for σ ≥ 1/2 and 1/2 − σ for σ ≤ 1/2.

Lindelöf's convexity result together with *μ*(1) = 0 and *μ*(0) = 1/2 implies that 0 ≤ *μ*(1/2) ≤ 1/4. The upper bound of 1/4 was lowered by [Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) and [Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._E._Littlewood) to 1/6 by applying [Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544)'s method of estimating [exponential sums](wikipedia://en/468937) to the [approximate functional equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximate_functional_equation). It has since been lowered to slightly less than 1/6 by several authors using long and technical [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285), as in the following table.

## Relation to the Riemann hypothesis {#Relation_to_the_Riemann_hypothesis}

Backlund[^o24] (1918–1919) showed that the Lindelöf hypothesis is equivalent to the following statement about the [zeros](wikipedia://en/264210) of the zeta function: for every *ε* > 0, the number of zeros with [real part](wikipedia://en/5826) at least 1/2 + *ε* and [imaginary part](wikipedia://en/5826) between *T* and *T* + 1 is o(log(*T*)) as *T* tends to infinity. The Riemann hypothesis implies that there are no zeros at all in this region and so implies the Lindelöf hypothesis. The number of zeros with imaginary part between *T* and *T* + 1 is known to be O(log(*T*)), so the Lindelöf hypothesis seems only slightly stronger than what has already been proved, but in spite of this it has resisted all attempts to prove it.

:::

## Means of powers (or moments) of the zeta function {#Means_of_powers_(or_moments)_of_the_zeta_function}

The Lindelöf hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that

$$\frac{1}{T} \int_0^T|\zeta(1/2+it)|^{2k}\,dt = O(T^{\varepsilon})$$

for all positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *k* and all positive real numbers ε. This has been proved for *k* = 1 or 2, but the case *k* = 3 seems much harder and is still an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335).

There is a much more precise conjecture about the asymptotic behavior of the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532): it is believed that

$$\int_0^T|\zeta(1/2+it)|^{2k} \, dt = T\sum_{j=0}^{k^2}c_{k,j}\log(T)^{k^2-j} + o(T)$$

for some constants *c*<sub>*k*,*j*</sub>. This has been proved by Littlewood for *k* = 1 and by Heath-Brown[^o26] for *k* = 2 (extending a result of Ingham[^o27] who found the leading term).

Conrey and Ghosh[^o28] suggested the value

$$\frac{42}{9!}\prod_ p \left((1-p^{-1})^4(1+4p^{-1}+p^{-2})\right)$$

for the leading coefficient when *k* is 3, and Keating and Snaith[^o29] used [random matrix theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_matrix_theory) to suggest some conjectures for the values of the coefficients for higher *k*. The leading coefficients are conjectured to be the product of an elementary factor, a certain product over [primes](wikipedia://en/23666), and the number of *n* × *n* [Young tableaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_tableaux) given by the [sequence](wikipedia://en/51421)

1, 1, 2, 42, 24024, 701149020, ... .

## Other consequences {#Other_consequences}

Denoting by *p*<sub>*n*</sub> the *n*-th prime number, let $g_n = p_{n + 1} - p_n.\$ A result by [Albert Ingham](wikipedia://en/5380138) shows that the Lindelöf hypothesis implies that, for any *ε* > 0,

$$g_n\ll p_n^{1/2+\varepsilon}$$

if *n* is [sufficiently large](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficiently_large).

A [prime gap](wikipedia://en/5075259) conjecture stronger than Ingham's result is [Cramér's conjecture](wikipedia://en/290441), which asserts that[^o31][^o32]

$$g_n = O\!\left((\log p_n)^2\right).$$

### The density hypothesis {#The_density_hypothesis}

*Image: DensityHypothesis.png.* The known zero-free region roughly speaking corresponds to the bottom right corner of the image, and the Riemann hypothesis would push the entire diagram down to the x-axis $A_{RH}(\sigma>1/2)=0$. At the other extreme, the upper boundary $A_{DH}(1-\sigma)=2(1-1/2)=1$ of this diagram corresponds to the trivial bound coming from the [Riemann-von Mangoldt formula](wikipedia://en/8077953).(Various other estimates do exist[^o35])

The density hypothesis says that $N(\sigma,T)\le N^{2(1-\sigma)+\epsilon}$, where $N(\sigma,T)$ denote the number of zeros $\rho$ of $\zeta(s)$with $\mathfrak{R}(s)\ge \sigma$ and $|\mathfrak{I}(s)|\le T$, and it would follow from the Lindelöf hypothesis.[^o36][^o37]

More generally let $N(\sigma,T)\le N^{A(\sigma)(1-\sigma)+\epsilon}$ then it is known that this bound roughly correspond to asymptotics for primes in short intervals of length $x^{1-1/A(\sigma)}$.[^o38][^o39]

[Ingham](wikipedia://en/5380138) showed that $A_I(\sigma)=\frac{3}{2-\sigma}$ in 1940,[^o40] [Huxley](wikipedia://en/5385232) that $A_H(\sigma)=\frac{3}{3\sigma-1}$ in 1971,[^o41] and [Guth](wikipedia://en/31875947) and [Maynard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Maynard) that $A_{GM}(\sigma)=\frac{15}{5\sigma+3}$ in 2024 (preprint)[^o42][^o43][^o44] and these coincide on

$$\sigma_{I,GM}=7/10<\sigma_{H,GM}=8/10<\sigma_{I,H}=3/4.$$

Therefore the latest work of Guth and Maynard gives the closest known value to $\sigma=1/2$ (as expected from the Riemann hypothesis) and improves the bound to $N(\sigma,T)\le N^{\frac{30}{13}(1-\sigma)+\epsilon}$ or equivalently the asymptotics to $x^{17/30}$.

In theory, improvements to Baker, [Harman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyn_Harman), and [Pintz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janos_Pintz) [estimates](wikipedia://en/2620450#Partial_results) for the Legendre conjecture and better [Siegel-zero](wikipedia://en/1967197)-free regions could also be expected, among others.

:::

## L-functions {#L-functions}

The Riemann zeta function belongs to a more general family of functions called [L-functions](wikipedia://en/245283). In 2010, new methods to obtain sub-convexity estimates for L-functions in the PGL(2) case were given by [Joseph Bernstein](wikipedia://en/5815383) and Andre Reznikov[^o46] and in the GL(1) and GL(2) case by [Akshay Venkatesh](wikipedia://en/6647964) and [Philippe Michel](wikipedia://en/45187389)[^o47] and in 2021 for the GL(*n*) case by Paul Nelson.[^o48][^o49]
