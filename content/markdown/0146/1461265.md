# Split-quaternion

*Split-quaternion multiplication*

::: {#o1 .table cue=table name="Split-quaternion multiplication"}

| × | 1 | i | j | k |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | i | j | k |
| i | i | −1 | k | −j |
| j | j | −k | 1 | −i |
| k | k | j | i | 1 |

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Split-quaternion"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **split-quaternions** or **coquaternions** form an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) introduced by [James Cockle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cockle_%28lawyer%29) in 1849 under the latter name. They form an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) of dimension four over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).

:::

After introduction in the 20th century of coordinate-free definitions of [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) and [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788), it was proved that the algebra of split-quaternions is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of the $2\times2$ [real matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_matrix). So the study of split-quaternions can be reduced to the study of real matrices, and this may explain why there are few mentions of split-quaternions in the mathematical literature of the 20th and 21st centuries. The split-quaternions are also equivalent to the [Clifford algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_algebras) $\operatorname{Cl}_{2,0}(\mathbf{R}) \cong \operatorname{Cl}_{1,1}(\mathbf{R})$ over the real numbers, and so the study of split-quaternions is also subsumed in the study of Clifford algebra and [geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/12939) in the mathematics and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) literature.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The *split-quaternions* are the [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) (with real coefficients) of four basis elements $1, i, j, k$ that satisfy the following product rules:

$$i^{2} = -1,$$

$$j^{2} = 1,$$

$$k^{2} = 1,$$

$$\operatorname{ij} = k = -\operatorname{ji}.$$

By [associativity](wikipedia://en/1335), these relations imply

$$\operatorname{jk} = -i = -\operatorname{kj},$$

$$\operatorname{ki} = j = -\operatorname{ik},$$

and also $\operatorname{ijk} = 1$.

So, the split-quaternions form a [real vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) of dimension four with $\{1, i, j, k\}$ as a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420). They form also a [noncommutative ring](wikipedia://en/24062516), by extending the above product rules by [distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity) to all split-quaternions.

The square matrices

$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{1} =\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad&\boldsymbol{i} =\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\\ \boldsymbol{j} =\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad&\boldsymbol{k} =\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

satisfy the same multiplication table as the corresponding split-quaternions. As these matrices form a basis of the two-by-two matrices, the unique linear [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that maps $1, i, j, k$ to $\boldsymbol{1}, \boldsymbol{i}, \boldsymbol{j}, \boldsymbol{k}$ (respectively) induces an [algebra isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_isomorphism) from the split-quaternions to the two-by-two real matrices.

The above multiplication rules imply that the eight elements $1, i, j, k, -1, -i, -j, -k$ form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under this multiplication, which is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the symmetry group of a square D<sub>4</sub>, the [dihedral group of order 8](wikipedia://en/78749515).

To see this, consider a square whose vertices are the points whose $x\text{,} \, y$ coordinates are $-1$ or $+1$ so that the vertices $\begin{pmatrix} x\\ y \end{pmatrix}$ of the square listed CW from azimuth +45° are

$$\bigg \{ \mathbf x_1=\begin{pmatrix} +1\\ +1 \end{pmatrix} \text{,} \; \mathbf x_2=\begin{pmatrix} +1\\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \text{,} \; \mathbf x_3=\begin{pmatrix} -1\\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \text{,} \; \mathbf x_4=\begin{pmatrix} -1\\ +1 \end{pmatrix}\bigg \}$$

The matrix $\boldsymbol{i}$ has determinant $+1$ and gives a clockwise rotation of a quarter of a turn, since $\mathbf x_2=\mathbf i \,\mathbf x_1\text{,}\;\mathbf x_3=\mathbf i \,\mathbf x_2\text{,}\;\mathbf x_4=\mathbf i \,\mathbf x_3\text{,}\; \mathbf x_1=\mathbf i \,\mathbf x_4$ The matrix $\boldsymbol{j}$ has determinant -1 and is the reflection about the first diagonal. It fixes the vertices $\mathbf x_1$ and $\mathbf x_3$ and reflects the vertices $\mathbf x_2$ and $\mathbf x_4$ into each other. The matrix $\boldsymbol{k}$ is a reflection about the $x$ axis. It does $\mathbf x_1 \leftrightarrow \mathbf x_2$ and $\mathbf x_3 \leftrightarrow \mathbf x_4$. The negative of the identity matrix is the square of the matrix $\mathbf i$, has determinant $+1$, and rotates the square by 180° about the origin. It does $\mathbf x_1 \leftrightarrow \mathbf x_3$ and $\mathbf x_2 \leftrightarrow \mathbf x_4$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Like the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) introduced by [Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1843, they form a four [dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) real [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112). But like the real algebra of 2×2 matrices – and unlike the real algebra of quaternions – the split-quaternions contain nontrivial [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441), [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) elements, and [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent_element_%28ring_theory%29). (For example, $\tfrac{1}{2}$(1 + j) is an idempotent zero-divisor, and i − j is nilpotent.) As an [algebra over the real numbers](wikipedia://en/191788), the algebra of split-quaternions is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_isomorphism) to the algebra of 2×2 real matrices by the above defined isomorphism.

This isomorphism allows identifying each split-quaternion with a 2×2 matrix. So every property of split-quaternions corresponds to a similar property of matrices, which is often named differently.

The *conjugate* of a split-quaternion $q = w + xi + yj + zk$, is $q^{\ast} = w - xi - yj - zk$. In term of matrices, the conjugate is the [cofactor matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_matrix) obtained by exchanging the diagonal entries and changing the sign of the other two entries.

The product of a split-quaternion with its conjugate is the [isotropic quadratic form](wikipedia://en/10917170):

$$N(q) = q q^* = w^2 + x^2 - y^2 - z^2,$$

which is called the [*norm*](wikipedia://en/990534#Composition_algebras) of the split-quaternion or the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the associated matrix.

The real part of a split-quaternion $q = w + xi + yj + zk$ is $w = (q^{\ast} + q)/2$. It equals the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of associated matrix.

The norm of a product of two split-quaternions is the product of their norms. Equivalently, the determinant of a product of matrices is the product of their determinants. This property means that split-quaternions form a [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635). As there are nonzero split-quaternions having a zero norm, split-quaternions form a [split algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_algebra).

A split-quaternion with a nonzero norm has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940), namely $q^{\ast}/N(q)$. In terms of matrices, this is equivalent to [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008) that asserts that a matrix is [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) if and only its determinant is nonzero, and, in this case, the inverse of the matrix is the quotient of the [adjugate matrix](wikipedia://en/202840) by the determinant. The adjugate matrix is the [transpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpose_matrix) of the [cofactor matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_matrix) and is also known as the adjoint or adjunct matrix.

The isomorphism between split-quaternions and 2×2 real matrices shows that the multiplicative group of split-quaternions with a nonzero norm is isomorphic with $\operatorname{GL}(2, \mathbb R),$ and the group of split quaternions of norm $1$ is isomorphic with $\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbb R).$

Geometrically, the split-quaternions can be compared to Hamilton's quaternions as [pencils of planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pencil_of_planes). In both cases the real numbers form the axis of a pencil. In Hamilton quaternions there is a sphere of imaginary units, and any pair of antipodal imaginary units generates a complex plane with the real line. For split-quaternions there are hyperboloids of hyperbolic and imaginary units that generate split-complex or ordinary complex planes, as described below in § Stratification.

:::

## Representation as complex matrices {#Representation_as_complex_matrices}

There is a representation of the split-quaternions as a [unital associative subalgebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_associative_algebra) of the $2\times2$ matrices with [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) entries. This representation can be defined by the [algebra homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) that maps a split-quaternion $w + xi + yj + zk$ to the matrix

$$\begin{pmatrix}w+xi& y+zi\\y-zi&w-xi\end{pmatrix}.$$

Here, $i$ ([italic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_type)) is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358), not to be confused with the split quaternion basis element $i$ ([upright roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_type)).

The image of this homomorphism is the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) formed by the matrices of the form

$$\begin{pmatrix}u & v \\ v^* & u^* \end{pmatrix},$$

where the superscript $^*$ denotes a [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918).

This homomorphism maps respectively the split-quaternions $i, j, k$ on the matrices

$$\begin{pmatrix}i & 0 \\0 &-i \end{pmatrix}, \quad\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\1 &0 \end{pmatrix},\quad \begin{pmatrix}0 & i \\-i &0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The isomorphism of algebras is completed by use of [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) to verify the identities involving i, j, and k. For instance,

$$j k = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 & i \\ -i & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-i & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix} = - i .$$

It follows that for a split quaternion represented as a complex matrix, the conjugate is the matrix of the cofactors, and the norm is the determinant.

With the representation of split quaternions as complex matrices, the matrices of determinant $1$ form the special unitary group [SU(1,1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%281,1%29), which is used to describe [hyperbolic motions](wikipedia://en/1229416#Disk_model_motions) of the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) in [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).[^o8]

## Generation from split-complex numbers {#Generation_from_split-complex_numbers}

Split-quaternions may be generated by [modified Cayley–Dickson construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley%E2%80%93Dickson_construction#Modified_Cayley%E2%80%93Dickson_construction)[^o10] similar to the method of [L. E. Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._E._Dickson) and [Adrian Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrian_Albert). for the division algebras **C**, **H**, and **O**. The multiplication rule

$$(a,b)(c,d)\ = \ (ac + d^* b, \ da + bc^* )$$

is used when producing the doubled product in the real-split cases. The doubled conjugate $(a,b)^* = (a^*, - b),$ so that

$$N(a,b) \ = \ (a,b)(a,b)^* \ = \ (a a^* - b b^* , 0).$$

If *a* and *b* are [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) and split-quaternion $q = (a,b) = ((w + z j), (y + xj)),$

then

$$N(q) = a a^* - b b^* = w^2 - z^2 - (y^2 - x^2) = w^2 + x^2 - y^2 - z^2 .$$

## Stratification {#Stratification}

In this section, the real [subalgebras](wikipedia://en/45239) generated by a single split-quaternion are studied and classified.

Let $p = w + xi + yj + zk$ be a split-quaternion. Its *real part* is $w = \tfrac{1}{2}(p + p^{*})$. Let $q = p - w = \tfrac{1}{2}(p - p^{*})$ be its *nonreal part*. One has $q^{*} = -q$, and therefore $p^2=w^2+2wq-N(q).$ It follows that $p^{2}$ is a real number if and only $p$ is either a real number ($q = 0$ and $p = w$) or a *purely nonreal split quaternion* ($w = 0$ and $p = q$).

The structure of the subalgebra $\mathbb R[p]$ generated by $p$ follows straightforwardly. One has

$$\mathbb R[p]=\mathbb R[q]=\{a+bq\mid a,b\in\mathbb R\},$$

and this is a [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29). Its [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) is two except if $p$ is real (in this case, the subalgebra is simply $\mathbb R$).

The nonreal elements of $\mathbb R[p]$ whose square is real have the form $aq$ with $a\in \mathbb R.$

Three cases have to be considered, which are detailed in the next subsections.

### Nilpotent case {#Nilpotent_case}

With above notation, if $q^2=0,$ (that is, if $q$ is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235)), then $N(q) = 0$, that is, $x^2-y^2-z^2=0.$ This implies that there exist $w$ and $t$ in $\mathbb R$ such that $0 \le t < 2\pi$ and

$$p=w+a\mathrm i + a\cos(t)\mathrm j + a\sin(t)\mathrm k.$$

This is a parametrization of all split-quaternions whose nonreal part is nilpotent.

This is also a parameterization of these subalgebras by the points of a circle: the split-quaternions of the form $\mathrm i + \cos(t)\mathrm j + \sin(t)\mathrm k$ form a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220); a subalgebra generated by a nilpotent element contains exactly one point of the circle; and the circle does not contain any other point.

The algebra generated by a nilpotent element is isomorphic to $\mathbb R[X]/\langle X^2\rangle$ and to the plane of [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169).

### Imaginary units {#Imaginary_units}

*Image: HyperboloidOfTwoSheets.svg.* Hyperboloid of two sheets, source of [imaginary units](wikipedia://en/52358)

This is the case where $N(q) > 0$. Letting $n=\sqrt{N(q)},$ one has

$$q^2 =-q^*q=N(q)=n^2=x^2-y^2-z^2.$$

It follows that $\tfrac{1}{n} q$ belongs to the [hyperboloid of two sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_of_two_sheets) of equation $x^2-y^2-z^2=1.$ Therefore, there are real numbers $n, t, u$ such that $0 \le t < 2\pi$ and

$$p=w+n\cosh(u)\mathrm i + n\sinh(u)\cos(t)\mathrm j + n\sinh(u)\sin(t)\mathrm k.$$

This is a parametrization of all split-quaternions whose nonreal part has a positive norm.

This is also a parameterization of the corresponding subalgebras by the pairs of opposite points of a hyperboloid of two sheets: the split-quaternions of the form $\cosh(u)\mathrm i + \sinh(u)\cos(t)\mathrm j + \sinh(u)\sin(t)\mathrm k$ form a hyperboloid of two sheets; a subalgebra generated by a split-quaternion with a nonreal part of positive norm contains exactly two opposite points on this hyperboloid, one on each sheet; and the hyperboloid does not contain any other point.

The algebra generated by a split-quaternion with a nonreal part of positive norm is isomorphic to $\mathbb R[X]/\langle X^2+1\rangle$ and to the field $\mathbb{C}$ of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).

### Hyperbolic units {#Hyperbolic_units}

*Image: HyperboloidOfOneSheet.PNG.* Hyperboloid of one sheet, source of [hyperbolic units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_unit).(the vertical axis is called $x$ in the article)

This is the case where $N(q) < 0$. Letting $n=\sqrt{-N(q)},$ one has

$$q^2 = -q^*q=N(q)=-n^2=x^2-y^2-z^2.$$

It follows that $\tfrac{1}{n} q$ belongs to the [hyperboloid of one sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_of_one_sheet) of equation $y^{2} + z^{2} - x^{2} = 1$. Therefore, there are real numbers $n, t, u$ such that $0 \le t < 2\pi$ and

$$p=w+n\sinh(u)\mathrm i + n\cosh(u)\cos(t)\mathrm j + n\cosh(u)\sin(t)\mathrm k.$$

This is a parametrization of all split-quaternions whose nonreal part has a negative norm.

This is also a parameterization of the corresponding subalgebras by the pairs of opposite points of a hyperboloid of one sheet: the split-quaternions of the form $\sinh(u)\mathrm i + \cosh(u)\cos(t)\mathrm j + \cosh(u)\sin(t)\mathrm k$ form a hyperboloid of one sheet; a subalgebra generated by a split-quaternion with a nonreal part of negative norm contains exactly two opposite points on this hyperboloid; and the hyperboloid does not contain any other point.

The algebra generated by a split-quaternion with a nonreal part of negative norm is isomorphic to $\mathbb R[X]/\langle X^2-1\rangle$ and to the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559). It is also isomorphic (as an algebra) to $\mathbb R^2$ by the mapping defined by $(1,0)\mapsto \frac{1+X}2, \quad (0,1)\mapsto \frac{1-X}2.$

### Stratification by the norm {#Stratification_by_the_norm}

As seen above, the purely nonreal split-quaternions of norm $-1, 1$ and $0$ form respectively a hyperboloid of one sheet, a hyperboloid of two sheets and a [circular cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_cone) in the space of non real quaternions.

These surfaces are pairwise [asymptote](wikipedia://en/3107) and do not intersect. Their [complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_complement) consist of six connected regions:

- the two regions located on the concave side of the hyperboloid of two sheets, where $N(q)>1$
- the two regions between the hyperboloid of two sheets and the cone, where $0<N(q)<1$
- the region between the cone and the hyperboloid of one sheet where $-1<N(q)<0$
- the region outside the hyperboloid of one sheet, where $N(q)<-1$

This stratification can be refined by considering split-quaternions of a fixed norm: for every real number $n \ne 0$ the purely nonreal split-quaternions of norm $n$ form an hyperboloid. All these hyperboloids are asymptote to the above cone, and none of these surfaces intersect any other. As the set of the purely nonreal split-quaternions is the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of these surfaces, this provides the desired stratification.

## Colour space {#Colour_space}

Split quaternions have been applied to [colour balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_balance)[^o17] The model refers to the [Jordan algebra](wikipedia://en/936371) of [symmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/126474) representing the algebra. The model reconciles [trichromacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichromacy) with [Hering's opponency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opponent_process) and uses the [Cayley–Klein model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley–Klein_model) of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) for chromatic distances.

## Historical notes {#Historical_notes}

The coquaternions were initially introduced (under that name)[^o19] in 1849 by [James Cockle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cockle) in the London–Edinburgh–Dublin [Philosophical Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Magazine). The introductory papers by Cockle were recalled in the 1904 *Bibliography*[^o20] of the [Quaternion Association](wikipedia://en/16416512).

In 1878 [W. K. Clifford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._K._Clifford) nearly described representation of split-quaternions with matrices:[^o21] He used *K* to express the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) $\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The only flaw was a missing minus sign in the equation

$$J K = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = -I .$$

[Alexander Macfarlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Macfarlane) called the structure of split-quaternion vectors an *exspherical system* when he was speaking at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in Paris in 1900.[^o22] Macfarlane considered the "hyperboloidal counterpart to spherical analysis" in a 1910 article "Unification and Development of the Principles of the Algebra of Space" in the *Bulletin* of the [Quaternion Association](wikipedia://en/16416512).[^o23]

[Hans Beck](wikipedia://en/78173324) compared split-quaternion transformations to the circle-permuting property of [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493) in 1910.[^o24] The split-quaternion structure has also been mentioned briefly in the *[Annals of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/2785)*.[^o25][^o26]
