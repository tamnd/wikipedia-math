# Voderberg tiling

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Voderberg tiling"}

*Image: Voderberg-1.png.* A partial Voderberg tiling. Note that all of the colored tiles are congruent.

**Voderberg tiling** is a mathematical [spiral](wikipedia://en/61563) [tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_tiling), invented in 1936 by mathematician Heinz Voderberg (1911–1945).[^o2] [Karl August Reinhardt](wikipedia://en/35727144) posed the question, "Is there a tile such that two copies can completely enclose a third copy?" Voderberg, his student, answered in the affirmative with Form eines Neunecks eine Lösung zu einem Problem von Reinhardt \["On a nonagon as a solution to a problem of Reinhardt"\].[^o3][^o4]

:::

Voderberg tiling is monohedral, consisting of a single shape that [tessellates](wikipedia://en/321671) the plane with congruent copies of itself. In this case, the [prototile](wikipedia://en/39372) is an elongated irregular [nonagon](wikipedia://en/414824), or nine-sided figure. The most interesting feature of this [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) is the fact that two copies of it can fully enclose a third one. For example, the lowest purple nonagon is enclosed by two yellow ones, all three of identical shape.[^o5] Before Voderberg's discovery, mathematicians had questioned whether this could be possible.

Because it has no [translational symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetries), Voderberg tiling is technically [non-periodic](wikipedia://en/868145), even though it exhibits an obvious repeating pattern and its [prototile](wikipedia://en/39372) can also be used to create a periodic tiling.

Vorderberg tiling was the first spiral tiling to be devised,[^o6] preceding later work by [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) and [Geoffrey C. Shephard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_C._Shephard) in the 1970s.[^o2]

A spiral tiling is depicted on the cover of Grünbaum and Shephard's 1987 book *[Tilings and patterns](wikipedia://en/76301624)*.[^o7]
