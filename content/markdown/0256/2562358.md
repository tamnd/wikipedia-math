# Spontaneous magnetization

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spontaneous magnetization"}

**Spontaneous magnetization** is the appearance of an ordered [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) state ([magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348)) at zero applied magnetic field in a [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) or [ferrimagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrimagnetism) material below a [critical point](wikipedia://en/1951419) called the [Curie temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_temperature) or $T_{C}$.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Heated to temperatures above $T_{C}$, ferromagnetic materials become [paramagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramagnetism) and their magnetic behavior is dominated by [spin waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_wave) or [magnons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnon), which are [boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) [collective excitations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_excitations) with energies in the meV range. The magnetization that occurs below $T_{C}$ is an example of the ["spontaneous" breaking](wikipedia://en/297466) of a [global symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_symmetry), a phenomenon that is described by [Goldstone's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldstone's_theorem). The term "symmetry breaking" refers to the choice of a [magnetization direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetization_direction) by the spins, which have [spherical symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_symmetry) above $T_{C}$, but a [preferred axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preferred_axis) (the magnetization direction) below $T_{C}$.

## Temperature dependence {#Temperature_dependence}

To a first order approximation, the temperature dependence of spontaneous magnetization at low temperatures is given by the Bloch $T^{3/2}$ law (by [Felix Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Bloch)):[^o4]

$$M(T) = M(0) \left[1-\left(\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{C}}} \right)^{3/2} \right],$$

where $M(0)$ is the spontaneous magnetization at [absolute zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_zero). The decrease in spontaneous magnetization at higher temperatures is caused by the increasing excitation of [spin waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_waves). In a [particle description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_description), the spin waves correspond to magnons, which are the massless [Goldstone bosons](wikipedia://en/628183) corresponding to the [broken symmetry](wikipedia://en/3667668). This is exactly true for an isotropic magnet.

[Magnetic anisotropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_anisotropy), that is the existence of an easy direction along which the moments align spontaneously in the crystal, corresponds however to "massive" magnons. This is a way of saying that they cost a minimum amount of energy to excite, hence they are very unlikely to be excited as $T\rightarrow 0$. Hence the magnetization of an anisotropic magnet is harder to destroy at low temperature and the temperature dependence of the magnetization deviates accordingly from the Bloch *T*<sup>3/2</sup> law. All real magnets are anisotropic to some extent.

Near the Curie temperature,

$$M(T) \propto \left(T_{\mathrm{C}}-T\right)^\beta,$$

where $\beta$ is a [critical exponent](wikipedia://en/2029677) that depends on the [universality class](wikipedia://en/353890) of the magnetic interaction. Experimentally the exponent is 0.34 for [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) and 0.51 for [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel).[^o5]

An empirical interpolation of the two regimes is given by

$$\frac {M(T)}{M(0)} = \left (1-(T/T_{\mathrm{C}}\right)^{\alpha})^{\beta},$$

it is easy to check two limits of this interpolation that follow laws similar to the Bloch law, for $T \rightarrow 0$, and the critical behavior, for $T \rightarrow T_{\mathrm{C}}$, respectively.
