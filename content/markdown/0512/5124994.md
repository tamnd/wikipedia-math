# Spherical model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spherical model"}

The **spherical model** is a model of [ferromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) similar to the [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744), which was solved in 1952 by [T. H. Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_H._Berlin) and [M. Kac](wikipedia://en/1062425). It has the remarkable property that for linear dimension *d* greater than four, the [critical exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_exponents) that govern the behaviour of the system near the critical point are independent of *d* and the geometry of the system. It is one of the few models of ferromagnetism that can be solved exactly in the presence of an external field.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

The model describes a set of particles on a lattice $\mathbb{L}$ containing *N* sites. Each site *j* of $\mathbb{L}$ contains a spin $\sigma_j$ which interacts only with its nearest neighbours and an external field *H*. It differs from the Ising model in that the $\sigma_j$ are no longer restricted to $\sigma_j \in \{1,-1\}$, but can take all real values, subject to the constraint that

$$\sum_{j=1}^N \sigma_j^2 = N$$

which in a homogeneous system ensures that the average of the square of any spin is one, as in the usual Ising model.

The [partition function](wikipedia://en/312252) generalizes from that of the [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744) to

$$Z_N = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \cdots \int_{-\infty}^\infty d\sigma_1 \cdots d\sigma_N \exp \left[ K \sum_{\langle jl \rangle} \sigma_j \sigma_l + h \sum_j \sigma_j \right] \delta \left[N - \sum_j \sigma_j^2 \right]$$

where $\delta$ is the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021), $\langle jl \rangle$ are the edges of the lattice, and $K=J/kT$ and $h= H/kT$, where *T* is the temperature of the system, *k* is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant) and *J* the coupling constant of the nearest-neighbour interactions.

Berlin and Kac saw this as an approximation to the usual Ising model, arguing that the $\sigma$-summation in the Ising model can be viewed as a sum over all corners of an *N*-dimensional [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) in $\sigma$-space. The becomes an [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) over the [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) of a hypersphere passing through all such corners.

It was rigorously proved by Kac and C. J. Thompson[^o4] that the spherical model is a limiting case of the [N-vector model](wikipedia://en/1165549).

:::

## Equation of state {#Equation_of_state}

Solving the partition function and using a calculation of the [free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy) yields an equation describing the [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348) *M* of the system

$$2J(1-M^2) = kTg'(H/2JM)$$

for the function *g* defined as

$$g(z) = (2 \pi)^{-d} \int_0^{2\pi} \ldots \int_0^{2\pi} d\omega_1 \cdots d\omega_d \ln [z+d -\cos \omega_1 - \cdots - \cos \omega_d]$$

The [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757) per site is given by

$$u =\frac{1}{2} kT - Jd - \frac{1}{2}H(M+M^{-1})$$

an exact relation relating internal energy and magnetization.

## Critical behaviour {#Critical_behaviour}

For $d \leq 2$ the [critical temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_temperature) occurs at [absolute zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_zero), resulting in no [phase transition](wikipedia://en/54423) for the spherical model. For *d* greater than 2, the spherical model exhibits the typical ferromagnetic behaviour, with a finite [Curie temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_temperature) where ferromagnetism ceases. The critical behaviour of the spherical model was derived in the completely general circumstances that the dimension *d* may be a real non-integer dimension.

The critical exponents $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ and $\gamma'$ in the zero-field case which dictate the behaviour of the system close to were derived to be

$$\alpha = \begin{cases} - \frac{4-d}{d-2} & \text{if} \ 2<d<4 \\ 0 & \text{if } d > 4 \end{cases}$$

$$\beta = \frac{1}{2}$$

$$\gamma = \begin{cases} \frac{2}{d-2} & \text{if } 2 <d<4 \\ 1 & \text{if } d > 4 \end{cases}$$

$$\delta = \begin{cases} \frac{d+2}{d-2} & \text{if } 2 < d < 4 \\ 3 & \text{if } d > 4 \end{cases}$$

which are independent of the dimension of *d* when it is greater than four, the dimension being able to take any real value.
