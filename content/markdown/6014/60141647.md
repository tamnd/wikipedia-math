# Word-representable graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Word-representable graph"}

In the mathematical field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **word-representable graph** is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) that can be characterized by a word (or sequence) whose entries alternate in a prescribed way. In particular, if the vertex set of the graph is *V*, one should be able to choose a word *w* over the alphabet *V* such that letters *a* and *b* alternate in *w* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the pair *ab* is an edge in the graph. (Letters *a* and *b* **alternate** in *w* if, after removing from *w* all letters but the copies of *a* and *b*, one obtains a word *abab*... or a word *baba*....) For example, the [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) labeled by *a*, *b*, *c* and *d* in clock-wise direction is word-representable because it can be represented by *abdacdbc*: the pairs *ab*, *bc*, *cd* and *ad* alternate, but the pairs *ac* and *bd* do not.

:::

The word *w* is *G*'s *word-representant*, and one says that that *w* *represents* *G*. The smallest (by the number of vertices) non-word-representable graph is the [wheel graph](wikipedia://en/1655876) *W*<sub>5</sub>, which is the only non-word-representable graph on 6 vertices.

The definition of a word-representable graph works both in labelled and unlabelled cases since any labelling of a graph is equivalent to any other labelling. Also, the class of word-representable graphs is [hereditary](wikipedia://en/13552465). Word-representable graphs generalise several important classes of graphs such as [circle graphs](wikipedia://en/1399873), [3-colorable graphs](wikipedia://en/426743) and [comparability graphs](wikipedia://en/6978672). Various generalisations of the theory of word-representable graphs accommodate representation of *any* graph.

## History {#History}

Word-representable graphs were introduced by [Sergey Kitaev](wikipedia://en/55632689)[^o3] in 2004 based on joint research with Steven Seif[^o4] on the *[Perkins semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkins_semigroup)*, which has played an important role in semigroup theory since 1960.[^o5] The first systematic study of word-representable graphs was undertaken in a 2008 paper by Kitaev and Artem Pyatkin,[^o6] starting development of the theory. One of key contributors to the area is [Magnús M. Halldórsson](http://www.ru.is/~mmh/).[^o7][^o8][^o9] Up to date, 35+ papers have been written on the subject, and the core of the book[^o5] by Sergey Kitaev and Vadim Lozin is devoted to the theory of word-representable graphs. A quick way to get familiar with the area is to read one of the survey articles.[^o10][^o11][^o12]

## Motivation to study the graphs {#Motivation_to_study_the_graphs}

According to,[^o5] word-representable graphs are relevant to various fields, thus motivating to study the graphs. These fields are [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), [combinatorics on words](wikipedia://en/20913490), and [scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduling). Word-representable graphs are especially important in graph theory, since they generalise several important classes of graphs, e.g. [circle graphs](wikipedia://en/1399873), [3-colorable graphs](wikipedia://en/426743) and [comparability graphs](wikipedia://en/6978672).

## Early results {#Early_results}

It was shown in [^o6] that a graph *G* is word-representable if it is **k-representable** for some *k*, that is, *G* can be represented by a word having *k* copies of each letter. Moreover, if a graph is *k*-representable then it is also (*k* + 1)-representable. Thus, the notion of the **representation number of a graph**, as the *minimum* *k* such that a graph is word-representable, is well-defined. Non-word-representable graphs have the representation number ∞. Graphs with representation number 1 are precisely the set of [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816), while graphs with representation number 2 are precisely the class of [circle](wikipedia://en/1399873) non-complete graphs. In particular, [forests](http://mathworld.wolfram.com/Forest.html) (except for single [trees](wikipedia://en/48560) on at most 2 vertices), [ladder graphs](wikipedia://en/19657952) and [cycle graphs](wikipedia://en/878962) have representation number 2. No classification for graphs with representation number 3 is known. However, there are examples of such graphs, e.g. [Petersen's graph](wikipedia://en/194926) and [prisms](http://mathworld.wolfram.com/PrismGraph.html). Moreover, the 3-[subdivision](wikipedia://en/353021) of any graph is 3-representable. In particular, for every graph *G* there exists a 3-representable graph *H* that contains *G* as a [minor](wikipedia://en/353042).[^o6]

A graph *G* is **permutationally representable** if it can be represented by a word of the form *p*<sub>1</sub>*p*<sub>2</sub>...*p<sub>k</sub>*, where *p<sub>i</sub>* is a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027). On can also say that *G* is **permutationally *k*-representable**. A graph is permutationally representable iff it is a [comparability graph](wikipedia://en/6978672).[^o4] A graph is word-representable implies that the [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/7309251) of each vertex is permutationally representable (i.e. is a [comparability graph](wikipedia://en/6978672)).[^o4] Converse to the last statement is not true.[^o7] However, the fact that the [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/7309251) of each vertex is a [comparability graph](wikipedia://en/6978672) implies that the [Maximum Clique problem](wikipedia://en/249254) is polynomially solvable on word-representable graphs.[^o8][^o9]

## Semi-transitive orientations {#Semi-transitive_orientations}

Semi-transitive orientations provide a powerful tool to study word-representable graphs. A directed graph is **semi-transitively oriented** iff it is [acyclic](http://mathworld.wolfram.com/AcyclicGraph.html) and for any directed path *u*<sub>1</sub>→*u*<sub>2</sub>→ ...→*u<sub>t</sub>*, *t* ≥ 2, either there is no edge from *u*<sub>1</sub> to *u<sub>t</sub>* or all edges *u<sub>i</sub>* → *u<sub>j</sub>* exist for 1 ≤ *i* \< *j* ≤ *t*. A key theorem in the theory of word-representable graphs states that a graph is word-representable iff it admits a semi-transitive orientation.[^o9] As a corollary to the proof of the key theorem one obtain an upper bound on word-representants: Each non-complete word-representable graph *G* is 2(*n* − *κ*(*G*))-representable, where *κ*(*G*) is the size of a maximal clique in *G*.[^o9] As an immediate corollary of the last statement, one has that the [recognition problem](https://www.encyclopediaofmath.org/index.php/Recognition_problem) of word-representability is in [NP](wikipedia://en/21562). In 2014, [Vincent Limouzy](http://fc.isima.fr/~limouzy/) observed that it is an [NP-complete problem](wikipedia://en/23385892) to recognise whether a given graph is word-representable.[^o5][^o10] Another important corollary to the key theorem is that any [3-colorable graph](wikipedia://en/426743) is word-representable. The last fact implies that many classical graph problems are NP-hard on word-representable graphs.

## Overview of selected results {#Overview_of_selected_results}

### Non-word-representable graphs {#Non-word-representable_graphs}

For *n* ≥ 2, the [wheel graph](wikipedia://en/1655876) *W*<sub>2*n*+1</sub>, which consists of a cycle of length 2*n* + 1 and an additional vertex connected to all vertices in the cycle, is not word-representable. In particular, *W*<sub>5</sub> is the smallest (by the number of vertices) non-word-representable graph. Taking any non-comparability graph and adding an apex (a vertex connected to any other vertex), we obtain a non-word-representable graph, which then can produce infinitely many non-word-representable graphs.[^o5] Any graph produced in this way will necessarily have a triangle (a cycle of length 3), and a vertex of degree at least 5. Non-word-representable graphs of maximum degree 4 exist [^o18] and non-word-representable triangle-free graphs exist.[^o8] Regular non-word representable graphs also exist.[^o5] Non-isomorphic non-word-representable connected graphs on at most eight vertices were first enumerated by Heman Z.Q. Chen. His calculations were extended in,[^o19] where it was shown that the numbers of non-isomorphic non-word-representable connected graphs on 5−11 vertices are given, respectively, by 0, 1, 25, 929, 54957, 4880093, 650856040. This is the sequence A290814 in the [Online Encyclopaedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS)](http://oeis.org/search?q=1%2C+25%2C+929%2C+54957%2C+4880093&language=english&go=Search).

### Operations on graphs and word-representability {#Operations_on_graphs_and_word-representability}

Operations preserving word-representability are removing a vertex, replacing a vertex with a module, Cartesian product, rooted product, subdivision of a graph, connecting two graphs by an edge and gluing two graphs in a vertex.[^o5] The operations not necessarily preserving word-representability are taking the complement, taking the line graph, [edge contraction](wikipedia://en/3758115),[^o5] gluing two graphs in a clique of size 2 or more,[^o38] tensor product, lexicographic product and strong product.[^o21] Edge-deletion, edge-addition and edge-lifting with respect to word-representability (equivalently, semi-transitive orientability) are studied in.[^o21]

### Graphs with high representation number {#Graphs_with_high_representation_number}

While each non-complete word-representable graph *G* is 2(*n* − *κ*(*G*))-representable, where *κ*(*G*) is the size of a maximal clique in *G*,[^o9] the highest known representation number is floor(*n*/2) given by [crown graphs](wikipedia://en/21062705) with an all-adjacent vertex.[^o9] Interestingly, such graphs are not the only graphs that require long representations.[^o23] Crown graphs themselves are shown to require long (possibly longest) representations among bipartite graphs.[^o24]

### Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

Known computational complexities for problems on word-representable graphs can be summarised as follows:[^o5][^o10]

### Representation of planar graphs {#Representation_of_planar_graphs}

Triangle-free [planar graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_graphs) are word-representable.[^o9] A K4-free near-triangulation is 3-colourable if and only if it is word-representable;[^o31] this result generalises studies in.[^o32][^o33] Word-representability of face subdivisions of triangular grid graphs is studied in [^o34] and word-representability of triangulations of grid-covered cylinder graphs is studied in.[^o35]

### Representation of split graphs {#Representation_of_split_graphs}

Word-representation of [split graphs](wikipedia://en/9928681) is studied in.[^o37][^o38] In particular,[^o37] offers a characterisation in terms of forbidden induced subgraphs of word-representable split graphs in which vertices in the independent set are of degree at most 2, or the size of the clique is 4, while a computational characterisation of word-representable split graphs with the clique of size 5 is given in.[^o38] Also, necessary and sufficient conditions for an orientation of a split graph to be semi-transitive are given in,[^o37] while in [^o38] [threshold graphs](wikipedia://en/11503485) are shown to be word-representable and the split graphs are used to show that gluing two word-representable graphs in any clique of size at least 2 may, or may not result in a word-representable graph, which solved a long-standing [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335).

### Graphs representable by pattern avoiding words {#Graphs_representable_by_pattern_avoiding_words}

A graph is ***p*-representable** if it can be represented by a word avoiding a [pattern](wikipedia://en/24360911) *p*. For example, 132-representable graphs are those that can be represented by words *w*<sub>1</sub>w<sub>2</sub>...*w<sub>n</sub>* where there are no 1 ≤ *a* \< *b* \< *c* ≤ *n* such that *w<sub>a</sub>* \< *w<sub>c</sub>* \< *w<sub>b</sub>*. In [^o40] it is shown that any 132-representable graph is necessarily a [circle graph](wikipedia://en/1399873), and any [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) and any [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962), as well as any graph on at most 5 vertices, are 132-representable. It was shown in [^o41] that not all circle graphs are 132-representable, and that 123-representable graphs are also a proper subclass of the class of circle graphs.

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

A number of generalisations [^o43][^o44][^o45] of the notion of a word-representable graph are based on the observation by [Jeff Remmel](wikipedia://en/75786487) that non-edges are defined by occurrences of the pattern 11 (two consecutive equal letters) in a word representing a graph, while edges are defined by avoidance of this pattern. For example, instead of the pattern 11, one can use the pattern 112, or the pattern, 1212, or any other binary pattern where the assumption that the alphabet is ordered can be made so that a letter in a word corresponding to 1 in the pattern is less than that corresponding to 2 in the pattern. Letting *u* be an ordered binary pattern we thus get the notion of a ***u*-representable graph**. So, word-representable graphs are just the class of 11-representable graphs. Intriguingly, **any graph** can be *u*-represented assuming *u* is of length at least 3.[^o46]

Another way to generalise the notion of a word-representable graph, again suggested by Remmel, is to introduce the "degree of tolerance" *k* for occurrences of a pattern *p* defining edges/non-edges. That is, we can say that if there are up to *k* occurrence of *p* formed by letters *a* and *b*, then there is an edge between *a* and *b*. This gives the notion of a *k*-*p*-representable graph, and *k*-11-representable graphs are studied in.[^o47] Note that 0-11-representable graphs are precisely word-representable graphs. The key results in [^o47] are that **any** graph is 2-11-representable and that word-representable graphs are a proper subclass of 1-11-representable graphs. Whether or not any graph is 1-11-representable is a challenging open problem.

For yet another type of relevant generalisation, [Hans Zantema](wikipedia://en/43715838) suggested the notion of a ***k*-semi-transitive orientation** refining the notion of a semi-transitive orientation.[^o23] The idea here is restricting ourselves to considering *only* directed paths of length not exceeding *k* while allowing violations of semi-transitivity on longer paths.

## Open problems {#Open_problems}

Open problems on word-representable graphs can be found in,[^o5][^o10][^o11][^o12] and they include:

- Characterise (non-)word-representable planar graphs.
- Characterise word-representable near-triangulations containing the complete graph *K*<sub>4</sub> (such a characterisation is known for *K*<sub>4</sub>-free planar graphs [^o31]).
- Classify graphs with representation number 3. (See [^o50] for the state-of-the-art in this direction.)
- Is the [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of a non-word-representable graph always non-word-representable?
- Are there any graphs on *n* vertices whose representation requires more than floor(*n*/2) copies of each letter?
- Is it true that out of all [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431) [crown graphs](wikipedia://en/21062705) require longest word-representants? (See [^o24] for relevant discussion.)
- Characterise word-representable graphs in terms of (induced) forbidden subgraphs.
- Which (hard) problems on graphs can be translated to words representing them and solved on words (efficiently)?

:::
