# Argument (complex analysis)

*Image: Complex\_number\_illustration\_modarg.svg.* Figure 1. This [Argand diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argand_diagram) represents the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) lying on a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29). For each point on the plane, $\arg$ is the function which returns the angle $\varphi$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Argument"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) (particularly in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759)), the **argument** of a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $z$, denoted $\arg(z)$, is the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between the positive [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [axis](wikipedia://en/7706) and the line joining the origin and $z$, represented as a point in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), shown as $\varphi$ in Figure 1. By convention the [positive real axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_real_axis) is drawn pointing rightward, the positive [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) axis is drawn pointing upward, and complex numbers with positive real part are considered to have an [anticlockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticlockwise) argument with positive sign.

:::

When any real-valued angle is considered, the argument is a [multivalued function](wikipedia://en/222367) operating on the nonzero complex numbers. The [principal value](wikipedia://en/408225) of this function is single-valued, typically chosen to be the unique value of the argument that lies within the interval −*π*, *π*.[^o2][^o3] In this article the multi-valued function will be denoted $\arg(z)$ and its principal value will be denoted $\operatorname{Arg}(z)$, but in some sources the capitalization of these symbols is exchanged.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Complex number illustration multiarg.svg.* Figure 2. Two choices for the argument $\varphi$

An **argument** of the nonzero complex number $z = x + iy$, denoted $\arg(z)$, is defined in two equivalent ways:

1. Geometrically, in the complex plane, as the [2D polar angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2D_polar_angle) $\varphi$ from the positive real axis to the vector representing $z$. The numeric value is given by the angle in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), and is positive if measured counterclockwise.
1. Algebraically, as any real quantity $\varphi$ such that

$$z = r (\cos \varphi + i \sin \varphi) = r e^{i\varphi}$$

for some positive real $r$ (see [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613)). The quantity $r$ is the *[modulus](wikipedia://en/991)* (or absolute value) of $z$, denoted $\lvert z\rvert$:

$$r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}.$$

The argument of zero is usually left undefined. The names *[magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301),* for the modulus, and *[phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29)*,[^o6][^o2] for the argument, are sometimes used equivalently. In some older mathematical texts, the term "amplitude" was used interchangeably with argument to denote the angle of a complex number. While this term is largely outdated in modern texts, it still appears in some regional educational resources, where it is sometimes used in introductory-level textbooks.[^o7]

Under both definitions, it can be seen that the argument of any non-zero complex number has many possible values: firstly, as a geometrical angle, it is clear that whole circle rotations do not change the point, so angles differing by an integer multiple of $2\pi$ [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) (a complete [turn](wikipedia://en/855329)) are the same, as reflected by figure 2 on the right. Similarly, from the [periodicity](wikipedia://en/76848) of [$\sin$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) and [$\cos$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine), the second definition also has this property.

:::

## Principal value {#Principal_value}

*Image: Principal value of arg.svg.* Figure 3. The principal value $\operatorname{Arg}$ of the blue point at $1 + i$ is $\pi/4$. The red line here is the branch cut and corresponds to the two red lines in figure 4 seen vertically above each other).

Because a complete rotation around the origin leaves a complex number unchanged, there are many choices which could be made for $\varphi$ by circling the origin any number of times. This is shown in figure 2, a representation of the [multi-valued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-valued) (set-valued) function $f(x,y)=\arg(x+iy)$, where a vertical line (not shown in the figure) cuts the surface at heights representing all the possible choices of angle for that point.

When a [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) function is required, then the usual choice, known as the *[principal value](wikipedia://en/408225)*, is the value in the open-closed [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) −*π*, *π* radians, that is from $-\pi$ to $\pi$ [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) excluding $-\pi$ radians itself (equiv., from −180 to +180 [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294), excluding −180° itself). This represents an angle of up to half a complete circle from the positive real axis in either direction.

Some authors define the range of the principal value as being in the closed–open interval 0, 2*π*.

::: {#o10 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

### Notation {#Notation}

The principal value sometimes has the initial letter capitalized, as in $\operatorname{Arg} z$, especially when a general version of the argument is also being considered. Note that notation varies, so $\arg$ and $\operatorname{Arg}$ may be interchanged in different texts.

The set of all possible values of the argument can be written in terms of $\operatorname{Arg}$ as:

$$\arg(z) = \{\operatorname{Arg}(z) + 2\pi n \mid n \in \mathbb Z\}.$$

:::

## Computing from the real and imaginary part {#Computing_from_the_real_and_imaginary_part}

If a complex number is known in terms of its real and imaginary parts, then the function that calculates the principal value $\operatorname{Arg}$ is called the [two-argument arctangent function, $\operatorname{atan}2$](wikipedia://en/3856703):

$$\operatorname{Arg}(x + iy) = \operatorname{atan2}(y,\, x)$$

The $\operatorname{atan}2$ function is available in the math libraries of many programming languages, sometimes under a different name, and usually returns a value in the range −π, π.[^o2] See *[atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703)* for further detail and alternative implementations, such as

$$\operatorname{atan2}(y, x) = 2 \operatorname{atan} \frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2} + x}\,,$$

which works except when $y=0$ and $x\le0$, in which case the value is instead $\pi$ if $x<0$ or undefined when $x=0$.

## Realizations of the function in computer languages {#Realizations_of_the_function_in_computer_languages}

### Wolfram language (Mathematica) {#Wolfram_language_(Mathematica)}

In [Wolfram language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfram_Language), there's `Arg[z]`:[^o14]

`Arg[x + y I]` $= \begin{cases} 0 &\text{if } x = 0 \text{ and } y = 0, \\[5mu] \text{undefined} &\text{if } |x| = \infty \text{ and } |y|=\infty, \\[5mu] 0 &\text{if } x = \infty \text{ and } |y|\ne\infty, \\[5mu] \pi &\text{if } x = -\infty \text{ and } |y|\ne\infty, \\[5mu] \pm\frac{\pi}{2} &\text{if } y = \pm\infty \text{ and } |x|\ne\infty, \\[5mu] \operatorname{Arg}(x + y i) &\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$

or using the language's `ArcTan`:

`Arg[x + y I]` $= \begin{cases} 0 &\text{if } x = 0 \text{ and } y = 0, \\[5mu] \text{ArcTan[x, y]} &\text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$

`ArcTan[x, y]` is $\operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$ extended to work with infinities. `ArcTan[0, 0]` is `Indeterminate` (i.e. it is [still](wikipedia://en/366723) defined), while `ArcTan[Infinity, -Infinity]` doesn't return anything (i.e. it is [undefined](wikipedia://en/9920876)).

### Maple {#Maple}

[Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29)'s `argument(z)` behaves the same as `Arg[z]` in Wolfram language, except that `argument(z)` also returns $\pi$ if `z` is the special floating-point value `−0.`.[^o16] Also, Maple doesn't have $\operatorname{atan2}$.

### MATLAB {#MATLAB}

[MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412)'s `angle(z)` behaves[^o18][^o19] the same as `Arg[z]` in Wolfram language, except that it is

$$\begin{cases} \frac{1\pi}{4} &\text{if } x = \infty \text{ and } y = \infty, \\[5mu] -\frac{1\pi}{4} &\text{if } x = \infty \text{ and } y = -\infty, \\[5mu] \frac{3\pi}{4} &\text{if } x = -\infty \text{ and } y = \infty, \\[5mu] -\frac{3\pi}{4} &\text{if } x = -\infty \text{ and } y = -\infty. \end{cases}$$

Unlike in Maple and Wolfram language, MATLAB's `atan2(y, x)` is equivalent to `angle(x + y*1i)`. That is, `atan2(0, 0)` is $0$.

## Identities {#Identities}

One of the main motivations for defining the principal value $\operatorname{Arg}$ is to be able to write complex numbers in modulus-argument form. Hence for any complex number $z$,

$$z = \left| z \right| e^{i \operatorname{Arg} z}.$$

This is only really valid if $z$ is non-zero, but can be considered valid for $z = 0$ if $\operatorname{Arg}0$ is considered as an [indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723)—rather than as being undefined.

Some further identities follow. If $z_{1}$ and $z_{2}$ are two non-zero complex numbers, then

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Arg}(z_1 z_2) &\equiv \operatorname{Arg}z_1 + \operatorname{Arg}z_2 \pmod{\left(-\pi,\pi\right]}, \\ \operatorname{Arg}\frac{z_1}{z_2} &\equiv \operatorname{Arg}z_1 - \operatorname{Arg}z_2 \pmod{\left(-\pi,\pi\right]}, \end{aligned}$$

where $(\operatorname{mod}\left(-\pi,\pi\right])$ means to add or subtract any integer multiple of $2\pi$ if necessary to bring the value into the interval of −*π*, *π* radians. In particular,

$$\operatorname{Arg}(-z) \equiv \operatorname{Arg}z+\pi\begin{cases}1,&\operatorname{Im}z<0,\\\operatorname{sgn}z,&\operatorname{Im}z=0,\\-1,&\operatorname{Im}z>0.\\\end{cases}$$

If $z \ne 0$ and $n$ is any integer, then[^o2]

$$\operatorname{Arg}\left(z^n\right) \equiv n \operatorname{Arg}z \pmod{\left(-\pi,\pi\right]}.$$

::: {#o22 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

$$\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{-1- i}i\right) = \left(\operatorname{Arg}(-1 - i) - \operatorname{Arg}i = -\frac{3\pi}4 - \frac\pi2 = -\frac{5\pi}4\right)\pmod{\left(-\pi,\pi\right]}=\frac{3\pi}4.$$

:::

### Using the complex logarithm {#Using_the_complex_logarithm}

From $z = |z| e^{i \operatorname{Arg}z}$, we get $i \operatorname{Arg}z = \ln \frac{z}{|z|}$, alternatively $\operatorname{Arg}(z) = \operatorname{Im}\ln \frac{z}{|z|} = \operatorname{Im}\ln z$. As we are taking the imaginary part, any normalization by a real scalar will not affect the result. This is useful when one has the [complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290) available.

### Differentiation {#Differentiation}

The argument function is not complex differentiable, as the limit

$$\lim\limits_{z\to z_0}\frac{\arg z-\arg z_0}{z-z_0}$$

does not exist for any $z_0\in\mathbb{C}$ and on any branch (indeed, for any chosen branch the numerator takes the same value). However, the [Wirtinger derivatives](wikipedia://en/28479408) may be applied to it. Begin from the logarithmic identity

$$\ln z=\operatorname{Ln}|z|+i\arg z.$$

Use that $|z|^{2}=z\bar{z}$:

$$\ln z=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Ln} z +\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Ln}\bar{z}+i\arg z.$$

Performing different branch cuts lets one apply the derivatives to all points of the principal logarithm in two goes. First, apply $\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$:

$$\frac{1}{z}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{z}+0+i\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\arg z.$$

Rearranging gives $\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\arg z=\frac{-i}{2z}$. Now apply $\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}$:

$$0=0+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\bar{z}}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\arg z.$$

This shows that $\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}}\arg z=\frac{i}{2\bar{z}}$. As both Wirtinger derivatives exist, one can then apply the Dolbeault derivatives,

$$\partial\arg(z)=\frac{-i}{2z}\,dz,$$

$$\bar{\partial}\arg(z)=\frac{i}{2\bar{z}}\,d\bar{z}.$$

This then means the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) exists, given by their sum,

$$d\arg(z)=\frac{-i}{2z}\,dz+\frac{i}{2\bar{z}}\,d\bar{z}.$$

This is an important [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519), as it is precisely the generator of the first [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) of the circle, $H^{1}_{dR}(S^{1})$. In other words,

$$d\arg(z)=\frac{-i}{2z}\,dz+\frac{i}{2\bar{z}}\,d\bar{z}=\frac{x\,dy-y\,dx}{x^{2}+y^{2}},$$

making this form closed but not exact in the usual sense (a byproduct of being multivalued):

$$\oint\limits_{S^1}d\arg(z)=2\pi>0$$

with the standard orientation.
