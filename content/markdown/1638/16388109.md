# Dunford–Pettis property

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dunford–Pettis property"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **Dunford–Pettis property**, named after [Nelson Dunford](wikipedia://en/21022291) and [B. J. Pettis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._J._Pettis), is a property of a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) stating that all weakly compact operators from this space into another Banach space are completely continuous. Many standard Banach spaces have this property, most notably, the space $C(K)$ of continuous functions on a [compact space](wikipedia://en/6042) and the space [$L^1(\mu)$](wikipedia://en/45194) of the Lebesgue integrable functions on a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270). [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) introduced the concept in the early 1950s (Grothendieck 1953), following the work of [Dunford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunford) and Pettis, who developed earlier results of [Shizuo Kakutani](wikipedia://en/2572542), [Kōsaku Yosida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kōsaku_Yosida), and several others. Important results were obtained more recently by [Jean Bourgain](wikipedia://en/1024131). Nevertheless, the Dunford–Pettis property is not completely understood.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Banach space $X$ has the **Dunford–Pettis property** if every continuous weakly [compact operator](wikipedia://en/577441) $T : X \to Y$ from $X$ into another Banach space $Y$ transforms weakly compact sets in $X$ into norm-compact sets in $Y$ (such operators are called [completely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_continuous)). An important equivalent definition is that for any [weakly convergent](wikipedia://en/33662) [sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29) $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ of $X$ and $f_1, f_2, \ldots$ of the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) $X^*,$ converging (weakly) to $x$ and $f,$ the sequence $f_1(x_1), f_2(x_2), \ldots, f_n(x_n), \ldots$ converges to $f(x).$

:::

::: {#o6 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Counterexamples"}

## Counterexamples {#Counterexamples}

- The second definition may appear counterintuitive at first, but consider an orthonormal basis $e_n$ of an infinite-dimensional, separable Hilbert space $H.$ Then $e_n \to 0$ weakly, but for all $n$ $$\langle e_n, e_n\rangle = 1.$$ Thus separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces cannot have the Dunford–Pettis property.
- Consider as another example the space $L^p(-\pi, \pi)$ where $1 < p < \infty.$ The sequences $x_n = e^{inx}$ in $L^p$ and $f_n = e^{inx}$ in $L^q = \left(L^p\right)^*$ both converge weakly to zero. But $$\langle f_n, x_n \rangle = \int\limits_{-\pi}^\pi 1\, {\rm d}x = 2 \pi.$$
- More generally, no infinite-dimensional [reflexive Banach space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_Banach_space) may have the Dunford–Pettis property. In particular, an infinite-dimensional [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) and more generally, [Lp spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) with $1 < p < \infty$ do not possess this property.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- If $K$ is a [compact Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Hausdorff_space), then the Banach space $C(K)$ of [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) with the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755) has the Dunford–Pettis property.

:::
