# Faddeeva function

*Image: Faddeeva.svg.* Faddeeva function on the complex plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Faddeeva function"}

The **Faddeeva function** or **Kramp function** is a scaled [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) complementary [error function](wikipedia://en/177111),

:::

$$w(z):=e^{-z^2}\operatorname{erfc}(-iz) = \operatorname{erfcx}(-iz) =e^{-z^2}\left(1+\frac{2i}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^z e^{t^2}\text{d}t\right).$$

It is related to the [Fresnel integral](wikipedia://en/271143), to [Dawson's integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dawson's_integral), and to the [Voigt function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voigt_function).

The function arises in various physical problems, typically relating to electromagnetic responses in complicated media.

- problems involving small-amplitude waves propagating through [Maxwellian](wikipedia://en/19830) [plasmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_%28physics%29), and in particular appears in the plasma's [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) from which [dispersion relations](wikipedia://en/853778) are derived, hence it is sometimes referred to as the **plasma dispersion function[^o2]**[^o23] (although this name is sometimes used instead for the rescaled function $Z(z)=i\sqrt{{\pi}}w(z)$ defined by *Fried and Conte*, 1961[^o2][^o3]).
- the infrared [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) functions of amorphous oxides have resonances (due to [phonons](wikipedia://en/85754)) that are sometimes too complicated to fit using simple harmonic oscillators. The [Brendel–Bormann oscillator model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brendel–Bormann_oscillator_model) uses an infinite superposition of oscillators having slightly different frequencies, with a Gaussian distribution.[^o4] The integrated response can be written in terms of the Faddeeva function.
- the Faddeeva function is also used in the analysis of electromagnetic waves of the type used in AM radio. Groundwaves are vertically polarised waves propagating over a lossy ground with finite resistivity and permittivity.
- the Faddeeva function also describes the changes of the [neutron cross sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_cross_section) of materials as temperature is varied.[^o5]

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

### Real and imaginary parts {#Real_and_imaginary_parts}

The decomposition into real and imaginary parts is usually written

$$w(x+iy)=V(x,y)+iL(x,y),$$

where *V* and *L* are called the real and imaginary *Voigt functions*, since *V(x,y)* is the [Voigt profile](wikipedia://en/1202098) (up to prefactors).

### Sign inversion {#Sign_inversion}

For sign-inverted arguments, the following both apply:

$$w(-z)=2e^{-z^2} - w(z)$$

and

$$w(-z)=w\left(z^*\right)^*$$

where \* denotes complex conjugate.

### Relation to the complementary error function {#Relation_to_the_complementary_error_function}

The Faddeeva function evaluated on imaginary arguments equals the scaled complementary error function ($\mathrm{erfcx}$):

$$w(iz)=\mathrm{erfcx}(z)=e^{z^2}\mathrm{erfc}(z),$$

where erfc is the [complementary error function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_error_function). For large real *x*:

$$\mathrm{erfcx}(x)\approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi} x}$$

### Derivative {#Derivative}

In some applications, it is necessary to know not only the original values of the Faddeeva function, but also its derivative (e.g. in [Non-linear least squares regression](wikipedia://en/15652764) in [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy)). Its derivative is given by:[^o12][^o13]

$$\frac{dw\left(z\right)}{dz} = \frac{2i}{\sqrt{\pi}} - 2\cdot z\cdot w\left(z\right)$$

This expression can also be broken down further in terms of changes in the real and imaginary part of the Faddeeva function $\Re\left(w\left(z\right)\right) = \Re_{w}$ and $\Im\left(w\left(z\right)\right) = \Im_{w}$. Basically, this requires knowledge about the real and imaginary part of the product $z\cdot w\left(z\right)$. Making use of the above definition $z = x + iy$, the derivative can therefore be split into [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) with respect to $x$ and $y$ as follows:

$\frac{d\Re_{w}}{dx} = 2\cdot\left(y\cdot\Im_{w} - x\cdot\Re_{w}\right) = \frac{d\Im_{w}}{dy}$6and6$\frac{d\Re_{w}}{dy} = -2\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} - x\cdot\Im_{w} - y\cdot\Re_{w}\right) = -\frac{d\Im_{w}}{dx}$

$\frac{d\Im_{w}}{dx} = 2\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} - x\cdot\Im_{w} - y\cdot\Re_{w}\right) = -\frac{d\Re_{w}}{dy}$6and6$\frac{d\Im_{w}}{dy} = 2\cdot\left(y\cdot\Im_{w} - x\cdot\Re_{w}\right) = \frac{d\Re_{w}}{dx}$

A practical example for the use of these partial derivatives can be found [here](wikipedia://en/1202098#Derivatives).

:::

## Integral representation {#Integral_representation}

The Faddeeva function occurs as

$$w(z)=\frac{i}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{e^{- t^2}}{z - t} \,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{2iz}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{- t^2}}{z^2 - t^2} \,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad \operatorname{Im}z > 0$$

meaning that it is a convolution of a Gaussian with a simple pole.

## History {#History}

The function was tabulated by [Vera Faddeeva](wikipedia://en/48501513) and N. N. Terentyev in 1954.[^o16] It appears as nameless function *w(z)* in *[Abramowitz and Stegun](wikipedia://en/1206236)* (1964), formula 7.1.3. The name **Faddeeva function** was apparently introduced by G. P. M. Poppe and C. M. J. Wijers in 1990;[^o17] previously, it was known as Kramp's function (probably after [Christian Kramp](wikipedia://en/308409)).[^o18]

Early implementations used methods by [Walter Gautschi](wikipedia://en/19686194) (1969–70; [ACM Algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACM_Transactions_on_Mathematical_Software) 363)[^o19] or by J. Humlicek (1982).[^o20] A more efficient algorithm was proposed by Poppe and Wijers (1990; ACM Algorithm 680).[^o21] J.A.C. Weideman (1994) proposed a particularly short algorithm that takes no more than eight lines of [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412) code.[^o22] Zaghloul and Ali pointed out deficiencies of previous algorithms and proposed a new one (2011; ACM Algorithm 916).[^o23] Another algorithm has been proposed by M. Abrarov and B.M. Quine (2011/2012).[^o24]

::: {#o26 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Implementations"}

## Implementations {#Implementations}

Two software implementations, which are free for non-commercial use only,[^o27] were published in [ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACM_Transactions_on_Mathematical_Software) (TOMS) as Algorithm 680 (in [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran),[^o28] later translated into [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29))[^o29] and Algorithm 916 by Zaghloul and Ali (in [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412)).[^o30]

A [free and open source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_open_source) C or C++ implementation derived from a combination of Algorithm 680 and Algorithm 916 (using different algorithms for different *z*) is also available under the [MIT License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_License),[^o31] and is maintained as a library package *libcerf*.[^o32] This implementation is also available as a [plug-in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plug-in_%28computing%29) for Matlab,[^o31] [GNU Octave](wikipedia://en/48707),[^o31] and in [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) via [Scipy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scipy) as `scipy.special.wofz` (which was originally the TOMS 680 code, but was replaced due to copyright concerns[^o33]).

For fast calculation of the plasma dispersion function $Z(z)$, the J-pole [Padé approximation](wikipedia://en/3090820) is found to be useful,[^o34] i.e.,

$$Z(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{b_j}{z-c_j}.$$

The above approximation is valid for the upper plane, and $z$ weakly close to the real axis, such that $Im(z)$ is not far from the real axis of the lower plane. For accurate calculation of $Im(z)<0$, one can use

$$Z(z)=[Z(z^*)]^*+2i\sqrt{\pi}\exp[-z^2].$$

A coding of the above J-pole approach and comparison with an accurate plasma dispersion function can be found.[^o35] Comphensive coefficients to rapid computation of the plasma dispersion function with rational and multi-pole approximation are also available.[^o36]
