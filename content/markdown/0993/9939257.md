# Diagrammatic reasoning

*Image: Wikipedia article-creation-2.svg.* Sample [flowchart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowchart) representing the [decision process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_process) to add a new article to Wikipedia.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Diagrammatic reasoning"}

**Diagrammatic reasoning** is [reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reasoning) by means of [visual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual) [representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depiction). The study of *diagrammatic reasoning* is about the understanding of concepts and ideas, visualized with the use of [diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram) and [imagery](wikipedia://en/71925) instead of by [linguistic](wikipedia://en/21173) or [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) means.

:::

## Diagram {#Diagram}

A [diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram) is a 2D geometric symbolic [representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depiction) of [information](wikipedia://en/18985062) according to some [visualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illustration) technique. Sometimes, the technique uses a [3D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_space) visualization which is then [projected](wikipedia://en/313741) onto the 2D surface. The term diagram in common sense can have two meanings:

- *visual information device*: Like the term "[illustration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illustration)" the diagram is used as a collective term standing for the whole class of technical genres, including [graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphics), technical drawings and [tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_%28information%29).[^o5]
- *specific kind of visual display*: This is only the genre, that shows qualitative data with shapes that are connected by lines, arrows, or other visual links.

In science the term is used in both ways. For example, Anderson (1997) stated more general "diagrams are pictorial, yet abstract, representations of information, and [maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map), [line graphs](wikipedia://en/675231), [bar charts](wikipedia://en/393311), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) [blueprints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueprint), and [architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architect)' [sketches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sketch_%28drawing%29) are all examples of diagrams, whereas photographs and video are not".[^o3] On the other hand, Lowe (1993) defined diagrams as specifically "abstract graphic portrayals of the subject matter they represent".[^o4]

In the specific sense diagrams and charts contrast [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics), technical illustrations, [infographics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infographics), maps, and [technical drawings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_drawing), by showing "abstract rather than [literal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation) representations of information".[^o5] The essences of a diagram can be seen as:[^o5]

- a *form* of visual [formatting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Content_format) devices
- a *display* that does not show [quantitative data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_data), but rather relationships and abstract information
- with *building blocks* such as geometrical shapes that are connected by lines, [arrows](wikipedia://en/51513), or other visual links.

Or as Bert S. Hall wrote, "diagrams are simplified figures, caricatures in a way, intended to convey essential meaning".[^o6] According to [Jan V. White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_V._White) (1984) "the characteristics of a good diagram are elegance, clarity, ease, pattern, simplicity, and validity".[^o5] Elegance for White means that what you are seeing in the diagram is "the simplest and most fitting solution to a problem".[^o7]

## Logical graph {#Logical_graph}

A [logical graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_graph) is a special type of [graph-theoretic](wikipedia://en/12401) structure in any one of several systems of graphical [syntax](wikipedia://en/546039) that [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) developed for [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065).

In his papers on *[qualitative logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_logic)*, *[entitative graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entitative_graph)*, and *[existential graphs](wikipedia://en/2235037)*, Peirce developed several versions of a graphical [formalism](wikipedia://en/396102), or a graph-theoretic [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939), designed to be interpreted for logic.

In the century since Peirce initiated this line of development, a variety of formal systems have branched out from what is abstractly the same formal base of graph-theoretic structures.

### Conceptual graph {#Conceptual_graph}

A [conceptual graph](wikipedia://en/346755) (CG) is a notation for logic based on the [existential graphs](wikipedia://en/2235037) of [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) and the [semantic networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_network) of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence). In the first published paper on conceptual graphs, [John F. Sowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Sowa) used them to represent the [conceptual schemas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_schema) used in database systems. His first book[^o10] applied them to a wide range of topics in artificial intelligence, computer science, and cognitive science. A linear notation, called the *Conceptual Graph Interchange Format (CGIF)*, has been standardized in the ISO standard for [Common Logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Logic).

*Image: Cat-on-mat.svg.* Elsie the cat is sitting on a mat

The diagram on the right is an example of the *display form* for a conceptual graph. Each box is called a *concept node*, and each oval is called a *relation node*. In CGIF, this CG would be represented by the following statement:

\[Cat Elsie\] \[Sitting \*x\] \[Mat \*y\] (agent ?x Elsie) (location ?x ?y)

In CGIF, brackets enclose the information inside the concept nodes, and parentheses enclose the information inside the relation nodes. The letters x and y, which are called *coreference labels*, show how the concept and relation nodes are connected. In the *Common Logic Interchange Format (CLIF)*, those letters are mapped to variables, as in the following statement:

(exists ((x Sitting) (y Mat)) (and (Cat Elsie) (agent x Elsie) (location x y)))

As this example shows, the asterisks on the coreference labels \*x and \*y in CGIF map to existentially quantified variables in CLIF, and the question marks on ?x and ?y map to bound variables in CLIF. A universal quantifier, represented *@every\*z* in CGIF, would be represented *forall (z)* in CLIF.

### Entitative graph {#Entitative_graph}

An [entitative graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entitative_graph) is an element of the [graphical](wikipedia://en/12401) [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) for [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) that [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) developed under the name of *qualitative logic* beginning in the 1880s, taking the coverage of the [formalism](wikipedia://en/396102) only as far as the [propositional or sentential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) aspects of logic are concerned.[^o12]

The [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) is:

- The blank page;
- Single letters, phrases;
- Objects (subgraphs) enclosed by a [simple closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_closed_curve) called a *cut*. A cut can be empty.

The [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) are:

- The blank page denotes *False*;
- Letters, phrases, subgraphs, and entire graphs can be *True**or*****False*;***
- To surround objects with a cut is equivalent to Boolean [complementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28order_theory%29). Hence an empty cut denotes *Truth*;
- All objects within a given cut are tacitly joined by [disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction).

A "proof" manipulates a graph, using a short list of rules, until the graph is reduced to an empty cut or the blank page. A graph that can be so reduced is what is now called a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) (or the complement thereof). Graphs that cannot be simplified beyond a certain point are analogues of the [satisfiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiable) [formulas](wikipedia://en/164040) of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983).

### Existential graph {#Existential_graph}

An [existential graph](wikipedia://en/2235037) is a type of [diagrammatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram) or visual notation for logical expressions, proposed by [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117), who wrote his first paper on [graphical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_graph) in 1882 and continued to develop the method until his death in 1914. Peirce proposed three systems of existential graphs:

- *alpha* – [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to [sentential logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentential_logic) and the [two-element Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/2584449);
- *beta* – isomorphic to [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) with identity, with all formulas closed;
- *gamma* – (nearly) isomorphic to [normal modal logic](wikipedia://en/911820).

*Alpha* nests in *beta* and *gamma*. *Beta* does not nest in *gamma*, quantified modal logic being more than even Peirce could envisage.

*Image: PeirceAlphaGraphs.svg.* Alpha Graphs

In *alpha* the [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) is:

- The blank page;
- Single letters or phrases written anywhere on the page;
- Any graph may be enclosed by a [simple closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_closed_curve) called a *cut* or *sep*. A cut can be empty. Cuts can nest and concatenate at will, but must never intersect.

Any well-formed part of a graph is a *subgraph*.

The [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) are:

- The blank page denotes *Truth*;
- Letters, phrases, subgraphs, and entire graphs may be *True* or *False*;
- To enclose a subgraph with a cut is equivalent to logical [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) or Boolean [complementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28order_theory%29). Hence an empty cut denotes *False*;
- All subgraphs within a given cut are tacitly [conjoined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_%28logic%29).

Hence the *alpha* graphs are a minimalist notation for [sentential logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentential_logic), grounded in the expressive adequacy of *And* and *Not*. The *alpha* graphs constitute a radical simplification of the [two-element Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/2584449) and the [truth functors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connective_%28logic%29).

## Characteristica universalis {#Characteristica_universalis}

[Characteristica universalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristica_universalis), commonly interpreted as *universal characteristic*, or *universal character* in English, is a universal and formal language imagined by the German philosopher [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) able to express mathematical, scientific, and metaphysical concepts. Leibniz thus hoped to create a language usable within the framework of a universal logical calculation or *[calculus ratiocinator](wikipedia://en/876255)*.

*Image: Characteristica universalis diagram.jpg.* Leibniz's diagrammatic reasoning.

Since the *characteristica universalis* is diagrammatic and employs [pictograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pictograms) (below left), the diagrams in Leibniz's work warrant close study. On at least two occasions, Leibniz illustrated his philosophical reasoning with diagrams. One diagram, the frontispiece to his 1666 *[De Arte Combinatoria](wikipedia://en/2265373)* (On the Art of Combinations), represents the Aristotelian theory of how all material things are formed from combinations of the elements earth, water, air, and fire.

*Image: LeibnizCharacters.jpg.* Basic elements of Leibniz's pictograms.

These four elements make up the four corners of a diamond (see picture to right). Opposing pairs of these are joined by a bar labeled "contraries" (earth-air, fire-water). At the four corners of the superimposed square are the four qualities defining the elements. Each adjacent pair of these is joined by a bar labeled "possible combination"; the diagonals joining them are labeled "impossible combination." Starting from the top, fire is formed from the combination of dryness and heat; air from wetness and heat; water from coldness and wetness; earth from coldness and dryness.[^o15]

## The Venn-II reasoning system {#The_Venn-II_reasoning_system}

In the early 1990s [Sun-Joo Shin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-Joo_Shin) presented an extension of Existential Graphs called Venn-II.[^o17] Syntax and semantics are given formally, together with a set of *Rules of Transformation* which are shown to be sound and complete. Proofs proceed by applying the rules (which remove or add syntactic elements to or from diagrams) sequentially. Venn-II is equivalent in expressive power to a first-order monadic language.
