# Lubrication theory

*Image: Thin fluid film with particles flowing down an inclined plane.jpg.* A thin layer of liquid mixed with particles flowing down an inclined plane.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lubrication theory"}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), **lubrication theory** describes the flow of [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) ([liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) or [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas)) in a geometry in which one dimension is significantly smaller than the others. An example is the flow above [air hockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_hockey) tables, where the thickness of the air layer beneath the puck is much smaller than the dimensions of the puck itself.

:::

[Internal flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_flow) are those where the fluid is fully bounded. Internal flow lubrication theory has many industrial applications because of its role in the design of [fluid bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_bearing). Here a key goal of lubrication theory is to determine the [pressure distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_distribution) in the fluid volume, and hence the [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) on the bearing components. The working fluid in this case is often termed a [lubricant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubricant).

Free film lubrication theory is concerned with the case in which one of the surfaces containing the fluid is a [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface). In that case, the position of the free surface is itself unknown, and one goal of lubrication theory is then to determine this. Examples include the flow of a [viscous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscous) fluid over an inclined plane or over topography.[^o2][^o3] [Surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) may be significant, or even dominant.[^o4] Issues of [wetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wetting) and [dewetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewetting) then arise. For very thin films (thickness less than one [micrometre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micrometre)), additional intermolecular forces, such as [Van der Waals forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waals_force) or [disjoining forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoining_force), may become significant.

## Theoretical basis {#Theoretical_basis}

Mathematically, lubrication theory can be seen as exploiting the disparity between two length scales. The first is the characteristic film thickness, $H$, and the second is a characteristic substrate length scale $L$. The key requirement for lubrication theory is that the ratio $\epsilon = H/L$ is small, that is, $\epsilon \ll 1$. The [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395) (or [Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/2047298), when fluid inertia may be neglected) are expanded in this small parameter, and the [leading-order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading-order) equations are then

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} & = 0 \\[6pt] \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} & = \mu\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2} \end{aligned}$$

where $x$ and $z$ are coordinates in the direction of the substrate and perpendicular to it respectively. Here $p$ is the fluid pressure, and $u$ is the fluid velocity component parallel to the substrate; $\mu$ is the fluid [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754). The equations show, for example, that pressure variations across the gap are small, and that those along the gap are proportional to the fluid viscosity. A more general formulation of the lubrication approximation would include a third dimension, and the resulting differential equation is known as the [Reynolds equation](wikipedia://en/38401360).

Further details can be found in the literature[^o6] or in the textbooks given in the bibliography.

## Applications {#Applications}

An important application area is [lubrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lubrication) of machinery components such as [fluid bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_bearing) and [mechanical seals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_%28mechanical%29). [Coating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coating) is another major application area including the preparation of [thin films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_films), [printing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing), [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting) and [adhesives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adhesive).

Biological applications have included studies of [red blood cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_blood_cells) in narrow capillaries and of liquid flow in the lung and eye.
