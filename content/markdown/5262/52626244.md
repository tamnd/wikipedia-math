# E series of preferred numbers

Scientific notation#E notation|E notation

*Image: E12 Series Ten Percent Tolerance.svg.* This graph shows how almost any value between 1 and 10 is within ±10% of an E12 series value, and its difference from the ideal value in a geometric sequence.

*Image: E12 values graph.svg.* Two decades of E12 values, which would give resistor values of 1 Ω to 82 Ω

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="E series of preferred numbers"}

The **E series** is a system of [preferred numbers](wikipedia://en/1469133) (also called preferred values) derived for use in [electronic components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_component). It consists of the **E3**, **E6**, **E12**, **E24**, **E48**, **E96**, and **E192** series,[^o15] where the number after the "E" designates the quantity of [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/164055) value "steps" per [decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decade_%28log_scale%29). Although it is theoretically possible to produce components of any value, in practice the need for inventory simplification has led the industry to settle on the E series for [resistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resistor), [capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor), [inductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor), and [zener diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zener_diode). Other types of electrical components, such as [fuses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuse_%28electrical%29), are either specified by the [Renard series](wikipedia://en/2253990) or are defined in relevant product standards, such as [IEC&nbsp;60228](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEC&nbsp;60228) for wires.

:::

## History {#History}

During the [Golden Age of Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age_of_Radio) (1920s to 1950s), numerous companies manufactured [vacuum-tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube)–based [AM radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM_broadcasting#Receivers) [receivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antique_radio) for consumer use. In the early years, many components were not standardized between AM radio manufacturers. The capacitance values of capacitors (previously called condensers)[^o16][^o18] and resistance values of resistors[^o17][^o19][^o27][^o28] were not standardized as they are today.[^o20]

In 1924, the [Radio Manufacturers Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Manufacturers_Association) (RMA) was formed in [Chicago, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago,_Illinois) by 50 AM radio manufacturers to license and share patents. This group created some of the earliest standards for electronics components. In 1936, the RMA adopted a preferred-number system for the resistance values of fixed-composition resistors.[^o30] Over time, resistor manufacturers migrated to the 1936 resistance value standard.[^o27][^o28]

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) (1940s), American and British [military production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology_during_World_War_II) was a major influence for establishing [numerous standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Standard#History) across many industries, especially in electronics, where it was essential to produce high quantities of standardized electronic components to build military devices, such as [wireless communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_communications), [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar), [jammers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_jamming_and_deception), [LORAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LORAN) (radio navigation homing receivers for aircraft), ASDIC ([sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar) for submarine navigation and detection), test equipment, and [more](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_World_War_II_electronic_warfare_equipment).

During the 1950s, the [mid-20th century baby boom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mid-20th_century_baby_boom) and the invention of the [transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor) kicked off demand for [consumer electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_electronics) goods. In the late 1950s, portable AM [transistor radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor_radio) manufacturing migrated from United States towards Japan, thus it was critical for the electronic industry to have international standards.

After worked on by the [RMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Manufacturers_Association),[^o29] the [International Electrotechnical Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Electrotechnical_Commission) (IEC) began work on an international standard for preferred values in 1948.[^o22] The first version of this **IEC Publication 63** (IEC 63) was released in 1952.[^o21] Later, IEC 63 was revised, amended, and renamed into the current version known as **IEC 60063:2015**.[^o23]

IEC 60063 release history:

- IEC 63:1952 (aka IEC 60063:1952), first edition, published 1952-01-01.[^o21]
- IEC 63:1963 (aka IEC 60063:1963), second edition, published 1963-01-01.[^o22]
- IEC 63:1967/AMD1:1967 (aka IEC 60063:1967/AMD1:1967), first amendment of second edition, published 1967.[^o22]
- IEC 63:1977/AMD2:1977 (aka IEC 60063:1977/AMD2:1977), second amendment of second edition, published 1977.[^o22]
- IEC 60063:2015, third edition, published 2015-03-27.[^o23]

## Overview {#Overview}

The E series of preferred numbers was chosen such that when a component is manufactured it will end up in a range of roughly equally spaced values ([geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670)) on a [logarithmic scale](wikipedia://en/164055). Each E series subdivides each [decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decade_%28log_scale%29) magnitude into steps of 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, and 192 values, termed *E3*, *E6*, and so forth to *E192*, with maximum errors of 40%, 20%, 10%, 5%, 2%, 1%, and 0.5%, respectively.[^n1] The E192 series is also used for 0.25% and 0.1% tolerance resistors.

Historically, the E series is split into two major groupings:

- E3, E6, E12, E24 are [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of E24. Values in this group are rounded to 2 [significant figures](wikipedia://en/317062).
- E48, E96, E192 are [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of E192. Values in this group are rounded to 3 significant figures.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

### Formula {#Formula}

The formula for each value is determined by the [m-th root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-th_root), but the calculated values do not match the official values of all E series values.[^o32]

$$V_n = \mathrm{round} (\sqrt[m]{10^n})$$

where:

$V_n$ is [rounded](wikipedia://en/169945) to 2 (E3, E6, E12, E24) or 3 (E48, E96, E192) [significant figures](wikipedia://en/317062),

$m$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) of $\{3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192\} ,$

$n$ is an integer of $\{0, 1, ..., m-1\} .$

exceptions:

The official values for E48 and E96 series match their calculated values, but all other series (E3, E6, E12, E24, E192) have one or more official values that do not match their calculated values (see subsets sections below).

:::

### E24 subsets {#E24_subsets}

For E3, E6, E12, and E24, the values from the formula are rounded to 2 significant figures, but eight official values (shown in **bold** and green) are different from the calculated values (shown in red). During the early half of the 20th century, electronic components had different sets of component values than today. In the late 1940s, standards organizations started working towards codifying a standard set of official component values, and they decided that changing some of the former established historical values was not practical. The first standard was accepted in Paris in 1950, then published as IEC 63 in 1952.[^o21] The official values of the E3, E6, and E12 series are [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of the following official E24 values.

The E3 series is rarely used,[^n1] except for some components with high variations like [electrolytic capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolytic_capacitor), where the given [tolerance](wikipedia://en/522062) is often unbalanced between negative and positive such as ${}^{+50\%}_{-30\%}$ or ${}^{+80\%}_{-20\%}$. The calculated constant tangential tolerance for this series gives $\textstyle\frac{\sqrt[3]{10} - 1}{\sqrt[3]{10} + 1} = 36.597%$. While the standard only specifies a tolerance greater than 20%, other sources indicate 40% or 50%. Currently, most electrolytic capacitors are manufactured with values in the E6 or E12 series, so the E3 series is mostly obsolete except as the foundation for the other E series numbers.

### E192 subsets {#E192_subsets}

For E48, E96, and E192, the values from the formula are rounded to 3 significant figures, but one value (shown in bold) is different from the calculated values.

- To calculate the E48 series: $m$ is 48, then $n$ is incremented from 0 to 47 through the formula. All official values of E48 series match their calculated values.
- To calculate the E96 series: $m$ is 96, then $n$ is incremented from 0 to 95 through the formula. All official values of E96 series match their calculated values.
- To calculate the E192 series: $m$ is 192, then $n$ is incremented from 0 to 191 through the formula, with one exception for $n=185$ where **\9.20** is the official value instead of the calculated 9.19 value.

Since some values of the E24 series do not exist in the E48, E96, or E192 series, some resistor manufacturers produce E24-valued resistors in 1%, 0.5%, 0.25%, and 0.1% tolerance. This allows easier purchasing migration between various tolerances.
