# Dionysodorus of Amisene

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dionysodorus of Amisene"}

**Dionysodorus of Amisene** (1st century AD) was a Greek mathematician from [Pontus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontus_%28region%29), now on the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea) coast of [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey). He was briefly described by [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo), as one of the learned men of the city of Amisus.[^o3][^o2] Despite the similarity of his demonym to the inland city of [Amaseia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaseia), where Strabo was born, translators H. C. Hamilton and W. Falconer instead footnote Amisus as being the modern city [Samsun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samsun), near Amaseia on the coast. Amisene is a name for the region containing both cities.[^o2]

:::

Strabo distinguishes him from Dionysodorus of Melos, a geometer,[^o2] whom [Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) describes as having calculated the circumference of the Earth, less accurately than the older calculation of [Eratosthenes](wikipedia://en/46117). He should also be distinguished from [Dionysodorus of Caunus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysodorus), who studied [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673).[^o3]

Dionysodorus of Amisene was long thought to be the Dionysodorus who studied conic sections until, in the early 20th century, Wilhelm Crönert found from a papyrus newly excavated at [Herculaneum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herculaneum) a connection from the study of conics to Dionysodorus of Caunus and his son, also named Dionysodorus, dating them to centuries before the time that Strabo wrote about.[^o3][^o4]
