# Relative likelihood

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Relative likelihood"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), when [selecting](wikipedia://en/3664073) a [statistical model](wikipedia://en/27576) for given data, the **relative likelihood** compares the relative plausibilities of different candidate models or of different values of a [parameter](wikipedia://en/413291) of a single model.

:::

## Relative likelihood of parameter values {#Relative_likelihood_of_parameter_values}

Assume that we are given some data $x$ for which we have a statistical model with parameter $\theta$. Suppose that the [maximum likelihood estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood_estimate) for $\theta$ is $\hat{\theta}$. Relative plausibilities of other $\theta$ values may be found by comparing the likelihoods of those other values with the likelihood of $\hat{\theta}$. The *relative likelihood* of $\theta$ is defined to be[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7]

$$\frac{~\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x)~}{~\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta} \mid x)~},$$

where $\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x)$ denotes the [likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968). Thus, the relative likelihood is the [likelihood ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood_ratio_test) with fixed denominator $\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta} \mid x)$.

The function

$$\theta \mapsto \frac{~\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x)~}{~\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta} \mid x)~}$$

is the *relative likelihood function*.

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Likelihood interval"}

### Likelihood region {#Likelihood_region}

A *likelihood region* is the set of all values of $\theta$ whose relative likelihood is greater than or equal to a given threshold. In terms of percentages, a *$p$% likelihood region* for $\theta$ is defined to be.[^o3][^o5][^o10]

$$\left\{\theta : \frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x)}{\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta\,} \mid x)} \ge \frac p {100} \right\}.$$

If $\theta$ is a single real parameter, a $p$% likelihood region will usually comprise an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) of real values. If the region does comprise an interval, then it is called a *likelihood interval*.[^o3][^o5][^o11]

Likelihood intervals, and more generally likelihood regions, are used for [interval estimation](wikipedia://en/160381) within likelihood-based statistics ("likelihoodist" statistics): They are similar to [confidence intervals](wikipedia://en/280911) in frequentist statistics and [credible intervals](wikipedia://en/3408427) in Bayesian statistics. Likelihood intervals are interpreted directly in terms of relative likelihood, not in terms of [coverage probability](wikipedia://en/22856967) (frequentism) or [posterior probability](wikipedia://en/357672) (Bayesianism).

Given a model, likelihood intervals can be compared to confidence intervals. If $\theta$ is a single real parameter, then under certain conditions, a 14.65% likelihood interval (about 1:7 likelihood) for $\theta$ will be the same as a 95% confidence interval (19/20 coverage probability).[^o3][^o10] In a slightly different formulation suited to the use of log-likelihoods (see [Wilks' theorem](wikipedia://en/45035#Asymptotic_distribution:_Wilks'_theorem)), the [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975) is twice the difference in log-likelihoods and the probability distribution of the test statistic is approximately a [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) with degrees-of-freedom (df) equal to the difference in df-s between the two models (therefore, the $e$<sup>−2</sup> likelihood interval is the same as the 0.954 confidence interval; assuming difference in df-s to be 1).[^o10][^o11]

:::

## Relative likelihood of models {#Relative_likelihood_of_models}

The definition of relative likelihood can be generalized to compare different [statistical models](wikipedia://en/27576). This generalization is based on [AIC](wikipedia://en/690512) (Akaike information criterion), or sometimes [AICc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akaike_Information_Criterion#AICc) (Akaike Information Criterion with correction).

Suppose that for some given data we have two statistical models, $M_{1}$ and $M_{2}$. Also suppose that $\operatorname{AIC}(M_{1}) \le \operatorname{AIC}(M_{2})$. Then the *relative likelihood* of $M_{2}$ with respect to $M_{1}$ is defined as follows.[^o13]

$$\exp \left( \frac{\operatorname{AIC}(M_1)-\operatorname{AIC}(M_2)}{2} \right)$$

To see that this is a generalization of the earlier definition, suppose that we have some model $M$ with a (possibly multivariate) parameter $\theta$. Then for any $\theta$, set $M_{2} = M(\theta)$, and also set $M_{1} = M($$\hat\theta$$)$. The general definition now gives the same result as the earlier definition.
