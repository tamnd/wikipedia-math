# Conformal geometric algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conformal geometric algebra"}

**Conformal geometric algebra** (**CGA**) is the [geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/12939) constructed over the resultant space of a map from points in an $n$-dimensional base space $\mathbf{R}^{p,q}$ to null vectors in $\mathbf{R}^{p+1,q+1}$. This allows operations on the base space, including reflections, rotations and translations to be represented using [versors](wikipedia://en/12939#Versor) of the geometric algebra; and it is found that points, lines, planes, circles and spheres gain particularly natural and computationally amenable representations.

:::

The effect of the mapping is that generalized (i.e. including zero curvature) [$k$-spheres](wikipedia://en/39782) in the base space map onto $(k + 2)$-[blades](wikipedia://en/21241368), and so that the effect of a translation (or *any* [conformal mapping](wikipedia://en/50627)) of the base space corresponds to a rotation in the higher-dimensional space. In the algebra of this space, based on the [geometric product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_product) of vectors, such transformations correspond to the algebra's characteristic sandwich operations, similar to the use of [quaternions for spatial rotation in 3D](wikipedia://en/186057), which combine very efficiently. A consequence of rotors representing transformations is that the representations of spheres, planes, circles and other geometrical objects, and equations connecting them, all transform covariantly. A geometric object (a $k$-sphere) can be synthesized as the wedge product of $k + 2$ linearly independent vectors representing points on the object; conversely, the object can be decomposed as the repeated [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) of vectors representing $k + 2$ distinct points in its surface. Some intersection operations also acquire a tidy algebraic form: for example, for the Euclidean base space $\mathbf{R}^{3}$, applying the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) to the dual of the tetravectors representing two spheres produces the dual of the trivector representation of their circle of intersection.

As this algebraic structure lends itself directly to effective computation, it facilitates exploration of the classical methods of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) and [inversive geometry](wikipedia://en/295844) in a concrete, easy-to-manipulate setting. It has also been used as an efficient structure to represent and facilitate calculations in [screw theory](wikipedia://en/1318037). CGA has particularly been applied in connection with the projective mapping of the everyday Euclidean space $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ into a five-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $\mathbf{R}^{4,1}$, which has been investigated for applications in robotics and computer vision. It can be applied generally to any [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546) – for example, [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) $\mathbf{R}^{3,1}$ to the space $\mathbf{R}^{4,2}$.

## Construction of CGA {#Construction_of_CGA}

### Notation and terminology {#Notation_and_terminology}

In this article, the focus is on the algebra $\mathcal G(4,1)$ as it is this particular algebra that has been the subject of most attention over time; other cases are briefly covered in a separate section. The space containing the objects being modelled is referred to here as the *base space*, and the [algebraic space](wikipedia://en/2394027) used to model these objects as the *representation* or *conformal* space. A *homogeneous subspace* refers to a linear subspace of the algebraic space.

The terms for objects: *point*, *line*, *circle*, *sphere*, *quasi-sphere* etc. are used to mean either the geometric object in the base space, or the homogeneous subspace of the representation space that represents that object, with the latter generally being intended unless indicated otherwise. Algebraically, any nonzero null element of the homogeneous subspace will be used, with one element being referred to as *normalized* by some criterion.

Boldface lowercase Latin letters are used to represent position vectors from the origin to a point in the base space. Italic symbols are used for other elements of the representation space.

### Base and representation spaces {#Base_and_representation_spaces}

The base space $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ is represented by extending a basis for the displacements from a chosen origin and adding two basis vectors $e_{-}$ and $e_{+}$ orthogonal to the base space and to each other, with $e_{-}^{2} = -1$ and $e_{+}^{2} = +1$, creating the representation space $\mathcal G(4,1)$.

It is convenient to use two null vectors $n_{o}$ and $n_{\infty}$ as basis vectors in place of $e_{+}$ and $e_{-}$, where $n_{o} = (e_{-} - e_{+})/2$, and $n_{\infty} = e_{-} + e_{+}$. It can be verified, where $\mathbf{x}$ is in the base space, that:

$$\begin{array}{lllll} {n_\text{o}}^2 & = 0 \qquad n_\text{o} \cdot n_\infty & = -1 \qquad & n_\text{o} \cdot \mathbf{x} & = 0 \\ {n_\infty}^2 & = 0 \qquad n_\text{o} \wedge n_\infty & = e_{-}e_{+} \qquad & n_\infty \cdot \mathbf{x} & = 0 \end{array}$$

These properties lead to the following formulas for the basis vector coefficients of a general vector $r$ in the representation space for a basis with elements $e_{i}$ orthogonal to every other basis element:

The coefficient of $n_{o}$ for $r$ is $-n_{\infty} \cdot r$

The coefficient of $n_{\infty}$ for $r$ is $-n_{o} \cdot r$

The coefficient of $e_{i}$ for $r$ is $e_{i}^{-1} \cdot r$.

### Mapping between the base space and the representation space {#Mapping_between_the_base_space_and_the_representation_space}

The mapping from a vector in the base space (being from the origin to a point in the affine space represented) is given by the formula:

$$g : \mathbf{x} \mapsto n_\text{o} + \mathbf{x} + \tfrac{1}{2} \mathbf{x}^2 n_\infty$$

Points and other objects that differ only by a nonzero scalar factor all map to the same object in the base space. When normalisation is desired, as for generating a simple reverse map of a point from the representation space to the base space or determining distances, the condition $g(\mathbf{x}) \cdot n_{\infty} = -1$ may be used.

*Image: Conformal Embedding.svg.* Change of normalisation: mapping the null cone from the hyperplane $r \cdot (n_{\infty} - n_{o}) = 1$ to the hyperplane $r \cdot n_{\infty} = -1$.

The forward mapping is equivalent to:

- first conformally projecting $\mathbf{x}$ from $e_{123}$ onto a unit 3-sphere in the space $e_{+} \land e_{123}$ (in 5-D this is in the subspace $r \cdot (-n_{o} - \tfrac{1}{2}n_{\infty}) = 0$);
- then lift this into a projective space, by adjoining $e_{-} = 1$, and identifying all points on the same ray from the origin (in 5-D this is in the subspace $r \cdot (-n_{o} - \tfrac{1}{2}n_{\infty}) = 1$);
- then change the normalisation, so the plane for the homogeneous projection is given by the $n_{o}$ co-ordinate having a value $1$, i.e. $r \cdot n_{\infty} = -1$.

### Inverse mapping {#Inverse_mapping}

An inverse mapping for $X$ on the null cone is given (Perwass eqn 4.37) by

$$X \mapsto \mathcal{P}^\perp_{n_\infty \wedge n_\text{o}}\left( \frac{X}{- X \cdot n_\infty}\right)$$

This first gives a [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) from the light-cone onto the plane $r \cdot n_{\infty} = -1$, and then throws away the $n_{o}$ and $n_{\infty}$ parts, so that the overall result is to map all of the equivalent points $\alpha X = \alpha(n_{o} + \mathbf{x} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{2}n_{\infty})$ to $\mathbf{x}$.

### Origin and point at infinity {#Origin_and_point_at_infinity}

The point $\mathbf{x} = 0$ in $\mathbf{R}^{p,q}$ maps to $n_{o}$ in $\mathbf{R}^{p+1,q+1}$, so $n_{o}$ is identified as the (representation) vector of the point at the origin.

A vector in $\mathbf{R}^{p+1,q+1}$ with a nonzero $n_{\infty}$ coefficient, but a zero $n_{o}$ coefficient, must (considering the inverse map) be the image of an *infinite* vector in $\mathbf{R}^{p,q}$. The direction $n_{\infty}$ therefore represents the (conformal) [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139). This motivates the subscripts ${}_{o}$ and ${}_{\infty}$ for identifying the null basis vectors.

The choice of the origin is arbitrary: any other point may be chosen, as the representation is of an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834). The origin merely represents a reference point, and is algebraically equivalent to any other point. As with any translation, changing the origin corresponds to a rotation in the representation space.

### As the solution of a pair of equations {#As_the_solution_of_a_pair_of_equations}

Given any nonzero [blade](wikipedia://en/21241368) $A$ of the representing space, the set of vectors that are solutions to a pair of homogeneous equations of the form[^o12]

$$X^2 = 0$$

$$X \wedge A = 0$$

is the union of homogeneous 1-d subspaces of null vectors, and is thus a representation of a set of points in the base space. This leads to the choice of a blade $A$ as being a useful way to represent a particular class of geometric objects. Specific cases for the blade $A$ (independent of the number of dimensions of the space) when the base space is Euclidean space are:

- a scalar: the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566)
- a vector: a single point
- a bivector: a pair of points
- a trivector: a generalized circle
- a 4-vector: a generalized sphere
- etc.

These each may split into three cases according to whether $A^{2}$ is positive, zero or negative, corresponding (in reversed order in some cases) to the object as listed, a degenerate case of a single point, or no points (where the nonzero solutions of $X \land A$ exclude null vectors).

The listed geometric objects (generalized [$n$-spheres](wikipedia://en/39782)) become [quasi-spheres](wikipedia://en/51406535) in the more general case of the base space being pseudo-Euclidean.[^o13]

[Flat](wikipedia://en/13005617) objects may be identified by the point at infinity being included in the solutions. Thus, if $n_{\infty} \land A = 0$, the object will be a line, plane, etc., for the blade $A$ respectively being of grade 3, 4, etc.

### As derived from points of the object {#As_derived_from_points_of_the_object}

A blade $A$ representing of one of this class of object may be found as the outer product of linearly independent vectors representing points on the object. In the base space, this [linear independence](wikipedia://en/101863) manifests as each point lying outside the object defined by the other points. So, for example, a fourth point lying on the generalized circle defined by three distinct points cannot be used as a fourth point to define a sphere.

### odds {#odds}

Points in **e**<sub>123</sub> map onto the null cone—the null *parabola* if we set $r \cdot n_\infty = -1$.

We can consider the locus of points in **e**<sub>123</sub> s.t. in conformal space $g(\mathbf{x}) \cdot A = 0$, for various types of geometrical object A.

We start by observing that $g(\mathbf{a}) \cdot g(\mathbf{b}) = -\tfrac12 \| \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \|^2$

compare:

- x. a = 0 => x perp a; x.(a∧b) = 0 => x perp a *and* x perp b
- x∧a = 0 => x parallel to a; x∧(a∧b) = 0 => x parallel to a *or* to b (or to some [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632))

the inner product and outer product representations are related by dualisation

x∧A = 0 \<=> x . A\* = 0 (*check*—works if x is 1-dim, A is n-1 dim)

(N.B. that because it's a homogeneous projective space, vectors of any length on a ray through the origin are equivalent, so g(x).A =0 is equivalent to g(x).g(a) = 0).

- A *sphere*: the locus of **x** is a *sphere* if A = S, a vector off the null cone.

If

$$\mathbf{S} = g(\mathbf{a}) - \tfrac12 \rho^2 \mathbf{e}_\infty$$

then **S**.**X** = 0 => $-\tfrac12 (\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{x})^2 + \tfrac12 \rho^2 = 0$

these are the points corresponding to a sphere

for a vector S off the null-cone, which directions are hyperbolically orthogonal? (cf [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) pix)

in 2+1 D, if S is (1,a,b), (using co-ords e-, {e+, e<sub>i</sub>}), the points hyperbolically orthogonal to S are those euclideanly orthogonal to (−1,a,b)—i.e., a plane; or in *n* dimensions, a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) through the origin. This would cut another plane not through the origin in a line (a [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) in an *n*-2 surface), and then the cone in two points (resp. some sort of *n*-3 conic surface). So it's going to probably look like some kind of conic. This is the surface that is the image of a sphere under *g*.

  - $\mathbf{P} = g(\mathbf{a}) - g(\mathbf{b})$ corresponds to a plane half way between **a** and **b**, with normal **a** - **b**

## Transformations {#Transformations}

- *reflections*

It can be verified that forming **P** g(**x**) **P** gives a new direction on the null-cone, g(**x'**), where **x'** corresponds to a reflection in the plane of points **p** in **R**<sup>3</sup> that satisfy g(**p**) . **P** = 0.

g(**x**) . A = 0 => **P** g(**x**) . A **P** = 0 => **P** g(**x**) **P** . **P** A **P** (and similarly for the wedge product), so the effect of applying **P** sandwich-fashion to any the quantities A in the section above is similarly to reflect the corresponding locus of points **x**, so the corresponding circles, spheres, lines and planes corresponding to particular types of A are reflected in exactly the same way that applying **P** to g(**x**) reflects a point **x**.

This reflection operation can be used to build up general translations and rotations:

- *translations*

Reflection in two parallel planes gives a translation,

$$g(\mathbf{x}^\prime) = \mathbf{P}_\beta \mathbf{P}_\alpha \; g(\mathbf{x}) \; \mathbf{P}_\alpha \mathbf{P}_\beta$$

If $\mathbf{P}_\alpha = \hat{\mathbf{a}} +\alpha \mathbf{e}_\infty$ and $\mathbf{P}_\beta = \hat{\mathbf{a}} +\beta \mathbf{e}_\infty$ then $\mathbf{x}^\prime = \mathbf{x} + 2 (\beta-\alpha) \hat{\mathbf{a}}$

- *rotations*

$g(\mathbf{x}^\prime) = \hat{\mathbf{b}}\hat{\mathbf{a}} \; g(\mathbf{x}) \; \hat{\mathbf{a}}\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ corresponds to an **x'** that is rotated about the origin by an angle 2 θ where θ is the angle between **a** and **b** -- the same effect that this rotor would have if applied directly to **x**.

- *general rotations*

rotations about a general point can be achieved by first translating the point to the origin, then rotating around the origin, then translating the point back to its original position, i.e. a sandwiching by the operator $\mathbf{TR{\tilde{T}}}$ so

$$g (\mathcal{G}x) = \mathbf{TR{\tilde{T}}} \; g(\mathbf{x}) \; \mathbf{T\tilde{R}\tilde{T}}$$

- *screws*

the effect a *[screw](wikipedia://en/1318037)*, or *motor*, (a rotation about a general point, followed by a translation parallel to the axis of rotation) can be achieved by sandwiching g(**x**) by the operator $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{T_2T_1R{\tilde{T_1}}}$.

**M** can also be parametrised $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{T^\prime R^\prime}$ ([Chasles' theorem](wikipedia://en/42392462))

- *inversions*

an [inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_transformation) is a reflection in a sphere – various operations that can be achieved using such inversions are discussed at [inversive geometry](wikipedia://en/295844). In particular, the combination of inversion together with the [Euclidean transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_transformation) translation and rotation is sufficient to express *any* [conformal mapping](wikipedia://en/50627) – i.e. any mapping that universally preserves angles. ([Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/12458499)).

- *dilations*

two inversions with the same centre produce a [dilation](wikipedia://en/1591770).
