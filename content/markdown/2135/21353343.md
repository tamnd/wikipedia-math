# Lindeberg's condition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lindeberg's condition"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), **Lindeberg's condition** is a [sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) (and under certain conditions also a necessary condition) for the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406) (CLT) to hold for a sequence of independent [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables).[^o2][^o3][^o4] Unlike the classical CLT, which requires that the random variables in question have finite [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) and be both [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically-distributed_random_variables), [Lindeberg's CLT](wikipedia://en/39406#Lindeberg_CLT) only requires that they have finite variance, satisfy Lindeberg's condition, and be [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence). It is named after the Finnish mathematician [Jarl Waldemar Lindeberg](wikipedia://en/1218207).[^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325), and $X_k : \Omega \to \mathbb{R},\,\, k \in \mathbb{N}$, be *independent* random variables defined on that space. Assume the expected values $\mathbb{E}\,[X_k] = \mu_k$ and variances $\mathrm{Var}\,[X_k] = \sigma_k^2$ exist and are finite. Also let $s_n^2 := \sum_{k=1}^n \sigma_k^2 .$

If this sequence of independent random variables $X_k$ satisfies **Lindeberg's condition**:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{s_n^2}\sum_{k = 1}^n \mathbb{E} \left[(X_k - \mu_k)^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{ | X_k - \mu_k | > \varepsilon s_n \}} \right] = 0$$

for all $\varepsilon > 0$, where **1**<sub>{…}</sub> is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790), then the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406) holds, i.e. the random variables

$$Z_n := \frac{\sum_{k = 1}^n (X_k - \mu_k)}{s_n}$$

[converge in distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_in_distribution) to a [standard normal random variable](wikipedia://en/21462) as $n \to \infty.$

Lindeberg's condition is sufficient, but not in general necessary (i.e. the inverse implication does not hold in general). However, if the sequence of independent random variables in question satisfies

$$\max_{k=1,\ldots,n} \frac{\sigma_k^2}{s_n^2} \to 0, \quad \text{ as } n \to \infty,$$

then Lindeberg's condition is both sufficient and necessary, i.e. it holds [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the result of central limit theorem holds.

:::

## Remarks {#Remarks}

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Feller's theorem"}

### Feller's theorem {#Feller's_theorem}

Feller's theorem can be used as an alternative method to prove that Lindeberg's condition holds.[^o11] Letting $S_n := \sum_{k=1}^n X_k$ and for simplicity $\mathbb{E}\,[X_k] = 0$, the theorem states

if $\forall \varepsilon > 0$, $\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \max_{1 \leq k \leq n} P(|X_k| > \varepsilon s_n) = 0$ and $\frac{S_n}{s_n}$ converges weakly to a standard [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) as $n \rightarrow \infty$ then $X_k$ satisfies the Lindeberg's condition.

This theorem can be used to disprove the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406) holds for $X_k$ by using [proof by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction). This procedure involves proving that Lindeberg's condition fails for $X_k$.

:::

## Interpretation {#Interpretation}

Because the Lindeberg condition implies $\max_{k=1,\ldots,n}\frac{\sigma^2_k}{s_n^2} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, it guarantees that the contribution of any individual random variable $X_k$ ($1\leq k\leq n$) to the variance $s_n^2$ is arbitrarily small, for sufficiently large values of $n$.

::: {#o14 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Consider the following informative example which satisfies the Lindeberg condition. Let $\xi_i$ be a sequence of zero mean, variance 1 iid random variables and $a_i$ a non-random sequence satisfying:

$$\max_i^n \frac{|a_i|}{\|a\|_2} \rightarrow 0$$

Now, define the normalized elements of the [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632):

$$X_{i} = \frac{a_i \xi_i}{\|a\|_2}$$

which satisfies the Lindeberg condition:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb E \left [ \left | X_i\right |^2 1(|X_i| > \varepsilon)\right ] \leq \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb E \left [ \left | X_i\right |^2 1 \left(|\xi_i| > \varepsilon \frac{\|a\|_2}{\max_i^n |a_i|} \right)\right ] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb E \left [ \left | \xi_i\right |^2 1 \left(|\xi_i| > \varepsilon \frac{\|a\|_2}{\max_i^n |a_i|} \right)\right ]$$

but $\xi_i^2$ is finite so by DCT and the condition on the $a_i$ we have that this goes to 0 for every $\varepsilon$.

:::
