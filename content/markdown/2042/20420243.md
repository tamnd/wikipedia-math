# Scott Aaronson

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Scott Aaronson"}

**Scott Joel Aaronson** (born May 21, 1981) is an American [theoretical computer scientist](wikipedia://en/323392) and Schlumberger Centennial Chair of Computer Science at the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin). His primary areas of research are [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543) and [quantum computing](wikipedia://en/25220).

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Aaronson grew up in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), though he spent a year in [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia) when his father was posted to [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong).[^o6] He enrolled in a school there that permitted him to skip ahead several years in math, but upon returning to the US, he had difficulties in school, getting bad grades and having run-ins with teachers. He enrolled in The Clarkson School, a [gifted education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifted_education) program run by [Clarkson University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarkson_University), which enabled Aaronson to apply for colleges while only in his freshman year of high school.[^o6] He was accepted into [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University), where he obtained his BSc in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) in 2000,[^o7] and where he resided at the [Telluride House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telluride_House).[^o8] He then attended the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), for his PhD, which he got in 2004 under the supervision of [Umesh Vazirani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umesh_Vazirani).

As a child, Aaronson was particularly interested in mathematics. In part due to this, he felt drawn to theoretical computing, particularly [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543). At Cornell, he became interested in quantum computing and devoted himself to computational complexity and quantum computing.[^o6]

## Career {#Career}

After [postdoctorates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctorate) at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) and the [University of Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Waterloo), he took a faculty position at MIT in 2007.[^o7] His primary area of research is quantum computing and computational complexity theory more generally.

In the summer of 2016 he moved from MIT to the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin) as David J. Bruton Jr. Centennial Professor of Computer Sciences #2 and as the founding director of UT Austin's new Quantum Information Center.[^o11] In summer 2022 he announced he would be working for a year at [OpenAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI) on theoretical foundations of [AI safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_safety).[^o12][^o13] He worked at the company for two years.[^o14]

## Popular work {#Popular_work}

He is a founder of the *Complexity Zoo* [wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiki), which catalogs all classes of [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/7543).[^o17][^o18] He is the author of the blog "Shtetl-Optimized".[^o19]

He also wrote the essay "Who Can Name The Bigger Number?".[^o21] The latter work, widely distributed in academic [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), uses the concept of [Busy Beaver Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Busy_Beaver_Number) as described by [Tibor Radó](wikipedia://en/1490393) to illustrate the limits of [computability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computability) in a pedagogic environment.

He has also taught a graduate-level survey course, "Quantum Computing Since Democritus",[^o22] for which notes are available online, and have been published as a book by [Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press).[^o23] It weaves together disparate topics into a cohesive whole, including [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), complexity, free will, [time travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_travel), the [anthropic principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic_principle) and more. Many of these interdisciplinary applications of computational complexity were later fleshed out in his article, "Why Philosophers Should Care About Computational Complexity".[^o24] Since then, Aaronson published a book entitled *[Quantum Computing Since Democritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Computing_Since_Democritus)* based on the course.

An article of Aaronson's, "The Limits of Quantum Computers", was published in *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*,[^o25] and he was a [guest speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guest_speaker) at the 2007 [Foundational Questions in Science Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_Questions_in_Science_Institute) conference.[^o26] Aaronson is frequently cited in the non-academic press, such as *[Science News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_News),*[^o27] *[The Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age),*[^o28] [ZDNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZDNet),[^o29] *[Slashdot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slashdot),*[^o30] *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist),*[^o31] *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times),*[^o32] and *[Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbes)* magazine.[^o33]

## Awards {#Awards}

- Aaronson is one of two winners of the 2012 [Alan T. Waterman Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_T._Waterman_Award).[^o35]
- Best Student Paper Awards at the [Computational Complexity Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_Complexity_Conference) for the papers "Limitations of Quantum Advice and One-Way Communication" (2004) [^o36] and "Quantum Certificate Complexity" (2003).[^o37][^o38]
- Danny Lewin Best Student Paper Award at the [Symposium on Theory of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symposium_on_Theory_of_Computing) for the paper "Lower Bounds for Local Search by Quantum Arguments" (2004).[^o39]
- 2009 [Presidential Early Career Award for Scientists and Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidential_Early_Career_Award_for_Scientists_and_Engineers)[^o40]
- 2009 [Sloan Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Research_Fellowship)[^o41]
- 2017 [Simons Investigator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simons_Investigator)[^o42]
- He was elected as an [ACM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACM_Fellow) in 2019 "for contributions to quantum computing and computational complexity".[^o43]
- He was awarded the 2020 ACM Prize in Computing "for groundbreaking contributions to quantum computing".[^o44]
- He was elected to the [US National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) in 2026.[^o45]
- Inaugural 2026 Luca Trevisan Award for Expository Work from [ACM SIGACT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACM_SIGACT)[^o46]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Aaronson is married to computer scientist [Dana Moshkovitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Moshkovitz).[^o11] Aaronson is [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews),[^o48][^o49][^o50] and has described himself as "radicalized in my Jewish and Zionist identities" after the International Olympiad on Informatics banned Israel from its competitions.[^o51]
