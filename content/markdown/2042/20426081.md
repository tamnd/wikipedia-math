# Clairaut's relation (differential geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Clairaut's relation"}

In classical [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), **Clairaut's relation**, named after [Alexis Claude de Clairaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Claude_de_Clairaut), is a formula that characterizes the great circle paths on the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090). The formula states that if $\gamma$ is a parametrization of a great circle then

:::

$$\rho(\gamma(t)) \sin \psi(\gamma(t)) = \text{constant},\,$$

where $\rho(P)$ is the distance from a point $P$ on the [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) to the $z$-axis, and $\psi(P)$ is the angle between the great circle and the [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) through the point $P$.

The relation remains valid for a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) on an arbitrary [surface of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549).

A statement of the general version of Clairaut's relation is:[^o2] Let $\gamma$ be a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) on a [surface of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549) $S$, let $\rho$ be the distance of a point of $S$ from the [axis of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_rotation), and let $\psi$ be the angle between $\gamma$ and the [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) of $S$. Then $\rho \sin \psi$ is constant along $\gamma$. Conversely, if $\rho \sin \psi$ is constant along some curve $\gamma$ in the surface, and if no part of $\gamma$ is part of some parallel of $S$, then $\gamma$ is a geodesic. Pressley (p. 185) explains this theorem as an expression of [conservation of angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum) about the [axis of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_revolution) when a particle moves along a geodesic under no forces other than those that keep it on the surface.

Now imagine a particle constrained to move on a surface of revolution, without external torque around the axis. By conservation of angular momentum:

$$r \, v_\theta = L,$$

where

- $r$ = distance to the axis,
- $v_\theta$ = component of velocity orthogonal to the meridian,
- $L$ = conserved angular momentum around the axis.

But geometrically,

$$v_\theta = |v| \sin \psi,$$

If we normalize so the speed $|v|=1$ (unit speed geodesics), we get:

$$r \sin \psi = \frac{L}{|v|} = \text{constant}.$$
