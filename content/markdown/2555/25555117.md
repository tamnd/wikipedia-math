# Eight-dimensional space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eight-dimensional space"}

**Eight-dimensional (8D) space** is a sequence of *n* [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) (when *n* = 8) that can be understood as a [location](wikipedia://en/593693) in *n*-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) space. Often such spaces are studied as [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), without any notion of distance. Eight-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is eight-dimensional space equipped with the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric).

:::

More generally the term may refer to an eight-dimensional vector space over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603), such as an eight-dimensional [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) vector space, which has 16 real dimensions. It may also refer to an eight-dimensional [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) such as an [8-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-sphere), or a variety of other geometric constructions.

## Geometry {#Geometry}

### 8-polytope {#8-polytope}

A [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) in eight dimensions is called an 8-polytope. The most studied are the [regular polytopes](wikipedia://en/518693), of which there are only [three in eight dimensions](wikipedia://en/505253#Convex_4): the [8-simplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-simplex), [8-cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-cube), and [8-orthoplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-orthoplex). A broader family are the [uniform 8-polytopes](wikipedia://en/6821715), constructed from fundamental symmetry domains of reflection, each domain defined by a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004). Each uniform polytope is defined by a ringed [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram). The [8-demicube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-demicube) is a unique polytope from the D<sub>8</sub> family, and [4<sub>21</sub>](wikipedia://en/19236362), [2<sub>41</sub>](wikipedia://en/19091447), and [1<sub>42</sub>](wikipedia://en/19091290) polytopes from the E<sub>8</sub> family.

### 7-sphere {#7-sphere}

The [7-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) or hypersphere in eight dimensions is the seven-dimensional surface equidistant from a point, e.g. the origin. It has symbol $S^{7}$, with formal definition for the 7-sphere with radius *r* of

$$S^7 = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{R}^8 : \|x\| = r\right\}.$$

The volume of the space bounded by this 7-sphere is

$$V_8\,=\frac{\pi^4}{24}\,R^8$$

which is 4.05871 × *r*<sup>8</sup>, or 0.01585 of the [8-cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-cube) that contains the 7-sphere.

### Kissing number problem {#Kissing_number_problem}

The [kissing number problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kissing_number_problem) has been solved in eight dimensions, thanks to the existence of the [4<sub>21</sub>](wikipedia://en/19236362) polytope and its associated [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246). The kissing number in eight dimensions is [240](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/240_%28number%29).

## Octonions {#Octonions}

The octonions are a [normed division algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_division_algebra) over the real numbers, the largest such algebra. Mathematically they can be specified by 8-tuplets of real numbers, so form an 8-dimensional vector space over the reals, with addition of vectors being the addition in the algebra. A normed algebra is one with a product that satisfies

$$\|xy\| \leq \|x\| \|y\|$$

for all *x* and *y* in the algebra. A normed [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) additionally must be finite-dimensional, and have the property that every non-zero vector has a unique multiplicative inverse. [Hurwitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/25167317) prohibits such a structure from existing in dimensions other than 1, 2, 4, or 8.

## Biquaternions {#Biquaternions}

The complexified [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) $\mathbb{C} \otimes \mathbb{H}$, or "[biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070)," are an eight-dimensional algebra dating to [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817)'s work in the 1850s. This algebra is equivalent (that is, [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic)) to the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) $C \ell _2 (\mathbb{C})$ and the [Pauli algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_algebra). It has also been proposed as a practical or pedagogical tool for doing calculations in [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), and in that context goes by the name [Algebra of physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_of_physical_space) (not to be confused with the [Spacetime algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime_algebra), which is 16-dimensional.)
