# Pursuit curve

*Image: Radiodrome-simple-y-bw.png.* A simple pursuit curve in which $P$ is the pursuer and $A$ is the pursued

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pursuit curve"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **curve of pursuit** is a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) constructed by analogy to having a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) or points representing pursuers and pursued; the curve of pursuit is the curve traced by the pursuers.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

With the paths of the pursuer and pursued [parameterized](wikipedia://en/453198) in time, the pursued is always on the pursuer's [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482). That is, given $F(t)$, the pursuer (follower), and $L(t)$, the pursued (leader), for every $t$ with $F(t) \ne 0$ there is an $x$ such that

$$L(t) = F(t) + xF'\!(t).$$

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: BourguerCourbepoursuite1732.jpg.* [Pierre Bouguer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bouguer)'s 1732 article studying pursuit curves

The pursuit curve was first studied by [Pierre Bouguer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bouguer) in 1732. In an article on [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation), Bouguer defined a curve of pursuit to explore the way in which one ship might maneuver while pursuing another.[^o5]

[Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) has occasionally been credited with first exploring curves of pursuit. However [Paul J. Nahin](wikipedia://en/48506394), having traced such accounts as far back as the late 19th century, indicates that these anecdotes are unfounded.[^o6]

## Single pursuer {#Single_pursuer}

*Image: Radiodrome-params-colour.png.* Curves of pursuit with different parameters

The path followed by a single pursuer, following a pursued that moves at constant speed on a [line](wikipedia://en/946975), is a [radiodrome](wikipedia://en/30272853).

It is a solution of the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) $1\,+\,\,y'\,^{2} = k^{2} (a\,-\,x)^{2} y''\,^{2}$, where $k$ is the ratio in speed of the pursued over the pursuer. The special case with $k = 1/2$ is the [Tschirnhausen cubic](wikipedia://en/10475041).

## Multiple pursuers {#Multiple_pursuers}

*Image: Four point pursuit curve.gif.* Curve of pursuit of vertices of a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) (the [mice problem](wikipedia://en/8532654) for *n*=4).

Typical drawings of curves of pursuit have each point acting as both pursuer and pursued, inside a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621), and having each pursuer pursue the adjacent point on the polygon. An example of this is the [mice problem](wikipedia://en/8532654), in which pursuers on the vertices of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) chase each other along [logarithmic spirals](wikipedia://en/42373).
