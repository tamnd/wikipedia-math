# Closed-form expression

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Closed-form expression"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **closed form** [expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_expression) or [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_formula) is one that is formed with [constants](wikipedia://en/24758132), [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), and a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) considered as *basic* and connected by [arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operation) (, and [integer powers](wikipedia://en/99491#Integer)) and [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947). Commonly, the basic functions that are allowed in closed forms are [*n*th root](wikipedia://en/235029), [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678), [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860), and [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367). However, the set of basic functions depends on the context. For example, if one adds [polynomial roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_root) to the basic functions, the functions that have a closed form are called [elementary functions](wikipedia://en/10412).

:::

The *closed-form problem* arises when new ways are introduced for specifying [mathematical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_objects), such as [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602), [series](wikipedia://en/15287), and [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532): given an object specified with such tools, a natural problem is to find, if possible, a *closed-form expression* of this object; that is, an expression of this object in terms of previous ways of specifying it.

## Example: roots of polynomials {#Example:_roots_of_polynomials}

The [quadratic formula](wikipedia://en/59217)

$$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$$

is a *closed form* of the solutions to the general [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) $ax^2+bx+c=0.$

More generally, in the context of [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation), a closed form of a solution is a [solution in radicals](wikipedia://en/1601833); that is, a closed-form expression for which the allowed functions are only $n$th-roots and field operations $(+, -, \times ,/).$ In fact, [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29) allows showing that if a solution of a polynomial equation has a closed form involving exponentials, logarithms or trigonometric functions, then it has also a closed form that does not involve these functions.

There are expressions in radicals for all solutions of [cubic equations](wikipedia://en/180787) (degree 3) and [quartic equations](wikipedia://en/251591) (degree 4). The size of these expressions increases significantly with the degree, limiting their usefulness.

In higher degrees, the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518) states that there are equations whose solutions cannot be expressed in radicals, and, thus, have no closed forms. A simple example is the equation $x^5-x-1=0.$ [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) provides an [algorithmic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_method) for deciding whether a particular polynomial equation can be solved in radicals.

## Symbolic integration {#Symbolic_integration}

[Symbolic integration](wikipedia://en/1652672) consists essentially of the search of closed forms for [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) of functions that are specified by closed-form expressions. In this context, the basic functions used for defining closed forms are commonly [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860), [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) and [polynomial roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_root). Functions that have a closed form for these basic functions are called [elementary functions](wikipedia://en/10412) and include [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367), [inverse trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/374220), [hyperbolic functions](wikipedia://en/56567), and [inverse hyperbolic functions](wikipedia://en/3804557).

The fundamental problem of symbolic integration is thus, given an elementary function specified by a closed-form expression, to decide whether its antiderivative is an elementary function, and, if it is, to find a closed-form expression for this antiderivative.

For [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210); that is, for fractions of two [polynomial functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function); antiderivatives are not always rational fractions, but are always elementary functions that may involve logarithms and polynomial roots. This is usually proved with [partial fraction decomposition](wikipedia://en/247288). The need for logarithms and polynomial roots is illustrated by the formula

$$\int\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\,dx=\sum_{\alpha \in \operatorname{Roots}(g(x))} \frac{f(\alpha)}{g'(\alpha)}\ln(x-\alpha),$$

which is valid if $f$ and $g$ are [coprime polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime_polynomials) such that $g$ is [square free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squarefree_polynomial) and $\deg f <\deg g.$

## Alternative definitions {#Alternative_definitions}

Changing the basic functions to include additional functions can change the set of equations with closed-form solutions. Many [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) cannot be expressed in closed form, unless one considers [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110) such as the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111) or [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) to be basic. It is possible to solve the quintic equation if general [hypergeometric functions](wikipedia://en/2055223) are included, although the solution is far too complicated algebraically to be useful. For many practical computer applications, it is entirely reasonable to assume that the gamma function and other special functions are basic since numerical implementations are widely available.

:::

This is a term that is sometimes understood as a synonym for closed-form (see "Wolfram Mathworld". <https://mathworld.wolfram.com/Analytic.html>) but this usage is contested (see "Math Stackexchange". <https://math.stackexchange.com/questions/1750296/what-is-the-difference-between-closed-form-expression-and-analytic-expression>). ## Comparison of different classes of expressions {#Comparison_of_different_classes_of_expressions}

The closed-form expressions do not include [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) or [continued fractions](wikipedia://en/739199); neither includes [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) or [limits](wikipedia://en/285773). Indeed, by the [Stone–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858), any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) on the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) can be expressed as a limit of polynomials, so any class of functions containing the polynomials and closed under limits will necessarily include all continuous functions.

Similarly, an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) or [system of equations](wikipedia://en/249617) is said to have a **closed-form solution** [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) at least one [solution](wikipedia://en/537048) can be expressed as a closed-form expression; and it is said to have an **analytic solution** if and only if at least one solution can be expressed as an analytic expression. There is a subtle distinction between a "closed-form *function*" and a "closed-form *number*" in the discussion of a "closed-form solution", discussed in (Chow 1999) and below. A closed-form or analytic solution is sometimes referred to as an **explicit solution** (in contrast to [implicit equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation)).

:::

## Dealing with non-closed-form expressions {#Dealing_with_non-closed-form_expressions}

### Transformation into closed-form expressions {#Transformation_into_closed-form_expressions}

The expression:

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x}{2^n}$$

is not in closed form because the summation entails an infinite number of elementary operations. However, by summing a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) this expression can be expressed in the closed form:[^o15]

$$f(x) = 2x.$$

### Differential Galois theory {#Differential_Galois_theory}

The integral of a closed-form expression may or may not itself be expressible as a closed-form expression. This study is referred to as [differential Galois theory](wikipedia://en/833506), by analogy with algebraic Galois theory.

The basic theorem of differential Galois theory is due to [Joseph Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810) in the 1830s and 1840s and hence referred to as **[Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/9482601)**.

A standard example of an elementary function whose antiderivative does not have a closed-form expression is:

$$e^{-x^2},$$

whose one antiderivative is ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a multiplicative constant) the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111):

$$\operatorname{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^x e^{-t^2} \, dt.$$

### Mathematical modelling and computer simulation {#Mathematical_modelling_and_computer_simulation}

Equations or systems too complex for closed-form or analytic solutions can often be analysed by [mathematical modelling](wikipedia://en/20590) and [computer simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_simulation) (for an example in physics, see[^o18]).

## Closed-form number {#Closed-form_number}

section

Three subfields of the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbf{C}$ have been suggested as encoding the notion of a "closed-form number"; in increasing order of generality, these are the Liouvillian numbers (not to be confused with [Liouville numbers](wikipedia://en/50964) in the sense of rational approximation), EL numbers and [elementary numbers](wikipedia://en/4778133). The **Liouvillian numbers**, denoted $\mathbf{L}$, form the smallest *[algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed)* subfield of $\mathbf{C}$ closed under exponentiation and logarithm (formally, intersection of all such subfields)—that is, numbers which involve *explicit* exponentiation and logarithms, but allow explicit and *implicit* polynomials (roots of polynomials); this is defined in (Ritt 1948, p. 60). $\mathbf{L}$ was originally referred to as **elementary numbers**, but this term is now used more broadly to refer to numbers defined explicitly or implicitly in terms of algebraic operations, exponentials, and logarithms. A narrower definition proposed in (Chow 1999, pp. 441–442), denoted $\mathbf{E}$, and referred to as **EL numbers**, is the smallest subfield of $\mathbf{C}$ closed under exponentiation and logarithm—this need not be algebraically closed, and corresponds to *explicit* algebraic, exponential, and logarithmic operations. "EL" stands both for "exponential–logarithmic" and as an abbreviation for "elementary".

Whether a number is a closed-form number is related to whether a number is [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325). Formally, Liouvillian numbers and elementary numbers contain the [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158), and they include some but not all transcendental numbers. In contrast, EL numbers do not contain all algebraic numbers, but do include some transcendental numbers. Closed-form numbers can be studied via [transcendental number theory](wikipedia://en/1157698), in which a major result is the [Gelfond–Schneider theorem](wikipedia://en/609487), and a major open question is [Schanuel's conjecture](wikipedia://en/2031353).

:::

## Numerical computations {#Numerical_computations}

For purposes of numeric computations, being in closed form is not in general necessary, as many limits and integrals can be efficiently computed. Some equations have no closed form solution, such as those that represent the [Three-body problem](wikipedia://en/1208420) or the [Hodgkin–Huxley model](wikipedia://en/2991090). Therefore, the future states of these systems must be computed numerically.

## Conversion from numerical forms {#Conversion_from_numerical_forms}

There is software that attempts to find closed-form expressions for numerical values, including RIES,[^o25] identify in [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29)[^o26] and [SymPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SymPy),[^o27] Plouffe's Inverter,[^o28] and the [Inverse Symbolic Calculator](wikipedia://en/9087019).[^o29]
