# Vertex-transitive graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vertex-transitive graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186) is a permutation of the [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) such that edges are mapped to edges and non-edges are mapped to non-edges.[^o2] A graph is a **vertex-transitive graph** if, given any two vertices $v_{1}$ and $v_{2}$ of $G$, there is an automorphism $f$ such that

:::

$$f(v_1) = v_2.\$$

In other words, a graph is vertex-transitive if its [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) [transitively](wikipedia://en/12781#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) on its vertices.[^o2] A graph is vertex-transitive [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its [graph complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_complement) is, since the group actions are identical.

Every [symmetric graph](wikipedia://en/1577896) without [isolated vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_vertex) is vertex-transitive, and every vertex-transitive graph is [regular](wikipedia://en/85821). However, not all vertex-transitive graphs are symmetric (for example, the edges of the [truncated tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/279651)), and not all regular graphs are vertex-transitive (for example, the [Frucht graph](wikipedia://en/23784721) and [Tietze's graph](wikipedia://en/20058152)).

## Finite examples {#Finite_examples}

*Image: Tuncated tetrahedral graph.png.* The edges of the [truncated tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/279651) form a vertex-transitive graph (also a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277)) which is not [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1577896).

Finite vertex-transitive graphs include the [symmetric graphs](wikipedia://en/1577896) (such as the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926), the [Heawood graph](wikipedia://en/1396870) and the vertices and edges of the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905)). The finite [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) (such as [cube-connected cycles](wikipedia://en/13379568)) are also vertex-transitive, as are the vertices and edges of the [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847) (though only two of these are symmetric). Potočnik, Spiga and Verret have constructed a census of all connected cubic vertex-transitive graphs on at most 1280 vertices.[^o4]

Although every Cayley graph is vertex-transitive, there exist other vertex-transitive graphs that are not Cayley graphs. The most famous example is the Petersen graph, but others can be constructed including the [line graphs](wikipedia://en/675231) of [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/584406) non-[bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431) graphs with [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) vertex degrees.[^o5]

## Properties {#Properties}

The [edge-connectivity](wikipedia://en/1614492) of a connected vertex-transitive graph is equal to the [degree](wikipedia://en/85821) *d*, while the [vertex-connectivity](wikipedia://en/1614492) will be at least 2(*d* + 1)/3.[^o2] If the degree is 4 or less, or the graph is also [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/584406), or the graph is a minimal [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277), then the vertex-connectivity will also be equal to *d*.[^o8]

:::

## Infinite examples {#Infinite_examples}

Infinite vertex-transitive graphs include:

- infinite [paths](wikipedia://en/638889) (infinite in both directions)
- infinite [regular](wikipedia://en/85821) [trees](wikipedia://en/48560), e.g. the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735)
- graphs of [uniform tessellations](wikipedia://en/12126635) (see a [complete list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_uniform_planar_tilings) of planar [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671)), including all [tilings by regular polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiling_by_regular_polygons)
- infinite [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277)
- the [Rado graph](wikipedia://en/7785594)

Two [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) vertex-transitive graphs are called [quasi-isometric](wikipedia://en/490861#Q) if the ratio of their [distance functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_function) is bounded from below and from above. A well known [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) stated that every infinite vertex-transitive graph is quasi-isometric to a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277). A counterexample was proposed by [Diestel](wikipedia://en/78071340) and [Leader](wikipedia://en/3756629) in 2001.[^o10] In 2005, Eskin, Fisher, and Whyte confirmed the counterexample.[^o11]
