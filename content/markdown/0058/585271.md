# Perfect group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Perfect group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is said to be **perfect** if it equals its own [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847), or equivalently, if the group has no [non-trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [quotients](wikipedia://en/11526).

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The smallest (non-trivial) perfect group is the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) *A*<sub>5</sub>. More generally, any [non-abelian](wikipedia://en/4791400) [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) is perfect since the commutator subgroup is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) with abelian quotient. However, a perfect group need not be simple; for example, the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) with 5 elements, SL(2,5) (or the [binary icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/3195145), which is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to it) is perfect but not simple (it has a non-trivial [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_%28group%29) containing $-\!\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}4 & 0 \\ 0 & 4\end{smallmatrix}\right)$).

The [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of any two simple non-abelian groups is perfect but not simple; the commutator of two elements is \[(*a*,*b*),(*c*,*d*)\] = (\[*a*,*c*\],\[*b*,*d*\]). Since commutators in each simple group form a generating set, pairs of commutators form a generating set of the direct product.

The fundamental group of $SO(3)/I_{60}$ is a perfect group of order 120.[^o4]

More generally, a [quasisimple group](wikipedia://en/1642729) (a perfect [central extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_extension_%28mathematics%29) of a simple group) that is a non-trivial extension (and therefore not a simple group itself) is perfect but not simple; this includes all the [insoluble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soluble_group) non-simple finite special linear groups SL(*n*,*q*) as extensions of the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) PSL(*n*,*q*) (SL(2,5) is an extension of PSL(2,5), which is isomorphic to *A*<sub>5</sub>). Similarly, the special linear group over the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) and [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) numbers is perfect, but the general linear group GL is never perfect (except when trivial or over $\mathbb{F}_2$, where it equals the special linear group), as the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) gives a non-trivial abelianization and indeed the commutator subgroup is SL.

A non-trivial perfect group, however, is necessarily not [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012); and 4 [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) its [order](wikipedia://en/493527) (if finite), moreover, if 8 does not divide the order, then 3 does.[^o5]

Every [acyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acyclic_group) is perfect, but the converse is not true: *A*<sub>5</sub> is perfect but not acyclic (in fact, not even [superperfect](wikipedia://en/9915961)), see (Berrick Hillman 2003). In fact, for $n\ge 5$ the alternating group $A_n$ is perfect but not superperfect, with $H_2(A_n,\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n \ge 8$.

Any quotient of a perfect group is perfect. A non-trivial finite perfect group that is not simple must then be an extension of at least one smaller simple non-abelian group. But it can be the extension of more than one simple group. In fact, the direct product of perfect groups is also perfect.

Every perfect group *G* determines another perfect group *E* (its [universal central extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_central_extension)) together with a [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection) *f*: *E* → *G* whose [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is in the center of *E,* such that *f* is universal with this property. The kernel of *f* is called the [Schur multiplier](wikipedia://en/1121587) of *G* because it was first studied by [Issai Schur](wikipedia://en/617522) in 1904; it is isomorphic to the [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) $H_2(G)$.

In the **plus construction** of [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500), if we consider the group $\operatorname{GL}(A) = \text{colim} \operatorname{GL}_n(A)$ for a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $A$, then the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of elementary matrices $E(R)$ forms a perfect subgroup.

:::

## Ore's conjecture {#Ore's_conjecture}

As the commutator subgroup is *generated* by commutators, a perfect group may contain elements that are products of commutators but not themselves commutators. [Øystein Ore](wikipedia://en/1643733) showed in 1951 that the alternating groups on five or more elements contained only commutators, and [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that this was so for all the finite non-abelian simple groups. Ore's conjecture was finally [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) in 2008. The proof relies on the [classification theorem](wikipedia://en/7445).[^o9]

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Grün's lemma"}

## Grün's lemma {#Grün's_lemma}

A basic fact about perfect groups is **Grün's lemma** (Grün 1935, Satz 4, p. 3), due to [Otto Grün](wikipedia://en/68331221): the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) of a perfect group by its [center](wikipedia://en/7125) is centerless (has trivial center).

> **Proof:** If *G* is a perfect group, let *Z*<sub>1</sub> and *Z*<sub>2</sub> denote the first two terms of the [upper central series](wikipedia://en/15202655#Upper_central_series) of *G* (i.e., *Z*<sub>1</sub> is the center of *G*, and *Z*<sub>2</sub>/*Z*<sub>1</sub> is the center of *G*/*Z*<sub>1</sub>). If *H* and *K* are subgroups of *G*, denote the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) of *H* and *K* by \[*H*, *K*\] and note that \[*Z*<sub>1</sub>, *G*\] = 1 and \[*Z*<sub>2</sub>, *G*\] ⊆ *Z*<sub>1</sub>, and consequently (the convention that \[*X*, *Y*, *Z*\] = \[\[*X*, *Y*\], *Z*\] is followed):
>
> $$[Z_2,G,G]=[[Z_2,G],G]\subseteq [Z_1,G]=1$$
>
> $$[G,Z_2,G]=[[G,Z_2],G]=[[Z_2,G],G]\subseteq [Z_1,G]=1.$$
>
> By the [three subgroups lemma](wikipedia://en/26406313) (or equivalently, by the [Hall-Witt identity](wikipedia://en/7193#Identities_%28group_theory%29)), it follows that \[*G*, *Z*<sub>2</sub>\] = \[\[*G*, *G*\], *Z*<sub>2</sub>\] = \[*G*, *G*, *Z*<sub>2</sub>\] = {1}. Therefore, *Z*<sub>2</sub> ⊆ *Z*<sub>1</sub> = *Z*(*G*), and the center of the quotient group *G* / *Z*(*G*) is the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059).

As a consequence, all [higher centers](wikipedia://en/7125#Higher_centers) (that is, higher terms in the [upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series)) of a perfect group equal the center.

:::

## Group homology {#Group_homology}

In terms of [group homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homology), a perfect group is precisely one whose first homology group vanishes: *H*<sub>1</sub>(*G*, **Z**) = 0, as the first homology group of a group is exactly the abelianization of the group, and perfect means trivial abelianization. An advantage of this definition is that it admits strengthening:

- A [superperfect group](wikipedia://en/9915961) is one whose first two homology groups vanish: $H_1(G,\mathbb{Z})=H_2(G,\mathbb{Z})=0$.
- An [acyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acyclic_group) is one *all* of whose (reduced) homology groups vanish $\tilde H_i(G;\mathbb{Z}) = 0.$ (This is equivalent to all homology groups other than $H_0$ vanishing.)

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Quasi-perfect group"}

## Quasi-perfect group {#Quasi-perfect_group}

Especially in the field of [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500), a group is said to be **quasi-perfect** if its commutator subgroup is perfect; in symbols, a quasi-perfect group is one such that *G*<sup>(1)</sup> = *G*<sup>(2)</sup> (the commutator of the commutator subgroup is the commutator subgroup), while a perfect group is one such that *G*<sup>(1)</sup> = *G* (the commutator subgroup is the whole group). See (Karoubi 1973, 301–411) and (Inassaridze 1995, 76).

:::
