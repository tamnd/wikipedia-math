# Maurice Kendall

*Image: Kendall Maurice.jpg.* Sir Maurice George Kendall

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Maurice Kendall"}

**Sir Maurice George Kendall**, [FBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy) (6 September 1907 – 29 March 1983) was a prominent British statistician. The [Kendall tau rank correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendall's_tau) is named after him.

:::

## Education and early life {#Education_and_early_life}

Maurice Kendall was born in [Kettering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kettering), Northamptonshire as the only child of engineering worker John Roughton Kendall and Georgina, née Brewer. His paternal grandfather was a publican, running The Woolpack at Kettering.[^o3][^o4]

As a child, he survived [cerebral meningitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meningitis), which was frequently fatal at that time. After growing up in [Derby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derby,_England), England, he studied mathematics at [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge), where he played [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket) and [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess) (with future champions [Conel Hugh O'Donel Alexander](wikipedia://en/2784711) and [Jacob Bronowski](wikipedia://en/241528)). After graduation as a Mathematics [Wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrangler_%28University_of_Cambridge%29) in 1929, he joined the [British Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Civil_Service) in the [Ministry of Agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Agriculture). In this position he became increasingly interested in using statistics towards agricultural questions, and one of his first published papers to the [Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/552664) involved studying crop productivity using [factor analysis](wikipedia://en/253492). He was elected a Fellow of the Society in 1934.[^o5]

## Work in statistics {#Work_in_statistics}

In 1938 and 1939 he began work, along with [Bernard Babington-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Babington_Smith)[^o7] known as BBS, on the question of [random number generation](wikipedia://en/2736939), developing both one of the first early mechanical devices to produce random digits, and formulated a series of tests for [statistical randomness](wikipedia://en/2551773) in a given set of digits which, with some small modifications, became fairly widely used.[^o8] He produced one of the second large collections of random digits[^o9] (100,000 in total, over twice as many as those published by [L. H. C. Tippett](wikipedia://en/9168194) in 1927), which was a commonly used tract until the publication of [RAND Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND_Corporation)'s *[A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates](wikipedia://en/1354812)* in 1955 (which was developed with a [roulette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roulette) wheel-like machine very similar to Kendall's and verified as "random" using his statistical tests).

In 1937, he aided the ageing statistician [G. Udny Yule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._Udny_Yule) in the revision of his standard statistical textbook, *Introduction to the Theory of Statistics*, commonly known for many years as "Yule and Kendall".[^o10] The two had met by chance in 1935, and were on close terms until Yule's death in 1951 (Yule was [godfather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godparent) to Kendall's second son).

During this period he also began work on the [rank correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/3316627) which currently bears his name ([Kendall's tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendall's_tau)), which eventually led to a monograph on *Rank Correlation* in 1948.[^o11]

In the late 1930s, he was additionally part of a group of five other statisticians who endeavoured to produce a reference work summarising recent developments in statistical theory, but it was cancelled on account of onset of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II).[^o12]

## War-time efforts {#War-time_efforts}

Kendall became Assistant general manager to the [British Chamber of Shipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Chamber_of_Shipping) by day and had air-raid warden duties by night. Despite these constraints on his time, he managed to produce volume one of *The Advanced Theory of Statistics* in 1943 and a second volume in 1946.

During the war he also produced a series of papers extending to work of [R.A. Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.A._Fisher) on the theory of [k-statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-statistics), and developed a number of extensions to this work through the 1950s. After the war, he worked on the theory and practice of [time series analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series_analysis), and conclusively demonstrated (with the meager [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing) resources available at the time) that unsmoothed sample [periodograms](wikipedia://en/485457) were unreliable estimators for the population spectrum.

## London School of Economics {#London_School_of_Economics}

In 1949 he accepted the second chair of statistics at the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics), [University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London). Here he worked part-time as the director of the new Research Techniques Division. From 1952 to 1957 he edited a two-volume work on *Statistical Sources in the United Kingdom*, which was a standard reference until the mid-1970s. In the 1950s he also worked on [multivariate analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_analysis), and developed the text *Multivariate Analysis* in 1957. In 1957, he also developed, with W. R. Buckland, a *Dictionary of Statistical Terms*, aimed at helping making the tools of statistics more available to potential users in industry and government.

In 1953, he published "The Analytics of Economic Time Series, Part 1: Prices"[^o15] in which he suggested that the movement of shares on the stock market was random (as likely to go up on a certain day as to go down). These results were disturbing to some financial economists and further debate and research then followed. This ultimately led to the creation of the [random walk hypothesis](wikipedia://en/2009882), and the closely related [efficient-market hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient-market_hypothesis) which states that random price movements indicate a well-functioning or efficient market.

## CEIR and WFS {#CEIR_and_WFS}

In 1961 he left the University of London and took a position as the managing director (later chairman) of a consulting company, CEIR (later known as Scientific Control Systems), and in the same year began a two-year term as president of the [Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/552664). In the 1960s he published and co-edited a number of volumes and monographs in statistical theory.

In 1972, he became director of the World Fertility Survey, a project sponsored by the [International Statistical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Statistical_Institute) and the United Nations which aimed to study [fertility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility) in developed and developing nations. He continued this work until 1980, when illness forced him to retire.

## Honours {#Honours}

section He was knighted by the British government in 1974 for his services to the theory of statistics, and received a medal from the United Nations in 1980 in recognition for his work on the World Fertility Survey. He was also elected a fellow of the [British Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy) and received the highest honour of the [Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/552664), the [Guy Medal](wikipedia://en/6752269) in Gold.

He additionally had served as president of the [Operational Research Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operational_Research_Society), the [Institute of Statisticians](wikipedia://en/3201873), and was elected a fellow of the [American Statistical Association](wikipedia://en/341988), the [Institute of Mathematical Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Mathematical_Statistics), the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society), and the [British Computer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Computer_Society).

At the time of his death in 1983, he was honorary president of the International Statistical Institute.

## Notes {#Notes}

## Family {#Family}

Kendall's first wife, Sheila (née Lester), predeceased him. He was survived by their daughter and two sons, and by his second wife, Ruth (née Whitfield) and their son.
