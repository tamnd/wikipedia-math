# Nerve complex

*Image: Constructing nerve.png.* Constructing the nerve of an [open good cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_cover_%28algebraic_topology%29) containing 3 sets in the plane.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nerve complex"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the **nerve complex** of a [set family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_family) is an [abstract complex](wikipedia://en/723105) that records the pattern of intersections between the sets in the family. It was introduced by [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245)[^o2] and now has many variants and generalisations, among them the **Čech nerve** of a cover, which in turn is generalised by [hypercoverings](wikipedia://en/54077383). It captures many of the interesting topological properties in an algorithmic or combinatorial way.[^o3]

:::

## Basic definition {#Basic_definition}

Let $I$ be a set of indices and $C$ be a family of sets $(U_i)_{i\in I}$. The **nerve** of $C$ is a set of finite subsets of the index set *$I$*. It contains all finite subsets $J\subseteq I$ such that the intersection of the $U_i$ whose subindices are in $J$ is non-empty:*[^o5]p. 81*

$$N(C) := \bigg\{J\subseteq I: \bigcap_{j\in J}U_j \neq \varnothing, J \text{ finite set} \bigg\}.$$

In Alexandrov's original definition, the sets $(U_i)_{i\in I}$ are [open subsets](wikipedia://en/39358) of some topological space $X$.

The set $N(C)$ may contain singletons (elements $i \in I$ such that $U_i$ is non-empty), pairs (pairs of elements $i,j \in I$ such that $U_i \cap U_j \neq \emptyset$), triplets, and so on. If $J \in N(C)$, then any subset of $J$ is also in $N(C)$, making $N(C)$ an [abstract simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/723105). Hence N(C) is often called the **nerve complex** of $C$.

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

1. Let *X* be the circle $S^1$ and $C = \{U_1, U_2\}$, where $U_1$ is an arc covering the upper half of $S^1$ and $U_2$ is an arc covering its lower half, with some overlap at both sides (they must overlap at both sides in order to cover all of $S^1$). Then $N(C) = \{ \{1\}, \{2\}, \{1,2\} \}$, which is an abstract 1-simplex.
1. Let *X* be the circle $S^1$ and $C = \{U_1, U_2, U_3\}$, where each $U_i$ is an arc covering one third of $S^1$, with some overlap with the adjacent $U_i$. Then $N(C) = \{ \{1\}, \{2\}, \{3\}, \{1,2\}, \{2,3\}, \{3,1\} \}$. Note that {1,2,3} is not in $N(C)$ since the common intersection of all three sets is empty; so $N(C)$ is an unfilled triangle.
1. Let $P$ be a finite set of points in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736) (no four points are cocircular) in $\mathbb{R}^2$, and let $C = \{V_p\}_{p \in P}$ be the set of cells of the [Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/177668) of $P$. Then the nerve $N(C)$ is the [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) of $P$.[^o8] Note that the Voronoi cells are closed sets, so this is a nerve of a closed cover rather than an open one; however, the definition of the nerve applies to arbitrary set families.

:::

## The Čech nerve {#The_Čech_nerve}

Given an [open cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) $C=\{U_i: i\in I\}$ of a topological space $X$, or more generally a cover in a [site](wikipedia://en/12910), we can consider the pairwise [fibre products](wikipedia://en/841689) $U_{ij}=U_i\times_XU_j$, which in the case of a topological space are precisely the intersections $U_i\cap U_j$. The collection of all such intersections can be referred to as $C\times_X C$ and the triple intersections as $C\times_X C\times_X C$.

By considering the natural maps $U_{ij}\to U_i$ and $U_i\to U_{ii}$, we can construct a [simplicial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_object) $S(C)_\bullet$ defined by $S(C)_n=C\times_X\cdots\times_XC$, n-fold fibre product. This is the **Čech nerve.**[^o10]

By taking connected components we get a [simplicial set](wikipedia://en/1027784), which we can realise topologically: $|S(\pi_0(C))|$.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Nerve theorems"}

## Nerve theorems {#Nerve_theorems}

The nerve complex $N(C)$ is a simple combinatorial object. Often, it is much simpler than the underlying topological space (the union of the sets in $C$). Therefore, a natural question is whether the topology of $N(C)$ is equivalent to the topology of $\bigcup C$.

In general, this need not be the case. For example, one can cover any [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) with two contractible sets $U_1$ and $U_2$ that have a non-empty intersection, as in example 1 above. In this case, $N(C)$ is an abstract 1-simplex, which is similar to a line but not to a sphere.

However, in some cases $N(C)$ does reflect the topology of *X*. For example, if a circle is covered by three open arcs, intersecting in pairs as in Example 2 above, then $N(C)$ is a 2-simplex (without its interior) and it is [homotopy-equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy-equivalent) to the original circle.[^o13]

A **nerve theorem** (or **nerve lemma**) is a theorem that gives sufficient conditions on *C* guaranteeing that $N(C)$ reflects, in some sense, the topology of *$\bigcup C$*. A **functorial nerve theorem** is a nerve theorem that is functorial in an appropriate sense, which is, for example, crucial in [topological data analysis](wikipedia://en/17740009).[^o14]

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Leray's nerve theorem"}

### Leray's nerve theorem {#Leray's_nerve_theorem}

The basic nerve theorem of [Jean Leray](wikipedia://en/245430) says that, if any intersection of sets in $N(C)$ is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414) (equivalently: for each finite $J\subset I$ the set $\bigcap_{i\in J} U_i$ is either empty or contractible; equivalently: *C* is a [good open cover](wikipedia://en/32160209)), then $N(C)$ is [homotopy-equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy-equivalent) to *$\bigcup C$*.

:::

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Borsuk's nerve theorem"}

### Borsuk's nerve theorem {#Borsuk's_nerve_theorem}

There is a discrete version, which is attributed to [Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk).[^o19]*[^o5]p. 81* Let *K<sub>1</sub>,...,K<sub>n</sub>* be [abstract simplicial complexes](wikipedia://en/723105), and denote their union by *K*. Let *U<sub>i</sub>* = ||*K<sub>i</sub>||* = the [geometric realization](wikipedia://en/723105) of *K<sub>i</sub>*, and denote the nerve of {*U<sub>1</sub>*, ... , *U<sub>n</sub>* } by *N*.

If, for each nonempty $J\subset I$, the intersection $\bigcap_{i\in J} U_i$ is either empty or contractible, then *N* is [homotopy-equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy-equivalent) to *K*.

A stronger theorem was proved by [Anders Bjorner](wikipedia://en/24753353).[^o20] If, for each nonempty $J\subset I$, the intersection $\bigcap_{i\in J} U_i$ is either empty or [(k-|J|+1)-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-connected_space), then for every *j* ≤ *k*, the *j*-th [homotopy group](wikipedia://en/297506) of *N* is isomorphic to the *j*-th [homotopy group](wikipedia://en/297506) of *K*. In particular, *N* is *k*-connected if-and-only-if *K* is *k*-connected.

:::

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Čech nerve theorem"}

### Čech nerve theorem {#Čech_nerve_theorem}

Another nerve theorem relates to the Čech nerve above: if $X$ is compact and all intersections of sets in *C* are contractible or empty, then the space $|S(\pi_0(C))|$ is [homotopy-equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy-equivalent) to $X$.

:::

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Homological nerve theorem"}

### Homological nerve theorem {#Homological_nerve_theorem}

The following nerve theorem uses the [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_groups) of intersections of sets in the cover.[^o26] For each finite $J\subset I$, denote $H_{J,j} := \tilde{H}_j(\bigcap_{i\in J} U_i)=$ the *j*-th [reduced homology](wikipedia://en/3110036) group of $\bigcap_{i\in J} U_i$.

If *H<sub>J,j</sub>* is the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) for all *J* in the *k*-skeleton of N(*C*) and for all *j* in {0, ..., *k*-dim(*J*)}, then N(*C*) is "homology-equivalent" to *X* in the following sense:

- $\tilde{H}_j(N(C)) \cong \tilde{H}_j(X)$ for all *j* in {0, ..., *k*};
- if $\tilde{H}_{k+1}(N(C))\not\cong 0$ then $\tilde{H}_{k+1}(X)\not\cong 0$ .

:::
