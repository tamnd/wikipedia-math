# Ternary plot

*Image: Flammability diagram methane.svg.* A ternary [flammability diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flammability_diagram), showing which mixtures of [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane), [oxygen gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_gas), and inert [nitrogen gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_gas) will burn

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ternary plot"}

A **ternary plot**, **ternary graph**, **triangle plot**, **simplex plot**, or **Gibbs triangle** is a [barycentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29) [plot](wikipedia://en/19774918) on three variables which [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) to a constant.[^o2] It graphically depicts the ratios of the three variables as positions in an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285). It is used in [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry), [petrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrology), [mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy), [metallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgy), and other physical sciences to show the compositions of systems composed of three species. Ternary plots are tools for analyzing [compositional data](wikipedia://en/500654) in the three-dimensional case.

:::

In [population genetics](wikipedia://en/219268), a triangle plot of genotype frequencies is called a [de Finetti diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Finetti_diagram). In [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924)[^o3] and [convex optimization](wikipedia://en/1674411),[^o4] it is often called a [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) plot.

In a ternary plot, the values of the three variables $a$, $b$, and $c$ must sum to some constant, $K$. Usually, this constant is represented as 1.0 or 100%. Because $a + b + c = K$ for all substances being graphed, any one variable is not independent of the others, so only two variables must be known to find a sample's point on the graph: for instance, $c$ must be equal to $K - a - b$. Because the three numerical values cannot vary independently—there are only two [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606)—it is possible to graph the combinations of all three variables in only two dimensions.

The advantage of using a ternary plot for depicting [chemical compositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_composition) is that three variables can be conveniently plotted in a two-dimensional graph. Ternary plots can also be used to create [phase diagrams](wikipedia://en/56398) by outlining the composition regions on the plot where different phases exist.

The values of a point on a ternary plot correspond (up to a constant) to its [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) or [barycentric coordinates](wikipedia://en/762954).

## Reading values on a ternary plot {#Reading_values_on_a_ternary_plot}

There are three equivalent methods that can be used to determine the values of a point on the plot:

1. **Parallel line** or **grid method**. The first method is to use a diagram grid consisting of lines parallel to the triangle edges. A parallel to a side of the triangle is the locus of points constant in the component situated in the vertex opposed to the side. Each component is 100% in a corner of the triangle and 0% at the edge opposite it, decreasing linearly with increasing distance (perpendicular to the opposite edge) from this corner. By drawing parallel lines at regular intervals between the zero line and the corner, fine divisions can be established for easy estimation.
1. **Perpendicular line** or **altitude method**. For diagrams that do not possess grid lines, the easiest way to determine the values is to determine the shortest (i.e. perpendicular) distances from the point of interest to each of the three sides. By [Viviani's theorem](wikipedia://en/10460511), the distances (or the ratios of the distances to the [triangle height](wikipedia://en/161241)) give the value of each component.
1. **Corner line** or **intersection method**. The third method does not require the drawing of perpendicular or parallel lines. Straight lines are drawn from each corner, through the point of interest, to the opposite side of the triangle. The lengths of these lines, as well as the lengths of the segments between the point and the corresponding sides, are measured individually. The ratio of the measured lines then gives the component value as a fraction of 100%.

A displacement along a parallel line (grid line) preserves the sum of two values, while motion along a perpendicular line increases (or decreases) the two values an equal amount, each half of the decrease (increase) of the third value. Motion along a line through a corner preserves the ratio of the other two values.

## Derivation from Cartesian coordinates {#Derivation_from_Cartesian_coordinates}

*Image: ternary plot visualisation.svg.* Derivation of a ternary plot from Cartesian coordinates

Figure (1) shows an [oblique projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_projection) of point $P(a,b,c)$ in a 3-dimensional [Cartesian space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_space) with axes $a$, $b$ and $c$, respectively.

If $a + b + c = K$ (a positive constant), $P$ is restricted to a plane containing $A(K,0,0)$, $B(0,K,0)$ and $C(0,0,K)$. If $a$, $b$ and $c$ each cannot be negative, $P$ is restricted to the triangle bounded by $A$, $B$ and $C$, as in (2).

In (3), the axes are rotated to give an [isometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_projection) view. The triangle, viewed face-on, appears [equilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilateral).

In (4), the distances of $P$ from lines $\operatorname{BC}$, $\operatorname{AC}$ and $\operatorname{AB}$ are denoted by $a'$, $b'$ and $c'$, respectively.

For any line $\mathbf{l} = \mathbf{s} + t \mathbf{n\text{̂}}$ in vector form ($\mathbf{n\text{̂}}$ is a unit vector) and a point $\mathbf{p}$, the [perpendicular distance](wikipedia://en/23972889) from $\mathbf{p}$ to $\mathbf{l}$ is

$$\left\| (\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{p}) - \bigl((\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{p}) \cdot \mathbf{\hat n}\bigr)\mathbf{\hat n} \right\| \,.$$

In this case, point $P$ is at

$$\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix}a\\b\\c\end{pmatrix} \,.$$

Line $\operatorname{BC}$ has

$$\mathbf{s} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\K\\0\end{pmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{\hat{n}} = \frac{\begin{pmatrix}0\\K\\0\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\K\end{pmatrix}}{\left\|\begin{pmatrix}0\\K\\0\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\K\end{pmatrix}\right\|} = \frac{\begin{pmatrix}0\\K\\-K\end{pmatrix}}{\sqrt{0^2+K^2+{(-K)}^2}} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\\-\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\end{pmatrix} \,.$$

Using the perpendicular distance formula,

$$\begin{aligned} a' & = \left\| \begin{pmatrix}-a\\K-b\\-c\end{pmatrix} - \left( \begin{pmatrix}-a\\K-b\\-c\end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix}0\\\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\\-\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\end{pmatrix} \right) \begin{pmatrix}0\\\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\\-\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\end{pmatrix} \right\| \\[10px] & = \left\| \begin{pmatrix}-a\\K-b\\-c\end{pmatrix} - \left( 0 + \frac{K-b}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{c}{\sqrt{2}} \right) \begin{pmatrix}0\\\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\\-\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\end{pmatrix} \right\| \\[10px] & = \left\| \begin{pmatrix}-a\\K-b-\frac{K-b+c}{2}\\-c+\frac{K-b+c}{2}\end{pmatrix} \right\| = \left\| \begin{pmatrix}-a\\\frac{K-b-c}{2}\\\frac{K-b-c}{2}\end{pmatrix} \right\| \\[10px] & = \sqrt{{(-a)}^2 + {\left(\frac{K-b-c}{2}\right)}^2 + {\left(\frac{K-b-c}{2}\right)}^2} = \sqrt{a^2 + \frac{{(K-b-c)}^2}{2}} \,. \end{aligned}$$

Substituting $K = a + b + c$,

$$a' = \sqrt{a^2 + \frac{{(a+b+c-b-c)}^2}{2}} = \sqrt{a^2 + \frac{a^2}{2}} = a\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} \,.$$

Similar calculation on lines $\operatorname{AC}$ and $\operatorname{AB}$ gives

$$b' = b\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} \quad\text{and}\quad c' = c\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} \,.$$

This shows that the distance of the point from the respective lines is linearly proportional to the original values $a$, $b$ and $c$.[^o7]

## Plotting a ternary plot {#Plotting_a_ternary_plot}

*Image: rectangular\_ternary\_plot.svg.* Analogue on a Cartesian grid by adding lines of slope −1. The scale of the *c* axis is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ that of the *a* and *b* axes. The cross denotes the point *a* = *b* = *c*.

[Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) are useful for plotting points in the triangle. Consider an equilateral ternary plot where $a = 100\%$ is placed at $(x,y) = (0,0)$ and $b = 100\%$ at $(1,0)$. Then $c = 100\%$ is $(\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}),$ and the triple $(a,b,c)$ is

$$\left(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2b+c}{a+b+c},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\frac{c}{a+b+c}\right) \,.$$
