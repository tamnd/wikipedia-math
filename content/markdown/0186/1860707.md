# Solomon W. Golomb

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Solomon W. Golomb"}

**Solomon Wolf Golomb** ( ;[^o3] May 30, 1932 – May 1, 2016) was an American mathematician, engineer, and professor of [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) at the [University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California), best known for his works on [mathematical games](wikipedia://en/26446).[^o4] He most notably invented [Cheskers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheskers) (a hybrid between [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess) and [checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkers)) in 1948. He also fully described [polyominoes](wikipedia://en/72634) in 1953.[^o5][^o6] He specialized in problems of [combinatorial analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_analysis), [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), and communications. [Pentomino](wikipedia://en/23712) board games, based on his work, would go on to inspire *[Tetris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetris)*.[^o7]

:::

## Achievements {#Achievements}

Golomb, a [Baltimore City College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore_City_College) high school graduate, received his bachelor's degree from [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University) and master's and doctorate in mathematics from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) in 1957. His dissertation was "Problems in the Distribution of the Prime Numbers."

While working at the [Glenn L. Martin Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_L._Martin_Company), he became interested in communications theory and began working on [shift register sequences](wikipedia://en/58992). He spent his [Fulbright year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulbright_Scholarship) at the [University of Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oslo) and then joined the [Jet Propulsion Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_Propulsion_Laboratory) at [Caltech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caltech), where he researched military and space communications. He joined the faculty of [USC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California) in 1963 and was awarded full tenure two years later.

Golomb pioneered the identification of the characteristics and merits of [maximum length shift register sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_length_sequence),[^o9] also known as [pseudorandom](wikipedia://en/45524) or pseudonoise sequences, which have extensive military, industrial, and consumer applications. Today, millions of cordless and cellular phones employ pseudorandom [direct-sequence spread spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-sequence_spread_spectrum) implemented with shift register sequences. His efforts made USC a center for communications research.

Golomb was the inventor of [Golomb coding](wikipedia://en/147230), a form of [entropy encoding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_encoding). [Golomb rulers](wikipedia://en/48211), used in astronomy and data encryption, are also named for him, as is one of the main generation techniques of [Costas arrays](wikipedia://en/2068445), the Lempel-Golomb generation method.

He was a regular columnist, writing Golomb's Puzzle Column in the [IEEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE) Information Society Newsletter. He was also a frequent contributor to *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)**s*****Mathematical Games*column (The column did much to publicize his discoveries about polyominoes and pentominoes) and a frequent participant in [Gathering 4 Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gathering_4_Gardner) conferences.[^o10] Among his contributions to recreational mathematics are*[Rep-tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rep-tiles)*. He also contributed a puzzle to each issue of the*Johns Hopkins Magazine,*a monthly publication of his undergraduate alma mater, for a column called "Golomb's Gambits," and was a frequent contributor to*[Word Ways: The Journal of Recreational Linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_Ways:_The_Journal_of_Recreational_Linguistics)*.[^o11]***

## Awards {#Awards}

Golomb was a member of the [National Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering) and the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences).

In 1985, he received the [Shannon Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_E._Shannon_Award) of the Information Theory Society of the IEEE.

In 1992, he received the U.S. [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) medal for his research. He has also received the Lomonosov Medal of the [Russian Academy of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Science) and the Kapitsa Medal of the Russian Academy of Natural Sciences.

In 2000, he was awarded the [IEEE Richard W. Hamming Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Richard_W._Hamming_Medal) for his exceptional contributions to information sciences and systems.[^o13] For over four decades, he was singled out as a major figure in coding and information theory, specifically for his ability to apply advanced mathematics to problems in digital communications.

In 2012, he became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o14] That same year, it was announced that he had been selected to receive the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science).[^o15] In 2014, he was elected as a fellow of the [Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1883376) "for contributions to coding theory, data encryption, communications, and mathematical games."[^o16]

In 2013, he was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science) 2011.[^o17]

In 2016, he was awarded the [Benjamin Franklin Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin_Medal_%28Franklin_Institute%29) in Electrical Engineering "for pioneering work in space communications and the design of digital spread spectrum signals, transmissions that provide security, interference suppression, and precise location for cryptography; missile guidance; defense, space, and cellular communications; radar; sonar; and GPS."[^o18]
