# Biggest little polygon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Biggest little polygon"}

*Image: Biggest little polygon.svg.* Biggest little polygon with 6 sides (on the left); on the right the regular polygon with same diameter but lower area.

In geometry, the **biggest little polygon** for a number $n$ is the $n$-sided [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) that has [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) one (that is, every two of its [points](wikipedia://en/593693) are within unit distance of each other) and that has the largest [area](wikipedia://en/1209) among all diameter-one $n$-gons. One non-unique solution when $n=4$ is a [square](wikipedia://en/659939), and the solution is a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) when $n$ is an odd number, but the solution is irregular otherwise.

:::

## Quadrilaterals {#Quadrilaterals}

For $n=4$, the area of an arbitrary [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) is given by the formula $S=\tfrac12pq\sin\theta$ where $p$ and $q$ are the two diagonals of the quadrilateral and $\theta$ is either of the angles they form with each other. In order for the diameter to be at most one, both $p$ and $q$ must themselves be at most one. Therefore, the quadrilateral has largest area when the three factors in the area formula are individually maximized, with $p=q=1$ and $\sin\theta=1$. The condition that $p=q$ means that the quadrilateral is an [equidiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/35012663) (its diagonals have equal length), and the condition that $\sin\theta=1$ means that it is an [orthodiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/30425383) (its diagonals cross at right angles). The quadrilaterals that are both equidiagonal and orthodiagonal are the [midsquare quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/45005169). They include the [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) with unit-length diagonals, which has area $\tfrac12$. Infinitely many other midsquare quadrilaterals also have diameter one and have the same area as the square, so in this case the solution is not unique.[^o3]

## Odd numbers of sides {#Odd_numbers_of_sides}

For odd values of $n$, it was shown by [Karl Reinhardt](wikipedia://en/35727144) in 1922 that a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) has largest area among all diameter-one polygons.[^o5]

## Even numbers of sides {#Even_numbers_of_sides}

In the case $n=6$, the unique optimal polygon is not regular. The solution to this case was published in 1975 by [Ronald Graham](wikipedia://en/240694), answering a question posed in 1956 by [Hanfried Lenz](wikipedia://en/14052690);[^o7] it takes the form of an irregular equidiagonal pentagon with an obtuse isosceles triangle attached to one of its sides, with the distance from the apex of the triangle to the opposite pentagon vertex equal to the diagonals of the pentagon.[^o8] Its area is 0.674981.... , a number that satisfies the equation

$$\begin{aligned} 0={}&4096x^{10}+8192x^9-3008x^8-30848x^7\\ &+21056x^6+146496x^5-221360x^4\\ &+1232x^3+14446x^2-78488x+1193.\\ \end{aligned}$$

Because the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of this number is the [insoluble group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insoluble_group) $S_{10}$, it cannot be expressed in [closed form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-form_formula) using [nested radicals](wikipedia://en/2503693).

Graham conjectured that the optimal solution for the general case of even values of $n$ consists in the same way of an equidiagonal $(n-1)$-gon with an isosceles triangle attached to one of its sides, its apex at unit distance from the opposite $(n-1)$-gon vertex. In the case $n=8$ this was verified by a computer calculation by Audet et al.[^o9] Graham's proof that his hexagon is optimal, and the computer proof of the $n=8$ case, both involved a case analysis of all possible $n$-vertex [thrackles](wikipedia://en/9797288) with straight edges.

The full conjecture of Graham, characterizing the solution to the biggest little polygon problem for all even values of $n$, was proven in 2007 by Foster and Szabo. Their proof constructs a polygon, formed by an equidiagonal $(n-1)$-gon with one more vertex, whose area is larger than any $n$-gon that is not of this form. However, they do not show that their construction produces optimal polygons. They leave as open questions whether the optimal solution is always unique and bilaterally symmetric, as it is in the cases $n=6$ and $n=8$.[^o10]
