# Epicyclic frequency

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Epicyclic frequency"}

In [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), particularly the study of [accretion disks](wikipedia://en/48400112), the **epicyclic frequency** is the frequency at which a radially displaced [fluid parcel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_parcel) will oscillate. It can be referred to as a "[Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Strutt,_3rd_Baron_Rayleigh) discriminant". When considering an astrophysical disc with differential rotation $\Omega$, the epicyclic frequency $\kappa$ is given by

:::

$\kappa^{2} \equiv \frac{2 \Omega}{R}\frac{d}{dR}(R^2 \Omega)$, where R is the radial co-ordinate.[^o2]

This quantity can be used to examine the 'boundaries' of an accretion disc: when $\kappa^{2}$ becomes negative, then small perturbations to the (assumed circular) orbit of a fluid parcel will become unstable, and the disc will develop an 'edge' at that point. For example, around a [Schwarzschild black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_black_hole), the [innermost stable circular orbit](wikipedia://en/49203606) (ISCO) occurs at three times the [event horizon](wikipedia://en/29320146), at $6GM/c^{2}$.

For a Keplerian disk, $\kappa = \Omega$.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

An astrophysical disk can be modeled as a fluid with negligible mass compared to the central object (e.g. a star) and with negligible pressure. We can suppose an [axial symmetry](wikipedia://en/6025673) such that $\Phi (r,z) = \Phi (r,-z)$. Starting from the equations of movement in [cylindrical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates) :

$$\begin{aligned} \ddot r - r \dot \theta^2 &= -\partial_r \Phi \\r \ddot \theta + 2 \dot r\dot\theta &= 0 \\ \ddot z &= -\partial_z \Phi \end{aligned}$$

The second line implies that the [specific angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_angular_momentum) is conserved. We can then define an effective potential $\Phi_{\text{eff}} = \Phi - \frac {1}{2} r^2\dot\theta^2 = \Phi + \frac{h^2}{2r^2}$ and so :

$$\begin{aligned}\ddot r &= -\partial_r \Phi_{\text{eff}}\\ \ddot z &= - \partial_z \Phi_{\text{eff}}\end{aligned}$$

We can apply a small perturbation $\delta\vec r = \delta r \vec e_r + \delta z \vec e_z$ to the [circular orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_orbit) :

$$\vec r = r_0 \vec e_r + \delta \vec r$$

So,

$$\ddot{\vec r} + \delta \ddot{\vec r} = -\vec \nabla \Phi_{\text{eff}}(\vec r + \delta \vec r)\approx-\vec \nabla \Phi_{\text{eff}} (\vec r) - \partial_r^2 \Phi_{\text{eff}}(\vec r)\delta r - \partial_z^2 \Phi_{\text{eff}}(\vec r)\delta z$$

And thus :

$$\begin{aligned} \delta \ddot r &= - \partial_r^2 \Phi_{\text{eff}} \delta r = -\Omega_r^2 \delta r\\\delta \ddot z &= - \partial_r^2 \Phi_{\text{eff}} \delta z = -\Omega_z^2 \delta z\end{aligned}$$

We then note

$$\kappa^2 = \Omega_r^2 = \partial_r^2\Phi_{\text{eff}} = \partial_r^2\Phi + \frac{3 h^2}{r^4}$$

In a circular orbit $h_c^2=r^3 \partial_r \Phi$. Thus :

$$\kappa^2 = \partial_r^2\Phi + \frac{3}{r}\partial_r \Phi$$

The frequency of a circular orbit is $\Omega_c^2 = \frac 1r \partial_r \Phi$ which finally yields :

$$\kappa^2=4\Omega_c^2 + 2r\Omega_c \frac{d\Omega_c}{dr}$$
