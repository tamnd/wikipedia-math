# Elementary topos

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Elementary topos"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **elementary topos** (plural *toposes* or *topoi*[^n1]) is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) which has properties making it resemble the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829). Elementary toposes can be used as [models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28mathematical_logic%29) of [intuitionistic](wikipedia://en/169262) [higher-order logic](wikipedia://en/562883).

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

An elementary topos (hereafter just topos) can be pictured as an alternate mathematical universe. It is a category which is sufficiently like the category of sets (the “standard mathematical universe”) that common mathematical constructions can be carried in it, such as subsets, [function spaces](wikipedia://en/340630), etc.

More precisely, a topos supports [intuitionistic](wikipedia://en/169262) [higher-order logic](wikipedia://en/562883) as an [internal language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_language)[^n2]. As such, toposes are widely used as models of [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics).

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

As originally defined by [Lawvere](wikipedia://en/872760) and [Tierney](wikipedia://en/51793007), an elementary topos is a category $\mathcal{E}$ such that:[^n3][^n4]

- $\mathcal{E}$ is [finitely complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_complete_category) (it has all finite [limits](wikipedia://en/39248)). Equivalently, it has a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object), [binary products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_product_%28category_theory%29), and [binary equalizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equalizer_%28mathematics%29#In_category_theory).
- $\mathcal{E}$ is [finitely cocomplete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_cocomplete_category) (it has all finite [colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit_%28category_theory%29)). Equivalently, it has an [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object), binary [coproducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coproduct_%28category_theory%29) and binary [coequalizers](wikipedia://en/431940).
- $\mathcal{E}$ has [exponential objects](wikipedia://en/2237858) (in addition to binary products, which are required for defining exponential objects). Together with having a terminal object and binary products, this means that $\mathcal{E}$ is [Cartesian closed](wikipedia://en/237037).
- $\mathcal{E}$ has a [subobject classifier](wikipedia://en/366909).

The requirement that $\mathcal{E}$ be finitely cocomplete was later observed to be redundant, as it follows from the rest.[^n5][^n6][^n7] (However, the general construction of finite colimits is relatively complicated; in most examples, one can find a more concrete description, which simplifies the translation of statements from the internal language.)

An even more minimalistic definition is possible: an elementary topos is a [finitely complete category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_complete_category) with [power objects](wikipedia://en/12665139).[^n8] (This essentially replaces the requirements to have exponentials $A^B$ and a subobject classifier $\Omega$ with just the special case of exponentials $\Omega^B$.)

Many other properties follow, such as being [regular](wikipedia://en/9790950) and moreover [exact](wikipedia://en/9790950#Exact_%28effective%29_categories)[^n9][^n10].

:::

::: {#o16 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Logical functor"}

## Logical functors {#Logical_functors}

A *logical functor* is a functor between topoi that preserves finite limits and power objects. Logical functors preserve the structures that topoi have. In particular, they preserve finite colimits, [subobject classifiers](wikipedia://en/366909), and [exponential objects](wikipedia://en/2237858).[^o17]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Having a subobject classifier is a strong requirement, which rules out most categories of algebraic structures such as [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), etc.

Every [Grothendieck topos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_topos) (a category equivalent to the category of sheaves on a [site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_%28mathematics%29)) is an elementary topos. Important special cases include:

- For any [small category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category) $\mathcal{C}$, the [presheaf category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presheaf_category) $\operatorname{Set}^{\mathcal{C}^\operatorname{op}}$.
- As a further special case of the previous example, for any [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$, the category of $G$-sets, i.e., sets equipped with an [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of $G$, with equivariant maps as morphisms.
- For any [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$, the category of [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) on $X$.
- The [classifying topos](wikipedia://en/39651142) for a geometric theory.

The category of finite sets is an elementary topos[^n11] (however, it lacks a [natural numbers object](wikipedia://en/918609)), and similarly for finite $G$-sets.

The [effective topos](wikipedia://en/30939514) is an important example of an elementary topos with natural numbers object. It is not a Grothendieck topos. It can be viewed as a universe of [computable](wikipedia://en/155414) mathematics. This example is generalized by [realizability toposes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realizability_topos).

A source of examples is the [fundamental theorem of topos theory](wikipedia://en/50176405), which states that for every elementary topos $\mathcal{E}$ and every object $\Gamma \in \mathcal{E}$, the [slice category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slice_category) $\mathcal{E}/\Gamma$ is an elementary topos.
