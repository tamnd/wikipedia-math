# Richard K. Guy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Richard K. Guy"}

**Richard Kenneth Guy** (30 September 1916 – 9 March 2020) was a British mathematician. He was a professor in the Department of [Mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at the [University of Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Calgary).[^o3] He is known for his work in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [recreational mathematics](wikipedia://en/26446), [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401).[^o4][^o5] He is best known for co-authorship (with [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and [Elwyn Berlekamp](wikipedia://en/655893)) of *[Winning Ways for your Mathematical Plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winning_Ways_for_your_Mathematical_Plays)* and authorship of *Unsolved Problems in Number Theory*.[^o6] He published more than 300 scholarly articles.[^o7] Guy proposed the partially tongue-in-cheek "[strong law of small numbers](wikipedia://en/18093481)", which says there are not enough small integers available for the many tasks assigned to them – thus explaining many coincidences and patterns found among numerous cultures.[^o8] For this paper he received the MAA [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_R._Halmos_–_Lester_R._Ford_Award) in 1989.[^o9]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

### Early life {#Early_life}

Guy was born 30 September 1916 in [Nuneaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuneaton), [Warwickshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warwickshire), England, to Adeline Augusta Tanner and William Alexander Charles Guy. Both of his parents were teachers, rising to the rank of headmistress and headmaster, respectively. He attended [Warwick School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warwick_School) for Boys, the third oldest school in Britain, but was not enthusiastic about most of the curriculum. He was good at sports and excelled in mathematics. At the age of 17 he read [Dickson](wikipedia://en/1790247)'s *[History of the Theory of Numbers](wikipedia://en/37174589)*. He said it was better than "the whole works of Shakespeare", solidifying his lifelong interest in mathematics.[^o12]

In 1935 Guy entered [Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge), as a result of winning several scholarships. To win the most important of these he had to travel to Cambridge and write exams for two days. His interest in games began while at Cambridge where he became an avid composer of [chess problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_problem).[^o13] In 1938, he was graduated with a [second-class honours degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_undergraduate_degree_classification); he would later state that his failure to get a first may have been related to his obsession with chess.[^o14] Although his parents strongly advised against it, Guy decided to become a teacher and got a teaching diploma at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham). He met his future wife, Nancy Louise Thirian, through her brother Michael, who was a fellow scholarship winner at Gonville and Caius. He and Louise shared loves of mountain climbing and dancing. They married in December 1940.

### War years {#War_years}

In November 1942, Guy received an emergency commission in the Meteorological Branch of the [Royal Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Air_Force), with the rank of [flight lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_lieutenant). He was posted to [Reykjavík](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reykjavík), and later to [Bermuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermuda), as a [meteorologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorologist). He tried to get permission for Louise to join him but was refused. While in Iceland, he did some glacier travel, skiing, and mountain climbing, marking the beginning of another long love affair, this one with snow and ice.[^o17] When Guy returned to England after the war, he went back to teaching, this time at [Stockport Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockport_Grammar_School), but stayed only two years. In 1947 the family moved to London, where he got a job teaching mathematics at [Goldsmiths' College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldsmiths,_University_of_London).[^o18]

### Later life and death {#Later_life_and_death}

In 1951 he moved to Singapore, where he taught at the [University of Malaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Malaya) until 1962. He then spent a few years at the [Indian Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Institute_of_Technology) in [Delhi, India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi,_India). While they were in India, he and Louise went mountaineering in the foothills of the [Himalayas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayas).[^o20] Guy moved to Canada in 1965, settling down at the [University of Calgary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Calgary) in Alberta, where he obtained a professorship.[^o21][^o22] Although he officially retired in 1982, he still went to the office five days a week to work, even as he passed the age of 100.[^o23] Along with George Thomas and [John Selfridge](wikipedia://en/995084), Guy taught at [Canada/USA Mathcamp](wikipedia://en/1786983) during its early years.[^o24]

In 1991 the University of Calgary awarded him an [honorary doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_degree). Guy said that they gave him the degree out of embarrassment, although the university stated that "his extensive research efforts and prolific writings in the field of number theory and combinatorics have added much to the underpinnings of game theory and its extensive application to many forms of human activity."[^o25] Guy and his wife Louise (who died in 2010) remained very committed to mountain hiking and environmentalism even in their later years. In 2014, he donated \$100,000 to the [Alpine Club of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_Club_of_Canada) for the training of amateur leaders.[^o26] In turn, the Alpine Club has honoured them by building the *Louise and Richard Guy Hut* near the base of [Mont des Poilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mont_des_Poilus).[^o27] They had three children, among them computer scientist and mathematician [Michael J. T. Guy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_J._T._Guy).

Guy died on 9 March 2020 at the age of 103.[^o28][^o29]

## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

I love mathematics so much, and I love anybody who can do it well, so I just like to hang on and try to copy them as best I can, even though I'm not really in their league.[^o31]

While teaching in Singapore in 1960 Guy met the Hungarian mathematician [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284). Erdős was noted for posing and solving difficult mathematical problems and shared several of them with Guy.[^o32] Guy later recalled "I made some progress in each of them. This gave me encouragement, and I began to think of myself as possibly being something of a research mathematician, which I hadn't done before."[^o33] Eventually he wrote four papers with Erdős, giving him an [Erdős number](wikipedia://en/9742) of 1,[^o34] and solved one of Erdős' problems.[^o35] Guy was intrigued by unsolved problems and wrote two books devoted to them.[^o36][^o37] Many number theorists got their start trying to solve problems from Guy's book *Unsolved problems in number theory*.[^o38]

Guy described himself as an amateur mathematician,[^o39] although his work was widely respected by professionals.[^o40] In a career that spans eight decades he wrote or co-authored more than a dozen books and collaborated with some of the most important mathematicians of the twentieth century.[^o41] [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284), [John H. Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_H._Conway), [Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095), and [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) were among his collaborators, as were [Elwyn Berlekamp](wikipedia://en/655893), [John L. Selfridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Selfridge), [Kenneth Falconer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Falconer_%28mathematician%29), [Frank Harary](wikipedia://en/1731731), [Lee Sallows](wikipedia://en/32308380), [Gerhard Ringel](wikipedia://en/1571968), [Béla Bollobás](wikipedia://en/614956), [C. B. Lacampagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._B._Lacampagne), [Bruce Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Sagan), and [Neil Sloane](wikipedia://en/400062).[^o42]

Over the course of his career Guy published more than 100 research papers in mathematics, including four with Erdős.[^o43][^o44][^o45][^o46][^o47]

Guy was influential in the field of [recreational mathematics](wikipedia://en/26446). He collaborated with Berlekamp and Conway on two volumes of *Winning Ways*, which [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) described in 1998 as "the greatest contribution to recreational mathematics in this century".[^o48][^o49] Guy was considered briefly as a replacement for Gardner when the latter retired from the Mathematical Games column at *Scientific American*.[^o50] Guy conducted extensive research on [Conway's Game of Life](wikipedia://en/37035), and in 1970, discovered [the game's glider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glider_%28Conway's_Life%29).[^o51][^o52] Around 1968, Guy discovered a [unistable polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unistable_polyhedron) with 19 faces; no such construct with fewer faces was found until 2012. As of 2016 Guy still was active in conducting mathematical work.[^o53] To mark his 100th birthday friends and colleagues organised a celebration of his life and a tribute song and video was released by [Gathering 4 Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gathering_4_Gardner).[^o54]

Guy was one of the original directors of the [Number Theory Foundation](wikipedia://en/13053993) and played an active role in supporting their efforts to "foster a spirit of cooperation and goodwill among the family of number theorists" for more than twenty years.[^o55][^o56]

## Chess problems {#Chess_problems}

From 1947 to 1951 Guy was the endings editor for *[British Chess Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Chess_Magazine)*.[^o58] He is known for almost 200 [endgame studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame_studies). Along with [Hugh Blandford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Blandford) and [John Roycroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Roycroft), he is one of the inventors of the [GBR code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GBR_code) (Guy–Blandford–Roycroft code), a system of representing the position of chess pieces on a chessboard. Publications including *[EG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EG_%28magazine%29)* use it to classify endgame types and to index endgame studies.[^o59]
