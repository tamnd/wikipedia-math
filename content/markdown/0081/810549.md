# Indecomposable module

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Indecomposable module"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) is **indecomposable** if it is non-zero and cannot be written as a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of two non-zero [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule).[^n1][^n2]

:::

Indecomposable is a weaker notion than [simple module](wikipedia://en/29430) (which is also sometimes called [irreducible](wikipedia://en/1001293) module): simple means "no proper submodule" *N* \< *M*, while indecomposable "not expressible as *N* ⊕ *P* = *M*".

A direct sum of indecomposables is called **completely decomposable**; this is weaker than being [semisimple](wikipedia://en/1001361), which is a direct sum of [simple modules](wikipedia://en/29430).

A direct sum decomposition of a module into indecomposable modules is called an [indecomposable decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indecomposable_decomposition).

## Motivation {#Motivation}

In many situations, all modules of interest are completely decomposable; the indecomposable modules can then be thought of as the "basic building blocks", the only objects that need to be studied. This is the case for modules over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) or [PID](wikipedia://en/25039), and underlies [Jordan normal form](wikipedia://en/312877) of [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator).

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Field {#Field}

Modules over [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) are [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370).[^n3] A vector space is indecomposable if and only if its [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) is 1. So every vector space is completely decomposable (indeed, semisimple), with infinitely many summands if the dimension is infinite.[^n4]

### Principal ideal domain {#Principal_ideal_domain}

Finitely-generated modules over [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039) (PIDs) are classified by the [structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/9902787): the primary decomposition is a decomposition into indecomposable modules, so every finitely-generated module over a PID is completely decomposable.

Explicitly, the modules of the form *R*/*p*<sup>*n*</sup> for [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) *p* (including *p* = 0, which yields *R*) are indecomposable. Every finitely-generated *R*-module is a direct sum of these. Note that this is simple if and only if *n* = 1 (or *p* = 0); for example, the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 4, **Z**/4, is indecomposable but not simple – it has the subgroup 2**Z**/4 of order 2, but this does not have a complement.

Over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) **Z**, modules are [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974). A finitely-generated abelian group is indecomposable if and only if it is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to **Z** or to a [factor group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_group) of the form **Z**/*p*<sup>*n*</sup>**Z** for some [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* and some positive integer *n*. Every [finitely-generated abelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely-generated_abelian_group) is a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/106341) of (finitely many) indecomposable abelian groups.

There are, however, other indecomposable abelian groups which are not finitely generated; examples are the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q** and the [Prüfer *p*-groups](wikipedia://en/4885031) **Z**(*p*<sup>∞</sup>) for any prime number *p*.

For a fixed positive integer *n*, consider the ring *R* of *n*-by-*n* [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with entries from the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) (or from any other field *K*). Then *K*<sup>*n*</sup> is a left *R*-module (the scalar multiplication is [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280)). This is [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) the only indecomposable module over *R*. Every left *R*-module is a direct sum of (finitely or infinitely many) copies of this module *K*<sup>*n*</sup>.

:::

## Facts {#Facts}

Every [simple module](wikipedia://en/29430) is indecomposable. The converse is not true in general, as is shown by the second example above.

By looking at the [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) of a module, one can tell whether the module is indecomposable: if and only if the endomorphism ring does not contain an [idempotent element](wikipedia://en/361940#Role_in_decompositions) different from 0 and 1.[^n1] (If *f* is such an [idempotent endomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent_endomorphism) of *M*, then *M* is the direct sum of ker(*f*) and im(*f*).)

A module of finite [length](wikipedia://en/816070) is indecomposable if and only if its endomorphism ring is [local](wikipedia://en/153391). Still more information about endomorphisms of finite-length indecomposables is provided by the [Fitting lemma](wikipedia://en/794492).

In the finite-length situation, decomposition into indecomposables is particularly useful, because of the [Krull–Schmidt theorem](wikipedia://en/21100715): every finite-length module can be written as a direct sum of finitely many indecomposable modules, and this decomposition is essentially unique (meaning that if you have a different decomposition into indecomposables, then the summands of the first decomposition can be paired off with the summands of the second decomposition so that the members of each pair are isomorphic).[^n5]
