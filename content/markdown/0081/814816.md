# Newtonian potential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Newtonian potential"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Newtonian potential**, or **Newton potential**, is an [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) in [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640) that acts as the inverse to the negative [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) on functions that are smooth and decay rapidly enough at infinity. As such, it is a fundamental object of study in [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342). In its general nature, it is a [singular integral operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_integral_operator), defined by [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) with a function having a [mathematical singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) at the origin, the Newtonian kernel $\Gamma$ which is the [fundamental solution](wikipedia://en/1820858) of the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation). It is named for [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), who first discovered it and proved that it was a [harmonic function](wikipedia://en/55762) in the [special case of three variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green's_function_for_the_three-variable_Laplace_equation), where it served as the fundamental [gravitational potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential) in [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation). In modern potential theory, the Newtonian potential is instead thought of as an [electrostatic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_potential).

:::

The Newtonian potential of a [compactly supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) [integrable function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) $f$ is defined as the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519)

$$u(x) = \Gamma * f(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \Gamma(x-y)f(y)\,dy$$

where the Newtonian kernel $\Gamma$ in dimension $d$ is defined by

$$\Gamma(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2\pi} \log{ | x | }, & d=2, \\ \frac{1}{d(2-d)\omega_d} | x | ^{2-d}, & d \neq 2. \end{cases}$$

Here $\omega_d$ is the volume of the unit [*d*-ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N_sphere) (sometimes sign conventions may vary; compare (Evans 1998) and (Gilbarg Trudinger 1983)). For example, for $d = 3$ we have $\Gamma(x) = -1/(4\pi |x|)$.

The Newtonian potential $w$ of $f$ is a solution of the [Poisson equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_equation)

$$\Delta w = f,$$

which is to say that the operation of taking the Newtonian potential of a function is a partial inverse to the Laplace operator. Then $w$ will be a classical solution, that is twice differentiable, if $f$ is bounded and locally [Hölder continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_continuous) as shown by [Otto Hölder](wikipedia://en/191652). It was an open question whether continuity alone is also sufficient. This was shown to be wrong by [Henrik Petrini](wikipedia://en/66494018) who gave an example of a continuous $f$ for which $w$ is not twice differentiable. The solution is not unique, since addition of any harmonic function to $w$ will not affect the equation. This fact can be used to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to the [Dirichlet problem](wikipedia://en/571109) for the Poisson equation in suitably regular domains, and for suitably well-behaved functions $f$: one first applies a Newtonian potential to obtain a solution, and then adjusts by adding a harmonic function to get the correct boundary data.

The Newtonian potential is defined more broadly as the convolution

$$\Gamma*\mu(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\Gamma(x-y) \, d\mu(y)$$

when $\mu$ is a compactly supported [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933). It satisfies the Poisson equation

$$\Delta w = \mu$$

in the sense of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29). Moreover, when the measure is [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_measure), the Newtonian potential is [subharmonic](wikipedia://en/3237359) on $\mathbb{R}^d$.

If $f$ is a compactly supported [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) (or, more generally, a finite measure) that is [rotationally invariant](wikipedia://en/39789), then the convolution of $f$ with $\Gamma$ satisfies for $x$ outside the support of $f$

$$f*\Gamma(x) =\lambda \Gamma(x),\quad \lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(y)\,dy.$$

In dimension $d=3$, this reduces to Newton's theorem that the potential energy of a small mass outside a much larger spherically symmetric mass distribution is the same as if all of the mass of the larger object were concentrated at its center.

When the measure $\mu$ is associated to a mass distribution on a sufficiently smooth hypersurface $S$ (a [Lyapunov surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyapunov_surface) of [Hölder class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_space) $C^{1,\alpha}$) that divides $\mathbb{R}^d$ into two regions $D_+$ and $D_-$, then the Newtonian potential of $\mu$ is referred to as a **simple layer potential**. Simple layer potentials are continuous and solve the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation) except on $S$. They appear naturally in the study of [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics) in the context of the [electrostatic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_potential) associated to a charge distribution on a closed surface. If $\mathrm{d}\mu=f\mathrm{d}H$ is the product of a continuous function on $S$ with the $(d-1)$-dimensional [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109), then at a point $y$ of $S$, the [normal derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_derivative) undergoes a jump discontinuity $f(y)$ when crossing the layer. Furthermore, the normal derivative of $w$ is a well-defined continuous function on $S$. This makes simple layers particularly suited to the study of the [Neumann problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neumann_problem) for the Laplace equation.
