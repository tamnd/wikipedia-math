# Length of a module

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Length of a module"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the **length** of a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over a ring $R$ is a generalization of the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) which measures its size.[^o2] <sup>page 153</sup> It is defined to be the length of the longest chain of [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule). For vector spaces (modules over a field), the length equals the dimension. If $R$ is an algebra over a field $k$, the length of a module is at most its dimension as a $k$-vector space.

:::

In [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a module over a [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) commutative ring $R$ can have finite length only when the module has [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) zero. Modules of finite length are [finitely generated modules](wikipedia://en/326430), but most finitely generated modules have infinite length. Modules of finite length are [Artinian modules](wikipedia://en/542336) and are fundamental to the theory of [Artinian rings](wikipedia://en/489273).

The [degree of an algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/2776558) inside an affine or projective space is the length of the coordinate ring of the zero-dimensional intersection of the variety with a [generic](wikipedia://en/383736) linear subspace of complementary dimension. More generally, the [intersection multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_multiplicity) of several varieties is defined as the length of the coordinate ring of the zero-dimensional intersection.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Length of a module {#Length_of_a_module}

Let $M$ be a (left or right) module over some [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$. Given a chain of submodules of $M$ of the form

$$M_0 \subsetneq M_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq M_n,$$

one says that $n$ is the *length* of the chain.[^o2] The *length* of $M$ is the largest length of any of its chains. If no such largest length exists, we say that $M$ has *infinite length*. Clearly, if the length of a chain equals the length of the module, one has $M_0=0$ and $M_n=M.$

### Length of a ring {#Length_of_a_ring}

The length of a ring $R$ is the length of the longest chain of [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977); that is, the length of $R$ considered as a module over itself by left multiplication. By contrast, the [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) of $R$ is the length of the longest chain of [*prime* ideals](wikipedia://en/24928).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Finite length and finite modules {#Finite_length_and_finite_modules}

If an $R$-module $M$ has finite length, then it is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430).[^o10] If *R* is a field, then the converse is also true.

### Relation to Artinian and Noetherian modules {#Relation_to_Artinian_and_Noetherian_modules}

An $R$-module $M$ has finite length if and only if it is both a [Noetherian module](wikipedia://en/542431) and an [Artinian module](wikipedia://en/542336)[^o2] (cf. [Hopkins' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopkins'_theorem)). Since all Artinian rings are Noetherian, this implies that a ring has finite length if and only if it is Artinian.

### Behavior with respect to short exact sequences {#Behavior_with_respect_to_short_exact_sequences}

Suppose

$$0\rightarrow L \rightarrow M \rightarrow N \rightarrow 0$$

is a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of $R$-modules. Then M has finite length if and only if *L* and *N* have finite length, and we have

$$\text{length}_R(M) = \text{length}_R(L) + \text{length}_R(N)$$

In particular, it implies the following two properties

- The direct sum of two modules of finite length has finite length
- The submodule of a module with finite length has finite length, and its length is less than or equal to its parent module.

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Jordan–Hölder theorem"}

### Jordan–Hölder theorem {#Jordan–Hölder_theorem}

A [composition series](wikipedia://en/297493) of the module *M* is a chain of the form

$$0=N_0\subsetneq N_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq N_n=M$$

such that

$$N_{i+1}/N_i \text{ is simple for }i=0,\dots,n-1$$

A module *M* has finite length if and only if it has a (finite) composition series, and the length of every such composition series is equal to the length of *M*.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Finite dimensional vector spaces {#Finite_dimensional_vector_spaces}

Any finite dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $k$ has a finite length. Given a basis $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ there is the chain

$$0 \subset \text{Span}_k(v_1) \subset \text{Span}_k(v_1,v_2) \subset \cdots \subset \text{Span}_k(v_1,\ldots, v_n) = V$$

which is of length $n$. It is maximal because given any chain,

$$V_0 \subset \cdots \subset V_m$$

the dimension of each inclusion will increase by at least $1$. Therefore, its length and dimension coincide.

### Artinian modules {#Artinian_modules}

Over a base ring $R$, [Artinian modules](wikipedia://en/542336) form a class of examples of finite modules. In fact, these examples serve as the basic tools for defining the order of vanishing in [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602).[^o19]

#### Zero module {#Zero_module}

The zero module is the only one with length 0.

#### Simple modules {#Simple_modules}

Modules with length 1 are precisely the [simple modules](wikipedia://en/29430).

#### Artinian modules over Z {#Artinian_modules_over_Z}

The length of the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (viewed as a module over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) **Z**) is equal to the number of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) factors of $n$, with multiple prime factors counted multiple times. This follows from the fact that the submodules of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are in one to one correspondence with the positive divisors of $n$, this correspondence resulting itself from the fact that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [principal ideal ring](wikipedia://en/6961771).

:::

## Use in multiplicity theory {#Use_in_multiplicity_theory}

For the needs of [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602), [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) introduced a general notion of the [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) of a point, as the length of an [Artinian local ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artinian_local_ring) related to this point.

The first application was a complete definition of the [intersection multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_multiplicity), and, in particular, a statement of [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217) that asserts that the sum of the multiplicities of the intersection points of $n$ [algebraic hypersurfaces](wikipedia://en/525101) in a $n$-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) is either infinite or is *exactly* the product of the degrees of the hypersurfaces.

This definition of multiplicity is quite general, and contains as special cases most of previous notions of algebraic multiplicity.

### Order of vanishing of zeros and poles {#Order_of_vanishing_of_zeros_and_poles}

A special case of this general definition of a multiplicity is the order of vanishing of a non-zero algebraic function $f \in R(X)^*$ on an algebraic variety. Given an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $X$ and a [subvariety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subvariety_%28mathematics%29) $V$ of [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) 1[^o19] the order of vanishing for a polynomial $f \in R(X)$ is defined as[^o25]

$$\operatorname{ord}_V(f) = \text{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{V,X}}\left( \frac{\mathcal{O}_{V,X}}{(f)} \right)$$

where $\mathcal{O}_{V,X}$ is the local ring defined by the stalk of $\mathcal{O}_X$ along the subvariety $V$[^o19] <sup>pages 426-227</sup>, or, equivalently, the [stalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalk_of_a_sheaf) of $\mathcal{O}_X$ at the generic point of $V$[^o26] <sup>page 22</sup>. If $X$ is an [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468), and $V$ is defined the by vanishing locus $V(f)$, then there is the isomorphism

$$\mathcal{O}_{V,X} \cong R(X)_{(f)}$$

This idea can then be extended to [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) $F = f/g$ on the variety $X$ where the order is defined as[^o19]

$$\operatorname{ord}_V(F) := \operatorname{ord}_V(f) - \operatorname{ord}_V(g)$$

which is similar to defining the order of zeros and poles in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759).

#### Example on a projective variety {#Example_on_a_projective_variety}

For example, consider a [projective surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_surface) $Z(h) \subset \mathbb{P}^3$ defined by a polynomial $h \in k[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]$, then the order of vanishing of a rational function

$$F = \frac{f}{g}$$

is given by

$$\operatorname{ord}_{Z(h)}(F) = \operatorname{ord}_{Z(h)}(f) - \operatorname{ord}_{Z(h)}(g)$$

where

$$\operatorname{ord}_{Z(h)}(f) = \text{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{Z(h),\mathbb{P}^3}}\left( \frac{\mathcal{O}_{Z(h),\mathbb{P}^3}}{(f)} \right)$$

For example, if $h = x_0^3 + x_1^3 + x_2^3 + x_2^3$ and $f = x^2 + y^2$ and $g = h^2(x_0 + x_1 - x_3)$ then

$$\operatorname{ord}_{Z(h)}(f) = \text{length}_{\mathcal{O}_{Z(h),\mathbb{P}^3}}\left( \frac{\mathcal{O}_{Z(h),\mathbb{P}^3}}{(x^2 + y^2)} \right) = 0$$

since $x^2 + y^2$ is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in the [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) $\mathcal{O}_{Z(h),\mathbb{P}^3}$. In the other case, $x_0 + x_1 - x_3$ is a unit, so the quotient module is isomorphic to

$$\frac{\mathcal{O}_{Z(h), \mathbb{P}^3}}{(h^2)}$$

so it has length $2$. This can be found using the maximal proper sequence

$$(0) \subset \frac{\mathcal{O}_{Z(h), \mathbb{P}^3}}{(h)} \subset \frac{\mathcal{O}_{Z(h), \mathbb{P}^3}}{(h^2)}$$

#### Zero and poles of an analytic function {#Zero_and_poles_of_an_analytic_function}

The order of vanishing is a generalization of the order of zeros and poles for [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759). For example, the function

$$\frac{(z-1)^3(z-2)}{(z-1)(z-4i)}$$

has zeros of order 2 and 1 at $1, 2 \in \mathbb{C}$ and a pole of order $1$ at $4i \in \mathbb{C}$. This kind of information can be encoded using the length of modules. For example, setting $R(X) = \mathbb{C}[z]$ and $V = V(z-1)$, there is the associated local ring $\mathcal{O}_{V,X}$ is $\mathbb{C}[z]_{(z-1)}$ and the quotient module

$$\frac{\mathbb{C}[z]_{(z-1)}}{((z-4i)(z-1)^2)}$$

Note that $z-4i$ is a unit, so this is isomorphic to the quotient module

$$\frac{\mathbb{C}[z]_{(z-1)}}{((z-1)^2)}$$

Its length is $2$ since there is the maximal chain

$$(0) \subset \frac{\mathbb{C}[z]_{(z-1)}}{((z-1))} \subset {\displaystyle {\frac {\mathbb {C} [z]_{(z-1)}}{((z-1)^{2})}}}$$

of submodules.[^o29] More generally, using the [Weierstrass factorization theorem](wikipedia://en/1585155) a meromorphic function factors as

$$F = \frac{f}{g}$$

which is a (possibly infinite) product of linear polynomials in both the numerator and denominator.
