# Divergence (statistics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Divergence"}

In [information geometry](wikipedia://en/487312), a **divergence** is a kind of [statistical distance](wikipedia://en/5187620): a [binary function](wikipedia://en/3943) which establishes the separation from one [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) to another on a [statistical manifold](wikipedia://en/36408395).

:::

The simplest divergence is [squared Euclidean distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squared_Euclidean_distance) (SED), and divergences can be viewed as generalizations of SED. The other most important divergence is [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy) (also called [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052)), which is central to [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773). There are numerous other specific divergences and classes of divergences, notably [*f*-divergences](wikipedia://en/15224289) and [Bregman divergences](wikipedia://en/4491248) (see ).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) $M$ of dimension $n$, a **divergence** on $M$ is a $C^2$-function $D: M\times M\to [0, \infty)$ satisfying:[^n1][^n2]

1. $D(p, q) \geq 0$ for all $p, q \in M$ (non-negativity),
1. $D(p, q) = 0$ if and only if $p=q$ (positivity),
1. At every point $p\in M$, $D(p, p+dp)$ is a positive-definite [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) for infinitesimal displacements $dp$ from $p$.

In applications to statistics, the manifold $M$ is typically the space of parameters of a [parametric family of probability distributions](wikipedia://en/19653337).

Condition 3 means that $D$ defines an inner product on the tangent space $T_pM$ for every $p\in M$. Since $D$ is $C^2$ on $M$, this defines a Riemannian metric $g$ on $M$.

Locally at $p\in M$, we may construct a local [coordinate chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_chart) with coordinates $x$, then the divergence is

$$D(x(p), x(p) + dx) = \textstyle\frac{1}{2} dx^T g_p(x) dx + O(|dx|^3)$$

where $g_p(x)$ is a matrix of size $n\times n$. It is the Riemannian metric at point $p$ expressed in coordinates $x$.

[Dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267) of condition 3 shows that divergence has the dimension of squared distance.[^n2]

The **dual divergence** $D^*$ is defined as

$$D^*(p, q) = D(q, p).$$

When we wish to contrast $D$ against $D^*$, we refer to $D$ as **primal divergence**.

Given any divergence $D$, its symmetrized version is obtained by averaging it with its dual divergence:[^n2]

$$D_S(p, q) = \textstyle\frac{1}{2}\big(D(p,q) + D(q, p)\big).$$

### Difference from other similar concepts {#Difference_from_other_similar_concepts}

Unlike [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), divergences are not required to be symmetric, and the asymmetry is important in applications.[^n2] Accordingly, one often refers asymmetrically to the divergence "of *q* from *p*" or "from *p* to *q*", rather than "between *p* and *q*". Secondly, divergences generalize *squared* distance, not linear distance, and thus do not satisfy the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941), but some divergences (such as the [Bregman divergence](wikipedia://en/4491248#Properties)) do satisfy generalizations of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).

In general statistics and probability, "divergence" generally refers to any kind of function $D(p, q)$, where $p, q$ are probability distributions or other objects under consideration, such that conditions 1, 2 are satisfied. Condition 3 is required for "divergence" as used in information geometry.

As an example, the [total variation distance](wikipedia://en/683570), a commonly used statistical divergence, does not satisfy condition 3.

:::

## Notation {#Notation}

Notation for divergences varies significantly between fields, though there are some conventions.

Divergences are generally notated with an uppercase 'D', as in $D(x, y)$, to distinguish them from metric distances, which are notated with a lowercase 'd'. When multiple divergences are in use, they are commonly distinguished with subscripts, as in $D_\text{KL}$ for [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) (KL divergence).

Often a different separator between parameters is used, particularly to emphasize the asymmetry. In [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), a double bar is commonly used: $D(p \parallel q)$; this is similar to, but distinct from, the notation for [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134), $P(A | B)$, and emphasizes interpreting the divergence as a relative measurement, as in [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy); this notation is common for the KL divergence. A colon may be used instead, as $D(p : q)$; this emphasizes the relative information supporting the two distributions.

The notation for parameters varies as well. Uppercase $P, Q$ interprets the parameters as probability distributions, while lowercase $p, q$ or $x, y$ interprets them geometrically as points in a space, and $\mu_1, \mu_2$ or $m_1, m_2$ interprets them as measures.

:::

## Geometrical properties {#Geometrical_properties}

Many properties of divergences can be derived if we restrict *S* to be a statistical manifold, meaning that it can be parametrized with a finite-dimensional coordinate system *θ*, so that for a distribution *p* ∈ *S* we can write *p* = *p*(*θ*).

For a pair of points *p*, *q* ∈ *S* with coordinates *θ*<sub>*p*</sub> and *θ*<sub>*q*</sub>, denote the partial derivatives of *D*(*p*, *q*) as

$$\begin{aligned} D((\partial_i)_p, q) \ \ &\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^i_p} D(p, q), \\ D((\partial_i\partial_j)_p, (\partial_k)_q) \ \ &\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ \ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^i_p} \tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^j_p}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta^k_q}D(p, q), \ \ \mathrm{etc.} \end{aligned}$$

Now we restrict these functions to a diagonal *p* = *q*, and denote [^o13]

$$\begin{aligned} D[\partial_i, \cdot]\ &:\ p \mapsto D((\partial_i)_p, p), \\ D[\partial_i, \partial_j]\ &:\ p \mapsto D((\partial_i)_p, (\partial_j)_p),\ \ \mathrm{etc.} \end{aligned}$$

By definition, the function *D*(*p*, *q*) is minimized at *p* = *q*, and therefore

$$\begin{aligned} & D[\partial_i, \cdot] = D[\cdot, \partial_i] = 0, \\ & D[\partial_i\partial_j, \cdot] = D[\cdot, \partial_i\partial_j] = -D[\partial_i, \partial_j] \ \equiv\ g_{ij}^{(D)}, \end{aligned}$$

where matrix *g*<sup>(*D*)</sup> is [positive semi-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrix) and defines a unique [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) on the manifold *S*.

Divergence *D*(·, ·) also defines a unique [torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_of_connection)-free [affine connection](wikipedia://en/607690) ∇<sup>(*D*)</sup> with coefficients

$$\Gamma_{ij,k}^{(D)} = -D[\partial_i\partial_j, \partial_k],$$

and the [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_affine_connection) to this connection ∇\* is generated by the dual divergence *D*\*.

Thus, a divergence *D*(·, ·) generates on a statistical manifold a unique dualistic structure (*g*<sup>(*D*)</sup>, ∇<sup>(*D*)</sup>, ∇<sup>(*D*\*)</sup>). The converse is also true: every torsion-free dualistic structure on a statistical manifold is induced from some globally defined divergence function (which however need not be unique).[^o14]

For example, when *D* is an [f-divergence](wikipedia://en/15224289)[^o15] for some function ƒ(·), then it generates the [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) *g*<sup>(*D*<sub>*f*</sub>)</sup> = *c·g* and the connection ∇<sup>(*D*<sub>*f*</sub>)</sup> = ∇<sup>(*α*)</sup>, where *g* is the canonical [Fisher information metric](wikipedia://en/488687), ∇<sup>(*α*)</sup> is the α-connection, *c* = ƒ′′(1), and *α* = 3 + 2ƒ′′′(1)/ƒ′′(1).

::: {#o17 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The two most important divergences are the [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy) ([Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052), KL divergence), which is central to [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773) and statistics, and the [squared Euclidean distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squared_Euclidean_distance) (SED). Minimizing these two divergences is the main way that [linear inverse problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_inverse_problem) are solved, via the [principle of maximum entropy](wikipedia://en/201718) and [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359), notably in [logistic regression](wikipedia://en/226631) and [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386).[^n3]

The two most important classes of divergences are the [*f*-divergences](wikipedia://en/15224289) and [Bregman divergences](wikipedia://en/4491248); however, other types of divergence functions are also encountered in the literature. The only divergence for probabilities over a finite [alphabet](wikipedia://en/4292269) that is both an *f*-divergence and a Bregman divergence is the Kullback–Leibler divergence.[^o19] The squared Euclidean divergence is a Bregman divergence (corresponding to the function $x^2$) but not an *f*-divergence.

### f-divergences {#f-divergences}

Given a convex function $f:[0, +\infty)\to (-\infty, +\infty]$ such that $f(0) = \lim_{t\to 0^+}f(t), f(1) = 0$, the *f*-divergence generated by $f$ is defined as

$$D_f(p, q) = \int p(x)f\bigg(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\bigg) dx.$$

::: {#o21 .table cue=table}

| [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052): | $D_\mathrm{KL}(p, q) = \int p(x)\ln\left( \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx$ |
|---|---|
| squared [Hellinger distance](wikipedia://en/13035709): | $H^2(p,\, q) = 2 \int \Big( \sqrt{p(x)} - \sqrt{q(x)}\, \Big)^2 dx$ |
| [Jensen–Shannon divergence](wikipedia://en/4019573): | $D_{JS}(p, q) = \frac{1}{2} \int p(x) \ln\left( p(x) \right) + q(x) \ln\left( q(x) \right) - (p(x) + q(x)) \ln\left( \frac{p(x) + q(x)}{2} \right) dx$ |
| α-divergence | $D^{(\alpha)}(p, q) = \frac{4}{1-\alpha^2}\bigg(1 - \int p(x)^\frac{1-\alpha}{2} q(x)^\frac{1+\alpha}{2} dx \bigg)$ |
| [chi-squared divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-squared_divergence): | $D_{\chi^2}(p, q) = \int \frac{(p(x) - q(x))^2}{p(x)} dx$ |
| (*α*,*β*)-product divergence: | $D_{\alpha,\beta}(p, q) = \frac{2}{(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)} \int \Big(1 - \Big(\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}\Big)^{\!\!\frac{1-\alpha}{2}} \Big) \Big(1 - \Big(\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}\Big)^{\!\!\frac{1-\beta}{2}} \Big) p(x) dx$ |

:::

### Bregman divergences {#Bregman_divergences}

Bregman divergences correspond to convex functions on convex sets. Given a [strictly convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_convex_function), continuously differentiable function $F$ on a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292), known as the *Bregman generator*, the [Bregman divergence](wikipedia://en/4491248) measures the convexity of: the error of the linear approximation of $F$ from $q$ as an approximation of the value at $p$:

$$D_F(p, q) = F(p)-F(q)-\langle \nabla F(q), p-q\rangle.$$

The dual divergence to a Bregman divergence is the divergence generated by the [convex conjugate](wikipedia://en/1001490) $F^{*}$ of the Bregman generator of the original divergence. For example, for the squared Euclidean distance, the generator is $x^2$, while for the relative entropy the generator is the [negative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_entropy) $x \log x$.

:::

## History {#History}

The use of the term "divergence" – both what functions it refers to, and what various statistical distances are called – has varied significantly over time, but by c. 2000 had settled on the current usage within information geometry, notably in the textbook (Amari Nagaoka 2000).[^n1]

The term "divergence" for a statistical distance was used informally in various contexts from c. 1910 to c. 1940. Its formal use dates at least to (Bhattacharyya 1943), entitled "On a measure of divergence between two statistical populations defined by their probability distributions", which defined the [Bhattacharyya distance](wikipedia://en/831261), and (Bhattacharyya 1946), entitled "On a Measure of Divergence between Two Multinomial Populations", which defined the [Bhattacharyya angle](wikipedia://en/46852034). The term was popularized by its use for the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) in (Kullback Leibler 1951) and its use in the textbook (Kullback 1959). The term "divergence" was used generally by (Ali Silvey 1966) for statistically distances. Numerous references to earlier uses of [statistical distances](wikipedia://en/5187620) are given in (Adhikari Joshi 1956) and (Kullback 1959, 6–7).

(Kullback Leibler 1951) actually used "divergence" to refer to the *symmetrized* divergence (this function had already been defined and used by [Harold Jeffreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jeffreys) in 1948[^n4]), referring to the asymmetric function as "the mean information for discrimination ... per observation",[^n5] while (Kullback 1959) referred to the asymmetric function as the "directed divergence".[^n6] (Ali Silvey 1966) referred generally to such a function as a "coefficient of divergence", and showed that many existing functions could be expressed as *f*-divergences, referring to Jeffreys' function as "Jeffreys' measure of divergence" (today "Jeffreys divergence"), and Kullback–Leibler's asymmetric function (in each direction) as "Kullback's and Leibler's measures of discriminatory information" (today "Kullback–Leibler divergence").[^n7]

The information geometry definition of divergence (the subject of this article) was initially referred to by alternative terms, including "quasi-distance" (Amari 1982, 369) and "contrast function" (Eguchi 1985), though "divergence" was used in (Amari 1985) for the $\alpha$-divergence, and has become standard for the general class.[^n1][^n2]

The term "divergence" is in contrast to a distance (metric), since the symmetrized divergence does not satisfy the triangle inequality.[^n8] For example, the term "Bregman distance" is still found, but "Bregman divergence" is now preferred.

Notationally, (Kullback Leibler 1951) denoted their asymmetric function as $I(1:2)$, while (Ali Silvey 1966) denote their functions with a lowercase 'd' as $d\left(P_1, P_2\right)$.
