# Eckmann–Hilton duality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eckmann–Hilton duality"}

In the mathematical disciplines of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), **Eckmann–Hilton duality** in its most basic form, consists of taking a given [diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625) for a particular concept and reversing the direction of all arrows, much as in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) with the idea of the [opposite category](wikipedia://en/372399). A significantly deeper form argues that the fact that the dual notion of a [limit](wikipedia://en/39248) is a [colimit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit) allows us to change the [Eilenberg&ndash;Steenrod axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilenberg&ndash;Steenrod_axioms) for [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) to give axioms for [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060). It is named after [Beno Eckmann](wikipedia://en/19613365) and [Peter Hilton](wikipedia://en/8951286).

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

An example is given by [currying](wikipedia://en/6600), which tells us that for any object $X$, a map $X \times I \to Y$ is the same as a map $X \to Y^I$, where $Y^I$ is the [exponential object](wikipedia://en/2237858), given by all maps from $I$ to $Y$. In the case of [topological spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_spaces), if we take $I$ to be the unit interval, this leads to a duality between $X \times I$ and $Y^I$, which then gives a duality between the [reduced suspension](wikipedia://en/1251702) $\Sigma X$, which is a quotient of $X \times I$, and the [loop space](wikipedia://en/2174332) $\Omega Y$, which is a subspace of $Y^I$. This then leads to the [adjoint relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) $\langle \Sigma X, Y \rangle = \langle X, \Omega Y \rangle$, which allows the study of [spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_%28homotopy_theory%29), which give rise to [cohomology theories](wikipedia://en/194060).

We can also directly relate [fibrations](wikipedia://en/316826) and [cofibrations](wikipedia://en/1697216): a fibration $p \colon E \to B$ is defined by having the [homotopy lifting property](wikipedia://en/522786), represented by the following diagram

and a cofibration $i \colon A \to X$ is defined by having the dual [homotopy extension property](wikipedia://en/4029308), represented by dualising the previous diagram:

*Image: Homotopy extension property.svg.*

The above considerations also apply when looking at the sequences associated to a fibration or a cofibration, as given a fibration $F \to E \to B$ we get the sequence

$$\cdots \to \Omega^2 B \to \Omega F \to \Omega E \to \Omega B \to F \to E \to B \,$$

and given a cofibration $A \to X \to X/A$ we get the sequence

$$A \to X \to X/A \to \Sigma A \to \Sigma X \to \Sigma \left (X/A \right ) \to \Sigma^2 A \to \cdots. \,$$

and more generally, the duality between the exact and coexact [Puppe sequences](wikipedia://en/4734210).

This also allows us to relate [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) and cohomology: we know that [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) are [homotopy classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_class) of maps from the [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) to our space, written $\pi_n(X,p) \cong \langle S^n,X \rangle$, and we know that the sphere has a single nonzero (reduced) [cohomology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group). On the other hand, cohomology groups are homotopy classes of maps to spaces with a single nonzero homotopy group. This is given by the [Eilenberg&ndash;MacLane spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilenberg&ndash;MacLane_space) $K(G,n)$ and the relation

$$H^n(X;G) \cong \langle X,K(G,n) \rangle.$$

A formalization of the above informal relationships is given by [Fuks duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuks_duality).
