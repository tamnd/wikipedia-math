# Hurwitz's theorem (complex analysis)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hurwitz's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and in particular the field of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), **Hurwitz's theorem** is a theorem associating the [zeroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29) of a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110), [compact](wikipedia://en/7442564) [locally uniformly convergent](wikipedia://en/50652#Definition) functions with that of their corresponding limit. The theorem is named after [Adolf Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let {*f<sub>k</sub>*} be a sequence of holomorphic functions on a connected [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) *G* that converge uniformly on [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subsets of *G* to a holomorphic function *f* which is not constantly zero on *G*. If *f* has a zero of order *m* at *z*<sub>0</sub> then for every small enough *ρ* > 0 and for sufficiently large *k* ∈ **N** (depending on *ρ*), *f<sub>k</sub>* has precisely *m* zeroes in the disk defined by |*z* − *z*<sub>0</sub>| \< *ρ*, including [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790#In_complex_analysis). Furthermore, these zeroes converge to *z*<sub>0</sub> as *k* → ∞.[^o4]

:::

## Remarks {#Remarks}

The theorem does not guarantee that the result will hold for arbitrary disks. Indeed, if one chooses a disk such that *f* has zeroes on its [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824), the theorem fails. An explicit example is to consider the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) **D** and the sequence defined by

$$f_n(z) = z-1+\frac 1 n, \qquad z \in \mathbb C$$

which converges uniformly to *f*(*z*) = *z* − 1. The function *f*(*z*) contains no zeroes in **D**; however, each *f<sub>n</sub>* has exactly one zero in the disk corresponding to the real value 1 − (1/*n*).

::: {#o7 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Hurwitz's theorem is used in the proof of the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215),[^o8] and also has the following two [corollaries](wikipedia://en/168865) as an immediate consequence:

- Let *G* be a connected, open set and {*f<sub>n</sub>*} a sequence of holomorphic functions which converge uniformly on compact subsets of *G* to a holomorphic function *f*. If each *f<sub>n</sub>* is nonzero everywhere in *G*, then *f* is either identically zero or also is nowhere zero.
- If {*f<sub>n</sub>*} is a sequence of [univalent functions](wikipedia://en/1534382) on a connected open set *G* that converge uniformly on compact subsets of *G* to a holomorphic function *f*, then either *f* is univalent or constant.[^o8]

:::

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Let *f* be an analytic function on an open subset of the complex plane with a zero of order *m* at *z*<sub>0</sub>, and suppose that {*f<sub>n</sub>*} is a sequence of functions converging uniformly on compact subsets to *f*. Fix some *ρ* > 0 such that *f*(*z*) ≠ 0 in 0 \< |*z* − *z*<sub>0</sub>| ≤ ρ. Choose δ such that |*f*(*z*)| > *δ* for *z* on the circle |*z* − *z*<sub>0</sub>| = *ρ*. Since *f<sub>k</sub>*(*z*) converges uniformly on the disc we have chosen, we can find *N* such that |*f<sub>k</sub>*(*z*)| ≥ *δ*/2 for every *k* ≥ *N* and every *z* on the circle, ensuring that the quotient *f<sub>k</sub>*′(*z*)/*f<sub>k</sub>*(*z*) is well defined for all *z* on the circle |*z* − *z*<sub>0</sub>| = *ρ*. By Weierstrass's theorem we have $f_k' \to f'$ uniformly on the disc, and hence we have another uniform convergence:

$$\frac{f_k'(z)}{f_k(z)} \to \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}.$$

Denoting the number of zeros of *f<sub>k</sub>*(*z*) in the disk by *N<sub>k</sub>*, we may apply the [argument principle](wikipedia://en/1138322) to find

$$m = \frac 1 {2\pi i}\int_{\vert z -z_0\vert = \rho} \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)} \,dz = \lim_{k\to\infty} \frac 1 {2\pi i} \int_{\vert z -z_0\vert = \rho} \frac{f'_k(z)}{f_k(z)} \, dz = \lim_{k\to\infty} N_k$$

In the above step, we were able to interchange the integral and the limit because of the uniform convergence of the integrand. We have shown that *N<sub>k</sub>* → *m* as *k* → ∞. Since the *N<sub>k</sub>* are integer valued, *N<sub>k</sub>* must equal *m* for large enough *k*.[^o4]

:::
