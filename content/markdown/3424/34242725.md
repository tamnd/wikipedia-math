# Clifton–Pohl torus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Clifton–Pohl torus"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Clifton–Pohl torus** is an example of a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Lorentzian manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold) that is not [geodesically complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesically_complete). While every compact [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) is also geodesically complete (by the [Hopf–Rinow theorem](wikipedia://en/681265)), this space shows that the same implication does not generalize to [pseudo-Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/287555).[^o2] It is named after Yeaton H. Clifton and [William F. Pohl](wikipedia://en/53393367), who described it in 1962 but did not publish their result.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Consider the manifold $\mathrm{M} = \mathbb{R}^2 \smallsetminus \{0\}$ with the metric

$$g= \frac{2\,dx\,dy}{x^2+y^2}=\frac{dx\otimes dy + dy\otimes dx}{x^2+y^2}$$

Any [homothety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation) is an [isometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry_%28Riemannian_geometry%29) of $M$, in particular including the map:

$$\lambda(x,y)=(2x, 2y)$$

Let $\Gamma$ be the subgroup of the [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694) generated by $\lambda$. Then $\Gamma$ has a proper, discontinuous [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on $M$. Hence the quotient $T = M/\Gamma$, which is topologically the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), is a Lorentz surface that is called the Clifton–Pohl torus.[^o2] Sometimes, by extension, a surface is called a Clifton–Pohl torus if it is a finite covering of the quotient of $M$ by any homothety of ratio different from $\pm 1$.

:::

## Geodesic incompleteness {#Geodesic_incompleteness}

It can be verified that the curve

$$\sigma(t) := \left(\frac 1 {1-t},0\right)$$

is a [null geodesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_geodesic) of *M* that is not complete (since it is not defined at $t=1$).[^o2] Consequently, $M$ (hence also $T$) is geodesically incomplete, despite the fact that $T$ is compact. Similarly, the curve

$$\sigma(t) := (\tan(t), 1)$$

is also a null geodesic that is incomplete. In fact, every null geodesic on $M$ or $T$ is incomplete.

The geodesic incompleteness of the Clifton–Pohl torus is better seen as a direct consequence of the fact that $(M,g)$ is extendable, i.e. that it can be seen as a subset of a bigger Lorentzian surface. It is a direct consequence of a simple change of coordinates. With

$$N =\left(-\pi/2,\pi/2\right)^2 \smallsetminus \{0\};$$

consider

$$F : N \to M$$

$$F(u,v) := (\tan(u),\tan(v)).$$

The metric $F^*g$ (i.e. the metric $g$ expressed in the coordinates $(u,v)$) reads

$$\widehat{g\,} =\frac{du\,dv}{\tfrac{1}{2}(\cos(u)^2\sin(v)^2+\sin(u)^2\cos(v)^2)}.$$

But this metric extends naturally from $N$ to $\mathbb R^2 \smallsetminus \Lambda$, where

$$\Lambda =\left\{\tfrac \pi 2 (k,\ell)\ \mid\ (k,\ell)\in \mathbb Z^2, k+\ell\equiv 0 \pmod 2 \right\}.$$

The surface $(\mathbb R^2 \smallsetminus \Lambda, \widehat{g\,})$, known as the extended Clifton–Pohl plane, is geodesically complete.[^o7]

## Conjugate points {#Conjugate_points}

The Clifton–Pohl tori are also remarkable by the fact that they were the first known non-flat Lorentzian tori with no [conjugate points](wikipedia://en/2850652).[^o7] The extended Clifton–Pohl plane contains a lot of pairs of conjugate points, some of them being in the boundary of $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)^2$ i.e. "at infinity" in $M$. Recall also that, by [Hopf–Rinow theorem](wikipedia://en/681265) no such tori exists in the Riemannian setting.[^o9]
