# Units of information

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Units of information"}

A **unit of information** is any [unit of measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement) of [digital data](wikipedia://en/8276) size. In digital [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing), a unit of information is used to describe the capacity of a digital [data storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_data_storage) device. In [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunication), a unit of information is used to describe the [throughput](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_throughput) of a [communication channel](wikipedia://en/156700). In [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), a unit of information is used to measure [information](wikipedia://en/18985062) contained in messages and the [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445) of random variables.

:::

Due to the need to work with data sizes that range from small to large, units of information cover a wide range of data sizes. Units are defined as multiples of a smaller unit except for the smallest unit which is based on convention and hardware design. [Multiplier prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_prefix) are used to describe relatively large sizes.

For [binary](wikipedia://en/238686) [hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware), the most common hardware today, the smallest unit is the [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit), which represents a value that is one of two possibilities, typically shown as 0 and 1. The [nibble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nibble), 4 bits, represents the value of a single [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) digit. The [byte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte) (8 bits, 2 nibbles) is possibly the most commonly known and used base unit to describe data size. The [word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_%28computer_architecture%29) is a size that varies by and has a special importance for a particular hardware context. On modern hardware, a word is typically 2, 4 or 8 bytes, but the size varies dramatically on older hardware. Larger sizes can be expressed as multiples of a base unit via [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) [metric prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix) (powers of ten) or the newer and generally more accurate [IEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Electrotechnical_Commission) [binary prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_prefix) (powers of two).

## Information theory {#Information_theory}

*Image: Units of information.svg.* Comparison of units of information: [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit), [trit](wikipedia://en/62950), [nat](wikipedia://en/3070481), [ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_%28unit%29). Quantity of information is the height of bars. Dark green level is the "nat" unit.

In 1928, [Ralph Hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Hartley) observed a fundamental storage principle,[^o22] which was further formalized by [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) in 1945: the information that can be stored in a system is proportional to the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of *N* possible states of that system, denoted log<sub>*b*</sub> *N*. Changing the base of the logarithm from *b* to a different number *c* has the effect of multiplying the value of the logarithm by a fixed constant, namely log<sub>*c*</sub> *N* = (log<sub>*c*</sub> *b*) log<sub>*b*</sub> *N*. Therefore, the choice of the base *b* determines the unit used to measure information. In particular, if *b* is a [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_negative_numbers) integer, then the unit is the amount of information that can be stored in a system with *b* possible states.

When *b* is 2, the unit is the [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_%28unit%29), equal to the [information content](wikipedia://en/542447) of one "bit". A system with 8 possible states, for example, can store up to log<sub>2</sub> 8 = 3 bits of information. Other units that have been named include:

**Base *b* = 3**

the unit is called "[trit](wikipedia://en/62950)", and is equal to log<sub>2</sub> 3 (≈ 1.585) bits.[^o23]

**Base *b* = 10**

the unit is called *[decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) [digit](wikipedia://en/280582)*, *[hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_%28unit%29)*, *ban*, *decit*, or *dit*, and is equal to log<sub>2</sub> 10 (≈ 3.322) bits.[^o22][^o24][^o25][^o26]

**Base *b* = *e*, the [base of natural logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_of_natural_logarithm)**

the unit is called a *[nat](wikipedia://en/3070481)*, *nit*, or *nepit* (from [Neperian](wikipedia://en/15993)), and is worth log<sub>2</sub> *e* (≈ 1.443) bits.[^o22]

The trit, ban, and nat are rarely used to measure storage capacity. But the nat, in particular, is often used in information theory, because natural logarithms are mathematically more convenient than logarithms in other bases.

## Units derived from bit {#Units_derived_from_bit}

Several conventional names are used for collections or groups of bits.

### Byte {#Byte}

Historically, a [byte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte) was the number of bits used to encode a [character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_%28computing%29) of text in the computer, which depended on computer hardware architecture, but today it almost always means eight bits – that is, an [octet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octet_%28computing%29). An 8-bit byte can represent 256 distinct values, such as non-negative integers from 0 to 255, or [signed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two's_complement) integers from −128 to 127. The [IEEE&nbsp;1541-2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE&nbsp;1541-2002) standard specifies "B" (upper case) as the symbol for byte ([IEC&nbsp;80000-13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEC&nbsp;80000-13) uses "o" for octet in French, but also allows "B" in English). Bytes, or multiples thereof, are almost always used to specify the sizes of computer files and the capacity of storage units. Most modern computers and peripheral devices are designed to manipulate data in whole bytes or groups of bytes, rather than individual bits.

### Nibble {#Nibble}

A group of four bits, or half a byte, is sometimes called a [nibble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nibble), nybble or nyble. This unit is most often used in the context of [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) number representations, since a nibble has the same number of possible values as one hexadecimal digit has.[^o30]

### Word, block, and page {#Word,_block,_and_page}

Computers usually manipulate bits in groups of a fixed size, conventionally called *[words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_%28computer_architecture%29)*. The number of bits in a word is usually defined by the size of the [registers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_%28computing%29) in the computer's [CPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPU), or by the number of data bits that are fetched from its [main memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_memory) in a single operation. In the [IA-32](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IA-32) architecture more commonly known as x86-32, a word is 32 bits, but other past and current architectures use words with 4, 8, 9, 12, 13, 16, 18, 20, 21, 22, 24, 25, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 35, 36, 38, 39, 40, 42, 44, 48, 50, 52, 54, 56, 60, 64, 72[^o27] bits or others.

Some [machine instructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_instruction) and [computer number formats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_number_format) use two words (a "double word" or "dword"), or four words (a "quad word" or "quad").

Computer [memory caches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_cache) usually operate on *[blocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_%28data_storage%29)* of memory that consist of several consecutive words. These units are customarily called *cache blocks*, or, in [CPU caches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPU_cache), *cache lines*.

[Virtual memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_memory) systems partition the computer's [main storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_storage) into even larger units, traditionally called *[pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Page_%28computer_memory%29)*.

### Multiplicative prefixes {#Multiplicative_prefixes}

A unit for a large amount of data can be formed using either a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix) or binary prefix with a base unit. For storage, the base unit is typically a byte. For communication throughput, a base unit of bit is common. For example, using the metric *kilo* prefix, a [kilobyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilobyte) is 1000 bytes and a [kilobit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilobit) is 1000 bits.

Use of metric prefixes is common. In the context of computing, some of these prefixes (primarily *kilo*, *mega* and *giga*) are used to refer to the nearest power of two. For example, 'kilobyte' often refers to 1024 bytes even though the standard meaning of kilo is 1000.[^o8]

:::

The [International Electrotechnical Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Electrotechnical_Commission) (IEC) standardized [binary prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_prefixes) for binary multiples to avoid ambiguity through their similarity to the standard metric terms. These are based on powers of 1024, which is a power of 2.[^o28]

| Ti | tebi | 2<sup>40</sup>, 1024<sup>4</sup> | tebibyte (TiB) |
| Pi | pebi | 2<sup>50</sup>, 1024<sup>5</sup> | pebibyte (PiB) |

## Size examples {#Size_examples}

- 1 bit: Answer to a yes/no question
- 1 byte: A number from 0 to 255
- 90 bytes: Enough to store a typical line of text from a book
- 512 bytes = 0.5 KiB: The typical [sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_sector) size of an old style [hard disk drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive) (modern [Advanced Format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Format) sectors are 4096 bytes).
- 1024 bytes = 1 KiB: A [block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_%28data_storage%29) size in some older [UNIX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNIX) [filesystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filesystem)
- 2048 bytes = 2 KiB: A [CD-ROM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD-ROM) sector
- 4096 bytes = 4 KiB: A [memory page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_page) in [x86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X86) (since [Intel 80386](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_80386)) and many other architectures, also the modern [Advanced Format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Format) hard disk drive sector size.
- 4 kB: About one page of text from a [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel)
- 120 kB: The text of a typical pocket book
- 1 MiB: A 1024×1024 pixel [bitmap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitmap) image with 256 colors (8 bpp color depth)
- 3 MB: A three-minute [song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song) (133 kbit/s)
- 650–900 MB – a CD-ROM
- 1 GB: 114 minutes of uncompressed CD-quality audio at 1.4 Mbit/s
- 16 GB: DDR5 DRAM laptop memory under \$40 (as of early 2024); about \$169 (as of early 2026) due to the [2025–present global memory supply shortage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025–present_global_memory_supply_shortage)
- 32 GB: SODIMM DDR5 DRAM laptop memory for \$377.99 (as of early 2026)
- 32/64/128 GB: Three common sizes of [USB flash drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USB_flash_drive)
- 1 TB: The size of a \$30 hard disk (as of early 2024)
- 6 TB: The size of a \$100 hard disk (as of early 2022)
- 12 TB game [hard disk drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive) (for Xbox).
- 16 TB: The size of a small/cheap \$130 (as of early 2024) enterprise SAS hard disk drive
- 26 TB: The size of \$325 (as of early 2026) "video" hard disk drive
- 44 TB: Largest [hard disk drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk_drive) (as of , still only sold to data centers; 36 TB in Feb 2025 was then also only sold to data centers).[^o12]
- 100 TB: Largest commercially available [solid-state drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_drive) (as of mid-2024)
- 200 TB: Largest solid-state drive constructed (prediction for mid-2022)
- \1.6 PB (1600 TB): Amount of possible storage in one 2U server (world record as of 2021, using 100 TB solid-states drives).[^o13]
- \1.3 ZB: Prediction of the volume of the whole internet in 2016
