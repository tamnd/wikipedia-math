# Econometrics of risk

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Econometrics of risk"}

The **[econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390) of [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk)** is a specialized field within [econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390) that focuses on the quantitative modeling and [statistical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_analysis) of risk in various [economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic) and [financial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial) contexts. It integrates [mathematical modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_modeling), [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), and [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577) to assess uncertainty, measure risk exposure, and predict potential financial losses. The discipline is widely applied in [financial markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_market), [insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance), [macroeconomic policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomic_policy), and [corporate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate) risk management.

:::

## Historical Development {#Historical_Development}

The econometrics of risk emerged from centuries of interdisciplinary advances in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), and [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216). Drawing on Sakai’s framework, its evolution is categorized into six distinct stages, each shaped by influential thinkers and historical events.[^o3]

\1. **Initial (Pre-1700)**

- **Foundations in [probability](wikipedia://en/22934)**: [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068) and [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) formalized [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) in 1654 through their correspondence on gambling problems ([“the problem of points”](wikipedia://en/11285518)). Pascal’s work extended to philosophical debates, such as [Pascal's wager](wikipedia://en/215539), framed through early utility concepts.
- **Risk-sharing mechanisms**: Early institutions like [Lloyd's Coffee House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd's_Coffee_House) (1688), [marine insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_insurance), and stock exchanges (e.g., [London Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange), founded in 1801, with precursors as early as 1571) addressed practical risks in trade and exploration.
- **Limitations**: [Mercantilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercantilism) dominated but lacked formal economic frameworks.

\2. **1700–1880: Bernoulli and Adam Smith**

- **[Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784)**: Introduced [expected utility theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility_theory) (1738) to resolve the [St. Petersburg paradox](wikipedia://en/466277), replacing expected monetary value with logarithmic utility functions.
- **[Adam Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Smith)**: In *[The Wealth of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations)* (1776), analyzed risk-bearing in markets, noting behavioral biases.
- **Key events**: The rise of insurance (e.g., [Tokio Marine Nichido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokio_Marine_Nichido), founded 1879) and political upheavals (e.g., [American independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Revolutionary_War) (1776); [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) (1789)) highlighted the need for systematic risk thinking.

\3. **1880–1940: Keynes and Knight**

- [**Keynes**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes): In *[A Treatise on Probability](wikipedia://en/28953088)* (1921), distinguished between [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk) (quantifiable) and [uncertainty](wikipedia://en/63778) (non-quantifiable), introducing concepts like [animal spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_spirits_%28Keynes%29).
- [**Frank H. Knight**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Knight): In *[Risk, Uncertainty and Profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk,_Uncertainty_and_Profit)* (1921), emphasized that profit arises from non-insurable uncertainty.
- **Context**: [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I), the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression), and the [1923 Great Kantō earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Great_Kantō_earthquake) shaped early economic risk studies.

\4. **1940–1970: Von Neumann and Morgenstern**

- **[Game theory](wikipedia://en/11924)**: [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) and [Oskar Morgenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Morgenstern) introduced the [expected utility hypothesis](wikipedia://en/736803) in their 1944 work *[Theory of Games and Economic Behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_Games_and_Economic_Behavior)*.
- [**Portfolio theory**](wikipedia://en/734434): [Harry Markowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Markowitz) (1952) developed [mean-variance analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean-variance_analysis) for optimizing risk-return trade-offs.
- **Post-war shifts**: Advances in computation and Cold War economics (e.g., [Monte Carlo simulations](wikipedia://en/56098)) supported risk modeling.

\5. **1970–2000: Arrow, Akerlof, Spence, and Stiglitz**

- **[Information asymmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_asymmetry)**: [George Akerlof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Akerlof)’s “[The Market for Lemons](wikipedia://en/216867)” (1970), [Michael Spence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Spence)’s signaling theory, and [Joseph Stiglitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stiglitz)’s screening models reshaped [credit risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_risk) modeling.
- **Volatility models**: [Robert F. Engle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_F._Engle)’s [ARCH](wikipedia://en/610752) (1982) and [Tim Bollerslev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Bollerslev)’s [GARCH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GARCH) (1986) enabled time-varying volatility estimation.
- **Crises**: Events like the [1973 oil crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1973_oil_crisis), the [Chernobyl disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernobyl_disaster) (1986), and the [dissolution of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union) (1991) exposed model weaknesses.

\6. **Uncertain age (2000–Present)**

- **Systemic risks**: The [2008 financial crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_financial_crisis) and [Fukushima nuclear disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fukushima_nuclear_disaster) (2011) revealed the limitations of [VaR](wikipedia://en/148633) models.
- **New tools**: [Machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), [extreme value theory](wikipedia://en/50732), and [Bayesian networks](wikipedia://en/203996) are increasingly applied to model [tail risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tail_risk).
- **Regulation**: [Basel III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel_III) and [Basel IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel_IV) standards emphasize [stress testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_testing) and [liquidity risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquidity_risk).

## Key econometric models in risk analysis {#Key_econometric_models_in_risk_analysis}

### Traditional latent variable models {#Traditional_latent_variable_models}

Econometric models frequently embed deterministic utility differences into a [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF), allowing analysts to estimate decision-making under uncertainty. A common example is the binary [logit](wikipedia://en/172068) model:

$$\Pr(y_i = 1) = F(x_i'\beta), \quad \text{where } F(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

This setup assumes a [homoscedastic](wikipedia://en/70780067) logistic error term, which can result in systematic distortions in the estimation of risk preferences if scale is ignored.[^o6]

### Contextual utility model {#Contextual_utility_model}

To address scale confounds in standard models, Wilcox (2011) proposed the contextual utility (CU) model. It divides the utility difference by the contextual range of all option pairs in the choice set:

$$\Pr(A \succ B) = F\left( \frac{U(A) - U(B)}{\max_{C,D} |U(C) - U(D)|} \right)$$

This model satisfies several desirable properties, including [monotonicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity), [stochastic dominance](wikipedia://en/3574224), and contextual [scale invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance).[^o8]

### Random preference models {#Random_preference_models}

Random preference models assume agents draw their preferences from a population distribution, generating heterogeneity in observed choices:

$$U_{it} = x_{it}'\beta_i + \epsilon_{it}, \quad \beta_i \sim N(\bar{\beta}, \Sigma)$$

This framework accounts for preference variation across individuals and enables richer modeling in panel data and experimental contexts.[^o10]

### Credit risk models {#Credit_risk_models}

[Binary classification models](wikipedia://en/205393) are extensively used in credit scoring. For instance, the [probit](wikipedia://en/1689101) model for default risk is:

$$\Pr(\text{Default}_i = 1) = \Phi(x_i'\beta)$$

Alternatively, in duration-based settings, proportional hazards models are common:

$$h(t|x_i) = h_0(t) \exp(x_i'\beta)$$

Here, $h_0(t)$ is the baseline hazard, and $x_i$ represents borrower characteristics.[^o12]

### Insurance risk models {#Insurance_risk_models}

Insurance econometrics often uses frequency-severity models. The expected aggregate claims are the product of the expected number of claims and the expected claim size:

$$\mathbb{E}[Z] = \mathbb{E}[N] \cdot \mathbb{E}[X], \quad Z = \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i$$

Typically, $N$ follows a [Poisson distribution,](wikipedia://en/23009144) and $X_i$ may follow [gamma](wikipedia://en/207079) or [Pareto distributions](wikipedia://en/53057).[^o14]

### Marketing risk models {#Marketing_risk_models}

In marketing analytics, rare event models are used to study infrequent purchases or churn behavior. The [zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP)](wikipedia://en/37915814) model is common:

$$\Pr(Y = 0) = \pi + (1 - \pi) e^{-\lambda}, \quad \Pr(Y = y) = (1 - \pi) \frac{\lambda^y e^{-\lambda}}{y!}, \quad y > 0$$

Mixed [logit](wikipedia://en/172068) models allow for random taste variation:

$$\Pr(y_{it} = j) = \int \frac{e^{x_{ijt}'\beta}}{\sum_k e^{x_{ikt}'\beta}} f(\beta) \, d\beta$$

These are useful when modeling [risk-averse](wikipedia://en/177700) consumer behavior and product choice under uncertainty.[^o16]

### Volatility models (ARCH/GARCH/SV) {#Volatility_models_(ARCH/GARCH/SV)}

[Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity](wikipedia://en/610752) models (ARCH) allow conditional variance to depend on past shocks, capturing volatility clustering. Bollerslev’s GARCH model generalizes ARCH by including lagged variances. Exponential GARCH (EGARCH) and other variants capture asymmetries (e.g. leverage effects). A distinct class is [stochastic volatility](wikipedia://en/5740025) (SV) models, which assume volatility follows its own latent stochastic process (e.g., Taylor 1986). These models are central to financial risk, used to forecast time-varying risk and derivative pricing.[^o18]

### Risk measures (VaR, expected shortfall) and quantile methods {#Risk_measures_(VaR,_expected_shortfall)_and_quantile_methods}

Econometricians estimate risk measures like [value at risk](wikipedia://en/148633) (VaR) and [expected shortfall](wikipedia://en/8307819) (ES) using both parametric and nonparametric methods. For example, [extreme value theory](wikipedia://en/50732) (EVT) can be used to model tail risk in financial returns, yielding estimates of high-quantile losses. [Jon Danielsson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Danielsson) (1998) notes that traditional models (often assuming normality) tend to underestimate tail risk, leading to applications of EVT to VaR estimation. Quantile regression is another tool for VaR forecasting: by directly modeling a conditional quantile of returns, one can estimate the maximum expected loss at a given confidence level.[^o20]

### Advanced techniques {#Advanced_techniques}

- **Copula models**: Are used for multivariate risk modeling where marginal distributions are known, and the dependency structure is modeled separately:

$$F(x_1, x_2, ..., x_n) = C(F_1(x_1), F_2(x_2), ..., F_n(x_n); \theta)$$

Here $C$ is the copula function (e.g., Clayton, Gumbel, Gaussian).[^o22]

- **Regularization techniques**: In high-dimensional settings, [LASSO](wikipedia://en/22349350) is used to prevent [overfitting](wikipedia://en/173332) and improve model selection:

$$\min_\beta \left( \frac{1}{2n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - x_i'\beta)^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \right)$$

LASSO is increasingly adopted in predictive risk modeling for credit scoring, insurance, and marketing applications.[^o23]
