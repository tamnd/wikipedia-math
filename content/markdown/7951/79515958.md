# Kokotsakis polyhedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kokotsakis polyhedron"}

A **Kokotsakis polyhedron** is a [polyhedral surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedral_surface) in three-dimensional space consisting of any [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) as its base and [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278) alternating with triangles as its lateral faces; for an $n$-sided polygonal base, there are $n$ quadrilaterals and $n$ triangles.[^n1]

:::

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Kokotsakis mesh"}

## Properties and history {#Properties_and_history}

The polyhedron was discovered when (Kokotsakis 1933) studied the meshes wherein the perimeter of a polygon is surrounded by other polygons, showing an infinitesimally [flexible](wikipedia://en/9070022) in the case of a quadrilateral base, which was later known as **Kokotsakis mesh**. More examples of this special case of a Kokotsakis polyhedron were discovered by other mathematicians.[^n2] Here, a polyhedron is flexible if the shape can be continuously changed while preserving the faces unchanged. Each of its vertexes is said to be "developable", meaning the sum of its plane angle is $2 \pi$, resulting in the polyhedral surface being an origami [crease pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crease_pattern), which satisfies [Kawasaki's theorem](wikipedia://en/14511776).[^n3] The work was done by (Izmestiev 2017) in which classifying the folding angle for a Kokotsakis polyhedron in the case of a quadrangular base.

(Stachel 2010) conjectured that there exists no polynomial system of irreducible [resultant](wikipedia://en/2195020) about the flexibility of a Kokotsakis polyhedron, which was later (Izmestiev 2017) disproved it by showing the Kokotsakis polyhedron is *orthodiagonal anti-involutive*, meaning the [planar angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_angle) has conditions such as all quadrilaterals are spherically [orthodiagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthodiagonal) (any intersecting two diagonals in a quadrilateral form a right angle) and elliptic (the sum and difference of the edges of a quadrilateral are not equal to $0 \bmod 2\pi$), and the [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) at common vertices are opposite.

:::

## Kokotsakis mesh {#Kokotsakis_mesh}

As mentioned above, the Kokotsakis mesh was studied by (Kokotsakis 1933), showing an infinitesimally flexible polyhedron in the case of a quadrilateral base. In general, the Kokotsakis mesh is defined as the infinite [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671) consisting of quadrilateral with congruent convex that is not trapezoidal and parallelogram.[^n4] In the case of a quadrangle mesh, it is [planar symmetric](wikipedia://en/53741) (exchanging adjacent vertices in-between), [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) (two adjacent vertices are translated, mapping the faces), [isogonal](wikipedia://en/581560) (opposite angles at every vertex is equal or [complementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_angle)), [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) (the faces are parallel to the planes), and line-symmetric (appearance is symmetrical by half-rotating between two adjacent vertices around an axis passing through).[^n5]

The Kokotsakis mesh can be used to construct cylindrical polyhedra.
