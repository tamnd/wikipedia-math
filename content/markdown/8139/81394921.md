# Hilberg's hypothesis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hilberg's hypothesis"}

**Hilberg's hypothesis** (also known as **Hilberg's law**) is a conjecture in [quantitative linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_linguistics) or a statistical law in [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773). It states that measures of information in [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173) texts or samples of particular [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) grow as a sublinear power of the sample length, possibly in addition to the term that is linear. It is closely related to [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218) and the [neural scaling law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_scaling_law) for [large language models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_models).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Hilberg's hypothesis was proposed by the German telecommunication engineer [Wolfgang Hilberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Hilberg)[^o3] in 1990,[^o4] based on data originally published by [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) in 1951 on the predictability of English text.[^o5] Hilberg observed that the amount of new information per character appears to decrease with context length in a manner consistent with a [power law](wikipedia://en/24522). His analysis implied that the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy) $H(n)$ of text blocks of length $n$ grows approximately as

$$H(n)-hn \propto n^{\beta},$$

where parameter $h$ is the [entropy rate](wikipedia://en/11071463) of the process and parameter $\beta\in(0,1)$ is called the Hilberg exponent.[^o6] The term proportional to $n^{\beta}$ represents a memory effect, suggesting that human language carries a large amount of information in a repetitive way. Hilberg originally assumed that $h=0$ and $\beta=1/2$ basing his hypothesis on a visually meagre evidence.[^o4] Hilberg's article was published in a local telecommunication journal and was initially noticed by physicists working in [complex systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_systems).[^o7][^o8][^o9][^o25]

Hilberg's hypothesis implies that written or spoken language exhibits [potentially infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_infinity) memory or [long-range statistical dependencies](wikipedia://en/1516694) that cannot be captured by [finite-state](wikipedia://en/10931) [hidden Markov models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_Markov_models). The refutation of finite-state models based on Hilberg's hypothesis is close in spirit to [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218) for the unbounded [arbitrary](wikipedia://en/3019808) lexicon, which needs to be learned and memorized by heart.[^o23] This mechanism differs to the refutation of finite-state models that is based on the unbounded stack of the [push-down automaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Push-down_automaton) for processing [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) of natural language, the latter argument developed by [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky) in [Syntactic Structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_Structures) in 1957.

By contrast to "Hilberg's hypothesis", the term "Hilberg's law" or "Hilberg's condition" often applies to any [stationary process](wikipedia://en/329898) that satisfies a power-law scaling of $H(n)-hn$.[^o23] Quantity $H(n)$ may represent various information measures, such as [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy), (resource-bounded) [Kolmogorov complexity](wikipedia://en/1635),[^o10] or the length of a [universal code](wikipedia://en/2522009).[^o11] In the context of [deep learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning), the term [neural scaling law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_scaling_law) is used for analogous power-law relations describing how performance of a [large language model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model), measured by [cross entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_entropy), improves with data size, model parameters, or computation.[^o12] Yet another expression involves the [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282) between two adjacent text blocks of length $n$,

$$I(n) = 2H(n) - H(2n).$$

Using this concept, Hilberg's law is equivalent to

$$I(n) \propto n^{\beta}.$$

This version does not depend on the precise value of the entropy rate and is used in theoretical studies.[^o13]

The value of the Hilberg exponent $\beta$ depends crucially on the applied information measure, or the compression algorithm in case of the universal code. Simultaneously, it exhibits a certain degree of [universality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_universal) across particular languages and writing systems, being $\beta\approx 0.8$ for the [prediction by partial matching](wikipedia://en/527918) code run on English, French, Russian, Korean, Chinese, and Japanese news corpora.[^o11][^o14]

## Examples of processes {#Examples_of_processes}

The intuitive meaning of Hilberg's hypothesis can be motivated by some [toy models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toy_models) of [stationary processes](wikipedia://en/329898), which link Hilberg's law with [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218) for an unbounded [arbitrary](wikipedia://en/3019808) lexicon that needs to be learned by heart. Equivalently, the following examples may be considered as idealized information sources that convey an unbounded amount of [algorithmically random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmically_random) knowledge in a repetitive fashion.[^o13] This knowledge can be either immutable, giving rise to non-ergodic or perigraphic processes,[^o16][^o18] or slowly evolving, yielding mixing processes.[^o19] A priori, this knowledge may encode also facts different to the knowledge of a particular lexicon.[^o16]

A simple model of a stochastic process that satisfies Hilberg's law $I(n) \propto n^{\beta}$ is given by the Santa Fe process. The Santa Fe process is a [stationary non-ergodic process](wikipedia://en/3102474) $(X_i)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}$ that consists of pairs $X_i=(K_i,Z_{K_i})$, where $(K_i)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}$ is a sequence of independent random natural numbers distributed according to [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218) $P(K_i=k) \propto k^{-1/\beta}$ and $(Z_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ is the uniform [Bernoulli process](wikipedia://en/102651), i.e., a sequence of fair coin flips.[^o16][^o17] Process $(K_i)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}$ is called narration and process $(Z_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ is called knowledge.[^o23] Individual variables $Z_k$ are called (independent elementary) facts.[^o16]

In general, Hilberg's law may arise for [non-ergodic](wikipedia://en/3102474) or perigraphic processes depending whether one considers [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy) or [Kolmogorov complexity](wikipedia://en/1635) as the chosen information measure. A perigraphic process is roughly an [algorithmically random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmically_random) [ergodic component](wikipedia://en/5456824) of a [non-ergodic](wikipedia://en/3102474) process with a [non-atomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonatomic_measure) [invariant sigma-algebra](wikipedia://en/76015102). An example of a perigraphic process is a modified Santa Fe process $(X_i)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}$ that consists of pairs $X_i=(K_i,z_{K_i})$ where $(z_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a fixed [algorithmically random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmically_random) binary sequence such as the [halting probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halting_probability).[^o18]

Hilberg's law may also arise for some [mixing processes](wikipedia://en/1308185). An example of such a process is a modified Santa Fe process $(X_i)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}$ that consists of pairs $X_i=(K_i,Z_{i,K_i})$ where $(Z_{i,k})_{i\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a collection of independent binary [Markov processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process), where the flipping probabilities $P(Z_{i,k}\neq Z_{i-1,k})$ are smaller than the mentioning probabilities $P(K_i=k)$.[^o19]

The default examples of Santa Fe processes have a positive entropy rate. There are also examples of processes with a vanishing entropy rate that obey Hilberg's law, called multiperiodic processes.[^o20]

## Related conditions {#Related_conditions}

Hilberg's hypothesis connects quantitative linguistics, information theory, and modern machine-learning research on scaling behavior. By means of mathematical [theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorems), Hilberg's law can be related to some similar conditions for [stationary processes](wikipedia://en/329898) over a [finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite), a [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite), or an [uncountable set](wikipedia://en/32248) of possible outcomes.

Hilberg's law can be deductively linked to [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218) and [Heaps' law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaps'_law) for word-like subwords or chunks by means of [grammar-based codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar-based_codes) and [prediction by partial matching](wikipedia://en/527918), [non-ergodic processes](wikipedia://en/3102474), and the [data-processing inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data-processing_inequality) for [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282).[^o16][^o18][^o13] There is a general result concerning stationary processes, called the theorem about facts and words, which states that the mutual information between two adjacent text blocks is sandwiched by the number of independent elementary facts and the number of distinct word-like strings expressed in the concatenated text block.[^o16][^o18][^o13]

Deductive links between Hilberg's law and the [neural scaling law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_scaling_law) are a subject of ongoing research.[^o17][^o10][^o22]

Hilberg's hypothesis has been discussed as evidence that language production involves infinite memory, contrasting with [finite-state](wikipedia://en/10931) [Markov models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_models) and [hidden Markov models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_Markov_models).[^o23] In general, Hilberg's law is incompatible with [finite-state](wikipedia://en/10931) [hidden Markov models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_Markov_models). The mutual information between past and future $E=\lim_{n\to\infty} I(n)$ is called [excess entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excess_entropy)[^o25] or [predictive information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predictive_information).[^o9][^o24] Condition $E<\infty$ holds for [finite-state](wikipedia://en/10931) [hidden Markov models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_Markov_models)[^o25] and non-degenerate [autoregressive moving-average](wikipedia://en/764848) [Gaussian processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_processes).[^o26] By contrast, condition $E=\infty$ holds for [non-ergodic processes](wikipedia://en/3102474) with a [non-atomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonatomic_measure) [invariant sigma-algebra](wikipedia://en/76015102).[^o13] There also exist [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite)-state [hidden Markov models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_Markov_models) that enjoy condition $E=\infty$[^o27] and Hilberg's law.[^o28][^o23]

A less direct is the link of Hilberg's law with the power-law logarithmic scaling of the maximal repetition length, empirically observed for natural language,[^o29] as this involves a power-law growth of [Renyi entropies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renyi_entropies) rather than the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy).[^o30]

In the case of stationary processes over a countable alphabet, it is unknown whether Hilberg's law (under a mild condition) implies a slow decay of the two-point mutual information $I(X_0;X_n)$, which seems to hold for natural language.[^o31][^o32] For the non-ergodic Santa Fe processes, one also observes $I(X_0;X_n)=\text{const}$ for $n\neq 0$.

In the case of [real-valued](wikipedia://en/20646438) non-degenerate [Gaussian processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_processes), condition $E=\infty$ is equivalent to $\sum_{k=1}^\infty k\alpha_k^2=\infty$, where $\alpha_k$ is the [partial autocorrelation function](wikipedia://en/16862495).[^o33] Additionally, if the Gaussian process has a positive and continuous [spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672) then condition $E=\infty$ is equivalent to $\sum_{k=1}^\infty k\rho_k^2=\infty$, where $\rho_k$ is the [autocorrelation function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocorrelation_function).[^o34] By contrast, the [long-range dependence](wikipedia://en/1516694) condition is $\sum_{k=1}^\infty |\rho_k|=\infty$,[^o35] which is equivalent to the previous condition if the autocorrelation function $\rho_k$ decays exactly according to a [power law](wikipedia://en/24522). Thus Hilberg's law implies [long-range dependence](wikipedia://en/1516694) if the process is Gaussian, the spectral density is positive and continuous, and the autocorrelation function follows a power law.
