# Operator norm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Operator norm"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **operator norm** measures the "size" of certain [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) by assigning each a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) called its operator norm. Formally, it is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) defined on the space of [bounded linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) between two given [normed vector spaces](wikipedia://en/21538). Informally, the operator norm $\|T\|$ of a linear map $T : X \to Y$ is the maximum factor by which it "lengthens" vectors. It is also called the **bound norm**.[^o2]

:::

## Introduction and definition {#Introduction_and_definition}

Given two normed vector spaces $V$ and $W$ (over the same base [field](wikipedia://en/10603), either the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$ or the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$), a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $A : V \to W$ is continuous [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there exists a real number $c$ such that[^o4]

$$\|Av\| \leq c \|v\| \quad \text{ for all } v\in V.$$

The norm on the left is the one in $W$ and the norm on the right is the one in $V$. Intuitively, the continuous operator $A$ never increases the length of any vector by more than a factor of $c.$ Thus the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a bounded set under a continuous operator is also bounded. Because of this property, the continuous linear operators are also known as [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961). In order to "measure the size" of $A,$ one can take the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of the numbers $c$ such that the above inequality holds for all $v \in V.$ This number represents the maximum scalar factor by which $A$ "lengthens" vectors. In other words, the "size" of $A$ is measured by how much it "lengthens" vectors in the "biggest" case. So we define the operator norm of $A$ as

$$\|A\|_\text{op} = \inf\{ c \geq 0 : \|Av\| \leq c \|v\| \text{ for all } v \in V \}.$$

The infimum is attained as the set of all such $c$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279), [nonempty](wikipedia://en/9566), and [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) from below.[^o5]

It is important to bear in mind that this operator norm depends on the choice of norms for the normed vector spaces $V$ and $W$.

## Examples {#Examples}

Every real $m$-by-$n$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) corresponds to a linear map from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m.$ Each pair of the plethora of (vector) [norms](wikipedia://en/990534) applicable to real vector spaces induces an operator norm for all $m$-by-$n$ matrices of real numbers; these induced norms form a subset of [matrix norms](wikipedia://en/1543735).

If we specifically choose the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm) on both $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{R}^m,$ then the matrix norm given to a matrix $A$ is the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of the largest [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of the matrix $A^{*} A$ (where $A^{*}$ denotes the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of $A$).[^o8] This is equivalent to assigning the largest [singular value](wikipedia://en/709116) of $A.$

Passing to a typical infinite-dimensional example, consider the [sequence space](wikipedia://en/1701075) $\ell^2,$ which is an [L<sup>*p*</sup> space](wikipedia://en/45194), defined by

$$\ell^2 = \left\{ (a_n)_{n \geq 1} : \; a_n \in \mathbb{C}, \; \sum_n |a_n|^2 < \infty \right\}.$$

This can be viewed as an infinite-dimensional analogue of the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{C}^n.$ Now consider a bounded sequence $s_{\bullet} = \left(s_n\right)_{n=1}^\infty.$ The sequence $s_{\bullet}$ is an element of the space $\ell^\infty,$ with a norm given by

$$\left\|s_{\bullet}\right\|_\infty = \sup _n \left|s_n\right|.$$

Define an operator $T_s$ by pointwise multiplication:

$$\left(a_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty} \;\stackrel{T_s}{\mapsto}\;\ \left(s_n \cdot a_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}.$$

The operator $T_s$ is bounded with operator norm

$$\left\|T_s\right\|_\text{op} = \left\|s_{\bullet}\right\|_\infty.$$

This discussion extends directly to the case where $\ell^2$ is replaced by a general $L^p$ space with $p > 1$ and $\ell^\infty$ replaced by $L^\infty.$

:::

## Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

Let $A : V \to W$ be a linear operator between normed spaces. The first four definitions are always equivalent, and if in addition $V \neq \{0\}$ then they are all equivalent:

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} \|A\|_\text{op} &= \inf &&\{ c \geq 0 ~&&:~ \| A v \| \leq c \| v \| ~&&~ \text{ for all } ~&&v \in V \} \\ &= \sup &&\{ \| Av \| ~&&:~ \| v \| \leq 1 ~&&~\mbox{ and } ~&&v \in V \} \\ &= \sup &&\{ \| Av \| ~&&:~ \| v \| < 1 ~&&~\mbox{ and } ~&&v \in V \} \\ &= \sup &&\{ \| Av \| ~&&:~ \| v \| \in \{0,1\} ~&&~\mbox{ and } ~&&v \in V \} \\ &= \sup &&\{ \| Av \| ~&&:~ \| v \| = 1 ~&&~\mbox{ and } ~&&v \in V \} \;\;\;\text{ this equality holds if and only if } V \neq \{ 0 \} \\ &= \sup &&\bigg\{ \frac{\| Av \|}{\| v \|} ~&&:~ v \ne 0 ~&&~\mbox{ and } ~&&v \in V \bigg\} \;\;\;\text{ this equality holds if and only if } V \neq \{ 0 \}. \\ \end{alignedat}$$

If $V = \{0\}$ then the sets in the last two rows will be empty, and consequently their [supremums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) over the set $[-\infty, \infty]$ will equal $-\infty$ instead of the correct value of $0.$ If the supremum is taken over the set $[0, \infty]$ instead, then the supremum of the empty set is $0$ and the formulas hold for any $V.$

Importantly, a linear operator $A : V \to W$ is not, in general, guaranteed to achieve its norm $\|A\|_\text{op} = \sup \{\|A v\| : \|v\| \leq 1, v \in V\}$ on the closed unit ball $\{v \in V : \|v\| \leq 1\},$ meaning that there might not exist any vector $u \in V$ of norm $\|u\| \leq 1$ such that $\|A\|_\text{op} = \|A u\|$ (if such a vector does exist and if $A \neq 0,$ then $u$ would necessarily have unit norm $\|u\| = 1$). R.C. James proved [James's theorem](wikipedia://en/17868139) in 1964, which states that a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $V$ is [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091) if and only if every [bounded linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_functional) $f \in V^*$ achieves its [norm](wikipedia://en/13525027) on the closed unit ball.[^n1] It follows, in particular, that every non-reflexive Banach space has some bounded linear functional (a type of bounded linear operator) that does not achieve its norm on the closed unit ball.

If $A : V \to W$ is bounded then[^n2]

$$\|A\|_\text{op} = \sup \left\{\left|w^*(A v)\right| : \|v\| \leq 1, \left\|w^*\right\| \leq 1 \text{ where } v \in V, w^* \in W^*\right\}$$

and[^n2]

$$\|A\|_\text{op} = \left\|{}^tA\right\|_\text{op}$$

where ${}^t A : W^* \to V^*$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/1442470) of $A : V \to W,$ which is the linear operator defined by $w^* \,\mapsto\, w^* \circ A.$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The operator norm is indeed a norm on the space of all [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961) between $V$ and $W$. This means

$$\|A\|_\text{op} \geq 0 \mbox{ and } \|A\|_\text{op} = 0 \mbox{ if and only if } A = 0,$$

$$\|aA\|_\text{op} = |a| \|A\|_\text{op} \mbox{ for every scalar } a ,$$

$$\|A + B\|_\text{op} \leq \|A\|_\text{op} + \|B\|_\text{op}.$$

The following inequality is an immediate consequence of the definition:

$$\|Av\| \leq \|A\|_\text{op} \|v\| \ \mbox{ for every }\ v \in V.$$

The operator norm is also compatible with the composition, or multiplication, of operators: if $V$, $W$ and $X$ are three normed spaces over the same base field, and $A : V \to W$ and $B : W \to X$ are two bounded operators, then it is a [sub-multiplicative norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-multiplicative_norm), that is:

$$\|BA\|_\text{op} \leq \|B\|_\text{op} \|A\|_\text{op}.$$

For bounded operators on $V$, this implies that operator multiplication is jointly continuous.

It follows from the definition that if a sequence of operators converges in operator norm, it [converges uniformly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converges_uniformly) on bounded sets.

:::

## Table of common operator norms {#Table_of_common_operator_norms}

By choosing different norms for the codomain, used in computing $\|Av\|$, and the domain, used in computing $\|v\|$, we obtain different values for the operator norm. Some common operator norms are easy to calculate, and others are [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard). Except for the NP-hard norms, all these norms can be calculated in $N^2$ operations (for an $N \times N$ matrix), with the exception of the $\ell_2 - \ell_2$ norm (which requires $N^3$ operations for the exact answer, or fewer if you approximate it with the [power method](wikipedia://en/5975550) or [Lanczos iterations](wikipedia://en/2593852)).

*Computability of Operator Norms[^o18]*

::: {#o17 .table cue=table name="Computability of Operator Norms"}

|  |  | Co-domain |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  | $\ell_1$ | $\ell_2$ | $\ell_\infty$ |
| Domain | $\ell_1$ | Maximum $\ell_1$ norm of a column | Maximum $\ell_2$ norm of a column | Maximum $\ell_{\infty}$ norm of a column |
|  | $\ell_2$ | NP-hard | Maximum singular value | Maximum $\ell_2$ norm of a row |
|  | $\ell_\infty$ | NP-hard | NP-hard | Maximum $\ell_1$ norm of a row |

:::

The norm of the [adjoint](wikipedia://en/217548) or transpose can be computed as follows. We have that for any $p, q,$ then $\|A\|_{p\rightarrow q} = \|A^*\|_{q'\rightarrow p'}$ where $p', q'$ are [Hölder conjugate](wikipedia://en/191538) to $p, q,$ that is, $1/p + 1/p' = 1$ and $1/q + 1/q' = 1.$

## Operators on a Hilbert space {#Operators_on_a_Hilbert_space}

Suppose $H$ is a real or complex [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). If $A : H \to H$ is a bounded linear operator, then we have

$$\|A\|_\text{op} = \left\|A^*\right\|_\text{op}$$

and

$$\left\|A^* A\right\|_\text{op} = \|A\|_\text{op}^2,$$

where $A^{*}$ denotes the [adjoint operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_operator) of $A$ (which in [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697) with the standard [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) corresponds to the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of the matrix $A$).

In general, the [spectral radius](wikipedia://en/386169) of $A$ is bounded above by the operator norm of $A$:

$$\rho(A) \leq \|A\|_\text{op}.$$

To see why equality may not always hold, consider the [Jordan canonical form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_canonical_form) of a matrix in the finite-dimensional case. Because there are non-zero entries on the superdiagonal, equality may be violated. The [quasinilpotent operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasinilpotent_operator) is one class of such examples. A nonzero quasinilpotent operator $A$ has spectrum $\{0\}.$ So $\rho(A) = 0$ while $\|A\|_\text{op} > 0.$

However, when a matrix $N$ is [normal](wikipedia://en/170366), its [Jordan canonical form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_canonical_form) is diagonal (up to unitary equivalence); this is the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495). In that case it is easy to see that

$$\rho(N) = \|N\|_\text{op}.$$

This formula can sometimes be used to compute the operator norm of a given bounded operator $A$: define the [Hermitian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_operator) $B = A^{*} A,$ determine its spectral radius, and take the [square root](wikipedia://en/2929701) to obtain the operator norm of $A.$

The space of bounded operators on $H,$ with the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) induced by operator norm, is not [separable](wikipedia://en/27855). For example, consider the [Lp space](wikipedia://en/45194) $L^2[0, 1],$ which is a Hilbert space. For $0 < t \leq 1,$ let $\Omega_t$ be the [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $[0, t],$ and $P_t$ be the [multiplication operator](wikipedia://en/976642) given by $\Omega_t,$ that is,

$$P_t (f) = f \cdot \Omega_t.$$

Then each $P_t$ is a bounded operator with operator norm 1 and

$$\left\|P_t - P_s\right\|_\text{op} = 1 \quad \mbox{ for all } \quad t \neq s.$$

But $\{P_t : 0 < t \leq 1\}$ is an [uncountable set](wikipedia://en/32248). This implies the space of bounded operators on $L^2([0, 1])$ is not separable, in operator norm. One can compare this with the fact that the sequence space $\ell^{\infty}$ is not separable.

The [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) of all bounded operators on a Hilbert space, together with the operator norm and the adjoint operation, yields a [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184).
