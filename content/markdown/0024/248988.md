# Product rule

*Image: Schema\_Règle\_produit.png.* Geometric illustration of a proof of the product rule[^o1]

Differential

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Product rule"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the **product rule** (or **Leibniz rule**[^o3] or **Leibniz product rule**) is a formula used to find the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of products of two or more [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functions_%28mathematics%29). For two functions, it may be stated in [Lagrange's notation](wikipedia://en/10265555#Lagrange's_notation) as

$$(u \cdot v)' = u ' \cdot v + u \cdot v'$$

or in [Leibniz's notation](wikipedia://en/410712) as

$$\frac{d}{dx} (u\cdot v) = \frac{du}{dx} \cdot v + u \cdot \frac{dv}{dx}.$$

:::

The rule may be extended or generalized to products of three or more functions, to a rule for higher-order derivatives of a product, and to other contexts.

## Discovery {#Discovery}

Discovery of this rule is credited to [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz), who demonstrated it using ["infinitesimals"](wikipedia://en/643021#Motivation) (a precursor to the modern [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28calculus%29)).[^o5] (However, J. M. Child, a translator of Leibniz's papers,[^o6] argues that it is due to [Isaac Barrow](wikipedia://en/97805).) Here is Leibniz's argument:[^o7] Let *u* and *v* be functions. Then *d(uv)* is the same thing as the difference between two successive *uv**s; let one of these be*****uv*, and the other*u+du*times*v+dv*; then:***

$$\begin{aligned} d(u\cdot v) & {} = (u + du)\cdot (v + dv) - u\cdot v \\ & {} = u\cdot dv + v\cdot du + du\cdot dv. \end{aligned}$$

Since the term *du*·*dv* is "negligible" (compared to *du* and *dv*), Leibniz concluded that

$$d(u\cdot v) = v\cdot du + u\cdot dv$$

and this is indeed the differential form of the product rule. If we divide through by the differential *dx*, we obtain

$$\frac{d}{dx} (u\cdot v) = v \cdot \frac{du}{dx} + u \cdot \frac{dv}{dx}$$

which can also be written in [Lagrange's notation](wikipedia://en/7921#Lagrange.27s_notation) as

$$(u\cdot v)' = v\cdot u' + u\cdot v'.$$

### First proofs {#First_proofs}

Both Leibniz and [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) gave [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) that are not [rigorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor) by modern standards. Leibniz reasoned with "[infinitely smaller quantities](wikipedia://en/160990)", interpreting products as [areas](wikipedia://en/1209) of [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048), while Newton reasoned with "[flowing quantities](wikipedia://en/52956308)".[^o9][^o10]

## Examples {#Examples}

- Suppose we want to differentiate $f(x)=x^2\text {sin}(x).$ By using the product rule, one gets the derivative $f'(x)=2x\cdot \text {sin}(x)+x^2\text {cos} (x)$ (since the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x,$ and the derivative of the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function is the cosine function).
- One special case of the product rule is the [constant multiple rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_multiple_rule), which states: if $c$ is a number, and $f(x)$ is a differentiable function, then $c\cdot f(x)$ is also differentiable, and its derivative is $(cf)'(x)=c \cdot f'(x).$ This follows from the product rule since the derivative of any constant is zero. This, combined with the sum rule for derivatives, shows that differentiation is [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation).
- The rule for [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) is derived from the product rule, as is (a weak version of) the [quotient rule](wikipedia://en/209128). (It is a "weak" version in that it does not prove that the quotient is differentiable but only says what its derivative is if it is differentiable.)

## Proofs {#Proofs}

### Limit definition of derivative {#Limit_definition_of_derivative}

Let $h(x) = f(x)g(x)$ and suppose that $f$ and $g$ are each differentiable at $x$. We want to prove that $h$ is differentiable at $x$ and that its derivative, $h(x)$, is given by $f(x)g(x) + f(x)g(x)$. To do this, $f(x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x+\Delta x)$ (which is zero, and thus does not change the value) is added to the numerator to permit its factoring, and then properties of limits are used.

$$\begin{aligned} h'(x) &= \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} \frac{h(x+\Delta x)-h(x)}{\Delta x} \\[5pt] &= \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} \frac{f(x+\Delta x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x)}{\Delta x} \\[5pt] &= \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} \frac{f(x+\Delta x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x+\Delta x)+f(x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x)}{\Delta x} \\[5pt] &= \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} \frac{\big[f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)\big] \cdot g(x+\Delta x) + f(x) \cdot \big[g(x+\Delta x)-g(x)\big]}{\Delta x} \\[5pt] &= \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} \frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x} \cdot \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} g(x+\Delta x) + \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} f(x) \cdot \lim_{\Delta x\to 0} \frac{g(x+\Delta x)-g(x)}{\Delta x} \\[5pt] &= f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x). \end{aligned}$$

The fact that $\lim_{\Delta x\to0} g(x+\Delta x) = g(x)$ follows from the fact that differentiable functions are continuous.

### Linear approximations {#Linear_approximations}

By definition, if $f, g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ are differentiable at $x$, then we can write [linear approximations](wikipedia://en/1107596):

$$f(x+h) = f(x) + f'(x)h + \varepsilon_1(h)$$

and

$$g(x+h) = g(x) + g'(x)h + \varepsilon_2(h),$$

where the error terms are small with respect to *h*: that is, $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\varepsilon_1(h)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\varepsilon_2(h)}{h} = 0,$ [also written](wikipedia://en/44578#Little-o_notation) $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2 \sim o(h)$. Then:

$$\begin{aligned} f(x+h)g(x+h) - f(x)g(x) &= (f(x) + f'(x)h +\varepsilon_1(h))(g(x) + g'(x)h + \varepsilon_2(h)) - f(x)g(x) \\[.5em] &= f(x)g(x) + f'(x)g(x)h + f(x)g'(x)h -f(x)g(x) + \text{error terms} \\[.5em] &= f'(x)g(x)h + f(x)g'(x)h + o(h) . \end{aligned}$$

The "error terms" consist of items such as $f(x)\varepsilon_2(h), f'(x)g'(x)h^2$ and $hf'(x)\varepsilon_1(h)$ which are easily seen to have magnitude $o(h).$ Dividing by $h$ and taking the limit $h\to 0$ gives the result.

### Quarter squares {#Quarter_squares}

This proof uses the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) and the [quarter square function](wikipedia://en/57411#Quarter_square_multiplication) $q(x)=\tfrac14x^2$ with derivative $q'(x) = \tfrac12 x$. We have:

$$uv=q(u+v)-q(u-v),$$

and differentiating both sides gives:

$$\begin{aligned} f' &= q'(u+v)(u'+v') - q'(u-v)(u'-v') \\[4pt] &= \left(\tfrac12(u+v)(u'+v')\right) - \left(\tfrac12(u-v)(u'-v')\right) \\[4pt] &= \tfrac12(uu' + vu' + uv' + vv') - \tfrac12(uu' - vu' - uv' + vv') \\[4pt] &= vu'+uv' . \end{aligned}$$

### Multivariable chain rule {#Multivariable_chain_rule}

The product rule can be considered a special case of the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) for several variables, applied to the multiplication function $m(u,v) = uv$:

$${d (uv) \over dx} = \frac{\partial(uv)}{\partial u}\frac{du}{dx}+\frac{\partial (uv)}{\partial v}\frac{dv}{dx} = v \frac{du}{dx} + u \frac{dv}{dx}.$$

### Non-standard analysis {#Non-standard_analysis}

Let *u* and *v* be continuous functions in *x*, and let *dx*, *du* and *dv* be [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990) within the framework of [non-standard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis), specifically the [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429). Using st to denote the [standard part function](wikipedia://en/19145800) that associates to a finite hyperreal number the real infinitely close to it, this gives

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d(uv)}{dx} &= \operatorname{st}\left(\frac{(u + du)(v + dv) - uv}{dx}\right) \\ &= \operatorname{st}\left(\frac{uv + u \cdot dv + v \cdot du + du \cdot dv -uv}{dx}\right) \\ &= \operatorname{st}\left(\frac{u \cdot dv + v \cdot du + du \cdot dv}{dx}\right) \\ &= \operatorname{st}\left(u \frac{dv}{dx} + (v + dv) \frac{du}{dx}\right) \\ &= u \frac{dv}{dx} + v \frac{du}{dx}. \end{aligned}$$

This was essentially [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz)'s proof exploiting the [transcendental law of homogeneity](wikipedia://en/35889821) (in place of the standard part above).

### Smooth infinitesimal analysis {#Smooth_infinitesimal_analysis}

In the context of Lawvere's approach to infinitesimals, let $dx$ be a nilsquare infinitesimal. Then $du = u'\ dx$ and $dv = v'\ dx$, so that

$$\begin{aligned} d(uv) & = (u + du)(v + dv) -uv \\ & = uv + u \cdot dv + v \cdot du + du \cdot dv - uv \\ & = u \cdot dv + v \cdot du + du \cdot dv \\ & = u \cdot dv + v \cdot du \end{aligned}$$

since $du \, dv = u' v' (dx)^2 = 0.$ Dividing by $dx$ then gives $\frac{d(uv)}{dx} = u \frac{dv}{dx} + v \frac{du}{dx}$ or $(uv)' = u \cdot v' + v \cdot u'$.

### Logarithmic differentiation {#Logarithmic_differentiation}

Let $h(x) = f(x) g(x)$. Taking the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of each function and the [natural log](wikipedia://en/21476) of both sides of the equation,

$$\ln|h(x)| = \ln|f(x) g(x)|$$

Applying properties of the absolute value and logarithms,

$$\ln|h(x)| = \ln|f(x)| + \ln|g(x)|$$

Taking the [logarithmic derivative](wikipedia://en/470327) of both sides and then solving for $h'(x)$:

$$\frac{h'(x)}{h(x)} = \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} + \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}$$

Solving for $h'(x)$ and substituting back $f(x) g(x)$ for $h(x)$ gives:

$$\begin{aligned} h'(x) &= h(x)\left(\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} + \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\right) \\ &= f(x) g(x)\left(\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)} + \frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\right) \\ &= f'(x) g(x) + f(x) g'(x). \end{aligned}$$

Note: Taking the absolute value of the functions is necessary for the [logarithmic differentiation](wikipedia://en/21899466) of functions that may have negative values, as logarithms are only [real-valued](wikipedia://en/22284647) for positive arguments. This works because $\tfrac{d}{dx}(\ln |u|) = \tfrac{u'}{u}$, which justifies taking the absolute value of the functions for logarithmic differentiation.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Product of more than two factors {#Product_of_more_than_two_factors}

The product rule can be generalized to products of more than two factors. For example, for three factors we have

$$\frac{d(uvw)}{dx} = \frac{du}{dx}vw + u\frac{dv}{dx}w + uv\frac{dw}{dx}.$$

For a collection of functions $f_1, \dots, f_k$, we have

$$\frac{d}{dx} \left [ \prod_{i=1}^k f_i(x) \right ] = \sum_{i=1}^k \left(\left(\frac{d}{dx} f_i(x) \right) \prod_{j=1,j\ne i}^k f_j(x) \right) = \left( \prod_{i=1}^k f_i(x) \right) \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{f'_i(x)}{f_i(x)} \right).$$

The [logarithmic derivative](wikipedia://en/470327) provides a simpler expression of the last form, as well as a direct proof that does not involve any [recursion](wikipedia://en/25407). The *logarithmic derivative* of a function $f$, denoted here $\operatorname{Logder}(f)$, is the derivative of the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of the function. It follows that

$$\operatorname{Logder}(f)=\frac {f'}f.$$

Using that the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms of the factors, the [sum rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_rule_in_differentiation) for derivatives gives immediately

$$\operatorname{Logder}(f_1\cdots f_k)= \sum_{i=1}^k\operatorname{Logder}(f_i).$$

The last above expression of the derivative of a product is obtained by multiplying both members of this equation by the product of the $f_i.$

### Higher derivatives {#Higher_derivatives}

It can also be generalized to the [general Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/1116115) for the *n*th derivative of a product of two factors, by symbolically expanding according to the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677):

$$d^n(uv) = \sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} \cdot d^{(n-k)}(u)\cdot d^{(k)}(v).$$

Applied at a specific point *x*, the above formula gives:

$$(uv)^{(n)}(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} \cdot u^{(n-k)}(x)\cdot v^{(k)}(x).$$

Furthermore, for the *n*th derivative of an arbitrary number of factors, one has a similar formula with [multinomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/597998):

$$\left(\prod_{i=1}^kf_i\right)^{\!\!(n)}=\sum_{j_1+j_2+\cdots+j_k=n}{n\choose j_1,j_2,\ldots,j_k}\prod_{i=1}^kf_i^{(j_i)}.$$

### Higher partial derivatives {#Higher_partial_derivatives}

For [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565), we have[^o27]

$${\partial^n \over \partial x_1\,\cdots\,\partial x_n} (uv) = \sum_S {\partial^{|S|} u \over \prod_{i\in S} \partial x_i} \cdot {\partial^{n-|S|} v \over \prod_{i\not\in S} \partial x_i}$$

where the index $S$ runs through all $2^{n}$ [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\{1, ..., n\}$, and $S$ is the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of $S$. For example, when $n = 3$,

$$\begin{aligned} & {\partial^3 \over \partial x_1\,\partial x_2\,\partial x_3} (uv) \\[1ex] = {} & u \cdot{\partial^3 v \over \partial x_1\,\partial x_2\,\partial x_3} + {\partial u \over \partial x_1}\cdot{\partial^2 v \over \partial x_2\,\partial x_3} + {\partial u \over \partial x_2}\cdot{\partial^2 v \over \partial x_1\,\partial x_3} + {\partial u \over \partial x_3}\cdot{\partial^2 v \over \partial x_1\,\partial x_2} \\[1ex] & + {\partial^2 u \over \partial x_1\,\partial x_2}\cdot{\partial v \over \partial x_3} + {\partial^2 u \over \partial x_1\,\partial x_3}\cdot{\partial v \over \partial x_2} + {\partial^2 u \over \partial x_2\,\partial x_3}\cdot{\partial v \over \partial x_1} + {\partial^3 u \over \partial x_1\,\partial x_2\,\partial x_3}\cdot v. \\[-3ex]&\end{aligned}$$

### Banach space {#Banach_space}

Suppose *X*, *Y*, and *Z* are [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) (which includes [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)) and *B* : *X* × *Y* → *Z* is a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [bilinear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_operator). Then *B* is differentiable, and its derivative at the point (*x*,*y*) in *X* × *Y* is the [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) *D*<sub>(*x*,*y*)</sub>*B* : *X* × *Y* → *Z* given by

$$(D_\left( x,y \right)\,B)\left( u,v \right) = B\left( u,y \right) + B\left( x,v \right)\qquad\forall (u,v)\in X \times Y.$$

This result can be extended[^o29] to more general topological vector spaces.

### In vector calculus {#In_vector_calculus}

The product rule extends to various product operations of vector functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$:[^o31]

- For [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373): $$(f \cdot \mathbf g)' = f'\cdot \mathbf g + f \cdot \mathbf g'$$
- For [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093): $$(\mathbf f \cdot \mathbf g)' = \mathbf f' \cdot \mathbf g + \mathbf f \cdot \mathbf g'$$
- For [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) of vector functions on $\mathbb{R}^3$: $$(\mathbf f \times \mathbf g)' = \mathbf f' \times \mathbf g + \mathbf f \times \mathbf g'$$

There are also analogues for other analogs of the derivative: if *f* and *g* are scalar fields then there is a product rule with the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461):

$$\nabla (f \cdot g) = \nabla f \cdot g + f \cdot \nabla g$$

Such a rule will hold for any continuous [bilinear](wikipedia://en/4365) product operation. Let *B* : *X* × *Y* → *Z* be a continuous bilinear map between vector spaces, and let *f* and *g* be differentiable functions into *X* and *Y*, respectively. The only properties of multiplication used in the proof using the limit definition of derivative is that multiplication is continuous and bilinear. So for any continuous bilinear operation,

$$H(f, g)' = H(f', g) + H(f, g').$$

This is also a special case of the product rule for bilinear maps in Banach space.

### Derivations in abstract algebra and differential geometry {#Derivations_in_abstract_algebra_and_differential_geometry}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the product rule is the defining property of a [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29). In this terminology, the product rule states that the derivative operator is a derivation on functions.

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a [tangent vector](wikipedia://en/191999) to a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) *M* at a point *p* may be defined abstractly as an operator on real-valued functions which behaves like a [directional derivative](wikipedia://en/487859) at *p*: that is, a [linear functional](wikipedia://en/214137) *v* which is a derivation,

$$v(fg) = v(f)\,g(p) + f(p) \, v(g).$$

Generalizing (and dualizing) the formulas of vector calculus to an *n*-dimensional manifold *M,* one may take [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) of degrees *k* and *l*, denoted $\alpha\in \Omega^k(M), \beta\in \Omega^\ell(M)$, with the wedge or [exterior product](wikipedia://en/221537) operation $\alpha\wedge\beta\in \Omega^{k+\ell}(M)$, as well as the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) $d:\Omega^m(M)\to\Omega^{m+1}(M)$. Then one has the [graded Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/2415863):

$$d(\alpha\wedge\beta)= d\alpha \wedge \beta + (-1)^{k} \alpha\wedge d\beta.$$

## Applications {#Applications}

Among the applications of the product rule is a proof that

$${d \over dx} x^n = nx^{n-1}$$

when *n* is a positive integer (this rule is true even if *n* is not positive or is not an integer, but the proof of that must rely on other methods). The proof is by [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881) on the exponent *n*. If *n* = 0 then *x*<sup>*n*</sup> is constant and *nx*<sup>*n* − 1</sup> = 0. The rule holds in that case because the derivative of a constant function is 0. If the rule holds for any particular exponent *n*, then for the next value, *n* + 1, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d x^{n+1}}{dx} &{}= \frac{d}{dx} \left( x^n\cdot x\right) \\[1ex] &{}= x \frac{d}{dx} x^n + x^n \frac{d}{dx} x & \text{(the product rule is used here)} \\[1ex] &{}= x\left(n x^{n-1}\right) + x^n\cdot 1 & \text{(the induction hypothesis is used here)} \\[1ex] &{}= \left(n + 1\right) x^n. \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, if the proposition is true for *n*, it is true also for *n* + 1, and therefore for all natural *n*.
