# Elastic-rebound theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Elastic-rebound theory"}

*Image: elasticRebound Thum.png.* Elastic rebound

:::

In [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), the **elastic-rebound theory** is an explanation for how [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) is released during an [earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake).

As the Earth's [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crust_%28geology%29) deforms, the rocks which span the opposing sides of a [fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_%28geology%29) are subjected to [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress). Slowly they [deform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28engineering%29), until their internal rigidity is exceeded. Then they [separate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fracture) with a rupture along the fault; the sudden movement releases [accumulated energy](wikipedia://en/22707107), and the rocks snap back almost to their original shape. The previously solid mass is divided between the two slowly moving plates, the energy released through the surroundings in a [seismic wave](wikipedia://en/89830).

## Theory {#Theory}

After the great [1906 San Francisco earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1906_San_Francisco_earthquake), geophysicist [Harry Fielding Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Fielding_Reid) examined the displacement of the ground surface along the [San Andreas Fault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Andreas_Fault) in the 50 years before the earthquake.[^o3] He found evidence for 3.2 meters of bending during that period.[^o4] He concluded that the quake must have been the result of the elastic rebound of the [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_%28materials_science%29) energy stored in the rocks on either side of the fault. Later measurements using the [global positioning system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_positioning_system) largely support Reid's theory as the basis of seismic movement.

## Explanation {#Explanation}

The two sides of an active but locked fault are slowly moving in different directions, where elastic strain energy builds up in any rock mass that adjoins them. Thus, if a road is built straight across the fault as in Time 1 of the figure panel, it is perpendicular to the [fault trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_trace) at point E, where the fault is locked. The overall fault movement (large arrows) causes the rocks across the locked fault to accrue [elastic deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_deformation), as in Time 2. This deformation may build at the rate of a few centimeters per year. When the accumulated [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_%28materials_science%29) is great enough to overcome the [strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensile_strength) of the rocks, the result is a sudden break, or a springing back to the original shape as much as possible, a jolt which is felt on the surface as an earthquake. This sudden movement results in the shift of the roadway's surface, as shown in Time 3. The stored energy is released partly as heat, partly in alteration of the rock, and partly as a [seismic wave](wikipedia://en/89830).
