# Measurable cardinal

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Measurable cardinal"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), a **measurable cardinal** is a certain kind of [large cardinal](wikipedia://en/383644) number. In order to define the concept, one introduces a two-valued [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on a cardinal $\kappa$, or more generally on any set. For a cardinal $\kappa$, it can be described as a subdivision of all of its [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) into large and small sets such that $\kappa$ itself is large, the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) and all [singletons](wikipedia://en/381798) $\{\alpha\}$ with $\alpha\in\kappa$ are small, [complements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_complement) of small sets are large and vice versa. The [intersection](wikipedia://en/41780044) of fewer than $\kappa$ large sets is again large.[^o2]

:::

It turns out that [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) cardinals endowed with a two-valued measure are large cardinals whose existence cannot be proved from [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC).[^o3]

The concept of a measurable cardinal was introduced by [Stanisław Ulam](wikipedia://en/41531) in 1930.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Formally, a measurable cardinal is an uncountable [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173) $\kappa$ such that there exists a $\kappa$-additive, non-trivial, 0-1-valued [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) $\mu$ on the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $\kappa$.

Here, $\kappa$-additive means that for every $\lambda<\kappa$ and every $\lambda$-sized collection $\{A_\beta\}_{\beta<\lambda}$ of pairwise disjoint subsets $A_\beta\subseteq\kappa$, we have

$$\mu\Big(\bigcup_{\beta<\lambda}A_\beta\Big)=\sum_{\beta<\lambda}\mu(A_\beta).$$

Equivalently, $\kappa$ is a measurable cardinal if and only if it is an uncountable cardinal with a $\kappa$-complete, non-principal [ultrafilter](wikipedia://en/31911). This means that the intersection of any *strictly less than* $\kappa$-many sets in the ultrafilter is also in the ultrafilter.

Equivalently, $\kappa$ is measurable if it is the [critical point](wikipedia://en/4888510) of a non-trivial [elementary embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_embedding) of the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe_%28set_theory%29) $V$ into a [transitive class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_class) $M$ and $^\kappa M\subseteq M$. This equivalence is due to [Jerome Keisler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Keisler) and [Dana Scott](wikipedia://en/161875), and uses the [ultrapower](wikipedia://en/273642) construction from [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858). Since $V$ is a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class), a technical problem that is not usually present when considering ultrapowers needs to be addressed, by what is now called [Scott's trick](wikipedia://en/22077085).

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

It is trivial to note that if $\kappa$ admits a non-trivial $\kappa$-additive measure, then $\kappa$ must be [regular](wikipedia://en/374128): by non-triviality and $\kappa$-additivity, any subset of cardinality less than $\kappa$ must have measure 0, and then by $\kappa$-additivity again, this means that the entire set must not be a union of fewer than $\kappa$ sets of cardinality less than $\kappa$. Finally, if $\lambda<\kappa$ then it can't be the case that $\kappa\leq 2^\lambda$. If this were the case, we could identify $\kappa$ with some collection of 0-1 sequences of length $\lambda$. For each position in the sequence, either the subset of sequences with 1 in that position or the subset with 0 in that position would have to have measure 1. The intersection of these $\lambda$-many measure 1 subsets would thus also have to have measure 1, but it would contain exactly one sequence, which would contradict the non-triviality of the measure. Thus, assuming the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) (as the trichotomy of cardinality between κ and 2<sup>λ</sup>), we can infer that $\kappa$ is a [strong limit cardinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_limit_cardinal), which completes the proof of its [inaccessibility](wikipedia://en/248076).

Although it follows from [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC) that every measurable cardinal is [inaccessible](wikipedia://en/248076) (and is [ineffable](wikipedia://en/270005), [Ramsey](wikipedia://en/326533), etc.), it is consistent with [ZF](wikipedia://en/152214) that a measurable cardinal can be a [successor cardinal](wikipedia://en/375210). It follows from ZF + [AD](wikipedia://en/1002300) that $\omega_1$ is measurable,[^o9] and that every subset of $\omega_1$ contains or is disjoint from a [closed and unbounded](wikipedia://en/1251559) subset.

Ulam showed that the smallest cardinal $\kappa$ that admits a non-trivial countably-additive two-valued measure must in fact admit a $\kappa$-additive measure. (If there were some collection of fewer than $\kappa$ measure-0 subsets whose union was $\kappa$, then the induced measure on this collection would be a counterexample to the minimality of $\kappa$.) From there, one can prove (with the axiom of choice) that the least such cardinal must be inaccessible.

If $\kappa$ is measurable and $p\in V_\kappa$ and $M$ (the ultrapower of $V$) satisfies $\psi(\kappa,p)$, then the set of $\alpha<\kappa$ such that $V$ satisfies $\psi(\alpha,p)$ is stationary in $\kappa$ (actually a set of measure 1). In particular, if $\psi$ is a $\Pi_1$ formula and $V$ satisfies $\psi(\kappa,p)$, then $M$ satisfies it and thus $V$ satisfies $\psi(\alpha,p)$ for a stationary set of $\alpha<\kappa$. This property can be used to show that $\kappa$ is a limit of most types of large cardinals that are weaker than measurable. Notice that the ultrafilter or measure witnessing that $\kappa$ is measurable cannot be in $M$ since the smallest such measurable cardinal would have to have another such below it, which is impossible.

If one starts with an elementary embedding $j_1$ of $V$ into $M_1$ with [critical point](wikipedia://en/4888510) $\kappa$, then one can define an ultrafilter $U$ on $\kappa$ as $\{S\subseteq\kappa \mid \kappa\in j_1(S)\}$. Then, taking an ultrapower of $V$ over $U$, we can get another elementary embedding $j_2$ of $V$ into $M_2$. However, it is important to remember that $j_2\neq j_1$. Thus, other types of large cardinals such as [strong cardinals](wikipedia://en/248104) may also be measurable, but not using the same embedding. It can be shown that a strong cardinal $\kappa$ is measurable and also has $\kappa$-many measurable cardinals below it.

Every measurable cardinal $\kappa$ is a 0-[huge cardinal](wikipedia://en/248117) because $^\kappa M\subseteq M$, that is, every function from $\kappa$ to $M$, is in $M$. Consequently, $V_{\kappa+1}\subseteq M$.

:::

## Implications of existence {#Implications_of_existence}

If a measurable cardinal exists, every set of reals at level $\mathbf{\Sigma}^1_2$ of the [projective hierarchy](wikipedia://en/1236542) is [Lebesgue-measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue-measurable_set).

## Real-valued measurable {#Real-valued_measurable}

A cardinal $\kappa$ is called **real-valued measurable** if there is a $\kappa$-additive [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) on the power set of $\kappa$ that vanishes on singletons. Real-valued measurable cardinals were introduced by (txt). (Banach Kuratowski 1929) showed that the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) implies that $\mathfrak{c}$ is not real-valued measurable. (txt) showed (see below for parts of Ulam's proof) that real valued measurable cardinals are weakly inaccessible (they are in fact [weakly Mahlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_Mahlo)). All measurable cardinals are real-valued measurable, and a real-valued measurable cardinal $\kappa$ is measurable if and only if $\kappa$ is greater than $\mathfrak{c}$. Thus a cardinal is measurable if and only if it is real-valued measurable and strongly inaccessible. A real valued measurable cardinal less than or equal to $\mathfrak{c}$ exists if and only if there is a [countably additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_additivity) extension of the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) to all sets of real numbers if and only if there is an [atomless](wikipedia://en/3058373) probability measure on the power set of some non-empty set.

(Solovay 1971) showed that existence of measurable cardinals in ZFC, real-valued measurable cardinals in ZFC, and measurable cardinals in ZF, are [equiconsistent](wikipedia://en/3435716).

### Weak inaccessibility of real-valued measurable cardinals {#Weak_inaccessibility_of_real-valued_measurable_cardinals}

Let $\mu$ be an outer measure on a set $X$. Say that a cardinal number $\alpha$ is an *Ulam number* if whenever[^o17][^o18]

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\mu(X)\leq\infty, \tag{1}$$

:::

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

\mu(\{x\})=0$ for every $x\in X$, (2)

:::

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

all $A\subseteq X$ are [$\mu$-measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carathéodory-measurable_set), (3)

:::

then $|X|\leq\alpha$ implies $\mu(X)=0$.

Equivalently, if $F$ is a set of pairwise disjoint subsets of $X$, $\alpha$ is an Ulam number if whenever

1. $$\mu(\textstyle\bigcup F)<\infty,$$
1. $\mu(A)=0$ for $A\in F$,
1. $\textstyle\bigcup G$ is $\mu$-measurable for every $G\subset F$,

then $|F|\leq\alpha$ implies $\mu(\textstyle\bigcup F)=0$.

The smallest infinite cardinal [$\aleph_{0}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph-zero) is an Ulam number. The class of Ulam numbers is closed under the [cardinal successor](wikipedia://en/375210) operation.[^o22] If an infinite cardinal $\beta$ has an immediate predecessor $\alpha$ that is an Ulam number, assume $\mu$ satisfies properties ((1))–((3)) with $X=\beta$. In the [von Neumann model](wikipedia://en/26547932#Von_Neumann_definition_of_ordinals) of ordinals and cardinals, for each $x\in\beta$, choose an [injective function](wikipedia://en/45196) $f_x:x\to\alpha$ and define the sets

$$U(b,a)=\{x\in\beta\mid f_x(b)=a\}.$$

Since the functions $f_x$ are injective, the sets

$\{U(b,a)\mid b\in\beta\}$ with $a\in\alpha$ fixed

and

$\{U(b,a)\mid a\in\alpha\}$ with $b\in\beta$ fixed

are pairwise disjoint. By property ((1)) of $\mu$, the set

$$\{b\in\beta\mid \mu(U(b,a))>0\}$$

is [countable](wikipedia://en/6026), and hence

$$\big|\{(b,a)\in\beta\times\alpha\mid \mu(U(b,a))>0\}\big| \leq \aleph_0\cdot\alpha.$$

Thus, there is a $b_0\in\beta$ such that

$\mu(U(b_0,a))=0$ for all $a\in\alpha$,

which implies, since $\alpha$ is an Ulam number and using the second definition, that

$$\mu\Big(\bigcup_{a\in\alpha}U(b_0,a)\Big)=0.$$

If $b_0<x<\beta$ and $f_x(b_0)=a_x$ then $x\in U(b_0,a_x)$. Thus

$$\beta=b_0\cup\{b_0\}\cup \bigcup_{a\in\alpha} U(b_0,a).$$

By property ((1)), $\mu(\{b_0\})=0$, and since $|b_0|\leq\alpha$, by ((3)), ((1)) and ((2)), $\mu(b_0)=0$. It follows that $\mu(\beta)=0$. The conclusion is that $\beta$ is an Ulam number.

There is a similar proof[^o23] that the supremum of a set $S$ of Ulam numbers with $|S|$ an Ulam number is again a Ulam number. Together with the previous result, this implies that a cardinal that is not an Ulam number is [weakly inaccessible](wikipedia://en/248076).
