# Ronald Graham

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Ronald Graham"}

**Ronald Lewis Graham** (October 31, 1935 – July 6, 2020) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) credited by the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) as "one of the principal architects of the rapid development worldwide of [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492) in recent years".[^o4] He was president of both the American Mathematical Society and the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772), and his honors included the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) for lifetime achievement and election to the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences).

:::

After graduate study at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), Graham worked for many years at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) and later at the [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego). He did important work in [scheduling theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheduling_algorithm), [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175), and [quasi-randomness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-randomness),[^o5] and many topics in mathematics are named after him. He published six books and about 400 papers, and had nearly 200 co-authors, including many collaborative works with his wife [Fan Chung](wikipedia://en/246562) and with [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284).

Graham has been featured in *[Ripley's Believe It or Not!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripley's_Believe_It_or_Not!)* for being not only "one of the world's foremost mathematicians", but also an accomplished trampolinist and juggler. He served as president of the [International Jugglers' Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Jugglers'_Association).[^o5][^o6][^o13]

## Biography {#Biography}

Graham was born in [Taft, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taft,_California), on October 31, 1935;[^o8] his father was an oil field worker and later merchant marine. Despite Graham's later interest in gymnastics, he was small and non-athletic.[^o9] He grew up moving frequently between California and Georgia, skipping several grades of school in these moves, and never staying at any one school longer than a year.[^o3][^o9] As a teenager, he moved to Florida with his then-divorced mother, where he went to but did not finish high school. Instead, at the age of 15, he won a [Ford Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Foundation) scholarship to the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago), where he learned [gymnastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnastics) but took no mathematics courses.[^o3]

After three years, when his scholarship expired, he moved to the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), officially as a student of electrical engineering but also studying [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) under [D. H. Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._H._Lehmer),[^o3] and winning a title as California state trampoline champion.[^o9] He enlisted in the [United States Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force) in 1955, when he reached the age of eligibility,[^o12] left Berkeley without a degree, and was stationed in [Fairbanks, Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairbanks,_Alaska), where he finally completed a bachelor's degree in physics in 1959 at the [University of Alaska Fairbanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Alaska_Fairbanks).[^o3] Returning to Berkeley for graduate study, he received his Ph.D. in mathematics in 1962. His dissertation, supervised by Lehmer, was *On Finite Sums of Rational Numbers*.[^o23] While a graduate student, he supported himself by performing on trampoline in a circus,[^o12] and married Nancy Young, an undergraduate mathematics student at Berkeley; they had two children.[^o3]

*Image: Ronald graham couple with erdos 1986.jpg.* Ronald Graham, his wife [Fan Chung](wikipedia://en/246562), and [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284), Japan 1986

After completing his doctorate, Graham went to work in 1962 at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) and later as Director of Information Sciences at [AT&T Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT&T_Labs), both in [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey). In 1963, at a conference in Colorado, he met the Hungarian mathematician [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) (1913–1996),[^o3] who became a close friend and frequent research collaborator. Graham was chagrined to be beaten in [ping-pong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ping-pong) by Erdős, then already middle-aged; he returned to New Jersey determined to improve his game, and eventually became Bell Labs champion and won a state title in the game.[^o3] Graham later popularized the concept of the [Erdős number](wikipedia://en/9742), a measure of distance from Erdős in the collaboration network of mathematicians;[^o10][^o12] his many works with Erdős include two books of [open problems](wikipedia://en/1333335)B1B5 and Erdős's final posthumous paper.A15 Graham divorced in the 1970s; in 1983 he married his Bell Labs colleague and frequent coauthor [Fan Chung](wikipedia://en/246562).[^o3]

While at Bell Labs, Graham also took a position at [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University) as University Professor of Mathematical Sciences in 1986, and served as president of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) from 1993 to 1994. He became Chief Scientist of the Labs in 1995.[^o3] He retired from AT&T in 1999 after 37 years of service,[^o11] and moved to the [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego) (UCSD), as the Irwin and Joan Jacobs Endowed Professor of Computer and Information Science.[^o3][^o12] At UCSD, he also became chief scientist at the [California Institute for Telecommunications and Information Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_for_Telecommunications_and_Information_Technology).[^o12][^o13] In 2003–04, he was president of the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772).[^o3]

Graham died of [bronchiectasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronchiectasis)[^o14] on July 6, 2020, aged 84, in [La Jolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Jolla), California.[^o8][^o15]

## Contributions {#Contributions}

Graham made important contributions in multiple areas of mathematics and theoretical computer science. He published about 400 papers, a quarter of those with Chung,[^o17] and six books, including *[Concrete Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_Mathematics)* with [Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) and [Oren Patashnik](wikipedia://en/4247261).B4 The Erdős Number Project lists him as having nearly 200 coauthors.[^o18] He was the [doctoral advisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctoral_advisor) of nine students, one each at the [City University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_New_York) and [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University) while he was at Bell Labs, and seven at UC San Diego.[^o23]

Notable topics in mathematics named after Graham include the [Erdős–Graham problem](wikipedia://en/3467973) on [Egyptian fractions](wikipedia://en/336349), the [Graham–Rothschild theorem](wikipedia://en/64862660) in the [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175) of [parameter words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_word) and [Graham's number](wikipedia://en/234232) derived from it, the [Graham–Pollak theorem](wikipedia://en/64527577) and [Graham's pebbling conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham's_pebbling_conjecture) in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the [Coffman–Graham algorithm](wikipedia://en/31501543) for approximate scheduling and graph drawing, and the [Graham scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_scan) algorithm for [convex hulls](wikipedia://en/40634). He also began the study of [primefree sequences](wikipedia://en/2716293), the [Boolean Pythagorean triples problem](wikipedia://en/50650284), the [biggest little polygon](wikipedia://en/33751968), and [square packing in a square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_packing_in_a_square).

Graham was one of the contributors to the publications of [G. W. Peck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._W._Peck), a pseudonymous mathematical collaboration named for the initials of its members, with Graham as the "G".[^o19] Graham also wrote a paper on the Erdős number, pseudonymously, as Tom Odda.[^o20][^o21]

### Number theory {#Number_theory}

Graham's doctoral dissertation was in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), on [Egyptian fractions](wikipedia://en/336349),[^o9] as is the [Erdős–Graham problem](wikipedia://en/3467973) on whether, for every partition of the integers into finitely many classes, one of these classes has a finite subclass whose reciprocals sum to one. A proof was published by [Ernie Croot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernie_Croot) in 2003.[^o24] Another of Graham's papers on Egyptian fractions was published in 2015 with [Steve Butler](wikipedia://en/58673886) and (nearly 20 years posthumously) Erdős; it was the last of Erdős's papers to be published, making Butler his 512th coauthor.A15[^o25]

In a 1964 paper, Graham began the study of [primefree sequences](wikipedia://en/2716293) by observing that there exist sequences of numbers, defined by the same [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) as the [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number), in which none of the sequence elements is prime.A64 The challenge of constructing more such sequences was later taken up by [Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) and others.[^o26] Graham's 1980 book with Erdős, *Old and new results in combinatorial number theory,* provides a collection of [open problems](wikipedia://en/1333335) from a broad range of subareas within number theory.B1

### Ramsey theory {#Ramsey_theory}

The [Graham–Rothschild theorem](wikipedia://en/64862660) in [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175) was published by Graham and [Bruce Rothschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Lee_Rothschild) in 1971, and applies Ramsey theory to [combinatorial cubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_cube) in [combinatorics on words](wikipedia://en/20913490).A71a Graham gave a [large number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_number) as an upper bound for an instance of this theorem, now known as [Graham's number](wikipedia://en/234232), which was listed in the *[Guinness Book of Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_Book_of_Records)* as the largest number ever used in a mathematical proof,[^o28] although it has since then been surpassed by even larger numbers such as [TREE(3)](wikipedia://en/3606300#TREE%283%29).[^o29]

Graham offered a monetary prize for solving the [Boolean Pythagorean triples problem](wikipedia://en/50650284), another problem in Ramsey theory; the prize was claimed in 2016.[^o30] Graham also published two books on Ramsey theory.B2B3

### Graph theory {#Graph_theory}

*Image: Graham–Pollak partition.svg.* Partition of the edges of the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_6$ into five complete bipartite subgraphs, according to the [Graham–Pollak theorem](wikipedia://en/64527577)

The [Graham–Pollak theorem](wikipedia://en/64527577), which Graham published with [Henry O. Pollak](wikipedia://en/19662367) in two papers in 1971 and 1972,A71bA72a states that if the edges of an $n$-vertex [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) are partitioned into [complete bipartite subgraphs](wikipedia://en/525320), then at least $n-1$ subgraphs are needed. Graham and Pollak provided a simple proof using [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422); despite the combinatorial nature of the statement and multiple publications of alternative proofs since their work, all known proofs require linear algebra.[^o32]

Soon after research in [quasi-random graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-random_graph) began with the work of Andrew Thomason, Graham published in 1989 a result with Chung and [R. M. Wilson](wikipedia://en/22615301) that has been called the "fundamental theorem of quasi-random graphs", stating that many different definitions of these graphs are equivalent.A89a[^o33]

[Graham's pebbling conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham's_pebbling_conjecture), appearing in a 1989 paper by Chung,[^o34] is an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335) on the [pebbling number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pebbling_number) of [Cartesian products of graphs](wikipedia://en/2856852).[^o35]

### Packing, scheduling, and approximation algorithms {#Packing,_scheduling,_and_approximation_algorithms}

Graham's early work on [job shop scheduling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_shop_scheduling)A66A69 introduced the worst-case [approximation ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximation_ratio) into the study of [approximation algorithms](wikipedia://en/563105), and laid the foundations for the later development of [competitive analysis](wikipedia://en/8198743) of [online algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_algorithm).[^o37] This work was later recognized to be important also for the theory of [bin packing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bin_packing),[^o38] an area that Graham later worked in more explicitly.A74

The [Coffman–Graham algorithm](wikipedia://en/31501543), which Graham published with [Edward G. Coffman Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_G._Coffman_Jr.) in 1972,A72b provides an optimal algorithm for two-machine scheduling, and a guaranteed [approximation algorithm](wikipedia://en/563105) for larger numbers of machines. It has also been applied in [layered graph drawing](wikipedia://en/33211287).[^o39]

In a survey article on scheduling algorithms published in 1979, Graham and his coauthors introduced a [three-symbol notation for classifying theoretical scheduling problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notation_for_theoretic_scheduling_problems) according to the system of machines they are to run on, the characteristics of the tasks and resources such as requirements for synchronization or non-interruption, and the performance measure to be optimized.A79 This classification has sometimes been called "Graham notation" or "Graham's notation".[^o40]

### Discrete and computational geometry {#Discrete_and_computational_geometry}

*Image: GrahamScanDemo.gif.* The [Graham scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_scan) algorithm for [convex hulls](wikipedia://en/40634)

[Graham scan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_scan) is a widely used and practical algorithm for [convex hulls](wikipedia://en/40634) of two-dimensional point sets, based on [sorting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorting) the points and then inserting them into the hull in sorted order.[^o42] Graham published the algorithm in 1972.A72c

The [biggest little polygon](wikipedia://en/33751968) problem asks for the polygon of largest area for a given diameter. Surprisingly, as Graham observed, the answer is not always a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306).A75a Graham's 1975 conjecture on the shape of these polygons was finally proven in 2007.[^o43]

In another 1975 publication, Graham and Erdős observed that for [packing unit squares into a larger square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_packing_in_a_square) with non-integer side lengths, one can use tilted squares to leave an uncovered area that is sublinear in the side length of the larger square, unlike the obvious packing with axis-aligned squares.A75b [Klaus Roth](wikipedia://en/787827) and [Bob Vaughan](wikipedia://en/4871951) proved that uncovered area at least proportional to the square root of the side length may sometimes be needed; proving a tight bound on the uncovered area remains an open problem.[^o44]

### Probability and statistics {#Probability_and_statistics}

In [nonparametric statistics](wikipedia://en/223369), a 1977 paper by [Persi Diaconis](wikipedia://en/65467) and Graham studied the statistical properties of [Spearman's footrule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spearman's_footrule), a measure of [rank correlation](wikipedia://en/3316627) that compares two [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) by summing, over each item, the distance between the positions of the item in the two permutations.A77 They compared this measure to other rank correlation methods, resulting in the "Diaconis–Graham inequalities"

$$I+E\le D\le 2I$$

where $D$ is Spearman's footrule, $I$ is the number of [inversions](wikipedia://en/21473801) between the two permutations (a non-normalized version of the [Kendall rank correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/7287830)), and $E$ is the minimum number of two-element swaps needed to obtain one permutation from the other.[^o46]

The [Chung–Diaconis–Graham random process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chung–Diaconis–Graham_random_process) is a [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451) on the integers modulo an odd integer $p$, in which at each step one doubles the previous number and then randomly adds zero, $1$, or $-1$ (modulo $p$). In a 1987 paper, Chung, Diaconis, and Graham studied the [mixing time](wikipedia://en/6101938) of this process, motivated by the study of [pseudorandom number generators](wikipedia://en/45524).A87[^o47]

### Juggling {#Juggling}

*Image: Ronald graham juggling.jpg.* Ronald Graham juggling a four-ball [fountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fountain_%28juggling%29) (1986)

Graham became a capable juggler beginning at age 15, and was practiced in juggling up to six balls.[^o6] (Although a published photo shows him juggling twelve balls,[^o13] it is a manipulated image.[^o5]) He taught [Steve Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Mills_%28juggler%29), a repeat winner of the International Jugglers' Association championships, how to juggle, and his work with Mills helped inspire Mills to develop the [Mills' Mess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mills'_Mess) juggling pattern. As well, Graham made significant contributions to the theory of juggling, including a sequence of publications on [siteswaps](wikipedia://en/520099). In 1972 he was elected president of the [International Jugglers' Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Jugglers'_Association).[^o6]

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

In 2003, Graham won the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822)'s annual [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) for Lifetime Achievement. The prize cited his contributions to [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492), his popularization of mathematics through his talks and writing, his leadership at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs), and his service as president of the society.[^o4] He was one of five inaugural winners of the [George Pólya Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya_Prize) of the [Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1883376), sharing it with fellow [Ramsey theorists](wikipedia://en/157175) Klaus Leeb, [Bruce Rothschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Lee_Rothschild), [Alfred Hales](wikipedia://en/38295597), and Robert I. Jewett.[^o50] He was also one of two inaugural winners of the [Euler Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Medal) of the [Institute of Combinatorics and its Applications](wikipedia://en/17545919), the other being [Claude Berge](wikipedia://en/5893760).[^o51]

Graham was elected to the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1985.[^o52] In 1999 he was inducted as an [ACM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACM_Fellow) "for seminal contributions to the analysis of algorithms, in particular the worst-case analysis of heuristics, the theory of scheduling, and computational geometry".[^o53] He became a Fellow of the [Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1883376) in 2009; the fellow award cited his "contributions to discrete mathematics and its applications".[^o54] In 2012 he became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o55]

Graham was an invited speaker at the 1982 [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) (held 1983 in Warsaw),[^o15] speaking on "Recent developments in Ramsey theory".A84 He was twice [Josiah Willard Gibbs Lecturer](wikipedia://en/45715334), in 2001 and 2015.[^o15] The [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) awarded him both the [Carl Allendoerfer Prize](wikipedia://en/3466784) for his paper "Steiner Trees on a Checkerboard" with Chung and [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) in *[Mathematics Magazine](wikipedia://en/4230329)* (1989),A89b[^o56] and the [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_R._Halmos_–_Lester_R._Ford_Award) for his paper "A whirlwind tour of computational geometry" with [Frances Yao](wikipedia://en/20452101) in the *[American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly)* (1990).A90[^o57] His book *Magical Mathematics* with [Persi Diaconis](wikipedia://en/65467)B6 won the [Euler Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Book_Prize).[^o58]

The proceedings of the *Integers 2005* conference was published as a [festschrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festschrift) for Ron Graham's 70th birthday.[^o59] Another festschrift, stemming from a conference held in 2015 in honor of Graham's 80th birthday, was published in 2018 as the book *Connections in discrete mathematics: a celebration of the work of Ron Graham*.[^o60]
