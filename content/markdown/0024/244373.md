# Scalar multiplication

*Image: Scalar multiplication by r=3.svg.* Scalar multiplication of a vector by a factor of 3 stretches the vector out.

*Image: Scalar multiplication of vectors2.svg.* The scalar multiplications −**a** and 2**a** of a vector **a**

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Scalar multiplication"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **scalar multiplication** is one of the basic operations defining a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422)[^o2][^o3][^o4] (or more generally, a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384)[^o5][^o6]). In common geometrical contexts, scalar multiplication of a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [Euclidean vector](wikipedia://en/32533) by a positive real number multiplies the magnitude of the vector without changing its [direction](wikipedia://en/54516626). Scalar multiplication is the multiplication of a vector by a scalar (where the product is a vector), and is to be distinguished from [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) of two vectors (where the product is a scalar).

:::

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In general, if *K* is a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29) and *V* is a vector space over *K*, then scalar multiplication is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) from *K* × *V* to *V*. The result of applying this function to *k* in *K* and **v** in *V* is denoted *k***v**.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

Scalar multiplication obeys the following rules *(vector in [boldface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boldface))*:

- [Additivity](wikipedia://en/22473529) in the scalar: (*c* + *d*)**v** = *c***v** + *d***v**;
- Additivity in the vector: *c*(**v** + **w**) = *c***v** + *c***w**;
- Compatibility of product of scalars with scalar multiplication: (*cd*)**v** = *c*(*d***v**);
- Multiplying by 1 does not change a vector: 1**v** = **v**;
- Multiplying by 0 gives the [zero vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_vector): 0**v** = **0**;
- Multiplying by −1 gives the [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312): (−1)**v** = −**v**.

Here, + is [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) either in the field or in the vector space, as appropriate; and 0 is the additive identity in either. [Juxtaposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_by_juxtaposition) indicates either scalar multiplication or the [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) operation in the field.

:::

:::

## Interpretation {#Interpretation}

The space of vectors may be considered a [coordinate space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_space) where elements are associated with a list of elements from *K*. The [units](wikipedia://en/289666) of the field form a group *K*<sup>×</sup> and the scalar-vector multiplication is a [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) on the coordinate space by *K*<sup>×</sup>. The zero of the field acts on the coordinate space to collapse it to the zero vector.

When *K* is the field of real numbers there is a [geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric) interpretation of scalar multiplication: it stretches or contracts vectors by a constant factor. As a result, it produces a vector in the same or opposite direction of the original vector but of a different length.[^o12]

As a special case, *V* may be taken to be *K* itself and scalar multiplication may then be taken to be simply the multiplication in the field.

When *V* is *K*<sup>*n*</sup>, scalar multiplication is equivalent to multiplication of each component with the scalar, and may be defined as such.

The same idea applies if *K* is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) and *V* is a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over *K*. *K* can even be a [rig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rig_%28algebra%29), but then there is no additive inverse. If *K* is not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), the distinct operations *left scalar multiplication* *c***v** and *right scalar multiplication* **v***c* may be defined.

## Scalar multiplication of matrices {#Scalar_multiplication_of_matrices}

The **left scalar multiplication** of a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ with a scalar $\lambda$ gives another matrix of the same size as $\mathbf{A}$. It is denoted by $\lambda\mathbf{A}$, whose entries of $\lambda\mathbf{A}$ are defined by

$$(\lambda \mathbf{A})_{ij} = \lambda\left(\mathbf{A}\right)_{ij}\,,$$

explicitly:

$$\lambda \mathbf{A} = \lambda \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} & \cdots & A_{1m} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{2m} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ A_{n1} & A_{n2} & \cdots & A_{nm} \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda A_{11} & \lambda A_{12} & \cdots & \lambda A_{1m} \\ \lambda A_{21} & \lambda A_{22} & \cdots & \lambda A_{2m} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \lambda A_{n1} & \lambda A_{n2} & \cdots & \lambda A_{nm} \\ \end{pmatrix}\,.$$

Similarly, even though there is no widely accepted definition, the **right scalar multiplication** of a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ with a scalar $\lambda$ could be defined to be

$$(\mathbf{A}\lambda)_{ij} = \left(\mathbf{A}\right)_{ij} \lambda\,,$$

explicitly:

$$\mathbf{A}\lambda = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} & \cdots & A_{1m} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{2m} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ A_{n1} & A_{n2} & \cdots & A_{nm} \\ \end{pmatrix}\lambda = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} \lambda & A_{12} \lambda & \cdots & A_{1m} \lambda \\ A_{21} \lambda & A_{22} \lambda & \cdots & A_{2m} \lambda \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ A_{n1} \lambda & A_{n2} \lambda & \cdots & A_{nm} \lambda \\ \end{pmatrix}\,.$$

When the entries of the matrix and the scalars are from the same commutative field, for example, the real number field or the complex number field, these two multiplications are the same, and can be simply called *scalar multiplication*. For matrices over a more general [field](wikipedia://en/10603) that is *not* commutative, they may not be equal.

For a real scalar and matrix:

$$\lambda = 2, \quad \mathbf{A} =\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

$$2 \mathbf{A} = 2 \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \!\cdot\! a & 2 \!\cdot\! b \\ 2 \!\cdot\! c & 2 \!\cdot\! d \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a \!\cdot\! 2 & b \!\cdot\! 2 \\ c \!\cdot\! 2 & d \!\cdot\! 2 \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \\ \end{pmatrix}2= \mathbf{A}2.$$

For quaternion scalars and matrices:

$$\lambda = i, \quad \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & j \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

$$i\begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & j \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} i^2 & 0 \\ 0 & ij \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & k \\ \end{pmatrix} \ne \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -k \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} i^2 & 0 \\ 0 & ji \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & j \\ \end{pmatrix}i\,,$$

where $i, j, k$ are the quaternion units. The non-commutativity of quaternion multiplication prevents the transition of changing $ij = +k$ to $ji = -k$.
