# Hamiltonian path

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hamiltonian path"}

*Image: Hamiltonian Cycle.svg.* A Hamiltonian cycle around a network of six vertices

*Image: Натурализация гамильтоновых циклов.jpg.* Examples of Hamiltonian cycles on a square grid graph 8x8

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **Hamiltonian path** (or **traceable path**) is a [path](wikipedia://en/638889) in an undirected or directed graph that visits each [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) exactly once. A **Hamiltonian cycle** (or **Hamiltonian circuit**) is a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) that visits each vertex exactly once. A Hamiltonian path that starts and ends at adjacent vertices can be completed by adding one more edge to form a Hamiltonian cycle, and removing any edge from a Hamiltonian cycle produces a Hamiltonian path. The computational problems of determining whether such paths and cycles exist in graphs are [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete); see [Hamiltonian path problem](wikipedia://en/149646) for details.

:::

Hamiltonian paths and cycles are named after [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817), who invented the [icosian game](wikipedia://en/18825354), now also known as *Hamilton's puzzle*, which involves finding a Hamiltonian cycle in the edge graph of the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407). Hamilton solved this problem using the [icosian calculus](wikipedia://en/316081), an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) based on [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) with many similarities to the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) (also invented by Hamilton). This solution does not generalize to arbitrary graphs.

Despite being named after Hamilton, Hamiltonian cycles in polyhedra had also been studied a year earlier by [Thomas Kirkman](wikipedia://en/7803789), who, in particular, gave an example of a polyhedron without Hamiltonian cycles.[^o2] Even earlier, Hamiltonian cycles and paths in the [knight's graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight's_graph) of the [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard), the [knight's tour](wikipedia://en/38501), had been studied in the 9th century in [Indian mathematics](wikipedia://en/1848052) by [Rudrata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudrata), and around the same time in [Islamic mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam) by [al-Adli ar-Rumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Adli_ar-Rumi). In 18th century Europe, knight's tours were published by [Abraham de Moivre](wikipedia://en/57327) and [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902).[^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A *Hamiltonian path* or *traceable path* is a [path](wikipedia://en/638889) that visits each vertex of the graph exactly once. A graph that contains a Hamiltonian path is called a **traceable graph**. A graph is **Hamiltonian-connected** if for every pair of vertices there is a Hamiltonian path between the two vertices.

A *Hamiltonian cycle*, *Hamiltonian circuit*, *vertex tour* or *graph cycle* is a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) that visits each vertex exactly once. A graph that contains a Hamiltonian cycle is called a **Hamiltonian graph**.

Similar notions may be defined for *[directed graphs](wikipedia://en/19721986)*, where each edge (arc) of a path or cycle can only be traced in a single direction (i.e., the vertices are connected with arrows and the edges traced "tail-to-head").

A [Hamiltonian decomposition](wikipedia://en/24004195) is an edge decomposition of a graph into Hamiltonian circuits.

A *Hamilton maze* is a type of logic puzzle in which the goal is to find the unique Hamiltonian cycle in a given graph.[^o6][^o7]

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- A [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with more than two vertices is Hamiltonian
- Every [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) is Hamiltonian
- Every [tournament](wikipedia://en/1427502) has an odd number of Hamiltonian paths ([Rédei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/László_Rédei) 1934)
- Every [platonic solid](wikipedia://en/23905), considered as a graph, is Hamiltonian[^o10]
- The [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of a finite [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) is Hamiltonian (see [Lovász conjecture](wikipedia://en/4367424) for a more general claim)
- [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) on [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569) with cyclic [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) are Hamiltonian.[^o11]
- The [flip graph](wikipedia://en/47526601) of a convex polygon or equivalently, the [rotation graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_rotation) of [binary trees](wikipedia://en/4321), is Hamiltonian.[^o12][^o13]

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Herschel Hamiltonian path.svg.* The [Herschel graph](wikipedia://en/24670148) is the smallest possible [polyhedral graph](wikipedia://en/24656270) that does not have a Hamiltonian cycle. A possible Hamiltonian path is shown.

Any Hamiltonian cycle can be converted to a Hamiltonian path by removing one of its edges, but a Hamiltonian path can be extended to a Hamiltonian cycle only if its endpoints are adjacent.

All Hamiltonian graphs are [biconnected](wikipedia://en/4989397), but a biconnected graph need not be Hamiltonian (see, for example, the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926)).[^o16]

An [Eulerian graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_graph) $G$ (a [connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph) in which every vertex has even degree) necessarily has an Euler tour, a closed walk passing through each edge of $G$ exactly once. This tour corresponds to a Hamiltonian cycle in the [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) $L(G)$, so the line graph of every Eulerian graph is Hamiltonian. Line graphs may have other Hamiltonian cycles that do not correspond to Euler tours, and in particular the line graph $L(G)$ of every Hamiltonian graph $G$ is itself Hamiltonian, regardless of whether the graph $G$ is Eulerian.[^o17]

A [tournament](wikipedia://en/1427502) (with more than two vertices) is Hamiltonian if and only if it is [strongly connected](wikipedia://en/684680).

The number of different Hamiltonian cycles in a complete undirected graph on $n$ vertices is $\tfrac{(n - 1)!}{2}$ and in a complete directed graph on $n$ vertices is $(n - 1)!$. These counts assume that cycles that are the same apart from their starting point are not counted separately.

## Bondy–Chvátal theorem {#Bondy–Chvátal_theorem}

The best vertex [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) characterization of Hamiltonian graphs was provided in 1972 by the [Bondy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._A._Bondy)–[Chvátal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Václav_Chvátal) theorem, which generalizes earlier results by [G. A. Dirac](wikipedia://en/1643806) (1952) and [Øystein Ore](wikipedia://en/1643733). Both Dirac's and Ore's theorems can also be derived from [Pósa's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pósa_theorem) (1962). Hamiltonicity has been widely studied with relation to various parameters such as graph [density](wikipedia://en/2777098), [toughness](wikipedia://en/8839340), [forbidden subgraphs](wikipedia://en/21682838) and [distance](wikipedia://en/1020021) among other parameters.[^o20] Dirac and Ore's theorems basically state that a graph is Hamiltonian if it has *enough edges*.

The Bondy–Chvátal theorem operates on the **closure** $\operatorname{cl}(G)$ of a graph $G$ with $n$ vertices, obtained by repeatedly adding a new edge $uv$ connecting a [nonadjacent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonadjacent) pair of vertices $u$ and $v$ with $\deg(v) + \deg(u) \ge n$ until no more pairs with this property can be found.

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Bondy–Chvátal Theorem (1976)"}

**Bondy–Chvátal Theorem (1976).**

A graph is Hamiltonian if and only if its closure is Hamiltonian.

:::

As complete graphs are Hamiltonian, all graphs whose closure is complete are Hamiltonian, which is the content of the following earlier theorems by Dirac and Ore.

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Dirac's Theorem (1952)"}

**Dirac's Theorem (1952).**

A [simple graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) with $n$ vertices ($n\geq 3$) is Hamiltonian if every vertex has degree $\tfrac{n}{2}$ or greater.

:::

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Ore's Theorem (1960)"}

**[Ore's Theorem](wikipedia://en/3100586) (1960).**

A [simple graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) with $n$ vertices ($n\geq 3$) is Hamiltonian if, for every pair of non-adjacent vertices, the sum of their degrees is $n$ or greater.

:::

The following theorems can be regarded as directed versions:

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Ghouila–Houiri (1960)"}

**Ghouila–Houiri (1960).**

A [strongly connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_connected_graph) [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) with $n$ vertices is Hamiltonian if every vertex has a full degree greater than or equal to $n$.

:::

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Meyniel (1973)"}

**Meyniel (1973).**

A [strongly connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_connected_graph) [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) with $n$ vertices is Hamiltonian if the sum of full degrees of every pair of distinct non-adjacent vertices is greater than or equal to $2n-1$

:::

The number of vertices must be doubled because each undirected edge corresponds to two directed arcs and thus the degree of a vertex in the directed graph is twice the degree in the undirected graph.

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Rahman–Kaykobad (2005)"}

**Rahman–[Kaykobad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad_Kaykobad) (2005).**

A [simple graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) with $n$ vertices has a Hamiltonian path if, for every non-adjacent vertex pairs the sum of their degrees and their shortest path length is greater than $n$.[^o27]

:::

The above theorem can only recognize the existence of a Hamiltonian path in a graph and not a Hamiltonian Cycle.

Many of these results have analogues for balanced [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431), in which the vertex degrees are compared to the number of vertices on a single side of the bipartition rather than the number of vertices in the whole graph.[^o28]

:::

## The Hamiltonian cycle polynomial {#The_Hamiltonian_cycle_polynomial}

An algebraic representation of the Hamiltonian cycles of a given weighted digraph (whose arcs are assigned weights from a certain ground field) is the [Hamiltonian cycle polynomial](wikipedia://en/61608254) of its weighted adjacency matrix defined as the sum of the products of the arc weights of the digraph's Hamiltonian cycles. This polynomial is not identically zero as a function in the arc weights if and only if the digraph is Hamiltonian. The relationship between the computational complexities of computing it and [computing the permanent](wikipedia://en/20749642) was shown by Grigoriy Kogan.[^o35]
