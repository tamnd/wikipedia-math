# Material implication (rule of inference)

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Material implication"}

**Material implication.**

*P implies Q* is [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) to *not-$P$ or $Q$*. Either form can replace the other in [logical proofs](wikipedia://en/1197184).

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Material implication"}

In **classical** [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), **material implication**[^o3][^o4] is a [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [rule of replacement](wikipedia://en/34770456) that allows a [conditional statement](wikipedia://en/658808) to be replaced by a [disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) in which the [antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755) is [negated](wikipedia://en/161019). The rule states that *P implies Q* is [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) to *not-$P$ or $Q$* and that either form can replace the other in [logical proofs](wikipedia://en/1197184). In other words, if $P$ is true, then $Q$ must also be true, while if $Q$ is not true, then $P$ cannot be true either; additionally, when $P$ is not true, $Q$ may be either true or false.

:::

$$P \to Q \Leftrightarrow \neg P \lor Q,$$

where "$\Leftrightarrow$" is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) [symbol](wikipedia://en/24028642) representing "can be replaced in a proof with", *P* and *Q* are any given logical [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29), and $\neg P \lor Q$ can be read as "(not *P*) or *Q*". To illustrate this, consider the following statements:

- $P$: Sam ate an [orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_%28fruit%29) for lunch.
- $Q$: Sam ate a [fruit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fruit) for lunch.

Then, to say "Sam ate an orange for lunch" implies "Sam ate a fruit for lunch" ($P \to Q$). Logically, if Sam did not eat a fruit for lunch, then Sam also cannot have eaten an orange for lunch (by [contraposition](wikipedia://en/8556497)). However, merely saying that Sam did not eat an orange for lunch provides no information on whether or not Sam ate a fruit (of any kind) for lunch.

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Suppose we are given that $P \to Q$. Then we have $\neg P \lor P$ by the [law of excluded middle](wikipedia://en/17637) (i.e. either $P$ must be true, or $P$ must not be true).

Subsequently, since $P \to Q$, $P$ can be replaced by $Q$ in the statement, and thus it follows that $\neg P \lor Q$ (i.e. either $Q$ must be true, or $P$ must not be true).

Suppose, conversely, we are given $\neg P \lor Q$. Then if $P$ is true, that rules out the first disjunct, so we have $Q$. In short, $P \to Q$.

This can also be expressed with a [truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250):

::: {#o7 .table cue=table}

| P | Q | ¬P | P → Q | ¬P ∨ Q |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | F | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | F |
| F | T | T | T | T |
| F | F | T | T | T |

:::

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

An example: we are given the conditional fact that if it is a bear, then it can swim. Then, all 4 possibilities in the truth table are compared to that fact.

1. If it is a bear, then it can swim — T
1. If it is a bear, then it can not swim — F
1. If it is not a bear, then it can swim — T because it doesn’t contradict our initial fact.
1. If it is not a bear, then it can not swim — T (as above)

Thus, the conditional fact can be converted to $\neg P \vee Q$, which is "it is not a bear" or "it can swim", where $P$ is the statement "it is a bear" and $Q$ is the statement "it can swim".

:::

## Differences with implication in intuitionistic logic {#Differences_with_implication_in_intuitionistic_logic}

[Intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262) does not treat $P \to Q$ as equivalent to $\neg P \vee Q$ because

$$P \to Q \cancel{\Rightarrow} \neg P \lor Q$$

Given $P \to Q$, one can constructively transform a proof of $P$ into a proof of $Q$. In particular, $P \to P$ holds in intuitionistic logic. If $P \to Q \Rightarrow \neg P \lor Q$ would hold, then $\neg P \lor P$ could be derived. However, the latter is the [law of excluded middle](wikipedia://en/17637), which is not accepted by intuitionistic logic (one cannot assume $\neg P \lor P$ without knowing which case applies).
