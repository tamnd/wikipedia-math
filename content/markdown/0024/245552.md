# Gaussian function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gaussian function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Gaussian function**, often simply referred to as a **Gaussian**, is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of the base form

$$f(x) = \exp (-x^2)$$

and with parametric extension

$$f(x) = a \exp\left( -\frac{(x - b)^2}{2c^2} \right)$$

for arbitrary [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) constants $a$, $b$ and non-zero $c$. It is named after the mathematician [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125). The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of a Gaussian is a characteristic symmetric "[bell curve](wikipedia://en/21462)" shape. The parameter $a$ is the height of the curve's peak, $b$ is the horizontal position of the center of the peak, and $c$ (the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), sometimes called the Gaussian [RMS](wikipedia://en/66819) width) controls the width of the "bell".

:::

Gaussian functions are often used to represent the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of a [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) $\mu = b$ and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) $\sigma^{2} = c^{2}$. In this case, the Gaussian is of the form[^o2]

$$g(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{\sigma^2} \right).$$

Gaussian functions are widely used in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) to describe the [normal distributions](wikipedia://en/21462), in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324) to define [Gaussian filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_filter), in [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing) where two-dimensional Gaussians are used for [Gaussian blurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_blur), and in mathematics to solve [heat equations](wikipedia://en/166415) and [diffusion equations](wikipedia://en/645335) and to define the [Weierstrass transform](wikipedia://en/17467553). They are also abundantly used in [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry) to form [basis sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_set_%28chemistry%29).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Gaussian functions arise by composing the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) with a [concave](wikipedia://en/263321) [quadratic function](wikipedia://en/187240):

$$f(x) = \exp\left(\alpha x^2 + \beta x + \gamma\right),$$

where

$$\alpha = -\tfrac1{2 c^2}, \qquad \beta = \frac{b}{c^2}, \qquad \gamma = \ln a-\frac{b^2}{2c^2}.$$

(Note: $a = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$ in $\ln a$, not to be confused with $\alpha = -\tfrac1{2 c^2}$)

The Gaussian functions are thus those functions whose [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) is a concave quadratic function.

The parameter $c$ is related to the [full width at half maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_width_at_half_maximum) (FWHM) of the peak according to

$$\text{FWHM} = 2 \sqrt{2 \ln 2}\,c \approx 2.35482\,c.$$

The function may then be expressed in terms of the FWHM, represented by $w$:

$$f(x) = a \exp\left(-\frac{4 (\ln 2) (x - b)^2 }{ w^2}\right).$$

Alternatively, the parameter $c$ can be interpreted by saying that the two [inflection points](wikipedia://en/379845) of the function occur at $x = b \pm c$.

The *full width at tenth of maximum* (FWTM) for a Gaussian could be of interest and is

$$\text{FWTM} = 2 \sqrt{2 \ln 10}\,c \approx 4.29193\,c.$$

Gaussian functions are [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478), and their [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) as $x \to \infty$ is 0 (for the above case of $b = 0$).

Gaussian functions are among those functions that are [elementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_function_%28differential_algebra%29) but lack elementary [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823); the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the Gaussian function is the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111):

$$\int \exp\left(-x^2\right) \,dx = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{2} \operatorname{erf} x + C.$$

Nonetheless, their improper integrals over the whole real line can be evaluated exactly, using the [Gaussian integral](wikipedia://en/567580)

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left(-x^2\right) \,dx = \sqrt{\pi},$$

and one obtains

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty a \exp\left(-\frac{(x - b)^2 }{ 2c^2}\right) \,dx = a|c|\sqrt{2\pi}.$$

*Image: Normal Distribution PDF.svg.* [Normalized](wikipedia://en/398189) Gaussian curves with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) $\mu$ and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) $\sigma^{2}$. The corresponding parameters are $a = \tfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}$, $b = \mu$ and $c = \sigma$.

This integral is 1 if and only if $a = \tfrac{1}{c\sqrt{2\pi}}$ (the [normalizing constant](wikipedia://en/398189)), and in this case the Gaussian is the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of a [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) $\mu = b$ and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) $\sigma^{2} = c^{2}$:

$$g(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right).$$

These Gaussians are plotted in the accompanying figure.

The product of two Gaussian functions is a Gaussian, and the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of two Gaussian functions is also a Gaussian, with variance being the sum of the original variances: $c^2 = c_1^2 + c_2^2$. The product of two Gaussian probability density functions (PDFs), though, is not in general a Gaussian PDF.

The Fourier [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/52247#Uncertainty_principle) becomes an equality if and only if (modulated) Gaussian functions are considered.[^o5]

Taking the [Fourier transform (unitary, angular-frequency convention)](wikipedia://en/52247#Angular_frequency_%28ω%29) of a Gaussian function with parameters $a = 1$, $b = 0$ and $c$ yields another Gaussian function, with parameters $c$, $b = 0$ and $\tfrac{1}{c}$.[^o6] So in particular the Gaussian functions with $b = 0$ and $c = a$ are kept fixed by the Fourier transform (they are [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of the Fourier transform with eigenvalue 1). A physical realization is that of the [diffraction pattern](wikipedia://en/799986#Diffraction_by_an_aperture_with_a_Gaussian_profile): for example, a [photographic slide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_slide) whose [transmittance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmittance) has a Gaussian variation is also a Gaussian function.

The fact that the Gaussian function is an eigenfunction of the continuous Fourier transform allows us to derive the following interesting identity from the [Poisson summation formula](wikipedia://en/502042):

$$\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}} \exp\left(-\pi \cdot \left(\frac{k}{c}\right)^2\right) = c \cdot \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}} \exp\left(-\pi \cdot (kc)^2\right).$$

## Integral of a Gaussian function {#Integral_of_a_Gaussian_function}

The integral of an arbitrary Gaussian function is

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty a \exp\left(-\frac{(x - b)^2}{2c^2}\right)\,dx = \ a \, |c| \, \sqrt{2\pi}.$$

An alternative form is

$$\begin{aligned} \int_{-\infty}^\infty k \exp\left(-f x^2 + g x + h\right)\,dx &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty k\exp\left(-f \left(x - \frac{g}{2f}\right)^2 + \frac{g^2}{4f} + h\right)\,dx \\ &= k\,\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{f}}\,\exp\left(\frac{g^2}{4f} + h\right), \end{aligned}$$

where $f$ must be strictly positive for the integral to converge.

### Relation to standard Gaussian integral {#Relation_to_standard_Gaussian_integral}

The integral

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty a\exp\left(-\frac{(x - b)^2}{2c^2}\right)\,dx$$

for some [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) constants $a, b, c > 0$ can be calculated by putting it into the form of a [Gaussian integral](wikipedia://en/567580). First, the constant $a$ can simply be factored out of the integral. Next, the variable of integration is changed from $x$ to $y = x - b$:

$$a\int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left(-\frac{y^2}{2c^2}\right)\,dy,$$

and then to $z = \frac{y}{\sqrt{2 c^2}}$:

$$a\sqrt{2 c^2} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left(-z^2\right)\,dz.$$

Then, using the [Gaussian integral identity](wikipedia://en/567580)

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp\left(-z^2\right)\,dz = \sqrt{\pi},$$

we have

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty a \exp\left(-\frac{(x-b)^2}{2c^2}\right)\,dx = a\sqrt{2\pi c^2}.$$

## Two-dimensional Gaussian function {#Two-dimensional_Gaussian_function}

*Image: Gaussian 2d surface.png.* 3d plot of a Gaussian function with a two-dimensional domain

Base form:

$$f(x,y) = \exp\left(-x^2-y^2\right)$$

In two dimensions, the argument of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) in the Gaussian function is any negative-definite [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478). Consequently, the [level sets](wikipedia://en/559622) of the Gaussian will always be ellipses.

A particular example of a two-dimensional Gaussian function is

$$f(x,y) = A \exp\left(-\left(\frac{(x - x_0)^2}{2\sigma_X^2} + \frac{(y - y_0)^2}{2\sigma_Y^2} \right)\right).$$

Here the coefficient *A* is the amplitude, *x*<sub>0</sub>, *y*<sub>0</sub> is the center, and *σ*<sub>*x*</sub>, *σ*<sub>*y*</sub> are the *x* and *y* spreads of the blob. The figure on the right was created using *A* = 1, *x*<sub>0</sub> = 0, *y*<sub>0</sub> = 0, *σ*<sub>*x*</sub> = *σ*<sub>*y*</sub> = 1.

The volume under the Gaussian function is given by

$$V = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x, y)\,dx \,dy = 2 \pi A \sigma_X \sigma_Y.$$

In general, a two-dimensional elliptical Gaussian function is expressed as

$$f(x, y) = A \exp\left(-\left(a(x - x_0)^2 + 2b(x - x_0)(y - y_0) + c(y - y_0)^2 \right)\right),$$

where the matrix

$$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}$$

is [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix).

Using this formulation, the figure on the right can be created using $A = 1$, $(x_{0}, y_{0}) = (0, 0)$, $a = c = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $b = 0$.

### Meaning of parameters for the general equation {#Meaning_of_parameters_for_the_general_equation}

For the general form of the equation the coefficient *A* is the height of the peak and $(x_{0}, y_{0})$ is the center of the blob.

If we set

$$\begin{aligned} a &= \frac{\cos^2\theta}{2\sigma_X^2} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{2\sigma_Y^2}, \\ b &= -\frac{\sin \theta \cos \theta}{2\sigma_X^2} + \frac{\sin \theta \cos \theta}{2\sigma_Y^2}, \\ c &= \frac{\sin^2\theta}{2\sigma_X^2} + \frac{\cos^2\theta}{2\sigma_Y^2}, \end{aligned}$$

then we rotate the blob by a positive, counter-clockwise angle $\theta$ (for negative, clockwise rotation, invert the signs in the *b* coefficient).[^o13]

To get back the coefficients $\theta$, $\sigma_X$ and $\sigma_Y$ from $a$, $b$ and $c$ use

$$\begin{aligned} \theta &= \frac{1}{2}\arctan\left(\frac{2b}{a-c}\right), \quad \theta \in \left[-\tfrac{\pi}4, \tfrac{\pi}4 \right], \\ \sigma_X^2 &= \frac{1}{2 (a \cdot \cos^2\theta + 2 b \cdot \cos\theta\sin\theta + c \cdot \sin^2\theta)}, \\ \sigma_Y^2 &= \frac{1}{2 (a \cdot \sin^2\theta - 2 b \cdot \cos\theta\sin\theta + c \cdot \cos^2\theta)}. \end{aligned}$$

Example rotations of Gaussian blobs can be seen in the following examples:

| *Image: Gaussian 2d 0 degrees.png.* $\theta = 0$ | *Image: Gaussian 2d 30 degrees.png.* $\theta = -\frac{\pi}{6}$ | *Image: Gaussian 2d 60 degrees.png.* $\theta = -\frac{\pi}{3}$ |
|---|---|---|

Using the following [Octave](wikipedia://en/48707) code, one can easily see the effect of changing the parameters:

```octave
A = 1;
x0 = 0; y0 = 0;

sigma_X = 1;
sigma_Y = 2;

[X, Y] = meshgrid(-5:.1:5, -5:.1:5);

for theta = 0:pi/100:pi
    a = cos(theta)^2 / (2 * sigma_X^2) + sin(theta)^2 / (2 * sigma_Y^2);
    b = sin(2 * theta) / (4 * sigma_X^2) - sin(2 * theta) / (4 * sigma_Y^2);
    c = sin(theta)^2 / (2 * sigma_X^2) + cos(theta)^2 / (2 * sigma_Y^2);

    Z = A * exp(-(a * (X - x0).^2 + 2 * b * (X - x0) .* (Y - y0) + c * (Y - y0).^2));

    surf(X, Y, Z);
    shading interp;
    view(-36, 36)
    waitforbuttonpress
end
```

Such functions are often used in [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing) and in computational models of [visual system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_system) function—see the articles on [scale space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_space) and [affine shape adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_shape_adaptation).

Also see [multivariate normal distribution](wikipedia://en/50347).

### Higher-order Gaussian or super-Gaussian function or generalized Gaussian function {#Higher-order_Gaussian_or_super-Gaussian_function_or_generalized_Gaussian_function}

A more general formulation of a Gaussian function with a flat-top and Gaussian fall-off can be taken by raising the content of the exponent to a power $P$:

$$f(x) = A \exp\left(-\left(\frac{(x - x_0)^2}{2\sigma_X^2}\right)^P\right).$$

This function is known as a super-Gaussian function and is often used for Gaussian beam formulation.[^o15] This function may also be expressed in terms of the [full width at half maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_width_at_half_maximum) (FWHM), represented by $w$:

$$f(x) = A \exp\left(-\ln 2\left(4\frac{(x - x_0)^2}{w^2}\right)^P\right).$$

In a two-dimensional formulation, a Gaussian function along $x$ and $y$ can be combined[^o16] with potentially different $P_X$ and $P_Y$ to form a rectangular Gaussian distribution:

$$f(x, y) = A \exp\left(-\left(\frac{(x - x_0)^2}{2\sigma_X^2}\right)^{P_X} - \left(\frac{(y - y_0)^2}{2\sigma_Y^2}\right)^{P_Y}\right).$$

or an elliptical Gaussian distribution:

$$f(x , y) = A \exp\left(-\left(\frac{(x - x_0)^2}{2\sigma_X^2} + \frac{(y - y_0)^2}{2\sigma_Y^2}\right)^P\right)$$

:::

## Multi-dimensional Gaussian function {#Multi-dimensional_Gaussian_function}

In an $n$-dimensional space a Gaussian function can be defined as

$$f(x) = \exp\left(-x^\mathsf{T} C x\right),$$

where $x = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 & \cdots & x_n\end{bmatrix}$ is a column of $n$ coordinates, $C$ is a [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) $n \times n$ matrix, and ${}^\mathsf{T}$ denotes [matrix transposition](wikipedia://en/173844).

The integral of this Gaussian function over the whole $n$-dimensional space is given as

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \exp\left(-x^\mathsf{T} C x\right) \, dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi^n}{\det C}}.$$

It can be easily calculated by diagonalizing the matrix $C$ and changing the integration variables to the eigenvectors of $C$.

More generally a shifted Gaussian function is defined as

$$f(x) = \exp\left(-x^\mathsf{T} C x + s^\mathsf{T} x\right),$$

where $s = \begin{bmatrix} s_1 & \cdots & s_n\end{bmatrix}$ is the shift vector and the matrix $C$ can be assumed to be symmetric, $C^\mathsf{T} = C$, and positive-definite. The following integrals with this function can be calculated with the same technique:

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} e^{-x^\mathsf{T} C x + v^\mathsf{T}x} \, dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi^n}{\det{C}}} \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{4} v^\mathsf{T} C^{-1} v\right) \equiv \mathcal{M}.$$

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} e^{- x^\mathsf{T} C x + v^\mathsf{T} x} \left(a^\mathsf{T} x\right) \, dx = \left(a^\mathsf{T} u\right) \cdot \mathcal{M}, \text{ where } u = \tfrac{1}{2} C^{-1} v.$$

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} e^{- x^\mathsf{T} C x + v^\mathsf{T} x} \left(x^\mathsf{T} D x\right) \, dx = \left( u^\mathsf{T} D u + \tfrac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr} \left(D C^{-1}\right) \right) \cdot \mathcal{M}.$$

$$\begin{aligned} & \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} e^{- x^\mathsf{T} C' x + s'^\mathsf{T} x} \left( -\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \Lambda \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \right) e^{-x^\mathsf{T} C x + s^\mathsf{T} x} \, dx \\ & \qquad = \left( 2 \operatorname{tr}\left(C' \Lambda C B^{- 1}\right) + 4 u^\mathsf{T} C' \Lambda C u - 2 u^\mathsf{T} \left(C' \Lambda s + C \Lambda s'\right) + s'^\mathsf{T} \Lambda s \right) \cdot \mathcal{M}, \end{aligned}$$

where

$$u = \tfrac{1}{2} B^{- 1} v,\quad v = s + s',\quad B = C + C'.$$

## Estimation of parameters {#Estimation_of_parameters}

A number of fields such as [stellar photometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photometry_%28astronomy%29), [Gaussian beam](wikipedia://en/41206) characterization, and [emission/absorption line spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_spectrum#Emission_spectroscopy) work with sampled Gaussian functions and need to accurately estimate the height, position, and width parameters of the function. There are three unknown parameters for a 1D Gaussian function (*a*, *b*, *c*) and five for a 2D Gaussian function $(A; x_0,y_0; \sigma_X,\sigma_Y)$.

The most common method for estimating the Gaussian parameters is to take the logarithm of the data and [fit a parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_fitting) to the resulting data set.[^o19][^o20] While this provides a simple [curve fitting](wikipedia://en/555425) procedure, the resulting algorithm may be biased by excessively weighting small data values, which can produce large errors in the profile estimate. One can partially compensate for this problem through [weighted least squares](wikipedia://en/2407860) estimation, reducing the weight of small data values, but this too can be biased by allowing the tail of the Gaussian to dominate the fit. In order to remove the bias, one can instead use an [iteratively reweighted least squares](wikipedia://en/11463665) procedure, in which the weights are updated at each iteration.[^o20] It is also possible to perform [non-linear regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_regression) directly on the data, without involving the [logarithmic data transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_data_transformation); for more options, see [probability distribution fitting](wikipedia://en/36074426).

### Parameter precision {#Parameter_precision}

Once one has an algorithm for estimating the Gaussian function parameters, it is also important to know how [precise](wikipedia://en/41932) those estimates are. Any [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) estimation algorithm can provide numerical estimates for the variance of each parameter (i.e., the variance of the estimated height, position, and width of the function). One can also use [Cramér–Rao bound](wikipedia://en/581124) theory to obtain an analytical expression for the lower bound on the parameter variances, given certain assumptions about the data.[^o22][^o23]

1. The noise in the measured profile is either [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically-distributed_random_variables) Gaussian, or the noise is [Poisson-distributed](wikipedia://en/23009144).
1. The spacing between each sampling (i.e. the distance between pixels measuring the data) is uniform.
1. The peak is "well-sampled", so that less than 10% of the area or volume under the peak (area if a 1D Gaussian, volume if a 2D Gaussian) lies outside the measurement region.
1. The width of the peak is much larger than the distance between sample locations (i.e. the detector pixels must be at least 5 times smaller than the Gaussian FWHM).

When these assumptions are satisfied, the following [covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/191752) **K** applies for the 1D profile parameters $a$, $b$, and $c$ under i.i.d. Gaussian noise and under Poisson noise:[^o22]

$$\mathbf{K}_{\text{Gauss}} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sqrt{\pi} \delta_X Q^2} \begin{pmatrix} \frac{3}{2c} &0 &-\frac{1}{a} \\ 0 &\frac{2c}{a^2} &0 \\ -\frac{1}{a} &0 &\frac{2c}{a^2} \end{pmatrix} \ , \qquad \mathbf{K}_\text{Poiss} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \begin{pmatrix} \frac{3a}{2c} &0 &-\frac{1}{2} \\ 0 &\frac{c}{a} &0 \\ -\frac{1}{2} &0 &\frac{c}{2a} \end{pmatrix} \ ,$$

where $\delta_X$ is the width of the pixels used to sample the function, $Q$ is the quantum efficiency of the detector, and $\sigma$ indicates the standard deviation of the measurement noise. Thus, the individual variances for the parameters are, in the Gaussian noise case,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{var} (a) &= \frac{3 \sigma^2}{2 \sqrt{\pi} \, \delta_X Q^2 c} \\ \operatorname{var} (b) &= \frac{2 \sigma^2 c}{\delta_X \sqrt{\pi} \, Q^2 a^2} \\ \operatorname{var} (c) &= \frac{2 \sigma^2 c}{\delta_X \sqrt{\pi} \, Q^2 a^2} \end{aligned}$$

and in the Poisson noise case,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{var} (a) &= \frac{3a}{2 \sqrt{2 \pi} \, c} \\ \operatorname{var} (b) &= \frac{c}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \, a} \\ \operatorname{var} (c) &= \frac{c}{2 \sqrt{2 \pi} \, a}. \end{aligned}$$

For the 2D profile parameters giving the amplitude $A$, position $(x_0,y_0)$, and width $(\sigma_X,\sigma_Y)$ of the profile, the following covariance matrices apply:[^o23]

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{K}_\text{Gauss} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\pi \delta_X \delta_Y Q^2} & \begin{pmatrix} \frac{2}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} &0 &0 &-\frac{1}{A \sigma_Y} &-\frac{1}{A \sigma_X} \\ 0 &\frac{2 \sigma_X}{A^2 \sigma_Y} &0 &0 &0 \\ 0 &0 &\frac{2 \sigma_Y}{A^2 \sigma_X} &0 &0 \\ -\frac{1}{A \sigma_y} &0 &0 &\frac{2 \sigma_X}{A^2 \sigma_y} &0 \\ -\frac{1}{A \sigma_X} &0 &0 &0 &\frac{2 \sigma_Y}{A^2 \sigma_X} \end{pmatrix} \\[6pt] \mathbf{K}_{\operatorname{Poisson}} = \frac{1}{2 \pi} & \begin{pmatrix} \frac{3A}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} &0 &0 &-\frac{1}{\sigma_Y} &-\frac{1}{\sigma_X} \\ 0 & \frac{\sigma_X}{A \sigma_Y} &0 &0 &0 \\ 0 &0 &\frac{\sigma_Y}{A \sigma_X} &0 &0 \\ -\frac{1}{\sigma_Y} &0 &0 &\frac{2 \sigma_X}{3A \sigma_Y} &\frac{1}{3A} \\ -\frac{1}{\sigma_X} &0 &0 &\frac{1}{3A} &\frac{2 \sigma_Y}{3A \sigma_X} \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

where the individual parameter variances are given by the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix.

## Discrete Gaussian {#Discrete_Gaussian}

*Image: Discrete Gaussian kernel.svg.* The [discrete Gaussian kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Gaussian_kernel) (solid), compared with the [sampled Gaussian kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampled_Gaussian_kernel) (dashed) for scales $t = \tfrac12,1,2,4.$

One may ask for a discrete analog to the Gaussian; this is necessary in discrete applications, particularly [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing). A simple answer is to sample the continuous Gaussian, yielding the [sampled Gaussian kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampled_Gaussian_kernel). However, this discrete function does not have the discrete analogs of the properties of the [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), and can lead to undesired effects, as described in the article [scale space implementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_space_implementation).

An alternative approach is to use the [discrete Gaussian kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Gaussian_kernel):[^o25]

$$T(n, t) = e^{-t} I_n(t)$$

where $I_n(t)$ denotes the [modified Bessel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_Bessel_function) of integer order.

This is the discrete analog of the continuous Gaussian in that it is the solution to the discrete [diffusion equation](wikipedia://en/645335) (discrete space, continuous time), just as the continuous Gaussian is the solution to the continuous diffusion equation.[^o25][^o26]

## Applications {#Applications}

Gaussian functions appear in many contexts in the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences), the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences), [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering). Some examples include:

- In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), Gaussian functions appear as the density function of the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), which is a limiting [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of complicated sums, according to the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406).
- Gaussian functions are the [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) for the (homogeneous and isotropic) [diffusion equation](wikipedia://en/645335) (and to the [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415), which is the same thing), a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) that describes the time evolution of a mass-density under [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion). Specifically, if the mass-density at time *t*=0 is given by a [Dirac delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta), which essentially means that the mass is initially concentrated in a single point, then the mass-distribution at time *t* will be given by a Gaussian function, with the parameter ***a*** being linearly related to $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{t}}$ and ***c*** being linearly related to $\sqrt{t}$; this time-varying Gaussian is described by the [heat kernel](wikipedia://en/3904336). More generally, if the initial mass-density is φ(*x*), then the mass-density at later times is obtained by taking the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of φ with a Gaussian function. The convolution of a function with a Gaussian is also known as a [Weierstrass transform](wikipedia://en/17467553).
- A Gaussian function is the [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) of the [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state) of the [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719).
- The [molecular orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital) used in [computational chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_chemistry) can be [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) of Gaussian functions called [Gaussian orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_orbital) (see also [basis set (chemistry)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_set_%28chemistry%29)).
- Mathematically, the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of the Gaussian function can be represented using [Hermite functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_functions). For unit variance, the *n*-th derivative of the Gaussian is the Gaussian function itself multiplied by the *n*-th [Hermite polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial), up to scale.
- Consequently, Gaussian functions are also associated with the [vacuum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_state) in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267).
- [Gaussian beams](wikipedia://en/41206) are used in optical systems, microwave systems and lasers.
- In [scale space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_space) representation, Gaussian functions are used as smoothing kernels for generating multi-scale representations in [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision) and [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing). Specifically, derivatives of Gaussians ([Hermite functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_functions)) are used as a basis for defining a large number of types of visual operations.
- Gaussian functions are used to define some types of [artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network).
- In [fluorescence microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescence_microscopy) a 2D Gaussian function is used to approximate the [Airy disk](wikipedia://en/513128), describing the intensity distribution produced by a [point source](wikipedia://en/7622892).
- In [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324) they serve to define [Gaussian filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_filter), such as in [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing) where 2D Gaussians are used for [Gaussian blurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_blur). In [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing), one uses a [discrete Gaussian kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Gaussian_kernel), which may be approximated by the [Binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668)[^o29] or sampling a Gaussian.
- In [geostatistics](wikipedia://en/51531) they have been used for understanding the variability between the patterns of a complex [training image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Training_image). They are used with kernel methods to cluster the patterns in the feature space.[^o30]
