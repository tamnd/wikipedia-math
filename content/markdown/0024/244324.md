# Improper rotation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Improper rotation"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **improper rotation**[^o2] (also called **rotation-reflection**,[^o3] **rotoreflection,**[^o2] **rotary reflection**,[^o4] or **rotoinversion**[^o5]) is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) that is a combination of a [rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_%28geometry%29) about an axis and a [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) in a plane perpendicular to that axis. Reflection and [inversion](wikipedia://en/12487536) are each a special case of improper rotation. Any improper rotation is an [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) and, in cases that keep the coordinate origin fixed, a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation).[^o6] It is used as a [symmetry operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_operation) in the context of [geometric symmetry](wikipedia://en/44362809), [molecular symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_symmetry) and [crystallography](wikipedia://en/663786), where an object that is unchanged by a combination of rotation and reflection is said to have *improper rotation symmetry*.

:::

There is a distinction between rotary reflection and rotary inversion symmetry operations and their associated symmetry elements. Rotary reflections are generally used to describe the symmetry of individual molecules and are defined as a 360°/n rotation about an n-fold rotation axis followed by a reflection over a mirror plane perpendicular to the n-fold rotation axis. Rotoinversions are generally used to describe the symmetry of crystals and are defined as a 360°/n rotation about an n-fold rotation axis followed by an inversion through the origin. Although rotary reflection operations have a rotoinversion analogue and vice versa, rotoreflections and rotoinversions of the same order need not be identical. For example, a 6-fold rotoinversion axis and its associated with symmetry operations are distinct from those resulting from a 6-fold reflection axis.

## Three dimensions {#Three_dimensions}

In 3 dimensions, improper rotation is equivalently defined as a combination of rotation about an axis and [inversion in a point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_point) on the axis.[^o2] For this reason it is also called a **rotoinversion** or **rotary inversion**. The two definitions are equivalent because [rotation by an angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_rotation) θ followed by reflection is the same transformation as rotation by θ + 180° followed by inversion (taking the point of inversion to be in the plane of reflection). In both definitions, the operations commute.

A three-dimensional symmetry that has only one [fixed point](wikipedia://en/3043958) is necessarily an improper rotation.[^o4]

An improper rotation of an object thus produces a rotation of its [mirror image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image). The axis is called the **rotation-reflection axis**.[^o10] This is called an ***n*-fold improper rotation** if the angle of rotation, before or after reflexion, is 360°/*n* (where *n* must be even).[^o10] There are several different systems for naming individual improper rotations:

- In the [Schoenflies notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoenflies_notation) the symbol ***S<sub>n</sub>*** (German, *Spiegel*, for *[mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror)*), where *n* must be even, denotes the symmetry group generated by an *n*-fold improper rotation. For example, the symmetry operation *S*<sub>6</sub> is the combination of a rotation of (360°/6)=60° and a mirror plane reflection. (This should not be confused with the same notation for [symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/28901)).[^o10]
- In [Hermann–Mauguin notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann–Mauguin_notation) the symbol $\overline{n}$ is used for an ***n*-fold rotoinversion**; i.e., rotation by an angle of rotation of 360°/*n* with inversion. If *n* is even it must be divisible by 4. (Note that $\overline{2}$ would be simply a reflection, and is normally denoted "m", for "mirror".) When *n* is odd this corresponds to a 2*n*-fold improper rotation (or rotary reflexion).
- The [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502) for *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> is \[2*n*<sup>\+</sup>,2<sup>\+</sup>\] and , as an index 4 subgroup of \[2*n*,2\], , generated as the product of 3 reflections.
- The [Orbifold notation](wikipedia://en/2915678) is *n*×, order 2*n*. *Image: Rotoreflection\_subgroup\_tree.png.* Subgroups for *S*<sub>2</sub> to *S*<sub>20</sub>.*C*<sub>1</sub> is the [identity group](wikipedia://en/193059).*S*<sub>2</sub> is the [central inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_inversion).*C*<sub>n</sub> are [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327).

### Subgroups {#Subgroups}

- The [direct subgroup](wikipedia://en/32088502#Subgroups) of *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> is *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, order *n*, [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 2, being the rotoreflection generator applied twice.
- For odd *n*, *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> contains an [inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_point), denoted *C*<sub>i</sub> or *S*<sub>2</sub>. *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> is the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887): *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> = *C*<sub>*n*</sub> × *S*<sub>2</sub>, if *n* is odd.
- For any *n*, if odd *p* is a divisor of *n*, then *S*<sub>2*n*/*p*</sub> is a subgroup of *S*<sub>2*n*</sub>, index *p*. For example *S*<sub>4</sub> is a subgroup of *S*<sub>12</sub>, index 3.

## As an indirect isometry {#As_an_indirect_isometry}

In a wider sense, an improper rotation may be defined as any **[indirect isometry](wikipedia://en/652164#Direct_and_indirect_isometries)**; i.e., an element of [E](wikipedia://en/652164)(3)\\E<sup>\+</sup>(3): thus it can also be a pure reflection in a plane, or have a [glide plane](wikipedia://en/412984). An indirect isometry is an [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) with an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620) that has a determinant of −1.

A **proper rotation** is an ordinary rotation. In the wider sense, a proper rotation is defined as a **direct isometry**; i.e., an element of E<sup>\+</sup>(3): it can also be the identity, a rotation with a translation along the axis, or a pure translation. A direct isometry is an affine transformation with an orthogonal matrix that has a determinant of 1.

In either the narrower or the wider senses, the composition of two improper rotations is a proper rotation, and the composition of an improper and a proper rotation is an improper rotation.

## Physical systems {#Physical_systems}

When studying the symmetry of a physical system under an improper rotation (e.g., if a system has a mirror symmetry plane), it is important to distinguish between [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) and [pseudovectors](wikipedia://en/166356) (as well as [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) and [pseudoscalars](wikipedia://en/475035), and in general between [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965) and [pseudotensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudotensor)), since the latter transform differently under proper and improper rotations (in 3 dimensions, pseudovectors are invariant under inversion).
