# Chirp Z-transform

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chirp Z-transform"}

The **chirp Z-transform** (**CZT**) is a generalization of the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) (DFT). While the DFT samples the [Z plane](wikipedia://en/171589) at uniformly-spaced points along the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), the chirp Z-transform samples along spiral arcs in the Z-plane, corresponding to straight lines in the [S plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_plane).[^o2][^o3] The DFT, real DFT, and zoom DFT can be calculated as special cases of the CZT.

:::

Specifically, the chirp Z transform calculates the Z transform at a finite number of points z<sub>k</sub> along a [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373) contour, defined as:[^o2][^o4]

$$X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n) z_{k}^{-n}$$

$$z_k = A\cdot W^{-k}, k=0,1,\dots,M-1$$

where *A* is the complex starting point, *W* is the complex ratio between points, and *M* is the number of points to calculate.

Like the DFT, the chirp Z-transform can be computed in O(*n* log *n*) operations where $n = \max(M, N)$. An O(*N* log *N*) algorithm for the inverse chirp Z-transform (ICZT) was described in 2003,[^o5][^o6] and in 2019.[^o7]

## Bluestein's algorithm {#Bluestein's_algorithm}

**Bluestein's algorithm**[^o9][^o10] expresses the CZT as a [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) and implements it efficiently using [FFT](wikipedia://en/11512)/IFFT.

As the DFT is a special case of the CZT, this allows the efficient calculation of [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) (DFT) of arbitrary sizes, including [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) sizes. (The other algorithm for FFTs of prime sizes, [Rader's algorithm](wikipedia://en/241408), also works by rewriting the DFT as a convolution.) It was conceived in 1968 by [Leo Bluestein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Bluestein).[^o9] Bluestein's algorithm can be used to compute more general transforms than the DFT, based on the (unilateral) [z-transform](wikipedia://en/171589) (Rabiner *et al.*, 1969).

Recall that the DFT is defined by the formula

$$X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N} nk } \qquad k = 0,\dots,N-1.$$

If we replace the product *nk* in the exponent by the identity

$$n k = \frac{-(k-n)^2}{2} + \frac{n^2}{2} + \frac{k^2}{2}$$

we thus obtain:

$$X_k = e^{-\frac{\pi i}{N} k^2 } \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \left( x_n e^{-\frac{\pi i}{N} n^2 } \right) e^{\frac{\pi i}{N} (k-n)^2 } \qquad k = 0,\dots,N-1.$$

This summation is precisely a convolution of the two sequences *a*<sub>*n*</sub> and *b*<sub>*n*</sub> defined by:

$$a_n = x_n e^{-\frac{\pi i}{N} n^2 }$$

$$b_n = e^{\frac{\pi i}{N} n^2 },$$

with the output of the convolution multiplied by *N* phase factors *b*<sub>*k*</sub><sup>\*</sup>. That is:

$$X_k = b_k^* \left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} a_n b_{k-n}\right) \qquad k = 0,\dots,N-1.$$

This convolution, in turn, can be performed with a pair of FFTs (plus the pre-computed FFT of complex [chirp](wikipedia://en/65635) *b*<sub>*n*</sub>) via the [convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/53268). The key point is that these FFTs are not of the same length *N*: such a convolution can be computed exactly from FFTs only by zero-padding it to a length greater than or equal to 2*N*–1. In particular, one can pad to a [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948) or some other [highly composite](wikipedia://en/1035039) size, for which the FFT can be efficiently performed by e.g. the [Cooley–Tukey algorithm](wikipedia://en/352702) in O(*N* log *N*) time. Thus, Bluestein's algorithm provides an O(*N* log *N*) way to compute prime-size DFTs, albeit several times slower than the Cooley–Tukey algorithm for composite sizes.[^o11]

The use of zero-padding for the convolution in Bluestein's algorithm deserves some additional comment. Suppose we zero-pad to a length *M* ≥ 2*N*–1. This means that *a*<sub>*n*</sub> is extended to an array *A*<sub>*n*</sub> of length *M*, where *A*<sub>*n*</sub> = *a*<sub>*n*</sub> for 0 ≤ *n* \< *N* and *A*<sub>*n*</sub> = 0 otherwise—the usual meaning of "zero-padding". However, because of the *b*<sub>*k*–*n*</sub> term in the convolution, both positive and *negative* values of *n* are required for *b*<sub>*n*</sub> (noting that *b*<sub>–*n*</sub> = *b*<sub>*n*</sub>). The periodic boundaries implied by the DFT of the zero-padded array mean that –*n* is equivalent to *M*–*n*. Thus, *b*<sub>*n*</sub> is extended to an array *B*<sub>*n*</sub> of length *M*, where *B*<sub>0</sub> = *b*<sub>0</sub>, *B*<sub>*n*</sub> = *B*<sub>*M*–*n*</sub> = *b*<sub>*n*</sub> for 0 \< *n* \< *N*, and *B*<sub>*n*</sub> = 0 otherwise.[^o11] *A* and *B* are then FFTed, multiplied pointwise, and inverse FFTed to obtain the convolution of *a* and *b*, according to the usual convolution theorem.[^o11]

Let us also be more precise about what type of convolution is required in Bluestein's algorithm for the DFT. If the sequence *b*<sub>*n*</sub> were periodic in *n* with period *N*, then it would be a cyclic convolution of length *N*, and the zero-padding would be for computational convenience only. However, this is not generally the case:

$$b_{n+N} = e^{\frac{\pi i}{N} (n+N)^2 } = b_n \left[ e^{\frac{\pi i}{N} (2Nn+N^2) } \right] = (-1)^N b_n .$$

Therefore, for *N* [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_numbers) the convolution is cyclic, but in this case *N* is [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) and one would normally use a more efficient FFT algorithm such as Cooley–Tukey. For *N* odd, however, then *b*<sub>*n*</sub> is [antiperiodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiperiodic_function) and we technically have a [negacyclic convolution](wikipedia://en/15743680) of length *N*. Such distinctions disappear when one zero-pads *a*<sub>*n*</sub> to a length of at least 2*N*−1 as described above, however. It is perhaps easiest, therefore, to think of it as a subset of the outputs of a simple linear convolution (i.e. no conceptual "extensions" of the data, periodic or otherwise).[^o11]

## z-transforms {#z-transforms}

Bluestein's algorithm can also be used to compute a more general transform based on the (unilateral) [z-transform](wikipedia://en/171589) (Rabiner *et al.*, 1969)[^o11]. In particular, it can compute any transform of the form:

$$X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n z^{nk} \qquad k = 0,\dots,M-1,$$

for an *arbitrary* [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) *z* and for *differing* numbers *N* and *M* of inputs and outputs. Given Bluestein's algorithm[^o11], such a transform can be used, for example, to obtain a more finely spaced interpolation of some portion of the spectrum (although the frequency resolution is still limited by the total sampling time, similar to a Zoom FFT), enhance arbitrary poles in transfer-function analyses, etc.

The algorithm was dubbed the *chirp* z-transform algorithm because, for the Fourier-transform case (|*z*| = 1), the sequence *b*<sub>*n*</sub> from above is a complex sinusoid of linearly increasing frequency, which is called a (linear) [chirp](wikipedia://en/65635) in [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) systems.[^o11]
