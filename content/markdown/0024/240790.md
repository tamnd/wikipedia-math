# Indicator function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Indicator function"}

*Image: Indicator function illustration.png.* A three-dimensional plot of an indicator function, shown over a square two-dimensional domain (set $X$): the "raised" portion overlays those two-dimensional points which are members of the "indicated" subset ($A$).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **indicator function** or a **characteristic function** of a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that maps elements of the subset to one, and all other elements to zero. That is, if $A$ is a subset of some set $X$, then the indicator function of $A$ is the function $\mathbf{1}_A$ defined by $\mathbf{1}_{A}\!(x) = 1$ if $x \in A,$ and $\mathbf{1}_{A}\!(x) = 0$ otherwise. Other common notations are $\text{𝟙}_{A}$ and $\chi_A.$

:::

The indicator function of $A$ is the [Iverson bracket](wikipedia://en/1721092) of the property of belonging to $A$; that is,

$$\mathbf{1}_{A}(x) = \left[\ x\in A\ \right].$$

For example, the [Dirichlet function](wikipedia://en/630005) is the indicator function of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) as a subset of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given an arbitrary set $X$, the indicator function of a subset $A$ of $X$ is the function

$$\mathbf{1}_A \colon X \rightarrow \{ 0, 1 \}$$

defined by

$$\operatorname\mathbf{1}_A\!( x ) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x \in A \\ 0 & \text{if } x \notin A \,. \end{cases}$$

The [Iverson bracket](wikipedia://en/1721092) provides the equivalent notation $\left[\ x\in A\ \right]$ or $[\![\,x \in A\,]\!]$, that can be used instead of $\mathbf{1}_{A}\!(x).$

The function $\mathbf{1}_A$ is sometimes denoted $\text{𝟙}_{A}$, $I_{A}$, $\chi_{A}$ or even just $A$.

:::

## Notation and terminology {#Notation_and_terminology}

The notation $\chi_A$ is also used to denote the [characteristic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_function_%28convex_analysis%29) in [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201), which is defined as if using the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of the standard definition of the indicator function.

A related concept in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) is that of a [dummy variable](wikipedia://en/285156). (This must not be confused with "dummy variables" as that term is usually used in mathematics, also called a [bound variable](wikipedia://en/147460).)

The term "[characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401)" has an unrelated meaning in [classic probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). For this reason, [traditional probabilists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_probabilists) use the term **indicator function** for the function defined here almost exclusively, while mathematicians in other fields are more likely to use the term *characteristic function* to describe the function that indicates membership in a set.

In [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180) and [modern many-valued logic](wikipedia://en/38024), predicates are the [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401) of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). That is, the strict true/false valuation of the predicate is replaced by a quantity interpreted as the degree of truth.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

The *indicator* or *characteristic* [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of a subset $A$ of some set $X$ [maps](wikipedia://en/516931) elements of $X$ to the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) $\{0,\, 1\}.$

This mapping is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) only when $A$ is a non-empty [proper subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subset) of $X$. If $A = X,$ then $\mathbf{1}_A \equiv 1.$ By a similar argument, if $A = \emptyset$ then $\mathbf{1}_A \equiv 0.$

If $A$ and $B$ are two subsets of $X,$ then

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{1}_{A\cap B}(x) ~&=~ \min\bigl\{\mathbf{1}_A(x),\ \mathbf{1}_B(x)\bigr\} ~~=~ \mathbf{1}_A(x) \cdot\mathbf{1}_B(x), \\ \mathbf{1}_{A\cup B}(x) ~&=~ \max\bigl\{\mathbf{1}_A(x),\ \mathbf{1}_B(x)\bigr\} ~=~ \mathbf{1}_A(x) + \mathbf{1}_B(x) - \mathbf{1}_A(x) \cdot \mathbf{1}_B(x)\,, \end{aligned}$$

and the indicator function of the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of $A$ i.e. $A^\complement$ is:

$$\mathbf{1}_{A^\complement} = 1 - \mathbf{1}_A.$$

More generally, suppose $A_1, \dotsc, A_n$ is a collection of subsets of $X$. For any $x \in X:$

$$\prod_{k \in I} \left(\ 1 - \mathbf{1}_{A_k}\!\left( x \right)\ \right)$$

is a product of $0$s and $1$s. This product has the value $1$ at precisely those $x \in X$ that belong to none of the sets $A_k$ and is 0 otherwise. That is

$$\prod_{k \in I} ( 1 - \mathbf{1}_{A_k}) = \mathbf{1}_{X - \bigcup_{k} A_k} = 1 - \mathbf{1}_{\bigcup_{k} A_k}.$$

Expanding the product on the left hand side,

$$\mathbf{1}_{\bigcup_{k} A_k}= 1 - \sum_{F \subseteq \{1, 2, \dotsc, n\}} (-1)^{|F|} \mathbf{1}_{\bigcap_F A_k} = \sum_{\emptyset \neq F \subseteq \{1, 2, \dotsc, n\}} (-1)^{|F|+1} \mathbf{1}_{\bigcap_F A_k}$$

where $|F|$ is the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of $F$. This is one form of the principle of [inclusion-exclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion-exclusion).

As suggested by the previous example, the indicator function is a useful notational device in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170). The notation is used in other places as well, for instance in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542): if $X$ is a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) with probability measure $\mathbb{P}$ and $A$ is a [measurable set](wikipedia://en/19873), then $\mathbf{1}_A$ becomes a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) whose [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) is equal to the probability of $A$:

$$\operatorname\mathbb{E}_X\left\{\ \mathbf{1}_A(x)\ \right\}\ =\ \int_{X} \mathbf{1}_A( x )\ \operatorname{d\ \mathbb{P} }(x) = \int_{A} \operatorname{d\ \mathbb{P} }(x) = \operatorname\mathbb{P}(A).$$

This identity is used in a simple proof of [Markov's inequality](wikipedia://en/238232).

In many cases, such as [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), the inverse of the indicator function may be defined. This is commonly called the [generalized Möbius function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Möbius_function), as a generalization of the inverse of the indicator function in elementary [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961). (See paragraph below about the use of the inverse in classical recursion theory.)

:::

## Mean, variance and covariance {#Mean,_variance_and_covariance}

Given a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) $\textstyle (\Omega, \mathcal F, \operatorname{P})$ with $A \in \mathcal F,$ the indicator random variable $\mathbf{1}_A \colon \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is defined by $\mathbf{1}_A (\omega) = 1$ if $\omega \in A,$ otherwise $\mathbf{1}_A (\omega) = 0.$

**[Mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean)**

$\ \operatorname\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{1}_A (\omega)) = \operatorname\mathbb{P}(A)\$ (also called "Fundamental Bridge").

**[Variance](wikipedia://en/32344)**

$$\ \operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{1}_A (\omega)) = \operatorname\mathbb{P}(A)(1 - \operatorname\mathbb{P}(A)).$$

**[Covariance](wikipedia://en/157059)**

$$\ \operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{1}_A (\omega), \mathbf{1}_B (\omega)) = \operatorname\mathbb{P}(A \cap B) - \operatorname\mathbb{P}(A) \operatorname\mathbb{P}(B).$$

## Characteristic function in recursion theory, Gödel's and Kleene's representing function {#Characteristic_function_in_recursion_theory,_Gödel's_and_Kleene's_representing_function}

[Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) described the *representing function* in his 1934 paper "On undecidable propositions of formal mathematical systems" (the symbol "$\neg$" indicates logical inversion, i.e. "NOT"):[^o9]p. 42

There shall correspond to each class or relation $R$ a representing function $\phi(x_1, \ldots x_n) = 0$ if $R(x_1,\ldots x_n)$ and $\phi(x_1,\ldots x_n) = 1$ if $\neg R(x_1,\ldots x_n).$

[Kleene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Kleene) offers up the same definition in the context of the [primitive recursive functions](wikipedia://en/24829) as a function $\varphi$ of a predicate $P$ takes on values $0$ if the predicate is true and $1$ if the predicate is false.[^o10]

For example, because the product of characteristic functions $\phi_1 * \phi_2 * \cdots * \phi_n = 0$ whenever any one of the functions equals $0$, it plays the role of logical OR: IF $\phi_1 = 0\$ OR $\ \phi_2 = 0$ OR ... OR $\phi_n = 0$ THEN their product is $0$. What appears to the modern reader as the representing function's logical inversion, i.e. the representing function is $0$ when the function $R$ is "true" or satisfied", plays a useful role in Kleene's definition of the logical functions OR, AND, and IMPLY,[^o10] the bounded-[^o10] and unbounded-[^o10] [mu operators](wikipedia://en/693197) and the CASE function.[^o10]

## Characteristic function in fuzzy set theory {#Characteristic_function_in_fuzzy_set_theory}

In classical mathematics, characteristic functions of sets only take values $1$ (members) or $0$ (non-members). In *[fuzzy set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuzzy_set_theory)*, characteristic functions are generalized to take value in the real unit interval 0, 1, or more generally, in some [algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) or [structure](wikipedia://en/4055928) (usually required to be at least a [poset](wikipedia://en/23572) or [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304)). Such generalized characteristic functions are more usually called [membership functions](wikipedia://en/1808639), and the corresponding "sets" are called *fuzzy* sets. Fuzzy sets model the gradual change in the membership [degree](wikipedia://en/537311) seen in many real-world [predicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematics%29) like "tall", "warm", etc.

## Smoothness {#Smoothness}

In general, the indicator function of a set is not smooth; it is continuous if and only if its [support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_%28math%29) is a [connected component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29). In the [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) of [finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_fields), however, every [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468) admits a ([Zariski](wikipedia://en/241744)) continuous indicator function.[^o13] Given a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of functions $f_\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_q\left[\ x_1, \ldots, x_n\right]$ let $V = \bigl\{\ x \in \mathbb{F}_q^n : f_\alpha(x) = 0\ \bigr\}$ be their vanishing locus. Then, the function $\mathbb{P}(x) = \prod\left(\ 1 - f_\alpha(x)^{q-1}\right)$ acts as an indicator function for $V.$ If $x \in V$ then $\mathbb{P}(x) = 1,$ otherwise, for some $f_\alpha,$ we have $f_\alpha(x) \neq 0$ which implies that $f_\alpha(x)^{q-1} = 1,$ hence $\mathbb{P}(x) = 0.$

Although indicator functions are not smooth, they admit [weak derivatives](wikipedia://en/1411087). For example, consider [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299)

$$H(x) \equiv \operatorname\mathbb{I}\!\bigl(x > 0\bigr)$$

The [distributional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative) of the Heaviside step function is equal to the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021), i.e.

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}H(x)}{\mathrm{d}x}= \delta(x)$$

and similarly the distributional derivative of

$$G(x) := \operatorname\mathbb{I}\!\bigl(x < 0\bigr)$$

is

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}G(x)}{\mathrm{d}x} = -\delta(x).$$

Thus the derivative of the Heaviside step function can be seen as the *inward normal derivative* at the *boundary* of the domain given by the positive half-line. In higher dimensions, the derivative naturally generalises to the inward normal derivative, while the Heaviside step function naturally generalises to the indicator function of some domain $D$. The surface of $D$ will be denoted by $S$. Proceeding, it can be derived that the inward [normal derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_derivative) of the indicator gives rise to a *[surface delta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_delta_function)*, which can be indicated by $\delta_S(\mathbf{x})$:

$$\delta_S(\mathbf{x}) = -\mathbf{n}_x \cdot \nabla_x \operatorname\mathbb{I}\!\bigl(\ \mathbf{x}\in D\ \bigr)\$$

where $n$ is the outward [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) of the surface $S$. This 'surface delta function' has the following property:[^o14]

$$-\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{n}_x\cdot\nabla_x \operatorname\mathbb{I}\!\bigl(\ \mathbf{x}\in D\ \bigr) \; \operatorname{d}^{n}\mathbf{x} = \oint_{S}\,f(\mathbf{\beta}) \; \operatorname{d}^{n-1}\mathbf{\beta}.$$

By setting the function $f$ equal to one, it follows that the [inward normal derivative of the indicator](wikipedia://en/37670148#Dirac_surface_delta_function) integrates to the numerical value of the [surface area](wikipedia://en/27725) $S$.
