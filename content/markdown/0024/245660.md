# Beta function

*Image: Beta function.svg.* [Contour plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_plot) of the beta function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Beta function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **beta function**, also called the [Euler integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_integral_%28disambiguation%29) of the first kind, is a [special function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_function) that is closely related to the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) and to [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668). It is defined by the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532)

:::

$$\mathrm{B}(z_1,z_2) = \int_0^1 t^{z_1-1}(1-t)^{z_2-1}\,dt$$

where $z_1, z_2$ are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) such that $\Re(z_1)>0$ and $\Re(z_2)>0$.

The beta function was studied by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) and [Adrien-Marie Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) and was given its name by [Jacques Binet](wikipedia://en/2252908); its symbol $\mathrm{B}$ is a [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) capital [beta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_%28letter%29).

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The beta function is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/22510359), meaning that $\mathrm{B}(z_1,z_2) = \mathrm{B}(z_2,z_1)$ for all inputs $z_1$ and $z_2$.[^o4]

A key property of the beta function is its close relationship to the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316):[^o4]

$$\mathrm{B}(z_1,z_2)=\frac{\Gamma(z_1)\,\Gamma(z_2)}{\Gamma(z_1+z_2)}$$

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of the relation"}

### Proof of the relation {#Proof_of_the_relation}

Write the product of two factorials as integrals. Since they are integrals in two separate variables, we can combine them into an [iterated integral](wikipedia://en/349053):

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma(z_1)\Gamma(z_2) &= \int_{u=0}^\infty\ e^{-u} u^{z_1-1}\,du \cdot\int_{v=0}^\infty\ e^{-v} v^{z_2-1}\,dv \\[6pt] &=\int_{v=0}^\infty\int_{u=0}^\infty\ e^{-u-v} u^{z_1-1}v^{z_2-1}\, du \,dv. \end{aligned}$$

Changing variables by $u = st$ and $v=s(1-t)$, because $u + v = s$ and $u/(u+v)=t$, we have that the limits of integrations for $s$ are $0$ to $\infty$ and the limits of integration for $t$ are $0$ to $1$. Thus

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma(z_1)\Gamma(z_2) &= \int_{s=0}^\infty\int_{t=0}^1 e^{-s} (st)^{z_1-1}(s(1-t))^{z_2-1}s\,dt \,ds \\[6pt] &= \int_{s=0}^\infty e^{-s}s^{z_1+z_2-1} \,ds\cdot\int_{t=0}^1 t^{z_1-1}(1-t)^{z_2-1}\,dt\\[1ex] &=\Gamma(z_1+z_2) \cdot \mathrm{B}(z_1,z_2). \end{aligned}$$

Dividing both sides by $\Gamma(z_1+z_2)$ gives the desired result. $\square$

The stated identity may be seen as a particular case of the identity for the [integral of a convolution](wikipedia://en/7519#Integration). Taking

$$\begin{aligned}f(u)&:=e^{-u} u^{z_1-1} \chi_{\mathbb{R}_+} \\ g(u)&:=e^{-u} u^{z_2-1} \chi_{\mathbb{R}_+}, \end{aligned}$$

one has:

$$\Gamma(z_1) \Gamma(z_2) = \int_{\mathbb{R}}f(u)\,du\cdot \int_{\mathbb{R}} g(u) \,du = \int_{\mathbb{R}}(f*g)(u)\,du =\mathrm{B}(z_1,z_2)\,\Gamma(z_1+z_2).$$

See *The Gamma Function*, page 18–19[^o7] for a derivation of this relation.

The beta function is also closely related to [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668). When $m$ (or $n$, by symmetry) is a positive integer, it follows from the definition of the gamma function $\Gamma$ that[^o4]

$$\mathrm{B}(m,n) =\frac{(m-1)!\,(n-1)!}{(m+n-1)!} = \frac{m + n}{mn} \bigg/ \binom{m + n}{m}.$$

:::

:::

## Derivative {#Derivative}

We have

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z_1} \mathrm{B}(z_1, z_2) = \mathrm{B}(z_1, z_2) \left( \frac{\Gamma'(z_1)}{\Gamma(z_1)} - \frac{\Gamma'(z_1 + z_2)}{\Gamma(z_1 + z_2)} \right) = \mathrm{B}(z_1, z_2) \big(\psi(z_1) - \psi(z_1 + z_2)\big),$$

and more generally

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial z_m} \mathrm{B}(z_1, z_2, \dots, z_n) = \mathrm{B}(z_1, z_2, \dots, z_n) \left(\psi(z_m) - \psi{\left( \sum_{k=1}^n z_k \right)}\right), \quad 1\le m\le n,$$

where $\psi(z)$ denotes the [digamma function](wikipedia://en/406935).

## Approximation {#Approximation}

[Stirling's approximation](wikipedia://en/151783) gives the asymptotic formula

$$\mathrm{B}(x,y) \sim \sqrt {2\pi } \frac{x^{x - 1/2} y^{y - 1/2} }{(x + y)^{x + y - 1/2} }$$

for large $x$ and $y$.

If on the other hand $x$ is large and $y$ is fixed, then $\mathrm{B}(x,y) \sim \Gamma(y)\,x^{-y}.$

::: {#o11 .statement cue=named-heading name="Other identities and formulas"}

## Other identities and formulas {#Other_identities_and_formulas}

The integral defining the beta function may be rewritten in a variety of ways, including the following:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{B}(z_1,z_2) &= 2\int_0^{\pi / 2}(\sin\theta)^{2z_1-1}(\cos\theta)^{2z_2-1}\,d\theta, \\[6pt] &= \int_0^\infty\frac{t^{z_1-1}}{(1+t)^{z_1+z_2}}\,dt, \\[6pt] &= n\int_0^1t^{nz_1-1}(1-t^n)^{z_2-1}\,dt, \\ &= (1-a)^{z_2} \int_0^1 \frac{(1-t)^{z_1-1}t^{z_2-1}}{(1-at)^{z_1+z_2}}dt \qquad \text{for any } a\in\mathbb{R}_{\leq 1}, \end{aligned}$$

where in the second-to-last identity $n$} is any positive [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438). One may move from the first integral to the second one by substituting $t = \tan^2(\theta)$.

For values $z=z_1=z_2\neq1$ we have:

$$\mathrm{B}(z,z) = \frac{1}{z}\int_0^{\pi / 2}\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt[z]{\sin\theta} + \sqrt[z]{\cos\theta}\right) ^{2z}}\,d\theta.$$

The beta function can be written as an infinite sum:[^o12]

$$\mathrm{B}(x,y) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(1-x)_n}{(y+n)\,n!}.$$

If $x$ and $y$ are equal we get

$$\mathrm{B}(z,z) = 2\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(2z+n-1)_n (-1)^n}{(z+n)n!} = \lim_{x \to 1^-}2\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-2z)_n x^n}{(z+n)n!}$$

where $(x)_n$ is the [rising factorial](wikipedia://en/230193). The beta function can also be written as an [infinite product](wikipedia://en/26382006)

$$\mathrm{B}(x,y) = \frac{x+y}{x y} \prod_{n=1}^\infty \left( 1+ \dfrac{x y}{n (x+y+n)}\right)^{-1}.$$

The beta function satisfies several identities analogous to corresponding identities for binomial coefficients, including a version of [Pascal's identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal's_identity)

$$\mathrm{B}(x,y) = \mathrm{B}(x, y+1) + \mathrm{B}(x+1, y),$$

which can be proved as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{B}(x, y+1) + \mathrm{B}(x+1, y) &= \frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y+1)}{\Gamma(x+y+1)} + \frac{\Gamma(x+1)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y+1)}\\[5pt] &= \frac{y\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y) + x\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{(x+y)\Gamma(x+y)}\\[5pt] &= \frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)} = \mathrm{B}(x,y). \end{aligned}$$

The above proof also shows the simple recurrence on one coordinate[^o13]

$$\mathrm{B}(x+1,y) = \mathrm{B}(x, y) \cdot \dfrac{x}{x+y}, \quad \mathrm{B}(x,y+1) = \mathrm{B}(x, y) \cdot \dfrac{y}{x+y}.$$

The positive integer values of the beta function are also the [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) of the function

$$h(a, b) = \frac{e^a-e^b}{a-b}.$$

Indeed, for all nonnegative integers $m$ and $n$,

$$\mathrm{B}(m+1, n+1) = \frac{\partial^{m+n}h}{\partial a^m \, \partial b^n}(0, 0).$$

The Pascal-like identity above implies that this function is a solution to the [first-order partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/4722013)

$$h = h_a + h_b.$$

For $x, y \geq 1$, the beta function may be written in terms of a [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) involving the [truncated power function](wikipedia://en/977527) $t \mapsto t_+^x$:

$$\mathrm{B}(x,y) \cdot\big(t \mapsto t_+^{x+y-1}\big) = \big(t\mapsto t_+^{x-1}\big) * \big(t \mapsto t_+^{y-1}\big)$$

Evaluation at particular points simplifies the expression significantly; for example,

$$\mathrm{B}(1,x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$$

and[^o14]

$$\mathrm{B}(x,1-x) = \dfrac{\pi}{\sin(\pi x)}, \qquad x \not \in \mathbb{Z}$$

By taking $x = 1/2$ in this last formula, it follows that $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$. Generalizing this into a bivariate identity for a product of beta functions leads to:

$$\mathrm{B}(x,y) \cdot \mathrm{B}(x+y,1-y) = \frac{\pi}{x \sin(\pi y)} .$$

Also, by the [Legendre duplication formula](wikipedia://en/8720712#Gamma_function%E2%80%93Legendre_formula), we get

$$2^{z-1}\mathrm{B}(z/2,z/2) = \mathrm{B}(1/2,z/2).$$

Euler's integral for the beta function may be converted into an integral over the [Pochhammer contour](wikipedia://en/26950225) $C$ as

$$\left(1-e^{2\pi i\alpha}\right)\left(1-e^{2\pi i\beta}\right)\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta) =\int_C t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-1} \, dt.$$

This Pochhammer contour integral converges for all values of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and so gives the [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) of the beta function.

Just as the gamma function for integers describes [factorials](wikipedia://en/10606), the beta function can define a [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) after adjusting indices:

$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{1}{(n+1)\,\mathrm{B}(n-k+1,\, k+1)}.$$

Moreover, for integer $n$, the beta function can be factored to give a closed form interpolation function for continuous values of $k$:

$$\binom{n}{k} = (-1)^n\, n! \cdot\frac{\sin (\pi k)}{\pi\prod_{i=0}^n (k-i)}.$$

:::

## Reciprocal beta function {#Reciprocal_beta_function}

The **reciprocal beta function** is the [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_function)

$$f(x,y)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(x,y)}$$

Interestingly, their integral representations closely relate as the [definite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integral) of [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) with product of its power and [multiple-angle](wikipedia://en/25804104#Multiple-angle_formulae):

$$\begin{aligned} \int_0^\pi \sin^{x-1}\theta\sin y\theta~d\theta &= \frac{\pi\sin\frac{y\pi}{2}}{2^{x-1} x \mathrm{B}{\left(\frac{x+y+1}{2},\frac{x-y+1}{2}\right)}}, \\[1ex] \int_0^\pi \sin^{x-1}\theta\cos y\theta~d\theta &= \frac{\pi\cos\frac{y\pi}{2}}{2^{x-1} x \mathrm{B}{\left(\frac{x+y+1}{2},\frac{x-y+1}{2}\right)}}, \\[1ex] \int_0^\frac{\pi}{2}\cos^{x-1}\theta\cos y\theta~d\theta &= \frac{\pi}{ 2^x x \mathrm{B}{\left(\frac{x+y+1}{2},\frac{x-y+1}{2}\right)}}. \end{aligned}$$

## Incomplete beta function {#Incomplete_beta_function}

The **incomplete beta function**, a generalization of the beta function, is defined as[^o18]

$$\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b) = \int_0^x t^{a-1}\,(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt.$$

For $x=1$, the incomplete beta function coincides with the complete beta function. For positive integers $a$ and $b$, the incomplete beta function is a polynomial of degree $a+b-1$ with rational coefficients.

Substituting $t = \sin^2\theta$ and $t = \frac1{1+s}$, we get

$$\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b) = 2 \int_0^{\arcsin \sqrt x} \sin^{2a-1\!}\theta \cos^{2b-1\!}\theta \, d\theta = \int_{\frac{1-x}x}^\infty \frac{s^{b-1}}{(1+s)^{a+b}} \, ds.$$

The **regularized incomplete beta function** (or **regularized beta function** for short) is defined in terms of the incomplete beta function and the complete beta function:

$$I_x(a,b) = \frac{\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b)}{\mathrm{B}(a,b)}.$$

The regularized incomplete beta function is the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of the [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074), and is related to the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) $F(k;\,n,p)$ of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ following a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) with probability of single success $p$ and number of Bernoulli trials $n$:

$$F(k;\,n,p) = \Pr\left(X \leq k\right) = I_{1-p}(n-k, k+1) = 1 - I_p(k+1,n-k).$$

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

$$\begin{aligned} I_0(a,b) &= 0, \\ I_1(a,b) &= 1, \\ I_x(a,1) &= x^a,\\ I_x(1,b) &= 1 - (1-x)^b, \\ I_x(a,b) &= 1 - I_{1-x}(b,a), \\ I_x(a+1,b) &= I_x(a,b)-\frac{x^a(1-x)^b}{a \mathrm{B}(a,b)}, \\ I_x(a,b+1) &= I_x(a,b)+\frac{x^a(1-x)^b}{b \mathrm{B}(a,b)}, \\ \int \mathrm{B}(x;a,b) \, dx &= x \mathrm{B}(x; a, b) - \mathrm{B}(x; a+1, b), \\ \mathrm{B}(x;a,b) &= (-1)^a \mathrm{B}\left(\frac{x}{x-1};a,1-a-b\right). \end{aligned}$$

:::

### Continued fraction expansion {#Continued_fraction_expansion}

The [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) expansion is

$$\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b) = \frac{x^{a} (1 - x)^{b}}{a \left(1 + \frac{{d}_{1}}{1 + \frac{{d}_{2}}{1 + \frac{{d}_{3}}{1 + \cdots}}}\right)},$$

with odd and even coefficients given by

$$\begin{aligned} {d}_{2m + 1} &= - \frac{(a + m) (a + b + m) x}{(a + 2 m) (a + 2 m + 1)}, \\[1ex] {d}_{2m} &= \frac{m (b - m) x}{(a + 2 m - 1) (a + 2 m)}. \end{aligned}$$

The $4 m$ and $4 m + 1$ convergents are less than $\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b)$, while the $4 m + 2$ and $4 m + 3$ convergents are greater than $\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b)$.

It converges rapidly for $x<(a+1)/(a+b+2)$. For $x > (a + 1)/(a + b + 2)$ or $1 - x < (b + 1)/(a + b + 2)$, the function may be evaluated more efficiently through the relation $\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b) = \mathrm{B}(a, b) - \mathrm{B}(1 - x;\,b,a)$.[^o19]

## Multivariate beta function {#Multivariate_beta_function}

The beta function can be extended to a function with more than two arguments:

$$\mathrm{B}(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots\alpha_n) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_1)\,\Gamma(\alpha_2) \cdots \Gamma(\alpha_n)}{\Gamma(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \cdots + \alpha_n)} .$$

This multivariate beta function is used in the definition of the [Dirichlet distribution](wikipedia://en/1117833). Its relationship to the beta function is analogous to the relationship between [multinomial coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinomial_coefficient) and binomial coefficients. For example, it satisfies a similar version of Pascal's identity:

$$\mathrm{B}(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots\alpha_n) = \mathrm{B}(\alpha_1+1,\alpha_2,\ldots\alpha_n)+\mathrm{B}(\alpha_1,\alpha_2+1,\ldots\alpha_n)+\cdots+\mathrm{B}(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\ldots\alpha_n+1) .$$

::: {#o25 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The beta function is useful in computing and representing the [scattering amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_amplitude) for [Regge trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regge_trajectories). Furthermore, it was the first known [scattering amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_matrix) in [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), first [conjectured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneziano_amplitude) by [Gabriele Veneziano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriele_Veneziano). It also occurs in the theory of the [preferential attachment](wikipedia://en/7915003) process, a type of stochastic [urn process](wikipedia://en/435084). The beta function is also important in statistics, e.g. for the [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) and [beta prime distribution](wikipedia://en/3126406).

:::

## Software implementation {#Software_implementation}

Even if unavailable directly, the complete and incomplete beta function values can be calculated using functions commonly included in [spreadsheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet) or [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427).

In [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel), for example, the complete beta function can be computed with the `[[Gamma_function#Log-gamma function|GammaLn]]` function (or `special.gammaln` in [Python's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) [SciPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciPy) package):

`Value = Exp(GammaLn(a) + GammaLn(b) − GammaLn(a + b))`

This result follows from the properties listed above.

The incomplete beta function cannot be directly computed using such relations and other methods must be used. In [GNU Octave](https://www.gnu.org/software/gsl/doc/html/specfunc.html#incomplete-beta-function), it is computed using a [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) expansion.

The incomplete beta function has existing implementation in common languages. For instance, `betainc` (incomplete beta function) in [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412) and [GNU Octave](wikipedia://en/48707), `pbeta` (probability of beta distribution) in [R](wikipedia://en/376707) and `betainc` in [SymPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SymPy). In [SciPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciPy), `special.betainc` computes the [regularized incomplete beta function](wikipedia://en/207074#Cumulative_distribution_function)—which is, in fact, the cumulative beta distribution. To get the actual incomplete beta function, one can multiply the result of `special.betainc` by the result returned by the corresponding `beta` function. In [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica), `Beta[x, a, b]` and `BetaRegularized[x, a, b]` give $\mathrm{B}(x;\,a,b)$ and $I_x(a,b)$, respectively.
