# Scientific law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Scientific law"}

**Scientific laws** or **laws of science** are statements, based on [repeated](wikipedia://en/47651) [experiments](wikipedia://en/59861) or [observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation), that describe or [predict](wikipedia://en/246066) a range of [natural phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_phenomena).[^o2] The term *law* has diverse usage in many cases (approximate, accurate, broad, or narrow) across all fields of [natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science) ([physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), [geoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoscience), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology)). Laws are developed from data and can be further developed through [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831); in all cases they are directly or indirectly based on [empirical evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence). It is generally understood that they implicitly reflect, though they do not explicitly assert, causal relationships fundamental to reality, and are discovered rather than invented.[^o3]

:::

Scientific laws summarize the results of experiments or observations, usually within a certain range of application. In general, the accuracy of a law does not change when a new theory of the relevant phenomenon is worked out, but rather the scope of the law's application, since the mathematics or statement representing the law does not change. As with other kinds of scientific knowledge, scientific laws do not express absolute certainty, as [mathematical laws](wikipedia://en/77662984) do. A scientific law may be contradicted, restricted, or extended by future observations.

A law can often be formulated as one or several statements or [equations](wikipedia://en/9284), so that it can predict the outcome of an experiment. Laws differ from [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypotheses) and [postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postulates), which are proposed during the [scientific process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) before and during validation by experiment and observation. Hypotheses and postulates are not laws, since they have not been verified to the same degree, although they may lead to the formulation of laws. Laws are narrower in scope than [scientific theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_theory), which may entail one or several laws.[^o4] Science distinguishes a law or theory from facts.[^o5] Calling a law a [fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_fact) is [ambiguous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambiguous), an [overstatement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overstatement), or an [equivocation](wikipedia://en/17690).[^o6] The nature of scientific laws has been much discussed in [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), but scientific laws are empirical conclusions reached by the scientific method; they are intended to be neither laden with [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology) commitments nor statements of logical absolutes.

[Social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science) such as [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) have also attempted to formulate scientific laws, though these generally have much less predictive power.

## Overview {#Overview}

A scientific law always applies to a [physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) under repeated conditions, and it implies that there is a causal relationship involving the elements of the system. [Factual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_fact) and well-confirmed statements like "Mercury is liquid at standard temperature and pressure" are considered too specific to qualify as scientific laws. A central problem in the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science), going back to [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume), is that of distinguishing causal relationships (such as those implied by laws) from principles that arise due to [constant conjunction](wikipedia://en/27477807).[^o8]

Laws differ from [scientific theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_theory) in that they do not posit a mechanism or explanation of phenomena: they are merely distillations of the results of repeated observation. As such, the applicability of a law is limited to circumstances resembling those already observed, and the law may be found to be false when extrapolated. [Ohm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm's_law) only applies to linear networks; [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) only applies in weak gravitational fields; the early laws of [aerodynamics](wikipedia://en/2819), such as [Bernoulli's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli's_principle), do not apply in the case of [compressible flow](wikipedia://en/219144) such as occurs in [transonic](wikipedia://en/247737) and [supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic) flight; [Hooke's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law) only applies to [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_%28physics%29) below the [elastic limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_limit); [Boyle's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyle's_law) applies with perfect accuracy only to the ideal gas, etc. These laws remain useful, but only under the specified conditions where they apply.

Many laws take [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) forms, and thus can be stated as an equation; for example, the [law of conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_conservation_of_energy) can be written as Δ*E* = 0, where *E* is the total amount of energy in the universe. Similarly, the [first law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/166404) can be written as d*U* = *δQ* − *δW*, and [Newton's second law](wikipedia://en/55212#Newton's_second_law) can be written as **F** = *d***p**/*dt*. While these scientific laws explain what our senses perceive, they are still empirical (acquired by observation or scientific experiment) and so are not like mathematical theorems which can be proved purely by mathematics.

Like theories and hypotheses, laws make predictions; specifically, they predict that new observations will conform to the given law. Laws can be [falsified](wikipedia://en/11283) if they are found in contradiction with new data.

Some laws are only approximations of other more general laws, and are good approximations with a restricted domain of applicability. For example, [Newtonian dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_dynamics) (which is based on Galilean transformations) is the low-speed limit of special relativity (since the Galilean transformation is the low-speed approximation to the Lorentz transformation). Similarly, the [Newtonian gravitation law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) is a low-mass approximation of general relativity, and [Coulomb's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law) is an approximation to quantum electrodynamics at large distances (compared to the range of weak interactions). In such cases it is common to use the simpler, approximate versions of the laws, instead of the more accurate general laws.

Laws are constantly being tested experimentally to increasing degrees of precision, which is one of the main goals of science. The fact that laws have never been observed to be violated does not preclude testing them at increased accuracy or in new kinds of conditions to confirm whether they continue to hold, or whether they break, and what can be discovered in the process. It is always possible for laws to be invalidated or proven to have limitations, by repeatable experimental evidence, should any be observed. Well-established laws have indeed been invalidated in some special cases, but the new formulations created to explain the discrepancies generalize upon, rather than overthrow, the originals. That is, the invalidated laws have been found to be only close approximations, to which other terms or factors must be added to cover previously unaccounted-for conditions, e.g. very large or very small scales of time or space, enormous speeds or masses, etc. This, rather than unchanging knowledge, physical laws are better viewed as a series of improving and more precise generalizations.

## Properties {#Properties}

Scientific laws are typically conclusions based on repeated scientific [experiments](wikipedia://en/59861) and [observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observations) over many years and which have become accepted universally within the [scientific community](wikipedia://en/338705). A scientific law is "[inferred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferred) from particular facts, applicable to a defined group or class of [phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomena), and expressible by the statement that a particular phenomenon always occurs if certain conditions be present".[^o11] The production of a summary description of our environment in the form of such laws is a fundamental aim of [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science).

Several general properties of scientific laws, particularly when referring to laws in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), have been identified. Scientific laws are:

- True, at least within their regime of validity. By definition, there have never been repeatable contradicting observations.
- Universal. They appear to apply everywhere in the universe.[^o12]
- Simple. They are typically expressed in terms of a single mathematical equation.
- Absolute. Nothing in the universe appears to affect them.[^o12]
- Stable. Unchanged since first discovered (although they may have been shown to be approximations of more accurate laws),
- All-encompassing. Everything in the universe apparently must comply with them (according to observations).
- Generally [conservative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29) of quantity.[^o13]
- Often expressions of existing homogeneities ([symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetries)) of [space](wikipedia://en/27667) and time.[^o13]
- Typically theoretically reversible in time (if non-[quantum](wikipedia://en/25202)), although [time itself is irreversible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_of_time).[^o13]
- Broad. In physics, laws exclusively refer to the broad domain of matter, motion, energy, and force itself, rather than more specific [systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) in the universe, such as [living systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology), e.g. the [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomechanics) of the [human body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_body).[^o14]

The term "scientific law" is traditionally associated with the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences), though the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences) also contain laws.[^o15] For example, [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218) is a law in the social sciences which is based on [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711). In these cases, laws may describe general trends or expected behaviors rather than being absolutes.

In natural science, [impossibility assertions](wikipedia://en/3710507) come to be widely accepted as overwhelmingly probable rather than considered proved to the point of being unchallengeable. The basis for this strong acceptance is a combination of extensive evidence of something not occurring, combined with an underlying [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_theory), very successful in making predictions, whose assumptions lead logically to the conclusion that something is impossible. While an impossibility assertion in natural science can never be absolutely proved, it could be refuted by the observation of a single [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151). Such a counterexample would require that the assumptions underlying the theory that implied the impossibility be re-examined.

Some examples of widely accepted impossibilities in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) are [perpetual motion machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_motion_machines), which violate the [law of conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_conservation_of_energy), exceeding the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), which violates the implications of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), which asserts the impossibility of simultaneously knowing both the position and the momentum of a particle, and [Bell's theorem](wikipedia://en/56369): no physical theory of local hidden variables can ever reproduce all of the predictions of quantum mechanics.

## Laws as consequences of mathematical symmetries {#Laws_as_consequences_of_mathematical_symmetries}

Some laws reflect mathematical symmetries found in nature (e.g. the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669) reflects identity of electrons, conservation laws reflect [homogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneity_%28physics%29) of [space](wikipedia://en/27667), time, and [Lorentz transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformations) reflect rotational symmetry of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758)). Many fundamental physical laws are mathematical consequences of various [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetries) of space, time, or other aspects of nature. Specifically, [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159) connects some conservation laws to certain symmetries. For example, conservation of energy is a consequence of the shift symmetry of time (no moment of time is different from any other), while conservation of momentum is a consequence of the symmetry (homogeneity) of space (no place in space is special, or different from any other). The indistinguishability of all particles of each fundamental type (say, electrons, or photons) results in the [Dirac](wikipedia://en/159225) and [Bose](wikipedia://en/188935) quantum statistics which in turn result in the Pauli exclusion principle for [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion) and in [Bose–Einstein condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_condensation) for [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson). [Special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) uses [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673) to express motion according to the symmetries of [hyperbolic rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_rotation), a transformation mixing [space](wikipedia://en/27667) and time. Symmetry between [inertial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial) and gravitational [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) results in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).

The [inverse square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_square_law) of interactions mediated by massless bosons is the mathematical consequence of the 3-dimensionality of [space](wikipedia://en/27667).

One strategy in the search for the most fundamental laws of nature is to search for the most general mathematical symmetry group that can be applied to the fundamental interactions.

## Laws of physics {#Laws_of_physics}

### Conservation laws {#Conservation_laws}

#### Conservation and symmetry {#Conservation_and_symmetry}

[Conservation laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_laws) are fundamental laws that follow from the homogeneity of space, time and [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29), in other words *symmetry*.

- **[Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159)**: Any quantity with a continuously differentiable symmetry in the action has an associated conservation law.
- **[Conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass)** was the first law to be understood since most macroscopic physical processes involving masses, for example, collisions of massive particles or fluid flow, provide the apparent belief that mass is conserved. Mass conservation was observed to be true for all chemical reactions. In general, this is only approximative because with the advent of relativity and experiments in nuclear and particle physics: mass can be transformed into energy and vice versa, so mass is not always conserved but part of the more general conservation of [mass–energy](wikipedia://en/422481).
- **[Conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088)**, **[momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_momentum)** and **[angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum)** for isolated systems can be found to be [symmetries in time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_translation_symmetry), translation, and rotation.
- **[Conservation of charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_charge)** was also realized since charge has never been observed to be created or destroyed and only found to move from place to place.

#### Continuity and transfer {#Continuity_and_transfer}

Conservation laws can be expressed using the general [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793) (for a conserved quantity) can be written in differential form as:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}=-\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}$$

where *ρ* is some quantity per unit volume, **J** is the [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) of that quantity (change in quantity per unit time per unit area). Intuitively, the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) (denoted ∇⋅) of a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) is a measure of flux diverging radially outwards from a point, so the negative is the amount piling up at a point; hence the rate of change of density in a region of space must be the amount of flux leaving or collecting in some region (see the main article for details). ### Laws of classical mechanics {#Laws_of_classical_mechanics}

::: {#o27 .statement cue=named-heading name="Principle of least action"}

#### Principle of least action {#Principle_of_least_action}

Classical mechanics, including [Newton's laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_laws), [Lagrange's equations](wikipedia://en/23371726), [Hamilton's equations](wikipedia://en/198319), etc., can be derived from the following principle:

$$\delta \mathcal{S} = \delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{\dot{q}}, t) \, dt = 0$$

where $\mathcal{S}$ is the [action](wikipedia://en/312881); the integral of the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726)

$$L(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{\dot{q}}, t) = T(\mathbf{\dot{q}}, t)-V(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{\dot{q}}, t)$$

of the physical system between two times *t*<sub>1</sub> and *t*<sub>2</sub>. The kinetic energy of the system is *T* (a function of the rate of change of the [configuration](wikipedia://en/473596) of the system), and [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) is *V* (a function of the configuration and its rate of change). The configuration of a system which has *N* [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321587) is defined by [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514) **q** = (*q*<sub>1</sub>, *q*<sub>2</sub>, ... *q<sub>N</sub>*).

There are [generalized momenta](wikipedia://en/706311) conjugate to these coordinates, **p** = (*p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *p<sub>N</sub>*), where:

$$p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}_i}$$

The action and Lagrangian both contain the dynamics of the system for all times. The term "path" simply refers to a curve traced out by the system in terms of the [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514) in the [configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596), i.e. the curve **q**(*t*), parameterized by time (see also *[Parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108)*).

The action is a *[functional](wikipedia://en/1374948)* rather than a *[function](wikipedia://en/185427)*, since it depends on the Lagrangian, and the Lagrangian depends on the path **q**(*t*), so the action depends on the *entire* "shape" of the path for all times (in the time interval from *t*<sub>1</sub> to *t*<sub>2</sub>). Between two instants of time, there are infinitely many paths, but one for which the action is stationary (to the first order) is the true path. The stationary value for the *entire continuum* of Lagrangian values corresponding to some path, *not just one value* of the Lagrangian, is required (in other words it is *not* as simple as "differentiating a function and setting it to zero, then solving the equations to find the points of [maxima and minima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) etc.", rather this idea is applied to the entire "shape" of the function, see [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) for more details on this procedure).[^o28]

Notice *L* is *not* the total energy *E* of the system due to the difference, rather than the sum:

$$E=T+V$$

The following[^o29][^o30] general approaches to classical mechanics are summarized below in the order of establishment. They are equivalent formulations. Newton's is commonly used due to simplicity, but Hamilton's and Lagrange's equations are more general, and their range can extend into other branches of physics with suitable modifications.

### Laws of gravitation and relativity {#Laws_of_gravitation_and_relativity}

Some of the more famous laws of nature are found in [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s theories of (now) [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), presented in his *[Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophiae_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica)*, and in [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s [theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/30001).

::: {#o34 .statement cue=named-heading name="Modern laws"}

#### Modern laws {#Modern_laws}

**[Special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962)**:

The two postulates of special relativity are not "laws" in themselves, but assumptions of their nature in terms of *relative motion*.

They can be stated as "the laws of physics are the same in all [inertial frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frames)" and "the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) is constant and has the same value in all inertial frames".

The said postulates lead to the [Lorentz transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformations) – the transformation law between two [frame of references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference) moving relative to each other. For any [4-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-vector)

$$A' =\Lambda A$$

this replaces the [Galilean transformation](wikipedia://en/295183) law from classical mechanics. The Lorentz transformations reduce to the Galilean transformations for low velocities much less than the speed of light *c*.

The magnitudes of 4-vectors are invariants – *not* "conserved", but the same for all inertial frames (i.e. every observer in an inertial frame will agree on the same value), in particular if *A* is the [four-momentum](wikipedia://en/204680), the magnitude can derive the famous invariant equation for mass–energy and momentum conservation (see [invariant mass](wikipedia://en/162321)):

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$$

in which the (more famous) [mass–energy equivalence](wikipedia://en/422481) *E* = *mc*<sup>2</sup> is a special case.

**[General relativity](wikipedia://en/12024)**:

General relativity is governed by the [Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540), which describe the curvature of space-time due to mass-energy equivalent to the gravitational field. Solving the equation for the geometry of space warped due to the mass distribution gives the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795). Using the geodesic equation, the motion of masses falling along the geodesics can be calculated.

**[Gravitoelectromagnetism](wikipedia://en/24794048)**:

In a relatively flat spacetime due to weak gravitational fields, gravitational analogues of Maxwell's equations can be found; the **GEM equations**, to describe an analogous *[gravitomagnetic field](wikipedia://en/24794048)*. They are well established by the theory, and experimental tests form ongoing research.[^o35]

\|- valign="top" | scope="col" style="width:300px;"|**[Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540) (EFE)**:

$$R_{\mu \nu} + \left ( \Lambda - \frac{R}{2} \right ) g_{\mu \nu} = \frac{8 \pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu \nu}\,\!$$

where Λ = [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992), *R<sub>μν</sub>* = [Ricci curvature tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_curvature_tensor), *T<sub>μν</sub>* = [stress–energy tensor](wikipedia://en/70671), *g<sub>μν</sub>* = [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) | scope="col" style="width:300px;"|**[Geodesic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_equation)**:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{g} = -4 \pi G \rho \,\!$$

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{0} \,\!$$

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{g} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{H}} {\partial t} \,\!$$

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \frac{4}{c^2}\left( - 4 \pi G\mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{g}} {\partial t} \right) \,\!$$

where *ρ* is the [mass density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) and **J** is the mass current density or [mass flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_flux). |- |colspan="2"| In addition there is the **gravitomagnetic Lorentz force**:

$$\mathbf{F} = \gamma(\mathbf{v}) m \left( \mathbf{g} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{H} \right)$$

### Laws of quantum mechanics {#Laws_of_quantum_mechanics}

Quantum mechanics has its roots in [postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postulates_of_quantum_mechanics). This leads to results which are not usually called "laws", but hold the same status, in that all of quantum mechanics follows from them. These postulates can be summarized as follows:

- The state of a physical system, be it a particle or a system of many particles, is described by a [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction).
- Every physical quantity is described by an [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operators_%28physics%29) acting on the system; the measured quantity has a [probabilistic nature](wikipedia://en/4313746).
- The [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) obeys the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874). Solving this wave equation predicts the time-evolution of the system's behavior, analogous to solving Newton's laws in classical mechanics.
- Two [identical particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identical_particles), such as two electrons, cannot be distinguished from one another by any means. Physical systems are classified by their symmetry properties.

These postulates in turn imply many other phenomena, e.g., [uncertainty principles](wikipedia://en/31883) and the [Pauli exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/24669).

$$i\hbar \frac{d}{dt} \left| \psi \right\rangle = \hat{H} \left| \psi \right\rangle$$

## Laws of chemistry {#Laws_of_chemistry}

**Chemical laws** are those laws of nature relevant to [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry). Historically, observations led to many empirical laws, though now it is known that chemistry has its foundations in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202).

**[Quantitative analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_analysis_%28chemistry%29)**:

The most fundamental concept in chemistry is the [law of conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_conservation_of_mass), which states that there is no detectable change in the quantity of matter during an ordinary [chemical reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction). Modern physics shows that it is actually [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) that is conserved, and that [energy and mass are related](wikipedia://en/422481); a concept which becomes important in [nuclear chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_chemistry). [Conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) leads to the important concepts of [equilibrium](wikipedia://en/5306), [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952), and [kinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_kinetics).

Additional laws of chemistry elaborate on the law of conservation of mass. [Joseph Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Proust)'s [law of definite composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_definite_composition) says that pure chemicals are composed of elements in a definite formulation; we now know that the structural arrangement of these elements is also important.

[Dalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton)'s [law of multiple proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_multiple_proportions) says that these chemicals will present themselves in proportions that are small whole numbers; although in many systems (notably [biomacromolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomolecule) and [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerals)) the ratios tend to require large numbers, and are frequently represented as a fraction.

The law of definite composition and the law of multiple proportions are the first two of the three laws of [stoichiometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometry), the proportions by which the chemical elements combine to form chemical compounds. The third law of stoichiometry is the [law of reciprocal proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_reciprocal_proportions), which provides the basis for establishing [equivalent weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_weight) for each chemical element. Elemental equivalent weights can then be used to derive [atomic weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_atomic_weight) for each element.

More modern laws of chemistry define the relationship between energy and its transformations.

**[Reaction kinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_kinetics) and [equilibria](wikipedia://en/5306)**:

- In equilibrium, molecules exist in mixture defined by the transformations possible on the timescale of the equilibrium, and are in a ratio defined by the intrinsic energy of the molecules—the lower the intrinsic energy, the more abundant the molecule. [Le Chatelier's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Chatelier's_principle) states that the system opposes changes in conditions from equilibrium states, i.e. there is an opposition to change the state of an equilibrium reaction.
- Transforming one structure to another requires the input of energy to cross an energy barrier; this can come from the intrinsic energy of the molecules themselves, or from an external source which will generally accelerate transformations. The higher the energy barrier, the slower the transformation occurs.
- There is a hypothetical intermediate, or *transition structure*, that corresponds to the structure at the top of the energy barrier. The [Hammond–Leffler postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammond's_postulate) states that this structure looks most similar to the product or starting material which has intrinsic energy closest to that of the energy barrier. Stabilizing this hypothetical intermediate through chemical interaction is one way to achieve [catalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis).
- All chemical processes are reversible (law of [microscopic reversibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopic_reversibility)) although some processes have such an energy bias, they are essentially irreversible.
- The reaction rate has the mathematical parameter known as the [rate constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_constant). The [Arrhenius equation](wikipedia://en/47011) gives the temperature and [activation energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activation_energy) dependence of the rate constant, an empirical law.

**[Thermochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermochemistry)**:

- [Dulong–Petit law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulong–Petit_law)
- [Gibbs–Helmholtz equation](wikipedia://en/204469)
- [Hess's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hess's_law)

**Gas laws**:

- [Raoult's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoult's_law)
- [Henry's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry's_law)

**Chemical transport**:

- [Fick's laws of diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fick's_laws_of_diffusion)

## Other fields {#Other_fields}

Some [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977) and [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) are referred to as laws because they provide logical foundation to empirical laws.

Examples of other observed phenomena sometimes described as laws include the [Titius–Bode law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titius–Bode_law) of planetary positions, [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218) of linguistics, and [Moore's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore's_law) of technological growth. Many of these laws fall within the scope of [uncomfortable science](wikipedia://en/416598). Other laws are pragmatic and observational, such as the [law of unintended consequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_unintended_consequences). By analogy, principles in other fields of study are sometimes loosely referred to as "laws". These include [Occam's razor](wikipedia://en/36797) as a principle of philosophy and the [Pareto principle](wikipedia://en/24562) of economics.

## History {#History}

The observation and detection of underlying regularities in nature date from [prehistoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prehistoric) times – the recognition of cause-and-effect relationships implicitly recognises the existence of laws of nature. The recognition of such regularities as independent scientific laws *[per se](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_se_%28phrase%29)*, though, was limited by their entanglement in [animism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animism), and by the attribution of many effects that do not have readily obvious causes—such as physical phenomena—to the actions of [gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity), spirits, [supernatural beings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural_being), etc. Observation and speculation about nature were intimately bound up with metaphysics and morality.

In Europe, systematic theorizing about nature (*[physis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physis)*) began with the early [Greek philosophers and scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science_in_classical_antiquity) and continued into the [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period) and [Roman imperial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) periods, during which times the intellectual influence of [Roman law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_law) increasingly became paramount.

> The formula "law of nature" first appears as "a live metaphor" favored by Latin poets [Lucretius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretius), [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil), [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid), [Manilius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Manilius), in time gaining a firm theoretical presence in the prose treatises of [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger) and [Pliny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder). Why this Roman origin? According to \[historian and classicist Daryn\] Lehoux's persuasive narrative,[^o61] the idea was made possible by the pivotal role of codified law and [forensic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_court) argument in Roman life and culture. For the Romans ... the place par excellence where ethics, law, nature, religion and politics overlap is the [law court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_court). When we read Seneca's [*Natural Questions*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturales_quaestiones), and watch again and again just how he applies standards of evidence, witness evaluation, argument and proof, we can recognize that we are reading one of the great Roman rhetoricians of the age, thoroughly immersed in forensic method. And not Seneca alone. Legal models of scientific judgment turn up all over the place, and for example prove equally integral to [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979)'s approach to verification, where the mind is assigned the role of magistrate, the senses that of disclosure of evidence, and dialectical reason that of the law itself.[^o62]

The precise formulation of what are now recognized as modern and valid statements of the laws of nature dates from the 17th century in Europe, with the beginning of accurate experimentation and the development of advanced forms of mathematics. During this period, [natural philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy) such as [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) (1642–1727) were influenced by a [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious) view – stemming from medieval concepts of [divine law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_law) – which held that God had instituted absolute, universal and immutable physical laws.[^o63][^o64] In chapter 7 of [*The World*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_%28Descartes%29), [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) (1596–1650) described "nature" as matter itself, unchanging as created by God, thus changes in parts "are to be attributed to nature. The rules according to which these changes take place I call the 'laws of nature'."[^o65] The modern [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) which took shape at this time (with [Francis Bacon](wikipedia://en/11319) (1561–1626) and [Galileo](wikipedia://en/29688374) (1564–1642)) contributed to a trend of [separating science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relationship_between_religion_and_science) from [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology), with minimal speculation about [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) and ethics. ([Natural law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law) in the political sense, conceived as universal (i.e., divorced from sectarian religion and accidents of place), was also elaborated in this period by scholars such as [Grotius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grotius) (1583–1645), [Spinoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinoza) (1632–1677), and [Hobbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hobbes) (1588–1679).)

The distinction between [natural law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_law) in the political-legal sense and law of nature or physical law in the scientific sense is a modern one, both concepts being equally derived from *[physis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physis)*, the Greek word (translated into Latin as *natura*) for *nature*.[^o66]
