# Elimination theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Elimination theory"}

In [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), **elimination theory** is the classical name for algorithmic approaches to eliminating some variables between [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) of several variables, in order to solve [systems of polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_of_polynomial_equations).

:::

Classical elimination theory culminated with the work of [Francis Macaulay](wikipedia://en/3592252) on [multivariate resultants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_resultant), as described in the chapter on *Elimination theory* in the first editions (1930) of [Bartel van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390)'s *[Moderne Algebra](wikipedia://en/37252648)*. After that, elimination theory was ignored by most algebraic geometers for almost thirty years, until the introduction of new methods for solving polynomial equations, such as [Gröbner bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gröbner_bases), which were needed for [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805).

## History and connection to modern theories {#History_and_connection_to_modern_theories}

The field of elimination theory was motivated by the need of methods for solving [systems of polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_of_polynomial_equations).

One of the first results was [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217), which bounds the number of solutions.

Except for Bézout's theorem, the general approach was to *eliminate* variables for reducing the problem to a single equation in one variable.

The case of linear equations was completely solved by [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035), an improvement over the older method of [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008), which does not proceed by elimination, and works only when the number of equations equals the number of variables. In the 19th century, this was extended to linear [Diophantine equations](wikipedia://en/9109) and [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) with the [Hermite normal form](wikipedia://en/9025098) and [Smith normal form](wikipedia://en/1106564).

Before the 20th century, different types of *eliminants* were introduced, including *[resultants](wikipedia://en/2195020)*, and various kinds of *[discriminants](wikipedia://en/55607)*. In general, these eliminants are also [invariant](wikipedia://en/363762) under various changes of variables, and are also fundamental in [invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762).

All these concepts are effective, in the sense that their definitions include a method of computation. Around 1890, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) introduced non-effective methods, and this was seen as a revolution, which led most algebraic geometers of the first half of the 20th century to try to "eliminate elimination". Nevertheless, [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215) may be considered to belong to elimination theory, as it asserts that a system of polynomial equations does not have any solution if and only if one may eliminate all unknowns to obtain the constant equation 1 = 0.

Elimination theory culminated with the work of [Leopold Kronecker](wikipedia://en/318450), and finally [Macaulay](wikipedia://en/3592252), who introduced [multivariate resultants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_resultant) and [U-resultants](wikipedia://en/2195020#U-resultant), providing complete elimination methods for systems of polynomial equations, which are described in the chapter on *Elimination theory* in the first editions (1930) of [van der Waerden's](wikipedia://en/91390) *[Moderne Algebra](wikipedia://en/37252648)*.

Later, elimination theory was considered old-fashioned and removed from subsequent editions of *Moderne Algebra*. It was generally ignored until the introduction of [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer), and more specifically of [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805), which again made relevant the design of efficient elimination algorithms, rather than merely existence and structural results. The main methods for this renewal of elimination theory are [Gröbner bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gröbner_bases) and [cylindrical algebraic decomposition](wikipedia://en/12218154), introduced around 1970.

## Connection to logic {#Connection_to_logic}

There is also a logical facet to elimination theory, as seen in the [Boolean satisfiability problem](wikipedia://en/4715). In the worst case, it is presumably hard to eliminate variables computationally. *[Quantifier elimination](wikipedia://en/2150441)* is a term used in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) to explain that, in some theories, every formula is equivalent to a formula without quantifiers. This is the case of the theory of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018), where elimination theory may be viewed as the theory of the methods to make quantifier elimination algorithmically effective. [Quantifier elimination over the reals](wikipedia://en/19389633) is another example, which is fundamental in [computational algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997#Computational_algebraic_geometry).
