# Glossary of group theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Glossary of group theory"}

A [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a set together with an [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) operation that admits an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) and such that there exists an [inverse](wikipedia://en/45199) for every element.

:::

Throughout this glossary, we use $e$ to denote the identity element of a group.

## A {#A}

::: {#o3 .entry cue=term name="abelian group"}

**abelian group**

A group $(G, \bullet)$ is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) if $\bullet$ is commutative, i.e. $g \bullet h = h \bullet g$ for all $g, h \in G$. Likewise, a group is *nonabelian* if this relation fails to hold for any pair $g, h \in G$.

:::

::: {#o4 .entry cue=term name="ascendant subgroup"}

**ascendant subgroup**

A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is [ascendant](wikipedia://en/4966647) if there is an ascending subgroup series starting from $H$ and ending at $G$, such that every term in the series is a normal subgroup of its successor. The series may be infinite. If the series is finite, then the subgroup is subnormal.

:::

::: {#o5 .entry cue=term name="automorphism"}

**automorphism**

An [automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_automorphism) of a group is an isomorphism of the group to itself.

:::

## C {#C}

::: {#o7 .entry cue=term name="center of a group"}

**center of a group**

The [center of a group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) $G$, denoted $Z(G)$, is the set of those group elements that commute with all elements of $G$, that is, the set of all $h \in G$ such that $hg = gh$ for all $g \in G$. $Z(G)$ is always a normal subgroup of $G$. A group $G$ is abelian if and only if $Z(G) = G$.

:::

::: {#o8 .entry cue=term name="centerless group"}

**centerless group**

A group $G$ is [centerless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centerless_group) if its center $Z(G)$ is trivial.

:::

::: {#o9 .entry cue=term name="central subgroup"}

**central subgroup**

A subgroup of a group is a [central subgroup](wikipedia://en/4938766) of that group if it lies inside the center of the group.

:::

::: {#o10 .entry cue=term name="centralizer"}

**centralizer**

For a subset $S$ of a group $G$, the [centralizer](wikipedia://en/145661) of $S$ in $G$, denoted $C_{G}(S)$, is the subgroup of $G$ defined by

$$\mathrm{C}_G(S)=\{ g \in G \mid gs=sg \text{ for all } s \in S\}.$$

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=term name="characteristic subgroup"}

**characteristic subgroup**

A subgroup of a group is a [characteristic subgroup](wikipedia://en/7198) of that group if it is mapped to itself by every automorphism of the parent group.

:::

::: {#o12 .entry cue=term name="characteristically simple group"}

**characteristically simple group**

A group is said to be [characteristically simple](wikipedia://en/4966729) if it has no proper nontrivial characteristic subgroups.

:::

::: {#o13 .entry cue=term name="class function"}

**class function**

A [class function](wikipedia://en/1001329) on a group $G$ is a function that it is constant on the conjugacy classes of $G$.

:::

::: {#o14 .entry cue=term name="class number"}

**class number**

The [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28group_theory%29) of a group is the number of its conjugacy classes.

:::

::: {#o15 .entry cue=term name="commutator"}

**commutator**

The [commutator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutator_%28group_theory%29) of two elements $g$ and $h$ of a group $G$ is the element $[g, h] = g^{-1}h^{-1}gh$. Some authors define the commutator as $[g, h] = ghg^{-1}h^{-1}$ instead. The commutator of two elements $g$ and $h$ is equal to the group's identity if and only if $g$ and $h$ commutate, that is, if and only if $gh = hg$.

:::

::: {#o16 .entry cue=term name="commutator subgroup"}

**commutator subgroup**

The [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) or derived subgroup of a group is the subgroup [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by all the commutators of the group.

:::

::: {#o17 .entry cue=term name="complete group"}

**complete group**

A group $G$ is said to be [complete](wikipedia://en/876770) if it is centerless and if every automorphism of $G$ is an [inner automorphism](wikipedia://en/61839).

:::

::: {#o18 .entry cue=term name="composition series"}

**composition series**

A [composition series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_series_%28group_theory%29) of a group $G$ is a [subnormal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subnormal_series) of finite length

$1 = H_0\triangleleft H_1\triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft H_n = G,$ with strict inclusions, such that each $H_{i}$ is a maximal strict normal subgroup of $H_{i+1}$. Equivalently, a composition series is a subnormal series such that each factor group $H_{i+1} / H_{i}$ is simple. The factor groups are called composition factors.

:::

::: {#o19 .entry cue=term name="conjugacy-closed subgroup"}

**conjugacy-closed subgroup**

A subgroup of a group is said to be [conjugacy-closed](wikipedia://en/4978117) if any two elements of the subgroup that are conjugate in the group are also conjugate in the subgroup.

:::

::: {#o20 .entry cue=term name="conjugacy class"}

**conjugacy class**

The [conjugacy classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugacy_classes) of a group $G$ are those subsets of $G$ containing group elements that are conjugate with each other.

:::

::: {#o21 .entry cue=term name="conjugate elements"}

**conjugate elements**

Two elements $x$ and $y$ of a group $G$ are [conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_%28group_theory%29) if there exists an element $g \in G$ such that $g^{-1}xg = y$. The element $g^{-1}xg$, denoted $x^{g}$, is called the conjugate of $x$ by $g$. Some authors define the conjugate of $x$ by $g$ as $gxg^{-1}$. This is often denoted ${}^{g}x$. Conjugacy is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). Its [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) are called [conjugacy classes](wikipedia://en/49176).

:::

::: {#o22 .entry cue=term name="conjugate subgroups"}

**conjugate subgroups**

Two subgroups $H_{1}$ and $H_{2}$ of a group $G$ are [conjugate subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_subgroups) if there is a $g \in G$ such that $gH_{1}g^{-1} = H_{2}$.

:::

::: {#o23 .entry cue=term name="contranormal subgroup"}

**contranormal subgroup**

A subgroup of a group $G$ is a [contranormal subgroup](wikipedia://en/3593761) of $G$ if its normal closure is $G$ itself.

:::

::: {#o24 .entry cue=term name="cyclic group"}

**cyclic group**

A [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) is a group that is [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by a single element, that is, a group such that there is an element $g$ in the group such that every other element of the group may be obtained by repeatedly applying the group operation to $g$ or its inverse.

:::

## D {#D}

::: {#o26 .entry cue=term name="derived subgroup"}

**derived subgroup**

Synonym for commutator subgroup.

:::

::: {#o27 .entry cue=term name="direct product"}

**direct product**

The [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of two groups $G$ and $H$, denoted $G \times H$, is the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of the underlying sets of $G$ and $H$, equipped with a component-wise defined binary operation $(g_{1}, h_{1}) \cdot (g_{2}, h_{2}) = (g_{1} \cdot g_{2}, h_{1} \cdot h_{2})$. With this operation, $G \times H$ itself forms a group.

:::

## E {#E}

::: {#o29 .entry cue=term name="exponent of a group"}

**exponent of a group**

The exponent of a group $G$ is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $g^{n} = e$ for all $g \in G$. It is the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) of the orders of all elements in the group. If no such positive integer exists, the exponent of the group is said to be infinite.

:::

## F {#F}

::: {#o31 .entry cue=term name="factor group"}

**factor group**

Synonym for quotient group.

:::

::: {#o32 .entry cue=term name="FC-group"}

**FC-group**

A group is an [FC-group](wikipedia://en/4966938) if every conjugacy class of its elements has finite cardinality.

:::

::: {#o33 .entry cue=term name="finite group"}

**finite group**

A [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is a group of finite order, that is, a group with a finite number of elements.

:::

::: {#o34 .entry cue=term name="finitely generated group"}

**finitely generated group**

A group $G$ is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) if there is a finite generating set, that is, if there is a finite set $S$ of elements of $G$ such that every element of $G$ can be written as the combination of finitely many elements of $S$ and of inverses of elements of $S$.

:::

## G {#G}

::: {#o36 .entry cue=term name="generating set"}

**generating set**

A [generating set](wikipedia://en/99945) of a group $G$ is a subset $S$ of $G$ such that every element of $G$ can be expressed as a combination (under the group operation) of finitely many elements of $S$ and inverses of elements of $S$. Given a subset $S$ of $G$. We denote by $\langle S\rangle$ the smallest subgroup of $G$ containing $S$. $\langle S\rangle$ is called the subgroup of $G$ generated by $S$.

:::

::: {#o37 .entry cue=term name="group automorphism"}

**group automorphism**

See automorphism.

:::

::: {#o38 .entry cue=term name="group homomorphism"}

**group homomorphism**

See homomorphism.

:::

::: {#o39 .entry cue=term name="group isomorphism"}

**group isomorphism**

See isomorphism.

:::

## H {#H}

::: {#o41 .entry cue=term name="homomorphism"}

**homomorphism**

Given two groups $(G, \bullet)$ and $(H, \cdot)$, a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from $G$ to $H$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $h : G \to H$ such that for all $a$ and $b$ in $G$, $h(a \bullet b) = h(a) \cdot h(b)$.

:::

## I {#I}

::: {#o43 .entry cue=term name="index of a subgroup"}

**index of a subgroup**

The [index](wikipedia://en/230581) of a subgroup $H$ of a group $G$, denoted $\lvert G : H\rvert$ or $[G : H]$ or $(G : H)$, is the number of [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) of $H$ in $G$. For a normal subgroup $N$ of a group $G$, the index of $N$ in $G$ is equal to the order of the quotient group $G / N$. For a finite subgroup $H$ of a finite group $G$, the index of $H$ in $G$ is equal to the quotient of the orders of $G$ and $H$.

:::

::: {#o44 .entry cue=term name="isomorphism"}

**isomorphism**

Given two groups $(G, \bullet)$ and $(H, \cdot)$, an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) between $G$ and $H$ is a [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) homomorphism from $G$ to $H$, that is, a one-to-one correspondence between the elements of the groups in a way that respects the given group operations. Two groups are *isomorphic* if there exists a group isomorphism mapping from one to the other. Isomorphic groups can be thought of as essentially the same, only with different labels on the individual elements.

:::

## L {#L}

::: {#o46 .entry cue=term name="lattice of subgroups"}

**lattice of subgroups**

The [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) of a group is the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) defined by its subgroups, [partially ordered](wikipedia://en/23572) by [set inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_inclusion).

:::

::: {#o47 .entry cue=term name="locally cyclic group"}

**locally cyclic group**

A group is [locally cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_cyclic_group) if every finitely generated subgroup is cyclic. Every cyclic group is locally cyclic, and every finitely-generated locally cyclic group is cyclic. Every locally cyclic group is abelian. Every subgroup, every quotient group and every homomorphic image of a locally cyclic group is locally cyclic.

:::

## N {#N}

::: {#o49 .entry cue=term name="no small subgroup"}

**no small subgroup**

A [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) has [no small subgroup](wikipedia://en/30970275) if there exists a neighborhood of the identity element that does not contain any nontrivial subgroup.

:::

::: {#o50 .entry cue=term name="normal closure"}

**normal closure**

The [normal closure](wikipedia://en/153099) of a subset $S$ of a group $G$ is the intersection of all normal subgroups of $G$ that contain $S$.

:::

::: {#o51 .entry cue=term name="normal core"}

**normal core**

The [normal core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_core) of a subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is the largest normal subgroup of $G$ that is contained in $H$.

:::

::: {#o52 .entry cue=term name="normal series"}

**normal series**

A [normal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_series) of a group $G$ is a sequence of normal subgroups of $G$ such that each element of the sequence is a normal subgroup of the next element:

$1 = A_0\triangleleft A_1\triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft A_n = G$ with

$$A_i\triangleleft G.$$

:::

::: {#o53 .entry cue=term name="normal subgroup"}

**normal subgroup**

A subgroup $N$ of a group $G$ is [normal](wikipedia://en/21918) in $G$ (denoted $N \text{◅} G$) if the conjugation of an element $n$ of $N$ by an element $g$ of $G$ is always in $N$, that is, if for all $g \in G$ and $n \in N$, $gng^{-1} \in N$. A normal subgroup $N$ of a group $G$ can be used to construct the quotient group $G / N$.

:::

::: {#o54 .entry cue=term name="normalizer"}

**normalizer**

For a subset $S$ of a group $G$, the [normalizer](wikipedia://en/145661) of $S$ in $G$, denoted $N_{G}(S)$, is the subgroup of $G$ defined by

$$\mathrm{N}_G(S)=\{ g \in G \mid gS=Sg \}.$$

:::

## O {#O}

::: {#o56 .entry cue=term name="orbit"}

**orbit**

Consider a group $G$ acting on a set $X$. The [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of an element $x$ in $X$ is the set of elements in $X$ to which $x$ can be moved by the elements of $G$. The orbit of $x$ is denoted by $G \cdot x$

:::

::: {#o57 .entry cue=term name="order of a group"}

**order of a group**

The [order of a group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) $(G, \bullet)$ is the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6173) (i.e. number of elements) of $G$. A group with finite order is called a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707).

:::

::: {#o58 .entry cue=term name="order of a group element"}

**order of a group element**

The [order of an element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group_element) $g$ of a group $G$ is the smallest [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$ such that $g^{n} = e$. If no such integer exists, then the order of $g$ is said to be infinite. The order of a finite group is [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisible) by the order of every element.

:::

## P {#P}

::: {#o60 .entry cue=term name="perfect core"}

**perfect core**

The [perfect core](wikipedia://en/4939190) of a group is its largest perfect subgroup.

:::

::: {#o61 .entry cue=term name="perfect group"}

**perfect group**

A [perfect group](wikipedia://en/585271) is a group that is equal to its own commutator subgroup.

:::

::: {#o62 .entry cue=term name="periodic group"}

**periodic group**

A group is [periodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_group) if every group element has finite order. Every [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is periodic.

:::

::: {#o63 .entry cue=term name="permutation group"}

**permutation group**

A [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) is a group whose elements are [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of a given [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $M$ (the [bijective functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective_function) from set $M$ to itself) and whose [group operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_operation) is the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of those permutations. The group consisting of all permutations of a set $M$ is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of $M$.

:::

::: {#o64 .entry cue=term name="p-group"}

**p*-group**

If $p$ is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), then a [$p$-group](wikipedia://en/24664) is one in which the order of every element is a power of $p$. A finite group is a $p$-group if and only if the order of the group is a power of $p$.

:::

::: {#o65 .entry cue=term name="p-subgroup"}

**p*-subgroup**

A subgroup that is also a $p$-group. The study of $p$-subgroups is the central object of the [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993).

:::

## Q {#Q}

::: {#o67 .entry cue=term name="quotient group"}

**quotient group**

Given a group $G$ and a normal subgroup $N$ of $G$, the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) is the set $G / N$ of [left cosets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_coset) $\{aN : a \in G\}$ together with the operation $aN \bullet bN = abN$. The relationship between normal subgroups, homomorphisms, and factor groups is summed up in the [fundamental theorem on homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/11527).

:::

## R {#R}

::: {#o69 .entry cue=term name="real element"}

**real element**

An element $g$ of a group $G$ is called a [real element](wikipedia://en/50475634) of $G$ if it belongs to the same conjugacy class as its inverse, that is, if there is a $h$ in $G$ with $g^{h} = g^{-1}$, where $g^{h}$ is defined as $h^{-1}gh$. An element of a group $G$ is real if and only if for all [representations](wikipedia://en/12695) of $G$ the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of the corresponding matrix is a real number.

:::

## S {#S}

::: {#o71 .entry cue=term name="serial subgroup"}

**serial subgroup**

A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is a [serial subgroup](wikipedia://en/40735495) of $G$ if there is a chain $C$ of subgroups of $G$ from $H$ to $G$ such that for each pair of consecutive subgroups $X$ and $Y$ in $C$, $X$ is a normal subgroup of $Y$. If the chain is finite, then $H$ is a subnormal subgroup of $G$.

:::

::: {#o72 .entry cue=term name="simple group"}

**simple group**

A [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) is a nontrivial group whose only normal subgroups are the trivial group and the group itself.

:::

::: {#o73 .entry cue=term name="subgroup"}

**subgroup**

A [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of a group $G$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $H$ of the elements of $G$ that itself forms a group when equipped with the restriction of the [group operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_operation) of $G$ to $H \times H$. A subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if it is nonempty and [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under products and inverses, that is, if and only if for every $a$ and $b$ in $H$, $ab$ and $a^{-1}$ are also in $H$.

:::

::: {#o74 .entry cue=term name="subgroup series"}

**subgroup series**

A [subgroup series](wikipedia://en/1545790) of a group $G$ is a sequence of subgroups of $G$ such that each element in the series is a subgroup of the next element:

$$1 = A_0 \leq A_1 \leq \cdots \leq A_n = G.$$

:::

::: {#o75 .entry cue=term name="subnormal subgroup"}

**subnormal subgroup**

A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is a [subnormal subgroup](wikipedia://en/2774415) of $G$ if there is a finite chain of subgroups of the group, each one normal in the next, beginning at $H$ and ending at $G$.

:::

::: {#o76 .entry cue=term name="symmetric group"}

**symmetric group**

Given a set $M$, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of $M$ is the set of all [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of $M$ (the set all [bijective functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective_function) from $M$ to $M$) with the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of the permutations as group operation. The symmetric group of a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of size $n$ is denoted $S_{n}$. (The symmetric groups of any two sets of the same size are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397).)

:::

## T {#T}

::: {#o78 .entry cue=term name="torsion group"}

**torsion group**

Synonym for periodic group.

:::

::: {#o79 .entry cue=term name="transitively normal subgroup"}

**transitively normal subgroup**

A subgroup of a group is said to be [transitively normal](wikipedia://en/4938714) in the group if every normal subgroup of the subgroup is also normal in the whole group.

:::

::: {#o80 .entry cue=term name="trivial group"}

**trivial group**

A [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) is a group consisting of a single element, namely the identity element of the group. All such groups are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397), and one often speaks of *the* trivial group.

:::

## Basic definitions {#Basic_definitions}

Both subgroups and normal subgroups of a given group form a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) under inclusion of subsets; this property and some related results are described by the [lattice theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theorem).

**[Kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of a group homomorphism**. It is the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of the identity in the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) of a group homomorphism. Every normal subgroup is the kernel of a group homomorphism and vice versa.

**[Direct product](wikipedia://en/8887)**, **[direct sum](wikipedia://en/106341)**, and **[semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246)** of groups. These are ways of combining groups to construct new groups; please refer to the corresponding links for explanation.

## Types of groups {#Types_of_groups}

**[Finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042)**. If there exists a finite set $S$ such that $\langle S\rangle = G$, then $G$ is said to be [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/99945). If $S$ can be taken to have just one element, $G$ is a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of finite order, an [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group), or possibly a group $\{e\}$ with just one element.

**[Simple group](wikipedia://en/47600)**. Simple groups are those groups having only $e$ and themselves as [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918). The name is misleading because a simple group can in fact be very complex. An example is the [monster group](wikipedia://en/47422), whose [order](wikipedia://en/493527) is about 10<sup>54</sup>. Every finite group is built up from simple groups via [group extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_problem), so the study of finite simple groups is central to the study of all finite groups. The finite simple groups are known and [classified](wikipedia://en/7445).

The structure of any finite abelian group is relatively simple; every finite abelian group is the direct sum of [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) p-groups. This can be extended to a complete classification of all [finitely generated abelian groups](wikipedia://en/58527), that is all abelian groups that are [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by a finite set.

The situation is much more complicated for the non-abelian groups.

**[Free group](wikipedia://en/59735)**. Given any set $A$, one can define a group as the smallest group containing the [free semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_semigroup) of $A$. The group consists of the finite strings (words) that can be composed by elements from $A$, together with other elements that are necessary to form a group. Multiplication of strings is defined by concatenation, for instance $(abb) \bullet (bca) = abbbca$.

Every group $(G, \bullet)$ is basically a factor group of a free group generated by $G$. Refer to *[Presentation of a group](wikipedia://en/99494)* for more explanation. One can then ask [algorithmic](wikipedia://en/775) questions about these presentations, such as:

- Do these two presentations specify isomorphic groups?; or
- Does this presentation specify the trivial group?

The general case of this is the [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563), and several of these questions are in fact unsolvable by any general algorithm.

**[General linear group](wikipedia://en/113564)**, denoted by $\operatorname{GL}(n, F)$, is the group of $n$-by-$n$ [invertible matrices](wikipedia://en/217122), where the elements of the matrices are taken from a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$ such as the real numbers or the complex numbers.

**[Group representation](wikipedia://en/12695)** (not to be confused with the *presentation* of a group). A *group representation* is a homomorphism from a group to a general linear group. One basically tries to "represent" a given abstract group as a concrete group of invertible [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), which is much easier to study.
