# Versine

Versin

versine

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Versine"}

The **versine** or **versed sine** is a [trigonometric function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) found in some of the earliest ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) [*Aryabhatiya*](wikipedia://en/26459760),[^o2] Section I) [trigonometric tables](wikipedia://en/150170). The versine of an angle is 1 minus its [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine).

:::

There are several related functions, most notably the **coversine** and **haversine**. The latter, half a versine, is of particular importance in the [haversine formula](wikipedia://en/590971) of navigation.

*Image: Unit-circle sin cos tan cot exsec excsc versin cvs.svg.* A [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) with [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function).[^o77]

## Overview {#Overview}

The **versine**[^o52][^o58][^o30][^o60][^o78] or **versed sine**[^o76][^o62][^o63][^o61][^o45] is a [trigonometric function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) already appearing in some of the earliest trigonometric tables. It is symbolized in formulas using the abbreviations $\operatorname{versin}$, $\operatorname{sinver}$,[^o26][^o31] $\operatorname{vers}$, or $\operatorname{siv}$.[^o27][^o28] In [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin), it is known as the sinus versus (flipped sine), versinus, versus, or sagitta (arrow).[^o49]

Expressed in terms of common [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) sine, cosine, and tangent, the versine is equal to

$$\operatorname{versin}\theta = 1 - \cos \theta = 2\sin^{2}\frac\theta2 = \sin\theta\,\tan\frac\theta2$$

There are several related functions corresponding to the versine:

- The **versed cosine**,[^o29][^o23] or **vercosine**, abbreviated $\operatorname{vercosin}$, $\operatorname{vercos}$, or $\operatorname{vcs}$
- The **coversed sine** or **coversine**[^o32] (in Latin, cosinus versus or coversinus), abbreviated $\operatorname{coversin}$, $\operatorname{covers}$,[^o33][^o34][^o35] $\operatorname{cosiv}$, or $\operatorname{cvs}$[^o36]

Special tables were also made of half of the versed sine, because of its particular use in the [haversine formula](wikipedia://en/590971) used historically in [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation).

- The **haversed sine**[^o39] or **haversine** (semiversus),[^o37][^o38] abbreviated $\operatorname{haversin}$, $\operatorname{semiversin}$, $\operatorname{semiversinus}$, $\operatorname{havers}$, $\operatorname{hav}$,[^o40][^o41] $\operatorname{hvs}$,[^o24] $\operatorname{sem}$, or $\operatorname{hv}$.[^o42] It is defined as $$\operatorname{hav} \theta = \sin^2 \left( \frac \theta 2 \right) = \frac {1 - \cos \theta} {2}$$

## History and applications {#History_and_applications}

### Versine and coversine {#Versine_and_coversine}

*Image: Versine.svg.* Sine, cosine, and versine of angle $\theta$ in terms of a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) with radius 1, centered at $O$. This figure also illustrates the reason why the versine was sometimes called the sagitta, Latin for [arrow](wikipedia://en/51513).[^o49][^o43] If the arc $ADB$ of the double-angle $\Delta = 2\theta$ is viewed as a "[bow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_%28weapon%29)" and the [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) $AB$ as its "string", then the versine $CD$ is clearly the "arrow shaft".

*Image: historical\_trigonometric\_functions\_graph.svg.* Graphs of historical trigonometric functions compared with sin and cos – in the SVG file, hover over or click a graph to highlight it

The ordinary *[sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine)* function ([see note on etymology](wikipedia://en/5010838#Etymology)) was sometimes historically called the sinus rectus ("straight sine"), to contrast it with the versed sine (sinus versus).[^o44] The meaning of these terms is apparent if one looks at the functions in the original context for their definition, a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071):

For a vertical [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) $AB$ of the unit circle, the sine of the angle $\theta$ (representing half of the subtended angle $\Delta$) is the distance $AC$ (half of the chord). On the other hand, the versed sine of $\theta$ is the distance $CD$ from the center of the chord to the center of the arc. Thus, the sum of $\cos(\theta)$ (equal to the length of line $OC$) and $\operatorname{versin}(\theta)$ (equal to the length of line $CD$) is the radius $OD$ (with length 1). Illustrated this way, the sine is vertical (rectus, literally "straight") while the versine is horizontal (versus, literally "turned against, out-of-place"); both are distances from $C$ to the circle.

This figure also illustrates the reason why the versine was sometimes called the sagitta, Latin for [arrow](wikipedia://en/51513).[^o49][^o43] If the arc $ADB$ of the double-angle $\Delta = 2\theta$ is viewed as a "[bow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_%28weapon%29)" and the chord $AB$ as its "string", then the versine $CD$ is clearly the "arrow shaft".

In further keeping with the interpretation of the sine as "vertical" and the versed sine as "horizontal", sagitta is also an obsolete synonym for the [abscissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscissa) (the horizontal axis of a graph).[^o43]

In 1821, [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) used the terms sinus versus (*siv*) for the versine and cosinus versus (*cosiv*) for the coversine.[^o27][^o28][^o23]

*Image: Unit-circle\_sin\_cos\_tan\_cot\_exsec\_excsc\_versin\_vercos\_coversin\_covercos.svg.* The trigonometric functions can be constructed geometrically in terms of a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) centered at $O$.

As $\theta$ goes to zero, $\operatorname{versin}(\theta)$ is the difference between two nearly equal quantities, so a user of a [trigonometric table](wikipedia://en/150170) for the cosine alone would need a very high accuracy to obtain the versine in order to avoid [catastrophic cancellation](wikipedia://en/19985542), making separate tables for the latter convenient.[^o45] Even with a calculator or computer, [round-off errors](wikipedia://en/432450) make it advisable to use the $\sin^{2}$ formula for small $\theta$.

Another historical advantage of the versine is that it is always non-negative, so its [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) is defined everywhere except for the single angle ($\theta = 0, 2\pi, ...$) where it is zero—thus, one could use [logarithmic tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_table) for multiplications in formulas involving versines.

In fact, the earliest surviving table of sine (half-[chord](wikipedia://en/297947)) values (as opposed to the [chords tabulated by Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/30684123) and other Greek authors), calculated from the [Surya Siddhantha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya_Siddhanta) of India dated back to the 3rd century BC, was a table of values for the sine and versed sine (in $3.75^{\circ}$ increments from $0$ to $90^{\circ}$).[^o44]

The versine appears as an intermediate step in the application of the [half-angle formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-angle_formula) $\sin^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{versin}(\theta)$, derived by [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979), that was used to construct such tables.

### Haversine {#Haversine}

The haversine, in particular, was important in [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) because it appears in the [haversine formula](wikipedia://en/590971), which is used to reasonably accurately compute distances on an astronomic [spheroid](wikipedia://en/82365) (see issues with the [Earth's radius vs. sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_bulge)) given angular positions (e.g., [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) and [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616)). One could also use $\sin^{2}(\tfrac{\theta}{2})$ directly, but having a table of the haversine removed the need to compute squares and square roots.[^o45]

An early utilization by [José de Mendoza y Ríos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_de_Mendoza_y_Ríos) of what later would be called haversines is documented in 1801.[^o31][^o46]

The first known English equivalent to a [table of haversines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_haversines) was published by James Andrew in 1805, under the name "Squares of Natural Semi-Chords".[^o47][^o48][^o49]

In 1835, the term *[haversine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haversine)* (notated naturally as *hav.* or [base-10 logarithmically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base-10_logarithm) as *log. haversine* or *log. havers.*) was coined[^o53] by [James Inman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Inman)[^o31][^o50][^o51] in the third edition of his work *Navigation and Nautical Astronomy: For the Use of British Seamen* to simplify the calculation of distances between two points on the surface of the Earth using [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405) for applications in navigation.[^o52][^o53] Inman also used the terms *nat. versine* and *nat. vers.* for versines.[^o52]

Other high-regarded tables of haversines were those of Richard Farley in 1856[^o47][^o54] and John Caulfield Hannyngton in 1876.[^o47][^o55]

The haversine continues to be used in navigation and has found new applications in recent decades, as in Bruce D. Stark's method for clearing [lunar distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance_%28navigation%29) utilizing [Gaussian logarithms](wikipedia://en/21422067) since 1995[^o56][^o57] or in a more compact method for [sight reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sight_reduction) since 2014.[^o42]

:::

### Modern uses {#Modern_uses}

While the usage of the versine, coversine and haversine as well as their inverse functions can be traced back centuries, the names for the other five [cofunctions](wikipedia://en/3986044) appear to be of much younger origin.

One period ($0 < \theta < 2\pi$) of a versine or, more commonly, a haversine waveform is also commonly used in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324) and [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) as the shape of a [pulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse_%28signal_processing%29) or a [window function](wikipedia://en/244097) (including [Hann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hann_window), [Hann–Poisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hann–Poisson_window) and [Tukey windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tukey_window)), because it smoothly ([continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) in value and [slope](wikipedia://en/29368)) "turns on" from [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) to [one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_%28number%29) (for haversine) and back to zero.[^o24] In these applications, it is named [Hann function](wikipedia://en/4326149) or [raised-cosine filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raised-cosine_filter).

### Circular rotations {#Circular_rotations}

The functions are circular rotations of each other.

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{versin}(\theta) &= \operatorname{coversin}\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \operatorname{vercosin}\left(\theta + \pi\right) \end{aligned}$$

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

These functions can be extended into the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).[^o59][^o32][^o39]

[Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series):[^o39]

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{versin}(z) &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k-1} z^{2k}}{(2k)!} \\ \operatorname{haversin}(z) &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k-1} z^{2k}}{2(2k)!} \end{aligned}$$

$\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\operatorname{versin}(\theta)}{\theta} = 0$[^o76]

$\begin{aligned} \frac{\operatorname{versin}(\theta) + \operatorname{coversin}(\theta)} {\operatorname{versin}(\theta) - \operatorname{coversin}(\theta)} - \frac{\operatorname{exsec}(\theta) + \operatorname{excsc}(\theta)} {\operatorname{exsec}(\theta) - \operatorname{excsc}(\theta)} &= \frac{2 \operatorname{versin}(\theta) \operatorname{coversin}(\theta)} {\operatorname{versin}(\theta) - \operatorname{coversin}(\theta)} \\[3pt] [\operatorname{versin}(\theta) + \operatorname{exsec}(\theta)]\, [\operatorname{coversin}(\theta) + \operatorname{excsc}(\theta)] &= \sin(\theta) \cos(\theta) \end{aligned}$[^o76]

## Approximations {#Approximations}

*Image: Comparison of Versine Approximations for Angles of 0 to 2 Pi.svg.* Comparison of the versine function with three approximations to the versine functions, for angles ranging from $0$ to $2\pi$

*Image: Comparison of Versine Approximations for Angles of 0 to Pi divided by 2.svg.* Comparison of the versine function with three approximations to the versine functions, for angles ranging from $0$ to $\pi/2$

When the versine $v$ is small in comparison to the radius $r$, it may be approximated from the half-chord length $L$ (the distance $AC$ shown above) by the formula[^o72]

$$v \approx \frac{L^2}{2r}.$$

Alternatively, if the versine is small and the versine, radius, and half-chord length are known, they may be used to estimate the arc length $s$ ($AD$ in the figure above) by the formula

$$s\approx L+\frac{v^2}{r}$$

This formula was known to the Chinese mathematician [Shen Kuo](wikipedia://en/1102000), and a more accurate formula also involving the sagitta was developed two centuries later by [Guo Shoujing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guo_Shoujing).[^o73]

A more accurate approximation used in engineering[^o74] is

$$v\approx \frac{s^\frac{3}{2} L^\frac{1}{2}}{8r}$$

## Arbitrary curves and chords {#Arbitrary_curves_and_chords}

The term *versine* is also sometimes used to describe deviations from straightness in an arbitrary planar curve, of which the above circle is a special case. Given a chord between two points in a curve, the perpendicular distance $v$ from the chord to the curve (usually at the chord midpoint) is called a *versine* measurement. For a straight line, the versine of any chord is zero, so this measurement characterizes the straightness of the curve. In the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) as the chord length $L$ goes to zero, the ratio $\tfrac{8v}{L^{2}}$ goes to the instantaneous [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770). This usage is especially common in [rail transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport), where it describes measurements of the straightness of the [rail tracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_tracks)[^o75] and it is the basis of the [Hallade method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallade_method) for [rail surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_surveying).

The term *[sagitta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagitta_%28optics%29)* (often abbreviated *sag*) is used similarly in [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), for describing the surfaces of [lenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29) and [mirrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror).
