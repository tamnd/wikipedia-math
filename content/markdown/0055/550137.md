# Elliptic operator

*Image: Laplace's equation on an annulus.svg.* A solution to [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) defined on an [annulus](wikipedia://en/356158). The [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706) is the most famous example of an elliptic operator.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Elliptic operator"}

In the theory of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations), **elliptic operators** are [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) that generalize the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706). They are defined by the condition that the coefficients of the highest-order derivatives be positive, which implies the key property that the [principal symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_symbol) is invertible, or equivalently that there are no real [characteristic](wikipedia://en/751933) directions.

:::

Elliptic operators are typical of [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342), and they appear frequently in [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics) and [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918). [Elliptic regularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_regularity) implies that their solutions tend to be [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) (if the coefficients in the operator are smooth). Steady-state solutions to [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/1575825) and [parabolic](wikipedia://en/7466971) equations generally solve elliptic equations.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $L$ be a [linear differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) of order *m* on a domain $\Omega$ in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> given by

$$Lu = \sum_{|\alpha| \le m} a_\alpha(x)\partial^\alpha u$$

where $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$ denotes a [multi-index](wikipedia://en/604268), and $\partial^\alpha u = \partial^{\alpha_1}_1 \cdots \partial_n^{\alpha_n}u$ denotes the partial derivative of order $\alpha_i$ in $x_i$.

Then $L$ is called *elliptic* if for every *x* in $\Omega$ and every non-zero $\xi$ in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>,

$$\sum_{|\alpha| = m} a_\alpha(x)\xi^\alpha \neq 0,$$

where $\xi^\alpha = \xi_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots \xi_n^{\alpha_n}$.

In many applications, this condition is not strong enough, and instead a *uniform ellipticity condition* may be imposed for operators of order *m* = 2*k*:

$$(-1)^k\sum_{|\alpha| = 2k} a_\alpha(x) \xi^\alpha > C |\xi|^{2k},$$

where *C* is a positive constant. Note that ellipticity only depends on the highest-order terms.[^o4]

A nonlinear operator

$$L(u) = F\left(x, u, \left(\partial^\alpha u\right)_{|\alpha| \le m}\right)$$

is elliptic if its [linearization](wikipedia://en/1118789) is; i.e. the first-order Taylor expansion with respect to *u* and its derivatives about any point is an elliptic operator.

::: {#o5 .example cue=inline-head name="Example 1"}

**Example 1**

The negative of the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) in **R**<sup>*d*</sup> given by

$$- \Delta u = - \sum_{i=1}^d \partial_i^2 u$$

is a uniformly elliptic operator. The Laplace operator occurs frequently in electrostatics. If ρ is the charge density within some region Ω of linear [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) ɛ, the electrical potential Φ must satisfy the equation

$$- \Delta \Phi = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon}.$$

This follows directly from the first of [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737), [Gauss' Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law), and the definition of the potential as: $- \nabla \Phi = \boldsymbol E \,.$

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=inline-head name="Example 2"}

**Example 2[^o7]**

Given a matrix-valued function *A*(*x*) which is uniformly positive definite for every *x*, having components *a*<sup>*ij*</sup>, the operator

$$Lu = -\partial_i\left(a^{ij}(x)\partial_ju\right) + b^j(x)\partial_ju + cu$$

is elliptic. This is the most general form of a second-order divergence form linear elliptic differential operator. The Laplace operator is obtained by taking *A* = *I*. These operators also occur in electrostatics in polarized media.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=inline-head name="Example 3"}

**Example 3**

For *p* a non-negative number, the p-Laplacian is a nonlinear elliptic operator defined by

$$L(u) = -\sum_{i = 1}^d\partial_i\left(|\nabla u|^{p - 2}\partial_i u\right).$$

A similar nonlinear operator occurs in [glacier mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheet_dynamics). The [Cauchy stress tensor](wikipedia://en/3323565) of ice, according to [Glen's flow law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen's_flow_law), is given by

$$\tau_{ij} = B\left(\sum_{k,l = 1}^3\left(\partial_lu_k\right)^2\right)^{-\frac{1}{3}} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \left(\partial_ju_i + \partial_iu_j\right)$$

for some constant *B*. The velocity of an ice sheet in steady state will then solve the nonlinear elliptic system

$$\sum_{j = 1}^3\partial_j\tau_{ij} + \rho g_i - \partial_ip = Q,$$

where *ρ* is the ice density, *g* is the gravitational acceleration vector, *p* is the pressure and *Q* is a forcing term.

:::

:::

## Elliptic regularity theorems {#Elliptic_regularity_theorems}

Let *L* be an elliptic operator of order 2*k* with coefficients having 2*k* continuous derivatives. The [Dirichlet problem](wikipedia://en/571109) for *L* is to find a function *u*, given a function *f* and some appropriate boundary values, such that *Lu = f* and such that *u* has the appropriate boundary values and normal derivatives. The existence theory for elliptic operators, using [Gårding's inequality](wikipedia://en/7999626), [Lax–Milgram lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax–Milgram_lemma) and [Fredholm alternative](wikipedia://en/3050954), states the sufficient condition for a [weak solution](wikipedia://en/3555416) *u* to exist in the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) *H*<sup>*k*</sup>.

For example, for a second-order elliptic operator as in **Example 2**,

- There is a number *γ>0* such that for each *μ>γ*, each $f\in L^2(U)$, there exists a unique solution $u\in H_{0}^{1}(U)$ of the boundary value problem $Lu+\mu u=f \text{ in }U, u=0\text{ on }\partial U$, which is based on [Lax-Milgram lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax-Milgram_lemma).
- Either (a) for any $f\in L^2(U)$, $Lu=f \text{ in }U, u=0\text{ on }\partial U$ (1) has a unique solution, or (b)$Lu=0 \text{ in }U, u=0\text{ on }\partial U$ has a solution $u\not\equiv 0$, which is based on the property of [compact operators](wikipedia://en/577441) and [Fredholm alternative](wikipedia://en/3050954).

This situation is ultimately unsatisfactory, as the weak solution *u* might not have enough derivatives for the expression *Lu* to be well-defined in the classical sense.

The *elliptic regularity theorem* guarantees that, provided *f* is square-integrable, *u* will in fact have *2k* square-integrable weak derivatives. In particular, if *f* is infinitely-often differentiable, then so is *u*.

For *L* as in **Example 2**,

- **Interior regularity**: If *m* is a natural number, $a^{ij},b^{j},c \in C^{m+1}(U), f\in H^{m}(U)$ (2) , $u\in H_{0}^{1}(U)$ is a weak solution to (1), then for any open set *V* in *U* with compact closure, $\|u\|_{H^{m+2}(V)}\le C(\|f\|_{H^{m}(U)}+\|u\|_{L^2(U)})$(3), where *C* depends on *U, V, L, m*, per se $u\in H_{loc}^{m+2}(U)$, which also holds if *m* is infinity by [Sobolev embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/4665038).
- **Boundary regularity**: (2) together with the assumption that $\partial U$ is $C^{m+2}$ indicates that (3) still holds after replacing *V* with *U,* i.e. $u\in H^{m+2}(U)$, which also holds if *m* is infinity.

Any differential operator exhibiting this property is called a [hypoelliptic operator](wikipedia://en/9282128); thus, every elliptic operator is hypoelliptic. The property also means that every [fundamental solution](wikipedia://en/1820858) of an elliptic operator is infinitely differentiable in any neighborhood not containing 0.

As an application, suppose a function $f$ satisfies the [Cauchy–Riemann equations](wikipedia://en/7583). Since the Cauchy-Riemann equations form an elliptic operator, it follows that $f$ is smooth.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

For *L* as in **Example 2** on *U*, which is an open domain with *C<sup>1</sup>* boundary, then there is a number *γ*>0 such that for each *μ*>*γ*, $L+\mu I: H_{0}^{1}(U)\rightarrow H_{0}^{1}(U)$ satisfies the assumptions of [Lax–Milgram lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax–Milgram_lemma).

- Invertibility: For each *μ*>*γ*, $L+\mu I:L^2(U)\rightarrow L^2(U)$ admits a [compact](wikipedia://en/577441) inverse.
- [Eigenvalues and eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429): If *A* is symmetric, *b<sup>i</sup>,c* are zero, then (1) Eigenvalues of *L*, are real, positive, countable, unbounded (2) There is an orthonormal basis of *L<sup>2</sup>(U)* composed of eigenvectors of *L*. (See [Spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495).)
- Generates a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/1644938) on *L<sup>2</sup>(U)*: −*L* generates a semigroup $\{S(t);t\geq 0\}$ of bounded linear operators on *L<sup>2</sup>(U)* s.t. $\frac{d}{dt}S(t)u_0=-LS(t)u_0, \|S(t)\|\leq e^{\gamma t}$ in the norm of *L<sup>2</sup>(U),* for every $u_0\in L^2(U)$, by [Hille–Yosida theorem](wikipedia://en/1578212).

## General definition {#General_definition}

Let $D$ be a (possibly nonlinear) differential operator between [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524) of the same rank having fiberwise inner products. Take its [principal symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol_of_a_differential_operator) $\sigma_\xi(D)$ with respect to a one-form $\xi$. (Basically, what we are doing is replacing the highest order [covariant derivatives](wikipedia://en/431848) by vector fields and evaluating against the one form $\xi$.)

We say $D$ is *weakly elliptic* if $\sigma_\xi(D)$ is a linear [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) for every non-zero $\xi$.

We say $D$ is (uniformly) *strongly elliptic* if for some constant $c > 0$,

$$||\sigma_\xi(D)(v)|| \geq c\|v\|$$

for all $\|\xi\|=1$ and all $v$.

The definition of ellipticity in the previous part of the article is *strong ellipticity*. Notice that the $\xi$ are covector fields or one-forms, but the $v$ are elements of the vector bundle upon which $D$ acts.

The quintessential example of a (strongly) elliptic operator is the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) (or its negative, depending upon convention). It is not hard to see that $D$ needs to be of even order for strong ellipticity to even be an option. Otherwise, just consider plugging in both $\xi$ and its negative. On the other hand, a weakly elliptic first-order operator, such as the [Dirac operator](wikipedia://en/1079448) can square to become a strongly elliptic operator, such as the Laplacian. The composition of weakly elliptic operators is weakly elliptic.

Weak ellipticity is nevertheless strong enough for the [Fredholm alternative](wikipedia://en/3050954), [Schauder estimates](wikipedia://en/23547303), and the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](wikipedia://en/324752). On the other hand, we need strong ellipticity for the [maximum principle](wikipedia://en/1436104), and to guarantee that the eigenvalues are discrete, and their only limit point is infinity.
