# Back-of-the-envelope calculation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Back-of-the-envelope calculation"}

*Image: Notes on a napkin.jpg.* Notes being made on a [napkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napkin)

A **back-of-the-envelope calculation** is a rough calculation, typically jotted down on any available scrap of paper such as an [envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envelope). It is more than a [guess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guess) but less than an accurate [calculation](wikipedia://en/239121) or [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285). The defining characteristic of back-of-the-envelope calculations is the use of simplified assumptions.

:::

A similar phrase in the US is **back of a napkin**, also used in the business world to describe sketching out a quick, rough idea of a business or product.[^o2] In British English, a similar [idiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiom) is **back of a fag packet**, referring to a [cigarette pack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cigarette_pack).

## History {#History}

In the natural sciences, *back-of-the-envelope calculation* is often associated with physicist [Enrico Fermi](wikipedia://en/10264),[^o4] who was well known for emphasizing ways that complex scientific equations could be approximated within an [order of magnitude](wikipedia://en/22657) using simple calculations. He went on to develop a series of sample calculations, which are called "Fermi Questions" or "Back-of-the-Envelope Calculations" and used to solve [Fermi problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_problem).[^o5][^o6]

Fermi was known for getting quick and accurate answers to problems that would stump other people. The most famous instance came during the [first atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_atomic_bomb) test in [New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico) on 16 July 1945. As the blast wave reached him, Fermi dropped bits of paper. By measuring the distance they were blown, he could compare to a previously computed table and thus estimate the bomb energy yield. He estimated 10 kilotons of TNT; the measured result was 18.6.[^o7][^o8]

Perhaps the most influential example of such a calculation was carried out over a period of a few hours by [Arnold Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Wilkins) after being asked to consider a problem by [Robert Watson Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Watson_Watt). Watt had learned that the Germans claimed to have invented a radio-based death ray, but Wilkins' one-page calculations demonstrated that such a thing was almost certainly impossible. When Watt asked what role radio might play, Wilkins replied that it might be useful for detection at long range, a suggestion that led to the rapid development of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) and the [Chain Home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_Home) system.[^o9]

Another example is [Victor Weisskopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Weisskopf)'s pamphlet *Modern Physics from an Elementary Point of View*.[^o10] In these notes Weisskopf used back-of-the-envelope calculations to calculate the size of a hydrogen atom, a star, and a mountain, all using elementary physics.

## Examples {#Examples}

In a video interview for the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley) on the 50th anniversary of the laser, Nobel laureate [Charles Townes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Townes) described how he pulled an envelope from his pocket while sitting in a park and wrote down calculations during his initial insight into lasers.[^o13]

During lunch with NFL commissioner [Pete Rozelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pete_Rozelle) in 1966, [Tiffany & Co.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiffany_&_Co.) vice president Oscar Riedner made a sketch on a cocktail napkin of what would become the [Vince Lombardi Trophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vince_Lombardi_Trophy), awarded annually to the winner of the [Super Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Bowl).[^o14]

An important Internet protocol, the [Border Gateway Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border_Gateway_Protocol), was sketched out in 1989 by engineers on the back of "three ketchup-stained napkins", and is still known as the three-napkin protocol.[^o15]

[UTF-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UTF-8), the dominant character encoding for the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web),[^o16] was designed by [Ken Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Thompson) and [Rob Pike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rob_Pike) on a placemat.[^o17]

The [Bailey bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailey_bridge) is a type of portable, pre-fabricated, truss bridge and was extensively used by British, Canadian and US military engineering units. [Donald Bailey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Bailey_%28civil_engineer%29) drew the original design for the bridge on the back of an envelope.[^o18]

The [Laffer Curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laffer_Curve), which claims to show the relationship between tax cuts and government income, was drawn by [Arthur Laffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Laffer) in 1974 on a bar napkin to show an aide to President [Gerald R. Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_R._Ford) why the federal government should cut taxes.[^o19]

Upon hearing that the [S-IV 2nd Stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_I#S-IV_stage) of the [Saturn I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_I) would need transport from California to Florida for launch as part of the [Apollo program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_program), [Jack Conroy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Conroy) sketched the cavernous cargo airplane, the [Pregnant Guppy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aero_Spacelines_Pregnant_Guppy).[^o20]

The [Video Toaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_Toaster) was designed on placemats in a Topeka pizza restaurant.[^o21]
