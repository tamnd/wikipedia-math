# Paul Lévy (mathematician)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paul Lévy"}

**Paul Pierre Lévy** (; 15 September 1886 – 15 December 1971) was a [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) mathematician who was active especially in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), introducing fundamental concepts such as [local time](wikipedia://en/5678101), [stable distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_distributions) and [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401). [Lévy processes](wikipedia://en/1025748), [Lévy flights](wikipedia://en/370874), [Lévy measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy_measure), [Lévy's constant](wikipedia://en/1067914), the [Lévy distribution](wikipedia://en/1346871), the [Lévy area](wikipedia://en/74751833), the [Lévy arcsine law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy_arcsine_law), and the [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) [Lévy C curve](wikipedia://en/1057085) are named after him.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Lévy was born in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989) to a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew) family which already included several mathematicians.[^o6] His father Lucien Lévy was an examiner at the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique). Lévy attended the École Polytechnique and published his first paper in 1905, at the age of nineteen, while still an undergraduate, in which he introduced the [Lévy–Steinitz theorem](wikipedia://en/57275032). His teacher and advisor was [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675). After graduation, he spent a year in military service and then studied for three years at the [École des Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_des_Mines), where he became a professor in 1913.[^o4]

During [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) Lévy conducted mathematical analysis work for the French Artillery. In 1920 he was appointed Professor of Analysis at the École Polytechnique, where his students included [Benoît Mandelbrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoît_Mandelbrot) and [Georges Matheron](wikipedia://en/4415402).

After the German invasion and occupation of France in June 1940, the Nazis moved the École Polytechnique to Lyon, and Lévy moved to Lyon to continue teaching. On 3 October 1940 the Vichy government [enacted a law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vichy_anti-Jewish_legislation) that required all Jewish faculty be fired. Lévy received his termination notice 19 December 1940, but the director of the École Polytechnique got Lévy reinstated by 14 March 1941. Increasing Nazi oppression prompted Lévy to flee Lyon and go live in hiding with his son-in-law Robert Piron, in Montbonnot, just one week before the German invasion of Vichy France on 11 November 1942. In hiding until the Allied liberation of France, Lévy continued his mathematics work. After the war, Lévy returned to the École Polytechnique in Paris and remained there until his retirement in 1959.[^o7][^o4]

Lévy made many fundamental contributions to probability theory and the nascent theory of stochastic processes. He introduced the notion of 'stable distribution' which share the property of stability under addition of independent variables and proved a general version of the Central Limit theorem, recorded in his 1937 book *Théorie de l'addition des variables aléatoires*, using the notion of characteristic function. He also introduced, independently from [Aleksandr Khinchin](wikipedia://en/1029748), the notion of infinitely divisible law and derived their characterization through the [Lévy–Khintchine representation](wikipedia://en/1025748#Lévy–Khintchine_representation).

In the 1930s, while investigating conditions under which the [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055) remained valid for dependent random variables, Lévy introduced the concept now known as a [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536). He considered partial sums $S_n$ satisfying

$$\operatorname{E}(S_{n+1}\mid S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_n)=S_n,$$

and used this conditional-expectation property to obtain almost-sure convergence results. [Joseph L. Doob](wikipedia://en/1547599) subsequently developed martingales into a general and widely applicable theory.[^o8]

His 1948 monograph on [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436), *Processus stochastiques et mouvement brownien*, contains a wealth of new concepts and results, including the [Lévy area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy_area), the [Lévy arcsine law](wikipedia://en/26388591), the [local time](wikipedia://en/5678101) of a Brownian path, and many other results.

Lévy received a number of honours, including membership at the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) and honorary membership at the [London Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/591097).

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

In 1913 Lévy married Suzanne Lévy (1892-1973). Her maternal grandfather was [philologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philology) [Henri Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Weil). The couple had three children, [Marie-Hélène](wikipedia://en/46727387) in 1913, Denise in 1916 and Jean Claude in 1918. [Marie-Hélène Schwartz](wikipedia://en/46727387) and her husband [Laurent Schwartz](wikipedia://en/317485) were also notable mathematicians.[^o10] Denise married Robert Piron, an engineer, and worked as a professor of German at the [lycée Molière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Molière_%28Paris%29). Jean Claude became a naval engineer.[^o11]
