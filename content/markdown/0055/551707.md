# The Magic Words are Squeamish Ossifrage

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="The Magic Words are Squeamish Ossifrage"}

"**The Magic Words are Squeamish Ossifrage**" was the solution to a challenge [ciphertext](wikipedia://en/175560) posed by the inventors of the [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) [cipher](wikipedia://en/5244) in 1977. The problem appeared in [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435)'s *[Mathematical Games column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Games_column)* in the August 1977 issue of *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*.[^o2] It was solved in 1993–94 by a large, joint computer project co-ordinated by [Derek Atkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Atkins), [Michael Graff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Graff), [Arjen Lenstra](wikipedia://en/6850292) and [Paul Leyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Leyland).[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6] More than 600 volunteers contributed [CPU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_processing_unit) time from about 1,600 machines (two of which were [fax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fax) machines) over six months. The coordination was done via the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) and was one of the first such projects.

:::

*Ossifrage* ('bone-breaker', from Latin) is an older name for the [bearded vulture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearded_vulture), a scavenger famous for dropping animal bones and live tortoises on top of rocks to crack them open. The 1993–94 effort began the tradition of using the words "squeamish ossifrage" in [cryptanalytic](wikipedia://en/5715) challenges.

The difficulty of [breaking the RSA cipher](wikipedia://en/1544741)—recovering a [plaintext](wikipedia://en/157935) message given a ciphertext and the public key—is connected to the difficulty of [factoring](wikipedia://en/15491) large numbers. While it is not known whether the two problems are mathematically equivalent, factoring is currently the only publicly known method of directly breaking RSA. The [decryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decryption) of the 1977 ciphertext involved the factoring of a 129-digit (426 bit) number, [RSA-129](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA-129), in order to recover the plaintext.

[Ron Rivest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Rivest) estimated in 1977 that factoring a 125-digit semiprime would require 40 [quadrillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1000000000000000_%28number%29) years, using the best [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) known and the fastest computers of the day.[^o7] In their original paper they recommended using 200-digit (663 bit) primes to provide a margin of safety against future developments,[^o8] though it may have only delayed the solution as a 200-digit semiprime was factored in 2005.[^o9][^o10] However, efficient factoring algorithms had not been studied much at the time, and a lot of progress was made in the following decades. Atkins et al. used the [quadratic sieve](wikipedia://en/582340) algorithm invented by [Carl Pomerance](wikipedia://en/2134159) in 1981. While the asymptotically faster [number field sieve](wikipedia://en/152734) had just been invented, it was not clear at the time that it would be better than the quadratic sieve for 129-digit numbers. The memory requirements of the newer algorithm were also a concern.[^o11]

There was a US\$100 prize associated with the challenge, which the winners donated to the [Free Software Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Software_Foundation).

In 2015, the same RSA-129 number was factored in about one day, with the CADO-NFS open source implementation of number field sieve, using a commercial cloud computing service for about \$30.[^o12]
