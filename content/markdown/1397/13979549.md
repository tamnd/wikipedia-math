# Ε-quadratic form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ε-quadratic form"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically the theory of [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478), an ***ε*-quadratic form** is a generalization of quadratic forms to skew-symmetric settings and to [\*-rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/*-ring); *ε* = ±1, accordingly for symmetric or skew-symmetric. They are also called $(-)^n$-quadratic forms, particularly in the context of [surgery theory](wikipedia://en/5961115).

:::

There is the related notion of ***ε*-symmetric forms**, which generalizes [symmetric forms](wikipedia://en/1803375), [skew-symmetric forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-symmetric_form) (= [symplectic forms](wikipedia://en/292852)), [Hermitian forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form), and [skew-Hermitian forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-Hermitian_form). More briefly, one may refer to quadratic, skew-quadratic, symmetric, and skew-symmetric forms, where "skew" means (−) and the \* (involution) is implied.

The theory is 2-local: [away from 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Away_from_2), *ε*-quadratic forms are equivalent to *ε*-symmetric forms: half the symmetrization map (below) gives an explicit isomorphism.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*ε*-symmetric forms and *ε*-quadratic forms are defined as follows.[^o4]

Given a module *M* over a [\*-ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/*-ring) *R*, let *B*(*M*) be the space of [bilinear forms](wikipedia://en/449477) on *M*, and let *T* : *B*(*M*) → *B*(*M*) be the "[conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548)" [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) *B*(*u*, *v*) ↦ *B*(*v*, *u*)\*. Since multiplication by −1 is also an involution and commutes with linear maps, −*T* is also an involution. Thus we can write *ε* = ±1 and *εT* is an involution, either *T* or −*T* (ε can be more general than ±1; see below). Define the ***ε*-symmetric forms** as the [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638) of *εT*, and the ***ε*-quadratic forms** are the [coinvariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coinvariant).

As an [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215),

$$0 \to Q^\varepsilon(M) \to B(M) \stackrel{1-\varepsilon T}{\longrightarrow} B(M) \to Q_\varepsilon(M) \to 0$$

As [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) and [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715),

$$Q^\varepsilon(M) := \mbox{ker}\,(1-\varepsilon T)$$

$$Q_\varepsilon(M) := \mbox{coker}\,(1-\varepsilon T)$$

The notation *Q*<sup>*ε*</sup>(*M*), *Q*<sub>*ε*</sub>(*M*) follows the standard notation *M<sup>G</sup>*, *M<sub>G</sub>* for the invariants and coinvariants for a [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29), here of the order 2 group (an involution).

Composition of the inclusion and quotient maps (but not 1 − *εT*) as $Q^\varepsilon(M) \to B(M) \to Q_\varepsilon(M)$ yields a map *Q*<sup>*ε*</sup>(*M*) → *Q*<sub>*ε*</sub>(*M*): every *ε*-symmetric form determines an *ε*-quadratic form.

### Symmetrization {#Symmetrization}

Conversely, one can define a reverse homomorphism "1 + *εT*": *Q*<sub>*ε*</sub>(*M*) → *Q*<sup>*ε*</sup>(*M*), called the **symmetrization map** (since it yields a symmetric form) by taking any lift of a quadratic form and multiplying it by 1 + *εT*. This is a symmetric form because (1 − *εT*)(1 + *εT*) = 1 − *T*<sup>2</sup> = 0, so it is in the kernel. More precisely, $(1 + \varepsilon T)B(M) < Q^\varepsilon(M)$. The map is well-defined by the same equation: choosing a different lift corresponds to adding a multiple of (1 − *εT*), but this vanishes after multiplying by 1 + *εT*. Thus every *ε*-quadratic form determines an *ε*-symmetric form.

Composing these two maps either way: *Q*<sup>*ε*</sup>(*M*) → *Q*<sub>*ε*</sub>(*M*) → *Q*<sup>*ε*</sup>(*M*) or *Q*<sub>*ε*</sub>(*M*) → *Q*<sup>*ε*</sup>(*M*) → *Q*<sub>*ε*</sub>(*M*) yields multiplication by 2, and thus these maps are bijective if 2 is invertible in *R*, with the inverse given by multiplication with 1/2.

An *ε*-quadratic form *ψ* ∈ *Q*<sub>*ε*</sub>(*M*) is called **non-degenerate** if the associated *ε*-symmetric form (1 + *εT*)(*ψ*) is non-degenerate.

### Generalization from \* {#Generalization_from_*}

If the \* is trivial, then *ε* = ±1, and "away from 2" means that 2 is invertible: 1/2 ∈ *R*.

More generally, one can take for *ε* ∈ *R* any element such that *ε*\**ε* = 1. *ε* = ±1 always satisfy this, but so does any element of norm 1, such as complex numbers of unit norm.

Similarly, in the presence of a non-trivial \*, *ε*-symmetric forms are equivalent to *ε*-quadratic forms if there is an element *λ* ∈ *R* such that *λ*\* + *λ* = 1. If \* is trivial, this is equivalent to 2*λ* = 1 or *λ* = 1/2, while if \* is non-trivial there can be multiple possible *λ*; for example, over the complex numbers any number with real part 1/2 is such a *λ*.

For instance, in the ring $R=\mathbf{Z}\left[\textstyle{\frac{1+i}{2}}\right]$ (the integral lattice for the quadratic form 2*x*<sup>2</sup> − 2*x* + 1), with [complex conjugation](wikipedia://en/173918), $\lambda=\textstyle{\frac{1\pm i}{2}}$ are two such elements, though 1/2 ∉ *R*.

:::

## Intuition {#Intuition}

In terms of matrices (we take *V* to be 2-dimensional), if \* is trivial:

- matrices $\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\c & d\end{pmatrix}$ correspond to bilinear forms
- the subspace of symmetric matrices $\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\b & c\end{pmatrix}$ correspond to symmetric forms
- the subspace of (−1)-symmetric matrices $\begin{pmatrix}0 & b\\-b & 0\end{pmatrix}$ correspond to [symplectic forms](wikipedia://en/292852)
- the bilinear form $\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\c & d\end{pmatrix}$ yields the quadratic form

$$ax^2 + bxy+cyx + dy^2 = ax^2 + (b+c)xy + dy^2\,,$$

- the map 1 + T from quadratic forms to symmetric forms maps $ex^2 + fxy + gy^2$

to $\begin{pmatrix}2e & f\\f & 2g\end{pmatrix}$, for example by lifting to $\begin{pmatrix}e & f\\0 & g\end{pmatrix}$ and then adding to transpose. Mapping back to quadratic forms yields double the original: $2ex^2 + 2fxy + 2gy^2 = 2(ex^2 + fxy + gy^2)$.

If $\bar{\cdot }$ is complex conjugation, then

- the subspace of symmetric matrices are the [Hermitian matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_matrices) $\begin{pmatrix}a & z\\ \bar z & c\end{pmatrix}$
- the subspace of skew-symmetric matrices are the [skew-Hermitian matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-Hermitian_matrices) $\begin{pmatrix}bi & z\\ -\bar z & di\end{pmatrix}$

### Refinements {#Refinements}

An intuitive way to understand an *ε*-quadratic form is to think of it as a **quadratic refinement** of its associated *ε*-symmetric form.

For instance, in defining a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) over a general field or ring, one quotients the [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628) by relations coming from the [symmetric form](wikipedia://en/1803375) *and* the quadratic form: *vw* + *wv* = 2*B*(*v*, *w*) and $v^2=Q(v)$. If 2 is invertible, this second relation follows from the first (as the quadratic form can be recovered from the associated bilinear form), but at 2 this additional refinement is necessary.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

An easy example for an *ε*-quadratic form is the **standard hyperbolic *ε*-quadratic form** $H_\varepsilon(R) \in Q_\varepsilon(R \oplus R^*)$. (Here, *R*\* := Hom<sub>*R*</sub>(*R*, *R*) denotes the dual of the *R*-module *R*.) It is given by the bilinear form $((v_1,f_1),(v_2,f_2)) \mapsto f_2(v_1)$. The standard hyperbolic *ε*-quadratic form is needed for the definition of [*L*-theory](wikipedia://en/696568).

For the field of two elements *R* = **F**<sub>2</sub> there is no difference between (+1)-quadratic and (−1)-quadratic forms, which are just called [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478). The [Arf invariant](wikipedia://en/6950659) of a [nonsingular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities) quadratic form over **F**<sub>2</sub> is an **F**<sub>2</sub>-valued invariant with important applications in both algebra and topology, and plays a role similar to that played by the [discriminant of a quadratic form](wikipedia://en/55607#Discriminant_of_a_quadratic_form) in characteristic not equal to two.

### Manifolds {#Manifolds}

The free part of the middle [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) (with integer coefficients) of an oriented even-dimensional manifold has an *ε*-symmetric form, via [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732), the [intersection form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_product). In the case of [singly even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singly_even) dimension 4*k* + 2, this is skew-symmetric, while for [doubly even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_even) dimension 4*k*, this is symmetric. Geometrically this corresponds to intersection, where two *n*/2-dimensional submanifolds in an *n*-dimensional manifold generically intersect in a 0-dimensional [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095) (a set of points), by adding [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149). For singly even dimension the order switches sign, while for doubly even dimension order does not change sign, hence the *ε*-symmetry. The simplest cases are for the product of spheres, where the product *S*<sup>2*k*</sup> × *S*<sup>2*k*</sup> and *S*<sup>2*k*+1</sup> × *S*<sup>2*k*+1</sup> respectively give the symmetric form $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and skew-symmetric form $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right).$ In dimension two, this yields a torus, and taking the [connected sum](wikipedia://en/490134) of *g* tori yields the surface of genus *g*, whose middle homology has the standard hyperbolic form.

With additional structure, this *ε*-symmetric form can be refined to an *ε*-quadratic form. For doubly even dimension this is integer valued, while for singly even dimension this is only defined up to parity, and takes values in **Z**/2. For example, given a [framed manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framed_manifold), one can produce such a refinement. For singly even dimension, the Arf invariant of this skew-quadratic form is the [Kervaire invariant](wikipedia://en/13816037).

Given an oriented surface Σ embedded in **R**<sup>3</sup>, the middle homology group *H*<sub>1</sub>(Σ) carries not only a skew-symmetric form (via intersection), but also a skew-quadratic form, which can be seen as a quadratic refinement, via self-linking. The skew-symmetric form is an invariant of the surface Σ, whereas the skew-quadratic form is an invariant of the embedding Σ ⊂ **R**<sup>3</sup>, e.g. for the [Seifert surface](wikipedia://en/1343951) of a [knot](wikipedia://en/366808). The [Arf invariant](wikipedia://en/6950659) of the skew-quadratic form is a framed [cobordism](wikipedia://en/391832) invariant generating the first stable [homotopy group](wikipedia://en/297506) $\pi^s_1$.

*Image: Hopfkeyrings.jpg.* In the standard embedding of the torus, a (1, 1) curve self-links, thus *Q*(1, 1) = 1.

For the standard embedded [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), the skew-symmetric form is given by $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0 & 1\\-1 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ (with respect to the standard [symplectic basis](wikipedia://en/42486404)), and the skew-quadratic refinement is given by *xy* with respect to this basis: *Q*(1, 0) = *Q*(0, 1) = 0: the basis curves don't self-link; and *Q*(1, 1) = 1: a (1, 1) self-links, as in the [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384). (This form has [Arf invariant](wikipedia://en/6950659) 0, and thus this embedded torus has [Kervaire invariant](wikipedia://en/13816037) 0.)

## Applications {#Applications}

A key application is in algebraic [surgery theory](wikipedia://en/5961115), where even [L-groups](wikipedia://en/696568) are defined as [Witt groups](wikipedia://en/5578523) of *ε*-quadratic forms, by [C.T.C.Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.T.C._Wall)

:::
