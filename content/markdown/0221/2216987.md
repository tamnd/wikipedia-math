# Covariance and correlation

Covariance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Covariance and correlation"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the mathematical concepts of **covariance** and **correlation** are very similar. Both describe the degree to which two [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) or [sets of random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) tend to deviate from their [expected values](wikipedia://en/9653) in similar ways.

:::

If *X* and *Y* are two random variables, with [means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) (expected values) *μ<sub>X</sub>* and *μ<sub>Y</sub>* and [standard deviations](wikipedia://en/27590) *σ<sub>X</sub>* and *σ<sub>Y</sub>*, respectively, then their covariance and correlation are as follows:

**[covariance](wikipedia://en/157059)**

$$\text{cov}_{XY} = \sigma_{XY} = E[(X-\mu_X)\,(Y-\mu_Y)]$$

**[correlation](wikipedia://en/157057)**

$$\text{corr}_{XY} = \rho_{XY} = E[(X-\mu_X)\,(Y-\mu_Y)]/(\sigma_X \sigma_Y)\,,$$

so that

$$\rho_{XY} = \sigma_{XY} / (\sigma_X \sigma_Y)$$

where *E* is the expected value operator. Notably, correlation is [dimensionless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless) while covariance is in units obtained by multiplying the units of the two variables.

If *Y* always takes on the same values as *X*, we have the covariance of a variable with itself (i.e. $\sigma_{XX}$), which is called the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) and is more commonly denoted as $\sigma_X^2,$ the square of the standard deviation. The *correlation* of a variable with itself is always 1 (except in the [degenerate case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_case) where the two variances are zero because *X* always takes on the same single value, in which case the correlation does not exist since its computation would involve [division by 0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_by_0)). More generally, the correlation between two variables is 1 (or –1) if one of them always takes on a value that is given exactly by a [linear function](wikipedia://en/152111) of the other with respectively a positive (or negative) [slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_%28mathematics%29).

Although the values of the theoretical covariances and correlations are linked in the above way, the probability distributions of [sample estimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean_and_sample_covariance) of these quantities are not linked in any simple way and they generally need to be treated separately.

## Multiple random variables {#Multiple_random_variables}

With any number of random variables in excess of 1, the variables can be stacked into a [random vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) whose *i*<sup>th</sup> element is the *i*<sup>th</sup> random variable. Then the variances and covariances can be placed in a [covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/191752), in which the (*i*, *j*) element is the covariance between the *i*<sup>th</sup> random variable and the *j*<sup>th</sup> one. Likewise, the correlations can be placed in a [correlation matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_matrix).

## Time series analysis {#Time_series_analysis}

In the case of a [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) which is [stationary](wikipedia://en/329898) in the wide sense, both the means and variances are constant over time (E(*X<sub>n+m</sub>*) = E(*X<sub>n</sub>*) =*μ<sub>X</sub>* and var(*X<sub>n+m</sub>*) = var(*X<sub>n</sub>*) and likewise for the variable *Y*). In this case the cross-covariance and cross-correlation are functions of the time difference:

**[cross-covariance](wikipedia://en/2198965)**

$$\sigma_{XY}(m)=E[ (X_n-\mu_X)\,(Y_{n+m}-\mu_Y)],$$

**[cross-correlation](wikipedia://en/714163)**

$$\rho_{XY}(m) =E[ (X_n-\mu_X)\,(Y_{n+m}-\mu_Y)]/(\sigma_{X} \sigma_{Y}).$$

If *Y* is the same variable as *X*, the above expressions are called the *autocovariance* and *autocorrelation*:

**[autocovariance](wikipedia://en/1202314)**

$$\sigma_{XX}(m)=E[ (X_n-\mu_X)\,(X_{n+m}-\mu_X)],$$

**[autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724)**

$$\rho_{XX}(m) =E[ (X_n-\mu_X)\,(X_{n+m}-\mu_X)]/(\sigma_{X}^2).$$
