# Circular symmetry

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Circular symmetry"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **circular symmetry** is a type of [continuous symmetry](wikipedia://en/3667668) for a [planar object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) that can be [rotated](wikipedia://en/701096) by any arbitrary [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) and map onto itself.

:::

Rotational circular symmetry is isomorphic with the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), or the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) SO(2), and [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) U(1). Reflective circular symmetry is isomorphic with the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) O(2).

## Two dimensions {#Two_dimensions}

A 2-dimensional object with circular symmetry would consist of [concentric circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric_circle) and [annular](wikipedia://en/356158) domains.

Rotational circular symmetry has all [cyclic symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_symmetry), Z<sub>*n*</sub> as subgroup symmetries. Reflective circular symmetry has all [dihedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_symmetry), Dih<sub>*n*</sub> as subgroup symmetries.

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Axi-symmetric"}

## Three dimensions {#Three_dimensions}

*Image: DoubleCone.png.* A double-cone is a [surface of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549), generated by a line.

In 3-dimensions, a [surface](wikipedia://en/329549) or [solid of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400) has **circular symmetry** around an axis, also called **cylindrical symmetry** or **axial symmetry**. An example is a right circular [cone](wikipedia://en/782427). Circular symmetry in 3 dimensions has all [pyramidal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramidal_symmetry), C<sub>*n*v</sub> as subgroups.

A [double-cone](wikipedia://en/782427#Other_mathematical_meanings.), [bicone](wikipedia://en/13953265), [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29), [toroid](wikipedia://en/14973218) and [spheroid](wikipedia://en/82365) have circular symmetry, and in addition have a [bilateral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_symmetry) perpendicular to the axis of system (or **half cylindrical symmetry**). These reflective circular symmetries have all discrete [prismatic symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prismatic_symmetry), D<sub>*n*h</sub> as subgroups.

:::

In four dimensions, an object can have circular symmetry, on two orthogonal axis planes, or **duocylindrical symmetry**. For example, the [duocylinder](wikipedia://en/2114445) and [Clifford torus](wikipedia://en/7950934) have circular symmetry in two orthogonal axes. A [spherinder](wikipedia://en/39661691) has spherical symmetry in one 3-space, and circular symmetry in the orthogonal direction.

## Spherical symmetry {#Spherical_symmetry}

*Image: Blender-meta-ball.png.* An unmarked [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) has *reflectional spherical symmetry*.

An analogous 3-dimensional equivalent term is **spherical symmetry**.

Rotational spherical symmetry is isomorphic with the [rotation group SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29), and can be parametrized by the [Davenport chained rotations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davenport_chained_rotations) pitch, yaw, and roll. Rotational spherical symmetry has all the discrete chiral 3D [point groups](wikipedia://en/2694525) as subgroups. Reflectional spherical symmetry is isomorphic with the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) O(3) and has the 3-dimensional discrete point groups as subgroups.

A [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) has spherical symmetry if it depends on the distance to the origin only, such as the [potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential) of a [central force](wikipedia://en/1888817). A [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) has spherical symmetry if it is in radially inward or outward direction with a magnitude and orientation (inward/outward) depending on the distance to the origin only, such as a central force.
