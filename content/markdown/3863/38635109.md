# Complexification (Lie group)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complexification"}

Semi-simple In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **complexification** or **universal complexification** of a [real Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Lie_group) is given by a continuous homomorphism of the group into a [complex Lie group](wikipedia://en/2187847) with the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) that every continuous homomorphism of the original group into another complex Lie group extends compatibly to a [complex analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analytic_function) homomorphism between the complex Lie groups. The complexification, which always exists, is unique [up to unique isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism). Its [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) is a quotient of the [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) of the Lie algebra of the original group. They are isomorphic if the original group has a quotient by a discrete [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) which is linear.

:::

For [compact Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Lie_group), the complexification, sometimes called the **Chevalley complexification** after [Claude Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040), can be defined as the group of complex characters of the [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) of [representative functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_function), i.e. the [matrix coefficients](wikipedia://en/11017946) of finite-dimensional [representations](wikipedia://en/292788) of the group. In any finite-dimensional faithful unitary representation of the compact group it can be realized concretely as a closed subgroup of the complex [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564). It consists of operators with [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) $g = u \bullet \exp iX$, where $u$ is a unitary operator in the compact group and $X$ is a [skew-adjoint operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-adjoint_operator) in its Lie algebra. In this case the complexification is a [complex algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) and its Lie algebra is the complexification of the Lie algebra of the compact Lie group.

## Universal complexification {#Universal_complexification}

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

If $G$ is a Lie group, a **universal complexification** is given by a complex Lie group $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ and a continuous homomorphism $\varphi: G \to G_{\mathbf{C}}$ with the universal property that, if $f: G \to H$ is an arbitrary continuous homomorphism into a complex Lie group $H$, then there is a unique complex analytic homomorphism $F: G_{\mathbf{C}} \to H$ such that $f = F \circ \varphi$.

Universal complexifications always exist and are unique up to a unique complex analytic isomorphism (preserving inclusion of the original group).

:::

### Existence {#Existence}

If $G$ is connected with Lie algebra $\text{𝖌}$, then its [universal covering group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_covering_group) $\mathbf{G}$ is simply connected. Let $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}$ be the simply connected complex Lie group with Lie algebra $\text{𝖌}_{\mathbf{C}} = \text{𝖌} \otimes \mathbf{C}$, let $\Phi: \mathbf{G} \to \mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}$ be the natural homomorphism (the unique morphism such that $\Phi_{*}: \text{𝖌} \hookrightarrow \text{𝖌} \otimes \mathbf{C}$ is the canonical inclusion) and suppose $\pi: \mathbf{G} \to G$ is the universal covering map, so that $\ker \pi$ is the fundamental group of $G$. We have the inclusion $\Phi(\ker \pi) \subset Z(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}})$, which follows from the fact that the kernel of the adjoint representation of $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}$ equals its centre, combined with the equality

$$(C_{\Phi(k)})_*\circ \Phi_* = \Phi_* \circ (C_k)_* = \Phi_*$$

which holds for any $k \in \ker \pi$. Denoting by $\Phi(\ker \pi)^{*}$ the smallest closed normal Lie subgroup of $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}$ that contains $\Phi(\ker \pi)$, we must now also have the inclusion $\Phi(\ker \pi)^{*} \subset Z(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}})$. We define the universal complexification of $G$ as

$$G_{\mathbf C}=\frac{\mathbf G_{\mathbf C}}{\Phi(\ker \pi)^*}.$$

In particular, if $G$ is simply connected, its universal complexification is just $\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}$.[^o6]

The map $\varphi: G \to G_{\mathbf{C}}$ is obtained by passing to the quotient. Since $\pi$ is a surjective submersion, smoothness of the map $\pi_{\mathbf{C}} \circ \Phi$ implies smoothness of $\varphi$.

*Image: Complexification-quotient-map.svg.* Construction of the complexification map

For non-connected Lie groups $G$ with identity component $G^{o}$ and component group $\Gamma = G / G^{o}$, the extension

$$\{1\} \rightarrow G^o \rightarrow G \rightarrow \Gamma \rightarrow \{1\}$$

induces an extension

$$\{1\} \rightarrow (G^o)_{\mathbf{C}} \rightarrow G_{\mathbf{C}} \rightarrow \Gamma \rightarrow \{1\}$$

and the complex Lie group $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ is a complexification of $G$.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of the universal property"}

#### Proof of the universal property {#Proof_of_the_universal_property}

The map $\varphi: G \to G_{\mathbf{C}}$ indeed possesses the universal property which appears in the above definition of complexification. The proof of this statement naturally follows from considering the following instructive diagram.

*Image: Universal property of complexification.svg.* Universal property of complexification

Here, $f\colon G\rightarrow H$ is an arbitrary smooth homomorphism of Lie groups with a complex Lie group as the codomain.

For simplicity, we assume $G$ is connected. To establish the existence of $F$, we first naturally extend the morphism of Lie algebras $f_*\colon \mathfrak g\rightarrow \mathfrak h$ to the unique morphism $\overline f_*\colon \mathfrak g_{\mathbf C}\rightarrow \mathfrak h$ of complex Lie algebras. Since $\mathbf G_{\mathbf C}$ is simply connected, Lie's second fundamental theorem now provides us with a unique complex analytic morphism $\overline F\colon \mathbf G_{\mathbf C}\rightarrow H$ between complex Lie groups, such that $(\overline F)_*=\overline f_*$. We define $F\colon G_{\mathbf C}\rightarrow H$ as the map induced by $\overline F$, that is: $F(g\,\Phi(\ker \pi)^*)=\overline F(g)$ for any $g\in\mathbf G_{\mathbf C}$. To show well-definedness of this map (i.e. $\Phi(\ker\pi)^*\subset \ker \overline F$), consider the derivative of the map $\overline F\circ \Phi$. For any $v\in T_e \mathbf G\cong \mathfrak g$, we have

$$(\overline F)_*\Phi_*v=(\overline F)_*(v\otimes 1)=f_*\pi_*v,$$

which (by simple connectedness of $\mathbf G$) implies $\overline F\circ\Phi=f\circ\pi$. This equality finally implies $\Phi(\ker\pi)\subset \ker \overline F$, and since $\ker \overline F$ is a closed normal Lie subgroup of $\mathbf G_{\mathbf C}$, we also have $\Phi(\ker \pi)^*\subset \ker \overline F$. Since $\pi_{\mathbb C}$ is a complex analytic surjective submersion, the map $F$ is complex analytic since $\overline F$ is. The desired equality $F\circ\varphi=f$ is imminent.

To show uniqueness of $F$, suppose that $F_1,F_2$ are two maps with $F_1\circ\varphi=F_2\circ\varphi=f$. Composing with $\pi$ from the right and differentiating, we get $(F_1)_*(\pi_{\mathbf C})_*\Phi_*=(F_2)_*(\pi_{\mathbf C})_*\Phi_*$, and since $\Phi_*$ is the inclusion $\mathfrak g\hookrightarrow \mathfrak g_{\mathbf C}$, we get $(F_1)_*(\pi_{\mathbf C})_*=(F_2)_*(\pi_{\mathbf C})_*$. But $\pi_{\mathbf C}$ is a submersion, so $(F_1)_*=(F_2)_*$, thus connectedness of $G$ implies $F_1=F_2$.

:::

### Uniqueness {#Uniqueness}

The universal property implies that the universal complexification is unique up to complex analytic isomorphism.

### Injectivity {#Injectivity}

If the original group is linear, so too is the universal complexification and the homomorphism between the two is an inclusion.[^o12] (Onishchik Vinberg 1994) give an example of a connected real Lie group for which the homomorphism is not injective even at the Lie algebra level: they take the product of $\mathbf{T}$ by the [universal covering group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_covering_group) of $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbf{R})$ and quotient out by the discrete cyclic subgroup generated by an [irrational rotation](wikipedia://en/9445837) in the first factor and a generator of the center in the second.

### Basic examples {#Basic_examples}

The following isomorphisms of complexifications of Lie groups with known Lie groups can be constructed directly from the general construction of the complexification.

- The complexification of the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997) of 2x2 matrices is

$$\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathbf C}\cong \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C).$$

This follows from the isomorphism of Lie algebras

$$\mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbf C}\cong \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbf C),$$

together with the fact that $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is simply connected.

- The complexification of the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) of 2x2 matrices is

$$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)_{\mathbf C}\cong \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)\times \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C).$$

This follows from the isomorphism of Lie algebras

$$\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbf C)_{\mathbf C}\cong \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbf C) \oplus \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbf C),$$

together with the fact that $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)$ is simply connected.

- The complexification of the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) of 3x3 matrices is

$$\mathrm{SO}(3)_{\mathbf C}\cong \frac{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)}{\mathbf Z_2}\cong \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3),$$

where $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ denotes the proper orthochronous [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489). This follows from the fact that $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the universal (double) cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, hence:

$$\mathfrak{so}(3)_{\mathbf C}\cong \mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbf C} \cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbf C).$$

We also use the fact that $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)$ is the universal (double) cover of $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$.

- The complexification of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group is

$$\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)_{\mathbf C}\cong \frac{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)\times \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)}{\mathbf Z_2}.$$

This follows from the same isomorphism of Lie algebras as in the second example, again using the universal (double) cover of the proper orthochronous Lorentz group.

- The complexification of the special orthogonal group of 4x4 matrices is

$$\mathrm{SO}(4)_{\mathbf C}\cong \frac{\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)\times \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)}{\mathbf Z_2}.$$

This follows from the fact that $\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the universal (double) cover of $\mathrm{SO}(4)$, hence $\mathfrak{so}(4)\cong \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$ and so $\mathfrak{so}(4)_{\mathbf C}\cong \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbf C)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbf C)$.

The last two examples show that Lie groups with isomorphic complexifications may not be isomorphic. Furthermore, the complexifications of Lie groups $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbf C)$ show that complexification is not an idempotent operation, i.e. $(G_{\mathbf C})_{\mathbf C}\not\cong G_{\mathbf C}$ (this is also shown by complexifications of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$).

## Chevalley complexification {#Chevalley_complexification}

### Hopf algebra of matrix coefficients {#Hopf_algebra_of_matrix_coefficients}

If $G$is a compact Lie group, the \*-algebra $A$ of matrix coefficients of finite-dimensional unitary representations is a uniformly dense \*-subalgebra of $C(G)$, the \*-algebra of complex-valued continuous functions on $G$. It is naturally a [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) with [comultiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comultiplication) given by

$$\displaystyle{\Delta f(g,h)= f(gh).}$$

The characters of $A$ are the \*-homomorphisms of $A$ into $\mathbf{C}$. They can be identified with the point evaluations $f \mapsto f(g)$ for $g$ in $G$ and the comultiplication allows the group structure on $G$ to be recovered. The homomorphisms of $A$ into $\mathbf{C}$ also form a group. It is a complex Lie group and can be identified with the complexification $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ of $G$. The \*-algebra $A$ is generated by the matrix coefficients of any faithful representation $\sigma$ of $G$. It follows that $\sigma$ defines a faithful complex analytic representation of $G_{\mathbf{C}}$.[^o17]

### Invariant theory {#Invariant_theory}

The original approach of (Chevalley 1946) to the complexification of a compact Lie group can be concisely stated within the language of classical [invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762), described in (Weyl 1946). Let $G$ be a closed subgroup of the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $U(V)$ where $V$ is a finite-dimensional complex inner product space. Its Lie algebra consists of all skew-adjoint operators $X$ such that $\exp tX$ lies in $G$ for all real $t$. Set $W = V \oplus \mathbf{C}$ with the trivial action of $G$ on the second summand. The group $G$ acts on $W^{\otimes N}$, with an element $u$ acting as $u^{\otimes N}$. The [commutant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutant) (or centralizer algebra) is denoted by $A_{N} = \operatorname{End}_{G} W^{\otimes N}$. It is generated as a \*-algebra by its unitary operators and its commutant is the \*-algebra spanned by the operators $u^{\otimes N}$. The complexification $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ of $G$ consists of all operators $g$ in $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ such that $g^{\otimes N}$ commutes with $A_{N}$ and $g$ acts trivially on the second summand in $\mathbf{C}$. By definition it is a closed subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(V)$. The defining relations (as a commutant) show that $G$ is an algebraic subgroup. Its intersection with $U(V)$ coincides with $G$, since it is *a priori* a larger compact group for which the irreducible representations stay irreducible and inequivalent when restricted to $G$. Since $A_{N}$ is generated by unitaries, an invertible operator $g$ lies in $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ if the unitary operator $u$ and positive operator $p$ in its [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) $g = u \cdot p$ both lie in $G_{\mathbf{C}}$. Thus $u$ lies in $G$ and the operator $p$ can be written uniquely as $p = \exp T$ with $T$ a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408). By the [functional calculus](wikipedia://en/965348) for polynomial functions it follows that $h^{\otimes N}$ lies in the commutant of $A_{N}$ if $h = \exp z T$ with $z$ in $\mathbf{C}$. In particular taking $z$ purely imaginary, $T$ must have the form $iX$ with $X$ in the Lie algebra of $G$. Since every finite-dimensional representation of $G$ occurs as a direct summand of $W^{\otimes N}$, it is left invariant by $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ and thus every finite-dimensional representation of $G$ extends uniquely to $G_{\mathbf{C}}$. The extension is compatible with the polar decomposition. Finally the polar decomposition implies that $G$ is a maximal compact subgroup of $G_{\mathbf{C}}$, since a strictly larger compact subgroup would contain all integer powers of a positive operator $p$, a closed infinite discrete subgroup.[^o21]

## Decompositions in the Chevalley complexification {#Decompositions_in_the_Chevalley_complexification}

### Cartan decomposition {#Cartan_decomposition}

The decomposition derived from the polar decomposition

$$\displaystyle{G_{\mathbf{C}} = G\cdot P =G \cdot \exp i\mathfrak{g},}$$

where $\text{𝖌}$ is the Lie algebra of $G$, is called the **[Cartan decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139145)** of $G_{\mathbf{C}}$. The exponential factor $P$ is invariant under conjugation by $G$ but is not a subgroup. The complexification is invariant under taking adjoints, since $G$ consists of unitary operators and $P$ of positive operators.

### Gauss decomposition {#Gauss_decomposition}

The **Gauss decomposition** is a generalization of the [LU decomposition](wikipedia://en/6243993) for the general linear group and a specialization of the [Bruhat decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139142). For $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ it states that with respect to a given orthonormal basis $e_{1}, ..., e_{n}$ an element $g$ of $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ can be factorized in the form

$$\displaystyle{g=XDY}$$

with $X$ lower [unitriangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitriangular_matrix), $Y$ upper unitriangular and $D$ diagonal if and only if all the [principal minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_minor) of $g$ are non-vanishing. In this case $X, Y$ and $D$ are uniquely determined.

In fact Gaussian elimination shows there is a unique $X$ such that $X^{-1} g$ is upper triangular.[^o25]

The upper and lower unitriangular matrices, $\mathbf{N}_{+}$ and $\mathbf{N}_{-}$, are closed unipotent subgroups of GL(*V*). Their Lie algebras consist of upper and lower strictly triangular matrices. The exponential mapping is a polynomial mapping from the Lie algebra to the corresponding subgroup by nilpotence. The inverse is given by the logarithm mapping which by unipotence is also a polynomial mapping. In particular there is a correspondence between closed connected subgroups of $\mathbf{N}_{\pm}$ and subalgebras of their Lie algebras. The exponential map is onto in each case, since the polynomial function $\log ( e^{A} e^{B} )$ lies in a given Lie subalgebra if $A$ and $B$ do and are sufficiently small.[^o26]

The Gauss decomposition can be extended to complexifications of other closed connected subgroups $G$ of $U(V)$ by using the root decomposition to write the complexified Lie algebra as[^o27]

$$\displaystyle{\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}} = \mathfrak{n}_- \oplus \mathfrak{t}_{\mathbf{C}} \oplus \mathfrak{n}_+,}$$

where $\text{𝖙}$ is the Lie algebra of a maximal torus $T$ of $G$ and $\text{𝖓}_{\pm}$ are the direct sum of the corresponding positive and negative root spaces. In the weight space decomposition of $V$ as eigenspaces of $T, \text{𝖙}$ acts as diagonally, $\text{𝖓}_{+}$ acts as lowering operators and $\text{𝖓}_{-}$ as raising operators. $\text{𝖓}_{\pm}$ are nilpotent Lie algebras acting as nilpotent operators; they are each other's adjoints on $V$. In particular $T$ acts by conjugation of $\text{𝖓}_{+}$, so that $\text{𝖙}_{\mathbf{C}} \oplus \text{𝖓}_{+}$ is a semidirect product of a nilpotent Lie algebra by an abelian Lie algebra.

By [Engel's theorem](wikipedia://en/1341657), if $\text{𝖆} \oplus \text{𝖓}$ is a semidirect product, with $\text{𝖆}$ abelian and $\text{𝖓}$ nilpotent, acting on a finite-dimensional vector space $W$ with operators in $\text{𝖆}$ diagonalizable and operators in $\text{𝖓}$ nilpotent, there is a vector $w$ that is an eigenvector for $\text{𝖆}$ and is annihilated by $\text{𝖓}$. In fact it is enough to show there is a vector annihilated by $\text{𝖓}$, which follows by induction on $\dim \text{𝖓}$, since the derived algebra $\text{𝖓}'$ annihilates a non-zero subspace of vectors on which $\text{𝖓} / \text{𝖓}'$ and $\text{𝖆}$ act with the same hypotheses.

Applying this argument repeatedly to $\text{𝖙}_{\mathbf{C}} \oplus \text{𝖓}_{+}$ shows that there is an orthonormal basis $e_{1}, ..., e_{n}$ of $V$ consisting of eigenvectors of $\text{𝖙}_{\mathbf{C}}$ with $\text{𝖓}_{+}$ acting as upper triangular matrices with zeros on the diagonal.

If $N_{\pm}$ and $T_{\mathbf{C}}$ are the complex Lie groups corresponding to $\text{𝖓}_{+}$ and $\text{𝖙}_{\mathbf{C}}$, then the Gauss decomposition states that the subset

$$\displaystyle{N_- T_{\mathbf{C}} N_+}$$

is a direct product and consists of the elements in $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ for which the principal minors are non-vanishing. It is open and dense. Moreover, if $\mathbf{T}$ denotes the maximal torus in $U(V)$,

$$\displaystyle{N_\pm=\mathbf{N}_\pm\cap G_{\mathbf{C}},\,\,\, T_{\mathbf{C}} = \mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{C}}\cap G_{\mathbf{C}}.}$$

These results are an immediate consequence of the corresponding results for $\operatorname{GL}(V)$.[^o28]

### Bruhat decomposition {#Bruhat_decomposition}

If $W = N_{G}(T) / T$ denotes the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) of $T$ and $B$ denotes the [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) $T_{\mathbf{C}} N_{+}$, the Gauss decomposition is also a consequence of the more precise **[Bruhat decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139142)**

$$\displaystyle{G_{\mathbf{C}} =\bigcup_{\sigma\in W} B\sigma B,}$$

decomposing $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ into a disjoint union of [double cosets](wikipedia://en/982771) of $B$. The complex dimension of a double coset $B\sigma B$ is determined by the length of $\sigma$ as an element of $W$. The dimension is maximized at the [Coxeter element](wikipedia://en/2148313) and gives the unique open dense double coset. Its inverse conjugates $B$ into the Borel subgroup of lower triangular matrices in $G_{\mathbf{C}}$.[^o30]

The Bruhat decomposition is easy to prove for $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbf{C})$.[^o31] Let $B$ be the Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices and $T_{\mathbf{C}}$ the subgroup of diagonal matrices. So $N(T_{\mathbf{C}}) / T_{\mathbf{C}} = S_{n}$. For $g$ in $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbf{C})$, take $b$ in $B$ so that $bg$ maximizes the number of zeros appearing at the beginning of its rows. Because a multiple of one row can be added to another, each row has a different number of zeros in it. Multiplying by a matrix $w$ in $N(T_{\mathbf{C}})$, it follows that $wbg$ lies in $B$. For uniqueness, if $w_{1}b w_{2} = b_{0}$, then the entries of $w_{1}w_{2}$ vanish below the diagonal. So the product lies in $T_{\mathbf{C}}$, proving uniqueness.

(Chevalley 1955) showed that the expression of an element $g$ as $g = b_{1}\sigma b_{2}$ becomes unique if $b_{1}$ is restricted to lie in the upper unitriangular subgroup $N_{\sigma} = N_{+} \cap \sigma N_{-} \sigma^{-1}$. In fact, if $M_{\sigma} = N_{+} \cap \sigma N_{+} \sigma^{-1}$, this follows from the identity

$$\displaystyle{N_+=N_\sigma\cdot M_\sigma.}$$

The group $N_{+}$ has a natural filtration by normal subgroups $N_{+}(k)$ with zeros in the first $k - 1$ superdiagonals and the successive quotients are Abelian. Defining $N_{\sigma}(k)$ and $M_{\sigma}(k)$ to be the intersections with $N_{+}(k)$, it follows by decreasing induction on $k$ that $N_{+}(k) = N_{\sigma}(k) \cdot M_{\sigma}(k)$. Indeed, $N_{\sigma}(k)N_{+}(k + 1)$ and $M_{\sigma}(k)N_{+}(k + 1)$ are specified in $N_{+}(k)$ by the vanishing of complementary entries $(i, j)$ on the $k$th superdiagonal according to whether $\sigma$ preserves the order $i < j$ or not.[^o33]

The Bruhat decomposition for the other classical simple groups can be deduced from the above decomposition using the fact that they are fixed point subgroups of folding automorphisms of $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbf{C})$.[^o34] For $\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbf{C})$, let $J$ be the $n \times n$ matrix with $1$'s on the antidiagonal and $0$'s elsewhere and set

$$\displaystyle{A=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & J\\ -J & 0\end{pmatrix}.}$$

Then $\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbf{C})$ is the fixed point subgroup of the involution $\theta(g) = A (g^{t})^{-1} A^{-1}\text{ of } \operatorname{SL}(2n,\mathbf{C})$. It leaves the subgroups $N_{\pm}, T_{\mathbf{C}}$ and $B$ invariant. If the basis elements are indexed by $n, n-1, ..., 1, -1, ..., -n$, then the Weyl group of $\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbf{C})$ consists of $\sigma$ satisfying $\sigma(j) = -j$, i.e. commuting with $\theta$. Analogues of $B, T_{\mathbf{C}}$ and $N_{\pm}$ are defined by intersection with $\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbf{C})$, i.e. as fixed points of $\theta$. The uniqueness of the decomposition $g = n\sigma b = \theta(n) \theta(\sigma) \theta(b)$ implies the Bruhat decomposition for $\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbf{C})$.

The same argument works for $\operatorname{SO}(n,\mathbf{C})$. It can be realised as the fixed points of $\psi(g) = B (g^{t})^{-1} B^{-1}$ in $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbf{C})$ where $B = J$.

### Iwasawa decomposition {#Iwasawa_decomposition}

The **[Iwasawa decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139148)**

$$\displaystyle{G_{\mathbf{C}} = G\cdot A \cdot N}$$

gives a decomposition for $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ for which, unlike the Cartan decomposition, the direct factor $A \cdot N$ is a closed subgroup, but it is no longer invariant under conjugation by $G$. It is the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of the [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) subgroup $N$ by the Abelian subgroup $A$.

For $U(V)$ and its complexification $\operatorname{GL}(V)$, this decomposition can be derived as a restatement of the [Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization process](wikipedia://en/82361).[^o36]

In fact let $e_{1}, ..., e_{n}$ be an orthonormal basis of $V$ and let $g$ be an element in $\operatorname{GL}(V)$. Applying the Gram–Schmidt process to $ge_{1}, ..., ge_{n}$, there is a unique orthonormal basis $f_{1}, ..., f_{n}$ and positive constants $a_{i}$ such that

$$\displaystyle{f_i= a_i ge_i + \sum_{j<i} n_{ji} ge_j.}$$

If $k$ is the unitary taking $(e_{i})$ to $(f_{i})$, it follows that $g^{-1}k$ lies in the subgroup $\mathbf{AN}$, where $\mathbf{A}$ is the subgroup of positive diagonal matrices with respect to $(e_{i})$ and $\mathbf{N}$ is the subgroup of upper [unitriangular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitriangular_matrix).[^o37]

Using the notation for the Gauss decomposition, the subgroups in the Iwasawa decomposition for $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ are defined by [^o38]

$$\displaystyle{A=\exp i\mathfrak{t} = \mathbf{A} \cap G_{\mathbf{C}}, \,\,\, N=\exp \mathfrak{n}_+=\mathbf{N} \cap G_{\mathbf{C}}.}$$

Since the decomposition is direct for $\operatorname{GL}(V)$, it is enough to check that $G_{\mathbf{C}} = GAN$. From the properties of the Iwasawa decomposition for $\operatorname{GL}(V)$, the map $G \times A \times N$ is a diffeomorphism onto its image in $G_{\mathbf{C}}$, which is closed. On the other hand, the dimension of the image is the same as the dimension of $G_{\mathbf{C}}$, so it is also open. So $G_{\mathbf{C}} = GAN$ because $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ is connected.[^o39]

(Zhelobenko 1973) gives a method for explicitly computing the elements in the decomposition.[^o41] For $g$ in $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ set $h = g*g$. This is a positive self-adjoint operator so its principal minors do not vanish. By the Gauss decomposition, it can therefore be written uniquely in the form $h = XDY$ with $X$ in $N_{-}$, $D$ in $T_{\mathbf{C}}$ and $Y$ in $N_{+}$. Since $h$ is self-adjoint, uniqueness forces $Y = X*$. Since it is also positive $D$ must lie in $A$ and have the form $D = \exp iT$ for some unique $T$ in $\text{𝖙}$. Let $a = \exp iT/2$ be its unique square root in $A$. Set $n = Y$ and $k = g n^{-1} a^{-1}$. Then $k$ is unitary, so is in $G$, and $g = kan$.

## Complex structures on homogeneous spaces {#Complex_structures_on_homogeneous_spaces}

The Iwasawa decomposition can be used to describe complex structures on the $G-\operatorname{orbit}$s in [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) of [highest weight vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_weight_vector) of finite-dimensional [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of $G$. In particular the identification between $G / T$ and $G_{\mathbf{C}} / B$ can be used to formulate the [Borel–Weil theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel–Weil_theorem). It states that each irreducible representation of $G$ can be obtained by [holomorphic induction](wikipedia://en/357452) from a character of $T$, or equivalently that it is realized in the space of [sections](wikipedia://en/367621) of a [holomorphic line bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_line_bundle) on $G / T$.

The closed connected subgroups of $G$ containing $T$ are described by [Borel–de Siebenthal theory](wikipedia://en/38613492). They are exactly the [centralizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) of tori $S \subseteq T$. Since every torus is generated topologically by a single element $x$, these are the same as centralizers $C_{G}(X)$ of elements $X$ in $\text{𝖙}$. By a result of Hopf $C_{G}(x)$ is always connected: indeed any element $y$ is along with $S$ contained in some maximal torus, necessarily contained in $C_{G}(x)$.

Given an irreducible finite-dimensional representation $V_{\lambda}$ with highest weight vector $v$ of weight $\lambda$, the stabilizer of $\mathbf{C} v$ in $G$ is a closed subgroup $H$. Since $v$ is an eigenvector of $T$, $H$ contains $T$. The complexification $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ also acts on $V$ and the stabilizer is a closed complex subgroup $P$ containing $T_{\mathbf{C}}$. Since $v$ is annihilated by every raising operator corresponding to a positive root $\alpha$, $P$ contains the Borel subgroup $B$. The vector $v$ is also a highest weight vector for the copy of $\mathbf{sl}_{2}$ corresponding to $\alpha$, so it is annihilated by the lowering operator generating $\text{𝖌}_{-\alpha}$ if $(\lambda, \alpha) = 0$. The Lie algebra $\mathbf{p}$ of $P$ is the direct sum of $\text{𝖙}_{\mathbf{C}}$ and root space vectors annihilating $v$, so that

$$\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}\oplus \bigoplus_{(\alpha,\lambda)=0} \mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}.}$$

The Lie algebra of $H = P \cap G$ is given by $\mathbf{p} \cap \text{𝖌}$. By the Iwasawa decomposition $G_{\mathbf{C}} = GAN$. Since $AN$ fixes $\mathbf{C} v$, the $G$-orbit of $v$ in the complex projective space of $V_{\lambda}$ coincides with the $G_{\mathbf{C}}$ orbit and

$$\displaystyle{G/H=G_{\mathbf{C}}/P.}$$

In particular

$$\displaystyle{G/T=G_{\mathbf{C}}/B.}$$

Using the identification of the Lie algebra of $T$ with its dual, $H$ equals the centralizer of $\lambda$ in $G$, and hence is connected. The group $P$ is also connected. In fact the space $G / H$ is simply connected, since it can be written as the quotient of the (compact) universal covering group of the compact semisimple group $G / Z$ by a connected subgroup, where $Z$ is the center of $G$.[^o44] If $P^{o}$ is the identity component of $P$, $G_{\mathbf{C}} / P$ has $G_{\mathbf{C}} / P^{o}$ as a covering space, so that $P = P^{o}$. The homogeneous space $G_{\mathbf{C}} / P$ has a complex structure, because $P$ is a complex subgroup. The orbit in complex projective space is closed in the Zariski topology by [Chow's theorem](wikipedia://en/630505#Chow.27s_theorem), so is a smooth projective variety. The Borel–Weil theorem and its generalizations are discussed in this context in (Serre 1954), (Helgason 1994), (Duistermaat Kolk 2000) and (Sepanski 2007).

The parabolic subgroup $P$ can also be written as a union of double cosets of $B$

$$\displaystyle{P=\bigcup_{\sigma\in W_\lambda} B\sigma B,}$$

where $W_{\lambda}$ is the stabilizer of $\lambda$ in the Weyl group $W$. It is generated by the reflections corresponding to the simple roots orthogonal to $\lambda$.[^o49]

:::

## Noncompact real forms {#Noncompact_real_forms}

There are other closed subgroups of the complexification of a compact connected Lie group *G* which have the same complexified Lie algebra. These are the other **real forms** of *G*<sub>**C**</sub>.[^o51]

### Involutions of simply connected compact Lie groups {#Involutions_of_simply_connected_compact_Lie_groups}

If *G* is a simply connected compact Lie group and σ is an automorphism of order 2, then the fixed point subgroup *K* = *G*<sup>σ</sup> is *automatically connected*. (In fact this is true for any automorphism of *G*, as shown for inner automorphisms by [Steinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Steinberg) and in general by [Borel](wikipedia://en/365672).) [^o53]

This can be seen most directly when the involution σ corresponds to a [Hermitian symmetric space](wikipedia://en/4127357). In that case σ is inner and implemented by an element in a one-parameter subgroup exp *tT* contained in the center of *G*<sup>σ</sup>. The innerness of σ implies that *K* contains a maximal torus of *G*, so has maximal rank. On the other hand, the centralizer of the subgroup generated by the torus *S* of elements exp *tT* is connected, since if *x* is any element in *K* there is a maximal torus containing *x* and *S*, which lies in the centralizer. On the other hand, it contains *K* since *S* is central in *K* and is contained in *K* since *z* lies in *S*. So *K* is the centralizer of *S* and hence connected. In particular *K* contains the center of *G*.[^o54]

For a general involution σ, the connectedness of *G*<sup>σ</sup> can be seen as follows.[^o55]

The starting point is the Abelian version of the result: if *T* is a maximal torus of a simply connected group *G* and σ is an involution leaving invariant *T* and a choice of positive roots (or equivalently a [Weyl chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_chamber)), then the fixed point subgroup *T*<sup>σ</sup> is connected. In fact the kernel of the exponential map from $\mathfrak{t}$ onto *T* is a lattice Λ with a **Z**-basis indexed by simple roots, which σ permutes. Splitting up according to orbits, *T* can be written as a product of terms **T** on which σ acts trivially or terms **T**<sup>2</sup> where σ interchanges the factors. The fixed point subgroup just corresponds to taking the diagonal subgroups in the second case, so is connected.

Now let *x* be any element fixed by σ, let *S* be a maximal torus in C<sub>*G*</sub>(*x*)<sup>σ</sup> and let *T* be the identity component of C<sub>*G*</sub>(*x*, *S*). Then *T* is a maximal torus in *G* containing *x* and *S*. It is invariant under σ and the identity component of *T*<sup>σ</sup> is *S*. In fact since *x* and *S* commute, they are contained in a maximal torus which, because it is connected, must lie in *T*. By construction *T* is invariant under σ. The identity component of *T*<sup>σ</sup> contains *S*, lies in C<sub>*G*</sub>(*x*)<sup>σ</sup> and centralizes *S*, so it equals *S*. But *S* is central in *T*, to *T* must be Abelian and hence a maximal torus. For σ acts as multiplication by −1 on the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{t}\ominus \mathfrak{s}$, so it and therefore also $\mathfrak{t}$ are Abelian.

The proof is completed by showing that σ preserves a Weyl chamber associated with *T*. For then *T*<sup>σ</sup> is connected so must equal *S*. Hence *x* lies in *S*. Since *x* was arbitrary, *G*<sup>σ</sup> must therefore be connected.

To produce a Weyl chamber invariant under σ, note that there is no root space $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha$ on which both *x* and *S* acted trivially, for this would contradict the fact that C<sub>*G*</sub>(*x*, *S*) has the same Lie algebra as *T*. Hence there must be an element *s* in *S* such that *t* = *xs* acts non-trivially on each root space. In this case *t* is a *regular element* of *T*—the identity component of its centralizer in *G* equals *T*. There is a unique [Weyl alcove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_Weyl_group) *A* in $\mathfrak{t}$ such that *t* lies in exp *A* and 0 lies in the closure of *A*. Since *t* is fixed by σ, the alcove is left invariant by σ and hence so also is the [Weyl chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_chamber) *C* containing it.

### Conjugations on the complexification {#Conjugations_on_the_complexification}

Let *G* be a simply connected compact Lie group with complexification *G*<sub>**C**</sub>. The map *c*(*g*) = (*g*\*)<sup>−1</sup> defines an automorphism of *G*<sub>**C**</sub> as a real Lie group with *G* as fixed point subgroup. It is conjugate-linear on $\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}$ and satisfies *c*<sup>2</sup> = id. Such automorphisms of either *G*<sub>**C**</sub> or $\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}$ are called **conjugations**. Since *G*<sub>**C**</sub> is also simply connected any conjugation *c*<sub>1</sub> on $\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}$ corresponds to a unique automorphism *c*<sub>1</sub> of *G*<sub>**C**</sub>.

The classification of conjugations *c*<sub>0</sub> reduces to that of involutions σ of *G* because given a *c*<sub>1</sub> there is an automorphism φ of the complex group *G*<sub>**C**</sub> such that

$$\displaystyle{c_0=\varphi\circ c_1\circ \varphi^{-1}}$$

commutes with *c*. The conjugation *c*<sub>0</sub> then leaves *G* invariant and restricts to an involutive automorphism σ. By simple connectivity the same is true at the level of Lie algebras. At the Lie algebra level *c*<sub>0</sub> can be recovered from σ by the formula

$$\displaystyle{c_0(X+iY)=\sigma(X)- i\sigma(Y)}$$

for *X*, *Y* in $\mathfrak{g}$.

To prove the existence of φ let ψ = *c*<sub>1</sub>*c* an automorphism of the complex group *G*<sub>**C**</sub>. On the Lie algebra level it defines a self-adjoint operator for the complex inner product

$$\displaystyle{(X,Y)=-B(X,c(Y)),}$$

where *B* is the [Killing form](wikipedia://en/982386) on $\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{C}}$. Thus ψ<sup>2</sup> is a positive operator and an automorphism along with all its real powers. In particular take

$$\displaystyle{\varphi=(\psi^2)^{1/4}}$$

It satisfies

$$\displaystyle{c_0c=\varphi c_1 \varphi^{-1} c=\varphi cc_1 \varphi=(\psi^2)^{1/2} \psi^{-1} =\varphi^{-1} cc_1 \varphi^{-1}=c \varphi c_1\varphi^{-1}=cc_0.}$$

### Cartan decomposition in a real form {#Cartan_decomposition_in_a_real_form}

For the complexification *G*<sub>**C**</sub>, the [Cartan decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139145) is described above. Derived from the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) in the complex [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), it gives a diffeomorphism

$$\displaystyle{G_{\mathbf{C}} = G\cdot \exp i\mathfrak{g} = G\cdot P = P\cdot G.}$$

On *G*<sub>**C**</sub> there is a conjugation operator *c* corresponding to *G* as well as an involution σ commuting with *c*. Let *c*<sub>0</sub> = *c* σ and let *G*<sub>0</sub> be the fixed point subgroup of *c*. It is closed in the matrix group *G*<sub>**C**</sub> and therefore a Lie group. The involution σ acts on both *G* and *G*<sub>0</sub>. For the Lie algebra of *G* there is a decomposition

$$\displaystyle{\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k} \oplus \mathfrak{p}}$$

into the +1 and −1 eigenspaces of σ. The fixed point subgroup *K* of σ in *G* is connected since *G* is simply connected. Its Lie algebra is the +1 eigenspace $\mathfrak{k}$. The Lie algebra of *G*<sub>0</sub> is given by

$$\displaystyle{\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k} \oplus \mathfrak{p}}$$

and the fixed point subgroup of σ is again *K*, so that *G* ∩ *G*<sub>0</sub> = *K*. In *G*<sub>0</sub>, there is a Cartan decomposition

$$\displaystyle{G_0=K\cdot \exp i\mathfrak{p} =K\cdot P_0 = P_0\cdot K}$$

which is again a diffeomorphism onto the direct and corresponds to the polar decomposition of matrices. It is the restriction of the decomposition on *G*<sub>**C**</sub>. The product gives a diffeomorphism onto a closed subset of *G*<sub>0</sub>. To check that it is surjective, for *g* in *G*<sub>0</sub> write *g* = *u* ⋅ *p* with *u* in *G* and *p* in *P*. Since *c*<sub>0</sub> *g* = *g*, uniqueness implies that σ*u* = *u* and σ*p* = *p*<sup>−1</sup>. Hence *u* lies in *K* and *p* in *P*<sub>0</sub>.

The Cartan decomposition in *G*<sub>0</sub> shows that *G*<sub>0</sub> is connected, simply connected and noncompact, because of the direct factor *P*<sub>0</sub>. Thus *G*<sub>0</sub> is a noncompact real semisimple Lie group.[^o58]

Moreover, given a maximal Abelian subalgebra $\mathfrak{a}$ in $\mathfrak{p}$, *A* = exp $\mathfrak{a}$ is a toral subgroup such that σ(*a*) = *a*<sup>−1</sup> on *A*; and any two such $\mathfrak{a}$'s are conjugate by an element of *K*. The properties of *A* can be shown directly. *A* is closed because the closure of *A* is a toral subgroup satisfying σ(*a*) = *a*<sup>−1</sup>, so its Lie algebra lies in $\mathfrak{m}$ and hence equals $\mathfrak{a}$ by maximality. *A* can be generated topologically by a single element exp *X*, so $\mathfrak{a}$ is the centralizer of *X* in $\mathfrak{m}$. In the *K*-orbit of any element of $\mathfrak{m}$ there is an element *Y* such that (X,Ad *k* Y) is minimized at *k* = 1. Setting *k* = exp *tT* with *T* in $\mathfrak{k}$, it follows that (*X*,\[*T*,*Y*\]) = 0 and hence \[*X*,*Y*\] = 0, so that *Y* must lie in $\mathfrak{a}$. Thus $\mathfrak{m}$ is the union of the conjugates of $\mathfrak{a}$. In particular some conjugate of *X* lies in any other choice of $\mathfrak{a}$, which centralizes that conjugate; so by maximality the only possibilities are conjugates of $\mathfrak{a}$.[^o59]

A similar statements hold for the action of *K* on $\mathfrak{a}_0=i\mathfrak{a}$ in $\mathfrak{p}_0$. Moreover, from the Cartan decomposition for *G*<sub>0</sub>, if *A*<sub>0</sub> = exp $\mathfrak{a}_0$, then

$$\displaystyle{G_0=KA_0K.}$$
