# Support (measure theory)

Support (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Support"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **support** (sometimes **topological support** or **spectrum**) of a [measure](wikipedia://en/45270) $\mu$ on a [measurable](wikipedia://en/43333) [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $(X, \operatorname{Borel}(X))$ is a precise notion of where in the space $X$ the measure "lives". It is defined to be the largest ([closed](wikipedia://en/47279)) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $X$ for which every [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of every point of the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) has positive measure.

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

A (non-negative) measure $\mu$ on a measurable space $(X, \Sigma)$ is really a function $\mu : \Sigma \to [0, +\infty].$ Therefore, in terms of the usual [definition](wikipedia://en/7964) of [support](wikipedia://en/381013), the support of $\mu$ is a subset of the [σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) $\Sigma:$

$$\operatorname{supp} (\mu) := \overline{\{A \in \Sigma \,\vert\, \mu(A) \neq 0\}},$$

where the overbar denotes [set closure](wikipedia://en/42309). However, this definition is somewhat unsatisfactory: we use the notion of closure, but we do not even have a topology on $\Sigma.$ What we really want to know is where in the space $X$ the measure $\mu$ is non-zero. Consider two examples:

1. [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) $\lambda$ on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}.$ It seems clear that $\lambda$ "lives on" the whole of the real line.
1. A [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) $\delta_p$ at some point $p \in \mathbb{R}.$ Again, intuition suggests that the measure $\delta_p$ "lives at" the point $p,$ and nowhere else.

In light of these two examples, we can reject the following candidate definitions in favour of the one in the next section:

1. We could remove the points where $\mu$ is zero, and take the support to be the remainder $X \setminus \{x \in X \mid \mu(\{x\}) = 0\}.$ This might work for the Dirac measure $\delta_p,$ but it would definitely not work for $\lambda:$ since the Lebesgue measure of any singleton is zero, this definition would give $\lambda$ empty support.
1. By comparison with the notion of [strict positivity](wikipedia://en/6607051) of measures, we could take the support to be the set of all points with a neighbourhood of positive measure: $$\{x \in X \mid \exists N_x \text{ open} \text{ such that } (x \in N_x \text{ and } \mu(N_x) > 0)\}$$ (or the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of this). It is also too simplistic: by taking $N_x = X$ for all points $x \in X,$ this would make the support of every measure except the zero measure the whole of $X.$

However, the idea of "local strict positivity" is not too far from a workable definition.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $(X, T)$ be a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450); let $B(T)$ denote the [Borel σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_algebra) on $X,$ i.e. the smallest sigma algebra on $X$ that contains all open sets $U \in T.$ Let $\mu$ be a measure on $(X, B(T))$. Then the **support** (or **spectrum**) of $\mu$ is defined as the set of all points $x$ in $X$ for which every [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $N_x$ of $x$ has [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) measure:

$$\operatorname{supp} (\mu) := \{x \in X \mid \forall N_x \in T \colon (x \in N_x \Rightarrow \mu (N_x) > 0)\}.$$

Some authors prefer to take the closure of the above set. However, this is not necessary: see "Properties" below.

An equivalent definition of support is as the largest $C \in B(T)$ (with respect to inclusion) such that every open set which has non-empty intersection with $C$ has positive measure, i.e. the largest $C$ such that:

$$(\forall U \in T)(U \cap C \neq \varnothing \implies \mu (U \cap C) > 0).$$

### Signed and complex measures {#Signed_and_complex_measures}

This definition can be extended to signed and complex measures. Suppose that $\mu : \Sigma \to [-\infty, +\infty]$ is a [signed measure](wikipedia://en/2985811). Use the [Hahn decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/2985858) to write

$$\mu = \mu^+ - \mu^-,$$

where $\mu^\pm$ are both non-negative measures. Then the **support** of $\mu$ is defined to be

$$\operatorname{supp} (\mu) := \operatorname{supp} (\mu^+) \cup \operatorname{supp} (\mu^-).$$

Similarly, if $\mu : \Sigma \to \mathbb{C}$ is a [complex measure](wikipedia://en/3057532), the **support** of $\mu$ is defined to be the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the supports of its real and imaginary parts.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

$\operatorname{supp} (\mu_1 + \mu_2) = \operatorname{supp} (\mu_1) \cup \operatorname{supp} (\mu_2)$ holds.

A measure $\mu$ on $X$ is [strictly positive](wikipedia://en/6607051) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it has support $\operatorname{supp}(\mu) = X.$ If $\mu$ is strictly positive and $x \in X$ is arbitrary, then any open neighbourhood of $x,$ since it is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358), has positive measure; hence, $x \in \operatorname{supp}(\mu),$ so $\operatorname{supp}(\mu) = X.$ Conversely, if $\operatorname{supp}(\mu) = X,$ then every non-empty open set (being an open neighbourhood of some point in its interior, which is also a point of the support) has positive measure; hence, $\mu$ is strictly positive. The support of a measure is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) in $X,$ as its complement is the union of the open sets of measure $0.$

In general the support of a nonzero measure may be empty: see the examples below. However, if $X$ is a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) topological space and $\mu$ is a [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933), a Borel set $A$ outside the support has [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero):

$$A \subseteq X \setminus \operatorname{supp} (\mu) \implies \mu (A) = 0.$$

The converse is true if $A$ is open, but it is not true in general: it fails if there exists a point $x \in \operatorname{supp}(\mu)$ such that $\mu(\{x\}) = 0$ (e.g. Lebesgue measure). Thus, one does not need to "integrate outside the support": for any [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C},$

$$\int_X f(x) \, \mathrm{d} \mu (x) = \int_{\operatorname{supp} (\mu)} f(x) \, \mathrm{d} \mu (x).$$

The concept of *support* of a measure and that of [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum) of a [self-adjoint linear operator](wikipedia://en/187408) on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) are closely related. Indeed, if $\mu$ is a [regular Borel measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_Borel_measure) on the line $\mathbb{R},$ then the multiplication operator $(Af)(x) = xf(x)$ is self-adjoint on its natural domain

$$D(A) = \{f \in L^2(\mathbb{R}, d\mu) \mid xf(x) \in L^2(\mathbb{R}, d\mu)\}$$

and its spectrum coincides with the [essential range](wikipedia://en/18380182) of the identity function $x \mapsto x,$ which is precisely the support of $\mu.$[^o8]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Lebesgue measure {#Lebesgue_measure}

In the case of Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ on the real line $\mathbb{R},$ consider an arbitrary point $x \in \mathbb{R}.$ Then any open neighbourhood $N_x$ of $x$ must contain some open [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $(x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon)$ for some $\epsilon > 0.$ This interval has Lebesgue measure $2 \epsilon > 0,$ so $\lambda(N_x) \geq 2 \epsilon > 0.$ Since $x \in \mathbb{R}$ was arbitrary, $\operatorname{supp}(\lambda) = \mathbb{R}.$

### Dirac measure {#Dirac_measure}

In the case of [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) $\delta_p,$ let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and consider two cases:

1. if $x = p,$ then every open neighbourhood $N_x$ of $x$ contains $p,$ so $\delta_p(N_x) = 1 > 0.$
1. on the other hand, if $x \neq p,$ then there exists a sufficiently small open ball $B$ around $x$ that does not contain $p,$ so $\delta_p(B) = 0.$

We conclude that $\operatorname{supp}(\delta_p)$ is the closure of the [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) set $\{p\},$ which is $\{p\}$ itself.

In fact, a measure $\mu$ on the real line is a Dirac measure $\delta_p$ for some point $p$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the support of $\mu$ is the singleton set $\{p\}.$ Consequently, Dirac measure on the real line is the unique measure with zero [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) (provided that the measure has variance at all).

### A uniform distribution {#A_uniform_distribution}

Consider the measure $\mu$ on the real line $\mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$\mu(A) := \lambda(A \cap (0, 1))$$

i.e. a [uniform measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) on the open interval $(0, 1).$ A similar argument to the Dirac measure example shows that $\operatorname{supp}(\mu) = [0, 1].$ Note that the boundary points 0 and 1 lie in the support: any open set containing 0 (or 1) contains an open interval about 0 (or 1), which must intersect $(0, 1),$ and so must have positive $\mu$-measure.

### A nontrivial measure whose support is empty {#A_nontrivial_measure_whose_support_is_empty}

The space of all [countable ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_ordinal) with the topology generated by "open intervals" is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637). The measure ("Dieudonné measure") that assigns measure 1 to Borel sets containing an unbounded closed subset and assigns 0 to other Borel sets is a Borel probability measure whose support is empty. [^o15]

### A nontrivial measure whose support has measure zero {#A_nontrivial_measure_whose_support_has_measure_zero}

On a compact Hausdorff space the support of a non-zero measure is always non-empty, but may have measure $0.$ An example of this is given by adding the first uncountable ordinal $\Omega$ to the previous example: the support of the measure is the single point $\Omega,$ which has measure $0.$

:::
