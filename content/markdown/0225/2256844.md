# Carathéodory's theorem (conformal mapping)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carathéodory's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Carathéodory's theorem** is a theorem in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), named after [Constantin Carathéodory](wikipedia://en/470625), which extends the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215). The theorem, published by Carathéodory in 1913, states that any [conformal mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_mapping) sending the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) to some region in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) bounded by a [Jordan curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve) extends continuously to a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) from the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) onto the Jordan curve. The result is one of Carathéodory's results on [prime ends](wikipedia://en/23404066) and the boundary behaviour of univalent holomorphic functions.

:::

## Proofs of Carathéodory's theorem {#Proofs_of_Carathéodory's_theorem}

The first proof of Carathéodory's theorem presented here is a summary of the short self-contained account in (Garnett Marshall 2005, 14–15); there are related proofs in (Pommerenke 1992) and (Krantz 2006).

Clearly if *f* admits an extension to a homeomorphism, then *∂U* must be a Jordan curve.

Conversely if *∂U* is a Jordan curve, the first step is to prove *f* extends continuously to the closure of *D*. In fact this will hold [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *f* is uniformly continuous on *D*: for this is true if it has a continuous extension to the closure of *D*; and, if *f* is uniformly continuous, it is easy to check *f* has limits on the unit circle and the same inequalities for [uniform continuity](wikipedia://en/32337) hold on the closure of *D*.

Suppose that *f* is not uniformly continuous. In this case there must be an *ε* > 0 and a point *ζ* on the unit circle and sequences *z*<sub>*n*</sub>, *w*<sub>*n*</sub> tending to *ζ* with |*f*(*z*<sub>*n*</sub>) − *f*(*w*<sub>*n*</sub>)| ≥ 2*ε*. This is shown below to lead to a contradiction, so that *f* must be uniformly continuous and hence has a continuous extension to the closure of *D*.

For 0 \< *r* \< 1, let *γ*<sub>*r*</sub> be the curve given by the arc of the circle $z - \zeta = r$ lying within *D*. Then *f* $\circ$ *γ*<sub>*r*</sub> is a Jordan curve. Its length can be estimated using the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128):

$$\displaystyle{\ell(f\circ \gamma_r) = \int_{\gamma_r} |f^\prime(z)|\, |dz|\le \left(\int_{\gamma_r}\,|dz|\right)^{1/2}\cdot \left(\int_{\gamma_r}|f^\prime(z)|^2\,|dz|\right)^{1/2}\le (2\pi r)^{1/2} \cdot\left(\int_{\{\theta:|\zeta +re^{i\theta}|<1\}} |f^\prime(\zeta + re^{i\theta})|^2\,r\,d\theta\right)^{1/2}.}$$

Hence there is a "length-area estimate":

$$\displaystyle{\int_0^{1} \ell(f\circ \gamma_r)^2 \, {dr\over r} \le 2\pi \int_{|z|<1} |f^\prime(z)|^2 \,dx\, dy = 2\pi \cdot {\rm Area}\,f(D)<\infty.}$$

The finiteness of the integral on the left hand side implies that there is a sequence *r*<sub>*n*</sub> decreasing to 0 with $\ell(f\circ \gamma_{r_n})$ tending to 0. But the [length of a curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_of_a_curve) *g*(*t*) for *t* in (*a*, *b*) is given by

$$\displaystyle{\ell(g) =\sup_{a< t_1< t_2 < \cdots <t_k<b}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} |g(t_{i+1})-g(t_i)|.}$$

The finiteness of $\ell(f\circ \gamma_{r_n})$ therefore implies that the curve has limiting points *a*<sub>*n*</sub>, *b*<sub>*n*</sub> at its two ends with $|a_n - b_n| \le \ell(f\circ \gamma_{r_n})$, so this distance, as well that of the curve, tends to 0. These two limit points must lie on *∂U*, because *f* is a homeomorphism between *D* and *U* and thus a sequence converging in *U* has to be the image under *f* of a sequence converging in *D*. By assumption there exist a homeomorphism *β* between the circle *∂D* and *∂U*. Since *β*<sup>−1</sup> is uniformly continuous, the distance between the two points *ξ*<sub>*n*</sub> and *η*<sub>*n*</sub> corresponding to *a*<sub>*n*</sub> and *b*<sub>*n*</sub> in *∂U* must tend to 0. So eventually the smallest circular arc in *∂D* joining *ξ*<sub>*n*</sub> and *η*<sub>*n*</sub> is defined. Denote *τ*<sub>*n*</sub> image of this arc under *β*. By uniform continuity of *β*, the diameter of *τ*<sub>*n*</sub> in *∂U* tends to 0. Together *τ*<sub>*n*</sub> and *f* $\circ$ *γ*<sub>*r*<sub>*n*</sub></sub> form a simple Jordan curve. Its interior *U*<sub>*n*</sub> is contained in *U* by the [Jordan curve theorem](wikipedia://en/267444) for *∂U* and *∂U*<sub>*n*</sub>: to see this, notice that *U* is the interior of *∂U*, as it is bounded, connected and it is both open and closed in the complement of *∂U*; so the exterior region of *∂U* is unbounded, connected and does not intersect *∂U*<sub>*n*</sub>, hence its closure is contained in the closure of the exterior of *∂U*<sub>*n*</sub>; taking complements, we get the desired inclusion. The diameter of *∂U*<sub>*n*</sub> tends to 0 because the diameters of τ<sub>*n*</sub> and *f* $\circ$ *γ*<sub>*r*<sub>*n*</sub></sub> tend to 0. Hence the diameter of *U*<sub>*n*</sub> tend to 0. (For $\bar U_n \times \bar U_n$ is compact set, hence $\bar U_n$ contains two points *u* and *v* such that distance between them is maximal. It is easy to see that *u* and *v* must lie in *∂U* and the diameters of both *U* and *∂U* equal $|u-v|$.)

Now if *V*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes the intersection of *D* with the disk |*z* − ζ| \< *r*<sub>*n*</sub>, then for all sufficiently large *n* *f*(*V*<sub>*n*</sub>) = *U*<sub>*n*</sub>. Indeed, the arc *γ*<sub>*r*<sub>*n*</sub></sub> divides *D* into *V*<sub>*n*</sub> and complementary region $V_n'$, so under the conformal homeomorphism *f* the curve *f* $\circ$ *γ*<sub>*r*<sub>*n*</sub></sub> divides *U* into $f(V_n)$ and a complementary region $f(V_n')$; *U*<sub>*n*</sub> is a connected component of *U* \\ *f* $\circ$ *γ*<sub>*r*<sub>*n*</sub></sub>, as it is connected and is both open and closed in this set, hence $U_n$ equals either $f(V_n)$ or $f(V_n')$. The diameter of $f(V_n')$ does not decrease with increasing *n*, for $n<n'$ implies $V_n' \subset V_{n'}'$. Since the diameter of *U*<sub>*n*</sub> tends to 0 as *n* goes to infinity, it is eventually less than that of $f(V_n')$ and then necessarily *f*(*V*<sub>*n*</sub>) = *U*<sub>*n*</sub>.

So the diameter of *f*(*V*<sub>*n*</sub>) tends to 0. On the other hand, passing to subsequences of (*z*<sub>*n*</sub>) and (*w*<sub>*n*</sub>) if necessary, it may be assumed that *z*<sub>*n*</sub> and *w*<sub>*n*</sub> both lie in *V*<sub>*n*</sub>. But this gives a contradiction since |*f*(*z*<sub>*n*</sub>) − *f*(*w*<sub>*n*</sub>)| ≥ *ε*. So *f* must be uniformly continuous on *U*.

Thus *f* extends continuously to the closure of *D*. Since *f*(*D*) = *U*, by compactness *f* carries the closure of *D* onto the closure of *U* and hence *∂D* onto *∂U*. If *f* is not one-one, there are points *u*, *v* on *∂D* with *u* ≠ *v* and *f*(*u*) = *f*(*v*). Let *X* and *Y* be the radial lines from 0 to *u* and *v*. Then $f(X \cup Y)$ is a Jordan curve. Arguing as before, its interior *V* is contained in *U* and is a connected component of $U \backslash f(X \cup Y)$. On the other hand, $D \backslash (X \cup Y)$ is the disjoint union of two open sectors *W*<sub>1</sub> and *W*<sub>2</sub>. Hence, for one of them, *W*<sub>1</sub> say, *f*(*W*<sub>1</sub>) = *V*. Let *Z* be the portion of *∂W*<sub>1</sub> on the unit circle, so that *Z* is a closed arc and *f*(*Z*) is a subset of both *∂U* and the closure of *V*. But their intersection is a single point and hence *f* is constant on *Z*. By the Schwarz reflection principle, *f* can be analytically continued by conformal reflection across the circular arc. Since non-constant holomorphic functions have isolated zeros, this forces *f* to be constant, a contradiction. So *f* is one-one and hence a homeomorphism on the closure of *D*.[^o7][^o8]

Two different proofs of Carathéodory's theorem are described in (Carathéodory 1954) and (Carathéodory 1998). The first proof follows Carathéodory's original method of proof from 1913 using properties of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on the circle: the continuous extension of the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) *g* of *f* to *∂U* is justified by [Fatou's theorem](wikipedia://en/10738834) on the boundary behaviour of bounded harmonic functions on the unit disk. The second proof is based on the method of (Lindelöf 1914), where a sharpening of the maximum modulus inequality was established for bounded holomorphic functions *h* defined on a bounded domain *V*: if *a* lies in *V*, then

\|*h*(*a*)| ≤ *m*<sup>*t*</sup> ⋅ *M*<sup>1 − *t*</sup>,

where 0 ≤ *t* ≤ 1, *M* is maximum modulus of *h* for sequential limits on *∂U* and *m* is the maximum modulus of *h* for sequential limits on *∂U* lying in a sector centred on *a* subtending an angle 2π*t* at *a*.[^o12]

## Continuous extension and the Carathéodory-Torhorst theorem {#Continuous_extension_and_the_Carathéodory-Torhorst_theorem}

An extension of the theorem states that a conformal isomorphism

$$g\colon \mathbb{D} \to U,$$

where $U$ is a simply connected subset of the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), extends continuously to the unit circle if and only if the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of $U$ is [locally connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_connected).

This result is often also attributed to Carathéodory, but was first stated and proved by [Marie Torhorst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Torhorst) in her 1918 thesis,[^o15] under the supervision of [Hans Hahn](wikipedia://en/1075212), using Carathéodory's theory of [prime ends](wikipedia://en/23404066). More precisely, Torhorst proved that local connectivity is equivalent to the domain having only prime ends of the first kind. By the theory of prime ends, the latter property, in turn, is equivalent to $g$ having a continuous extension.
