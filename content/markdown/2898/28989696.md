# James Clerk Maxwell

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="James Clerk Maxwell"}

**James Clerk Maxwell** (13 June 1831 – 5 November 1879) was a Scottish physicist and mathematician[^o4] who was responsible for the [classical theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_theory) of [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426), which was the first theory to describe electricity, [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism) and light as different manifestations of the same phenomenon. [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) for electromagnetism achieved the [second great unification in physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unification_%28physics%29#Unification_of_magnetism,_electricity,_light_and_related_radiation),[^o5] where [the first one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unification_of_theories_in_physics#Unification_of_gravity_on_Earth_with_astronomical_behaviors) had been realised by [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627). Maxwell was also key in the creation of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481).

:::

Maxwell graduated from [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), in 1854, where he earned distinction in mathematics and the [Smith's Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith's_Prize). He remained at Cambridge briefly, publishing early mathematical work and investigations into [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), particularly the principles of colour combination and colour-blindness. He later held the Chair of Natural Philosophy at [Marischal College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marischal_College) in [Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen), where he studied the [rings of Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn) and correctly proposed that they were composed of numerous small particles,[^o6] work that earned him the [Adams Prize](wikipedia://en/1098110) in 1859. During this time he married [Katherine Mary Dewar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Clerk_Maxwell), who assisted him in his laboratory work. From 1860 to 1865, he served as the Professor of Natural Philosophy at [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London), where he developed his theory of electromagnetic fields. His publication of "*[A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Dynamical_Theory_of_the_Electromagnetic_Field)*" in 1865 demonstrated that [electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_force) and [magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/36563) travel through space as [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) moving at the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), proposing that light is an undulation in the same medium that is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena.[^o7] His unification of light and electrical phenomena led to his prediction of the existence of [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave).

Maxwell was the first to derive the [Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/19830), a statistical means of describing aspects of the [kinetic theory of gases](wikipedia://en/64204), which he worked on sporadically throughout his career.[^o8] He presented the first durable [colour photograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_photograph) in 1861, and showed that any colour can be produced with a mixture of any three primary colours, those being red, green, and blue, the basis for [colour television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_television).[^o11] He worked on analysing the rigidity of rod-and-joint frameworks ([trusses](wikipedia://en/397263)) like those in many bridges. He devised modern [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267) and helped to establish the [CGS system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre–gram–second_system_of_units) of measurement. He was the first to recognize [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295), and the first to emphasize the [butterfly effect](wikipedia://en/4024). His 1863 paper *On Governors* serves as an important foundation for [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) and [cybernetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics), and was also the earliest mathematical analysis on [control systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_system).[^o9][^n1] In 1867, he proposed the thought experiment known as [Maxwell's demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_demon), which challenges how information affects entropy in thermodynamics. In his seminal 1867 paper *On the Dynamical Theory of Gases* he introduced the [Maxwell model](wikipedia://en/480282) for describing the behavior of a [viscoelastic](wikipedia://en/711288) material and originated the [Maxwell-Cattaneo equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_heat_conduction#Hyperbolic_model_%28relativistic%29) for describing the transport of heat in a medium.

In 1871, Maxwell returned to Cambridge as the first [Cavendish Professor of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Professor_of_Physics), overseeing the construction of the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory). As a result of his work he is regarded as a founder of the modern field of [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering).[^o11] His discoveries helped usher in the era of modern physics, laying the foundations for such fields as [relativity](wikipedia://en/30001), also being the one to introduce the term into physics,[^o12] and [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202).[^n2][^o14]

## Life {#Life}

### Early life, 1831–1839 {#Early_life,_1831–1839}

*Image: James Clerk Maxwell's birthplace at 14 India Street.jpg.* Clerk Maxwell's birthplace at 14 India Street in Edinburgh is now the home of the [James Clerk Maxwell Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell_Foundation).

James Clerk Maxwell was born on 13 June 1831[^o17] at 14 India Street, [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh), to [John Clerk Maxwell of Middlebie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Clerk_Maxwell_of_Middlebie), an advocate, and Frances Cay,[^o18][^o19] daughter of [Robert Hodshon Cay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hodshon_Cay) and sister of [John Cay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cay). (His birthplace now houses a museum operated by the [James Clerk Maxwell Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell_Foundation).) His father was a man of comfortable means[^o20] of the Clerk family of [Penicuik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicuik), holders of the [baronetcy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baronetcy) of [Clerk of Penicuik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerk_baronets). His father's brother was the [6th baronet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_George_Clerk,_6th_Baronet).[^o21] He had been born "John Clerk", adding "Maxwell" to his own after he inherited (as an infant in 1793) the Middlebie estate, a Maxwell property in [Dumfriesshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dumfriesshire).[^o18] James was a first cousin of both the artist [Jemima Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jemima_Blackburn)[^o22] (the daughter of his father's sister) and the civil engineer [William Dyce Cay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Dyce_Cay) (the son of his mother's brother). Cay and Maxwell were close friends and Cay acted as his best man when Maxwell married.[^o23]

Maxwell's parents met and married when they were well into their thirties;[^o24] his mother was nearly 40 when he was born. They had had one earlier child, a daughter named Elizabeth, who died in infancy.[^o25]

When Maxwell was young his family moved to [Glenlair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenlair_House), in Kirkcudbrightshire, which his parents had built on the estate which comprised 1500 acre.[^o26] All indications suggest that Maxwell had maintained an unquenchable curiosity from an early age.[^o27] By the age of three, everything that moved, shone, or made a noise drew the question: "what's the go o' that?".[^o28] In a passage added to a letter from his father to his sister-in-law Jane Cay in 1834, his mother described this innate sense of inquisitiveness: He is a very happy man, and has improved much since the weather got moderate; he has great work with doors, locks, keys, etc., and "show me how it doos" is never out of his mouth. He also investigates the hidden course of streams and bell-wires, the way the water gets from the pond through the wall....[^o29]

### Education, 1839–1847 {#Education,_1839–1847}

Recognising the boy's potential, Maxwell's mother Frances took responsibility for his early education, which in the [Victorian era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_times) was largely the job of the woman of the house.[^o31] At eight he could recite long passages of [John Milton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Milton) and the whole of the [119th psalm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm_119) (176 verses). Indeed, his knowledge of [scripture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_text) was already detailed; he could give chapter and verse for almost any quotation from the Psalms. His mother was taken ill with [abdominal cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdominal_cancer) and, after an unsuccessful operation, died in December 1839 when he was eight years old. His education was then overseen by his father and his father's sister-in-law Jane, both of whom played pivotal roles in his life.[^o31] His formal schooling began unsuccessfully under the guidance of a 16-year-old hired tutor. Little is known about the young man hired to instruct Maxwell, except that he treated the younger boy harshly, chiding him for being slow and wayward.[^o31] The tutor was dismissed in November 1841. James' father took him to [Robert Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Davidson_%28inventor%29)'s demonstration of electric propulsion and magnetic force on 12 February 1842, an experience with profound implications for the boy.[^o32]

In 1841, at age ten, Maxwell was sent to the prestigious [Edinburgh Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_Academy).[^o33] He lodged during term times at the house of his aunt Isabella. During this time his passion for drawing was encouraged by his older cousin Jemima.[^o34] The young Maxwell, having been raised in isolation on his father's countryside estate, did not fit in well at school.[^o35] The first year had been full, obliging him to join the second year with classmates a year his senior.[^o35] His mannerisms and [Galloway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galloway) accent struck the other boys as rustic. Having arrived on his first day of school wearing a pair of homemade shoes and a tunic, he earned the unkind nickname of "Daftie".[^o36] He never seemed to resent the epithet, bearing it without complaint for many years.[^o37] Social isolation at the Academy ended when he met [Lewis Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Campbell_%28classicist%29) and [Peter Guthrie Tait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Guthrie_Tait), two boys of a similar age who were to become notable scholars later in life. They remained lifelong friends.[^o18]

Maxwell was fascinated by [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) at an early age, rediscovering the [regular polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polyhedra) before he received any formal instruction.[^o34] Despite his winning the school's scripture biography prize in his second year, his academic work remained unnoticed[^o34] until, at the age of 13, he won the school's mathematical medal and first prize for both English and poetry.[^o38]

Maxwell's interests ranged far beyond the school syllabus and he did not pay particular attention to examination performance.[^o38] He wrote his first [scientific paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_paper) at the age of 14. In it, he described a mechanical means of drawing [mathematical curves](wikipedia://en/89246) with a piece of twine, and the properties of [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277), [Cartesian ovals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_ovals), and related curves with more than two [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607). The work,[^o18][^o40] of 1846, "On the description of oval curves and those having a plurality of foci"[^o39] was presented to the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh) by [James Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_David_Forbes), a professor of [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy) at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh),[^o18][^o40] because Maxwell was deemed too young to present the work himself.[^o41] The work was not entirely original, since [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) had also examined the properties of such [multifocal ellipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multifocal_ellipse) in the 17th century, but Maxwell had simplified their construction.[^o41]

### University of Edinburgh, 1847–1850 {#University_of_Edinburgh,_1847–1850}

*Image: Edinburgh University 1827.jpg.* Old College, University of Edinburgh

Maxwell left the Academy in 1847 at age 16 and began attending classes at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh).[^o43] He had the opportunity to attend the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), but decided, after his first term, to complete the full course of his undergraduate studies at Edinburgh. The academic staff of the university included some highly regarded names; his first-year tutors included [Sir William Hamilton](wikipedia://en/206346), who lectured him on [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), [Philip Kelland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Kelland) on mathematics, and [James Forbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_David_Forbes) on [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy).[^o18] He did not find his classes demanding,[^o44] and was, therefore, able to immerse himself in private study during free time at the university and particularly when back home at Glenlair.[^o45] There he would experiment with improvised chemical, electric, and magnetic apparatus; however, his chief concerns regarded the properties of [polarised light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarized_light).[^o46] He constructed shaped blocks of [gelatine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelatine), subjected them to various [stresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28physics%29), and with a pair of [polarising prisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicol_prism) given to him by [William Nicol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Nicol_%28inventor%29), viewed the coloured fringes that had developed within the jelly.[^o47] Through this practice he discovered [photoelasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelasticity), which is a means of determining the stress distribution within physical structures.[^o48]

At age 18, Maxwell contributed two papers for the [Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh). One of these, "On the Equilibrium of Elastic Solids", laid the foundation for an important discovery later in his life, which was the temporary [double refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_refraction) produced in [viscous](wikipedia://en/18963754) liquids by [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress).[^o49] His other paper was "Rolling Curves" and, just as with the paper "Oval Curves" that he had written at the Edinburgh Academy, he was again considered too young to stand at the rostrum to present it himself. The paper was delivered to the Royal Society by his tutor Kelland instead.[^o50]

### University of Cambridge, 1850–1856 {#University_of_Cambridge,_1850–1856}

*Image: YoungJamesClerkMaxwell.jpg.* A young Maxwell at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), holding one of his [colour wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_wheel)

In October 1850, already an accomplished mathematician, Maxwell left Scotland for the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge). He initially attended [Peterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterhouse,_Cambridge), but before the end of his first term transferred to [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), where he believed it would be easier to obtain a [fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow).[^o52] At Trinity he was elected to the elite secret society known as the [Cambridge Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Apostles).[^o53] Maxwell's intellectual understanding of his Christian faith and of science grew rapidly during his Cambridge years. He joined the "Apostles", an exclusive debating society of the intellectual elite, where through his essays he sought to work out this understanding.

Now my great plan, which was conceived of old, ... is to let nothing be wilfully left unexamined. Nothing is to be holy ground consecrated to Stationary Faith, whether positive or negative. All fallow land is to be ploughed up and a regular system of rotation followed. ... Never hide anything, be it weed or no, nor seem to wish it hidden. ... Again I assert the Right of Trespass on any plot of Holy Ground which any man has set apart. ... Now I am convinced that no one but a Christian can actually purge his land of these holy spots. ... I do not say that no Christians have enclosed places of this sort. Many have a great deal, and every one has some. But there are extensive and important tracts in the territory of the Scoffer, the Pantheist, the Quietist, Formalist, Dogmatist, Sensualist, and the rest, which are openly and solemnly Tabooed. ..."

Christianity—that is, the religion of the Bible—is the only scheme or form of belief which disavows any possessions on such a tenure. Here alone all is free. You may fly to the ends of the world and find no God but the Author of Salvation. You may search the Scriptures and not find a text to stop you in your explorations. ...

The Old Testament and the Mosaic Law and Judaism are commonly supposed to be "Tabooed" by the orthodox. Sceptics pretend to have read them and have found certain witty objections ... which too many of the orthodox unread admit, and shut up the subject as haunted. But a Candle is coming to drive out all Ghosts and Bugbears. Let us follow the light.[^o54]

In the summer of his third year, Maxwell spent some time at the [Suffolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otley,_Suffolk) home of the Rev. [C. B. Tayler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Benjamin_Tayler), the uncle of a classmate, G. W. H. Tayler. The love of God shown by the family impressed Maxwell, particularly after he was nursed back from ill health by the minister and his wife.[^o55]

On his return to Cambridge, Maxwell writes to his recent host a chatty and affectionate letter including the following testimony,[^o54]

... I have the capacity of being more wicked than any example that man could set me, and ... if I escape, it is only by God's grace helping me to get rid of myself, partially in science, more completely in society, —but not perfectly except by committing myself to God ...

In November 1851, Maxwell studied under [William Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hopkins), whose success in nurturing mathematical genius had earned him the nickname of "[senior wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_Wrangler_%28University_of_Cambridge%29)-maker".[^o56]

In 1854, Maxwell graduated from Trinity with a degree in mathematics. He scored second highest in the final examination, coming behind [Edward Routh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Routh) and earning himself the title of Second Wrangler. He was later declared equal with Routh in the more exacting ordeal of the [Smith's Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith's_Prize) examination.[^o57] Immediately after earning his degree, Maxwell read his paper "On the Transformation of Surfaces by Bending" to the [Cambridge Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Philosophical_Society).[^o58] This is one of the few purely mathematical papers he had written, demonstrating his growing stature as a mathematician.[^o59] Maxwell decided to remain at Trinity after graduating and applied for a fellowship, which was a process that he could expect to take a couple of years.[^o60] Buoyed by his success as a research student, he would be free, apart from some tutoring and examining duties, to pursue scientific interests at his own leisure.[^o60]

The nature and perception of colour was one such interest which he had begun at the University of Edinburgh while he was a student of Forbes.[^o61] With the coloured [spinning tops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinning_top) invented by Forbes, Maxwell was able to demonstrate that white light would result from a mixture of red, green, and blue light.[^o61] His paper "Experiments on Colour" laid out the principles of colour combination and was presented to the Royal Society of Edinburgh in March 1855.[^o62] Maxwell was this time able to deliver it himself.[^o62]

Maxwell was made a fellow of Trinity on 10 October 1855, sooner than was the norm,[^o62] and was asked to prepare lectures on [hydrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatics) and [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics) and to set examination papers.[^o63] The following February he was urged by Forbes to apply for the newly vacant [Chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chair_%28academic%29) of Natural Philosophy at [Marischal College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marischal_College), [Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen).[^o64][^o65] His father assisted him in the task of preparing the necessary references, but died on 2 April at Glenlair before either knew the result of Maxwell's candidacy.[^o65] He accepted the professorship at Aberdeen, leaving Cambridge in November 1856.[^o63]

### Marischal College, Aberdeen, 1856–1860 {#Marischal_College,_Aberdeen,_1856–1860}

*Image: Saturn HST 2004-03-22.jpg.* Maxwell proved that the [rings of Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn) were made of numerous small particles.

The 25-year-old Maxwell was a good 15 years younger than any other professor at Marischal. He engaged himself with his new responsibilities as head of a department, devising the syllabus and preparing lectures.[^o67] He committed himself to lecturing 15 hours a week, including a weekly *[pro bono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_bono)* lecture to the local working men's college.[^o67] He lived in Aberdeen with his cousin [William Dyce Cay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Dyce_Cay), a Scottish civil engineer, during the six months of the academic year and spent the summers at Glenlair, which he had inherited from his father.[^o21]

Later, his former student described Maxwell as follows:

> In the late 1850s shortly before 9 am any winter's morning you might well have seen the young James Clerk Maxwell, in his mid to late 20s, a man of middling height, with frame strongly knit, and a certain spring and elasticity in his gait; dressed for comfortable ease rather than elegance; a face expressive at once of sagacity and good humour, but overlaid with a deep shade of thoughtfulness; features boldly put pleasingly marked; eyes dark and glowing; hair and beard perfectly black, and forming a strong contrast to the pallor of his complexion.[^o68]

*Image: James Clark Maxwell and his wife by Jemima Blackburn.jpg.* James Clerk Maxwell and his wife, painted by [Jemima Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jemima_Blackburn)

He focused his attention on a problem that had eluded scientists for 200 years: the nature of [Saturn's rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rings_of_Saturn). It was unknown how they could remain stable without breaking up, drifting away or crashing into Saturn.[^o69] The problem took on a particular resonance at that time because [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge), had chosen it as the topic for the 1857 [Adams Prize](wikipedia://en/1098110).[^o70] Maxwell devoted two years to studying the problem, proving that a regular solid ring could not be stable, while a fluid ring would be forced by wave action to break up into blobs. Since neither was observed, he concluded that the rings must be composed of numerous small particles he called "brick-bats", each independently orbiting Saturn.[^o70] Maxwell was awarded the £130 Adams Prize in 1859 for his essay "On the stability of the motion of Saturn's rings";[^o71] he was the only entrant to have made enough headway to submit an entry.[^o72] His work was so detailed and convincing that when [George Biddell Airy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Biddell_Airy) read it he commented, "It is one of the most remarkable applications of mathematics to physics that I have ever seen."[^o73] It was considered the final word on the issue until direct observations by the *[Voyager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_program)* flybys of the 1980s confirmed Maxwell's prediction that the rings were composed of particles.[^o74] It is now understood, however, that the rings' particles are not totally stable, being pulled by gravity onto Saturn. The rings are expected to vanish entirely over the next 300 million years.[^o75]

In 1857 Maxwell befriended the Reverend [Daniel Dewar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dewar), who was then the Principal of Marischal.[^o76] Through him Maxwell met Dewar's daughter, [Katherine Mary Dewar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Clerk_Maxwell). They were engaged in February 1858 and married in Aberdeen on 2 June 1858. On the marriage record, Maxwell is listed as Professor of Natural Philosophy in Marischal College, Aberdeen.[^o77] Katherine was seven years Maxwell's senior. Comparatively little is known of her, although it is known that she helped in his lab and worked on experiments in [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754).[^o78] Maxwell's biographer and friend, Lewis Campbell, adopted an uncharacteristic reticence on the subject of Katherine, though describing their married life as "one of unexampled devotion".[^o79]

In 1860 Marischal College merged with the neighbouring [King's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Aberdeen) to form the [University of Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Aberdeen). There was no room for two professors of Natural Philosophy, so Maxwell, despite his scientific reputation, found himself laid off. He was unsuccessful in applying for Forbes's recently vacated chair at Edinburgh, the post instead going to [Tait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Tait_%28physicist%29). Maxwell was granted the Chair of Natural Philosophy at [King's College, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_London), instead.[^o80] After recovering from a near-fatal bout of [smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox) in 1860, he moved to London with his wife.[^o81]

### King's College, London, 1860–1865 {#King's_College,_London,_1860–1865}

*Image: Maxwell IEEE Plaque KCL.jpg.* Commemoration of Maxwell's equations at King's College. Two identical [IEEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Electrical_and_Electronics_Engineers) Milestone Plaques are at Maxwell's birthplace in Edinburgh and the family home at Glenlair.[^o83]

Maxwell's time at King's was probably the most productive of his career. He was awarded the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society)'s [Rumford Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumford_Medal) in 1860 for his work on colour and was later elected to the Society in 1861.[^o84] This period of his life would see him display the world's first light-fast colour photograph, further develop his ideas on the [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) of gases, and propose a system of defining physical quantities—now known as [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267). Maxwell would often attend lectures at the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution), where he came into regular contact with [Michael Faraday](wikipedia://en/19727). The relationship between the two men could not be described as close, because Faraday was 40 years Maxwell's senior and showed signs of [senility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senility). They nevertheless maintained a strong respect for each other's talents.[^o85]

This time is especially noteworthy for the advances Maxwell made in the fields of electricity and magnetism. He examined the nature of both electric and magnetic fields in his two-part paper "[On Physical Lines of Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Physical_Lines_of_Force)", which was published in 1861. In it, he provided a conceptual model for [electromagnetic induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_induction), consisting of tiny spinning cells of [magnetic flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux). Two more parts were later added to and published in that same paper in early 1862. In the first additional part, he discussed the nature of [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics) and [displacement current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_current). In the second additional part, he dealt with the rotation of the plane of the [polarisation of light](wikipedia://en/41564) in a magnetic field, a phenomenon that had been discovered by Faraday and is now known as the [Faraday effect](wikipedia://en/518692).[^o86]

### Later years, 1865–1879 {#Later_years,_1865–1879}

In 1865 Maxwell resigned the chair at King's College, London, and returned to Glenlair with Katherine. In his paper "On governors" (1868) he mathematically described the behaviour of [governors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_%28device%29)—devices that control the speed of steam engines—thereby establishing the theoretical basis of control engineering.[^o88] In his paper "On reciprocal figures, frames and diagrams of forces" (1870) he discussed the rigidity of various designs of lattice.[^o89][^o90] He wrote the textbook *Theory of Heat* (1871) and the treatise *Matter and Motion* (1876). Maxwell was also the first to make explicit use of [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267), in 1871,[^o91][^o12][^o92] and helped to establish the [CGS system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centimetre–gram–second_system_of_units) of measurement.[^o93]

Maxwell has been credited as being the first to grasp the concept of [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295), as he acknowledged the significance of systems that exhibit "sensitive dependence on initial conditions."[^o94] He was also the first to emphasize the "[butterfly effect](wikipedia://en/4024)" in the 1870s in two discussions.[^o12][^o95]

In 1871 he returned to Cambridge to become the first [Cavendish Professor of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Professor_of_Physics).[^o96] Maxwell was put in charge of the development of the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory), supervising every step in the progress of the building and of the purchase of the collection of apparatus.[^o97] One of Maxwell's last great contributions to science was the editing (with copious original notes) of the research of [Henry Cavendish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Cavendish), from which it appeared that Cavendish researched, amongst other things, such questions as the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) of the Earth and the composition of water.[^o98] He was elected as a member to the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society) in 1876.[^o99]

### Death {#Death}

*Image: JCM Grave-1.jpg.* The gravestone at Parton Kirk (Galloway) of James Clerk Maxwell, his parents and his wife

In April 1879 Maxwell began to have difficulty in swallowing, the first symptom of his fatal illness.[^o101]

Maxwell died in Cambridge of abdominal cancer on 5 November 1879 at the age of 48.[^o43] His mother had died at the same age of the same type of cancer.[^o102] The minister who regularly visited him in his last weeks was astonished at his lucidity and the immense power and scope of his memory, but comments more particularly,

... his illness drew out the whole heart and soul and spirit of the man: his firm and undoubting faith in the Incarnation and all its results; in the full sufficiency of the Atonement; in the work of the Holy Spirit. He had gauged and fathomed all the schemes and systems of philosophy, and had found them utterly empty and unsatisfying—"unworkable" was his own word about them—and he turned with simple faith to the Gospel of the Saviour.

As death approached Maxwell told a Cambridge colleague,[^o54]

I have been thinking how very gently I have always been dealt with. I have never had a violent shove all my life. The only desire which I can have is like David to serve my own generation by the will of God, and then fall asleep.

Maxwell is buried at [Parton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parton,_Dumfries_and_Galloway) Kirk, near [Castle Douglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Douglas) in Galloway close to where he grew up.[^o103] The extended biography *The Life of James Clerk Maxwell*, by his former schoolfellow and lifelong friend Professor [Lewis Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Campbell_%28classicist%29), was published in 1882.[^o104][^o105] His collected works were issued in two volumes by the [Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press) in 1890.[^o106]

The executors of Maxwell's estate were his physician [George Edward Paget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Edward_Paget), [G. G. Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._G._Stokes), and Colin Mackenzie, who was Maxwell's cousin. Overburdened with work, Stokes passed Maxwell's papers to [William Garnett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Garnett_%28professor%29), who had effective custody of the papers until about 1884.[^o107]

There is a memorial inscription to him near the choir screen at [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey).[^o108]

### Personal life {#Personal_life}

*Image: James Clark Maxwell by Jemima Blackburn.jpg.* James Clerk Maxwell, painted by [Jemima Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jemima_Blackburn)

As a great lover of [Scottish poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_poetry), Maxwell memorised poems and wrote his own.[^o110] The best known is *Rigid Body Sings*, closely based on "[Comin' Through the Rye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comin'_Through_the_Rye)" by [Robert Burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Burns), which he apparently used to sing while accompanying himself on a guitar. It has the opening lines[^o111]

Gin a body meet a body Flyin' through the air. Gin a body hit a body, Will it fly? And where? A collection of his poems was published by his friend [Lewis Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Campbell_%28classicist%29) in 1882.[^o112]

Descriptions of Maxwell remark upon his remarkable intellectual qualities being matched by social awkwardness.[^o113]

Maxwell wrote the following aphorism for his own conduct as a scientist:

> He that would enjoy life and act with freedom must have the work of the day continually before his eyes. Not yesterday's work, lest he fall into despair, not to-morrow's, lest he become a visionary—not that which ends with the day, which is a worldly work, nor yet that only which remains to eternity, for by it he cannot shape his action. Happy is the man who can recognize in the work of to-day a connected portion of the work of life, and an embodiment of the work of eternity. The foundations of his confidence are unchangeable, for he has been made a partaker of Infinity. He strenuously works out his daily enterprises, because the present is given him for a possession.[^o114]

Maxwell was an evangelical [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterianism) and in his later years became an [Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_of_the_Church_of_Scotland) of the [Church of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Scotland).[^o115] Maxwell's religious beliefs and related activities have been the focus of a number of papers.[^o116][^o117][^o118][^o119] Attending both Church of Scotland (his father's denomination) and [Episcopalian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Episcopal_Church) (his mother's denomination) services as a child, Maxwell underwent an [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism) conversion in April 1853. One facet of this conversion may have aligned him with an [antipositivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipositivism) position.[^o118]

## Scientific legacy {#Scientific_legacy}

### Recognition {#Recognition}

In a survey of the 100 most prominent physicists conducted by *[Physics World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_World)*, Maxwell was voted the third greatest physicist of all time, behind only [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736).[^o122] Another survey of rank-and-file physicists by *PhysicsWeb* also voted him third.[^o123]

Many physicists regard Maxwell as the 19th-century scientist having the greatest influence on 20th-century physics. His contributions to the science are considered by many to be of the same magnitude as those of Newton and Einstein.[^o124] On the centenary of Maxwell's birthday, his work was described by Einstein as the "most profound and the most fruitful that physics has experienced since the time of Newton".[^o125] When Einstein visited the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) in 1922, he was told by his host that he had done great things because he stood on Newton's shoulders; Einstein replied: "No I don't. I stand on the shoulders of Maxwell."[^o126] Tom Siegfried described Maxwell as "one of those once-in-a-century geniuses who perceived the physical world with sharper senses than those around him".[^o127]

### Electromagnetism {#Electromagnetism}

*Image: Postcard-from-Maxwell-to-Tait.jpg.* A postcard from Maxwell to [Peter Tait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Guthrie_Tait)

Maxwell had studied and commented on electricity and magnetism as early as 1855 when his paper "On Faraday's lines of force" was read to the [Cambridge Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Philosophical_Society).[^o129] The paper presented a simplified model of Faraday's work and how electricity and magnetism are related. He reduced all of the current knowledge into a linked set of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) with 20 equations in 20 variables. This work was later published as "[On Physical Lines of Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Physical_Lines_of_Force)" in March 1861.[^o130]

Around 1862, while lecturing at King's College, Maxwell calculated that the speed of propagation of an electromagnetic field is approximately that of the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). He considered this to be more than just a coincidence, commenting, "We can scarcely avoid the conclusion that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena.[^o73]

Working on the problem further, Maxwell showed that the [equations](wikipedia://en/2924436) predict the existence of waves of [oscillating electric and magnetic fields](wikipedia://en/9426) that travel through empty space at a speed that could be predicted from simple electrical experiments; using the data available at the time, Maxwell obtained a velocity of 310740000 m/s.[^o131] In his 1865 paper "[A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Dynamical_Theory_of_the_Electromagnetic_Field)", Maxwell wrote, "The agreement of the results seems to show that light and magnetism are affections of the same substance, and that light is an electromagnetic disturbance propagated through the field according to electromagnetic laws".[^o7]

His famous twenty equations, in their modern form of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564), first appeared in fully developed form in his textbook *[A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_on_Electricity_and_Magnetism)* in 1873.[^o132] Most of this work was done by Maxwell at Glenlair during the period between holding his London post and his taking up the Cavendish chair.[^o73] [Oliver Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831) reduced the complexity of Maxwell's theory down to four [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564),[^o133] known now collectively as Maxwell's Laws or [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737). Although potentials became much less popular in the nineteenth century,[^o134] the use of scalar and vector potentials is now standard in the solution of Maxwell's equations.[^o135] His work achieved the [second great unification in physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unification_of_theories_in_physics#Unification_of_magnetism,_electricity,_light_and_related_radiation).[^o136]

As Barrett and Grimes (1995) describe:[^o137]

> Maxwell expressed electromagnetism in the algebra of [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions) and made the electromagnetic potential the centerpiece of his theory. In 1881 Heaviside replaced the electromagnetic potential field by force fields as the centerpiece of electromagnetic theory. According to Heaviside, the electromagnetic potential field was arbitrary and needed to be "assassinated". (*sic*) A few years later there was a debate between Heaviside and [\[Peter Guthrie\] Tate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Guthrie_Tait) (*sic*) about the relative merits of [vector analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_analysis) and [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions). The result was the realization that there was no need for the greater physical insights provided by [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions) if the theory was purely local, and vector analysis became commonplace.

Maxwell was proved correct, and his quantitative connection between light and electromagnetism is considered one of the great accomplishments of 19th-century [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416).[^o138]

Maxwell also introduced the concept of the *electromagnetic field* in comparison to force lines that Faraday described.[^o139] By understanding the propagation of electromagnetism as a field emitted by active particles, Maxwell could advance his work on light. At that time, Maxwell believed that the propagation of light required a medium for the waves, dubbed the [luminiferous aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminiferous_aether).[^o139] Over time, the existence of such a medium, permeating all space and yet apparently undetectable by mechanical means, proved impossible to reconcile with experiments such as the [Michelson–Morley experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_experiment).[^o140] Moreover, it seemed to require an absolute [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference) in which the equations were valid, with the distasteful result that the equations changed form for a moving observer. These difficulties inspired Albert Einstein to formulate the theory of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962); in the process, Einstein dispensed with the luminiferous aether as "superfluous".[^o141]

Einstein acknowledged the groundbreaking work of Maxwell, stating that:[^o142]One scientific epoch ended and another began with James Clerk Maxwell.He also acknowledged the influence that his work had on his relativity theory:[^o142]The special theory of relativity owes its origins to Maxwell's equations of the electromagnetic field.

### Colour vision {#Colour_vision}

*Image: Tartan Ribbon.jpg.* First durable colour photographic image, demonstrated by Maxwell in an 1861 lecture

Along with most physicists of the time, Maxwell had a strong interest in psychology. Following in the steps of Isaac Newton and [Thomas Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_%28scientist%29), he was particularly interested in the study of [colour vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_vision). From 1855 to 1872, Maxwell published at intervals a series of investigations concerning the perception of colour, [colour-blindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour-blindness), and colour theory, and was awarded the [Rumford Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumford_Medal) for "On the Theory of Colour Vision".[^o144]

Newton had demonstrated, using prisms, that white light, such as [sunlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight), is composed of a number of [monochromatic components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_color) which could then be recombined into white light.[^o145] Newton also showed that an orange paint made of yellow and red could look exactly like a monochromatic orange light, although being composed of two monochromatic yellow and red lights. Hence the paradox that puzzled physicists of the time: two complex lights (composed of more than one monochromatic light) could look alike but be physically different, called *[metameres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamerism_%28color%29)*. Thomas Young later proposed that this paradox could be explained by colours being perceived through a limited number of channels in the eyes, which he proposed to be threefold,[^o146] the *[trichromatic colour theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young–Helmholtz_theory)*. Maxwell used the recently developed [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) to prove Young's theory. Any monochromatic light stimulating three receptors should be able to be equally stimulated by a set of three different monochromatic lights (in fact, by any set of three different lights). He demonstrated that to be the case,[^o147] inventing colour matching experiments and [Colourimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colourimetry).

Maxwell was also interested in applying his theory of colour perception, namely in [colour photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour_photography). Stemming directly from his psychological work on colour perception: if a sum of any three lights could reproduce any perceivable colour, then colour photographs could be produced with a set of three coloured filters. In the course of his 1855 paper, Maxwell proposed that, if three black-and-white photographs of a scene were taken through [red, green, and blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RGB_color_model) [filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_%28optics%29), and transparent prints of the images were projected onto a screen using three projectors equipped with similar filters, when superimposed on the screen the result would be perceived by the human eye as a complete reproduction of all the colours in the scene.[^o148]

During an 1861 Royal Institution lecture on colour theory, Maxwell presented the world's first demonstration of colour photography by this principle of three-colour analysis and synthesis. [Thomas Sutton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Sutton_%28photographer%29), inventor of the [single-lens reflex camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-lens_reflex_camera), took the picture. He photographed a [tartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartan) ribbon three times, through red, green, and blue filters, also making a fourth photograph through a yellow filter, which, according to Maxwell's account, was not used in the demonstration. Because Sutton's [photographic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_plate) were insensitive to red and barely sensitive to green, the results of this pioneering experiment were far from perfect. It was remarked in the published account of the lecture that "if the red and green images had been as fully photographed as the blue", it "would have been a truly-coloured image of the riband. By finding photographic materials more sensitive to the less refrangible rays, the representation of the colours of objects might be greatly improved."[^o84][^o149][^o150] Researchers in 1961 concluded that the seemingly impossible partial success of the red-filtered exposure was due to [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet) light, which is strongly reflected by some red dyes, not entirely blocked by the red filter used, and within the range of sensitivity of the [wet collodion process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collodion_process) Sutton employed.[^o151]

### Kinetic theory and thermodynamics {#Kinetic_theory_and_thermodynamics}

*Image: Maxwell's demon.svg.* [Maxwell's demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_demon), a thought experiment where entropy decreases

Maxwell also investigated the [kinetic theory](wikipedia://en/64204) of gases. He was key in the creation of statistical mechanics.[^o153][^n3] Originating with [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784), this theory was advanced by the successive labours of [John Herapath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Herapath), [John James Waterston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_James_Waterston), [James Joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Prescott_Joule), and particularly [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius), to such an extent as to put its general accuracy beyond a doubt; but it received enormous development from Maxwell, who in this field appeared as an experimenter (on the laws of gaseous friction) as well as a mathematician.[^o155]

Between 1859 and 1866, he developed the theory of the distributions of velocities in particles of a gas, work later generalised by [Ludwig Boltzmann](wikipedia://en/544255).[^o156][^o157] The formula, called the [Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/19830), gives the fraction of gas molecules moving at a specified velocity at any given temperature. In the [kinetic theory](wikipedia://en/64204), temperatures and heat involve only molecular movement. This approach generalised the previously established laws of thermodynamics and explained existing observations and experiments in a better way than had been achieved previously. His work on [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) led him to devise the [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535) that came to be known as [Maxwell's demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_demon), where the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/133017) is violated by an imaginary being capable of sorting particles by energy.[^o158][^o159]

In 1871, he established [Maxwell's thermodynamic relations](wikipedia://en/921525), which are statements of equality among the second derivatives of the [thermodynamic potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_potentials) with respect to different thermodynamic variables. In 1874, he constructed a [plaster thermodynamic visualisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_thermodynamic_surface) as a way of exploring phase transitions, based on the American scientist [Josiah Willard Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332)'s graphical [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) papers.[^o160][^o161]

In his 1867 paper *On the Dynamical Theory of Gases* he introduced the [Maxwell model](wikipedia://en/480282) for describing the behavior of a [viscoelastic](wikipedia://en/711288) material and originated the [Maxwell-Cattaneo equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_heat_conduction#Hyperbolic_model_%28relativistic%29) for describing the transport of heat in a medium.[^o162]

[Peter Guthrie Tait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Guthrie_Tait) called Maxwell the "leading molecular scientist" of his time.[^o8] Another person added after Maxwell's death that "only one man lived who could understand Gibbs's papers. That was Maxwell, and now he is dead."[^o163]

### Control theory {#Control_theory}

Maxwell published the paper "On governors" in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society*, vol. 16 (1867–1868).[^o165] This paper is considered a central paper of the early days of [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039).[^o166] Here "governors" refers to the [governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Governor_%28device%29) or the [centrifugal governor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifugal_governor) used to regulate [steam engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine).
