# Bounded set (topological vector space)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bounded set"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) in a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) is called **bounded** or **von Neumann bounded**, if every [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of the [zero vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_vector) can be *inflated* to include the set. A set that is not bounded is called **unbounded**.

:::

Bounded sets are a natural way to define [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [polar topologies](wikipedia://en/1789978) on the [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) in a [dual pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_pair), as the [polar set](wikipedia://en/1764022) of a bounded set is an [absolutely convex](wikipedia://en/1734292) and [absorbing set](wikipedia://en/1728179). The concept was first introduced by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) and [Andrey Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) in 1935.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose $X$ is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) over a [topological field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_field) $\mathbb{K}.$

A subset $B$ of $X$ is called **[von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) bounded** or just **bounded** in $X$ if any of the following equivalent conditions are satisfied:

Definition: For every neighborhood $V$ of the origin there exists a real $r > 0$ such that $B \subseteq s V$[^o4] for all scalars $s$ satisfying $|s| \geq r.$[^n1]

This was the definition introduced by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) in 1935.[^n1] $B$ is [absorbed](wikipedia://en/1728179) by every [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) of the origin.[^n2] For every neighborhood $V$ of the origin there exists a scalar $s$ such that $B \subseteq s V.$ For every neighborhood $V$ of the origin there exists a real $r > 0$ such that $s B \subseteq V$ for all scalars $s$ satisfying $|s| \leq r.$[^n1] For every neighborhood $V$ of the origin there exists a real $r > 0$ such that $t B \subseteq V$ for all real $0 < t \leq r.$[^n3] Any one of statements (1) through (5) above but with the word "neighborhood" replaced by any of the following: "[balanced](wikipedia://en/1734238) neighborhood," "open balanced neighborhood," "closed balanced neighborhood," "open neighborhood," "closed neighborhood".

e.g. Statement (2) may become: $B$ is bounded if and only if $B$ is absorbed by every [balanced](wikipedia://en/1734238) neighborhood of the origin.[^n1]

If $X$ is [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) then the adjective "convex" may be also be added to any of these 5 replacements. For every sequence of scalars $s_1, s_2, s_3, \ldots$ that converges to $0$ and every sequence $b_1, b_2, b_3, \ldots$ in $B,$ the sequence $s_1 b_1, s_2 b_2, s_3 b_3, \ldots$ converges to $0$ in $X.$[^n1]

This was the definition of "bounded" that [Andrey Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161) used in 1934, which is the same as the definition introduced by [Stanisław Mazur](wikipedia://en/1023162) and [Władysław Orlicz](wikipedia://en/362718) in 1933 for metrizable TVS. Kolmogorov used this definition to prove that a TVS is seminormable if and only if it has a bounded convex neighborhood of the origin.[^n1] For every sequence $b_1, b_2, b_3, \ldots$ in $B,$ the sequence $\left(\tfrac{1}{i} b_i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ converges to $0$ in $X.$[^n4] Every [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) subset of $B$ is bounded (according to any defining condition other than this one).[^n1]

If $\mathcal{B}$ is a [neighborhood basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_basis) for $X$ at the origin then this list may be extended to include:

Any one of statements (1) through (5) above but with the neighborhoods limited to those belonging to $\mathcal{B}.$

e.g. Statement (3) may become: For every $V \in \mathcal{B}$ there exists a scalar $s$ such that $B \subseteq s V.$

If $X$ is a [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) space whose topology is defined by a family $\mathcal{P}$ of continuous [seminorms](wikipedia://en/292067), then this list may be extended to include:

$p(B)$ is bounded for all $p \in \mathcal{P}.$[^n1] There exists a sequence of non-zero scalars $s_1, s_2, s_3, \ldots$ such that for every sequence $b_1, b_2, b_3, \ldots$ in $B,$ the sequence $b_1 s_1, b_2 s_2, b_3 s_3, \ldots$ is bounded in $X$ (according to any defining condition other than this one).[^n1] For all $p \in \mathcal{P},$ $B$ is bounded (according to any defining condition other than this one) in the [semi normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi_normed_space) $(X, p).$ B is weakly bounded, i.e. every continuous linear functional is bounded on B[^o17]

If $X$ is a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) with [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\|\cdot\|$ (or more generally, if it is a [seminormed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminormed_space) and $\|\cdot\|$ is merely a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067)),[^o18] then this list may be extended to include:

$B$ is a *norm bounded* subset of $(X, \|\cdot\|).$ By definition, this means that there exists a real number $r > 0$ such that $\|b\| \leq r$ for all $b \in B.$[^n1] $\sup_{b \in B} \|b\| < \infty.$

Thus, if $L : (X, \|\cdot\|) \to (Y, \|\cdot\|)$ is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) between two normed (or seminormed) spaces and if $B$ is the closed (alternatively, open) unit ball in $(X, \|\cdot\|)$ centered at the origin, then $L$ is a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) (which recall means that its [operator norm](wikipedia://en/246722) $\|L\| := \sup_{b \in B} \|L(b)\| < \infty$ is finite) if and only if the image $L(B)$ of this ball under $L$ is a norm bounded subset of $(Y, \|\cdot\|).$ $B$ is a subset of some (open or closed) ball.[^o20]

This ball need not be centered at the origin, but its radius must (as usual) be positive and finite.

If $B$ is a vector subspace of the TVS $X$ then this list may be extended to include:

$B$ is contained in the closure of $\{0\}.$[^n1]

In other words, a vector subspace of $X$ is bounded if and only if it is a subset of (the vector space) $\operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}.$

Recall that $X$ is a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) if and only if $\{0\}$ is closed in $X.$ So the only bounded vector subspace of a Hausdorff TVS is $\{0\}.$

A subset that is not bounded is called unbounded.

### Bornology and fundamental systems of bounded sets {#Bornology_and_fundamental_systems_of_bounded_sets}

The collection of all bounded sets on a topological vector space $X$ is called the von Neumann bornology or the (canonical) [bornology](wikipedia://en/1804457#Bornology_of_a_topological_vector_space) of $X.$

A base or fundamental system of bounded sets of $X$ is a set $\mathcal{B}$ of bounded subsets of $X$ such that every bounded subset of $X$ is a subset of some $B \in \mathcal{B}.$[^n1] The set of all bounded subsets of $X$ trivially forms a fundamental system of bounded sets of $X.$

::: {#o25 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

In any [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) TVS, the set of closed and bounded [disks](wikipedia://en/1734292) are a base of bounded set.[^n1]

:::

## Examples and sufficient conditions {#Examples_and_sufficient_conditions}

Unless indicated otherwise, a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) need not be [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) nor [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844).

Finite sets are bounded.[^n1] Every [totally bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_bounded) subset of a TVS is bounded.[^n1] Every [relatively compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_compact_set) in a topological vector space is bounded. If the space is equipped with the [weak topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_topology_%28polar_topology%29) the converse is also true. The set of points of a [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) is bounded, the set of points of a [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_net) [net](wikipedia://en/22170) need not be bounded. The closure of the origin (referring to the closure of the set $\{0\}$) is always a bounded closed vector subspace. This set $\operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is the unique largest (with respect to set inclusion $\,\subseteq\,$) bounded vector subspace of $X.$ In particular, if $B \subseteq X$ is a bounded subset of $X$ then so is $B + \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}.$

**Unbounded sets**

A set that is not bounded is said to be *unbounded*.

Any vector subspace of a TVS that is not a contained in the closure of $\{0\}$ is unbounded

There exists a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) $X$ having a bounded subset $B$ and also a dense vector subspace $M$ such that $B$ is not contained in the closure (in $X$) of any bounded subset of $M.$[^n5]

### Stability properties {#Stability_properties}

In any TVS, finite [unions](wikipedia://en/47949), finite [Minkowski sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_sum), scalar multiples, translations, subsets, [closures](wikipedia://en/42309), [interiors](wikipedia://en/55610), and [balanced hulls](wikipedia://en/1734238) of bounded sets are again bounded.[^n1] In any [locally convex TVS](wikipedia://en/518844), the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/6292) (also called the [convex envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_envelope)) of a bounded set is again bounded.[^n6] However, this may be false if the space is not locally convex, as the (non-locally convex) [Lp space](wikipedia://en/45194) $L^p$ spaces for $0 < p < 1$ have no nontrivial open convex subsets.[^n6] The image of a bounded set under a [continuous linear map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_linear_map) is a bounded subset of the codomain.[^n1] A subset of an arbitrary [(Cartesian) product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space) of TVSs is bounded if and only if its image under every coordinate projections is bounded. If $S \subseteq X \subseteq Y$ and $X$ is a topological vector subspace of $Y,$ then $S$ is bounded in $X$ if and only if $S$ is bounded in $Y.$[^n1]

In other words, a subset $S \subseteq X$ is bounded in $X$ if and only if it is bounded in every (or equivalently, in some) topological vector superspace of $X.$

::: {#o38 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

A [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844) has a bounded neighborhood of zero [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its topology can be defined by a single [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067).

The [polar](wikipedia://en/1764022) of a bounded set is an [absolutely convex](wikipedia://en/1734292) and [absorbing set](wikipedia://en/1728179).

::: {#o40 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Mackey's countability condition[^n7].**

If $B_1, B_2, B_3, \ldots$ is a countable sequence of bounded subsets of a [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844) $X,$ then there exists a bounded subset $B$ of $X$ and a sequence $r_1, r_2, r_3, \ldots$ of positive real numbers such that $B_i \subseteq r_i B$ for all $i \in \mathbb{N}$ (or equivalently, such that $\tfrac{1}{r_1} B_1 \cup \tfrac{1}{r_2} B_2 \cup \tfrac{1}{r_3} B_3 \cup \cdots \subseteq B$).

:::

Using the definition of uniformly bounded sets given below, Mackey's countability condition can be restated as: If $B_1, B_2, B_3, \ldots$ are bounded subsets of a [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) [locally convex space](wikipedia://en/518844) then there exists a sequence $t_1, t_2, t_3, \ldots$ of positive real numbers such that $t_1 B_1, \, t_2 B_2, \, t_3 B_3, \ldots$ are uniformly bounded. In words, given any countable family of bounded sets in a metrizable locally convex space, it is possible to scale each set by its own positive real so that they become uniformly bounded.

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Uniformly bounded sets {#Uniformly_bounded_sets}

A [family of sets](wikipedia://en/723043) $\mathcal{B}$ of subsets of a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $Y$ is said to be uniformly bounded in $Y,$ if there exists some bounded subset $D$ of $Y$ such that

$$B \subseteq D \quad \text{ for every } B \in \mathcal{B},$$

which happens if and only if its union

$$\cup \mathcal{B} ~:=~ \bigcup_{B \in \mathcal{B}} B$$

is a bounded subset of $Y.$ In the case of a [normed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) (or [seminormed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminormed_space)) space, a family $\mathcal{B}$ is uniformly bounded if and only if its union $\cup \mathcal{B}$ is *norm bounded*, meaning that there exists some real $M \geq 0$ such that $\|b\| \leq M$ for every $b \in \cup \mathcal{B},$ or equivalently, if and only if $\sup_{\stackrel{b \in B}{B \in \mathcal{B}}} \|b\| < \infty.$

A set $H$ of maps from $X$ to $Y$ is said to be uniformly bounded maps on a set $C \subseteq X$ if the family $H(C) := \{h(C) : h \in H\}$ is uniformly bounded in $Y,$ which by definition means that there exists some bounded subset $D$ of $Y$ such that $h(C) \subseteq D \text{ for all } h \in H,$ or equivalently, if and only if $\cup H(C) := \bigcup_{h \in H} h(C)$ is a bounded subset of $Y.$ A set $H$ of linear maps between two normed (or seminormed) spaces $X$ and $Y$ is uniformly bounded on some (or equivalently, every) open ball (and/or non-degenerate closed ball) in $X$ if and only if their [operator norms](wikipedia://en/246722) are uniformly bounded; that is, if and only if $\sup_{h \in H} \|h\| < \infty.$

::: {#o45 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=math-theorem name="Proposition"}

**Proposition[^n8].**

Let $H \subseteq L(X, Y)$ be a set of continuous linear operators between two [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ and $Y$ and let $C \subseteq X$ be any bounded subset of $X.$ Then $H$ is uniformly bounded on $C$ (that is, the [family](wikipedia://en/723043) $\{h(C) : h \in H\}$ is uniformly bounded in $Y$) if any of the following conditions are satisfied:

$H$ is [equicontinuous](wikipedia://en/460642).

$C$ is a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) Hausdorff [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of $X$ and for every $c \in C,$ the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) $H(c) := \{h(c) : h \in H\}$ is a bounded subset of $Y.$

:::

::: {#o46 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof of part (1)"}

Assume $H$ is equicontinuous and let $W$ be a neighborhood of the origin in $Y.$ Since $H$ is equicontinuous, there exists a neighborhood $U$ of the origin in $X$ such that $h(U) \subseteq W$ for every $h \in H.$ Because $C$ is bounded in $X,$ there exists some real $r > 0$ such that if $t \geq r$ then $C \subseteq t U.$ So for every $h \in H$ and every $t \geq r,$ $h(C) \subseteq h(t U) = t h(U) \subseteq t W,$ which implies that $\bigcup_{h \in H} h(C) \subseteq t W.$ Thus $\bigcup_{h \in H} h(C)$ is bounded in $Y.$ [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)

:::

::: {#o48 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof of part (2)"}

Let $W$ be a [balanced](wikipedia://en/1734238) neighborhood of the origin in $Y$ and let $V$ be a closed balanced neighborhood of the origin in $Y$ such that $V + V \subseteq W.$ Define

$$E ~:=~ \bigcap_{h \in H} h^{-1}(V),$$

which is a closed subset of $X$ (since $V$ is closed while every $h : X \to Y$ is continuous) that satisfies $h(E) \subseteq V$ for every $h \in H.$ Note that for every non-zero scalar $n \neq 0,$ the set $n E$ is closed in $X$ (since scalar multiplication by $n \neq 0$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660)) and so every $C \cap n E$ is closed in $C.$

It will now be shown that $C \subseteq \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} n E,$ from which $C = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} (C \cap n E)$ follows. If $c \in C$ then $H(c)$ being bounded guarantees the existence of some positive integer $n = n_c \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $H(c) \subseteq n_c V,$ where the linearity of every $h \in H$ now implies $\tfrac{1}{n_c} c \in h^{-1}(V);$ thus $\tfrac{1}{n_c} c \in \bigcap_{h \in H} h^{-1}(V) = E$ and hence $C \subseteq \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} n E,$ as desired.

Thus $C = (C \cap 1 E) \cup (C \cap 2 E) \cup (C \cap 3 E) \cup \cdots$ expresses $C$ as a countable union of closed (in $C$) sets. Since $C$ is a [nonmeager subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonmeager_subset) of itself (as it is a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550) by the [Baire category theorem](wikipedia://en/55227)), this is only possible if there is some integer $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $C \cap n E$ has non-empty interior in $C.$ Let $k \in \operatorname{Int}_C (C \cap n E)$ be any point belonging to this open subset of $C.$ Let $U$ be any balanced open neighborhood of the origin in $X$ such that

$$C \cap (k + U) ~\subseteq~ \operatorname{Int}_C (C \cap n E).$$

The sets $\{k + p U : p > 1\}$ form an increasing (meaning $p \leq q$ implies $k + p U \subseteq k + q U$) cover of the compact space $C,$ so there exists some $p > 1$ such that $C \subseteq k + p U$ (and thus $\tfrac{1}{p}(C - k) \subseteq U$). It will be shown that $h(C) \subseteq p n W$ for every $h \in H,$ thus demonstrating that $\{h(C) : h \in H\}$ is uniformly bounded in $Y$ and completing the proof. So fix $h \in H$ and $c \in C.$ Let

$$z ~:=~ \tfrac{p - 1}{p} k + \tfrac{1}{p} c.$$

The convexity of $C$ guarantees $z \in C$ and moreover, $z \in k + U$ since

$$z - k = \tfrac{-1}{p} k + \tfrac{1}{p} c = \tfrac{1}{p} (c - k) \in \tfrac{1}{p}(C - k) \subseteq U.$$

Thus $z \in C \cap (k + U),$ which is a subset of $\operatorname{Int}_C (C \cap n E).$ Since $n V$ is balanced and $|1 - p| = p - 1 < p,$ we have $(1 - p) n V \subseteq p n V,$ which combined with $h(E) \subseteq V$ gives

$$p n h(E) + (1 - p) n h(E) ~\subseteq~ p n V + (1 - p) n V ~\subseteq~ p n V + p n V ~\subseteq~ p n (V + V) ~\subseteq~ p n W.$$

Finally, $c = p z + (1 - p) k$ and $k, z \in n E$ imply

$$h(c) ~=~ p h(z) + (1 - p) h(k) ~\in~ p n h(E) + (1 - p) n h(E) ~\subseteq~ p n W,$$

as desired. [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)

:::

Since every [singleton subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) of $X$ is also a bounded subset, it follows that if $H \subseteq L(X, Y)$ is an [equicontinuous](wikipedia://en/460642) set of [continuous linear operators](wikipedia://en/1802169) between two [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ and $Y$ (not necessarily [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) or locally convex), then the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) $H(x) := \{h(x) : h \in H\}$ of every $x \in X$ is a bounded subset of $Y.$

### Bounded subsets of topological modules {#Bounded_subsets_of_topological_modules}

The definition of bounded sets can be generalized to [topological modules](wikipedia://en/1616141). A subset $A$ of a topological module $M$ over a [topological ring](wikipedia://en/60097) $R$ is bounded if for any neighborhood $N$ of $0_M$ there exists a neighborhood $w$ of $0_R$ such that $w A \subseteq B.$

:::
