# Clausius–Clapeyron relation

Clapeyron equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Clausius–Clapeyron relation"}

The **Clausius–Clapeyron relation**, in [chemical thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_thermodynamics), specifies the temperature dependence of pressure, most importantly [vapor pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor_pressure), at a discontinuous [phase transition](wikipedia://en/54423) between two [phases of matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_matter) of a single constituent. It is named after [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius)[^o2] and [Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoît_Paul_Émile_Clapeyron).[^o3] However, this relation was in fact originally derived by [Sadi Carnot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Léonard_Sadi_Carnot) in his *[Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflections_on_the_Motive_Power_of_Fire)*, which was published in 1824 but largely ignored until it was rediscovered by Clausius, Clapeyron, and [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin) decades later.[^o4] Kelvin said of Carnot's argument that "nothing in the whole range of Natural Philosophy is more remarkable than the establishment of general laws by such a process of reasoning."[^o5]

:::

Kelvin and his brother [James Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Thomson_%28engineer%29) confirmed the relation experimentally in 1849–50, and it was historically important as a very early successful application of theoretical thermodynamics.[^o6] Its relevance to meteorology and [climatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climatology) is the increase of the water-holding capacity of the atmosphere by about 7% for every 1 °C (1.8 °F) rise in temperature.

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Exact Clapeyron equation {#Exact_Clapeyron_equation}

On a [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure)–[temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) (*P*–*T*) diagram, for any phase change the line separating the two phases is known as the [coexistence curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binodal). The Clapeyron relation[^o10][^o11] gives the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of the [tangents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangents) to this curve. Mathematically,

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta v} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v},$$

where $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}T$ is the slope of the tangent to the [coexistence curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binodal) at any point, $L$ is the molar change in enthalpy ([latent heat](wikipedia://en/164610), the amount of energy absorbed in the transformation), $T$ is the [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050), $\Delta v$ is the [molar volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_volume) change of the phase transition, and $\Delta s$ is the [molar entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_entropy) change of the phase transition. Alternatively, the specific values may be used instead of the molar ones.

### Clausius–Clapeyron equation {#Clausius–Clapeyron_equation}

The Clausius–Clapeyron equation[^o13]p. 509 applies to vaporization of liquids where vapor follows [ideal gas law](wikipedia://en/59881) using the [ideal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant) $R$ and liquid volume is neglected as being much smaller than vapor volume *V*. It is often used to calculate vapor pressure of a liquid.[^o14]

$$\frac{\mathrm{d} P}{\mathrm{d} T} = \frac {P L}{T^2 R},$$

$$v = \frac{1}{n}= \frac{V}{N} = \frac{RT}{P}.$$

where *N* is the [amount of substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amount_of_substance) and *n* is the [number density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_density). The equation expresses this in a more convenient form just in terms of the latent heat, for moderate temperatures and pressures.

:::

## Derivations {#Derivations}

*Image: Phase-diag2.svg.* A typical [phase diagram](wikipedia://en/56398). The dotted green line gives the [anomalous behavior of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water#Density_of_water_and_ice). The Clausius–Clapeyron relation can be used to find the relationship between pressure and temperature along [phase boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_boundaries).

### Derivation from Gibbs–Duhem relation {#Derivation_from_Gibbs–Duhem_relation}

Suppose two phases, $\alpha$ and $\beta$, are in contact and at equilibrium with each other. Their chemical potentials are related by

$$\mu_\alpha = \mu_\beta.$$

Furthermore, along the [coexistence curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binodal),

$$\mathrm{d}\mu_\alpha = \mathrm{d}\mu_\beta.$$

One may therefore use the [Gibbs–Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs–Duhem) relation

$$\mathrm{d}\mu = M(-s \, \mathrm{d}T + v \, \mathrm{d}P)$$

(where $s$ is the specific [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891), $v$ is the [specific volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_volume), and $M$ is the [molar mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_mass)) to obtain

$$-(s_\beta - s_\alpha) \, \mathrm{d}T + (v_\beta - v_\alpha) \, \mathrm{d}P = 0.$$

Rearrangement gives

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{s_\beta - s_\alpha}{v_\beta - v_\alpha} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v},$$

from which the derivation of the Clapeyron equation continues as in the previous section.

### Ideal gas approximation at low temperatures {#Ideal_gas_approximation_at_low_temperatures}

When the [phase transition](wikipedia://en/54423) of a substance is between a [gas phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) and a condensed phase ([liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) or [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid)), and occurs at temperatures much lower than the [critical temperature](wikipedia://en/1951419) of that substance, the [specific volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_volume) of the gas phase $v_\text{g}$ greatly exceeds that of the condensed phase $v_\text{c}$. Therefore, one may approximate

$$\Delta v = v_\text{g} \left(1 - \frac{v_\text{c}}{v_\text{g}}\right) \approx v_\text{g}$$

at low [temperatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperatures). If [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) is also low, the gas may be approximated by the [ideal gas law](wikipedia://en/59881), so that

$$v_\text{g} = \frac{RT}{P},$$

where $P$ is the pressure, $R$ is the [specific gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant#Specific_gas_constant), and $T$ is the temperature. Substituting into the Clapeyron equation

$$\frac{\mathrm{d} P}{\mathrm{d} T} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta v},$$

we can obtain the **Clausius–Clapeyron equation**[^o13]p. 509

$$\frac{\mathrm{d} P}{\mathrm{d} T} = \frac {P L}{T^2 R}$$

for low temperatures and pressures,[^o13]p. 509 where $L$ is the [specific latent heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_latent_heat) of the substance. Instead of the specific, corresponding molar values (i.e. $L$ in kJ/mol and $R$ = 8.31 J/(mol⋅K)) may also be used.

Let $(P_1, T_1)$ and $(P_2, T_2)$ be any two points along the [coexistence curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binodal) between two phases $\alpha$ and $\beta$. In general, $L$ varies between any two such points, as a function of temperature. But if $L$ is approximated as constant,

$$\frac{\mathrm{d} P}{P} \cong \frac {L}{R} \frac {\mathrm{d}T}{T^2},$$

$$\int_{P_1}^{P_2} \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{P} \cong \frac{L}{R} \int_{T_1}^{T_2} \frac{\mathrm{d} T}{T^2},$$

$$\ln P\Big|_{P=P_1}^{P_2} \cong -\frac{L}{R} \cdot \left.\frac{1}{T}\right|_{T=T_1}^{T_2},$$

or[^o18]p. 672[^o19]

$$\ln \frac{P_2}{P_1} \cong -\frac{L}{R} \left( \frac{1}{T_2} - \frac {1}{T_1} \right).$$

These last equations are useful because they relate [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_vapor_pressure) or [saturation vapor pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturation_vapor_pressure) and temperature to the latent heat of the phase change *without* requiring specific-volume data. For instance, for water near its [normal boiling point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_boiling_point), with a molar enthalpy of vaporization of 40.7 kJ/mol and $R$ = 8.31 J/(mol⋅K),

$$P_\text{vap}(T) \cong 1~\text{bar} \cdot \exp\left[-\frac{40\,700~\text{K}}{8.31} \left( \frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{373~\text{K}} \right) \right].$$

### Clapeyron's derivation {#Clapeyron's_derivation}

In the original work by Clapeyron, the following argument is advanced.[^o21] Clapeyron considered a Carnot process of saturated water vapor with horizontal isobars. As the pressure is a function of temperature alone, the isobars are also isotherms. If the process involves an infinitesimal amount of water, $\mathrm{d}x$, and an infinitesimal difference in temperature $\mathrm{d}T$, the heat absorbed is

$$Q = L\,\mathrm{d}x,$$

and the corresponding work is

$$W = \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}T}\,\mathrm{d}T(V'' - V'),$$

where $V'' - V'$ is the difference between the volumes of $\mathrm{d}x$ in the liquid phase and vapor phases. The ratio $W/Q$ is the efficiency of the Carnot engine, $\mathrm{d}T/T$. Substituting and rearranging gives

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{L}{T(v'' - v')},$$

where lowercase $v'' - v'$ denotes the change in *specific volume* during the transition.

## Applications {#Applications}

### Chemistry and chemical engineering {#Chemistry_and_chemical_engineering}

For transitions between a gas and a condensed phase with the approximations described above, the expression may be rewritten as [^o11]

$$\ln \left( \frac{P_1}{P_0} \right) = \frac{L}{R} \left( \frac{1}{T_0}-\frac{1}{T_1} \right)$$

where $P_0, P_1$ are the pressures at temperatures $T_0, T_1$ respectively and $R$ is the [ideal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant). For a liquid–gas transition, $L$ is the [molar latent heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_latent_heat) (or [molar enthalpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_enthalpy)) of [vaporization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaporization); for a solid–gas transition, $L$ is the molar latent heat of [sublimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_%28chemistry%29). If the latent heat is known, then knowledge of one point on the [coexistence curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binodal), for instance (1 bar, 373 K) for water, determines the rest of the curve. Conversely, the relationship between $\ln P$ and $1/T$ is linear, and so [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) is used to estimate the latent heat.

### Meteorology and climatology {#Meteorology_and_climatology}

[Atmospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere) [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) drives many important [meteorologic](wikipedia://en/19904) phenomena (notably, [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation)), motivating interest in its [dynamics](wikipedia://en/9087). The Clausius–Clapeyron equation for water vapor under typical atmospheric conditions (near [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure)) is

$$\frac{\mathrm{d} e_\text{s}}{\mathrm{d} T} = \frac{L_\text{v}(T) e_\text{s}}{R_\text{v} T^2},$$

where

$e_\text{s}$ is [saturation vapor pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturation_vapor_pressure),; $T$ is [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050),; $L_\text{v}$ is the [specific latent heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_latent_heat) of [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation) of water, and; $R_\text{v}$ is the [gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant) of water vapor. The temperature dependence of the latent heat $L_\text{v}(T)$ can be neglected in this application. The **[August](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Ferdinand_August)–[Roche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Édouard_Roche)–[Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Gustav_Magnus) formula** provides a solution under that approximation:[^o26][^o27]

$$e_\text{s}(T) = 6.1094 \exp \left( \frac{17.625 T}{T + 243.04} \right),$$

where $e_\text{s}$ is in [hPa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascals) and $T$ is in [degrees Celsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_Celsius) (whereas everywhere else on this page, $T$ is an absolute temperature in units of kelvin).

The above formula is also sometimes called the *Magnus* or *Magnus–Tetens* approximation, though this attribution is historically inaccurate;[^o28] see also the [discussion of the accuracy of different approximating formulae for saturation vapour pressure of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapour_pressure_of_water#Accuracy_of_different_formulations).

Under typical atmospheric conditions, the [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) in the [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent) $T + 243.04$ depends weakly on $T$. Therefore, the August–Roche–Magnus formula implies that saturation vapor pressure changes approximately [exponentially](wikipedia://en/9678) with temperature under typical atmospheric conditions, and hence the water-holding capacity of the atmosphere increases by about 7% for every 1 °C rise in temperature.[^o29]

:::

## Example {#Example}

One of the uses of this equation is to determine if a phase transition will occur in a given situation. Consider the question of how much pressure is needed to melt ice at a temperature ${\Delta T}$ below 0 °C. Note that water is unusual in that its change in volume upon melting is negative. We can assume

$$\Delta P = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta v} \, \Delta T,$$

and substituting in $L = 3.34 \times 10^5~\mathrm{J/kg}$ (latent heat of fusion for water),; $T = 273 \, \mathrm{K}$ (absolute temperature in [kelvins](wikipedia://en/19593121)),; $\Delta v = -9.05 \times 10^{-5}~\mathrm{m^3 / kg}$ (change in specific volume from solid to liquid), we obtain

$$\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta T} = -13.5~\text{MPa}/\text{K}.$$

To provide a rough example of how much pressure this is, to melt ice at −7 °C (the temperature many [ice skating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_skating) rinks are set at) would require balancing a small car on an area around 1cm<sup>2</sup>. This shows that ice skating cannot be simply explained by pressure-caused melting point depression, and in fact the mechanism is quite complex.[^o32]

:::

## Second derivative {#Second_derivative}

While the Clausius–Clapeyron relation gives the slope of the coexistence curve, it does not provide any information about its curvature or [second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219). The second derivative of the coexistence curve of phases 1 and 2 is given by[^o34]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 P}{\mathrm{d} T^2} &= \frac{1}{v_2 - v_1} \left[\frac{c_{p2} - c_{p1}}{T} - 2(v_2\alpha_2 - v_1\alpha_1) \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}T}\right] \\ {} &+ \frac{1}{v_2 - v_1}\left[(v_2 \kappa_{T2} - v_1 \kappa_{T1}) \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}T}\right)^2\right], \end{aligned}$$

where subscripts 1 and 2 denote the different phases, $c_p$ is the [specific heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_heat_capacity) at constant pressure, $\alpha = (1/v)(\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}T)_P$ is the [thermal expansion coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_expansion_coefficient), and $\kappa_T = -(1/v)(\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}P)_T$ is the [isothermal compressibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isothermal_compressibility).
