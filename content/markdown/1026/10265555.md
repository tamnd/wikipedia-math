# Notation for differentiation

Differential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Notation for differentiation"}

In [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), there is no single standard **notation for differentiation**. Instead, several notations for the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) or a [dependent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable) have been proposed by various mathematicians, including [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281), [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange), and [Arbogast](wikipedia://en/91156). The usefulness of each notation depends on the context in which it is used, and it is sometimes advantageous to use more than one notation in a given context. For more specialized settings—such as [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) in [multivariable calculus](wikipedia://en/615222), [tensor analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_analysis), or [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640)—other notations, such as subscript notation or the [∇](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabla_symbol) operator are common. The most common notations for differentiation (and its opposite operation, [antidifferentiation](wikipedia://en/2823) or [indefinite integration](wikipedia://en/2823)) are listed below.

:::

## Leibniz's notation {#Leibniz's_notation}

The original notation employed by [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) is used throughout mathematics. It is particularly common when the equation $y = f(x)$ is regarded as a functional relationship between [dependent and independent variables](wikipedia://en/437701) $y$ and $x$. Leibniz's notation makes this relationship explicit by writing the derivative as:

$$\frac{dy}{dx}.$$

Furthermore, the derivative of $f$ at $x$ is therefore written

$$\frac{df}{dx}(x)\text{ or }\frac{d f(x)}{dx}\text{ or }\frac{d}{dx} f(x).$$

Higher derivatives are written as:[^n1]

$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}, \frac{d^3y}{dx^3}, \frac{d^4y}{dx^4}, \ldots, \frac{d^ny}{dx^n}.$$

This is a suggestive notational device that comes from formal manipulations of symbols, as in,

$$\frac{d\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{dx} = \left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^2y = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}.$$

The value of the derivative of $y$ at a point $x = a$ may be expressed in two ways using Leibniz's notation:

$$\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=a} \text{ or } \frac{dy}{dx}(a).$$

Leibniz's notation allows one to specify the variable for differentiation (in the denominator). This is especially helpful when considering [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565). It also makes the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) easy to remember and recognize:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}.$$

Leibniz's notation for differentiation does not require assigning meaning to symbols such as $dx$ or $dy$ (known as [differentials](wikipedia://en/677191)) on their own, and some authors do not attempt to assign these symbols meaning. Leibniz treated these symbols as [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990). Later authors have assigned them other meanings, such as infinitesimals in [non-standard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis), or [exterior derivatives](wikipedia://en/220782). Commonly, $dx$ is left undefined or equated with $\Delta x$, while $dy$ is assigned a meaning in terms of $dx$, via the equation

$$dy = \frac{dy}{dx} \cdot dx,$$

which may also be written as, for example,

$$df = f'(x) \cdot dx$$

(see below). Such equations give rise to the terminology found in some texts wherein the derivative is referred to as the "differential coefficient" (the [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) of $dx$).

Some authors and journals set the differential symbol $d$ in [roman type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_type) instead of [italic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_type): $dx$. The [ISO/IEC 80000](wikipedia://en/9007520) scientific style guide recommends this style.

## Lagrange's notation {#Lagrange's_notation}

One of the most common modern notations for differentiation is named after [Joseph Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange), although it was in fact invented by [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) and popularized by the former. In Lagrange's notation, a [prime mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_%28symbol%29) denotes a derivative – hence it is sometimes called **prime notation**. If *f* is a function, then its derivative evaluated at *x* is written

$$f'(x).$$

It first appeared in print in 1749.[^o6]

Higher derivatives are indicated using additional prime marks, as in $f''(x)$ for the [second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219) and $f'''(x)$ for the [third derivative](wikipedia://en/9092737). The use of repeated prime marks eventually becomes unwieldy; some authors continue by employing [Roman numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numeral), usually in lower case,[^o7][^o8] as in

$$f^{\mathrm{iv}}(x), f^{\mathrm{v}}(x), f^{\mathrm{vi}}(x), \ldots,$$

to denote fourth, fifth, sixth, and higher order derivatives. Other authors use Arabic numerals in parentheses, as in

$$f^{(4)}(x), f^{(5)}(x), f^{(6)}(x), \ldots.$$

This notation also makes it possible to describe the *n*th derivative, where *n* is a variable. This is written

$$f^{(n)}(x).$$

When there are two independent variables for a function $f(x,y)$, the following notation is sometimes used:[^o9]

$$\begin{aligned} f^\prime &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = f_x \\[5pt] f_\prime &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = f_y \\[5pt] f^{\prime\prime} &= \frac{\partial ^2 f}{\partial x^2} = f_{xx} \\[5pt] f_\prime^\prime &= \frac{\partial ^2 f}{\partial y \partial x}\ = f_{xy} \\[5pt] f_{\prime\prime} &= \frac{\partial ^2 f}{\partial y^2} = f_{yy} \end{aligned}$$

### Lagrange's notation for antidifferentiation {#Lagrange's_notation_for_antidifferentiation}

When taking the antiderivative, Lagrange followed Leibniz's notation:[^o11]

$$f(x) = \int f'(x)\,dx = \int y'\,dx.$$

However, because integration is the inverse operation of differentiation, Lagrange's notation for higher order derivatives extends to integrals as well. Repeated integrals of *f* may be written as

$f^{(-1)}(x)$ for the first integral (this is easily confused with the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) $f^{-1}(x)$),

$f^{(-2)}(x)$ for the second integral,

$f^{(-3)}(x)$ for the third integral, and

$f^{(-n)}(x)$ for the *n*th integral.

## D-notation {#D-notation}

This notation is sometimes called **Euler's notation** although it was introduced by [Louis François Antoine Arbogast](wikipedia://en/91156),[^o13] and it seems that [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) did not use it.

This notation uses a [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) denoted as $D$ (**D operator**)[^o14] or $D\text{̃}$ (**Newton–Leibniz operator**).[^o15] When applied to a function $f(x)$, it is defined by

$$(Df)(x) = \frac{df(x)}{dx}.$$

Higher derivatives are notated as "powers" of *D* (where the superscripts denote iterated [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of *D*), as in[^o9]

$D^2f$ for the second derivative,

$D^3f$ for the third derivative, and

$D^nf$ for the *n*th derivative.

D-notation leaves implicit the variable with respect to which differentiation is being done. However, this variable can also be made explicit by putting its name as a subscript: if *f* is a function of a variable *x*, this is done by writing[^o9]

$D_x f$ for the first derivative,

$D^2_x f$ for the second derivative,

$D^3_x f$ for the third derivative, and

$D^n_x f$ for the *n*th derivative.

When *f* is a function of several variables, it is common to use "[∂](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/∂)", a stylized cursive lower-case d, rather than "$D$". As above, the subscripts denote the derivatives that are being taken. For example, the second partial derivatives of a function $f(x,y)$ are:[^o9]

$$\begin{aligned} & \partial_{xx} f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}, \\[5pt] & \partial_{xy} f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}, \\[5pt] & \partial_{yx} f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}, \\[5pt] & \partial_{yy} f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}. \end{aligned}$$

See .

D-notation is useful in the study of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) and in [differential algebra](wikipedia://en/1816587).

### D-notation for antiderivatives {#D-notation_for_antiderivatives}

D-notation can be used for antiderivatives in the same way that Lagrange's notation is[^o17] as follows[^o15]

$D^{-1}f(x)$ for a first antiderivative,

$D^{-2}f(x)$ for a second antiderivative, and

$D^{-n}f(x)$ for an *n*th antiderivative.

## Newton's notation {#Newton's_notation}

[Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s notation for differentiation (also called the **dot notation**, **[fluxions](wikipedia://en/52956308)**, or sometimes, crudely, the **flyspeck notation**[^o19] for differentiation) places a dot over the dependent variable. That is, if *y* is a function of *t*, then the derivative of *y* with respect to *t* is

$$\dot y$$

Higher derivatives are represented using multiple dots, as in

$$\ddot y, \overset{...}{y}$$

Newton extended this idea quite far:[^o20]

$$\begin{aligned} \ddot{y} &\equiv \frac{d^2y}{dt^2} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right) = \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(\dot{y}\Bigr) = \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(f'(t)\Bigr) = D_t^2 y = f''(t) = y''_t \\[5pt] \overset{...}{y} &= \dot{\ddot{y}} \equiv \frac{d^3y}{dt^3} = D_t^3 y = f'''(t) = y'''_t \\[5pt] \overset{\,4}{\dot{y}} &= \overset{....}{y} = \ddot{\ddot{y}} \equiv \frac{d^4y}{dt^4} = D_t^4 y = f^{\rm IV}(t) = y^{(4)}_t \\[5pt] \overset{\,5}{\dot{y}} &= \ddot{\overset{...}{y}} = \dot{\ddot{\ddot{y}}} = \ddot{\dot{\ddot{y}}} \equiv \frac{d^5y}{dt^5} = D_t^5 y = f^{\rm V}(t) = y^{(5)}_t \\[5pt] \overset{\,6}{\dot{y}} &= \overset{...}{\overset{...}{y}} \equiv \frac{d^6y}{dt^6} = D_t^6 y = f^{\rm VI}(t) = y^{(6)}_t \\[5pt] \overset{\,7}{\dot{y}} &= \dot{\overset{...}{\overset{...}{y}}} \equiv \frac{d^7y}{dt^7} = D_t^7 y = f^{\rm VII}(t) = y^{(7)}_t \\[5pt] \overset{\,10}{\dot{y}} &= \ddot{\ddot{\ddot{\ddot{\ddot{y}}}}} \equiv \frac{d^{10}y}{dt^{10}} = D_t^{10} y = f^{\rm X}(t) = y^{(10)}_t \\[5pt] \overset{\,n}{\dot{y}} &\equiv \frac{d^ny}{dt^n} = D_t^n y = f^{(n)}(t) = y^{(n)}_t \end{aligned}$$

Newton's notation is generally used when the independent variable denotes [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time). If location $y$ is a function of *t*, then $\dot y$ denotes [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903)[^o21] and $\ddot y$ denotes [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443).[^o22] This notation is popular in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416). It also appears in areas of mathematics connected with physics such as [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309).

When taking the derivative of a dependent variable *y* = *f*(*x*), an alternative notation exists:[^o23]

$$\frac{\dot{y}}{\dot{x}} = \dot{y}:\dot{x} \equiv \frac{dy}{dt}:\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}} = \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl(f(x)\Bigr) = D y = f'(x) = y'.$$

Newton developed the following partial differential operators using side-dots on a curved X ( ⵋ ). Definitions given by Whiteside are below:[^o24][^o25]

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{X} \ &=\ f(x,y) \,, \\[5pt] \cdot\mathcal{X} \ &=\ x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = xf_x\,, \\[5pt] \mathcal{X}\!\cdot \ &=\ y\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = yf_y\,, \\[5pt] \colon\!\mathcal{X}\,\text{ or }\,\cdot\!\left(\cdot\mathcal{X}\right) \ &=\ x^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = x^2 f_{xx}\,, \\[5pt] \mathcal{X}\colon\,\text{ or }\,\left(\mathcal{X}\cdot\right)\!\cdot \ &=\ y^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} = y^2 f_{yy}\,, \\[5pt] \cdot\mathcal{X}\!\cdot\ \ &=\ xy\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \, \partial y} = xy f_{xy}\,, \end{aligned}$$

### Newton's notation for integration {#Newton's_notation_for_integration}

Newton developed many different notations for [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) in his *Quadratura curvarum* (1704) and [later works](wikipedia://en/252953): he wrote a small vertical bar or prime above the dependent variable ($y\text{̍}$ ), a prefixing rectangle ($\text{▭}y$), or the inclosure of the term in a rectangle ($y$) to denote the *[fluent](wikipedia://en/252953)* or time integral ([absement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absement)).

$$\begin{aligned} y &= \Box \dot{y} \equiv \int \dot{y} \,dt = \int f'(t) \,dt = D_t^{-1} (D_t y) = f(t) + C_0 = y_t + C_0 \\ \overset{\,\prime}{y} &= \Box y \equiv \int y \,dt = \int f(t) \,dt = D_t^{-1} y = F(t) + C_1 \end{aligned}$$

To denote multiple integrals, Newton used two small vertical bars or primes ($y\text{̎}$), or a combination of previous symbols $\text{▭}y\text{̍}$ $y\text{̍}$, to denote the second time integral (absity).

$$\overset{\,\prime\prime}{y} = \Box \overset{\,\prime}{y} \equiv \int \overset{\,\prime}{y} \,dt = \int F(t) \,dt = D_t^{-2} y = g(t) + C_2$$

Higher order time integrals were as follows:[^o27]

$$\begin{aligned} \overset{\,\prime\prime\prime}{y} &= \Box \overset{\,\prime\prime}{y} \equiv \int \overset{\,\prime\prime}{y} \,dt = \int g(t) \,dt = D_t^{-3} y = G(t) + C_3 \\ \overset{\,\prime\prime\prime\prime}{y} &= \Box \overset{\,\prime\prime\prime}{y} \equiv \int \overset{\,\prime\prime\prime}{y} \,dt = \int G(t) \,dt = D_t^{-4} y = h(t) + C_4 \\ \overset{\;n}\overset{\,\prime}{y} &= \Box \overset{\;n-1}\overset{\,\prime}y \equiv \int \overset{\;n-1}\overset{\,\prime}y \,dt = \int s(t) \,dt = D_t^{-n} y = S(t) + C_n \end{aligned}$$

This [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) did not become widespread because of printing difficulties and the [Leibniz–Newton calculus controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz–Newton_calculus_controversy).

## Partial derivatives {#Partial_derivatives}

When more specific types of differentiation are necessary, such as in [multivariate calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_calculus) or [tensor analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_analysis), other notations are common.

For a function *f* of a single independent variable *x*, we can express the derivative using subscripts of the independent variable:

$$\begin{aligned} f_x &= \frac{df}{dx} \\[5pt] f_{x x} &= \frac{d^2f}{dx^2}. \end{aligned}$$

This type of notation is especially useful for taking [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) of a function of several variables.

Partial derivatives are generally distinguished from ordinary derivatives by replacing the differential operator *d* with a "[∂](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/∂)" symbol. For example, we can indicate the partial derivative of *f*(*x*, *y*, *z*) with respect to *x*, but not to *y* or *z* in several ways:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = f_x = \partial_x f.$$

What makes this distinction important is that a non-partial derivative such as $\textstyle \frac{df}{dx}$ *may*, depending on the context, be interpreted as a rate of change in $f$ relative to $x$ when all variables are allowed to vary simultaneously, whereas with a partial derivative such as $\textstyle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ it is explicit that only one variable should vary.

Other notations can be found in various subfields of mathematics, physics, and engineering; see for example the [Maxwell relations](wikipedia://en/921525) of [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952). The symbol $\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_{\!S}$ is the derivative of the temperature *T* with respect to the volume *V* while keeping constant the entropy (subscript) *S*, while $\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_{\!P}$ is the derivative of the temperature with respect to the volume while keeping constant the pressure *P*. This becomes necessary in situations where the number of variables exceeds the degrees of freedom, so that one has to choose which other variables are to be kept fixed.

Higher-order partial derivatives with respect to one variable are expressed as

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2} = f_{xx}, \\[5pt] & \frac{\partial^3f}{\partial x^3} = f_{xxx}, \end{aligned}$$

and so on. Mixed partial derivatives can be expressed as

$$\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \, \partial x} = f_{xy}.$$

In this last case the variables are written in inverse order between the two notations, explained as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} & (f_x)_y = f_{xy}, \\[5pt] & \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y \, \partial x}. \end{aligned}$$

So-called [multi-index notation](wikipedia://en/604268) is used in situations when the above notation becomes cumbersome or insufficiently expressive. When considering functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$, we define a multi-index to be an ordered list of $n$ non-negative integers: $\alpha = (\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n), \ \alpha_i \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. We then define, for $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to X$, the notation

$$\partial^\alpha f = \frac{\partial^{\alpha_1}}{\partial x_1^{\alpha_1}} \cdots \frac{\partial^{\alpha_n}}{\partial x_n^{\alpha_n}} f$$

In this way some results (such as the [Leibniz rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_rule_%28generalized_product_rule%29)) that are tedious to write in other ways can be expressed succinctly -- some examples can be found in the [article on multi-indices](wikipedia://en/604268).[^o29]

## Notation in vector calculus {#Notation_in_vector_calculus}

[Vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640) concerns [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) and [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) of [vector](wikipedia://en/62641) or [scalar fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_fields). Several notations specific to the case of three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) are common.

Assume that $(x, y, z)$ is a given [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), that **A** is a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) with components $\mathbf{A} = (A_x, A_y, A_z)$, and that $\varphi = \varphi(x,y,z)$ is a [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879).

The differential operator introduced by [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817), written [∇](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabla_symbol) and called [del](wikipedia://en/151925) or nabla, is symbolically defined in the form of a vector,

$$\nabla = \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right)\!,$$

where the terminology *symbolically* reflects that the operator ∇ will also be treated as an ordinary vector.

- **[Gradient](wikipedia://en/12461)**: The gradient $\mathrm{grad\,} \varphi$ of the scalar field $\varphi$ is a vector, which is symbolically expressed by the [multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) of ∇ and scalar field *$\varphi$*,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{grad} \varphi &= \left( \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial z} \right) \\ &= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right) \varphi \\ &= \nabla \varphi \end{aligned}$$

- **[Divergence](wikipedia://en/8328)**: The divergence $\mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{A}$ of the vector field **A** is a scalar, which is symbolically expressed by the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of ∇ and the vector **A**,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{div} \mathbf{A} &= {\partial A_x \over \partial x} + {\partial A_y \over \partial y} + {\partial A_z \over \partial z} \\ &= \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right) \cdot \mathbf{A} \\ &= \nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} \end{aligned}$$

- **[Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian)**: The Laplacian $\operatorname{div} \operatorname{grad} \varphi$ of the scalar field $\varphi$ is a scalar, which is symbolically expressed by the scalar multiplication of ∇<sup>2</sup> and the scalar field *φ*,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{div} \operatorname{grad} \varphi &= \nabla \cdot (\nabla \varphi) \\ &= (\nabla \cdot \nabla) \varphi \\ &= \nabla^2 \varphi \\ &= \Delta \varphi \\ \end{aligned}$$

- **[Rotation](wikipedia://en/6123)**: The rotation $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathbf{A}$, or $\mathrm{rot}\,\mathbf{A}$, of the vector field **A** is a vector, which is symbolically expressed by the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) of ∇ and the vector **A**,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{curl} \mathbf{A} &= \left( {\partial A_z \over {\partial y} } - {\partial A_y \over {\partial z} }, {\partial A_x \over {\partial z} } - {\partial A_z \over {\partial x} }, {\partial A_y \over {\partial x} } - {\partial A_x \over {\partial y} } \right) \\ &= \left( {\partial A_z \over {\partial y} } - {\partial A_y \over {\partial z} } \right) \mathbf{i} + \left( {\partial A_x \over {\partial z} } - {\partial A_z \over {\partial x} } \right) \mathbf{j} + \left( {\partial A_y \over {\partial x} } - {\partial A_x \over {\partial y} } \right) \mathbf{k} \\ &= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \cfrac{\partial}{\partial x} & \cfrac{\partial}{\partial y} & \cfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ A_x & A_y & A_z \end{vmatrix} \\ &= \nabla \times \mathbf{A} \end{aligned}$$

Many symbolic operations of derivatives can be generalized in a straightforward manner by the gradient operator in Cartesian coordinates. For example, the single-variable [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) has a direct analogue in the multiplication of scalar fields by applying the gradient operator, as in

$$(f g)' = f' g+f g' ~~~ \Longrightarrow ~~~ \nabla(\phi \psi) = (\nabla \phi) \psi + \phi (\nabla \psi).$$

Many other rules from single variable calculus have [vector calculus analogues](wikipedia://en/3114930#First_derivative_identities) for the gradient, divergence, curl, and Laplacian.

Further notations have been developed for more exotic types of spaces. For calculations in [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space), the [d'Alembert operator](wikipedia://en/293511), also called the d'Alembertian, wave operator, or box operator is represented as $\Box$, or as $\Delta$ when not in conflict with the symbol for the Laplacian.
