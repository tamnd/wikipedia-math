# Parametric statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Parametric statistics"}

**Parametric statistics** is a branch of [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) that is concerned with the analysis of and [inference](wikipedia://en/27577) from data assuming that the underlying distribution, from which the observed data was drawn, can be described by a finite set of (unknown) [parameters](wikipedia://en/413291).[^o2][^o3] In contrast, [nonparametric statistics](wikipedia://en/223369) does not assume explicit (finite-parametric) mathematical forms for distributions when modeling data. However, it may make some assumptions about that distribution, such as continuity or symmetry, or even an explicit mathematical shape but have a model for a distributional parameter that is not itself finite-parametric.

:::

Most well-known statistical methods are parametric.[^o4] Regarding nonparametric (and semiparametric) models, [Sir David Cox](wikipedia://en/1990771) has said, "These typically involve fewer assumptions of structure and distributional form but usually contain strong assumptions about independencies".[^o5]

## Overview {#Overview}

The main goal of [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577) is to provide methods to systematically analyse data and infer properties of the probability distribution from which the data was drawn. The fundamental assumption underlying parametric statistics is that the data distribution belongs to a more general family of distributions that can be parameterized by a finite number of [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_p$, which are unknown. Such a family of distributions is called a [parametric model](wikipedia://en/2883137). Typical questions in this setting are:[^o3]

- [Parameter estimation](wikipedia://en/160379): Which choice of parameters best explains the observed data or leads to best predictions?
- [Interval estimation](wikipedia://en/160381): What are suitable upper- and lower bounds $\overline{T}$ and $\underline{T}$, such that the "best" parameter lies in the interval $[\underline{T},\overline{T}]$ with high probability?
- [Hypothesis testing](wikipedia://en/30284): Is it plausible that the "best" parameter has a certain value or lies within a certain range?
- [Model (mis-)specification](wikipedia://en/11249626): What is an appropriate family of distributions that can explain the observed data?

## Parameter estimation {#Parameter_estimation}

In the typical setting of parameter estimation, the chosen parametric family of distributions has either a density function or a density mass function $L_\theta$, called the [likelihood](wikipedia://en/44968) depending on the parameters $\theta=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_p)$, and a finite sample of independent and identically distributed data $X_1,\dots,X_n$ is given. The goal is to estimate $\theta$ (or a function thereof) based on the observed data.

### Frequentist approaches {#Frequentist_approaches}

In a frequentist approach, the data is assumed to be distributed according to $L_{\theta^*}$ for some "true" parameter $\theta^*$. The goal is to recover $\theta^*$ from the observed data.[^o9] The most common methods for parameter estimation are the following.

- [Maximum Likelihood estimation](wikipedia://en/140806) (MLE): The model parameters are chosen such that the probability (or probability density) of the given observation is maximal. Under certain assumptions on the likelihood, the MLE is (strongly) [consistent](wikipedia://en/610028), asymptotically [efficient](wikipedia://en/27250866) and [asymptotically normal](wikipedia://en/10043#Asymptotic_normality).
- [Method of moments](wikipedia://en/2175504) (MoM): If the model parameters can be expressed as functions $g_1,\dots,g_p$ of the [moments of the distribution](wikipedia://en/368684), then the moment estimates of the parameters are $g_1\left(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i,\dots,\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^p\right),\dots,g_p\left(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i,\dots,\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^p\right)$. If the functions $g_1,\dots,g_p$ are [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122), then the moment estimator is strongly consistent (due to the [strong law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055)). If the functions $g_1,\dots,g_p$ are even differentiable, then the moment estimator is also asymptotically normal (due to the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406) and the [delta method](wikipedia://en/2826026)).
- [Least square estimation](wikipedia://en/82359) (LSE): This method applies to a [regression](wikipedia://en/826997) setting, where the data arises in pairs $(X_1,Y_1),\dots,(X_n,Y_n)$ and a regression function $f$ is to be determined. The model parameters are chosen such that the sum of squared differences $\sum_{i=1}^n(Y_i-f_\theta(X_i))^2$ between the observed data and the model prediction is minimal. In fact, LSE is a special case of MLE, where the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X$ is normally distributed.[^o10]

### Bayesian approaches {#Bayesian_approaches}

In a [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/49571) approach, the data is not assumed to be generated by a distribution $L_{\theta^*}$ for some true $\theta^*$. Instead, the set of all possible (or plausible) model parameters is initially weighted with an à-priori distribution $\pi$ reflecting the statistician's prior belief. Given the observed data, the parameter distribution is updated via [Bayes' rule](wikipedia://en/49569), yielding the à-posteriori distribution $p_\theta$ that is proportional to the likelihood $L_\theta$ times the prior $\pi$. Bayesian estimators therefore rather give a best estimate given the statisticians beliefs.[^o9]

- [Posterior mean estimation](wikipedia://en/6978815#Posterior_mean): The estimator takes the average of the posterior distribution.
- [Posterior median estimation](wikipedia://en/6978815#Posterior_median_and_other_quantiles): The estimator takes the median of the posterior distribution.
- [Maximum à-posteriori estimation](wikipedia://en/1792433) (MAP): The estimator takes the parameter that maximizes the posterior distribution. If the prior is the [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution), then MAP estimation is equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation.

### Optimality of estimators {#Optimality_of_estimators}

#### Uniformly minimum-variance unbiased estimators {#Uniformly_minimum-variance_unbiased_estimators}

Uniformly minimum-variance unbiased estimators (UMVUE), sometimes called best unbiased estimators as well[^o14], are estimators that have minimum variance among all unbiased estimators. Due to the [bias-variance decomposition](wikipedia://en/40678189), they are optimal in the sense that they minimise the [mean squared error](wikipedia://en/201816) among all unbiased estimators.

Let's assume a function of the model parameters $q(\theta)$ needs to be estimated (e.g., mean, variance, median, or $\theta$ itself). Most generally, if an UMVU estimator of $q(\theta)$ with finite variance exists, then it must be unique due to the [Rao–Blackwell theorem](wikipedia://en/400010)[^o15]. If there exists a [complete](wikipedia://en/194618) [sufficient statistic](wikipedia://en/140841) $T$ in the chosen [statistical model](wikipedia://en/27576), which is often easy to determine using the [Neyman–Fisher theorem](wikipedia://en/140841#Fisher–Neyman_factorization_theorem), then it holds the [Lehmann–Scheffé theorem](wikipedia://en/342602): An estimators of the form $f(T)$ that is unbiased, is automatically UMVU. If such an estimator has finite variance for all $\theta$, then it is also the unique UMVUE.

#### Best linear unbiased estimator {#Best_linear_unbiased_estimator}

A best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) has minimum variance among all unbiased estimators $\hat\theta$ that are linear in the data, that is,

$$\hat\theta=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i X_i,$$

for some coefficients $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n\in\mathbb{R}$. In a sense, BLUEs are UMVU estimators with the additional restriction of being linear.[^o17]

In the [regression](wikipedia://en/826997) setting, the [Gauss–Markov theorem](wikipedia://en/170353) states that the least squares estimator is the unique BLUE. This theorem also shows (considering the special case of constant regression) that the [empirical mean](wikipedia://en/10005756) is a BLUE for estimating the mean.

::: {#o19 .statement cue=named-heading name="Information inequality and exponential families"}

#### Information inequality and exponential families {#Information_inequality_and_exponential_families}

In [regular](wikipedia://en/581124#Regularity_conditions) statistical models, it can be shown that the variance of unbiased estimators cannot be arbitrarily small: Any unbiased estimator $T$ of the quantity $q(\theta)$ is bounded from below by the universal bound[^o20]

$$\mathrm{Var}_\theta[T(X)]\geq \nabla q(\theta)I(\theta)^{-1} \nabla q(\theta)^T,$$

where $I(\theta)$ is the [Fisher-information matrix](wikipedia://en/598971) of the statistical model. The right hand-side is the so-called [Cramér–Rao bound](wikipedia://en/581124).

Estimators for which the variance equals the Cramér–Rao bound are called [efficient](wikipedia://en/27250866) and are almost UMVU, since they minimize the variance over all *[regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_estimator)* unbiased estimators (there might be non-regular estimators with smaller variance). On the contrary, not every UMVUE is efficient. In fact, an estimator $T$ is efficient if and only if (i) the statistical model is an [exponential family](wikipedia://en/339174) and (ii) $T$ is the natural sufficient statistic.[^o21]

:::

## Parametric models {#Parametric_models}

The choice of the [model](wikipedia://en/27576), that is, the probability distribution from which the data is assumed to be drawn in density estimation problems or an assumed functional depence between pairs of data $X$ and $Y$ in regression/classification problems, lies at the core of parametric procedures. Here is a list of common models used in practice.

### Density estimation {#Density_estimation}

- [Exponential families](wikipedia://en/339174) (e.g. [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906), [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476), [Gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079), [Chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424), [Erlang distribution](wikipedia://en/199028), [Beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074), [Gumbel distribution](wikipedia://en/270827), [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057), ([Negative-](wikipedia://en/45177))[Binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876), [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144), [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922))
- [Laplace distribution](wikipedia://en/1106213)
- [Uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/1699223)
- [Weibull distribution](wikipedia://en/206948)

### Regression {#Regression}

- [linear model](wikipedia://en/17904) (special cases thereof are [ANOVA](wikipedia://en/634) and [ANCOVA](wikipedia://en/404926))
- [generalized linear model](wikipedia://en/747122) (GLM)
- [neural networks](wikipedia://en/21523)

### Classification {#Classification}

- [logistic regression](wikipedia://en/226631)
- [linear discriminant analysis](wikipedia://en/1470657) (LDA)
- [quadratic discriminant analysis](wikipedia://en/1784975) (QDA)
- [neural networks](wikipedia://en/21523)

## Example {#Example}

The [normal family of distributions](wikipedia://en/21462) all have the same general shape and are *parameterized* by [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) and [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590). That means that if the mean and standard deviation are known and if the distribution is normal, the probability of any future observation lying in a given range is known.

Suppose that we have a sample of 99 test scores with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 1. If we assume all 99 test scores are random observations from a normal distribution, then we predict there is a 1% chance that the 100th test score will be higher than 102.33 (that is, the mean plus 2.33 standard deviations), assuming that the 100th test score comes from the same distribution as the others. Parametric statistical methods are used to compute the 2.33 value above, given 99 [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) observations from the same normal distribution.

A [non-parametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-parametric_statistics) estimate of the same thing is the maximum of the first 99 scores. We don't need to assume anything about the distribution of test scores to reason that before we gave the test it was equally likely that the highest score would be any of the first 100. Thus there is a 1% chance that the 100th score is higher than any of the 99 that preceded it.

:::

## History {#History}

Parametric statistics was mentioned by [R. A. Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) in his work *[Statistical Methods for Research Workers](wikipedia://en/1645218)* in 1925, which created the foundation for modern statistics.
