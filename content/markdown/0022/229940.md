# Multiplicative inverse

Reciprocal (mathematics)

*Image: Reciprocal function.png.* The reciprocal function, $y = \tfrac{1}{x}.$ For every non-zero $x$ coordinate, the corresponding $y$ coordinate on the graph represents its multiplicative inverse. The graph forms a [rectangular hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multiplicative inverse"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **multiplicative inverse** or **reciprocal** for a [number](wikipedia://en/21690) $x$, denoted by $\tfrac{1}{x}$ or $x^{-1}$, is a number which when [multiplied by](wikipedia://en/20845) $x$ yields the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity), 1. The multiplicative inverse of a [fraction](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\tfrac{a}{b}$ is $\tfrac{b}{a}.$ Dividing 1 by a real number yields its multiplicative inverse. For example, the reciprocal of 5 is one fifth (1/5 or 0.2), and the reciprocal of 0.25 is 1 divided by 0.25, or 4. The **reciprocal function**, the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f(x)$ that maps $x$ to $\tfrac{1}{x},$ is one of the simplest examples of a function which is its own inverse (an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450)).

:::

Multiplying by a number is the same as [dividing](wikipedia://en/53696) by its reciprocal and vice versa. For example, multiplication by 4/5 (or 0.8) will give the same result as division by 5/4 (or 1.25). Therefore, multiplication by a number followed by multiplication by its reciprocal yields the original number (since the product of the number and its reciprocal is 1).

The term *reciprocal* was in common use at least as far back as the third edition of *[Encyclopædia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Britannica)* (1797) to describe two numbers whose product is 1; geometrical quantities in inverse proportion are described as reciprocall in a 1570 translation of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*.[^o2]

In the phrase *multiplicative inverse*, the qualifier *multiplicative* is often omitted and then tacitly understood (in contrast to the [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312)). Multiplicative inverses can be defined over many mathematical domains as well as numbers. In these cases it can happen that $ab \ne ba$; then "inverse" typically implies that an element is both a left and right [inverse](wikipedia://en/45199).

The notation $f^{-1}$ is sometimes also used for the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) of the function $f$, which is for most functions not equal to the multiplicative inverse. For example, the multiplicative inverse $\tfrac{1}{\sin x} = (\sin x)^{-1}$ is the [cosecant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosecant) of $x$, and not the [inverse sine of $x$](wikipedia://en/374220) denoted by $\sin^{-1} x$ or $\arcsin x$. The terminology difference *reciprocal* versus *inverse* is not sufficient to make this distinction, since many authors prefer the opposite naming convention, probably for historical reasons (for example in [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), the inverse function is preferably called the bijection réciproque).

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples and counterexamples"}

## Examples and counterexamples {#Examples_and_counterexamples}

In the real numbers, [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) does not have a reciprocal ([division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663) is [undefined](wikipedia://en/9920876)) because no real number multiplied by 0 produces 1 (the product of any number with zero is zero). With the exception of zero, reciprocals of every [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) are real, reciprocals of every [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) are rational, and reciprocals of every [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) are complex. The property that every element other than zero has a multiplicative inverse is part of the definition of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), of which these are all examples. On the other hand, no [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) other than 1 and −1 has an integer reciprocal, and so the integers are not a field.

In [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087), the [modular multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/9815338) of $a$ is also defined: it is the number $x$ such that $ax \equiv 1\pmod{n}$. This multiplicative inverse exists [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $a$ and $n$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime). For example, the inverse of $3 \bmod 11$ is four because $4 \cdot 3 \equiv 1\pmod{11}$. The [extended Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/9815338#Extended_Euclidean_algorithm) may be used to compute it.

The [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434) are an algebra in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, but which nonetheless has divisors of zero, that is, nonzero elements $x$, $y$ such that $xy = 0$.

A [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) has an inverse [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) has an inverse in the coefficient [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). The linear map that has the matrix $A^{-1}$ with respect to some base is then the inverse function of the map having $A$ as matrix in the same base. Thus, the two distinct notions of the inverse of a function are strongly related in this case, but they still do not coincide, since the multiplicative inverse of $Ax$ would be $(Ax)^{-1}$, not $A^{-1}x$.

These two notions of an inverse function do sometimes coincide, for example for the function $f(x)=x^i=e^{i\ln(x)}$ where $\ln$ is the [principal branch of the complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290#Definition) and $e^{-\pi}<|x|<e^{\pi}$:

$$\left(\tfrac1f\circ f \right)(x) = \frac1f(f(x)) = \frac{1}{f(f(x))} = \frac{1}{e^{i\ln(e^{i\ln(x)})} } = \frac{1}{e^{ii\ln(x)}} = \frac{1}{e^{-\ln(x)}} = x.$$

The [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) are related by the reciprocal identity: the cotangent is the reciprocal of the tangent; the secant is the reciprocal of the cosine; the cosecant is the reciprocal of the sine.

A ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse is a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067); likewise an [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_%28ring_theory%29) in which this holds is a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625).

:::

## Complex numbers {#Complex_numbers}

*Image: Complex inversion function.svg.* The inverses $P'$, $Q'$, $R'$, and $S'$ of the complex numbers $P$, $Q$, $R$ and $S$ are constructed by a composition of an inversion in the unit circle and a reflection over the real axis.

As mentioned above, the reciprocal of every nonzero complex number $z = a + bi$ is complex. It can be found by multiplying both top and bottom of $\tfrac{1}{z}$ by its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) $\bar z = a - bi$ and using the property that $z\bar z = \|z\|^2$, the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of $z$ squared, which is the real number $a^{2} + b^{2}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{z} &= \frac{\bar z}{z \bar z} \\[2pt] &= \frac{\bar z}{\|z\|^2} \\[2pt] &= \frac{a - bi}{a^2 + b^2} \\[2pt] &= \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2+b^2}i. \end{aligned}$$

The intuition is that $\tfrac{\bar z}{\|z\|}$ gives us the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) with a [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301) reduced to a value of 1, so dividing again by $\lvert z\rvert$ ensures that the magnitude is now equal to the reciprocal of the original magnitude as well, hence:

$$\frac{1}{z} = \frac{\bar z}{\|z\|^2}$$

In particular, if $||z|| = 1$ ($z$ has unit magnitude), then $\tfrac{1}{z} = \bar z.$ Consequently, the [imaginary units](wikipedia://en/52358), $\pm i$, have [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) equal to multiplicative inverse, and are the only complex numbers with this property. For example, additive and multiplicative inverses of $i$ are $-(i) = -i$ and $(i)^{-1} = -i$, respectively.

For a complex number in polar form $z = r(\cos\,\varphi + i\,\sin\,\varphi)$, the reciprocal simply takes the reciprocal of the magnitude and the negative of the angle:

$$\frac{1}{z} = \frac{1}{r}\bigl(\cos(-\varphi) + i \sin(-\varphi)\bigr).$$

*Image: Reciprocal integral.svg.* Geometric intuition for the integral of $\tfrac{1}{x}.$ The three integrals from 1 to 2, from 2 to 4, and from 4 to 8 are all equal. Each region is the previous region halved vertically and doubled horizontally. Extending this, the integral from 1 to $2^{k}$ is $k$ times the integral from 1 to 2, just as $\ln 2^{k} = k \ln 2$.

Geometrically in the complex plane the inverse of a complex number can be found by performing an [inversion](wikipedia://en/295844) in the unit circle followed by a reflection over the real axis (see drawing).

## Calculus {#Calculus}

In real [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of $\tfrac{1}{x} = x^{-1}$ is given by the [power rule](wikipedia://en/147912) with the power −1:

$$\frac{d}{dx} x^{-1} = (-1)x^{(-1)-1} = -x^{-2} = -\frac{1}{x^2}.$$

The power rule for integrals ([Cavalieri's quadrature formula](wikipedia://en/30839740)) cannot be used to compute the integral of $\tfrac{1}{x},$ because doing so would result in [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663):

$$\int \frac{dx}{x} = \frac{x^0}{0} + C$$

Instead the integral is given by:

$$\int_1^a \frac{dx}{x} = \ln a, \qquad \int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln x + C.$$

where $\ln$ is the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476). To show this, note that $\frac{d}{dy} e^y = e^y$, so if $x = e^y$ and $y = \ln x$, we have:[^o7]

$$\begin{aligned} &\frac{dx}{dy} = x \\[2pt] \Rightarrow \quad &\frac{dx}{x} = dy \\[2pt] \Rightarrow \quad &\int \frac{dx}{x} = \int dy = y + C = \ln x + C. \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o9 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithms"}

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

The reciprocal may be computed by hand with the use of [long division](wikipedia://en/313384).

Computing the reciprocal is important in many [division algorithms](wikipedia://en/3336479), since the quotient $\tfrac{a}{b}$ can be computed by first computing $\tfrac{1}{b}$ and then multiplying it by $a$. Noting that $f(x) = \tfrac1x - b$ has a [zero](wikipedia://en/264210) at $x = \tfrac1b,$ [Newton's method](wikipedia://en/22145) can find that zero, starting with a guess $x_{0}$ and iterating using the rule:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} = x_n - \frac{\frac{1}{x_n} - b}{\frac{-1}{x_n^2}} = 2x_n - bx_n^2 = x_n(2 - bx_n).$$

This continues until the desired precision is reached. For example, suppose we wish to compute $1/17 \approx 0.0588$ with three digits of precision. Taking $x_{0} = 0.1$, the following sequence is produced:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= 0.1(2 - 17 \times 0.1) &&= 0.03 \\ x_2 &= 0.03(2 - 17 \times 0.03) &&= 0.0447 \\ x_3 &= 0.0447(2 - 17 \times 0.0447) &&\approx 0.0554 \\ x_4 &= 0.0554(2 - 17 \times 0.0554) &&\approx 0.0586 \\ x_5 &= 0.0586(2 - 17 \times 0.0586) &&\approx 0.0588 \end{aligned}$$

A typical initial guess can be found by rounding $b$ to a nearby power of 2, then using [bit shifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit_shift) to compute its reciprocal.

In [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics), for a real number $x$ to have a reciprocal, it is not sufficient that $x \ne 0$. There must instead be given a *rational* number $r$ such that $0 < r < \lvert x\rvert$. In terms of the approximation [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) described above, this is needed to prove that the change in $y$ will eventually become arbitrarily small.

*Image: X to x power showing minimum.svg.* Graph of $f(x) = x^{x}$ showing the minimum at $\bigl(\tfrac{1}{e},\ e^{-1/e} \bigr).$

This iteration can also be generalized to a wider sort of inverses; for example, [matrix inverses](wikipedia://en/217122#Newton's_method).

## Reciprocals of irrational numbers {#Reciprocals_of_irrational_numbers}

Every real or complex number excluding zero has a reciprocal, and reciprocals of certain [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689) can have important special properties. Examples include the reciprocal of $e$ (≈ 0.367879) and the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386#Golden_ratio_conjugate_and_powers)'s reciprocal (≈ 0.618034). The first reciprocal is special because no other positive number can produce a lower number when put to the power of itself; $f\bigl(\tfrac{1}{e}\bigr)$ is the [global minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_optimum) of $f(x) = x^{x}$. The second number is the only positive number that is equal to its reciprocal plus one:

$$\varphi = \frac{1}{\varphi} + 1.$$

Its [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) is the only negative number that is equal to its reciprocal minus one:

$$-\varphi = -\frac{1}{\varphi} - 1.$$

The function

$$f(n) = \tfrac{1}{2}n + \sqrt{ \left(\tfrac{1}{2}n \right)^{\! 2} + 1}$$

can be used to find the irrational number which differs from its reciprocal by an integer $n$, because in general $f(n) - f(n)^{-1} = n$. For example:

$$\begin{aligned} & f(4) = 2+\sqrt 5; \qquad f^{-1}(4) = \frac{1}{2 + \sqrt 5} = -2 + \sqrt 5; \\ & \therefore \, f(4) - f^{-1}(4) = 4. \end{aligned}$$

Such irrational numbers share an evident property: they have the same [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532) as their reciprocal, since these numbers differ by an integer.

The reciprocal function plays an important role in [simple continued fractions](wikipedia://en/46802), which have a number of remarkable properties relating to the representation of (both rational and) irrational numbers.

## Further remarks {#Further_remarks}

If the multiplication is associative, an element $x$ with a multiplicative inverse cannot be a [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441) ($x$ is a zero divisor if some nonzero $y$, $xy = 0$). To see this, it is sufficient to multiply the equation $xy = 0$ by the inverse of $x$ (on the left), and then simplify using associativity. In the absence of associativity, the [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434) provide a counterexample.

The converse does not hold: an element which is not a [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441) is not guaranteed to have a multiplicative inverse. Within $\mathbb{Z},$ all integers except $-1, 0, 1$ provide examples; they are not zero divisors nor do they have inverses in $\mathbb{Z}.$ If the ring or algebra is [finite](wikipedia://en/11742), however, then all elements $a$ which are not zero divisors do have a (left and right) inverse. For, first observe that the map $f(x) = ax$ must be [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective): $f(x) = f(y)$ implies $x = y$:

$$\begin{aligned} ax = ay && \Rightarrow & \quad ax-ay = 0 \\ && \Rightarrow &\quad a(x-y) = 0 \\ && \Rightarrow &\quad x-y = 0 \\ && \Rightarrow &\quad x = y. \end{aligned}$$

Distinct elements map to distinct elements, so the image consists of the same finite number of elements, and the map is necessarily [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective). Specifically, $f$ (namely multiplication by $a$) must map some element $x$ to $1$, $ax = 1$, so that $x$ is an inverse for $a$.

## Applications {#Applications}

The expansion of the reciprocal $\tfrac{1}{q}$ in any base can also act[^o14] as a source of [pseudo-random numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-random_numbers), if $q$ is a "suitable" [safe prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_prime), a prime of the form $2p + 1$ where $p$ is also a prime. A sequence of pseudo-random numbers of length $q - 1$ will be produced by the expansion.

:::
