# Delta operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Delta operator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **delta operator** is a shift-equivariant [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $Q\colon\mathbb{K}[x] \longrightarrow \mathbb{K}[x]$ on the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in a variable $x$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb{K}$ that reduces [degrees](wikipedia://en/5930652) by one.

:::

To say that $Q$ is **shift-equivariant** means that if $g(x) = f(x + a)$, then

$${ (Qg)(x) = (Qf)(x + a)}.\,$$

In other words, if $f$ is a "shift" of $g$, then $Qf$ is also a shift of $Qg$, and has the same "shifting vector" $a$.

To say that an operator *reduces degree by one* means that if $f$ is a polynomial of degree $n$, then $Qf$ is either a polynomial of degree $n-1$, or, in case $n = 0$, $Qf$ is 0.

Sometimes a *delta operator* is defined to be a shift-equivariant linear transformation on polynomials in $x$ that maps $x$ to a nonzero constant. Seemingly weaker than the definition given above, this latter characterization can be shown to be equivalent to the stated definition when $\mathbb{K}$ has [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero, since shift-equivariance is a fairly strong condition.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The forward [difference operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_operator)

$$(\Delta f)(x) = f(x + 1) - f(x)\,$$

is a delta operator.

- [Differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) with respect to *x*, written as *D*, is also a delta operator.
- Any operator of the form

$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty c_k D^k$$

(where *D*<sup>*n*</sup>(ƒ) = ƒ<sup>(*n*)</sup> is the *n*<sup>th</sup> [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921)) with $c_1\neq0$ is a delta operator. It can be shown that all delta operators can be written in this form. For example, the difference operator given above can be expanded as

$$\Delta=e^D-1=\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{D^k}{k!}.$$

- The generalized derivative of [time scale calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_scale_calculus) which unifies the forward difference operator with the derivative of [standard calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) is a delta operator.
- In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) and [cybernetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics), the term "discrete-time delta operator" (δ) is generally taken to mean a difference operator

$${(\delta f)(x) = {{ f(x+\Delta t) - f(x) } \over {\Delta t} }},$$

the [Euler approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_approximation) of the usual derivative with a discrete sample time $\Delta t$. The delta-formulation obtains a significant number of numerical advantages compared to the shift-operator at fast sampling.

:::

## Basic polynomials {#Basic_polynomials}

Every delta operator *$Q$* has a unique sequence of "basic polynomials", a [polynomial sequence](wikipedia://en/195990) defined by three conditions:

- $$p_0(x)=1 ;$$
- $$p_{n}(0)=0;$$
- $$(Qp_n)(x)=np_{n-1}(x) \text{ for all } n \in \mathbb N.$$

Such a sequence of basic polynomials is always of [binomial type](wikipedia://en/200077), and it can be shown that no other sequences of binomial type exist. If the first two conditions above are dropped, then the third condition says this polynomial sequence is a [Sheffer sequence](wikipedia://en/218596)—a more general concept.
