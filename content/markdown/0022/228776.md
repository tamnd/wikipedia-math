# Indicative conditional

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Indicative conditional"}

An **indicative conditional** is a natural-language [conditional sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence) (an "if" sentence) used to talk about what may actually be the case, as in: "*If Leona is at home, she isn't in Paris.*" Indicatives are commonly contrasted with [counterfactual conditionals](wikipedia://en/491578), which typically bear special grammatical marking (e.g., "would have") and are used to discuss ways things might have been but are not.

:::

Indicative conditionals are central in [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939) (especially [conditional logic)](wikipedia://en/81395508), and [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics). Debates concern (i) what semantic value, if any, such conditionals have; (ii) how their contribution composes with surrounding material; and (iii) how competing accounts explain observed patterns of assertion, reasoning, and embedding. Prominent proposals include truth-functional analyses, pragmatics-augmented accounts, probabilistic ("suppositional") approaches, possible-worlds semantics, and restrictor treatments of *if*.

## Scope and classification {#Scope_and_classification}

Many authors reserve "indicative" for conditionals whose matrix clause is in the indicative mood (e.g., with *is*, *will*), in contrast to counterfactuals (with *would*). Others argue that some "future-open" indicatives pattern more like counterfactuals. Despite disagreements in classification, there is broad consensus that everyday "if A, B" claims used to guide belief and action are a distinctive target for theory.[^o3]

## Competing theories {#Competing_theories}

### Material conditional and its limitations {#Material_conditional_and_its_limitations}

Early formal work identified natural-language indicatives with the truth-functional [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808): "*If A then B*" is false only in the case *A ∧ ¬B* and otherwise true (equivalently *¬A ∨ B*). This analysis validates familiar inferences (e.g., [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900)), but faces well-known "paradoxes of material implication": with a true consequent (*B*) or false antecedent (*A*), any "*if A, B*" comes out true—even when *A* and *B* are intuitively unrelated.[^o6]

#### Gricean and assertability responses {#Gricean_and_assertability_responses}

A classic response (inspired by [H. P. Grice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Grice)) keeps the material truth conditions but explains everyday resistance via [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics): speakers are expected to make the strongest, most informative appropriate assertion; when one knows ¬A, asserting "*If A, B*" can be true yet misleading.[^o8] Others (notably Jackson) supplement material truth with special rules of [assertability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assertability) keyed to how robustly one would continue to accept *B* upon learning *A* (often cashed out as high [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) *P(B|A)*).[^o9] Critics argue that many tensions arise at the level of [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief) and probability, not merely assertion norms.[^o3]

### Suppositional / probabilistic theories {#Suppositional_/_probabilistic_theories}

The [Ramsey test](wikipedia://en/44316374) holds that to assess "*if A, B*" one should *suppose A* and then evaluate *B* under that supposition. Developed by Ernest W. Adams, the suppositional view takes the degree of belief in "*if A, B*" to be *P(B|A)* and offers a probabilistic account of valid inference (arguments are "good" when they preserve or suitably constrain probability).[^o11] This explains why many everyday inferences (e.g., [modus tollens](wikipedia://en/18901) with high but sub-certain premises) can be risky, and why rules like *strengthening the antecedent* and *transitivity* often fail in practice.[^o3]

A challenge for propositional semantics is [Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lewis_%28philosopher%29)'s "triviality" results: in general there is no proposition ⟦A⇒B⟧ whose probability always equals *P(B|A)*. This pressures the idea that indicative conditionals are standard truth-evaluable propositions with classical truth conditions.[^o12]

### Possible-worlds and strict-style accounts {#Possible-worlds_and_strict-style_accounts}

[Robert Stalnaker](wikipedia://en/4050095) proposed that "*if A, B*" is true at a world *w* just in case *B* holds at the contextually *nearest* (most similar) *A*-world to *w*; for indicatives, conversational context constrains which worlds count as live possibilities.[^o14] Such accounts vindicate many Adams-style patterns but face issues about similarity metrics, uniqueness of nearest worlds, and probabilistic judgments (e.g., lottery-like "short straw" cases). Context-dependent [strict conditional](wikipedia://en/663772) views and the influential "*if* as a [restrictor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restrictor) of modals" approach (Kratzer) treat *if* not as a binary connective but as narrowing the domain of a modal/quantifier; "bare" conditionals are often analyzed as containing an unpronounced epistemic necessity operator.[^o15]

### Dynamic, multidimensional, and related semantics {#Dynamic,_multidimensional,_and_related_semantics}

To reconcile probabilistic behavior with compositional embedding, several frameworks treat conditionals as non-classical contents. One line (de Finetti; later, Jeffrey, van Fraassen) models "*if A, B*" as a three-valued or random-variable-like object whose expectation equals *P(B|A)*; another (Bradley) represents conditionals via ordered pairs/tuples of worlds encoding both the actual state and the "potential A-state," enabling truth conditions for many embeddings while preserving the *P(B|A)* link.[^o17][^o18]

### Embedding and notable problems {#Embedding_and_notable_problems}

Indicatives embedded under negation, disjunction, or in antecedents raise hard questions for all theories (e.g., the [import–export](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Import–export_%28logic%29) principle relating "*if A and B, C*" to "*if A, if B, C*"). [Vann McGee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vann_McGee)'s "election" example challenges [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900) for certain nested indicatives if their readings shift across embeddings; different frameworks diagnose the phenomenon differently (scope/reading shifts vs. probabilistic risk vs. ambiguity).[^o20][^o21]

### Heuristics vs. semantics {#Heuristics_vs._semantics}

Some authors separate our fast, reliable-enough [heuristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristics) for evaluating conditionals (Ramsey-Adams suppositional reasoning) from their underlying "semantic" treatment (e.g., material truth conditions that rationalize long-run practice). Tensions remain because material truth values often overstate the probability of conditionals with unlikely antecedents (by *P(¬A) + P(A)·P(B|A)*).[^o23][^o3]

## Psychology of reasoning {#Psychology_of_reasoning}

Experimental work on indicatives, causal conditionals and counterfactuals finds robust endorsement of [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900); rates for [modus tollens](wikipedia://en/18901) are variable and context-sensitive, improving under causal or counterfactual formulations and with enriched background knowledge.[^o25][^o26][^o27] Probabilistic ("suppositional") accounts have been argued to align closely with observed reasoning patterns, especially where participants condition on the antecedent and assess the likelihood of the consequent.[^o3]
