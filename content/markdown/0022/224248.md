# Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem

*Image: Triangledissection.svg.* By the Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem, a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) can be cut into parts and rearranged into a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) of equal area.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem**,[^o2] named after [William Wallace](wikipedia://en/16955407), [Farkas Bolyai](wikipedia://en/1159131) and [P. Gerwien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._Gerwien), is a theorem related to [dissections](wikipedia://en/2915839) of [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621). It answers the question of whether one polygon can be formed from another by cutting it into a finite number of pieces and recomposing these by [translations](wikipedia://en/204682) and [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789). The Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem states that this can be done [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) two polygons have the same [area](wikipedia://en/1209).

:::

[Wallace](wikipedia://en/16955407) had proven the same result already in 1807.

According to other sources, Bolyai and Gerwien had independently proved the theorem in 1833 and 1835, respectively.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

There are several ways in which this theorem may be formulated. The most common version uses the concept of "equidecomposability" of polygons: two polygons are equidecomposable if they can be split into [finitely many](wikipedia://en/11742) [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) that only differ by some [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) (in fact only by a combination of a translation and a rotation). In this case the Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem states that two polygons are equidecomposable if and only if they have the same area.

Another formulation is in terms of **scissors congruence**: two polygons are scissors-congruent if they can be decomposed into finitely many polygons that are pairwise [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330). Scissors-congruence is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). In this case the Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem states that the [equivalence classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_classes) of this relation contain precisely those polygons that have the same area.

:::

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proof sketch"}

## Proof sketch {#Proof_sketch}

The theorem can be understood in a few steps. Firstly, every polygon can be cut into triangles. There are a few methods for this. For [convex polygons](wikipedia://en/660019) one can cut off each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) in turn, while for [concave polygons](wikipedia://en/605156) this requires more care. A general approach that works for non-simple polygons as well would be to choose a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) not parallel to any of the sides of the polygon and draw a line parallel to this one through each of the vertices of the polygon. This will divide the polygon into triangles and [trapezoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trapezoids), which in turn can be converted into triangles.

Secondly, each of these triangles can be transformed into a right triangle and subsequently into a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) with one side of length 1. Alternatively, a triangle can be transformed into one such rectangle by first turning it into a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) and then turning this into such a rectangle. By doing this for each triangle, the polygon can be decomposed into a rectangle with unit width and height equal to its area.

Since this can be done for any two polygons, a "common subdivision" of the rectangle in between proves the theorem. That is, cutting the common rectangle (of size 1 by its area) according to both polygons will be an intermediate between both polygons.

:::

## Notes about the proof {#Notes_about_the_proof}

First of all, this proof requires an intermediate polygon. In the formulation of the theorem using scissors-congruence, the use of this intermediate can be reformulated by using the fact that scissor-congruences are transitive. Since both the first polygon and the second polygon are scissors-congruent to the intermediate, they are scissors-congruent to one another.

The proof of this theorem is constructive and doesn't require the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), even though some other dissection problems (e.g. [Tarski's circle-squaring problem](wikipedia://en/216811)) do need it. In this case, the decomposition and reassembly can actually be carried out "physically": the pieces can, in theory, be [cut with scissors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve) from paper and reassembled by hand.

Nonetheless, the number of pieces required to compose one polygon from another using this procedure generally far exceeds the minimum number of polygons needed.[^o8]

## Degree of decomposability {#Degree_of_decomposability}

Consider two equidecomposable polygons *P* and *Q*. The minimum number *n* of pieces required to compose one polygon *Q* from another polygon *P* is denoted by σ(*P*,*Q*).

Depending on the polygons, it is possible to estimate upper and lower bounds for σ(*P*,*Q*). For instance, [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) proved that if *P* is convex and the [diameters](wikipedia://en/68283333) of *P* and *Q* are respectively given by d(*P*) and d(*Q*), then[^o10]

$$\sigma(P,Q) \ge \frac{d(P)}{d(Q)}.$$

If *P<sub>x</sub>* is a rectangle of sides *a*·*x* and *a*·(1/*x*) and *Q* is a square of side length *a*, then *P<sub>x</sub>* and *Q* are equidecomposable for every *x* > 0. An upper bound for σ(*P<sub>x</sub>*,*Q*) is given by[^o10]

$$\sigma(P_x,Q) \le 2 + \left\lceil \sqrt{x^2 - 1} \right\rceil, \quad\text{for } x \ge 1.$$

Since σ(*P<sub>x</sub>*,*Q*) = σ(*P*<sub>(1/*x*)</sub>,*Q*), we also have that

$$\sigma\left(P_\frac{1}{x},Q\right) \le 2 + \left\lceil \frac{\sqrt{1-x^2}}{x} \right\rceil, \quad\text{for } x \le 1.$$

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

The analogous statement about [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) in three dimensions, known as [Hilbert's third problem](wikipedia://en/152703), is false, as proven by [Max Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602) in 1900. The problem has also been considered in some [non-Euclidean geometries](wikipedia://en/58610). In two-dimensional [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291) and [spherical](wikipedia://en/174026) geometry, the theorem holds. However, the problem is still open for these geometries in three dimensions.
