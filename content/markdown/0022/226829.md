# Four-velocity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Four-velocity"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), in particular in [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) and [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), a **four-velocity** is a [four-vector](wikipedia://en/226424) in four-dimensional [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758)[^o2] that represents the relativistic counterpart of [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903), which is a [three-dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956) in space.

:::

Physical [events](wikipedia://en/24651576) correspond to mathematical points in time and space, the set of all of them together forming a mathematical model of physical four-dimensional spacetime. The history of an object traces a curve in spacetime, called its [world line](wikipedia://en/165487). If the object has [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_in_special_relativity), so that its speed is necessarily less than the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), the world line may be [parametrized](wikipedia://en/453198) by the [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) of the object. The four-velocity is the rate of change of [four-position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-position) with respect to the proper time along the curve. The velocity, in contrast, is the rate of change of the position in (three-dimensional) space of the object, as seen by an observer, with respect to the observer's time.

The value of the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301#Pseudo-Euclidean_space) of an object's four-velocity, i.e. the quantity obtained by applying the [metric tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_tensor_%28general_relativity%29) $g$ to the four-velocity $\mathbf{U}$, that is $\lVert \mathbf{U}\rVert^{2} = \mathbf{U} \cdot \mathbf{U} = g_{\mu\nu}U^{\nu}U^{\mu}$, is always equal to $\pm c^{2}$, where $c$ is the speed of light. Whether the plus or minus sign applies depends on the choice of [metric signature](wikipedia://en/463225). For an object at rest its four-velocity is parallel to the direction of the time coordinate with $U^{0} = c$. A four-velocity is thus the normalized future-directed timelike tangent vector to a world line, and is a [contravariant vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_vector). Though it is a vector, addition of two four-velocities does not yield a four-velocity: the space of four-velocities is not itself a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).[^o3]

## Velocity {#Velocity}

The path of an object in three-dimensional space (in an inertial frame) may be expressed in terms of three spatial coordinate functions $x^{i}(t)$ of time $t$, where $i$ is an [index](wikipedia://en/320026) which takes values 1, 2, 3.

The three coordinates form the 3d [position vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_vector), written as a [column vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector)

$$\vec{x}(t) = \begin{bmatrix} x^1(t) \\[0.7ex] x^2(t) \\[0.7ex] x^3(t) \end{bmatrix} \, .$$

The components of the velocity $\vec{u}$ (tangent to the curve) at any point on the world line are

$$\vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u^1 \\ u^2 \\ u^3 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{d \vec{x}}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{dx^1}{dt} \\ \tfrac{dx^2}{dt} \\ \tfrac{dx^3}{dt} \end{bmatrix}.$$

Each component is simply written

$$u^i = \frac{dx^i}{dt}$$

## Theory of relativity {#Theory_of_relativity}

In [Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein)'s [theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/30001), the path of an object moving relative to a particular frame of reference is defined by four coordinate functions $x^{\mu}(\tau)$, where $\mu$ is a spacetime index which takes the value 0 for the timelike component, and 1, 2, 3 for the spacelike coordinates. The zeroth component is defined as the time coordinate multiplied by $c$,

$$x^0 = ct\,,$$

Each function depends on one parameter *τ* called its [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418). As a column vector,

$$\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x^0(\tau) \\ x^1(\tau) \\ x^2(\tau) \\ x^3(\tau) \\ \end{bmatrix}\,.$$

### Time dilation {#Time_dilation}

From [time dilation](wikipedia://en/297839), the [differentials](wikipedia://en/23997203) in [coordinate time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_time) $t$ and [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) $\tau$ are related by

$$dt = \gamma(u) d\tau$$

where the [Lorentz factor](wikipedia://en/732446),

$$\gamma(u) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{u^2}{c^2}}}\,,$$

is a function of the [Euclidean norm](wikipedia://en/990534#Euclidean_norm) $u$ of the 3d velocity vector $\vec{u}$:

$$u = \left\|\ \vec{u}\ \right\| = \sqrt{ \left(u^1\right)^2 + \left(u^2\right)^2 + \left(u^3\right)^2} \,.$$

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition of the four-velocity"}

### Definition of the four-velocity {#Definition_of_the_four-velocity}

The four-velocity is the tangent four-vector of a [timelike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timelike_curve) [world line](wikipedia://en/165487). The four-velocity $\mathbf{U}$ at any point of world line $\mathbf{X}(\tau)$ is defined as:

$$\mathbf{U} = \frac{d\mathbf{X}}{d \tau}$$

where $\mathbf{X}$ is the [four-position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-position) and $\tau$ is the [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418).[^o9]

The four-velocity defined here using the proper time of an object does not exist for world lines for massless objects such as photons travelling at the speed of light; nor is it defined for [tachyonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tachyon) world lines, where the tangent vector is [spacelike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacelike).

:::

### Components of the four-velocity {#Components_of_the_four-velocity}

The relationship between the time $t$ and the coordinate time $x^{0}$ is defined by

$$x^0 = ct .$$

Taking the derivative of this with respect to the proper time $\tau$, we find the $U^{\mu}$ velocity component for $\mu = 0$:

$$U^0 = \frac{dx^0}{d\tau} = \frac{d(ct)}{d\tau} = c\frac{dt}{d\tau} = c \gamma(u)$$

and for the other 3 components to proper time we get the $U^{\mu}$ velocity component for $\mu = 1, 2, 3$:

$$U^i = \frac{dx^i}{d\tau} = \frac{dx^i}{dt} \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{dx^i}{dt} \gamma(u) = \gamma(u) u^i$$

where we have used the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) and the relationships

$$u^i = {dx^i \over dt } \,,\quad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \gamma (u)$$

Thus, we find for the four-velocity $\mathbf{U}$:

$$\mathbf{U} = \gamma \begin{bmatrix} c \\ \vec{u} \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

Written in standard four-vector notation this is:

$$\mathbf{U} = \gamma \left(c, \vec{u}\right) = \left(\gamma c, \gamma \vec{u}\right)$$

where $\gamma c$ is the temporal component and $\gamma \vec{u}$ is the spatial component.

In terms of the synchronized clocks and rulers associated with a particular slice of flat spacetime, the three spacelike components of four-velocity define a traveling object's [proper velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_velocity) $\gamma \vec{u} = d\vec{x} / d\tau$ i.e. the rate at which distance is covered in the reference map frame per unit [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) elapsed on clocks traveling with the object.

Unlike most other four-vectors, the four-velocity has only 3 independent components $u_x, u_y, u_z$ instead of 4. The $\gamma$ factor is a function of the three-dimensional velocity $\vec{u}$.

When certain Lorentz scalars are multiplied by the four-velocity, one then gets new physical four-vectors that have 4 independent components.

For example:

- [Four-momentum](wikipedia://en/204680): $$\mathbf{P} = m_o\mathbf{U} = \gamma m_o\left(c, \vec{u}\right) = m\left(c, \vec{u}\right) = \left(mc, m\vec{u}\right) = \left(mc, \vec{p}\right) = \left(\frac{E}{c},\vec{p}\right),$$ where $m_o$ is the [rest mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_Mass)
- [Four-current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-current): $$\mathbf{J} = \rho_o\mathbf{U} = \gamma \rho_o\left(c, \vec{u}\right) = \rho\left(c, \vec{u}\right) = \left(\rho c, \rho\vec{u}\right) = \left(\rho c, \vec{j}\right) ,$$ where $\rho_o$ is the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density)

Effectively, the $\gamma$ factor combines with the Lorentz scalar term to make the 4th independent component

$$m = \gamma m_o$$

and

$$\rho = \gamma \rho_o.$$

### Magnitude {#Magnitude}

Using the differential of the four-position in the rest frame, the magnitude of the four-velocity can be obtained by the [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric) with signature $(-, +, +, +)$:

$$\left\|\mathbf{U}\right\|^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu} U^\mu U^\nu = \eta_{\mu\nu} \frac{dX^\mu}{d\tau} \frac{dX^\nu}{d\tau} = - c^2 \,,$$

in short, the magnitude of the four-velocity for any object is always a fixed constant:

$$\left\|\mathbf{U}\right\|^2 = - c^2$$

In a moving frame, the same norm is:

$$\left\|\mathbf{U}\right\|^2 = {\gamma(u)}^2 \left( - c^2 + \vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} \right) ,$$

so that:

$$- c^2 = {\gamma(u)}^2 \left( - c^2 + \vec{u} \cdot \vec{u} \right) ,$$

which reduces to the definition of the Lorentz factor.
