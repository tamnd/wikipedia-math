# Accumulation point

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Accumulation point"}

In mathematics, a **limit point**, **accumulation point**, or **cluster point** of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ in a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is a point $x$ that can be "approximated" by points of $S$ in the sense that every [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $x$ contains a point of $S$ other than $x$ itself. A limit point of a set $S$ does not itself have to be an element of $S.$ There is also a closely related concept for [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838). A **cluster point** or **accumulation point** of a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ is a point $x$ such that, for every neighbourhood $V$ of $x,$ there are infinitely many natural numbers $n$ such that $x_n \in V.$ This definition of a cluster or accumulation point of a sequence generalizes to [nets](wikipedia://en/22170) and [filters](wikipedia://en/12646549).

:::

The similarly named notion of a [limit point of a sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point_of_a_sequence)[^n1] (respectively, a [limit point of a filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point_of_a_filter),[^n2] a [limit point of a net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point_of_a_net)) by definition refers to a point that the [sequence converges to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_sequence) (respectively, the [filter converges to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_filter), the [net converges to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_net)). Importantly, although "limit point of a set" is synonymous with "cluster/accumulation point of a set", this is not true for sequences (nor nets or filters). That is, the term "limit point of a sequence" is not synonymous with "cluster/accumulation point of a sequence".

The limit points of a set should not be confused with [adherent points](wikipedia://en/8140539) (also called points of [closure](wikipedia://en/42309)) for which every neighbourhood of $x$ contains *some* point of $S$. Unlike for limit points, an adherent point $x$ of $S$ may have a neighbourhood not containing points other than $x$ itself. A limit point can be characterized as an adherent point that is not an [isolated point](wikipedia://en/390557).

Limit points of a set should also not be confused with [boundary points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_point). For example, $0$ is a boundary point (but not a limit point) of the set $\{0\}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ with [standard topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_topology). However, $0.5$ is a limit point (though not a boundary point) of interval $[0, 1]$ in $\mathbb{R}$ with standard topology (for a less trivial example of a limit point, see the first caption).[^o4][^o5][^o6]

This concept profitably generalizes the notion of a [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) and is the underpinning of concepts such as [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) and [topological closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_closure). Indeed, a set is closed if and only if it contains all of its limit points, and the topological closure operation can be thought of as an operation that enriches a set by uniting it with its limit points.

*Image: Rational sequence with 2 accumulation points.svg.* With respect to the usual [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/30450#Examples_of_topological_spaces), the sequence of rational numbers $x_n=(-1)^n \frac{n}{n+1}$ has no [limit](wikipedia://en/285773#Topological_spaces) (i.e. does not converge), but has two accumulation points (which are considered limit points here), viz. -1 and +1. Thus, thinking of sets, these points are limit points of the set $S = \{x_n\}.$

## Definition {#Definition}

### Accumulation points of a set {#Accumulation_points_of_a_set}

*Image: Diagonal argument.svg.* A sequence enumerating all positive [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). Each positive [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) is a cluster point.

Let $S$ be a subset of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X.$ A point $x$ in $X$ is a **limit point** or **cluster point** or **accumulation point of the set** $S$ if every [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $x$ contains at least one point of $S$ different from $x$ itself.

It does not make a difference if we restrict the condition to open neighbourhoods only. It is often convenient to use the "open neighbourhood" form of the definition to show that a point is a limit point and to use the "general neighbourhood" form of the definition to derive facts from a known limit point.

If $X$ is a [$T_1$ space](wikipedia://en/148721) (such as a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018)), then $x \in X$ is a limit point of $S$ if and only if every neighbourhood of $x$ contains infinitely many points of $S.$[^n3] In fact, $T_1$ spaces are characterized by this property.

If $X$ is a [Fréchet–Urysohn space](wikipedia://en/28480560) (which all [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) and [first-countable spaces](wikipedia://en/904940) are), then $x \in X$ is a limit point of $S$ if and only if there is a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of points in $S \setminus \{x\}$ whose [limit](wikipedia://en/285773) is $x.$ In fact, Fréchet–Urysohn spaces are characterized by this property.

The set of limit points of $S$ is called the [derived set](wikipedia://en/1567335) of $S.$

#### Special types of accumulation point of a set {#Special_types_of_accumulation_point_of_a_set}

If every neighbourhood of $x$ contains infinitely many points of $S,$ then $x$ is a specific type of limit point called an **ω-accumulation point** of $S.$

If every neighbourhood of $x$ contains [uncountably many](wikipedia://en/32248) points of $S,$ then $x$ is a specific type of limit point called a **[condensation point](wikipedia://en/19472545)** of $S.$

If every neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ is such that the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of $U \cap S$ equals the cardinality of $S,$ then $x$ is a specific type of limit point called a **complete accumulation point** of $S.$

### Accumulation points of sequences and nets {#Accumulation_points_of_sequences_and_nets}

In a topological space $X,$ a point $x \in X$ is said to be a **cluster point of a sequence** or **accumulation point of a sequence** $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ if, for every [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $V$ of $x,$ there are infinitely many $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $x_n \in V.$ It is equivalent to say that for every neighbourhood $V$ of $x$ and every $n_0 \in \mathbb{N},$ there is some $n \geq n_0$ such that $x_n \in V.$ If $X$ is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) or a [first-countable space](wikipedia://en/904940) (or, more generally, a [Fréchet–Urysohn space](wikipedia://en/28480560)), then $x$ is a cluster point of $x_{\bullet}$ if and only if $x$ is a limit of some subsequence of $x_{\bullet}.$ The set of all cluster points of a sequence is sometimes called the [limit set](wikipedia://en/5492198).

Note that there is already the notion of [limit of a sequence](wikipedia://en/285773#Topological_spaces) to mean a point $x$ to which the sequence converges (that is, every neighborhood of $x$ contains all but finitely many elements of the sequence). That is why we do not use the term limit point of a sequence as a synonym for accumulation point of the sequence.

The concept of a [net](wikipedia://en/22170) generalizes the idea of a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838). A net is a function $f : (P,\leq) \to X,$ where $(P,\leq)$ is a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165) and $X$ is a topological space. A point $x \in X$ is said to be a [**cluster point of a net**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster_point_of_a_net) or [**accumulation point of a net**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accumulation_point_of_a_net) $f$ if, for every [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $V$ of $x$ and every $p_0 \in P,$ there is some $p \geq p_0$ such that $f(p) \in V,$ equivalently, if $f$ has a [subnet](wikipedia://en/12687840) which converges to $x.$ Cluster points in nets encompass the idea of both condensation points and ω-accumulation points. [Clustering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster_point_of_a_filter) and [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point_of_a_filter) are also defined for [filters](wikipedia://en/12646549).

:::

## Relation between accumulation point of a sequence and accumulation point of a set {#Relation_between_accumulation_point_of_a_sequence_and_accumulation_point_of_a_set}

Every sequence $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ in $X$ is by definition just a map $x_{\bullet} : \mathbb{N} \to X$ so that its [image](wikipedia://en/579311) $\operatorname{Im} x_{\bullet} := \left\{ x_n : n \in \mathbb{N} \right\}$ can be defined in the usual way.

- If there exists an element $x \in X$ that occurs infinitely many times in the sequence, $x$ is an accumulation point of the sequence. But $x$ need not be an accumulation point of the corresponding set $\operatorname{Im} x_{\bullet}.$ For example, if the sequence is the constant sequence with value $x,$ we have $\operatorname{Im} x_{\bullet} = \{ x \}$ and $x$ is an isolated point of $\operatorname{Im} x_{\bullet}$ and not an accumulation point of $\operatorname{Im} x_{\bullet}.$
- If no element occurs infinitely many times in the sequence, for example if all the elements are distinct, any accumulation point of the sequence is an $\omega$-accumulation point of the associated set $\operatorname{Im} x_{\bullet}.$

Conversely, given a countable infinite set $A \subseteq X$ in $X,$ we can enumerate all the elements of $A$ in many ways, even with repeats, and thus associate with it many sequences $x_{\bullet}$ that will satisfy $A = \operatorname{Im} x_{\bullet}.$

- Any $\omega$-accumulation point of $A$ is an accumulation point of any of the corresponding sequences (because any neighborhood of the point will contain infinitely many elements of $A$ and hence also infinitely many terms in any associated sequence).
- A point $x \in X$ that is not an $\omega$-accumulation point of $A$ cannot be an accumulation point of any of the associated sequences without infinite repeats (because $x$ has a neighborhood that contains only finitely many (possibly even none) points of $A$ and that neighborhood can only contain finitely many terms of such sequences).

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Every [limit](wikipedia://en/285773#Topological_spaces) of a non-constant sequence is an accumulation point of the sequence. And by definition, every limit point is an [adherent point](wikipedia://en/8140539).

The closure $\operatorname{cl}(S)$ of a set $S$ is a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of its limit points $L(S)$ and isolated points $I(S)$; that is,

$$\operatorname{cl} (S) = L(S) \cup I(S)\quad\text{and}\quad L(S) \cap I(S) = \emptyset.$$

A point $x \in X$ is a limit point of $S \subseteq X$ if and only if it is in the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of $S \setminus \{ x \}.$

::: {#o16 .proof cue=math-proof}

We use the fact that a point is in the closure of a set if and only if every neighborhood of the point meets the set. Now, $x$ is a limit point of $S,$ if and only if every neighborhood of $x$ contains a point of $S$ other than $x,$ if and only if every neighborhood of $x$ contains a point of $S \setminus \{x\},$ if and only if $x$ is in the closure of $S \setminus \{x\}.$

:::

If we use $L(S)$ to denote the set of limit points of $S,$ then we have the following characterization of the closure of $S$: The closure of $S$ is equal to the union of $S$ and $L(S).$ This fact is sometimes taken as the definition of [closure](wikipedia://en/42309).

::: {#o17 .proof cue=math-proof}

("Left subset") Suppose $x$ is in the closure of $S.$ If $x$ is in $S,$ we are done. If $x$ is not in $S,$ then every neighbourhood of $x$ contains a point of $S,$ and this point cannot be $x.$ In other words, $x$ is a limit point of $S$ and $x$ is in $L(S).$

("Right subset") If $x$ is in $S,$ then every neighbourhood of $x$ clearly meets $S,$ so $x$ is in the closure of $S.$ If $x$ is in $L(S),$ then every neighbourhood of $x$ contains a point of $S$ (other than $x$), so $x$ is again in the closure of $S.$ This completes the proof.

:::

A corollary of this result gives us a characterisation of closed sets: A set $S$ is closed if and only if it contains all of its limit points.

::: {#o18 .proof cue=math-proof}

*Proof* 1: $S$ is closed if and only if $S$ is equal to its closure if and only if $S=S\cup L(S)$ if and only if $L(S)$ is contained in $S.$

*Proof* 2: Let $S$ be a closed set and $x$ a limit point of $S.$ If $x$ is not in $S,$ then the complement to $S$ comprises an open neighbourhood of $x.$ Since $x$ is a limit point of $S,$ any open neighbourhood of $x$ should have a non-trivial intersection with $S.$ However, a set can not have a non-trivial intersection with its complement. Conversely, assume $S$ contains all its limit points. We shall show that the complement of $S$ is an open set. Let $x$ be a point in the complement of $S.$ By assumption, $x$ is not a limit point, and hence there exists an open neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ that does not intersect $S,$ and so $U$ lies entirely in the complement of $S.$ Since this argument holds for arbitrary $x$ in the complement of $S,$ the complement of $S$ can be expressed as a union of open neighbourhoods of the points in the complement of $S.$ Hence the complement of $S$ is open.

:::

No [isolated point](wikipedia://en/390557) is a limit point of any set.

::: {#o19 .proof cue=math-proof}

If $x$ is an isolated point, then $\{x\}$ is a neighbourhood of $x$ that contains no points other than $x.$

:::

A space $X$ is [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) if and only if no subset of $X$ has a limit point.

::: {#o20 .proof cue=math-proof}

If $X$ is discrete, then every point is isolated and cannot be a limit point of any set. Conversely, if $X$ is not discrete, then there is a singleton $\{ x \}$ that is not open. Hence, every open neighbourhood of $\{ x \}$ contains a point $y \neq x,$ and so $x$ is a limit point of $X.$

:::

If a space $X$ has the [trivial topology](wikipedia://en/382447) and $S$ is a subset of $X$ with more than one element, then all elements of $X$ are limit points of $S.$ If $S$ is a singleton, then every point of $X \setminus S$ is a limit point of $S.$

::: {#o21 .proof cue=math-proof}

As long as $S \setminus \{ x \}$ is nonempty, its closure will be $X.$ It is only empty when $S$ is empty or $x$ is the unique element of $S.$

:::
