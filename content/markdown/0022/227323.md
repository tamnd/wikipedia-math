# Wilson's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wilson's theorem"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Wilson's theorem** states that a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *n* > 1 is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the product of all the [positive integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) less than *n* is one less than a multiple of *n*. That is (using the notations of [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087)), the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) $(n - 1)! = 1 \times 2 \times 3 \times \cdots \times (n - 1)$ satisfies

:::

$$(n-1)!\ \equiv\; -1 \pmod n$$

exactly when *n* is a prime number. In other words, any integer *n* > 1 is a prime number if, and only if, (*n* − 1)! + 1 is divisible by *n*.[^o2]

## History {#History}

The theorem was first stated by [Ibn al-Haytham](wikipedia://en/1645) c. 1000 AD. [Edward Waring](wikipedia://en/9723) announced the theorem in 1770 without proving it, crediting his student [John Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilson_%28mathematician_and_judge%29) for the discovery.[^o5] [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) gave the first proof in 1771.[^o6] There is evidence that [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) was also aware of the result a century earlier, but never published it.[^o7]

## Proofs {#Proofs}

As a [biconditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biconditional) (if and only if) statement, the proof has two halves: to show that equality *does not* hold when $n$ is composite, and to show that it *does* hold when $n$ is prime.

### Composite modulus {#Composite_modulus}

Suppose that $n$ is composite. Therefore, it is divisible by some prime number $q$ where $2 \leq q < n$. Because $q$ divides $n$, there is an integer $k$ such that $n = qk$. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that $(n-1)!$ were [congruent](wikipedia://en/20087#Congruence) to $-1$ modulo ${n}$. Then $(n-1)!$ would also be congruent to $-1$ modulo ${q}$: indeed, if $(n-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod{n}$ then $(n-1)! = nm - 1 = (qk)m - 1 = q(km) - 1$ for some integer $m$, and consequently $(n-1)!$ is one less than a multiple of $q$. On the other hand, since $2 \leq q \leq n - 1$, one of the factors in the expanded product $(n - 1)! = (n - 1) \times (n - 2) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1$ is $q$. Therefore $(n - 1)! \equiv 0 \pmod{q}$. This is a contradiction; therefore it is not possible that $(n - 1)! \equiv -1\pmod{n}$ when $n$ is composite.

In fact, more is true. With the sole exception of the case $n = 4$, where $3! = 6 \equiv 2 \pmod{4}$, if $n$ is composite then $(n - 1)!$ is congruent to 0 modulo $n$. The proof can be divided into two cases: First, if $n$ can be factored as the product of two unequal numbers, $n = ab$, where $2 \leq a < b < n$, then both $a$ and $b$ will appear as factors in the product $(n - 1)! = (n - 1)\times (n - 2) \times \cdots \times 2 \times 1$ and so $(n - 1)!$ is divisible by $ab = n$. If $n$ has no such factorization, then it must be the square of some prime $q$ larger than 2. But then $2q < q^2 = n$, so both $q$ and $2q$ will be factors of $(n-1)!$, and so $n$ divides $(n-1)!$ in this case, as well.

### Prime modulus {#Prime_modulus}

The first two proofs below use the fact that the [residue classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residue_class) modulo a prime number form a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) (specifically, a [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field)).[^o15]

::: {#o17 .proof cue=heading name="Elementary proof"}

#### Elementary proof {#Elementary_proof}

The result is trivial when $p = 2$, so assume $p$ is an odd prime, $p \geq 3$. Since the residue classes modulo $p$ form a field, every non-zero residue $a$ has a unique multiplicative inverse $a^{-1}$. [Euclid's lemma](wikipedia://en/826617) implies that the only values of $a$ for which $a \equiv a^{-1}\pmod{p}$ are $a \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{p}$. Therefore, with the exception of $\pm 1$, the factors in the expanded form of $(p - 1)!$ can be arranged in disjoint pairs such that product of each pair is congruent to 1 modulo $p$. This proves Wilson's theorem.

For example, for $p = 11$, one has

$$10! = [(1\cdot10)]\cdot[(2\cdot6)(3\cdot4)(5\cdot9)(7\cdot8)] \equiv [-1]\cdot[1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1] \equiv -1 \pmod{11}.$$

:::

::: {#o19 .proof cue=heading name="Proof using Fermat's little theorem"}

#### Proof using Fermat's little theorem {#Proof_using_Fermat's_little_theorem}

Again, the result is trivial for *p* = 2, so suppose *p* is an odd prime, *p* ≥ 3. Consider the polynomial

$$g(x)=(x-1)(x-2) \cdots (x-(p-1)).$$

*g* has degree *p* − 1, leading term *x*<sup>*p* − 1</sup>, and constant term (*p* − 1)!. Its *p* − 1 roots are 1, 2, ..., *p* − 1.

Now consider

$$h(x)=x^{p-1}-1.$$

*h* also has degree *p* − 1 and leading term *x*<sup>*p* − 1</sup>. Modulo *p*, [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453) says it also has the same *p* − 1 roots, 1, 2, ..., *p* − 1.

Finally, consider

$$f(x)=g(x)-h(x).$$

*f* has degree at most *p* − 2 (since the leading terms cancel), and modulo *p* also has the *p* − 1 roots 1, 2, ..., *p* − 1. But [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/3281385) says it cannot have more than *p* − 2 roots. Therefore, *f* must be identically zero (mod *p*), so its constant term is (*p* − 1)! + 1 ≡ 0 (mod *p*). This is Wilson's theorem.

:::

::: {#o21 .proof cue=heading name="Proof using elementary Group Theory"}

#### Proof using elementary Group Theory {#Proof_using_elementary_Group_Theory}

Let *p* be an odd prime. Let $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ denote the [multiplicative group of integers modulo *p*](wikipedia://en/1195234), such that $(p-1)! \equiv \prod_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times} x \mod p$. Since $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327), there exists a [group isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) $\varphi: \mathbb{F}_p^\times \to \mathbb{Z}_{p-1}$, where $\mathbb{Z}_{p-1}$ is the [additive group of integers modulo p - 1](wikipedia://en/20087). Therefore:

$$\varphi\!\left(\prod_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times} x\right) = \sum_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times} \varphi(x) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}_{p-1}} n \equiv \frac{p(p-1)}{2} \mod (p-1)$$

Since *p* was an odd prime, $\frac{p(p-1)}{2}$ has order 2 inside $\mathbb{Z}_{p-1}$. Since isomorphisms preserve the orders of elements, it follows that $\varphi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\right) = \prod_{x \in \mathbb{F}_p^\times} x$ also has order exactly 2. The only such element in $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ is $p - 1$.

:::

::: {#o23 .proof cue=heading name="Proof using the Sylow theorems"}

#### Proof using the Sylow theorems {#Proof_using_the_Sylow_theorems}

It is possible to deduce Wilson's theorem from a particular application of the [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993). Let *p* be a prime. It is immediate to deduce that the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_p$ has exactly $(p-1)!$ elements of order *p*, namely the *p*-cycles $C_p$. On the other hand, each Sylow *p*-subgroup in $S_p$ is a copy of $C_p$. Hence it follows that the number of Sylow *p*-subgroups is $n_p=(p-2)!$. The third Sylow theorem implies

$$(p-2)! \equiv 1 \pmod p.$$

Multiplying both sides by (*p* − 1) gives

$$(p-1)! \equiv p-1 \equiv -1 \pmod p,$$

that is, the result.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Primality tests {#Primality_tests}

In practice, Wilson's theorem is useless as a [primality test](wikipedia://en/183751) because computing (*n* − 1)! modulo *n* for large *n* is [computationally complex](wikipedia://en/7543).[^o27]

### Quadratic residues {#Quadratic_residues}

Using Wilson's Theorem, for any odd prime *p* = 2*m* + 1, we can rearrange the left hand side of

$$1\cdot 2\cdots (p-1)\ \equiv\ -1\ \pmod{p}$$

to obtain the equality

$$1\cdot(p-1)\cdot 2\cdot (p-2)\cdots m\cdot (p-m)\ \equiv\ 1\cdot (-1)\cdot 2\cdot (-2)\cdots m\cdot (-m)\ \equiv\ -1 \pmod{p}.$$

This becomes

$$\prod_{j=1}^m\ j^2\ \equiv(-1)^{m+1} \pmod{p}$$

or

$$(m!)^2 \equiv(-1)^{m+1} \pmod{p}.$$

We can use this fact to prove part of a famous result: for any prime *p* such that [*p* ≡ 1 (mod 4)](wikipedia://en/2669448), the number (−1) is a square ([quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091)) mod *p*. For this, suppose *p* = 4*k* + 1 for some integer *k*. Then we can take *m* = 2*k* above, and we conclude that (*m*!)<sup>2</sup> is congruent to (−1) (mod *p*).

::: {#o30 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas for primes"}

### Formulas for primes {#Formulas_for_primes}

Wilson's theorem has been used to construct [formulas for primes](wikipedia://en/509009#Formulas_based_on_Wilson's_theorem), but they are too slow to have practical value.

:::

### p-adic gamma function {#p-adic_gamma_function}

Wilson's theorem allows one to define the [p-adic gamma function](wikipedia://en/33869124).

:::

## Gauss's generalization {#Gauss's_generalization}

[Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) proved[^o33][^o34] that

$$\prod_{k = 1 \atop \gcd(k,m)=1}^{m-1} \!\!k \ \equiv \begin{cases} -1 \pmod{m} & \text{if } m=4,\;p^\alpha,\;2p^\alpha \\ \;\;\,1 \pmod{m} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where *p* represents an odd prime and $\alpha$ a positive integer. That is, the product of the positive integers less than $m$ and relatively prime to $m$ is one less than a multiple of $m$ when $m$ is equal to 4, or a power of an odd prime, or twice a power of an odd prime; otherwise, the product is one more than a multiple of $m$. The values of *m* for which the product is −1 are precisely the ones where there is a [primitive root modulo *m*](wikipedia://en/186864).
