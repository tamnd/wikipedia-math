# Bernoulli polynomials

*Image: Bernoulli polynomials.svg.* Bernoulli polynomials

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bernoulli polynomials"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Bernoulli polynomials**, named after [Jacob Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/64740), combine the [Bernoulli numbers](wikipedia://en/4964) and [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668). They are used for [series expansion](wikipedia://en/1575813) of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), and with the [Euler–MacLaurin formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–MacLaurin_formula).

:::

These [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) occur in the study of many [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110) and, in particular, the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) and the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952). They are an [Appell sequence](wikipedia://en/1649947) (i.e. a [Sheffer sequence](wikipedia://en/218596) for the ordinary [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) operator). For the Bernoulli polynomials, the number of crossings of the *x*-axis in the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) does not go up with the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652). In the limit of large degree, they approach, when appropriately scaled, the [sine and cosine functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function).

A similar set of polynomials, based on a generating function, is the family of **Euler polynomials**.

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Representations"}

## Representations {#Representations}

The Bernoulli polynomials *B*<sub>*n*</sub> can be defined by a [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993). They also admit a variety of derived representations.

### Generating functions {#Generating_functions}

The [exponential generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_generating_function) for the Bernoulli polynomials is

$$\frac{t e^{xt}}{e^t-1}= \sum_{n=0}^\infty B_n(x) \frac{t^n}{n!}.$$

The exponential generating function for the Euler polynomials is

$$\frac{2 e^{xt}}{e^t+1}= \sum_{n=0}^\infty E_n(x) \frac{t^n}{n!}.$$

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Explicit formula"}

### Explicit formula {#Explicit_formula}

$$B_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} B_{n-k} x^k,$$

$$E_m(x)= \sum_{k=0}^m {m \choose k} \frac{E_k}{2^k} \left(x-\tfrac12\right)^{m-k} .$$

for $n \geq 0$, where $B_k$ are the [Bernoulli numbers](wikipedia://en/4964), and $E_k$ are the [Euler numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_numbers). It follows that $B_n(0) = B_n$ and $E_m\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\big) = \tfrac{1}{2^m} E_m$.

:::

### Representation by a differential operator {#Representation_by_a_differential_operator}

The Bernoulli polynomials are also given by

$$\ B_n(x) = \frac{ D }{\ e^D -1\ }\ x^n\$$

where $\ D \equiv \frac{ \mathrm{d} }{\ \mathrm{d} x\ }\$ is differentiation with respect to $x$ and the fraction is expanded as a [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012). It follows that

$$\ \int_a^x\ B_n(u)\ \mathrm{d}\ u = \frac{\ B_{n+1}(x) - B_{n+1}(a)\ }{ n + 1 } ~.$$

cf. below. By the same token, the Euler polynomials are given by

$$\ E_n(x) = \frac{ 2 }{\ e^D + 1\ }\ x^n ~.$$

### Representation by an integral operator {#Representation_by_an_integral_operator}

The Bernoulli polynomials are also the unique polynomials determined by

$$\int_x^{x+1} B_n(u)\,du = x^n.$$

The [integral transform](wikipedia://en/430912)

$$(Tf)(x) = \int_x^{x+1} f(u)\,du$$

on polynomials *f*, simply amounts to

$$\begin{aligned} (Tf)(x) = {e^D - 1 \over D}f(x) & {} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty {D^n \over (n+1)!}f(x) \\ & {} = f(x) + {f'(x) \over 2} + {f''(x) \over 6} + {f'''(x) \over 24} + \cdots . \end{aligned}$$

This can be used to produce the inversion formulae below.

### Integral recurrence {#Integral_recurrence}

In,[^o10][^o11] it is deduced and proved that the Bernoulli polynomials can be obtained by the following integral recurrence

$$B_{m}(x)=m \int_{0}^{x} B_{m-1}(t)\,dt-m\int_{0}^{1} \int_0^t B_{m-1}(s)\,ds dt.$$

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="Another explicit formula"}

### Another explicit formula {#Another_explicit_formula}

An explicit formula for the Bernoulli polynomials is given by

$$B_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \biggl[ \frac{1}{k + 1} \sum_{\ell=0}^k (-1)^\ell { k \choose \ell } (x + \ell)^n \biggr].$$

That is similar to the series expression for the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). Indeed, there is the relationship

$$B_n(x) = -n \zeta(1 - n,\,x)$$

where $\zeta(s,\,q)$ is the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952). The latter generalizes the Bernoulli polynomials, allowing for non-integer values of $n$.

The inner sum may be understood to be the $n$th [forward difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference) of $x^m,$ that is,

$$\Delta^n x^m = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^{n - k}{n \choose k}(x + k)^m$$

where $\Delta$ is the [forward difference operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference_operator). Thus, one may write

$$B_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{(-1)^k}{k + 1}\Delta^k x^n.$$

This formula may be derived from an identity appearing above as follows. Since the forward difference operator $\Delta$ equals

$$\Delta = e^D - 1$$

where $D$ is differentiation with respect to $x$, we have, from the [Mercator series](wikipedia://en/3995885),

$$\frac{ D }{e^D - 1} = \frac{\log(\Delta + 1)}{\Delta} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-\Delta)^n }{n + 1}.$$

As long as this operates on an $m$th-degree polynomial such as $x^m,$ one may let $n$ go from $0$ only up to $m$.

An integral representation for the Bernoulli polynomials is given by the [Nörlund–Rice integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nörlund–Rice_integral), which follows from the expression as a finite difference.

An explicit formula for the Euler polynomials is given by

$$E_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n \left[ \frac{1}{2^k}\sum_{\ell=0}^n (-1)^\ell {k \choose \ell}(x + \ell)^n \right] .$$

The above follows analogously, using the fact that

$$\frac{2}{e^D + 1} = \frac{1}{1 + \tfrac12 \Delta} = \sum_{n = 0}^\infty \bigl( {-\tfrac{1}{2}} \Delta \bigr)^n .$$

## Sums of *p*th powers {#Sums_of_pth_powers}

Using either the above integral representation of $x^n$ or the identity $B_n(x + 1) - B_n(x) = nx^{n-1}$, we have

$$\sum_{k=0}^x k^p = \int_0^{x+1} B_p(t) \, dt = \frac{B_{p+1}(x+1)-B_{p+1}}{p+1}$$

(assuming 0<sup>0</sup> = 1).

## Explicit expressions for low degrees {#Explicit_expressions_for_low_degrees}

The first few Bernoulli polynomials are:

$$\begin{aligned} B_0(x) & = 1, & B_4(x) & = x^4 - 2x^3 + x^2 - \tfrac{1}{30}, \\[4mu] B_1(x) & = x - \tfrac{1}{2}, & B_5(x) & = x^5 - \tfrac{5}{2}x^4 + \tfrac{5}{3}x^3 - \tfrac{1}{6}x, \\[4mu] B_2(x) & = x^2 - x + \tfrac{1}{6}, & B_6(x) & = x^6 - 3x^5 + \tfrac{5}{2}x^4 - \tfrac{1}{2}x^2 + \tfrac{1}{42}, \\[-2mu] B_3(x) & = x^3 - \tfrac{3}{2}x^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}x \vphantom\Big|, \qquad & &\ \,\, \vdots \end{aligned}$$

The first few Euler polynomials are:

$$\begin{aligned} E_0(x) & = 1, & E_4(x) & = x^4 - 2x^3 + x, \\[4mu] E_1(x) & = x - \tfrac{1}{2}, & E_5(x) & = x^5 - \tfrac{5}{2}x^4 + \tfrac{5}{2}x^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}, \\[4mu] E_2(x) & = x^2 - x, & E_6(x) & = x^6 - 3x^5 + 5x^3 - 3x, \\[-1mu] E_3(x) & = x^3 - \tfrac{3}{2}x^2 + \tfrac{1}{4}, \qquad \ \ & &\ \,\, \vdots \end{aligned}$$

## Maximum and minimum {#Maximum_and_minimum}

At higher $n$ the amount of variation in $B_n(x)$ between $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ gets large. For instance, $B_{16}(0) = B_{16}(1) = {}$$-\tfrac{3617}{510} \approx -7.09,$ but $B_{16}\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr) = {}$$\tfrac{118518239}{3342336} \approx 7.09.$ [Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D.H._Lehmer) (1940)[^o17] showed that the maximum value ($M_{n}$) of $B_n(x)$ between $0$ and $1$ obeys

$$M_n < \frac{2n!}{(2\pi)^n}$$

unless $n$ is $2 \operatorname{modulo} 4$, in which case

$$M_n = \frac{2\zeta (n)\,n!}{(2\pi)^n}$$

(where $\zeta(x)$ is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809)), while the minimum ($m_{n}$) obeys

$$m_n > \frac{ -2 n!}{(2\pi)^n}$$

unless $n = 0 \operatorname{modulo} 4$ , in which case

$$m_n = \frac{-2 \zeta(n)\,n! }{(2\pi)^n}.$$

These limits are quite close to the actual maximum and minimum, and Lehmer gives more accurate limits as well.

## Differences and derivatives {#Differences_and_derivatives}

The Bernoulli and Euler polynomials obey many relations from [umbral calculus](wikipedia://en/214124):

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta B_n(x) &= B_n(x+1)-B_n(x)=nx^{n-1}, \\[3mu] \Delta E_n(x) &= E_n(x+1)-E_n(x)=2(x^n-E_n(x)). \end{aligned}$$

($\Delta$ is the [forward difference operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forward_difference_operator)). Also,

$$E_n(x+1) + E_n(x) = 2x^n.$$

These [polynomial sequences](wikipedia://en/195990) are [Appell sequences](wikipedia://en/1649947):

$$\begin{aligned} B_n'(x) &= n B_{n-1}(x), \\[3mu] E_n'(x) &= n E_{n-1}(x). \end{aligned}$$

### Translations {#Translations}

$$\begin{aligned} B_n(x+y) &= \sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} B_k(x) y^{n-k} \\[3mu] E_n(x+y) &= \sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} E_k(x) y^{n-k} \end{aligned}$$

These identities are also equivalent to saying that these polynomial sequences are [Appell sequences](wikipedia://en/1649947). ([Hermite polynomials](wikipedia://en/195984) are another example.)

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Symmetries"}

### Symmetries {#Symmetries}

$$\begin{aligned} B_n(1-x) &= \left(-1\right)^n B_n(x), && n \ge 0, \text{ and in particular for } n \ne 1,~B_n(0) = B_n(1)\\[3mu] E_n(1-x) &= \left(-1\right)^n E_n(x) \\[1ex] \left(-1\right)^n B_n(-x) &= B_n(x) + nx^{n-1} \\[3mu] \left(-1\right)^n E_n(-x) &= -E_n(x) + 2x^n \\[1ex] B_n\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr) &= \left(\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}-1\right) B_n, && n \geq 0\text{ from the multiplication theorems below.} \end{aligned}$$

[Zhi-Wei Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhi-Wei_Sun) and Hao Pan [^o22] established the following surprising symmetry relation: If $r + s + t = n$ and $x + y + z = 1$, then

$$r[s,t;x,y]_n+s[t,r;y,z]_n+t[r,s;z,x]_n=0,$$

where

$$[s,t;x,y]_n=\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k{s \choose k}{t\choose {n-k}} B_{n-k}(x)B_k(y).$$

:::

## Fourier series {#Fourier_series}

The [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of the Bernoulli polynomials is also a [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258), given by the expansion

$$B_n(x) = -\frac{n!}{(2\pi i)^n}\sum_{k\not=0 }\frac{e^{2\pi ikx}}{k^n}= -2 n! \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{\cos\left(2 k \pi x- \frac{n \pi} 2 \right)}{(2 k \pi)^n}.$$

Note the simple large *n* limit to suitably scaled trigonometric functions.

This is a special case of the analogous form for the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952)

$$B_n(x) = -\Gamma(n+1) \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{ \exp (2\pi ikx) + e^{i\pi n} \exp (2\pi ik(1-x)) } { (2\pi ik)^n }.$$

This expansion is valid only for $0 \le x \le 1$ when $n \ge 2$ and is valid for $0 < x < 1$ when $n = 1$.

The Fourier series of the Euler polynomials may also be calculated. Defining the functions

$$\begin{aligned} C_\nu(x) &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac {\cos((2k+1)\pi x)} {(2k+1)^\nu} \\[3mu] S_\nu(x) &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac {\sin((2k+1)\pi x)} {(2k+1)^\nu} \end{aligned}$$

for $\nu > 1$, the Euler polynomial has the Fourier series

$$\begin{aligned} C_{2n}(x) &= \frac{\left(-1\right)^n}{4(2n-1)!} \pi^{2n} E_{2n-1} (x) \\[1ex] S_{2n+1}(x) &= \frac{\left(-1\right)^n}{4(2n)!} \pi^{2n+1} E_{2n} (x). \end{aligned}$$

Note that the $C_\nu$ and $S_\nu$ are odd and even, respectively:

$$\begin{aligned} C_\nu(x) &= -C_\nu(1-x) \\ S_\nu(x) &= S_\nu(1-x). \end{aligned}$$

They are related to the [Legendre chi function](wikipedia://en/1269900) $\chi_\nu$ as

$$\begin{aligned} C_\nu(x) &= \operatorname{Re} \chi_\nu (e^{ix}) \\ S_\nu(x) &= \operatorname{Im} \chi_\nu (e^{ix}). \end{aligned}$$

## Inversion {#Inversion}

The Bernoulli and Euler polynomials may be inverted to express the [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) in terms of the polynomials.

Specifically, evidently from the above section on integral operators, it follows that

$$x^n = \frac {1}{n+1} \sum_{k=0}^n {n+1 \choose k} B_k (x)$$

and

$$x^n = E_n (x) + \frac {1}{2} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} {n \choose k} E_k (x).$$

## Relation to falling factorial {#Relation_to_falling_factorial}

The Bernoulli polynomials may be expanded in terms of the [falling factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falling_factorial) $(x)_k$ as

$$B_{n+1}(x) = B_{n+1} + \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{n+1}{k+1} \left\{ \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right\} (x)_{k+1}$$

where $B_n = B_n(0)$ and

$$\left\{ \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right\} = S(n,k)$$

denotes the [Stirling number of the second kind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_number_of_the_second_kind). The above may be inverted to express the falling factorial in terms of the Bernoulli polynomials:

$$(x)_{n+1} = \sum_{k=0}^n \frac{n+1}{k+1} \left[ \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right] \left(B_{k+1}(x) - B_{k+1} \right)$$

where

$$\left[ \begin{matrix} n \\ k \end{matrix} \right] = s(n,k)$$

denotes the [Stirling number of the first kind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling_number_of_the_first_kind).

## Multiplication theorems {#Multiplication_theorems}

The [multiplication theorems](wikipedia://en/8720712) were given by [Joseph Ludwig Raabe](wikipedia://en/2933867) in 1851:

For a natural number $m\ge1$,

$$B_n(mx)= m^{n-1} \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} B_n{\left(x+\frac{k}{m}\right)}$$

$$\begin{aligned} E_n(mx) &= m^n \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \left(-1\right)^k E_n{\left(x+\frac{k}{m}\right)} & \text{ for odd } m \\[1ex] E_n(mx) &= \frac{-2}{n+1} m^n \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \left(-1\right)^k B_{n+1}{\left(x+\frac{k}{m}\right)} & \text{ for even } m \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Integrals {#Integrals}

Two definite integrals relating the Bernoulli and Euler polynomials to the Bernoulli and Euler numbers are:[^o29]

- $$\int_0^1 B_n(t) B_m(t)\,dt = (-1)^{n-1} \frac{m!\, n!}{(m+n)!} B_{n+m} \quad \text{for } m,n \geq 1$$
- $$\int_0^1 E_n(t) E_m(t)\,dt = (-1)^{n} 4 (2^{m+n+2}-1)\frac{m!\,n!}{(m+n+2)!} B_{n+m+2}$$

Another integral formula states[^o30]

- $$\int_0^{1}E_{n}\left( x +y\right)\log(\tan \frac{\pi}{2}x)\,dx= n! \sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac {n+1}2\right\rfloor} \frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{ \pi^{2k}} \left( 2-2^{-2k} \right)\zeta(2k+1) \frac{y^ {n+1-2k}}{(n +1- 2k)!}$$

with the special case for $y=0$

- $$\int_0^{1}E_{2n-1}\left( x \right)\log(\tan \frac{\pi}{2}x)\,dx= \frac{(-1)^{n-1}(2n-1)!}{\pi^{2n}}\left( 2-2^{-2n} \right)\zeta(2n+1)$$
- $$\int_0^{1}B_{2n-1}\left( x \right)\log(\tan \frac{\pi}{2}x)\,dx= \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{\pi^{2n}}\frac{2^{2n-2}}{(2n-1)!}\sum_{k=1}^{n}( 2^{2k+1}-1 )\zeta(2k+1)\zeta(2n-2k)$$
- $$\int_0^{1}E_{2n}\left( x \right)\log(\tan \frac{\pi}{2}x)\,dx=\int_0^{1}B_{2n}\left( x \right)\log(\tan \frac{\pi}{2}x)\,dx=0$$
- $$\int_{0}^{1}{{{B}_{2n-1}}\left( x \right)\cot \left( \pi x \right)dx}=\frac{2\left( 2n-1 \right)!}{{{\left( -1 \right)}^{n-1}}{{\left( 2\pi \right)}^{2n-1}}}\zeta \left( 2n-1 \right)$$

## Periodic Bernoulli polynomials {#Periodic_Bernoulli_polynomials}

A **periodic Bernoulli polynomial** $P_{n}(x)$ is a Bernoulli polynomial evaluated at the [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532) of the argument $x$. These functions are used to provide the [remainder term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remainder_term) in the [Euler–Maclaurin formula](wikipedia://en/9637) relating sums to integrals. The first polynomial is a [sawtooth function](wikipedia://en/52147).

Strictly these functions are not polynomials at all and more properly should be termed the periodic Bernoulli functions.

The following properties are of interest, valid for all $x$:

- $P_k(x)$ is continuous for all $k > 1$
- $P_k'(x)$ exists and is continuous for $k > 2$
- $P'_k(x) = k P_{k-1}(x)$ for $k > 2$
