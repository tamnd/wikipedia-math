# De Rham cohomology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="De Rham cohomology"}

*Image: Irrotationalfield.svg.* Vector field corresponding to a differential form on the [punctured plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctured_plane) that is closed but not exact, showing that the de Rham cohomology of this space is non-trivial.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **de Rham cohomology** (named after [Georges de Rham](wikipedia://en/398699)) is a tool belonging both to [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and to [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562), capable of expressing basic topological information about [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) in a form particularly adapted to computation and the concrete representation of [cohomology classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_class). It is a [cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_theory) based on the existence of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) with prescribed properties.

:::

On any smooth manifold, every [exact form](wikipedia://en/404181) is closed, but the converse may fail to hold. Roughly speaking, this failure is related to the possible existence of ["holes"](wikipedia://en/59414446#In_mathematics) in the manifold, and the **de Rham cohomology groups** comprise a set of [topological invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant) of smooth manifolds that precisely quantify this relationship.[^n1]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The **de Rham complex** is the [cochain complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain_complex) of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) on some [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) $M$, with the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) as the differential:

$$0 \to \Omega^0(M) \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^1(M) \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^2(M) \xrightarrow{d} \Omega^3(M) \to \cdots,$$

where $\Omega^0(M)$ is the space of [smooth functions](wikipedia://en/1408000) on $M$, $\Omega^1(M)$ is the space of [$1$-forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-form), and so forth. Forms that are the image of other forms under the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782), plus the constant $0$ function in $\Omega^0(M)$, are called **exact** and forms whose exterior derivative is $0$ are called **closed** (see *[Closed and exact differential forms](wikipedia://en/404181)*); the relationship $d^2=0$ then says that exact forms are closed.

In contrast, closed forms are not necessarily exact. An illustrative case is a circle as a manifold, and the $1$-form corresponding to the derivative of angle from a reference point at its centre, typically written as $\text{d}\theta$ (described at *[Closed and exact differential forms](wikipedia://en/404181)*). There is no function $\theta$ defined on the whole circle such that $\text{d}\theta$ is its derivative; the increase of $2\pi$ in going once around the circle in the positive direction implies a [multivalued function](wikipedia://en/222367) $\theta$. Removing one point of the circle obviates this, at the same time changing the topology of the manifold.

One prominent example when all closed forms are exact is when the underlying space is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414) to a point or, more generally, if it is [simply connected](wikipedia://en/523879) (no-holes condition). In this case the exterior derivative $d$ restricted to closed forms has a local inverse called a [homotopy operator](wikipedia://en/404181).[^n2][^n3] Since it is also [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235),[^n2] it forms a dual [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) with the arrows reversed[^n4] compared to the de Rham complex. This is the situation described in the [Poincaré lemma](wikipedia://en/600030).

The idea behind de Rham cohomology is to define [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of closed forms on a manifold. One classifies two closed forms $\alpha,\beta\in\Omega^k(M)$ as **cohomologous** if they differ by an exact form, that is, if $\alpha-\beta$ is exact. This classification induces an equivalence relation on the space of closed forms in $\Omega^k(M)$. One then defines the $k$-th **de Rham cohomology group** $H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$ to be the set of equivalence classes, that is, the set of closed forms in $\Omega^k(M)$ modulo the exact forms.

Note that, for any manifold $M$ composed of $m$ disconnected components, each of which is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), we have that

$$H^{0}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\cong\mathbb{R}^m.$$

This follows from the fact that any smooth function on $M$ with zero derivative everywhere is separately constant on each of the connected components of $M$.

:::

## De Rham cohomology computed {#De_Rham_cohomology_computed}

One may often find the general de Rham cohomologies of a manifold using the above fact about the zero cohomology and a [Mayer–Vietoris sequence](wikipedia://en/378677). Another useful fact is that the de Rham cohomology is a [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) invariant. While the computation is not given, the following are the computed de Rham cohomologies for some common [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) objects:

### The $n$-sphere {#The_-sphere}

For the [$n$-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782), $S^n$, and also when taken together with a product of open intervals, we have the following. Let $n>0$, $m\geq 0$, and $I$ be an open real interval. Then

$$H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{k}(S^n \times I^m) \simeq \begin{cases} \mathbb{R} & k = 0\text{ or }k = n, \\ 0 & k \ne 0\text{ and }k \ne n. \end{cases}$$

### The $n$-torus {#The_-torus}

The $n$-torus is the Cartesian product

$$T^n = \underbrace{ S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1 }_{n}.$$

Similarly, allowing $n \geq 1$ here, we obtain $H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{k}(T^n) \simeq \mathbb{R} ^{n \choose k}.$

We can also find explicit generators for the de Rham cohomology of the torus directly using differential forms. Given a quotient manifold $\pi: X \to X/G$ and a differential form $\omega \in \Omega^k(X)$ we can say that $\omega$ is $G$**\-invariant** if given any diffeomorphism induced by $G$, $\cdot g:X \to X$ we have $(\cdot g)^*(\omega) = \omega$. In particular, the pullback of any form on $X/G$ is $G$-invariant. Also, the pullback is an injective morphism. In our case of $\mathbb{R}^n/\mathbb{Z}^n$ the differential forms $dx_i$ are $\mathbb{Z}^n$-invariant since $d (x_i + k) = dx_i$. But, notice that $x_i + \alpha$ for $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ is not an invariant $0$-form. This with injectivity implies that

$$[dx_i] \in H^1_{dR}(T^n)$$

Since the cohomology ring of a torus is generated by $H^1$, taking the exterior products of these forms gives all of the explicit [representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_%28mathematics%29) for the de Rham cohomology of a torus.

### Punctured Euclidean space {#Punctured_Euclidean_space}

Punctured Euclidean space is $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the origin removed.

$$H^k_{\text{dR}}(\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\{0\}) \cong \begin{cases} \mathbb{R}^2 & n = 1, k = 0\\ \mathbb{R} & n > 1, k = 0, n - 1\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}.$$

### The Möbius strip {#The_Möbius_strip}

We may deduce from the fact that the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817), $M$, can be [deformation retracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) to the $1$-sphere (i.e. the real unit circle), that:

$$H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{k}(M) \simeq H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{k}(S^1).$$

## De Rham theorem {#De_Rham_theorem}

[Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/29040) is an expression of [duality](wikipedia://en/609737) between de Rham cohomology and the [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) of [chains](wikipedia://en/224765). It says that the pairing of differential forms and chains, via integration, gives a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from de Rham cohomology $H^{k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$ to [singular cohomology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology) $H^k(M;\mathbb{R}).$ de Rham's theorem, proved by [Georges de Rham](wikipedia://en/398699) in 1931, states that for a smooth manifold $M$, this map is in fact an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828).

More precisely, consider the map

$$I: H_{\mathrm{dR}}^p(M) \to H^p(M; \mathbb{R}),$$

defined as follows: for any $[\omega] \in H_{\mathrm{dR}}^p(M)$, let $I(\omega)$ be the element of $\text{Hom}(H_p(M), \mathbb{R} ) \simeq H^p(M; \mathbb{R} )$ that acts as follows:

$$H_p(M) \ni [c] \longmapsto \int_c \omega.$$

The theorem of de Rham asserts that this is an isomorphism between de Rham cohomology and singular cohomology.

The [exterior product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product) endows the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/106341) of these groups with a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) structure. A further result of the theorem is that the two [cohomology rings](wikipedia://en/1069723) are isomorphic (as [graded rings](wikipedia://en/103094)), where the analogous product on singular cohomology is the [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684).

:::

## Sheaf-theoretic de Rham isomorphism {#Sheaf-theoretic_de_Rham_isomorphism}

For any smooth manifold $M$, let $\underline{\mathbb{R}}$ be the [constant sheaf](wikipedia://en/2783581) on $M$ associated to the abelian group $\mathbb{R}$; in other words, $\underline{\mathbb{R}}$ is the sheaf of locally constant real-valued functions on $M$. Then we have a [natural isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism)

$$H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \cong H^*(M, \underline{\mathbb{R}})$$

between the de Rham cohomology and the [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) of $\underline{\mathbb{R}}$. (Note that this shows that de Rham cohomology may also be computed in terms of [Čech cohomology](wikipedia://en/856614); indeed, since every smooth manifold is [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) we have that sheaf cohomology is isomorphic to the Čech cohomology $\check{H}^*(\mathcal{U}, \underline{\mathbb{R}})$ for any [good cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_cover_%28algebraic_topology%29) $\mathcal{U}$ of $M$.)

::: {#o20 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

The standard proof proceeds by showing that the de Rham complex, when viewed as a complex of sheaves, is an [acyclic resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acyclic_resolution) of $\underline{\mathbb{R}}$. In more detail, let $m$ be the dimension of $M$ and let $\Omega^k$ denote the [sheaf of germs](wikipedia://en/245466) of $k$-forms on $M$ (with $\Omega^0$ the sheaf of $C^{\infty}$ functions on $M$). By the [Poincaré lemma](wikipedia://en/600030), the following sequence of sheaves is exact (in the [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) of sheaves):

$$0\to\underline{\mathbb{R}}\to\Omega^0\xrightarrow{d_0}\Omega^1\xrightarrow{d_1}\Omega^2\xrightarrow{d_2}\cdots\xrightarrow{d_{m-1}}\Omega^m\to 0.$$

This [long exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence) now breaks up into [short exact sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of sheaves

$$0\to\operatorname{im}d_{k-1}\xrightarrow{\subset}\Omega^k\xrightarrow{d_k}\operatorname{im}d_k\to 0,$$

where by exactness we have isomorphisms $\operatorname{im}d_{k-1}\cong\ker d_k$ for all $k$. Each of these induces a long exact sequence in cohomology. Since the sheaf $\Omega^0$ of $C^{\infty}$ functions on $M$ admits [partitions of unity](wikipedia://en/48636), any $\Omega^0$-module is a [fine sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_sheaf); in particular, the sheaves $\Omega^k$ are all fine. Therefore, the sheaf cohomology groups $H^i(M,\Omega^k)$ vanish for $i>0$ since all fine sheaves on paracompact spaces are acyclic. So the long exact cohomology sequences themselves ultimately separate into a chain of isomorphisms. At one end of the chain is the sheaf cohomology of $\underline{\mathbb{R}}$ and at the other lies the de Rham cohomology.

:::

## Related ideas {#Related_ideas}

The de Rham cohomology has inspired many mathematical ideas, including [Dolbeault cohomology](wikipedia://en/3189601), [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590), and the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](wikipedia://en/324752). However, even in more classical contexts, the theorem has inspired a number of developments. Firstly, the [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590) proves that there is an isomorphism between the cohomology consisting of harmonic forms and the de Rham cohomology consisting of closed forms modulo exact forms. This relies on an appropriate definition of harmonic forms and of the Hodge theorem. For further details see [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590).

### Harmonic forms {#Harmonic_forms}

If $M$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), then each equivalence class in $H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$ contains exactly one [harmonic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_form). That is, every member $\omega$ of a given equivalence class of closed forms can be written as

$$\omega=\alpha+\gamma$$

where $\alpha$ is exact and $\gamma$ is harmonic; i.e. $\Delta\gamma = 0$.

Any [harmonic function](wikipedia://en/55762) on a compact connected Riemannian manifold is constant. Thus, this particular representative element can be understood to be an extremum (a minimum) of all cohomologously equivalent forms on the manifold. For example, on a 2-[torus](wikipedia://en/74800), one may envision a constant 1-form as one where all of the "hair" is combed neatly in the same direction (and all of the "hair" having the same length). In this case, there are two cohomologically distinct combings; all of the others are linear combinations. In particular, this implies that the first [Betti number](wikipedia://en/346262) of a 2-torus is two. More generally, on an $n$-dimensional torus $T^n$, one can consider the various combings of $k$-forms on the torus. There are $\textstyle\binom{n}{k}$ such combings that can be used to form the basis vectors for $H^k_{\text{dR}}(T^n)$; the $k$-th Betti number for the de Rham cohomology group for the $n$-torus is thus $\textstyle\binom{n}{k}$.

More precisely, one may equip a [differential manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_manifold) $M$ with some auxiliary [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric). Then the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) $\Delta$ is defined by

$$\Delta=d\delta+\delta d$$

with $d$ the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) and $\delta$ the [codifferential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codifferential). The Laplacian is a homogeneous (in [grading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra)) [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) acting upon the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519): we can look at its action on each component of degree $k$ separately.

If $M$ is compact and [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented), the [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) of the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the Laplacian acting upon the space of $k$-forms is then equal (by [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590)) to that of the de Rham cohomology group in degree $k$: the Laplacian picks out a unique harmonic form in each cohomology class of [closed forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_form_%28calculus%29). In particular, the space of all harmonic $k$-forms on $M$ is isomorphic to $H^k(M;\mathbb{R})$. The dimension of each such space is finite, and is given by the $k$-th Betti number.

### Hodge decomposition {#Hodge_decomposition}

Let $M$ be a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_manifold) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652). The *Hodge decomposition* states that any $k$-form on $M$ uniquely splits into the sum of three $L^{2}$ components:

$$\omega=\alpha+\beta+\gamma,$$

where $\alpha$ is exact, $\beta$ is co-exact, and $\gamma$ is harmonic.

One says that a form $\beta$ is co-closed if $\delta \beta = 0$ and co-exact if $\beta = \delta \eta$ for some form $\eta$, and that $\gamma$ is harmonic if the Laplacian is zero, $\Delta\gamma = 0$. This follows by noting that exact and co-exact forms are orthogonal; the orthogonal complement then consists of forms that are both closed and co-closed: that is, of harmonic forms. Here, orthogonality is defined with respect to the $L^{2}$ inner product on $\Omega^k(M)$:

$$(\alpha,\beta)=\int_M \alpha \wedge {\star\beta}.$$

By use of [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964) or [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29), the decomposition can be extended for example to a complete (oriented or not) Riemannian manifold.[^n5]
