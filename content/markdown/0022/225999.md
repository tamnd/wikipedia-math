# Unitary operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unitary operator"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a **unitary operator** is a [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873) [bounded operator](wikipedia://en/455961) on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) that preserves the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product). Non-trivial examples include rotations, reflections, and the [Fourier operator](wikipedia://en/1091252). Unitary operators generalize [unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222). Unitary operators are usually taken as operating *on* a Hilbert space, but the same notion serves to define the concept of [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) *between* Hilbert spaces.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

::: {#o4 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition 1"}

**Definition 1.** A *unitary operator* is a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) $U : H \to H$ on a Hilbert space $H$ that satisfies $U*U = UU* = I$, where $U*$ is the [adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) of $U$, and $I : H \to H$ is the [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) operator.

The weaker condition $U*U = I$ defines an *[isometry](wikipedia://en/254777)*. The other weaker condition, $UU* = I$, defines a *coisometry*. Thus a unitary operator is a bounded linear operator that is both an isometry and a coisometry,[^o5] or, equivalently, a [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873) isometry.[^o6]

An equivalent definition is the following:

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition 2"}

**Definition 2.** A *unitary operator* is a bounded linear operator $U : H \to H$ on a Hilbert space $H$ for which the following hold:

- $U$ is [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873), and
- $U$ preserves the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) of the Hilbert space, $H$. In other words, for all [vectors](wikipedia://en/32370) $x$ and $y$ in $H$ we have:

$$\langle Ux, Uy \rangle_H = \langle x, y \rangle_H.$$

The notion of isomorphism in the [category](wikipedia://en/5869) of Hilbert spaces is captured if domain and range are allowed to differ in this definition. Isometries preserve [Cauchy sequences](wikipedia://en/6085); hence the [completeness](wikipedia://en/7832) property of Hilbert spaces is preserved[^o8]

The following, seemingly weaker, definition is also equivalent:

:::

::: {#o9 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition 3"}

**Definition 3.** A *unitary operator* is a bounded linear operator $U : H \to H$ on a Hilbert space $H$ for which the following hold:

- the range of $U$ is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $H$, and
- $U$ preserves the inner product of the Hilbert space, $H$. In other words, for all vectors $x$ and $y$ in $H$ we have:

$$\langle Ux, Uy \rangle_H = \langle x, y \rangle_H.$$

To see that definitions 1 and 3 are equivalent, notice that $U$ preserving the inner product implies $U$ is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) (thus, a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator)). The fact that $U$ has dense range ensures it has a bounded inverse $U^{-1}$. It is clear that $U^{-1} = U*$.

Thus, unitary operators are just [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of Hilbert spaces, i.e., they preserve the structure (the vector space structure, the inner product, and hence the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954)) of the space on which they act. The [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all unitary operators from a given Hilbert space $H$ to itself is sometimes referred to as the *Hilbert group* of $H$, denoted $\operatorname{Hilb}(H)$ or $U(H)$.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069) is trivially a unitary operator.
- [Rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) in $\mathbf{R}^{2}$ are the simplest nontrivial example of unitary operators. Rotations do not change the length of a vector or the angle between two vectors. This example can be expanded to $\mathbf{R}^{3}$. In even higher dimensions, this can be extended to the [Givens rotation](wikipedia://en/680237).
- Reflections, like the [Householder transformation](wikipedia://en/485424).
- $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ times a [Hadamard matrix](wikipedia://en/507553).
- In general, any operator in a Hilbert space that acts by permuting an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) is unitary. In the finite dimensional case, such operators are the [permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849).
- On the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $\mathbf{C}$ of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), multiplication by a number of [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) $1$, that is, a number of the form $e^{i\theta}$ for $\theta \in \mathbf{R}$, is a unitary operator. $\theta$ is referred to as a phase, and this multiplication is referred to as multiplication by a phase. Notice that the value of $\theta$ modulo $2\pi$ does not affect the result of the multiplication, and so the independent unitary operators on $\mathbf{C}$ are parametrized by a circle. The corresponding group, which, as a set, is the circle, is called $U(1)$.
- The [Fourier operator](wikipedia://en/1091252) is a unitary operator, i.e. the operator that performs the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) (with proper normalization). This follows from [Parseval's theorem](wikipedia://en/706435).
- [Quantum logic gates](wikipedia://en/888587) are unitary operators. Not all gates are [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/187408).
- More generally, [unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222) are precisely the unitary operators on finite-dimensional [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), so the notion of a unitary operator is a generalization of the notion of a unitary matrix. [Orthogonal matrices](wikipedia://en/105620) are the special case of unitary matrices in which all entries are real.[^o12] They are the unitary operators on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$.
- The [bilateral shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_shift) on the [sequence space](wikipedia://en/45194) $\ell^{2}$ indexed by the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) is unitary.
- The [unilateral shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unilateral_shift) (right shift) is an isometry; its conjugate (left shift) is a coisometry.
- Unitary operators are used in [unitary representations](wikipedia://en/292788).
- A **unitary element** is a generalization of a unitary operator. In a [unital algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra), an element $U$ of the algebra is called a unitary element if $U*U = UU* = I$, where $I$ is the multiplicative [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962).[^o13]
- Any composition of the above.

:::

## Linearity {#Linearity}

The [linearity](wikipedia://en/91591) requirement in the definition of a unitary operator can be dropped without changing the meaning because it can be derived from linearity and [positive-definiteness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_bilinear_form) of the [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product):

$$\begin{aligned} \| \lambda U(x) -U(\lambda x) \|^2 &= \langle \lambda U(x) -U(\lambda x), \lambda U(x)-U(\lambda x) \rangle \\[5pt] &= \| \lambda U(x) \|^2 + \| U(\lambda x) \|^2 - \langle U(\lambda x), \lambda U(x) \rangle - \langle \lambda U(x), U(\lambda x) \rangle \\[5pt] &= |\lambda|^2 \| U(x)\|^2 + \| U(\lambda x) \|^2 - \overline{\lambda} \langle U(\lambda x), U(x) \rangle - \lambda \langle U(x), U(\lambda x) \rangle \\[5pt] &= |\lambda|^2 \| x \|^2 + \| \lambda x \|^2 - \overline{\lambda} \langle \lambda x, x \rangle - \lambda \langle x, \lambda x \rangle \\[5pt] &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

Analogously we obtain

$$\| U(x+y)-(Ux+Uy)\| = 0.$$

## Properties {#Properties}

- The [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of a unitary operator $U$ lies on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). That is, for any complex number $\lambda$ in the spectrum, one has $|\lambda| = 1$. This can be seen as a consequence of the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) for [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167). By the theorem, $U$ is unitarily equivalent to multiplication by a [Borel-measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_measurable) $f$ on $L^{2}(\mu)$, for some finite [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $(X, \mu)$. Now $UU* = I$ implies $|f(x)|^{2} = 1$, $\mu$-a.e. This shows that the essential range of $f$, therefore the spectrum of $U$, lies on the unit circle.
- A linear map is unitary if it is surjective and isometric. (Use [Polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965) to show the only if part.)
