# Solèr's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Solèr's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Solèr's theorem** is a result concerning certain [infinite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370). It states that any [orthomodular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthomodular_lattice) form that has an infinite orthonormal set is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) or [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440).[^o2][^o3] Originally proved by [Maria Pia Solèr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Pia_Solèr), the result is significant for [quantum logic](wikipedia://en/663426)[^o4][^o5] and the foundations of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202).[^o6][^o7] In particular, Solèr's theorem helps to fill a gap in the effort to use [Gleason's theorem](wikipedia://en/6796998) to rederive quantum mechanics from [information-theoretic](wikipedia://en/14773) postulates.[^o8][^o9] It is also an important step in the Heunen–Kornell axiomatisation of the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of Hilbert spaces.[^o10]

:::

Physicist [John C. Baez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Baez) notes,

> Nothing in the assumptions mentions the continuum: the hypotheses are purely algebraic. It therefore seems quite magical that \[the [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) over which the Hilbert space is defined\] is forced to be the real numbers, complex numbers or quaternions.[^o7]

Writing a decade after Solèr's original publication, Pitowsky calls her theorem "celebrated".[^o8]

::: {#o12 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $\mathbb K$ be a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067). That means it is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) in which one can add, subtract, multiply, and divide but in which the multiplication need not be [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390). Suppose this ring has a conjugation, i.e. an operation $x \mapsto x^*$ for which

$$\begin{aligned} & (x+y)^* = x^* + y^*, \\ & (xy)^* = y^* x^* \text{ (the order of multiplication is inverted), and } \\ & (x^*)^* = x. \end{aligned}$$

Consider a vector space *V* with scalars in $\mathbb K$, and a mapping

$$(u,v) \mapsto \langle u,v\rangle \in \mathbb K$$

which is $\mathbb K$ -linear in left (or in the right) entry, satisfying the identity

$$\langle u,v\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle^*.$$

This is called a Hermitian form. Suppose this form is non-degenerate in the sense that

$$\langle u,v\rangle = 0 \text{ for all values of } u \text{ only if } v=0.$$

For any subspace *S* let $S^\bot$ be the orthogonal complement of *S*. Call the subspace "closed" if $S^{\bot\bot} = S.$

Call this whole vector space, and the Hermitian form, "orthomodular" if for every closed subspace *S* we have that $S + S^\bot$ is the entire space. (The term "orthomodular" derives from the study of quantum logic. In quantum logic, the [distributive law](wikipedia://en/103118) is taken to fail due to the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883), and it is replaced with the "modular law," or in the case of infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, the "orthomodular law."[^o7])

A set of vectors $u_i \in V$ is called "orthonormal" if

$$\langle u_i, u_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}.$$

The result is this:

If this space has an infinite orthonormal set, then the division ring of scalars is either the field of real numbers, the field of complex numbers, or the ring of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440).
