# Covering relation

other uses of "Cover" in mathematics

*Image: Hasse diagram of powerset of 3.svg.* The [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of three elements, [partially ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) by [inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_%28set_theory%29).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Covering relation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), the **covering relation** of a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) is the [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) which holds between [comparable](wikipedia://en/3834818) elements that are immediate neighbours. The covering relation is commonly used to graphically express the partial order by means of the [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a set with a partial order $\le$. As usual, let $<$ be the relation on $X$ such that $x<y$ if and only if $x\le y$ and $x\neq y$.

Let $x$ and $y$ be elements of $X$.

Then $y$ **covers** $x$, written $x\lessdot y$, if $x<y$ and there is no element $z$ such that $x<z<y$. Equivalently, $y$ covers $x$ if the [interval](wikipedia://en/23572#Intervals) $[x,y]$ is the two-element set $\{x,y\}$. In more intuitive words, $x\lessdot y$ if $y$ immediately supersedes or succeeds $x$ in terms of their respective poset's order relation.

When $x\lessdot y$, it is said that $y$ is a cover of $x$. Some authors also use the term cover to denote any such pair $(x,y)$ in the covering relation.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In a finite [linearly ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_ordered) set $\{1, 2, ..., n\}$, $i+1$ covers $i$ for all $i$ between $1$ and $n-1$, and there are no other covering relations.
- In the [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of a set *S*, a subset *B* of *S* covers a subset *A* of *S* if and only if *B* is obtained from *A* by adding one element not in *A*.
- In [Young's lattice](wikipedia://en/14836297), formed by the [partitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29) of all nonnegative integers, a partition *λ* covers a partition *μ* if and only if the [Young diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_diagram) of *λ* is obtained from the Young diagram of *μ* by adding an extra cell.
- The Hasse diagram depicting the covering relation of a [Tamari lattice](wikipedia://en/20353278) is the [skeleton](wikipedia://en/2977910) of an [associahedron](wikipedia://en/19698446).
- The covering relation of any finite [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883) forms a [median graph](wikipedia://en/16970848).
- On the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) with the usual total order ≤, no number covers another. Any such partial order in which the covering relation is empty is called "[dense](wikipedia://en/6320997)."

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- If a partially ordered set is finite, its covering relation is the [transitive reduction](wikipedia://en/3757117) of the partial order relation. Such partially ordered sets are therefore completely described by their Hasse diagrams. On the other hand, in a [dense order](wikipedia://en/6320997), such as the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) with the standard order, no element covers another.

:::
