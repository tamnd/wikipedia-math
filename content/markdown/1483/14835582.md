# Algebraic combinatorics

*Image: fano plane.svg.* The Fano [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321), derived from the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404). Matroids are one of many kinds of objects studied in algebraic combinatorics.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic combinatorics"}

**Algebraic combinatorics** is an area of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) that employs methods of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), notably [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) and [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), in various [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) contexts and, conversely, applies combinatorial techniques to problems in [algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384).

:::

## History {#History}

The term "algebraic combinatorics" was introduced in the late 1970s.[^n1] Through the early or mid-1990s, typical combinatorial objects of interest in algebraic combinatorics either admitted much [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28mathematics%29) ([association schemes](wikipedia://en/2238152), [strongly regular graphs](wikipedia://en/1399856), posets with a [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29)) or possessed a rich algebraic structure, frequently of representation theoretic origin ([symmetric functions](wikipedia://en/22510359), [Young tableaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_tableaux)). This period is reflected in the area 05E, *Algebraic combinatorics*, of the [AMS](wikipedia://en/198822) [Mathematics Subject Classification](wikipedia://en/3008091), introduced in 1991.

## Scope {#Scope}

Algebraic combinatorics has come to be seen more expansively as an area of mathematics where the interaction of combinatorial and algebraic methods is particularly strong and significant. Thus the combinatorial topics may be [enumerative](wikipedia://en/3925533) in nature or involve [matroids](wikipedia://en/244321), [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471), [partially ordered sets](wikipedia://en/23572), or [finite geometries](wikipedia://en/267061). On the algebraic side, besides group theory and representation theory, [lattice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory) and [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) are commonly used.

## Important topics {#Important_topics}

### Symmetric functions {#Symmetric_functions}

The [ring of symmetric functions](wikipedia://en/364460) is a specific limit of the rings of [symmetric polynomials](wikipedia://en/1440207) in *n* indeterminates, as *n* goes to infinity. This ring serves as universal structure in which relations between symmetric polynomials can be expressed in a way independent of the number *n* of indeterminates (but its elements are neither polynomials nor functions). Among other things, this ring plays an important role in the [representation theory of the symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/874641).

### Association schemes {#Association_schemes}

An [association scheme](wikipedia://en/2238152) is a collection of [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931) satisfying certain compatibility conditions. Association schemes provide a unified approach to many topics, for example [combinatorial designs](wikipedia://en/2761968) and [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869).[^n2][^n3] In algebra, association schemes generalize [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), and the theory of association schemes generalizes the [character theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_character) of [linear representations](wikipedia://en/12695) of groups.[^n4][^n5][^n6]

### Strongly regular graphs {#Strongly_regular_graphs}

A [strongly regular graph](wikipedia://en/1399856) is defined as follows. Let *G* = (*V*,*E*) be a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821) with *v* vertices and degree *k*. *G* is said to be **strongly regular** if there are also [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) λ and μ such that:

- Every two [adjacent vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacent_vertices) have λ common neighbours.
- Every two non-adjacent vertices have μ common neighbours.

A graph of this kind is sometimes said to be a srg(*v*, *k*, λ, μ).

Some authors exclude graphs which satisfy the definition trivially, namely those graphs which are the disjoint union of one or more equal-sized [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816),[^n7][^n8] and their [complements](wikipedia://en/1175649), the [Turán graphs](wikipedia://en/360581).

### Young tableaux {#Young_tableaux}

A [Young tableau](wikipedia://en/683368) (pl.: *tableaux*) is a [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) object useful in [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) and [Schubert calculus](wikipedia://en/3061740). It provides a convenient way to describe the [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695) of the [symmetric](wikipedia://en/28901) and [general linear](wikipedia://en/113564) groups and to study their properties. Young tableaux were introduced by [Alfred Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Young_%28mathematician%29), a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) at [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), in 1900. They were then applied to the study of the symmetric group by [Georg Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius) in 1903. Their theory was further developed by many mathematicians, including [Percy MacMahon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_MacMahon), [W. V. D. Hodge](wikipedia://en/384430), [G. de B. Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_de_Beauregard_Robinson), [Gian-Carlo Rota](wikipedia://en/101891), [Alain Lascoux](wikipedia://en/32555229), [Marcel-Paul Schützenberger](wikipedia://en/2817337) and [Richard P. Stanley](wikipedia://en/1633397).

### Matroids {#Matroids}

A [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) is a structure that captures and generalizes the notion of [linear independence](wikipedia://en/101863) in [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370). There are many equivalent ways to define a matroid, the most significant being in terms of independent sets, bases, circuits, closed sets or flats, closure operators, and rank functions.

Matroid theory borrows extensively from the terminology of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), largely because it is the abstraction of various notions of central importance in these fields. Matroids have found applications in geometry, [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [combinatorial optimization](wikipedia://en/420555), [network theory](wikipedia://en/766409) and [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869).[^n9][^n10]

### Finite geometries {#Finite_geometries}

A [finite geometry](wikipedia://en/267061) is any [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) system that has only a [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) number of [points](wikipedia://en/593693). The familiar [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) is not finite, because a Euclidean line contains infinitely many points. A geometry based on the graphics displayed on a computer screen, where the [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel) are considered to be the points, would be a finite geometry. While there are many systems that could be called finite geometries, attention is mostly paid to the finite [projective](wikipedia://en/242135) and [affine spaces](wikipedia://en/298834) because of their regularity and simplicity. Other significant types of finite geometry are finite [Möbius or inversive planes](wikipedia://en/38245519) and [Laguerre planes](wikipedia://en/38234611), which are examples of a general type called [Benz planes](wikipedia://en/17750374), and their higher-dimensional analogs such as higher finite [inversive geometries](wikipedia://en/295844).

Finite geometries may be constructed via [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), starting from [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615); the affine and [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350) so constructed are called [Galois geometries](wikipedia://en/25197704). Finite geometries can also be defined purely axiomatically. Most common finite geometries are Galois geometries, since any finite [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) of dimension three or greater is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to a projective space over a finite field (that is, the projectivization of a vector space over a finite field). However, dimension two has affine and projective planes that are not isomorphic to Galois geometries, namely the [non-Desarguesian planes](wikipedia://en/21092112). Similar results hold for other kinds of finite geometries.
