# Matrix analytic method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matrix analytic method"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **matrix analytic method** is a technique to compute the stationary [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) which has a repeating structure (after some point) and a state space which grows unboundedly in no more than one dimension.[^o2][^o3] Such models are often described as [M/G/1 type Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M/G/1_type_Markov_chain) because they can describe transitions in an M/G/1 queue.[^o4][^o5] The method is a more complicated version of the [matrix geometric method](wikipedia://en/39587395) and is the classical solution method for M/G/1 chains.[^o6]

:::

## Method description {#Method_description}

An M/G/1-type stochastic matrix is one of the form[^o4]

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} B_0 & B_1 & B_2 & B_3 & \cdots \\ A_0 & A_1 & A_2 & A_3 & \cdots \\ & A_0 & A_1 & A_2 & \cdots \\ & & A_0 & A_1 & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{pmatrix}$$

where *B*<sub>*i*</sub> and *A*<sub>*i*</sub> are *k* × *k* matrices. (Note that unmarked matrix entries represent zeroes.) Such a matrix describes the [embedded Markov chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embedded_Markov_chain) in an M/G/1 queue.[^o8][^o9] If *P* is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/60876#Reducibility) and [positive recurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_recurrent) then the stationary distribution is given by the solution to the equations[^o4]

$$P \pi = \pi \quad \text{ and } \quad \mathbf e^\text{T}\pi = 1$$

where **e** represents a vector of suitable dimension with all values equal to 1. Matching the structure of *P*, *π* is partitioned to *π*<sub>1</sub>, *π*<sub>2</sub>, *π*<sub>3</sub>, …. To compute these probabilities the column stochastic matrix *G* is computed such that[^o4]

$$G = \sum_{i=0}^\infty G^i A_i.$$

*G* is called the auxiliary matrix.[^o10] Matrices are defined[^o4]

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{A}_{i+1} &= \sum_{j=i+1}^\infty G^{j-i-1}A_j \\ \overline{B}_i &= \sum_{j=i}^\infty G^{j-i}B_j \end{aligned}$$

then *π*<sub>0</sub> is found by solving[^o4]

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{B}_0 \pi_0 &= \pi_0\\ \quad \left(\mathbf e^{\text{T}} + \mathbf e^{\text{T}}\left(I - \sum_{i=1}^\infty \overline{A}_i\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^\infty \overline{B}_i\right) \pi_0 &= 1 \end{aligned}$$

and the *π*<sub>*i*</sub> are given by **Ramaswami's formula**,[^o4] a numerically stable relationship first published by Vaidyanathan Ramaswami in 1988.[^o11]

$$\pi_i = (I-\overline{A}_1)^{-1} \left[ \overline{B}_{i+1} \pi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \overline{A}_{i+1-j}\pi_j \right], i \geq 1.$$

## Computation of *G* {#Computation_of_G}

There are two popular [iterative methods](wikipedia://en/15237) for computing *G*,[^o13][^o14]

- functional iterations
- [cyclic reduction](wikipedia://en/39291274).
