# Lebesgue's density theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lebesgue's density theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Lebesgue's density theorem** states that for any [Lebesgue measurable set](wikipedia://en/18198) $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, the "density" of $A$ is 0 or 1 at [almost every](wikipedia://en/44688) point in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Additionally, the "density" of $A$ is 1 at almost every point of $A$. Intuitively, this means that the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of $A$, the set of points in $A$ for which all [neighborhoods](wikipedia://en/1529485) are partially in $A$ and partially outside $A$, is of [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero).

:::

*Image: Lebesgue's Density Theorem.jpg.* Lebesgue's density theorem, applied to the inside of a square, its corners, edges, inside, and outside

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $\mu$ be the Lebesgue measure on the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) and $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a Lebesgue measurable set. Let $x\in \mathbb{R}^n$ and let $B$<sub>ε</sub>$(x)$ denote the [open ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) of radius $\varepsilon$ centered at $x$. Define the density at a point $x$

$$\qquad\qquad d_A(x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mu(A\cap B_\varepsilon(x))}{\mu(B_\varepsilon(x))}$$

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Lebesgue's density theorem"}

**Lebesgue's density theorem.**

For a measurable set $A\subseteq \mathbb R^n$, the density of $A$ is 0 or 1 [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688)[^o5]. If $0<\mu(A)<\infty$, then there are always points of $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ That is, the set of points which do not have density 0 or 1, $\{x\in \mathbb R^n\mid d_a(x)\notin\{0,1\}\}$, has measure 0. Furthermore if $0<\mu(A)<\infty$, then there are always points of $A$ where the density either does not exist or exists but is neither 0 nor 1.[^o6]

:::

For example, given a square in the plane, the density at every point inside the square is 1, on the edges is 1/2, and at the corners is 1/4. The set of points in the plane at which the density is neither 0 nor 1 is non-empty (the square boundary), but it is of measure zero.

The Lebesgue density theorem is a particular case of the [Lebesgue differentiation theorem](wikipedia://en/5766547).

Thus, this theorem is also true for every finite [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) on $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ instead of Lebesgue measure, as proven in sections 2.8–2.9 of [Federer's](wikipedia://en/12185345) *Geometric Measure Theory*, 1969.

:::
