# Locally integrable function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Locally integrable function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **locally integrable function** (sometimes also called **locally summable function**)[^o2] is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) which is integrable (so its integral is finite) on every [compact subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_subset) of its [domain of definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_of_definition). The importance of such functions lies in the fact that their [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) is similar to [p-integrable function spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) ($L^p$ spaces), but its members are not required to satisfy any growth restriction on their behaviour at the boundary of their domain (at infinity if the domain is unbounded): in other words, locally integrable functions can grow arbitrarily fast at the domain boundary, but are still manageable in a way similar to ordinary integrable functions.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Standard definition {#Standard_definition}

(Definition 1).[^o6] Let $\Omega$ be an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $f:\Omega\to\mathbb C$ be a [Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/18198) [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055). If $f$ on $\Omega$ is such that

$$\int_K | f |\, \mathrm{d}x <+\infty,$$

i.e. $f$ is [Lebesgue integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integrable) on all [compact subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) $K$ of $\Omega$, then $f$ is called *locally integrable*. The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all such functions is denoted by $L_{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$:

$$L_{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)=\bigl\{f\colon \Omega\to\mathbb{C}\text{ measurable} : f|_K \in L_1(K)\ \forall\, K \subset \Omega,\, K \text{ compact}\bigr\},$$

where $\left.f\right|_K$ denotes the [restriction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restriction_of_a_function) of $f$ to the set $K$.

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="An alternative definition"}

### An alternative definition {#An_alternative_definition}

(Definition 2).[^o9] Let $\Omega$ be an open set in the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f:\Omega\to \mathbb{C}$ such that

$$\int_\Omega | f \varphi|\, \mathrm{d}x <+\infty,$$

for each [test function](wikipedia://en/301505) $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ is called *locally integrable*, and the set of such functions is denoted by $L_{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$. Here, $C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ denotes the set of all infinitely differentiable functions $\varphi\colon\Omega\to\mathbb R$ with [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) contained in $\Omega$.

This definition has its roots in the approach to measure and integration theory based on the concept of a [continuous linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_linear_functional#Continuous_linear_functionals) on a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752), developed by the [Nicolas Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) school.[^o10] It is also the one adopted by (Strichartz 2003) and by (Maz'ya Shaposhnikova 2009, 34).[^o13] This "distribution theoretic" definition is equivalent to the standard one, as the following lemma proves:

(Lemma 1). A given function $f:\Omega\to \mathbb{C}$ is locally integrable according to (Definition 1) if and only if it is locally integrable according to (Definition 2), i.e.,

$$\int_K | f |\, \mathrm{d}x <+\infty \quad \forall\, K \subset \Omega,\, K \text{ compact} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \int_\Omega | f \varphi|\, \mathrm{d}x <+\infty \quad \forall\, \varphi \in C^\infty_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega).$$

::: {#o14 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof of"}

**Proof of** (Lemma 1)

**Only if part**: Let $\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ be a test function. It is [bounded](wikipedia://en/276881) by its [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm) $\lVert\varphi\rVert_\infty$, measurable, and has a [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support), let's call it $K$. Hence,

$$\int_\Omega | f \varphi|\, \mathrm{d}x = \int_K |f|\,|\varphi|\, \mathrm{d}x \le\|\varphi\|_\infty\int_K | f |\, \mathrm{d}x<\infty$$

by (Definition 1).

**If part**: Let $K$ be a compact subset of the open set $\Omega$. We will first construct a test function $\varphi_K\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ which majorises the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) $\chi_K$ of $K$. The [usual set distance](wikipedia://en/39378#Distances_between_sets_and_between_a_point_and_a_set)[^o15] between $K$ and the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) $\partial\Omega$ is strictly greater than zero, i.e.,

$$\Delta:=d(K,\partial\Omega)>0,$$

hence it is possible to choose a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\delta$ such that $\Delta >2\delta>0$ (if $\partial\Omega$ is the empty set, take $\Delta =\infty$). Let $K_\delta$ and $K_{2\delta}$ denote the [closed](wikipedia://en/42309#Closure_of_a_set) [$\delta$-neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485#In_a_metric_space) and $2\delta$-neighborhood of $K$, respectively. They are likewise compact and satisfy

$$K\subset K_\delta\subset K_{2\delta}\subset\Omega,\qquad d(K_\delta,\partial\Omega)=\Delta-\delta>\delta>0.$$

Now use [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) to define the function $\varphi_K : \Omega\to \mathbb{R}$ by

$$\varphi_K(x)={\chi_{K_\delta}\ast\varphi_\delta(x)}= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\chi_{K_\delta}(y)\,\varphi_\delta(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y,$$

where $\varphi_\delta$ is a [mollifier](wikipedia://en/1596063) constructed by using the [standard positive symmetric one](wikipedia://en/1596063#Concrete_example). Obviously $\varphi_K$ is non-negative in the sense that $\varphi_K \ge 0$, infinitely differentiable, and its support is contained in $K_{2\delta}$. In particular, it is a test function. Since $\varphi_K(x)=1$ for all $x\in K$, we have that $\chi_K \le \varphi_K$.

Let $f$ be a locally integrable function according to (Definition 2). Then

$$\int_K|f|\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_\Omega|f|\chi_K\,\mathrm{d}x \le\int_\Omega|f|\varphi_K\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.$$

Since this holds for every compact subset $K$ of $\Omega$, the function $f$ is locally integrable according to (Definition 1). □

:::

### General definition of local integrability on a generalized measure space {#General_definition_of_local_integrability_on_a_generalized_measure_space}

The classical (Definition 1) of a locally integrable function involves only [measure theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) and [topological](wikipedia://en/30450)[^o17] concepts and thus can be carried over to abstract [complex-valued](wikipedia://en/5826) functions on a topological [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $(X, \Sigma, \mu)$.[^o18] Nevertheless, the concept of a locally integrable function can be defined even on a generalised measure space $(X, \mathcal C, \mu)$, where $\mathcal C$ is no longer required to be a [sigma-algebra](wikipedia://en/29586) but only a [ring of sets](wikipedia://en/2686481) and, notably, $X$ does not need to carry the structure of a topological space.

(Definition 1A).[^o19] Let $(X, \mathcal C, \mu)$ be an [ordered triple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_triple) where $X$ is a nonempty set, $\mathcal C$ is a ring of sets, and $\mu$ is a [positive measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_measure) on $\mathcal C$. Moreover, let $f$ be a function from $X$ to a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $B$ or to the [extended real number line](wikipedia://en/51698) $\overline\mathbb{R}$. Then $f$ is said to be *locally integrable with respect to* $\mu$ if for every set $K\in \mathcal C$, the function $f\cdot \chi_K$ is integrable with respect to $\mu$.

The equivalence of (Definition 1) and (Definition 1A) when $X$ is a topological space can be proven by constructing a ring of sets $\mathcal C$ from the set $\mathcal K$ of compact subsets of $X$ by the following steps.

1. It is evident that $\emptyset\in\mathcal K$ and, moreover, the operations of union $\cup$ and intersection $\cap$ make $\mathcal K$ a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) with [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound) $\vee\equiv\cup$ and greatest lower bound $\wedge\equiv\cap$.[^o20]
1. The class of sets $\mathcal D$ defined as $\mathcal D \triangleq \{A\setminus B\mid A,B \in \mathcal K\}$ is a [semiring of sets](wikipedia://en/2686481#semiring)[^o20] such that $\mathcal D\supset \mathcal K$ because of the condition $\emptyset\in\mathcal K$.
1. The class of sets $\mathcal C$ defined as $\mathcal C \triangleq \{ \cup_{i=1}^n A_i\mid A_i\in \mathcal D \text{ and } A_i\cap A_j=\emptyset\text{ if }i\neq j\}$, i.e., the class formed by finite unions of pairwise disjoint sets of $\mathcal D$, is a [ring of sets](wikipedia://en/2686481), precisely the minimal one generated by $\mathcal K$.[^o21]

By means of this abstract framework, (Dinculeanu 1966, 163–188) lists and proves several properties of locally integrable functions. Nevertheless, even if working in this more general framework is possible without any need of defining a topological structure, all the definitions and properties presented in the following sections deal explicitly only with this latter important case, since the most common applications of such functions are to [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) on Euclidean spaces,[^o6] and thus their domain are invariably subsets of a topological space.

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Locally Lp"}

### Generalization: locally *p*-integrable functions {#Generalization:_locally_p-integrable_functions}

(Definition 3).[^o25] Let $\Omega$ be an open set in the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $f : \Omega\to\mathbb{C}$ be a Lebesgue measurable function. If, for a given $p$ with $1\le p\le+\infty$, $f$ satisfies

$$\int_K | f|^p \,\mathrm{d}x <+\infty,$$

i.e., it belongs to [$L_p(K)$](wikipedia://en/45194) for all [compact subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) $K$ of $\Omega$, then $f$ is called *locally* $p$-*integrable* or also $p$-*locally integrable*.[^o25] The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all such functions is denoted by $L_{p,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$:

$$L_{p,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)=\left\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}\text{ measurable }\left|\ f|_K \in L_p(K),\ \forall\, K \subset \Omega, K \text{ compact}\right.\right\}.$$

An alternative definition, completely analogous to the one given for locally integrable functions, can also be given for locally $p$-integrable functions: it can also be and proven equivalent to the one in this section.[^o26] Despite their apparent higher generality, locally $p$-integrable functions form a subset of locally integrable functions for every $p$ such that $1 <p \le +\infty$.[^o27]

:::

- $L_{p,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega),$ adopted by (Maz'ya Poborchi 1997, 4) and (Maz'ya Shaposhnikova 2009, 44).
- $L_p(\Omega,\mathrm{loc}),$ adopted by (Maz'ja 1985, 6) and (Maz'ya 2011, 2).

## Properties {#Properties}

### *L*<sub>*p*,loc</sub> is a complete metric space for all *p* ≥ 1 {#Lp,loc_is_a_complete_metric_space_for_all_p_≥_1}

(Theorem 1).[^o41] $L_{p,\text{loc}}$ is a [complete metrizable space](wikipedia://en/7832): its topology can be generated by the following [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29):

$$d(u,v)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{2^k}\frac{\Vert u - v\Vert_{p,\omega_k}}{1+\Vert u - v\Vert_{p,\omega_k}}\qquad u, v\in L_{p,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega),$$

where $\{\omega_k\}_{k\ge 1}$ is a family of non empty open sets such that

- $\omega_k\Subset \omega_{k+ 1}$, meaning that $\omega_k$ *is compactly contained in* $\omega_{k+ 1}$ i.e. each of them is a set whose closure is compact and strictly included in the set of higher index.[^o42]
- $\cup_{k}\omega_k= \Omega$ and finally
- ${\Vert\cdot\Vert}_{p,\omega_k}\to\mathbb{R}^+$, $k\in \mathbb{N}$ is an [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of [seminorms](wikipedia://en/292067), defined as

$${\Vert u \Vert}_{p,\omega_k} = \left (\int_{\omega_k} | u(x)|^p \,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p}\qquad\forall\, u\in L_{p,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega).$$

In (Gilbarg Trudinger 2001, 147), (Maz'ya Poborchi 1997, 5), (Maz'ja 1985, 6) and (Maz'ya 2011, 2), this theorem is stated but not proved on a formal basis:[^o47] a complete proof of a more general result, which includes it, can be found in (Meise Vogt 1997, 40).

### *L*<sub>*p*</sub> is a subspace of *L*<sub>1,loc</sub> for all *p* ≥ 1 {#Lp_is_a_subspace_of_L1,loc_for_all_p_≥_1}

(Theorem 2). Every function $f$ belonging to $L_{p,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$, $1\le p\le+\infty$, where $\Omega$ is an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of $\mathbb{R}^n$, is locally integrable.

::: {#o50 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**. The case $p=1$ is trivial, therefore in the sequel of the proof it is assumed that $1< p\le+\infty$. Consider the [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) $\chi_K$ of a compact subset $K$ of $\Omega$: then, for $p\le+\infty$,

$$\left|{\int_\Omega|\chi_K|^q\,\mathrm{d}x}\right|^{1/q}=\left|{\int_K \mathrm{d}x}\right|^{1/q}=|K|^{1/q}<+\infty,$$

where

- $q$ is a [positive number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) such that $1 / p +1 /q =1$ for a given $1\le p\le+\infty$,
- $\vert K\vert$ is the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) of the [compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) $K$.

Then for any $f$ belonging to $L_{p}(\Omega)$ the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) by $f\chi_K$ is [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) by [Hölder's inequality](wikipedia://en/191538) i.e. belongs to $L_{1}(\Omega)$ and

$${\int_K|f|\,\mathrm{d}x}={\int_\Omega|f\chi_K|\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\left|{\int_\Omega|f|^p\,\mathrm{d}x}\right|^{1/p}\left|{\int_K \mathrm{d}x}\right|^{1/q}=\|f\|_p|K|^{1/q}<+\infty,$$

therefore

$$f\in L_{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega).$$

Note that since the following inequality is true

$${\int_K|f|\,\mathrm{d}x}={\int_\Omega|f\chi_K|\,\mathrm{d}x}\leq\left|{\int_K|f|^p \,\mathrm{d}x}\right|^{1/p}\left|{\int_K \mathrm{d}x}\right|^{1/q}=\|f \chi_K\|_p|K|^{1/q}<+\infty,$$

the theorem is true also for functions $f$ belonging only to the space of locally $p$-integrable functions, therefore the theorem implies also the following result.

(Corollary 1). Every function $f$ in $L_{p,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$, $1<p\leq+\infty$, is locally integrable, i. e. belongs to $> L_{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$.

**Note:** If $\Omega$ is an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that is also bounded, then one has the standard inclusion $L_p(\Omega) \subset L_1(\Omega)$ which makes sense given the above inclusion $L_1(\Omega)\subset L_{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$. But the first of these statements is not true if $\Omega$ is not bounded; then it is still true that $L_p(\Omega) \subset L_{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega)$ for any $p$, but not that $L_p(\Omega)\subset L_1(\Omega)$. To see this, one typically considers the function $u(x)=1$, which is in $L_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ but not in $L_p(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for any finite $p$.

:::

### *L*<sub>1,loc</sub> is the space of densities of absolutely continuous measures {#L1,loc_is_the_space_of_densities_of_absolutely_continuous_measures}

(Theorem 3). A function $f$ is the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_function_%28measure_theory%29) of an [absolutely continuous measure](wikipedia://en/252670#Absolute_continuity_of_measures) if and only if $f\in L_{1,\text{loc}}$.

The proof of this result is sketched by (Schwartz 1998, 18). Rephrasing its statement, this theorem asserts that every locally integrable function defines an absolutely continuous measure and conversely that every absolutely continuous measures defines a locally integrable function: this is also, in the abstract measure theory framework, the form of the important [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746) given by [Stanisław Saks](wikipedia://en/362649) in his treatise.[^o53]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The constant function $1$ defined on the real line is locally integrable but not globally integrable since the real line has infinite measure. More generally, [constants](wikipedia://en/24758132), [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122)[^o56] and [integrable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) are locally integrable.[^o57]
- The function $f(x) = 1/x$ for $x \in (0, 1)$ is locally but not globally integrable on $(0, 1)$. It is locally integrable since any compact set $K \subset (0, 1)$ has positive distance from $0$ and $f$ is hence bounded on $K$. This example underpins the initial claim that locally integrable functions do not require the satisfaction of growth conditions near the boundary in bounded domains.
- The function

$$f(x)= \begin{cases} 1/x &x\neq 0,\\ 0 & x=0, \end{cases} \quad x \in \mathbb R$$

is not locally integrable at $x= 0$: it is indeed locally integrable near this point since its integral over every compact set not including it is finite. Formally speaking, $1/x \in L_{1, loc}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)$:[^o58] however, this function can be extended to a distribution on the whole $\mathbb{R}$ as a [Cauchy principal value](wikipedia://en/408195).[^o59]

- The preceding example raises a question: does every function which is locally integrable in $\Omega\subsetneq \mathbb{R}$ admit an extension to the whole $\mathbb{R}$ as a distribution? The answer is negative, and a counterexample is provided by the following function:

$$f(x)= \begin{cases} e^{1/x} &x\neq 0,\\ 0 & x=0, \end{cases}$$

does not define any distribution on $\mathbb{R}$.[^o60]

- The following example, similar to the preceding one, is a function belonging to $L_{1,\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}, 0)$ which serves as an elementary [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) in the application of the theory of distributions to [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) with [irregular singular coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irregular_singularity):

$$f(x)= \begin{cases} k_1 e^{1/x^2} &x>0,\\ 0 & x=0,\\ k_2 e^{1/x^2} &x<0, \end{cases}$$

where $k_1$ and $k_2$ are [complex constants](wikipedia://en/5826), is a general solution of the following elementary [non-Fuchsian differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuchsian_differential_equation) of first order

$$x^3\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}x}+2f=0.$$

Again it does not define any distribution on the whole $\mathbb{R}$, if $k_1$ or $k_2$ are not zero: the only distributional global solution of such equation is therefore the zero distribution, and this shows how, in this branch of the theory of differential equations, the methods of the theory of distributions cannot be expected to have the same success achieved in other branches of the same theory, notably in the theory of linear differential equations with constant coefficients.[^o61]

:::

::: {#o63 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Locally integrable functions play a prominent role in [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) and they occur in the definition of various classes of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) and [function spaces](wikipedia://en/340630), like [functions of bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673). Moreover, they appear in the [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746) by characterizing the absolutely continuous part of every measure.
