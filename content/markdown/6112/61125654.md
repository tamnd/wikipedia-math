# Daniel Revuz

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Daniel Revuz"}

**Daniel Revuz** (born 1936) is a French mathematician specializing in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), particularly in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) applied to [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes). He is the author of several reference works on [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436), [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chains), and [martingales](wikipedia://en/274536).

:::

## Family and early life {#Family_and_early_life}

Revuz is the son of mathematicians Germaine and André Revuz, and is one of six children. His family spent parts of his childhood in [Poitiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poitiers) and [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul) before settling in Paris in 1950.

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Revuz graduated from [Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytechnique) in 1956 and received his doctorate from the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) in 1969 under [Jacques Neveu](wikipedia://en/56512426) and [Paul-André Meyer](wikipedia://en/3653083). He taught at [Paris Diderot University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Diderot_University) at the Laboratory for Probability Theory of the [Institut Mathématique de Jussieu](wikipedia://en/57642956).

### Research {#Research}

From his doctoral thesis work Revuz published two articles in 1970, in which he established a theory of one-to-one correspondence between positive Markov additive functionals and associated measures. This theory and the associated measures are now known respectively as "Revuz correspondence" and "Revuz measures."

In 1991, Revuz co-authored a research monograph with [Marc Yor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc_Yor) on stochastic processes and stochastic analysis called "*Continuous Martingales and Brownian Motion*". The book was highly praised upon its publication. [Wilfrid Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_Kendall) called it "*the* book for a capable graduate student starting out on research in probability."
