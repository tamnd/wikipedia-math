# Robin boundary condition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Robin boundary condition"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Robin boundary condition** ( , ), or **third-type boundary condition**, is a type of [boundary condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_condition), named after [Victor Gustave Robin](wikipedia://en/19368992) (1855–1897).[^o2] It is used when solving [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) and [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753).

:::

The Robin boundary condition specifies a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of the value of a function and the value of its [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) at the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of a given domain. It is a generalization of the [Dirichlet boundary condition](wikipedia://en/423504), which specifies only the function's value, and the [Neumann boundary condition](wikipedia://en/735443), which specifies only the function's derivative. A common physical example is in [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726), where a surface might lose heat to the environment via [convection](wikipedia://en/47526). The rate of heat flow (related to the derivative of temperature) would be proportional to the difference between the surface temperature (the value of the temperature function) and the ambient temperature.

Other equivalent names in use are **Fourier-type condition** and **radiation condition**.[^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Robin boundary conditions are a weighted combination of [Dirichlet boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/423504) and [Neumann boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/735443). This contrasts to [mixed boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/2784886), which are boundary conditions of different types specified on different subsets of the boundary. Robin boundary conditions are also called **impedance boundary conditions**, from their application in [electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) problems, or **convective boundary conditions**, from their application in [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726) problems (Hahn, 2012).

If Ω is the domain on which the given equation is to be solved and ∂Ω denotes its [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824), the Robin boundary condition is:[^o6]

$$a u + b \frac{\partial u}{\partial n} =g \qquad \text{on } \partial \Omega$$

for some non-zero constants *a* and *b* and a given function *g* defined on ∂Ω. Here, *u* is the unknown solution defined on Ω and $\tfrac{\partial u}{\partial n}$ denotes the [normal derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_derivative) at the boundary. More generally, *a* and *b* are allowed to be (given) functions, rather than constants.

In one dimension, if, for example, Ω = \[0,1\], the Robin boundary condition becomes the conditions:

$$\begin{aligned} a u(0) - bu'(0) &=g(0) \\ a u(1) + bu'(1) &=g(1) \end{aligned}$$

Notice the change of sign in front of the term involving a derivative: that is because the normal to \[0,1\] at 0 points in the negative direction, while at 1 it points in the positive direction.

:::

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

## Application {#Application}

Robin boundary conditions are commonly used in solving [Sturm&ndash;Liouville problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm&ndash;Liouville_problems) which appear in many contexts in science and engineering.

In addition, the Robin boundary condition is a general form of the **insulating boundary condition** for [convection–diffusion equations](wikipedia://en/19662199). Here, the convective and diffusive fluxes at the boundary sum to zero:

$$u_x(0)\,c(0) -D \frac{\partial c(0)}{\partial x}=0$$

where *D* is the diffusive constant, *u* is the convective velocity at the boundary and *c* is the concentration. The second term is a result of [Fick's law of diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fick's_law_of_diffusion).

Robin boundary conditions on the [electrostatic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_potential) $\phi$ naturally appear when efficiently modelling electrostatic capacitance or [field effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_effect_%28semiconductor%29) involving ionic or semiconducting electronic media separated by an insulating dielectric, where the Robin boundary condition models a conductor's surface that has a linear 'softness' of the surface charge [Debye layer](wikipedia://en/1158235) i.e. a linear [quantum capacitance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_capacitance) that appears in series with the ordinary dielectric geometrical capacitance.[^o9] A single Robin boundary condition on $\phi$ is also able to completely capture the geometrical capacitance in the 1D-like (parallel-plate capacitor or planar interface) case, such as when modelling a [Stern layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stern_layer).[^o10]
