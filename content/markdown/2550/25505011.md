# Edwards curve

*Image: Edward-curves.svg.* Edwards curves of equation *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = 1 + *d* ·*x*<sup>2</sup>·*y*<sup>2</sup> over the real numbers for *d* = −300 (red), *d* = −$\sqrt{8}$ (yellow) and *d* = 0.9 (blue)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Edwards curve"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Edwards curves** are a family of [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) studied by [Harold Edwards](wikipedia://en/26012311) in 2007. The concept of elliptic curves over [finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_fields) is widely used in [elliptic curve cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography). Applications of Edwards curves to [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) were developed by [Daniel J. Bernstein](wikipedia://en/198983) and [Tanja Lange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanja_Lange): they pointed out several advantages of the Edwards form in comparison to the more well known [Weierstrass form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_form).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The equation of an Edwards curve over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* which does not have [characteristic 2](wikipedia://en/1066621) is:

$$x^2 + y^2 = 1 + d\ x^2 y^2 \,$$

for some [scalar](wikipedia://en/161879) $d\in K\setminus\{0,1\}$. Also the following form with parameters *c* and *d* is called an Edwards curve:

$$x^2 + y^2 = c^2(1 + d\ x^2 y^2) \,$$

where *c*, *d* ∈ *K* with *cd*(1 − *c*<sup>4</sup>·*d*) ≠ 0.

Every Edwards curve is [birationally equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birationally_equivalent) to an elliptic curve in [Montgomery form](wikipedia://en/25780671), and thus admits an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) law once one chooses a point to serve as a neutral element. If *K* is finite, then a sizeable fraction of all elliptic curves over *K* can be written as Edwards curves. Often elliptic curves in Edwards form are defined having c=1, [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000). In the following sections, it is assumed that c=1.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="The group law"}

## The group law {#The_group_law}

(See also [Weierstrass curve group law](wikipedia://en/10225#The_group_law))

Every Edwards curve $x^2 + y^2 = 1 + dx^2y^2$ over field *K* with characteristic not equal to 2 with $d \ne 1$ is birationally equivalent to an elliptic curve over the same field: $(1/e)v^2 = u^3 + (4/e - 2)u^2 + u$, where $e=1-d, u=\frac{1+y}{1-y}, v=\frac{2(1+y)}{x(1-y)}$ and the point $P = (0,1)$ is mapped to the infinity *O*. This birational mapping induces a group on any Edwards curve.

::: {#o8 .statement cue=named-heading name="Edwards addition law"}

### Edwards addition law {#Edwards_addition_law}

On any elliptic curve the sum of two points is given by a rational expression of the coordinates of the points, although in general one may need to use several formulas to cover all possible pairs. For the Edwards curve, taking the [neutral element](wikipedia://en/14962) to be the point (0, 1), the sum of the points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ is given by the formula

$$(x_1,y_1) + (x_2,y_2) = \left( \frac{x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1}{1 + dx_1 x_2 y_1 y_2}, \frac{y_1 y_2 - x_1 x_2}{1 - dx_1 x_2 y_1 y_2} \right) \,$$

The [opposite](wikipedia://en/228312) of any point $(x,y)$ is $(-x,y)$. The point $(0,-1)$ has order 2, and the points $(\pm 1,0)$ have order 4. In particular, an Edwards curve always has a point of order 4 with coordinates in *K*.

If *d is not a square* in *K* and $\{(x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2)\} \in \{(x,y)|x^2+y^2=1+d x^2 y^2 \}^2$, then there are no exceptional points: the denominators $1 + dx_{1}x_{2}y_{1}y_{2}$ and $1 - dx_{1}x_{2}y_{1}y_{2}$ are always nonzero. Therefore, the Edwards addition law is complete when *d* is not a square in *K*. This means that the formulas work for all pairs of input points on the Edwards curve with no exceptions for doubling, no exception for the neutral element, no exception for negatives, etc.[^o9] In other words, it is defined for all pairs of input points on the Edwards curve over *K* and the result gives the sum of the input points.

If *d is a square* in *K*, then the same operation can have exceptional points, i.e. there can be pairs of points $(x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2) \in \{(x,y)|x^2+y^2=1+d x^2 y^2 \}$ such that one of the denominators becomes zero: either $1 + dx_{1}x_{2}y_{1}y_{2} = 0$ or $1 - dx_{1}x_{2}y_{1}y_{2} = 0$.

The addition formula above is faster than other unified formulas and has the strong property of completeness, which refers to its property of not requiring exceptions or branches in its computation. One of the attractive features of the Edwards Addition law is that it is strongly *unified* i.e. it can also be used to double a point, simplifying protection against [side-channel attacks](wikipedia://en/667678).[^o9] The fact that the Edwards Addition law is a non-branching algorithm means that it can avoid leaking timing information related to branching via the amount of power drawn by the computer.

::: {#o10 .example cue=inline-head name="Example of addition law"}

**Example of addition law**:

Consider the elliptic curve in the Edwards form with *d*=2

$${\displaystyle}x^2 + y^2 = 1 + 2 x^2 y^2$$

and the point $P_1=(1,0)$ on it. It is possible to prove that the sum of *P*<sub>1</sub> with the neutral element (0,1) gives again P<sub>1</sub>. Indeed, using the formula given above, the coordinates of the point given by this sum are:

$$x_3 = \frac{x_1 y_2 + y_1 x_2}{1 + 2x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2} = 1$$

$$y_3 = \frac{y_1 y_2 - x_1 x_2}{1 - 2x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2} = 0$$

:::

### An analogue on the circle {#An_analogue_on_the_circle}

*Image: Edwards Curve,Clock group diagram.jpg.* Clock group

To understand better the concept of "addition" of points on a curve, a nice example is given by the classical [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177):

take the circle of radius 1

$${\displaystyle}x^2+y^2=1$$

and consider two points $P_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1})$, P<sub>2</sub>=(x<sub>2</sub>,y<sub>2</sub>) on it. Let α<sub>1</sub> and α<sub>2</sub> be the angles such that:

$${\displaystyle}P_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1})=(\sin{\alpha_{1}},\cos{\alpha_{1}})$$

$${\displaystyle}P_{2}=(x_{2},y_{2})=(\sin{\alpha_{2}},\cos{\alpha_{2}})$$

The sum of P<sub>1</sub> and P<sub>2</sub> is, thus, given by the sum of "their angles". That is, the point P<sub>3</sub>=P<sub>1</sub>+P<sub>2</sub> is a point on the circle with coordinates ($x_{3},y_{3}$), where:

$${\displaystyle}x_{3}=\sin({\alpha}_{1}+{\alpha}_{2})=\sin{\alpha}_{1}\cos{\alpha}_{2}+\sin{\alpha}_{2}\cos{\alpha}_{1}=x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1}$$

$${\displaystyle}y_{3}=\cos({\alpha}_{1}+{\alpha}_{2})=\cos{\alpha}_{1}\cos{\alpha}_{2}-\sin{\alpha}_{1}\sin{\alpha}_{2}=y_{1}y_{2}-x_{1}x_{2}.$$

In this way, the addition formula for points on the circle of radius 1 is:

$${\displaystyle}(x_1,y_1)+(x_2,y_2) = (x_1y_2+x_2y_1,y_1y_2-x_1x_2).$$

### Addition on Edwards curves {#Addition_on_Edwards_curves}

*Image: Sum of two points on an Edwards curve.svg.* Sum of two points on the Edwards curve with d = -30

*Image: Double Point on Edwards Curve.svg.* Doubling a point on the Edwards curve with d=-30

The points on an elliptic curve form an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974): one can add points and take integer multiples of a single point. When an elliptic curve is described by a non-singular [cubic equation](wikipedia://en/180787), then the sum of two points *P* and *Q*, denoted *P* + *Q*, is directly related to third point of intersection between the curve and the [line that passes through *P* and *Q*](wikipedia://en/10225#The_group_law).

The birational mapping between an Edwards curve and the corresponding cubic elliptic curve maps the straight lines into conic sections[^o13] $Axy + Bx + Cy + D = 0$. In other words, for the Edwards curves the three points $P$, $Q$ and $-(P+Q)$ lie on a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052).

Given two distinct non-identity points $P_1=(x_1,y_1), P_2=(x_2,y_2), P_1 \ne P_2$, the coefficients of the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) are (up to scalars):

$$A = (x_1 - x_2) + (x_{1}y_{2} - x_{2}y_{1}),$$

$$B = (x_{2}y_{2} - x_{1}y_{1}) + y_{1}y_{2}(x_{2} - x_{1}),$$

$$C = x_{1}x_{2}(y_{1} - y_{2}), D = C$$

In the case of doubling a point $P=(x,y)$ the inverse point $-2P$ lies on the conic that touches the curve at the point $P$. The coefficients of the quadratic form that defines the conic are (up to scalars):

$$A = dx^2y - 1,$$

$$B = y - x^2,$$

$$C = x(1 - y), D = C$$

:::

## Projective homogeneous coordinates {#Projective_homogeneous_coordinates}

In the context of cryptography, [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) are used to prevent [field inversions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography) that appear in the affine formula. To avoid inversions in the original Edwards addition formulas, the curve equation can be written in [projective coordinates](wikipedia://en/242135) as:

$$(X^2+Y^2)Z^2=Z^4+dX^2Y^2.$$

A projective point $(X : Y : Z)$ corresponds to the [affine point](wikipedia://en/298834) $(X/Z : Y/Z)$ on the Edwards curve.

The identity element is represented by $(0 : 1 : 1 )$. The inverse of $(X : Y : Z)$ is $(-X : Y : Z)$.

The addition formula in homogeneous coordinates is given by: $(X_1 : Y_1 : Z_1)+(X_2 : Y_2 : Z_2 )=(X_3 : Y_3 : Z_3 )$

where

$$X_3 = Z_{1}Z_{2}(X_{1}Y_{2} + X_{2}Y_{1})(Z_{1}^{2}Z_{2}^{2} - dX_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})$$

$$Y_3 = Z_{1}Z_{2}(Y_{1}Y_{2} - X_{1}X_{2})(Z_{1}^{2}Z_{2}^{2} + dX_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})$$

$$Z_3 = (Z_{1}^{2}Z_{2}^{2} - dX_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})(Z_{1}^{2}Z_{2}^{2} + dX_{1}X_{2}Y_{1}Y_{2})$$

::: {#o16 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithm"}

### Algorithm {#Algorithm}

Addition of two points on the Edwards curve could be computed more efficiently[^o17] in the extended Edwards form $(X:Y:Z:T)$, where $T=XY/Z$:

$$(X_3:Y_3:Z_3:T_3) = (X_1:Y_1:Z_1:T_1) + (X_2:Y_2:Z_2:T_2)$$

$$A = X_{1}X_{2}; B = Y_{1}Y_{2}; C = dT_{1}T_{2}; D = Z_{1}Z_{2};$$

$$E = (X_{1} + Y_{1})(X_{2} + Y_{2}) - A - B; F = D - C; G = D + C; H = B - A;$$

$$X_3 = E \cdot F; Y_3 = G \cdot H; Z_3 = F \cdot G; T_3 = E \cdot H;$$

:::

## Doubling {#Doubling}

*Doubling* can be performed with exactly the same formula as addition. Doubling refers to the case in which the inputs (*x*<sub>1</sub>, *y*<sub>1</sub>) and (*x*<sub>2</sub>, *y*<sub>2</sub>) are equal.

Doubling a point $P=(x,y)$:

$$\begin{aligned} (x,y)+(x,y) & = \left( \frac{2xy}{1+dx^2y^2}, \frac{y^2-x^2}{1-dx^2y^2} \right) \\[6pt] & = \left( \frac{2xy}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{y^2-x^2}{2-x^2-y^2} \right) \end{aligned}$$

The denominators were simplified based on the curve equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1 + dx^2y^2$. Further speedup is achieved by computing $2xy$ as $(x + y)^2 - x^2 - y^2$. This reduces the cost of doubling in homomorphic coordinates to 3**M** + 4**S** + 3**C** + 6**a**, while general addition costs 10**M** + 1**S** + 1**C** + 1**D** + 7**a**. Here **M** is field multiplications, **S** is field squarings, **D** is the cost of multiplying by the curve parameter *d*, and **a** is field addition.

::: {#o19 .example cue=inline-head name="Example of doubling"}

**Example of doubling**

As in the previous example for the addition law, consider the Edwards curve with d=2:

$$x^2 + y^2 = 1 + 2 x^2 y^2$$

and the point $P=(1,0)$. The coordinates of the point $P_2 = 2P_1$ are:

$$x_2 = \frac{2xy}{x^2 + y^2} = 0$$

$$y_2 = \frac{y^2-x^2}{2 - (x^2 + y^2)} = -1$$

The point obtained from doubling *P* is thus $P_2=(0,-1)$.

:::

## Mixed addition {#Mixed_addition}

Mixed addition is the case when Z<sub>2</sub> is known to be 1. In such a case A=Z<sub>1</sub><sup>.</sup>Z<sub>2</sub> can be eliminated and the total cost reduces to 9**M**+1**S**+1**C**+1**D**+7**a**

::: {#o22 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithm"}

### Algorithm {#Algorithm}

A= Z<sub>1</sub><sup>.</sup>Z<sub>2</sub> // in other words, A= Z<sub>1</sub>

B= Z<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>

C=X<sub>1</sub>.X<sub>2</sub>

D=Y<sub>1</sub><sup>.</sup>Y<sub>2</sub>

E=d<sup>.</sup>C<sup>.</sup>D

F=B-E

G=B+E

X<sub>3</sub>= A<sup>.</sup>F((X<sub>I</sub>+Y<sub>1</sub>)<sup>.</sup>(X<sub>2</sub>+Y<sub>2</sub>)-C-D)

Y<sub>3</sub>= A<sup>.</sup>G<sup>.</sup>(D-C)

Z<sub>3</sub>=C<sup>.</sup>F<sup>.</sup>G

:::

## Tripling {#Tripling}

*Tripling* can be done by first doubling the point and then adding the result to itself. By applying the curve equation as in doubling, we obtain

$$3(x_1,y_1) = \left( \frac{(x_1^2+ y_1^2)^2 - (2 y_1)^2}{4(x_1^2-1)x_1^2 - (x_1^2-y_1^2)^2}x_1, \frac{(x_1^2+ y_1^2)^2 - (2x_1)^2}{-4(y_1^2-1)y_1^2+(x_1^2-y_1^2)^2}y_1 \right). \,$$

There are two sets of formulas for this operation in standard Edwards coordinates. The first one costs 9**M** + 4**S** while the second needs 7**M** + 7**S**. If the **S/M** ratio is very small, specifically below 2/3, then the second set is better while for larger ratios the first one is to be preferred.[^o24] Using the addition and doubling formulas (as mentioned above) the point (*X*<sub>1</sub> : *Y*<sub>1</sub> : *Z*<sub>1</sub>) is symbolically computed as 3(*X*<sub>1</sub> : *Y*<sub>1</sub> : *Z*<sub>1</sub>) and compared with (*X*<sub>3</sub> : *Y*<sub>3</sub> : *Z*<sub>3</sub>)

::: {#o25 .example cue=inline-head name="Example of tripling"}

**Example of tripling**

Giving the Edwards curve with d=2, and the point P<sub>1</sub>=(1,0), the point 3P<sub>1</sub> has coordinates:

$$x_3 = \frac{(x_1^2+ y_1^2)^2 - (2 y_1)^2}{4(x_1^2-1)x_1^2 - (x_1^2-y_1^2)^2}x_1 = -1$$

$$y_3 = \frac{(x_1^2+ y_1^2)^2 - 2(x_1 )^2}{-4 (y_1^2-1)y_1^2+(x_1^2-y_1^2)^2}y_1 = 0$$

So, $3P_{1}=(-1,0)=P-_{1}$. This result can also be found considering the doubling example: 2P<sub>1</sub>=(0,1), so 3P<sub>1</sub> = 2P<sub>1</sub> + P<sub>1</sub> = (0,-1) + P<sub>1</sub> = -P<sub>1</sub>.

**Algorithm**

`A=X<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>`

`B=Y<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>`

`C=(2Z<sub>1</sub>)<sup>2</sup>`

`D=A+B`

`E=D<sup>2</sup>`

`F=2D.(A-B)`

`G=E-B.C`

`H=E-A.C`

`I=F+H`

`J=F-G`

`X<sub>3</sub>=G.J.X1`

`Y<sub>3</sub>=H.I.Y1`

`Z<sub>3</sub>=I.J.Z1`

This formula costs 9**M** + 4**S**

:::

## Inverted Edwards coordinates {#Inverted_Edwards_coordinates}

Bernstein and Lange introduced an even faster coordinate system for elliptic curves called the *Inverted Edward coordinates*[^o27] in which the coordinates (*X* : *Y* : *Z*) satisfy the curve (*X*<sup>2</sup> + *Y*<sup>2</sup>)*Z*<sup>2</sup> = (*dZ*<sup>4</sup> + *X*<sup>2</sup>*Y*<sup>2</sup>) and corresponds to the affine point (*Z*/*X*, *Z*/*Y*) on the Edwards curve *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = 1 + *dx*<sup>2</sup>*y*<sup>2</sup> with XYZ ≠ 0.

**Inverted Edwards coordinates**, unlike standard Edwards coordinates, do not have complete addition formulas: some points, such as the neutral element, must be handled separately. But the addition formulas still have the advantage of strong unification: they can be used without change to double a point.

For more information about operations with these coordinates see http://hyperelliptic.org/EFD/g1p/auto-edwards-inverted.html

## Extended Coordinates for Edward Curves {#Extended_Coordinates_for_Edward_Curves}

There is another coordinates system with which an Edwards curve can be represented. These new coordinates are called **extended coordinates**[^o29] and are even faster than inverted coordinates. For more information about the time-cost required in the operations with these coordinates see: http://hyperelliptic.org/EFD/g1p/auto-edwards.html
