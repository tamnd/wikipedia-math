# Gelfand pair

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gelfand pair"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Gelfand pair** is a pair (*G*, *K*) consisting of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* and a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *K* (called an **Euler subgroup** of *G*) that satisfies a certain property on [restricted representations](wikipedia://en/880754). The theory of Gelfand pairs is closely related to the topic of [spherical functions](wikipedia://en/17708915) in the classical theory of [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110), and to the theory of [Riemannian symmetric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_symmetric_space) in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625). Broadly speaking, the theory exists to abstract from these theories their content in terms of [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) and [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200).

:::

When *G* is a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707), the simplest definition is, roughly speaking, that the (*K*, *K*)-double [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) in *G* commute. More precisely, the [Hecke algebra](wikipedia://en/55907272), the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on *G* that are invariant under translation on either side by *K*, should be [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29) for the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) on *G*.

In general, the definition of Gelfand pair is roughly that the restriction to *K* of any [irreducible representation](wikipedia://en/385982) of *G* contains the [trivial representation](wikipedia://en/450573) of *K* with multiplicity no more than 1. In each case, one should specify the class of considered representations and the meaning of "contains".

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

In each area, the class of representations and the definition of containment for representations is slightly different. Explicit definitions of several such cases are given here.

### Finite group case {#Finite_group_case}

When *G* is a finite group, the following are equivalent:

- (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair.
- The algebra of (*K*, *K*)-double invariant functions on *G* with multiplication defined by convolution is commutative.
- For any irreducible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the space $\pi$<sup>*K*</sup> of *K*-[invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) vectors in $\pi$ is no more than one-dimensional.
- For any irreducible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the dimension of Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is less than or equal to 1, where **C** denotes the [trivial representation](wikipedia://en/450573).
- The [permutation representation](wikipedia://en/59487764) of *G* on the cosets of *K* is multiplicity-free; that is, it decomposes into a [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_representations) of distinct [absolutely irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_irreducible) representations in [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero.
- The [centralizer algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer_algebra) ([Schur algebra](wikipedia://en/26339669)) of the permutation representation is commutative.
- (*G*/*N*, *K*/*N*) is a Gelfand pair, where *N* is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of *G* contained in *K*.

### Compact group case {#Compact_group_case}

When *G* is a [compact topological group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_topological_group), the following are equivalent:

- (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair.
- The algebra of (*K*, *K*)-double invariant [compactly supported](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) continuous [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) on *G* with multiplication defined by convolution is commutative.
- For any [continuous](wikipedia://en/12781#Continuous_group_actions), [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844), irreducible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the space $\pi$<sup>*K*</sup> of *K*-[invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) vectors in $\pi$ is no more than one-dimensional.
- For any continuous, locally convex, irreducible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the dimension of Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$,**C**) is less than or equal to 1.
- The representation *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*) of *G* is multiplicity-free; that is, it is a direct sum of distinct [unitary](wikipedia://en/292788) irreducible representations.

### Lie group with compact subgroup {#Lie_group_with_compact_subgroup}

When *G* is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) and *K* is a [compact subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_subgroup), the following are equivalent:

- (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair.
- The algebra of (*K*, *K*)-double invariant [compactly supported](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) continuous [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) on *G* with multiplication defined by convolution is commutative.
- The algebra *D*(*G*/*K*)<sup>*G*</sup> of *G*-invariant [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) on *G*/*K* is commutative.
- For any [continuous](wikipedia://en/12781#Continuous_group_actions), [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844), irreducible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the space $\pi$<sup>*K*</sup> of *K*-[invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) vectors in $\pi$ is no more than one-dimensional.
- For any continuous, locally convex, irreducible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the dimension of Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is less than or equal to 1.
- The representation *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*) of *G* is multiplicity-free; that is, it is a [direct integral](wikipedia://en/1256105) of distinct [unitary](wikipedia://en/292788) irreducible representations.

For a classification of such Gelfand pairs, see.[^o7]

Classical examples of such Gelfand pairs are (*G*, *K*), where *G* is a [reductive Lie group](wikipedia://en/1088386#Lie_group_case) and *K* is a [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726).

### Locally compact topological group with compact subgroup {#Locally_compact_topological_group_with_compact_subgroup}

When *G* is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and *K* is a compact subgroup, the following are equivalent:

- (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair.
- The algebra of (*K*, *K*)-double invariant [compactly supported](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) continuous [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) on *G* with multiplication defined by convolution is commutative.
- For any [continuous](wikipedia://en/12781#Continuous_group_actions) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [irreducible representation](wikipedia://en/385982) $\pi$ of *G*, the space $\pi$<sup>*K*</sup> of *K*-[invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) vectors in $\pi$ is no more than one-dimensional.
- For any continuous, locally convex, irreducible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the dimension of Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is less than or equal to 1.
- The representation *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*) of *G* is multiplicity-free; that is, it is a [direct integral](wikipedia://en/1256105) of distinct [unitary](wikipedia://en/292788) irreducible representations.

In that setting, *G* has an [Iwasawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenkichi_Iwasawa)–[Monod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Monod) decomposition, namely *G = K P* for some [amenable](wikipedia://en/657430) subgroup *P* of *G*.[^o9] This is the abstract analogue of the [Iwasawa decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139148) of [semisimple Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group).

### Lie group with closed subgroup {#Lie_group_with_closed_subgroup}

When *G* is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) and *K* is a [closed subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subgroup), the pair (*G*,*K*) is called a **generalized Gelfand pair** if for any irreducible [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788) $\pi$ of*G* on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), the dimension of Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is less than or equal to 1, where $\pi$<sup>∞</sup> denotes the subrepresentation of [smooth vectors](wikipedia://en/12781#Continuous_group_actions).

### Reductive group over a local field with closed subgroup {#Reductive_group_over_a_local_field_with_closed_subgroup}

When *G* is a [reductive group](wikipedia://en/1088386) over a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) and *K* is a closed subgroup, there are three (possibly non-equivalent) notions of the Gelfand pair appearing in the literature:

> ((GP1)) For any irreducible admissible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the dimension of Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is less than or equal to 1.
>
> ((GP2)) For any irreducible admissible representation $\pi$ of *G*, we have $\dim \operatorname{Hom}_K(\pi, \mathbf{C}) \cdot \dim \operatorname{Hom}_K(\tilde{\pi}, \mathbf{C}) \leq 1$, where $\tilde{\pi}$ denotes the [smooth](wikipedia://en/12781#Continuous_group_actions) [dual](wikipedia://en/1442471).
>
> ((GP3)) For any irreducible [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788) $\pi$ of *G* on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), the dimension of Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is less than or equal to 1.

Here, *admissible representation* is the usual notion of [admissible representation](wikipedia://en/11055227) when the local field is non-[Archimedean](wikipedia://en/264158). When the local field is Archimedean, *admissible representation* instead means smooth [Fréchet](wikipedia://en/233801) [representation of moderate growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_moderate_growth) such that the corresponding Harish–Chandra module is [admissible](wikipedia://en/11055227#Real_reductive_groups).

If the local field is Archimedean, then (GP3) is the same as the generalized Gelfand property defined in the previous case.

Clearly, (GP1) ⇒ (GP2) ⇒ (GP3).

:::

## Strong Gelfand pairs {#Strong_Gelfand_pairs}

A pair (*G*, *K*) is called a **strong Gelfand pair** if the pair (*G* × *K*, Δ*K*) is a Gelfand pair, where Δ*K* ≤ *G* × *K* is the diagonal subgroup: $\{(k,k) \in G \times K:k \in K\}$. Sometimes, this property is also called the **multiplicity one** property.

Each of the above cases can be adapted to strong Gelfand pairs. For example, let *G* be a finite group. Then the following are equivalent:

- (*G*, *K*) is a strong Gelfand pair.
- The algebra of functions on *G* invariant with respect to conjugation by *K* (with multiplication defined by convolution) is commutative.
- For any [irreducible representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_representations) $\pi$ of *G* and *τ* of *K*, the space Hom<sub>*K*</sub>(*τ*,$\pi$) is no more than one-dimensional.
- For any irreducible representations $\pi$ of *G* and *τ* of *K*, the space Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$,*τ*) is no more than one-dimensional.

## Criteria for Gelfand property {#Criteria_for_Gelfand_property}

### Locally compact topological group with compact subgroup {#Locally_compact_topological_group_with_compact_subgroup}

In this case, there is a classical criterion due to [Gelfand](wikipedia://en/364643) for the pair (*G*, *K*) to be Gelfand: Suppose that there exists an [involutive](wikipedia://en/293450) [anti-automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-automorphism) *σ* of *G* such that any (*K*, *K*) double coset is *σ*-invariant. Then the pair (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair.

This criterion is equivalent to the following one: Suppose that there exists an involutive anti-automorphism *σ* of *G* such that any function on *G* which is invariant with respect to both right and left translations by *K* is *σ*-invariant. Then the pair (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair.

### Reductive group over a local field with closed subgroup {#Reductive_group_over_a_local_field_with_closed_subgroup}

In this case, there is a criterion due to Gelfand and [Kazhdan](wikipedia://en/3052833) for the pair (*G*, *K*) to satisfy (GP2). Suppose that there exists an [involutive](wikipedia://en/293450) [anti-automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-automorphism) *σ* of *G* such that any (*K*, *K*)-double invariant [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) on *G* is *σ*-invariant. Then the pair (*G*, *K*) satisfies (GP2) (see [^o17][^o18][^o19]).

If the above statement holds only for [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_function_on_a_group) distributions, then the pair satisfies (GP3) (see the next case).

The property (GP1) often follows from (GP2). For example, this holds if there exists an involutive anti-automorphism of*G* that preserves *K* and preserves every closed conjugacy class. For *G* = GL(*n*), the transposition can serve as such an involution.

### Lie group with closed subgroup {#Lie_group_with_closed_subgroup}

In this case, there is the following criterion for the pair (*G*, *K*) to be a generalized Gelfand pair. Suppose that there exists an [involutive](wikipedia://en/293450) [anti-automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-automorphism) *σ* of *G* such that any *K* × *K* invariant positive definite [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) on *G* is *σ*-invariant. Then the pair (*G*, *K*) is a generalized Gelfand pair (see [^o21]).

### Criteria for strong Gelfand property {#Criteria_for_strong_Gelfand_property}

All the above criteria can be turned into criteria for strong Gelfand pairs by replacing the two-sided action of *K* × *K* by the conjugation action of *K*.

:::

## Twisted Gelfand pairs {#Twisted_Gelfand_pairs}

A pair (*G*, *K*) is called a **twisted Gelfand pair** with respect to the character χ of the group *K*, if the Gelfand property holds true when the trivial representation is replaced with the character χ. For example, in the case when *K* is compact, it means that the dimension of Hom<sub>K</sub>($\pi$, χ) is less than or equal to 1. The criterion for Gelfand pairs can be adapted to the case of twisted Gelfand pairs.

## Symmetric pairs {#Symmetric_pairs}

The Gelfand property is often satisfied by *symmetric pairs*. A pair (*G*, *K*) is called a **symmetric pair** if there exists an [involutive](wikipedia://en/293450) [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) *θ* of *G* such that *K* is a union of connected components of the group of *θ*-invariant elements: *G*<sup>*θ*</sup>.

If *G* is a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [reductive group](wikipedia://en/1088386) over **R** and *K* = *G*<sup>*θ*</sup> is a compact subgroup, then (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair. Example: *G* = GL(*n*, **R**) and *K* = O(*n*, **R**), the subgroup of [orthogonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_matrices).

In general, it is an interesting question when a symmetric pair of a reductive group over a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) has the Gelfand property. For investigations of symmetric pairs of rank one, see.[^o25][^o26]

An example of high-rank Gelfand symmetric pair is $(\text{GL}(n + k), \text{GL}(n) \times \text{L}(k))$. This was [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) in [^o27] over non-Archimedean local fields and later in [^o28] for all local fields of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero.

For more details on this question for high-rank symmetric pairs, see.[^o29]

## Spherical pairs {#Spherical_pairs}

In the context of [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924), the analogs of Gelfand pairs are called *spherical pairs*. Namely, a pair (*G*, *K*) of algebraic groups is called a **spherical pair** if one of the following equivalent conditions holds:

- There exists an open (*B*, *K*)-double coset in *G*, where *B* is the [Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356) of *G*.
- There is a finite number of (*B*, *K*)-double coset in *G*.
- For any algebraic representation $\pi$ of *G*, we have $\text{dim} \ \pi^K \leq 1$.

In this case, the space *G*/*H* is called *spherical space*.

It is [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that any spherical pair (*G*, *K*) over a local field satisfies the following weak version of the Gelfand property: For any admissible representation $\pi$ of *G*, the space Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is finite-dimensional; moreover, the bound for this dimension does not depend on $\pi$. This conjecture is proven for a large class of spherical pairs including all the symmetric pairs.[^o31]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Classification {#Classification}

Gelfand pairs are often used for classification of irreducible representations in the following way:

Let (*G*, *K*) be a Gelfand pair. An irreducible representation of G is called *K*-distinguished if Hom<sub>*K*</sub>($\pi$, **C**) is one-dimensional. The representation Ind${}^{G}_{K}$(**C**) is a model for all *K*-distinguished representations, that is, any *K*-distinguished representation appears there with multiplicity exactly 1. A similar notion exists for twisted Gelfand pairs.

::: {#o35 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example:** If *G* is a reductive group over a local field and K is its maximal compact subgroup, then *K*-distinguished representations are called [spherical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_representation), and such representations can be classified via the [Satake correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satake_correspondence). The notion of spherical representation is in the basis of the notion of [Harish-Chandra module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harish-Chandra_module).

:::

::: {#o36 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example:** If *G* is [split reductive group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_reductive_group) over a local field and *K* is its [maximal unipotent subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_unipotent_subgroup), then the pair (*G*, *K*) is a twisted Gelfand pair with regard to any [non-degenerate character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate_character) *ψ* (see [^o17][^o37]). In this case, *K*-distinguished representations are called [generic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_representation) (or non-degenerate) and are easy to classify. Almost any irreducible representation is generic. The unique (up to scalar) imbedding of a generic representation to Ind${}^{G}_{K}$(*ψ*) is called a [Whittaker model](wikipedia://en/20679744).

In the case of *G* = GL(*n*) there is a finer version of the result above; namely, there exist a finite sequence of subgroups *K<sub>i</sub>* and characters $\psi_i$ such that (*G*, *K<sub>i</sub>*) is a twisted Gelfand pair with regard to $\psi_i$ and any irreducible unitary representation is *K<sub>i</sub>* distinguished for exactly one *i* (see [^o38][^o39]).

:::

### Gelfand–Zeitlin construction {#Gelfand–Zeitlin_construction}

One can also use Gelfand pairs for constructing bases for irreducible representations.

Suppose we have a sequence $\{1\} \subset G_1 \subset \cdots \subset G_n$ such that $(G_i, G_{i-1})$ is a strong Gelfand pair. For simplicity let us assume that *G<sub>n</sub>* is compact. Then this gives a canonical decomposition of any irreducible representation of *G<sub>n</sub>* to one-dimensional subrepresentations. When *G<sub>n</sub>* = U(*n*) (the unitary group), this construction is called a [Gelfand–Zeitlin basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelfand–Zeitlin_basis). Since the representations of U(*n*) are the same as algebraic representations of GL(*n*), we also obtain a basis of any algebraic irreducible representation of GL(*n*). However, the constructed basis is not canonical as it depends on the choice of the embeddings $U(i) \subset U(i+1)$.

### Splitting of periods of automorphic forms {#Splitting_of_periods_of_automorphic_forms}

A more recent use of Gelfand pairs is for the splitting of [periods of automorphic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periods_of_automorphic_forms).

Let *G* be a reductive group defined over a [global field](wikipedia://en/288358) *F* and let *K* be an algebraic subgroup of *G*. Suppose that for any [place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_of_a_global_field) $\nu$ of *F*, the pair (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair over the [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28ring_theory%29) $F_\nu$. Let *m* be an [automorphic form](wikipedia://en/782146) over *G*, then its *H*-period splits as a product of local factors (i.e. factors that depend only on the behavior of *m* at each place $\nu$).

Now suppose we are given a family of automorphic forms with a complex parameter *s*. Then the period of those forms is an analytic function that splits into a product of local factors. Often this means that this function is a certain [L-function](wikipedia://en/245283) and this gives an [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) and [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377) for this L-function.

Usually those periods do not converge and one should regularize them.

### Generalization of representation theory {#Generalization_of_representation_theory}

A possible approach to representation theory is to consider the representation theory of a group *G* as a [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) on the group *G* with regard to the two-sided action of *G* × *G*. Indeed, to know all the irreducible representations of *G* is equivalent to know the decomposition of the space of functions on *G* as a *G* × *G* representation. In this approach, representation theory can be generalized by replacing the pair (*G* × *G*, *G*) by any spherical pair (*G*, *K*). Then we will be led to the question of harmonic analysis on the space *G*/*K* with regard to the action of *G*.

Now the Gelfand property for the pair (*G*, *K*) is an analog of the [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696).

Using this approach, any concept of representation theory can be generalized to the case of spherical pair. For example, the [relative trace formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_trace_formula) is obtained from the [trace formula](wikipedia://en/20419621) by this procedure.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Finite groups {#Finite_groups}

A few common examples of Gelfand pairs are:

- $(\text{Sym}(n+1), \text{Sym}(n))$, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) acting on *n*+1 points and a point stabilizer that is naturally isomorphic to on *n* points.
- $(\text{AGL}(n,q), \text{GL}(n,q))$, the [affine (general linear) group](wikipedia://en/298828) and a point stabilizer that is naturally isomorphic to the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564).

If (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair, then (*G*/*N*, *K*/*N*) is a Gelfand pair for every *G*-[normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) *N* of *K*. For many purposes it suffices to consider *K* without any such non-identity normal subgroups. The action of *G* on the cosets of *K* is thus faithful, so one is then looking at permutation groups *G* with point stabilizers *K*. To be a Gelfand pair is equivalent to $[1_K,\chi\downarrow^G_K] \leq 1$ for every *χ* in Irr(*G*). Since $[1_K,\chi\downarrow^G_K] = [1\uparrow_K^G,\chi]$ by [Frobenius reciprocity](wikipedia://en/55690071) and $1\uparrow_K^G$ is the character of the permutation action, a permutation group defines a Gelfand pair if and only if the permutation character is a so-called **multiplicity-free** permutation character. Such multiplicity-free permutation characters were determined for the [sporadic groups](wikipedia://en/324806) in (Breuer Lux 1996).

This gives rise to a class of examples of finite groups with Gelfand pairs: the [2-transitive groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-transitive_group). A [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) *G* is **2-transitive** if the [stabilizer](wikipedia://en/12781#Orbits_and_stabilizers) *K* of a point acts [transitively](wikipedia://en/12781#Transitivity_properties) on the remaining points. In particular, *G* the symmetric group on *n*+1 points and *K* the symmetric group on *n* points forms a Gelfand pair for every *n*≥ 1. This follows because the character of a 2-transitive permutation action is of the form 1 + *χ* for some irreducible character *χ* and the [trivial character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_character) 1, (Isaacs 1994, 69).

Indeed, if *G* is a transitive permutation group whose point stabilizer *K* has at most four orbits (including the trivial orbit containing only the stabilized point), then its Schur ring is commutative and (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair, (Wielandt 1964, 86). If *G* is a [primitive group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_group) of degree twice a prime with point stabilizer *K*, then again (*G*, *K*) is a Gelfand pair, (Wielandt 1964, 97).

The Gelfand pairs (Sym(*n*), *K*) were classified in (Saxl 1981). Roughly speaking, *K* must be contained as a subgroup of small [index](wikipedia://en/230581) in one of the following groups unless *n* is smaller than 18:

- Sym(*n* − *k*) × Sym(*k*)
- Sym(*n*/2) wr Sym(2), Sym(2) wr Sym(*n*/2) for [even](wikipedia://en/143135) *n*, where wr denotes the [wreath product](wikipedia://en/146625)
- Sym(*n* − 5) × AGL(1, 5)
- Sym(*n* − 6) × PGL(2, 5)
- Sym(*n* − 9) × PΓL(2, 8)

Gelfand pairs for classical groups have been investigated as well.

### Symmetric pairs with compact *K* {#Symmetric_pairs_with_compact_K}

- (GL(*n*, **R**), O(*n*, **R**))
- (GL(*n*, **C**), U(*n*))
- (O(*n* + *k*, **R**), O(*n*, **R**) × O(*k*, **R**))
- (U(*n* + *k*), U(*n*) × U(*k*))
- (*G*, *K*) where *G* is a [reductive Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductive_Lie_group) and *K* is a [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726)

### Symmetric Gelfand pairs of rank one {#Symmetric_Gelfand_pairs_of_rank_one}

Let *F* be a local field of characteristic zero.

- (SL(*n* + 1, *F*), GL(*n*, *F*)) for *n* > 5
- (Sp(2*n* + 2, *F*), Sp(2*n*, *F*)) × Sp(2, *F*)) for *n* > 4
- (SO(*V* ⊕ *F*), SO(*V*)) where *V* is a vector space over *F* with a [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form) [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478)

### Symmetric pairs of high rank {#Symmetric_pairs_of_high_rank}

Let *F* be a local field of characteristic zero. Let *G* be a [reductive group](wikipedia://en/1088386) over *F*. The following are examples of symmetric Gelfand pairs of high rank:

- (*G* × *G*, Δ*G*), follows from [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696)
- (GL(*n* + *k*, *F*), GL(*n*, *F*) × GL(*k*, *F*))[^o27][^o28]
- (GL(2*n*, *F*), Sp(2*n*, *F*))[^o54][^o55]
- (O(*n* + *k*, **C**), O(*n*, **C**) × O(*k*, **C**))[^o56]
- (GL(*n*, **C**), O(*n*, **C**))[^o56]
- (GL(*n*, *E*), GL(*n*, *F*)) where *E* is a [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) of *F*[^o29][^o57]

### Strong Gelfand pairs {#Strong_Gelfand_pairs}

The following pairs are strong Gelfand pairs:

- (Sym(*n* + 1), Sym(*n*)), proven using the involutive anti-automorphism *g* ↦ *g*<sup>−1</sup>
- (GL(*n* + 1, *F*), GL(*n*, *F*)) where *F* is a local field of characteristic zero[^o59][^o60][^o61]
- (O(*V* ⊕ *F*), O(*V*)) where *V* is a vector space over *F* with a [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form) [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478)[^o59][^o61]
- U(*V* ⊕ *E*), U(*V*)) where *E* is a [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) of *F* and *V* is a vector space over *E* with a [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form) [Hermitian form](wikipedia://en/860138#Hermitian_form)[^o59][^o61]

Those four examples can be rephrased as the statement that the following are Gelfand pairs:

- (Sym(*n* + 1) × Sym(*n*), Δ Sym(*n*))
- (GL(*n* + 1, *F*) × GL(*n*, *F*), Δ GL(*n*, *F*))
- (O(*V* ⊕ *F*) × O(*V*), Δ O(*V*))
- (U(*V* ⊕ *E*) × U(*V*), Δ U(*V*))
