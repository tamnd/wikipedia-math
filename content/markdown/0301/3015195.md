# Newton–Euler equations

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Newton–Euler equations"}

In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), the **Newton–Euler** equations describe the combined translational and [rotational dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_dynamics) of a [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769).[^o2][^o3] [^o4][^o5][^o6]

:::

Traditionally the Newton–Euler equations is the grouping together of [Euler's two laws of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_laws_of_motion) for a rigid body into a single equation with 6 components, using [column vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector) and [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859). These laws relate the motion of the [center of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_gravity) of a rigid body with the sum of [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) and [torques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torques) (or synonymously [moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_%28physics%29)) acting on the rigid body.

## Center of mass frame {#Center_of_mass_frame}

With respect to a [coordinate frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_frame) whose origin coincides with the body's [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) for **τ**([torque](wikipedia://en/30400)) and an [inertial frame of reference](wikipedia://en/14838) for **F**([force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force)), they can be expressed in matrix form as:

$$\left(\begin{matrix} {\mathbf F} \\ {\boldsymbol \tau} \end{matrix}\right) = \left(\begin{matrix} m {\mathbf I_3} & 0 \\ 0 & {\mathbf I}_{\rm cm} \end{matrix}\right) \left(\begin{matrix} \mathbf a_{\rm cm} \\ {\boldsymbol \alpha} \end{matrix}\right) + \left(\begin{matrix} 0 \\ {\boldsymbol \omega} \times \left({\mathbf I}_{\rm cm} \, {\boldsymbol \omega}\right) \end{matrix}\right),$$

where

**F** = total [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) acting on the center of mass

*m* = mass of the body

**I**<sub>3</sub> = the 3×3 [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718)

**a**<sub>cm</sub> = acceleration of the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961)

**v**<sub>cm</sub> = velocity of the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961)

**τ** = total torque acting about the center of mass

**I**<sub>cm</sub> = [moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700) about the center of mass

**ω** = [angular velocity](wikipedia://en/65927) of the body

**α** = [angular acceleration](wikipedia://en/65929) of the body

## Arbitrary reference frame {#Arbitrary_reference_frame}

With respect to a [coordinate frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_frame) located at point **P** that is fixed in the body and *not* coincident with the center of mass, the equations assume the more complex form:

$$\left(\begin{matrix} {\mathbf F} \\ {\boldsymbol \tau}_{\rm p} \end{matrix}\right) = \left(\begin{matrix} m {\mathbf I_3} & -m [{\mathbf c}]^{\times}\\ m [{\mathbf c}]^{\times} & {\mathbf I}_{\rm cm} - m[{\mathbf c}]^{\times}[{\mathbf c}]^{\times}\end{matrix}\right) \left(\begin{matrix} \mathbf a_{\rm p} \\ {\boldsymbol \alpha} \end{matrix}\right) + \left(\begin{matrix} m[{\boldsymbol \omega}]^{\times}[{\boldsymbol \omega}]^{\times} {\mathbf c} \\ {[\boldsymbol \omega]}^\times ({\mathbf I}_{\rm cm} - m [{\mathbf c}]^\times[{\mathbf c}]^\times)\, {\boldsymbol \omega} \end{matrix}\right),$$

where **c** is the vector from **P** to the center of mass of the body expressed in the [body-fixed frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body-fixed_frame), and

$$[\mathbf{c}]^{\times} \equiv \left(\begin{matrix} 0 & -c_z & c_y \\ c_z & 0 & -c_x \\ -c_y & c_x & 0 \end{matrix}\right) \qquad \qquad [\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\omega}}]^{\times} \equiv \left(\begin{matrix} 0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\ \omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\ -\omega_y & \omega_x & 0 \end{matrix}\right)$$

denote [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055) [cross product matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_product_matrix).

The left hand side of the equation—which includes the sum of external forces, and the sum of external moments about **P**—describes a spatial [wrench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrench_%28screw_theory%29#Wrench), see [screw theory](wikipedia://en/1318037).

The inertial terms are contained in the *spatial inertia* matrix

$$\left(\begin{matrix} m {\mathbf I_3} & - m [{\mathbf c}]^{\times}\\ m [{\mathbf c}]^{\times} & {\mathbf I}_{\rm cm} - m [{\mathbf c}]^{\times}[{\mathbf c}]^{\times}\end{matrix}\right),$$

while the [fictitious forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictitious_forces) are contained in the term:[^o9]

$$\left(\begin{matrix} m{[\boldsymbol \omega]}^\times {[\boldsymbol \omega]}^\times {\mathbf c} \\ {[\boldsymbol \omega]}^\times ({\mathbf I}_{\rm cm} - m [{\mathbf c}]^\times[{\mathbf c}]^\times)\, {\boldsymbol \omega} \end{matrix}\right) .$$

When the center of mass is not coincident with the coordinate frame (that is, when **c** is nonzero), the translational and angular accelerations (**a** and **α**) are coupled, so that each is associated with force and torque components.

## Applications {#Applications}

The Newton–Euler equations are used as the basis for more complicated "multi-body" formulations ([screw theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrench_%28screw_theory%29)) that describe the dynamics of systems of rigid bodies connected by joints and other constraints. Multi-body problems can be solved by a variety of numerical algorithms.[^o3][^o9][^o12]
