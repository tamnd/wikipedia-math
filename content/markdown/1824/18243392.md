# Additive combinatorics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Additive combinatorics"}

**Additive combinatorics** is an area of [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). One major area of study in additive combinatorics are *inverse problems*: given the size of the [sumset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumset) $A + B$ is small, what can we say about the structures of $A$ and $B$? In the case of the integers, the classical [Freiman's theorem](wikipedia://en/2240299) provides a partial answer to this question in terms of [multi-dimensional arithmetic progressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-dimensional_arithmetic_progression).

:::

Another typical problem is to find a lower bound for $\lvert A + B\rvert$ in terms of $\lvert A\rvert$ and $\lvert B\rvert$. This can be viewed as an inverse problem with the given information that $\lvert A + B\rvert$ is sufficiently small and the structural conclusion is then of the form that either $A$ or $B$ is the empty set; however, in literature, such problems are sometimes considered to be direct problems as well. Examples of this type include the [Erdős–Heilbronn Conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdős–Heilbronn_conjecture) (for a [restricted sumset](wikipedia://en/7960510)) and the [Cauchy–Davenport Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy–Davenport_theorem). The methods used for tackling such questions often come from many different fields of mathematics, including [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986), [analysis](wikipedia://en/1134), [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), and [linear-algebraic](wikipedia://en/18422) and polynomial methods.

## History of additive combinatorics {#History_of_additive_combinatorics}

Although additive combinatorics is a fairly new branch of combinatorics (the term *additive combinatorics* was coined by [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) and [Van H. Vu](wikipedia://en/21419416) in their 2006 book of the same name), a much older problem, the [Cauchy–Davenport theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy–Davenport_theorem), is one of the most fundamental results in this field.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Cauchy–Davenport theorem"}

### Cauchy–Davenport theorem {#Cauchy–Davenport_theorem}

Suppose that *$A$* and *$B$* are finite subsets of the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime $p$, then the following inequality holds.

$$|A+B| \ge \min(|A|+|B|-1,p)$$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Vosper's theorem"}

### Vosper's theorem {#Vosper's_theorem}

Now we have the inequality for the cardinality of the sum set $A + B$, it is natural to ask the inverse problem, namely under what conditions on $A$ and $B$ does the equality hold? Vosper's theorem answers this question. Suppose that $\lvert A\rvert,\lvert B\rvert \ge 2$ (that is, barring edge cases) and

$$|A+B| \le |A|+|B|-1 \le p-2,$$

then $A$ and $B$ are arithmetic progressions with the same difference. This illustrates the structures that are often studied in additive combinatorics: the combinatorial structure of $A + B$ as compared to the algebraic structure of arithmetic progressions.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement cue=named-heading name="Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequality"}

### Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequality {#Plünnecke–Ruzsa_inequality}

A useful theorem in additive combinatorics is [Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequality](wikipedia://en/62472235). This theorem gives an upper bound on the cardinality of $\lvert nA - mA\rvert$ in terms of the doubling constant of $A$. For instance using Plünnecke–Ruzsa inequality, we are able to prove a version of Freiman's Theorem in finite fields.

:::

## Basic notions {#Basic_notions}

### Operations on sets {#Operations_on_sets}

Let *$A$* and *$B$* be finite subsets of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974); then the sum set is defined to be

$$A+B = \{a+b : a \in A, b \in B\}.$$

For example, we can write $\{1,2,3,4\} + \{1,2,3\} = \{2,3,4,5,6,7\}$. Similarly, we can define the difference set of *$A$* and *$B$* to be

$$A-B = \{a-b : a \in A, b \in B\}.$$

The $k$-fold sumset of the set $A$ with itself is denoted by

$$kA = \underbrace{A+A+\cdots+A}_{k\text{ terms }} = \{a_1+\cdots+a_k : a_1 \in A, \dots, a_k \in A\},$$

which must not be confused with

$$k \cdot A = \{ka : a \in A\}.$$

### Doubling constant {#Doubling_constant}

Let *$A$* be a subset of an abelian group. The doubling constant measures how big the sum set $|A+A|$ is compared to its original size $\lvert A\rvert$. We define the doubling constant of $A$ to be

$$K = \dfrac{|A+A|}{|A|}.$$

## Ruzsa distance {#Ruzsa_distance}

Let *$A$* and *$B$* be two subsets of an abelian group. We define the Ruzsa distance between these two sets to be the quantity

$$d(A,B) = \log \dfrac{|A-B|}{\sqrt{|A||B|}}.$$

The [Ruzsa triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/62472742) asserts that the Ruzsa distance obeys the triangle inequality:

$$d(B,C) \le d(A,B) + d(A,C).$$

However, since $d(A,A)$ cannot be zero, the Ruzsa distance is not actually a [metric](wikipedia://en/20018).
