# Road coloring theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Road coloring theorem"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) the **road coloring theorem**, known previously as the road coloring [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138), deals with [synchronized instructions](wikipedia://en/13667880). The issue involves whether by using such instructions, one can reach or locate an object or destination from any other point within a network (which might be a representation of city streets or a [maze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze)).[^o2] In the real world, this phenomenon would be as if you called a friend to ask for directions to his house, and he gave you a set of directions that worked no matter where you started from. This [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) also has implications in [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263).

:::

The theorem was first conjectured by [Roy Adler](wikipedia://en/41177893) and [Benjamin Weiss](wikipedia://en/40423267).[^n1] It was proved by [Avraham Trahtman](wikipedia://en/15669513) in 2009.[^n2]

## Example and intuition {#Example_and_intuition}

*Image: Road coloring conjecture.svg.* A directed graph with a synchronizing coloring

The image to the right shows a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) on eight [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) in which each vertex has [out-degree](wikipedia://en/1374448#Directed_graphs) 2. (Each vertex in this case also has in-degree 2, but that is not necessary for a synchronizing coloring to exist.) The edges of this graph have been colored red and blue to create a synchronizing coloring.

For example, consider the vertex marked in yellow. No matter where in the graph you start, if you traverse all nine edges in the walk "blue-red-red—blue-red-red—blue-red-red", you will end up at the yellow vertex. Similarly, if you traverse all nine edges in the walk "blue-blue-red—blue-blue-red—blue-blue-red", you will always end up at the vertex marked in green, no matter where you started.

The road coloring theorem states that for a certain category of directed graphs, it is always possible to create such a coloring.

### Mathematical description {#Mathematical_description}

Let *G* be a finite, [strongly connected](wikipedia://en/684680), [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) where all the vertices have the same [out-degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Out-degree) *k*. Let *A* be the alphabet containing the letters 1, ..., *k*. A *synchronizing coloring* (also known as a *collapsible coloring*) in *G* is a labeling of the edges in *G* with letters from *A* such that (1) each vertex has exactly one outgoing edge with a given label and (2) for every vertex *v* in the graph, there exists a word *w* over *A* such that all paths in *G* corresponding to *w* terminate at *v*.

The terminology *synchronizing coloring* is due to the relation between this notion and that of a [synchronizing word](wikipedia://en/13667880) in [finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_automata) theory.

For such a coloring to exist at all, it is [necessary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_conditions) that *G* be [aperiodic](wikipedia://en/7220840).[^n3] The road coloring theorem states that aperiodicity is also *[sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_conditions)* for such a coloring to exist. Therefore, the road coloring problem can be stated briefly as:

*Every finite strongly connected aperiodic graph of uniform out-degree has a synchronizing coloring.*

## Previous partial results {#Previous_partial_results}

Previous partial or special-case results include the following:

- If *G* is a finite strongly connected [aperiodic](wikipedia://en/76848) directed graph with no [multiple edges](wikipedia://en/5719783), and *G* contains a [simple cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_cycle) of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) length which is a proper subset of *G*, then *G* has a synchronizing coloring.[^n4]
- If *G* is a finite strongly connected aperiodic directed graph (multiple edges allowed) and every vertex has the same in-degree and out-degree *k*, then *G* has a synchronizing coloring.[^n5]
