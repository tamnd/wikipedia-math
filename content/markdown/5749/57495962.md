# Connected relation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Connected relation"}

In mathematics, a [relation on a set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_on_a_set) is called **connected** or **complete** or **total** if it relates (or "compares") all distinct pairs of elements of the set in one direction or the other while it is called **strongly connected** if it relates all pairs of elements. As described in the terminology section below, the terminology for these properties is not uniform. This notion of "total" should not be confused with that of a total relation in the sense that for all $x \in X$ there is a $y \in X$ so that $x \mathrel{R} y$ (see [serial relation](wikipedia://en/12982585)).

:::

Connectedness features prominently in the definition of [total orders](wikipedia://en/30330): a total (or linear) order is a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) in which any two elements are comparable; that is, the order relation is connected. Similarly, a [strict partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_partial_order) that is connected is a strict total order. A relation is a total order [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is both a partial order and strongly connected. A relation is a [strict total order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_total_order) if, and only if, it is a strict partial order and just connected. A strict total order can never be strongly connected (except on an empty domain).

Some authors do however use the term *connected* with a much looser meaning, which applies to precisely those orders whose [comparability graphs](wikipedia://en/6978672) are [connected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph). This applies, for instance, to [fences](wikipedia://en/21644788), of which none of the nontrivial examples are total orders.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A relation $R$ on a set $X$ is called **connected** when for all $x, y \in X,$

$$\text{ if } x \neq y \text{ then } x \mathrel{R} y \quad \text{or} \quad y \mathrel{R} x,$$

or, equivalently, when for all $x, y \in X,$

$$x \mathrel{R} y \quad \text{or} \quad y \mathrel{R} x \quad \text{or} \quad x = y.$$

A relation with the property that for all $x, y \in X,$

$$x \mathrel{R} y \quad \text{or} \quad y \mathrel{R} x$$

is called **strongly connected**.[^o4][^o5][^o6]

::: {#o8 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

### Terminology {#Terminology}

The main use of the notion of connected relation is in the context of orders, where it is used to define total, or linear, orders. In this context, the property is often not specifically named. Rather, total orders are defined as partial orders in which any two elements are comparable.[^o9][^o10] Thus, total is used more generally for relations that are connected or strongly connected.[^o11] However, this notion of "total relation" must be distinguished from the property of being [serial](wikipedia://en/12982585), which is also called total. Similarly, connected relations are sometimes called complete,[^o12] although this, too, can lead to confusion: The [universal relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_relation) is also called complete,[^o13] and "[complete](wikipedia://en/549445)" has several other meanings in [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924). Connected relations are also called connex[^o14][^o15] or said to satisfy trichotomy[^o16] (although the more common definition of [trichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichotomy_%28mathematics%29) is stronger in that exactly one of the three options $x \mathrel{R} y, y \mathrel{R} x, x = y$ must hold).

When the relations considered are not orders, being connected and being strongly connected are importantly different properties. Sources which define both then use pairs of terms such as weakly connected and connected,[^o17] complete and strongly complete,[^o18] total and complete,[^o11] semiconnex and connex,[^o19] or connex and strictly connex,[^o20] respectively, as alternative names for the notions of connected and strongly connected as defined above.

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

Let $R$ be a [homogeneous relation](wikipedia://en/12987179). The following are equivalent:[^o19]

- $R$ is strongly connected;
- $$U \subseteq R \cup R^\top;$$
- $$\overline{R} \subseteq R^\top;$$
- $\overline{R}$ is [asymmetric](wikipedia://en/1229368),

where $U$ is the [universal relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_relation) and $R^\top$ is the [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020) of $R.$

The following are equivalent:[^o19]

- $R$ is connected;
- $$\overline{I} \subseteq R \cup R^\top;$$
- $$\overline{R} \subseteq R^\top \cup I;$$
- $\overline{R}$ is [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/1176),

where $\overline{R}$ is the [complementary relation](wikipedia://en/54347) of $R$, $I$ is the [identity relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_relation) and $R^\top$ is the [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020) of $R$.

## Properties {#Properties}

- The edge relation[^o25] $E$ of a [tournament](wikipedia://en/1427502) graph $G$ is always a connected relation on the set of $G$'s vertices.
- If a strongly connected relation is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459), it is the [universal relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_relation).
- A relation is strongly connected if, and only if, it is connected and reflexive.[^o26]
- A connected relation on a set $X$ cannot be [antitransitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitransitive), provided $X$ has at least 4 elements.[^o27] On a 3-element set $\{ a, b, c \},$ for example, the relation $\{ (a, b), (b, c), (c, a) \}$ has both properties.
- If $R$ is a connected relation on $X,$ then all, or all but one, elements of $X$ are in the [range](wikipedia://en/579311#Generalization_to_binary_relations) of $R.$[^o28] Similarly, all, or all but one, elements of $X$ are in the domain of $R.$
