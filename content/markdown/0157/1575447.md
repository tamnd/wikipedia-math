# Shear modulus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shear modulus"}

*Image: Shear scherung.svg.* Shear strain

In [solid mechanics](wikipedia://en/458866), the **shear modulus** or **modulus of rigidity**, denoted by *G*, or sometimes *S* or *μ*, is a measure of the [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29) shear stiffness of a material and is defined as the ratio of [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) to [shear strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_strain):

$$\begin{aligned} G &:= \frac {\tau_{xy}} {\gamma_{xy}} = \frac{\frac{F}{A}}{\frac{\Delta x}{l}} = \frac{F l}{A \Delta x}\\ \tau_{xy} &= \frac{F}{A}\\ \gamma_{xy} &= \frac{\Delta x}{l} \end{aligned}$$

where $\tau_{xy}$ is the shear stress, $\gamma_{xy}$ is the shear strain, $F$ is the force, $A$ is the area, $\Delta x$ is the traverse displacement, $l$ is the initial length or height.

:::

The derived [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) unit of shear modulus is the [pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_%28unit%29) (Pa), although it is usually expressed in [gigapascals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_%28unit%29) (GPa) or in thousand [pounds per square inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pounds_per_square_inch) (ksi). Its [dimensional form](wikipedia://en/8267) is M<sup>1</sup>L<sup>−1</sup>T<sup>−2</sup>, replacing *force* by *mass* times *acceleration*.

The shear modulus is one of several quantities for measuring the stiffness of materials. All of them arise in the generalized [Hooke's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law):

- [Young's modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young's_modulus) *E* describes the material's strain response to uniaxial stress in the direction of this stress (like pulling on the ends of a wire or putting a weight on top of a column, with the wire getting longer and the column losing height).
- [Poisson's ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson's_ratio) *ν* describes the response in the directions orthogonal to this uniaxial stress (the wire getting thinner and the column thicker).
- The [bulk modulus](wikipedia://en/1092110) *K* describes the material's response to (uniform) [hydrostatic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure#Fluid_pressure) (like the pressure at the bottom of the ocean or a deep swimming pool).
- The **shear modulus** *G* describes the material's response to shear stress (like cutting it with dull scissors).

These moduli are not independent, and for [isotropic](wikipedia://en/14865#Materials_science) materials they are connected via the equations[^o29]

$$E = 2G(1+\nu) = 3K(1-2\nu)$$

The shear modulus is concerned with the deformation of a solid when it experiences a force perpendicular to one of its surfaces while its opposite face experiences an opposing force (such as friction). In the case of an object shaped like a rectangular prism, it will deform into a [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975). [Anisotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropic) materials such as [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood), [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper) and also essentially all single crystals exhibit differing material response to stress or strain when tested in different directions. In this case, one may need to use the full [tensor-expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law#Tensor_expression_of_Hooke.27s_law) of the elastic constants, rather than a single scalar value.

One possible definition of a [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) would be a material with zero shear modulus.

## Shear waves {#Shear_waves}

*Image: SpiderGraph ShearModulus.GIF.* Influences of selected glass component additions on the shear modulus of a specific base glass.[^o31]

In homogeneous and [isotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic) solids, there are two kinds of waves, [pressure waves](wikipedia://en/1343550) and [shear waves](wikipedia://en/1937145). The velocity of a shear wave, $(v_s)$ is controlled by the shear modulus,

$$v_s = \sqrt{\frac {G} {\rho} }$$

where

G is the shear modulus

$\rho$ is the solid's [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density).

## Shear modulus of metals {#Shear_modulus_of_metals}

*Image: CuShearMTS.svg.* Shear modulus of copper as a function of temperature. The experimental data[^o33][^o39] are shown with colored symbols.

The shear modulus of metals is usually observed to decrease with increasing temperature. At high pressures, the shear modulus also appears to increase with the applied pressure. Correlations between the melting temperature, vacancy formation energy, and the shear modulus have been observed in many metals.[^o34]

Several models exist that attempt to predict the shear modulus of metals (and possibly that of alloys). Shear modulus models that have been used in plastic flow computations include:

1. the Varshni–Chen–Gray model developed by [Y. P. Varshni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y._P._Varshni)[^o35] and used in conjunction with the mechanical threshold stress (MTS) plastic [flow stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_stress) model.[^o36][^o37]
1. the Steinberg–Cochran–Guinan (SCG) shear modulus model developed by M. W. Guinan and D. J. Steinberg[^o38] and used in conjunction with the Steinberg-Cochran-Guinan-Lund (SCGL) flow stress model.
1. the Nadal–Le Poac (NP) shear modulus model by Marie-Hélène Nadal and Philippe Le Poac[^o39] that uses [Lindemann theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindemann_criterion) to determine the temperature dependence and the SCG model for pressure dependence of the shear modulus.

### Varshni–Chen–Gray model {#Varshni–Chen–Gray_model}

The Varshni–Chen–Gray model (sometimes referred to as the Varshni equation) has the form:

$$\mu(T) = \mu_0 - \frac{D}{\exp(T_0/T) - 1}$$

where $\mu_0$ is the shear modulus at $T=0K$, and $D$ and $T_0$ are material constants.

### SCG model {#SCG_model}

The Steinberg–Cochran–Guinan (SCG) shear modulus model is pressure dependent and has the form

$$\mu(p,T) = \mu_0 + \frac{\partial \mu}{\partial p} \frac{p}{\eta^\frac{1}{3}} + \frac{\partial \mu}{\partial T}(T - 300) ; \quad \eta := \frac{\rho}{\rho_0}$$

where, μ<sub>0</sub> is the shear modulus at the reference state (*T* = 300 K, *p* = 0, η = 1), *p* is the pressure, and *T* is the temperature.

### NP model {#NP_model}

The Nadal–Le Poac (NP) shear modulus model is a modified version of the SCG model. The empirical temperature dependence of the shear modulus in the SCG model is replaced with an equation based on [Lindemann melting theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindemann_criterion). The NP shear modulus model has the form:

$$\mu(p,T) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{J}\left(\hat{T}\right)} \left[ \left(\mu_0 + \frac{\partial \mu}{\partial p} \frac{p}{\eta^\frac{1}{3}} \right) \left(1 - \hat{T}\right) + \frac{\rho}{Cm}~T \right]; \quad C := \frac{\left(6\pi^2\right)^\frac{2}{3}}{3} f^2$$

where

$$\mathcal{J}(\hat{T}) := 1 + \exp\left[-\frac{1 + 1/\zeta} {1 + \zeta/\left(1 - \hat{T}\right)}\right] \quad \text{for} \quad \hat{T} := \frac{T}{T_m}\in[0, 6+ \zeta],$$

and μ<sub>0</sub> is the shear modulus at [absolute zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_zero) and [ambient pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_pressure), ζ is an area, *m* is the [atomic mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_mass), and *f* is the [Lindemann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindemann_criterion).

## Shear relaxation modulus {#Shear_relaxation_modulus}

The **shear relaxation modulus** $G(t)$ is the [time-dependent generalization of the shear modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_modulus)[^o44] $G$:

$$G=\lim_{t\to \infty} G(t).$$
