# Paul Seymour (mathematician)

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Paul Seymour"}

**Paul D. Seymour** (born 1950) is a British [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) known for his work in [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492), especially [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). He (with others) was responsible for important progress on [regular matroids](wikipedia://en/13415343) and [totally unimodular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_unimodular_matrix), the [four colour theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_colour_theorem), [linkless embeddings](wikipedia://en/772031), [graph minors and structure](wikipedia://en/351769), the [perfect graph](wikipedia://en/670531) conjecture, the [Hadwiger conjecture](wikipedia://en/1657860), [claw-free graphs](wikipedia://en/12415907), [χ-boundedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Χ-bounded), and the [Erdős–Hajnal conjecture](wikipedia://en/42585591). Many of his recent papers are available from his website.[^o3]

:::

Seymour is currently the [Albert Baldwin Dod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Baldwin_Dod) Professor of [Mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University).[^o4] He won a [Sloan Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Fellowship) in 1983, and the [Ostrowski Prize](wikipedia://en/3159411) in 2003;[^o5] and (sometimes with others) won the [Fulkerson Prize](wikipedia://en/1425916) in 1979, 1994, 2006 and 2009, and the [Pólya Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya_Prize) in 1983 and 2004. He received an honorary doctorate from the [University of Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Waterloo) in 2008, one from the [Technical University of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Denmark) in 2013, and one from the [École normale supérieure de Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_normale_supérieure_de_Lyon) in 2022. He was an invited speaker in the 1986 [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) and a plenary speaker in the 1994 [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842). He became a Fellow of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society) in 2022.[^o6]

## Early life {#Early_life}

Seymour was born in [Plymouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plymouth), England in 1950.[^o8] Seymour was a student at [Plymouth College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plymouth_College),[^o9] and then studied at [Exeter College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_College,_Oxford), gaining a [BA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) degree in 1971, [MS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science) degree in 1972, and [D.Phil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) and [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) in 1975.[^o13] His doctoral dissertation, *Matroids, Hypergraphs and the Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem*, was supervised by [Aubrey William Ingleton](wikipedia://en/43130429).

## Career {#Career}

From 1974 to 1976 he was a college research fellow at [University College of Swansea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swansea_University). Then he returned to Oxford for 1976–1980 as a Junior Research Fellow at [Merton College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College,_Oxford), with the year 1978–79 at [University of Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Waterloo).[^o12] He became an associate and then a full professor at [Ohio State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_State_University), [Columbus, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus,_Ohio), between 1980 and 1983, where he began research with [Neil Robertson](wikipedia://en/1575704), a fruitful collaboration that continued for many years. From 1983 until 1996, he was a senior scientist at [Bellcore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellcore) (Bell Communications Research), [Morristown, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morristown,_New_Jersey) (now [Telcordia Technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telcordia_Technologies)). He was also an adjunct professor at [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University) from 1984 to 1987 and at the University of Waterloo from 1988 to 1993. He became professor at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in 1996.[^o13] At Princeton he was given the Albert Baldwin Dod Professorship in 2016.[^o8]

He is Editor-in-Chief (jointly with [Carsten Thomassen](wikipedia://en/7460839)) for the *[Journal of Graph Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Graph_Theory)*,[^o14] and an editor for *[Combinatorica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorica)*[^o15] and the *[Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Combinatorial_Theory)*.[^o16]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Seymour's brother [Leonard W. Seymour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_W._Seymour) is Professor of [gene therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_therapy) at [Oxford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University).[^o18] In 1979, Seymour got married and had two children.[^o8]

## Major contributions {#Major_contributions}

Combinatorics in Oxford in the 1970s was dominated by [matroid theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matroid_theory), due to the influence of [Dominic Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominic_Welsh) and [Aubrey William Ingleton](wikipedia://en/43130429). Much of Seymour's early work, up to about 1980, was on matroid theory, and included three important matroid results: his D.Phil. thesis on matroids with the max-flow min-cut property[^o20] (for which he won his first Fulkerson prize); a characterisation by excluded minors of the matroids representable over the three-element field;[^o21] and a theorem that all [regular matroids](wikipedia://en/13415343) consist of graphic and cographic matroids, and a special matroid called *R*<sub>10</sub>, pieced together in a simple way[^o22] (which won his first [Pólya prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya_Prize)). There were several other significant papers from this period: a paper with Welsh on the critical probabilities for bond percolation on the square lattice;[^o23] a paper on edge-multicolouring of cubic graphs,[^o24] which foreshadows the matching lattice theorem of [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938); a paper proving that all bridgeless graphs admit nowhere-zero 6-flows,[^o25] a step towards [Tutte's nowhere-zero 5-flow conjecture](wikipedia://en/9909979); and a paper solving the two-paths problem (also introducing the [cycle double cover](wikipedia://en/21486302) conjecture),[^o26] which was the engine behind much of Seymour's future work.

In 1980 he moved to Ohio State University, and began work with Neil Robertson. This led eventually to Seymour's most important accomplishment, the so-called "Graph Minors Project", a series of 23 papers (joint with Robertson), published over the next thirty years, with several significant results: the [graph minors structure theorem](wikipedia://en/21318521), that for any fixed graph, all graphs that do not contain it as a minor can be built from graphs that are essentially of bounded genus by piecing them together at small cutsets in a tree structure;[^o27] a proof of a conjecture of [Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Wagner) that in any infinite set of graphs, one of them is a minor of another (and consequently that any property of graphs that can be characterised by excluded minors can be characterised by a finite list of excluded minors);[^o28] a proof of a similar conjecture of [Nash-Williams](wikipedia://en/16550517) that in any infinite set of graphs, one of them can be immersed in another;[^o29] and polynomial-time algorithms to test if a graph contains a fixed graph as a minor, and to solve the k vertex-disjoint paths problem for all fixed k.[^o30]

In about 1990 [Robin Thomas](wikipedia://en/24641679) began to work with Robertson and Seymour. Their collaboration resulted in several important joint papers over the next ten years: a proof of a conjecture of [Sachs](wikipedia://en/15095266), characterising by excluded minors the graphs that admit [linkless embeddings](wikipedia://en/772031) in 3-space;[^o31] a proof that every graph that is not five-colourable has a six-vertex complete graph as a minor (the four-colour theorem is assumed to obtain this result, which is a case of [Hadwiger's conjecture](wikipedia://en/1657860));[^o32] with [Dan Sanders](wikipedia://en/23418323), a new, simplified, computer based proof of the [four-colour theorem](wikipedia://en/10949);[^o33] and a description of the bipartite graphs that admit [Pfaffian orientations](wikipedia://en/56281176).[^o34] In the same period, Seymour and Thomas also published several significant results: (with [Noga Alon](wikipedia://en/4939651)) a separator theorem for graphs with an excluded minor,[^o35] extending the [planar separator theorem](wikipedia://en/18978005) of [Richard Lipton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lipton) and [Robert Tarjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Tarjan); a paper characterizing [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086) in terms of [brambles](wikipedia://en/36601188);[^o36] and a polynomial-time algorithm to compute the [branch-width](wikipedia://en/16823137) of planar graphs.[^o37]

In 2000, Robertson, Seymour, and Thomas were supported by the [American Institute of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/3248005) to work on the [strong perfect graph conjecture](wikipedia://en/670531), a famous open question that had been raised by [Claude Berge](wikipedia://en/5893760) in the early 1960s. Seymour's student [Maria Chudnovsky](wikipedia://en/19731900) joined them in 2001, and in 2002 the four jointly proved the conjecture.[^o38] Seymour continued to work with Chudnovsky, and obtained several more results about induced subgraphs, in particular (with [Cornuéjols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gérard_Cornuéjols), Liu, and [Vušković](wikipedia://en/66718247)) a polynomial-time algorithm to test whether a graph is perfect,[^o39] and a general description of all claw-free graphs.[^o40] Other important results in this period include: (with Seymour's student [Sang-il Oum](wikipedia://en/64105796)) [fixed-parameter tractable](wikipedia://en/603026) algorithms to approximate the [clique-width](wikipedia://en/16795502) of graphs (within an exponential bound) and the branch-width of matroids (within a linear bound);[^o41] and (with Chudnovsky) a proof that the roots of the independence polynomial of every claw-free graph are real.[^o42]

In the 2010s, Seymour worked mainly on [χ-boundedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Χ-bounded) and the [Erdős–Hajnal conjecture](wikipedia://en/42585591). In a series of papers with Alex Scott and partly with Chudnovsky, they proved two conjectures of [András Gyárfás](wikipedia://en/614969), that every graph with bounded clique number and sufficiently large chromatic number has an induced cycle of odd length at least five,[^o43] and has an induced cycle of length at least any specified number.[^o44] The series culminated in a paper of Scott and Seymour proving that for every fixed k, every graph with sufficiently large chromatic number contains either a large complete subgraph or induced cycles of all lengths modulo k,[^o45] which leads to the resolutions of two conjectures of [Gil Kalai](wikipedia://en/19709056) and Roy Meshulam connecting the chromatic number of a graph with the [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) of its [independence complex](wikipedia://en/64659151). There was also a polynomial-time algorithm (with Chudnovsky, Scott, and Chudnovsky and Seymour's student Sophie Spirkl) to test whether a graph contains an induced cycle with length more than three and odd.[^o46] Most recently, the four jointly resolved the 5-cycle case of the Erdős–Hajnal conjecture, which says that every graph without an induced copy of the 5-cycle contains an independent set or a clique of polynomial size.[^o47]
