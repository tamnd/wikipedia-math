# Symmetric graph

*Image: Petersen1 tiny.svg.* The [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) is a ([cubic](wikipedia://en/980508)) symmetric graph. Any pair of adjacent vertices can be mapped to another by an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186), since any five-vertex ring can be mapped to any other.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symmetric graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ is **symmetric** or **arc-transitive** if, given any two ordered pairs of adjacent [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) $(u_1,v_1)$ and $(u_2,v_2)$ of $G$, there is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186)

:::

$$f : V(G) \rightarrow V(G)$$

such that

$f(u_1) = u_2$ and $f(v_1) = v_2.$[^o2]

In other words, a graph is symmetric if its [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) acts [transitively](wikipedia://en/12781#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) on [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) of adjacent vertices (that is, upon edges considered as having a direction).[^o3] Such a graph is sometimes also called **1-arc-transitive**[^o3] or **flag-transitive**.[^o4]

By definition (ignoring $u_{1}$ and $u_{2}$), a symmetric graph without [isolated vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_vertex) must also be [vertex-transitive](wikipedia://en/581175).[^o2] Since the definition above maps one edge to another, a symmetric graph must also be [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/584406). However, an edge-transitive graph need not be symmetric, since $a\text{—}b$ might map to $c\text{—}d$, but not to $d\text{—}c$. [Star graphs](wikipedia://en/19310129) are a simple example of being edge-transitive without being vertex-transitive or symmetric. As a further example, [semi-symmetric graphs](wikipedia://en/2554240) are edge-transitive and [regular](wikipedia://en/85821), but not vertex-transitive.

Every [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) symmetric graph must thus be both vertex-transitive and edge-transitive, and the converse is true for graphs of [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) degree.[^o4] However, for [even](wikipedia://en/143135) degree, there exist connected graphs which are vertex-transitive and edge-transitive, but not symmetric.[^o5] Such graphs are called [half-transitive](wikipedia://en/24229305).[^o6] The smallest connected half-transitive graph is [Holt's graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holt's_graph), with degree 4 and 27 vertices.[^o2][^o7] Confusingly, some authors use the term "symmetric graph" to mean a graph which is vertex-transitive and edge-transitive, rather than an arc-transitive graph. Such a definition would include half-transitive graphs, which are excluded under the definition above.

A [distance-transitive graph](wikipedia://en/7768222) is one where instead of considering pairs of adjacent vertices (i.e. vertices a distance of 1 apart), the definition covers two pairs of vertices, each the same distance apart. Such graphs are automatically symmetric, by definition.[^o2]

A $t$-arc is defined to be a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of $t + 1$ vertices, such that any two consecutive vertices in the sequence are adjacent, and with any repeated vertices being more than 2 steps apart. A **$t$-transitive graph** is a graph such that the automorphism group acts transitively on $t$-arcs, but not on ($t + 1$)-arcs. Since 1-arcs are simply edges, every symmetric graph of degree 3 or more must be $t$-transitive for some $t$, and the value of $t$ can be used to further classify symmetric graphs. The cube is 2-transitive, for example.[^o2]

Note that conventionally the term "symmetric graph" is not complementary to the term "[asymmetric graph](wikipedia://en/20249418)," as the latter refers to a graph that has no nontrivial symmetries at all.

## Examples {#Examples}

Two basic families of symmetric graphs for any number of vertices are the [cycle graphs](wikipedia://en/878962) (of degree 2) and the [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816). Further symmetric graphs are formed by the vertices and edges of the regular and quasiregular polyhedra: the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968), [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361), [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280), and [icosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/15023). Extension of the cube to n dimensions gives the hypercube graphs (with 2<sup>n</sup> vertices and degree n). Similarly extension of the octahedron to n dimensions gives the graphs of the [cross-polytopes](wikipedia://en/716401), this family of graphs (with 2n vertices and degree 2n − 2) are sometimes referred to as the [cocktail party graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocktail_party_graph) - they are complete graphs with a set of edges making a perfect matching removed. Additional families of symmetric graphs with an even number of vertices 2n, are the evenly split [complete bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/525320) K<sub>n,n</sub> and the [crown graphs](wikipedia://en/21062705) on 2n vertices. Many other symmetric graphs can be classified as [circulant graphs](wikipedia://en/3498787) (but not all).

The [Rado graph](wikipedia://en/7785594) forms an example of a symmetric graph with infinitely many vertices and infinite degree.

### Cubic symmetric graphs {#Cubic_symmetric_graphs}

Combining the symmetry condition with the restriction that graphs be [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508) (i.e. all vertices have degree 3) yields quite a strong condition, and such graphs are rare enough to be listed. They all have an even number of vertices. The **Foster census** and its extensions provide such lists.[^o11] The Foster census was begun in the 1930s by [Ronald M. Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._M._Foster) while he was employed by [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs),[^o12] and in 1988 (when Foster was 92[^o2]) the then current Foster census (listing all cubic symmetric graphs up to 512 vertices) was published in book form.[^o13] The first thirteen items in the list are cubic symmetric graphs with up to 30 vertices[^o14][^o15] (ten of these are also [distance-transitive](wikipedia://en/7768222); the exceptions are as indicated):

| 6 | 2 | 4 | The [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) *K*<sub>3,3</sub> | distance-transitive, 3-arc-transitive |
| 8 | 3 | 4 | The vertices and edges of the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) | distance-transitive, 2-arc-transitive |

Other well known cubic symmetric graphs are the [Dyck graph](wikipedia://en/24041180), the [Foster graph](wikipedia://en/11197065) and the [Biggs&ndash;Smith graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biggs&ndash;Smith_graph). The ten distance-transitive graphs listed above, together with the [Foster graph](wikipedia://en/11197065) and the [Biggs&ndash;Smith graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biggs&ndash;Smith_graph), are the only cubic distance-transitive graphs.

## Properties {#Properties}

The [vertex-connectivity](wikipedia://en/1614492) of a symmetric graph is always equal to the [degree](wikipedia://en/85821) *d*.[^o4] In contrast, for [vertex-transitive graphs](wikipedia://en/581175) in general, the vertex-connectivity is bounded below by 2(*d* + 1)/3.[^o3]

A *t*-transitive graph of degree 3 or more has [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) at least 2(*t* − 1). However, there are no finite *t*-transitive graphs of degree 3 or more for *t* ≥ 8. In the case of the degree being exactly 3 (cubic symmetric graphs), there are none for *t* ≥ 6.
