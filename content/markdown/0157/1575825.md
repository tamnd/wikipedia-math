# Hyperbolic partial differential equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperbolic partial differential equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **hyperbolic partial differential equation** of order $n$ is a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) (PDE) that, roughly speaking, has a well-posed [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383) for the first $n - 1$ derivatives. More precisely, the [Cauchy problem](wikipedia://en/1725027) can be locally solved for arbitrary initial data along any non-characteristic [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101). Many of the equations of [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) are hyperbolic, and so the study of hyperbolic equations is of substantial contemporary interest. The model hyperbolic equation is the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691). In one spatial dimension, this is

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$$

The equation has the property that, if $u$ and its first time derivative are arbitrarily specified initial data on the line $t = 0$ (with sufficient smoothness properties), then there exists a solution for all time $t$.

:::

The solutions of hyperbolic equations are "wave-like". If a disturbance is made in the initial data of a hyperbolic differential equation, then not every point of space feels the disturbance at once. Relative to a fixed time coordinate, disturbances have a finite [propagation speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propagation_speed). They travel along the [characteristics](wikipedia://en/751933) of the equation. This feature qualitatively distinguishes hyperbolic equations from [elliptic partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/662787) and [parabolic partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/7466971). A perturbation of the initial (or boundary) data of an elliptic or parabolic equation is felt at once by essentially all points in the domain.

Although the definition of hyperbolicity is fundamentally a qualitative one, there are precise criteria that depend on the particular kind of differential equation under consideration. There is a well-developed theory for linear [differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_operators), due to [Lars Gårding](wikipedia://en/8000370), in the context of [microlocal analysis](wikipedia://en/6108841). Nonlinear differential equations are hyperbolic if their linearizations are hyperbolic in the sense of Gårding. There is a somewhat different theory for first order systems of equations coming from systems of [conservation laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A partial differential equation is hyperbolic at a point $P$ provided that the [Cauchy problem](wikipedia://en/1725027) is uniquely solvable in a neighborhood of $P$ for any initial data given on a [non-characteristic hypersurface](wikipedia://en/751933) passing through $P$. Here the prescribed initial data consist of all (transverse) derivatives of the function on the surface up to one less than the order of the differential equation.

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

By a linear change of variables, any equation of the form

$$A\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + 2B\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\partial y} + C\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2} + \text{(lower order derivative terms)} = 0$$

with [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607)

$$B^2 - A C > 0$$

can be transformed to the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691), apart from lower order terms which are inessential for the qualitative understanding of the equation.[^o7]p. 400 This definition is analogous to the definition of a planar [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052#Quadratic_equation).

The one-dimensional [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691):

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} - c^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 0$$

is an example of a hyperbolic equation. The two-dimensional and three-dimensional wave equations also fall into the category of hyperbolic PDE. This type of second-order hyperbolic partial differential equation may be transformed to a hyperbolic system of first-order differential equations.[^o7]p. 402

:::

## Hyperbolic systems of first-order equations {#Hyperbolic_systems_of_first-order_equations}

The following is a system of first-order partial differential equations for $s$ unknown [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) $\vec u = (u_1, \ldots, u_s)$, $\vec u = \vec u (\vec x,t)$, where $\vec x \in \mathbb{R}^d$:

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="∗"}

$$\frac{\partial \vec u}{\partial t} + \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} \vec {f}^j (\vec u) = 0, \tag{∗}$$

:::

where $\vec {f}^j \in C^1(\mathbb{R}^s, \mathbb{R}^s)$ are once [continuously](wikipedia://en/6122) [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) functions, [nonlinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear) in general.

Next, for each $\vec {f}^j$ define the $s \times s$ [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix)

$$A^j := \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1^j}{\partial u_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1^j}{\partial u_s} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_s^j}{\partial u_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_s^j}{\partial u_s} \end{pmatrix} ,\text{ for }j = 1, \ldots, d.$$

The system ((∗)) is **hyperbolic** if for all $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_d \in \mathbb{R}$ the matrix $A := \alpha_1 A^1 + \cdots + \alpha_d A^d$ has only [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) and is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325).

If the matrix $A$ has $s$ *distinct* real eigenvalues, it follows that it is diagonalizable. In this case the system ((∗)) is called **strictly hyperbolic**.

If the matrix $A$ is symmetric, it follows that it is diagonalizable and the eigenvalues are real. In this case the system ((∗)) is called **symmetric hyperbolic**.

::: {#o11 .statement cue=named-heading name="Hyperbolic system and conservation laws"}

### Hyperbolic system and conservation laws {#Hyperbolic_system_and_conservation_laws}

There is a connection between a hyperbolic system and a [conservation law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29). Consider a hyperbolic system of one partial differential equation for one unknown function $u = u(\vec x, t)$. Then the system ((∗)) has the form

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="∗∗"}

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \sum_{j=1}^d \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} {f^j} (u) = 0. \tag{∗∗}$$

:::

Here, $u$ can be interpreted as a quantity that moves around according to the [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) given by $\vec f = (f^1, \ldots, f^d)$. To see that the quantity $u$ is conserved, [integrate](wikipedia://en/15532) ((∗∗)) over a domain $\Omega$

$$\int_{\Omega} \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} \, d\Omega + \int_{\Omega} \nabla \cdot \vec f(u)\, d\Omega = 0.$$

If $u$ and $\vec f$ are sufficiently smooth functions, we can use the [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864) and change the order of the integration and $\partial / \partial t$ to get a conservation law for the quantity $u$ in the general form

$$\frac{ d}{ dt} \int_{\Omega} u \, d\Omega + \int_{\partial\Omega} \vec f(u) \cdot \vec n \, d\Gamma = 0,$$

which means that the time rate of change of $u$ in the domain $\Omega$ is equal to the net flux of $u$ through its boundary $\partial\Omega$. Since this is an equality, it can be concluded that $u$ is conserved within $\Omega$.

:::
