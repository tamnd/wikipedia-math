# Theta representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Theta representation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **theta representation** is a particular representation of the [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). It gains its name from the fact that the [Jacobi theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_theta_function) is invariant under the action of a discrete subgroup of the Heisenberg group. The representation was popularized by [David Mumford](wikipedia://en/394594).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

The theta representation is a representation of the continuous Heisenberg group $H_3(\mathbb{R})$ over the field of the real numbers. In this representation, the group elements act on a particular [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). The construction below proceeds first by defining [operators](wikipedia://en/22213) that correspond to the Heisenberg group generators. Next, the Hilbert space on which these act is defined, followed by a demonstration of the [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) to the usual representations.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Operators and group law"}

### Operators and group law {#Operators_and_group_law}

Let *f*(*z*) be a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110), let *a* and *b* be [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), and let $\tau$ be an arbitrary fixed [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) in the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826); that is, so that the imaginary part of $\tau$ is positive. Define the operators *S<sub>a</sub>* and *T<sub>b</sub>* such that they act on holomorphic functions as

$$(S_a f)(z) = f(z+a)= \exp(a\partial_z)f(z)$$

and

$$(T_b f)(z) = \exp(i\pi b^2 \tau +2\pi ibz) f(z+b\tau) = \exp(i\pi b^2 \tau + 2\pi i bz + b\tau\partial_z) f(z).$$

It can be seen that each operator generates a one-parameter subgroup:

$$S_{a_1}\left(S_{a_2}f\right)=S_{a_1+a_2}f$$

and

$$T_{b_1}\left(T_{b_2}f\right)=T_{b_1+b_2}f.$$

However, *S* and *T* do not commute:

$$S_aT_b=\exp(2\pi iab)T_bS_a.$$

Thus $S$ and $T$ together with a [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999) phase form a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), the continuous real [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518), parametrizable as $H=U(1)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, where $U(1)$ is the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993).

A general group element $U_\tau(\lambda,a,b)\in H$ then acts on a holomorphic function *f*(*z*) as

$$U_\tau(\lambda,a,b) f(z) = \lambda(S_aT_bf)(z) = \lambda\exp(i\pi b^2\tau+2\pi ibz)f(z+a+b\tau),$$

where $\lambda\in U(1)$. The subgroup $U(1)$ is the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) $Z(H)$ of $H$, and is also its [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) $[H,H]$. The parameter $\tau$ on $U_\tau(\lambda,a,b)$ serves only to remind that every different value of $\tau$ gives rise to a different representation of the action of the group.

:::

### Hilbert space {#Hilbert_space}

The action of the group elements $U_\tau(\lambda,a,b)$ is unitary and irreducible on a certain Hilbert space of functions. For a fixed value of $\tau$, define a norm on [entire functions](wikipedia://en/10372) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) as

$$\Vert f \Vert_\tau ^2 = \int_{\mathbb{C}} \exp\left(\frac{-2\pi y^2}{\Im\tau}\right) |f(x+iy)|^2\,dx\,dy.$$

Here, $\Im\tau$ is the imaginary part of $\tau$ and the domain of integration is the entire complex plane. Let $\mathcal H_\tau$ be the set of entire functions *f* with finite norm. The subscript $\tau$ is used only to indicate that the space depends on the choice of parameter $\tau$. This $\mathcal H_\tau$ forms a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). The action of $U_\tau(\lambda,a,b)$ given above is unitary on $\mathcal H_\tau$, that is, $U_\tau(\lambda,a,b)$ preserves the norm on this space. Finally, the action of $U_\tau(\lambda,a,b)$ on $\mathcal H_\tau$ is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/385982).

This norm is closely related to that used to define the [Segal–Bargmann space](wikipedia://en/40731477).

### Relation with the Weyl representation {#Relation_with_the_Weyl_representation}

The above theta representation of the Heisenberg group is isomorphic to the canonical [Weyl representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_representation) of the Heisenberg group. In particular, this implies that $\mathcal H_\tau$ and [$L^2(\mathbb{R})$](wikipedia://en/45194) are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) as [$H$-modules](wikipedia://en/276410). Let

$$M(a,b,c)= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & a & c\\ 0 & 1 & b\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

stand for a general group element of $H_3(\mathbb{R})$. In the canonical Weyl representation, for every real number *h*, there is a representation $\rho_h$ acting on $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ as

$$\rho_h(M(a,b,c))\psi(x)=\exp(ibx+ihc)\psi(x+ha)$$

for $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{R})$.

Here, *h* is the [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant). Each such representation is [unitarily inequivalent](wikipedia://en/292788). The corresponding theta representation is:

$$M(a,0,0)\mapsto S_{ah},$$

$$M(0,b,0)\mapsto T_{b/2\pi},$$

$$M(0,0,c)\mapsto e^{ihc}.$$

:::

## Theta functions {#Theta_functions}

The Heisenberg group can be used to give a unified account of [theta functions](wikipedia://en/449745) in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). For $\tau$ in the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826), the standard [Jacobi theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_theta_function) is

$$\vartheta(z,\tau)= \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z} \exp(\pi i n^2\tau+2\pi inz).$$

It is an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) of $z$ satisfying the transformation laws

$$\vartheta(z+1,\tau)=\vartheta(z,\tau), \qquad \vartheta(z+\tau,\tau) = e^{-\pi i\tau-2\pi iz}\vartheta(z,\tau).$$

More generally, for integers $a$ and $b$,

$$\vartheta(z+a+b\tau,\tau) = \exp(-\pi ib^2\tau-2\pi ibz)\vartheta(z,\tau).$$

Define the subgroup $\Gamma_\tau\subset H$ as

$$\Gamma_\tau=\{U_\tau(1,a,b)\in H:a,b\in\mathbb Z\}.$$

The preceding transformation laws say exactly that $\vartheta(z,\tau)$ is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under $\Gamma_\tau$. It can be shown that the Jacobi theta function is the unique such entire function, up to scalar multiple.[^o9]

Thus $\vartheta(z,\tau)$ is not an ordinary function on the [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225)

$$E_\tau=\mathbb C/(\mathbb Z+\tau\mathbb Z),$$

but rather a section of a [line bundle](wikipedia://en/361366) on $E_\tau$. In one common convention, this line bundle is obtained from $\mathbb C\times\mathbb C$ by the identifications

$$(z,w)\sim(z+1,w), \qquad (z,w)\sim(z+\tau,e^{-\pi i\tau-2\pi iz}w).$$

The exponential factors in the theta transformation laws are the corresponding [factors of automorphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_of_automorphy). In this sense, a theta function is a function on the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) whose transformation law allows it to descend as a section of a line bundle on the quotient torus.[^o9]

The Heisenberg group appears because translations of $z$ must be accompanied by scalar factors in order to preserve these transformation laws. Thus translations in the two period directions define a [projective representation](wikipedia://en/348860), and the corresponding central extension is a Heisenberg group.

### Theta functions with characteristics {#Theta_functions_with_characteristics}

Theta functions with [rational characteristics](wikipedia://en/7193470) are obtained by applying Heisenberg operators to $\vartheta$. For $a,b\in\mathbb Q$, define

$$\vartheta_{a,b}(z,\tau) = (S_bT_a\vartheta)(z,\tau) = e^{\pi ia^2\tau+2\pi ia(z+b)} \vartheta(z+a\tau+b,\tau).$$

Equivalently,

$$\vartheta_{a,b}(z,\tau) = \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z} \exp\!\left(\pi i(n+a)^2\tau +2\pi i(n+a)(z+b)\right).$$

Changing $a$ and $b$ by integers changes these functions only by simple scalar factors, so the characteristics are naturally considered modulo $\mathbb Z$.

This gives a concrete finite-dimensional form of the Heisenberg representation. If $L$ is the degree-one theta line bundle on $E_\tau$, then

$$\dim H^0(E_\tau,L^{\otimes N})=N.$$

A basis of this space may be chosen from theta functions with characteristics. The action of translations by $N$-torsion points, together with the necessary scalar factors, gives a finite Heisenberg group acting on $H^0(E_\tau,L^{\otimes N})$. This is the finite-dimensional analogue of the Schrödinger representation.[^o9]

## Theta groups {#Theta_groups}

More generally, one can associate to a line bundle $L$ on an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) $A$ its theta group $G(L)$. Let

$$K(L)=\{x\in A\mid t_x^*L\simeq L\},$$

where $t_x:A\to A$ denotes translation by $x$. The theta group consists of pairs $(x,\phi)$, where $x\in K(L)$ and $\phi:L\to t_x^*L$ is an isomorphism of line bundles. It fits into a central extension

$$1\longrightarrow \mathbb G_m \longrightarrow G(L) \longrightarrow K(L) \longrightarrow 1.$$

When $L$ is ample, $K(L)$ is finite, and $G(L)$ is a finite algebraic analogue of the Heisenberg group.[^o12][^o13]

The group $G(L)$ acts naturally on the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of sections $H^0(A,L)$. This action is the algebro-geometric analogue of the Schrödinger representation of the real Heisenberg group. When $L$ is ample, the commutator in $G(L)$ induces a nondegenerate alternating pairing on $K(L)$, analogous to the symplectic form used in the construction of the ordinary Heisenberg group. A finite version of the Stone–von Neumann theorem describes the resulting irreducible representation with prescribed central character.[^o13]

[David Mumford](wikipedia://en/394594) used this Heisenberg-group formalism to give an algebraic theory of theta functions and to study equations defining abelian varieties.[^o12][^o13]
