# Murray's law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Murray's law"}

In [biophysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysics) [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), **Murray's law** is a potential relationship between [radii](wikipedia://en/1780815) at [junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junction_%28traffic%29) in a network of fluid-carrying [tubular](wikipedia://en/859283) [pipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport). Its simplest version proposes that whenever a branch of [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) $r$ splits into two branches of radii $r_1$ and $r_2$, then the three radii should obey the equation

$$r^3 = r_1^3 + r_2^3\text{.}$$

If network flow is [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminar_flow) and [leak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leak)-free, then systems that obey Murray's law minimize the resistance to flow through the network. For [turbulent](wikipedia://en/154664) networks, the law takes the same form but with a different [characteristic exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_law) $\alpha$.

:::

Murray's law is observed in the [vascular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_vessel) and [respiratory systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_systems) of animals, [xylem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem) in plants, and the respiratory system of insects. In principle, Murray's law also applies to [biomimetic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomimetics), but human designs rarely exploit the law.

Murray's law is named after [Cecil D. Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_D._Murray), a [physiologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiologist) at [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College), who first argued that efficient transport might determine the structure of the human [vascular system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_system).

## Assumptions {#Assumptions}

Murray's law assumes material is passively transported by the flow of fluid in a network of [tubular](wikipedia://en/859283) [pipes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport),[^o3] and that the network requires energy to maintain both flow and structural integrity.[^o4] Variation in the fluid [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) across scales will affect the Murray's law exponent, but is usually too small to matter.[^n1]

At least two different conditions are known in which the cube exponent is optimal.

In the first, organisms have free (variable) circulatory volume. Also, maintenance energy is not proportional to the pipe material, but instead the quantity of working fluid. The latter assumption is justified in metabolically active biological fluids, such as [blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood).[^o6] It is also justified for metabolically *inactive* fluids, such as air, as long as the energetic "cost" of the infrastructure scales with the [cross-sectional area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-sectional_area) of each tube; such is the case for all known biological tubules.[^o7]

In the second, organisms have fixed circulatory volume and pressure, but wish to minimize the resistance to flow through the system. Equivalently, maintenance is negligible and organisms with to maximize the [volumetric flow rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volumetric_flow_rate).[^n2]

Although most derivations of Murray's law assume a [steady state](wikipedia://en/1326107) [flow field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_%28fluid%29), the same results apply for flow in tubes that have a moderate to small width, relative to the flow [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125).[^n3]

## Derivation in laminar, mass-conservative networks with non-negligible upkeep {#Derivation_in_laminar,_mass-conservative_networks_with_non-negligible_upkeep}

*Image: Flux de Poiseuille.png.* Poiseuille flow in a cylinder of [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) $h$; the [velocity field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_field) at height $y$ is $u(y)$.

Murray's original derivation uses the first set of assumptions described above. She begins with the [Hagen–Poiseuille equation](wikipedia://en/17016531), which states that for fluid of [dynamic viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_viscosity) $\mu$, flowing [laminarly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminar_flow) through a [cylindrical](wikipedia://en/859283) pipe of radius $r$ and length $l$, the [volumetric flow rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volumetric_flow_rate) $Q$ associated with a pressure drop $\Delta p$ is

$$Q = \frac{\pi}{8\mu}\frac{r^4}{l}\Delta p$$

and the corresponding [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28physics%29) consumed is[^o3]

$$P = \frac{8\mu}{\pi}\frac{lQ^2}{r^4}\text{.}$$

Said pipe contains volume $\pi lr^{2}$. If the maintenance power density is $\lambda$, then the total power consumed (from both flow and upkeep) is

$$\sum{P}=\frac{8\mu}{\pi}\frac{lQ^2}{r^4}+\pi\lambda lr^2\text{.}$$

Minimizing this quantity depends on precisely which variables the organism is free to manipulate, but the minimum invariably occurs when the two terms are proportional to each other.[^o11] In that minimal case, the proportionality determines a relationship between $Q$ and $r$. Canceling common factors and taking a square root,

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$Q\propto r^3\text{.} \tag{1}$$

:::

That is, when using as little energy as possible, the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_%28physics%29) flowing through the pipe must be proportional to the cube of the pipe's radius. Since flow is leakless, the total flow rate into a junction [must](wikipedia://en/410793) be the total flow rate out:

$$\sum_{\text{in}}{Q}=\sum_{\text{out}}{Q}\text{.}$$

Substituting ((1)) then gives Murray's law with $\alpha=3$.[^o13] This characteristic exponent also holds for laminar flow of [non-Newtonian fluid models](wikipedia://en/75367), such as [power-law](wikipedia://en/482843), [Bingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bingham_fluid) and [Herschel-Bulkley](wikipedia://en/18659441) fluids.[^o14]

For the limit of high [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856) [turbulent flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulent_Flow), transport resistance is minimized when $\alpha=\tfrac{7}{3}$ in[^o15][^o16]

$$Q \propto r^\alpha.$$

Depending on the fluid type, the channel wall roughness and the Reynolds number of the flow in the network, the characteristic exponent $\alpha$ decreases from 3 towards $\tfrac{7}{3}$ with increasing Reynolds number.[^o17]

## Exponents for other networks {#Exponents_for_other_networks}

If the network does not rely on transported material getting "swept up in the flow", but instead expects it to passively diffuse, then resistance to transport is minimized when $\alpha=2$: that is,

$$\sum_\text{in}{r^2}=\sum_\text{out}{r^2}\text{.}$$

The same law would apply to a [direct-current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct-current) [electrical grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_grid) composed of wires of only one material, but varying [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007).[^n4]

In general, networks intermediate between diffusion and laminar flow are expected to have characteristic exponents between 2 and 3, at least approximately.[^o20][^n5]

## Experimental tests {#Experimental_tests}

Murray's law has been verified in chicks; dog intestines and lungs; cat [mesentery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesentery); and human intestines and lung capillaries.[^n6][^n7] Mice [genetically engineered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetically_engineer) to lack the blood-vessel-wall protein [elastin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastin) have smaller and thinner blood vessels, but still obey Murray's law.[^n8]

In humans, large vessels, such as the [aorta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aorta) or [trachea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trachea), do not appear to obey Murray's law, instead obeying a Murray's law with exponent close to 2.[^n7] But flow in those vessels is also partially turbulent, and so should exhibit an exponent nearer to $\tfrac{7}{3}$ than to 3.[^o27]

*Image: Murray materials in leaf and insect with a parent pore (radius r0) connecting to many children pores (radius ri). .jpg.* The Murray law in plants and insects.[^n5]

Insects do not have a fully-fledged circulatory system, instead relying on passive [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) through the [haemocoel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemocoel). For those networks, Murray's law predicts constant cross-sectional area, which is observed.[^n4]

The same arguments that imply Murray's law also imply that the distribution of tubules should exhibit a specific [power law](wikipedia://en/24522) scaling with size. Plant [xylem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem) is known to exhibit that scaling except in scenarios where the passages double as [structural supports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_support).[^n9][^n10]

## History {#History}

The first phenomenon now recognized as Murray's law is [Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_%28scientist%29)'s rule for circulatory systems, which states that two identical subcapillaries should combine to form a capillary with radius about $1.26\approx\sqrt[3]{2}$ times larger, and dates to the [early 19th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_era).[^n11] Bryn Mawr physiologist [Cecil D. Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cecil_D._Murray) published the law's modern, general formulation in 1926,[^o34][^n11] but it languished in a disciplinary no-man's-land for the next fifty years: too trivial for physicists and too complicated for biologists.[^n11] Interest in the law revived in the 1970s.[^n11]

## Applications {#Applications}

In circulatory system governed by Murray's law with $\alpha=3$, [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) on vessel walls is roughly constant. Consequently, variations in shear stress are a sign of deviation from Murray's law; Rodbard and Zamir suggest that such variations stimulate [homeostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeostasis) growth or contraction.[^n12]

Murray's law rarely applies to engineered materials, because man-made transport routes attempt to reduce flow resistance by minimizing branching and maximizing diameter.[^o41]

### Murray materials {#Murray_materials}

*Image: Murray materials.jpg.* Zheng achieved Murray fractal branching through layering porous zinc oxide nanoparticle sheets.[^n5]

Materials that obey Murray's law at the microscale, known as **Murray materials**, are expected to have favorable flow characteristics, but their construction is difficult, because it requires tight control over [pore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pore_structure) size typically over a wide range of scales.[^n5][^n13]

Lim *et al* propose designing [microfluidic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microfluidic) "[labs on a chip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lab-on-a-chip)" in accord with Murray's law to minimize flow resistance during analysis. Conventional lithography does not support such construction, because it cannot produce channels of varying depth.[^n14]

Seeking long-lived [lithium battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_battery) [electrodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrode), Zheng *et al* constructed Murray materials out of layers of [sintered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sintering) [zinc oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc_oxide) [nanoparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanoparticle). The evaporation rate of the dissolved zinc oxide [solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvent) controlled the size of the pores in each layer; the network was then just layers of ZnO with different pore sizes placed atop each other.[^n5]

Because [power plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_plant) [working fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_fluid) typically funnel into many small tubules for efficient [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726), Murray's law may be appropriate for [nuclear reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactor) design.[^o48]
