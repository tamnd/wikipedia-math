# Banzhaf power index

*Image: BanzhafPowerIndex.jpg.* Computer model of the Banzhaf power index from the [Wolfram Demonstrations Project](wikipedia://en/13989702)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Banzhaf power index"}

The **Banzhaf power index**, named after [John Banzhaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Banzhaf) (originally invented by [Lionel Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Penrose) in 1946 and sometimes called **Penrose–Banzhaf index**; also known as the **Banzhaf–Coleman index** after [James Samuel Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Samuel_Coleman)), is a [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28social_and_political%29) index defined by the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) of changing an [outcome](wikipedia://en/3736689) of a [vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting) where voting rights are not necessarily equally divided among the voters or [shareholders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shareholder).

:::

To calculate the power of a voter using the Banzhaf index, list all the winning coalitions, then count the critical voters. A *critical voter* is a voter who, if changing their vote from yes to no, would cause the measure to fail. A voter's power is measured as the fraction of all swing votes that they could cast. There are some algorithms for calculating the power index, e.g., [dynamic programming](wikipedia://en/125297) techniques, enumeration methods and [Monte Carlo methods](wikipedia://en/56098).[^n1]

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Voting game {#Voting_game}

#### Simple voting game {#Simple_voting_game}

A simple voting game, taken from *Game Theory and Strategy* by Philip D. Straffin:[^n2]

\[6; 4, 3, 2, 1\]

The numbers in the brackets mean a measure requires 6 votes to pass, and voter A can cast four votes, B three votes, C two, and D one. The winning groups, with underlined swing voters, are as follows:

AB, AC, ABC, ABD, ACD, BCD, ABCD

There are 12 total swing votes, so by the Banzhaf index, [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28social_and_political%29) is divided thus:

A = 5/12, B = 3/12, C = 3/12, D = 1/12

#### U.S. Electoral College {#U.S._Electoral_College}

Consider the [United States Electoral College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_College_%28United_States%29). Each state has different levels of voting power. There are a total of 538 [electoral votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_vote). A [majority vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_vote) is 270 votes. The Banzhaf power index would be a mathematical representation of how likely a single state would be able to swing the vote. A state such as [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California), which is allocated 55 electoral votes, would be more likely to swing the vote than a state such as [Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana), which has 3 electoral votes.

Assume the United States is having a [presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_presidential_election) between a [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_%28United_States%29) (R) and a [Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Party_%28United_States%29) (D). For simplicity, suppose that only three states are participating: California (55 electoral votes), [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas) (38 electoral votes), and [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_%28state%29) (29 electoral votes).

The possible [outcomes](wikipedia://en/3736689) of the election are:

::: {#o9 .table cue=table}

| California (55) | Texas (38) | New York (29) | R votes | D votes | States that could swing the vote |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R | R | R | 122 | 0 | none |
| R | R | D | 93 | 29 | California (D would win 84–38), Texas (D would win 67–55) |
| R | D | R | 84 | 38 | California (D would win 93–29), New York (D would win 67–55) |
| R | D | D | 55 | 67 | Texas (R would win 93–29), New York (R would win 84–38) |
| D | R | R | 67 | 55 | Texas (D would win 93–29), New York (D would win 84–38) |
| D | R | D | 38 | 84 | California (R would win 93–29), New York (R would win 67–55) |
| D | D | R | 29 | 93 | California (R would win 84–38), Texas (R would win 67–55) |
| D | D | D | 0 | 122 | none |

:::

The Banzhaf power index of a state is the proportion of the possible outcomes in which that state could swing the election. In this example, all three states have the same index: 4/12 or 1/3.

However, if New York is replaced by Georgia, with only 16 electoral votes, the situation changes dramatically.

::: {#o10 .table cue=table}

| California (55) | Texas (38) | Georgia (16) | R votes | D votes | States that could swing the vote |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R | R | R | 109 | 0 | California (D would win 55–54) |
| R | R | D | 93 | 16 | California (D would win 71–38) |
| R | D | R | 71 | 38 | California (D would win 93–16) |
| R | D | D | 55 | 54 | California (D would win 109–0) |
| D | R | R | 54 | 55 | California (R would win 109–0) |
| D | R | D | 38 | 71 | California (R would win 93–16) |
| D | D | R | 16 | 93 | California (R would win 71–38) |
| D | D | D | 0 | 109 | California (R would win 55–54) |

:::

In this example, the Banzhaf index gives California 1 and the other states 0, since California alone has more than half the votes.

:::

## History {#History}

What is known today as the Banzhaf power index was originally introduced by [Lionel Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Penrose) in 1946[^n3] and went largely forgotten.[^n4] It was reinvented by [John F. Banzhaf III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Banzhaf_III) in 1965,[^n5] but it had to be reinvented once more by [James Samuel Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Samuel_Coleman) in 1971[^n6] before it became part of the mainstream literature.

Banzhaf wanted to prove objectively that the [Nassau County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nassau_County,_New_York) board's voting system was unfair. As given in *Game Theory and Strategy*, votes were allocated as follows:[^n2]

- Hempstead #1: 9
- Hempstead #2: 9
- North Hempstead: 7
- Oyster Bay: 3
- Glen Cove: 1
- Long Beach: 1

This is 30 total votes, and a simple majority of 16 votes was required for a measure to pass.

In Banzhaf's notation, \[Hempstead #1, Hempstead #2, North Hempstead, Oyster Bay, Glen Cove, Long Beach\] are A-F in \[16; 9, 9, 7, 3, 1, 1\]

There are 32 winning coalitions, and 48 swing votes:

AB AC BC ABC ABD ABE ABF ACD ACE ACF BCD BCE BCF ABCD ABCE ABCF ABDE ABDF ABEF ACDE ACDF ACEF BCDE BCDF BCEF ABCDE ABCDF ABCEF ABDEF ACDEF BCDEF ABCDEF

The Banzhaf index gives these values:

- Hempstead #1 = 16/48
- Hempstead #2 = 16/48
- North Hempstead = 16/48
- Oyster Bay = 0/48
- Glen Cove = 0/48
- Long Beach = 0/48

Banzhaf argued that a voting arrangement that gives 0% of the power to 16% of the population is unfair.

Today, the Banzhaf power index is an accepted way to measure voting power, along with the alternative [Shapley–Shubik power index](wikipedia://en/12055540). Both measures have been applied to the analysis of voting in the [Council of the European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_the_European_Union).[^n7]

However, Banzhaf's analysis has been critiqued as treating votes like coin-flips, and an empirical model of voting rather than a random voting model as used by Banzhaf brings different results.[^n8]
