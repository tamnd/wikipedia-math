# Berge's theorem

*Image: Berge's theorem.svg.* A [matching](wikipedia://en/581797), denoted in blue, is shown to *not* be a [maximum cardinality matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_cardinality_matching) because of the presence of an **augmenting path**, with endpoints at the yellow vertices.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Berge's theorem"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), **Berge's theorem** states that a [matching](wikipedia://en/581797) *M* in a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) *G* is [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_cardinality_matching) (contains the largest possible number of edges) if and only if there is no **augmenting path** (a path that starts and ends on free (unmatched) vertices, and alternates between edges in and not in the matching) with *M*.

:::

It was proven by [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) mathematician [Claude Berge](wikipedia://en/5893760) in 1957 (though already observed by [Petersen](wikipedia://en/76861) in 1891 and [Kőnig](wikipedia://en/1327120) in 1931).

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

To prove Berge's theorem, we first need a [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634). Take a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) *G* and let *M* and *M* be two [matchings](wikipedia://en/581797) in *G*. Let *G* be the resultant [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) from taking the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of *M* and *M*; i.e. (*M* - *M*) ∪ (*M* - *M*). *G* will consist of connected components that are one of the following:

1. An isolated [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899).
1. An even [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) whose edges alternate between *M* and *M*.
1. A [path](wikipedia://en/638889) whose edges alternate between *M* and *M*, with distinct endpoints.

The lemma can be proven by observing that each [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) in *G* can be incident to at most 2 edges: one from *M* and one from *M*. Graphs where every vertex has degree less than or equal to 2 must consist of either isolated [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899), [cycles](wikipedia://en/168609), and [paths](wikipedia://en/638889). Furthermore, each path and cycle in *G* must alternate between *M* and *M*. In order for a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) to do this, it must have an equal number of edges from *M* and *M*, and therefore be of even length.

Let us now prove the [contrapositive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrapositive) of Berge's theorem: *G* has a [matching](wikipedia://en/581797) larger than *M* if and only if *G* has an augmenting path. Clearly, an augmenting path *P* of *G* can be used to produce a [matching](wikipedia://en/581797) *M* that is larger than *M* — just take *M* to be the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of *P* and *M* (*M* contains exactly those edges of *G* that appear in exactly one of *P* and *M*). Hence, the reverse direction follows.

For the forward direction, let *M* be a [matching](wikipedia://en/581797) in *G* larger than *M*. Consider *D*, the symmetric difference of *M* and *M*. Observe that *D* consists of paths and even [cycles](wikipedia://en/168609) (as observed by the earlier lemma). Since *M* is larger than *M*, *D* contains a component that has more edges from *M* than *M*. Such a component is a path in *G* that starts and ends with an edge from *M*, so it is an augmenting path.

:::

## Corollaries {#Corollaries}

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary 1"}

### Corollary 1 {#Corollary_1}

Let *M* be a maximum matching and consider an alternating chain such that the edges in the path alternates between being and not being in *M*. If the alternating chain is a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) or a [path](wikipedia://en/638889) of even length starting on an unmatched vertex, then a new maximum matching *M* can be found by interchanging the edges found in *M* and not in *M*. For example, if the alternating chain is (*m*<sub>1</sub>, *n*<sub>1</sub>, *m*<sub>2</sub>, *n*<sub>2</sub>, ...), where *m*<sub>i</sub> ∈ *M* and *n*<sub>i</sub> ∉ *M*, interchanging them would make all *n*<sub>i</sub> part of the new matching and make all *m*<sub>i</sub> not part of the matching.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary 2"}

### Corollary 2 {#Corollary_2}

An edge is considered "free" if it belongs to a maximum matching but does not belong to all maximum matchings. An edge *e* is free if and only if, in an arbitrary maximum matching *M*, edge *e* belongs to an even alternating path starting at an unmatched vertex or to an alternating [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609). By the first corollary, if edge *e* is part of such an alternating chain, then a new maximum matching, *M*, must exist and *e* would exist either in *M* or *M*, and therefore be free. Conversely, if edge *e* is free, then *e* is in some maximum matching *M* but not in *M*. Since *e* is not part of both *M* and *M*, it must still exist after taking the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of *M* and *M*. The [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of *M* and *M* results in a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) consisting of isolated vertices, even alternating cycles, and alternating paths. Suppose to the contrary that *e* belongs to some odd-length path component. But then one of *M* and *M* must have one fewer edge than the other in this component, meaning that the component as a whole is an augmenting path with respect to that matching. By the original lemma, then, that matching (whether *M* or *M*) cannot be a maximum matching, which contradicts the assumption that both *M* and *M* are maximum. So, since *e* cannot belong to any odd-length path component, it must either be in an alternating cycle or an even-length alternating path.

:::
