# Dirichlet form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirichlet form"}

In [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342) (the study of [harmonic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_functions)) and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), **Dirichlet forms** generalize the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) (the [mathematical operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_operator) on scalar fields). Dirichlet forms can be defined on any [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270), without the need for mentioning [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives). This allows mathematicians to study the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation) and [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415) on spaces that are not [manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifolds), for example, [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals). The benefit on these spaces is that one can do this without needing a [gradient operator](wikipedia://en/12461), and in particular, one can even weakly define a "Laplacian" in this manner if starting with the Dirichlet form.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

When working on $\mathbb{R}^n$, the "classical" Dirichlet form is given by:

$$\mathcal{E}(u, v ) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \nabla u(x) \cdot \nabla v(x) \; dx$$

where one often discusses $\mathcal{E}(u) := \mathcal{E}(u, u) = \|\nabla u\|_2^2$ which is often referred to as the "energy" of the function $u(x)$, see [Dirichlet Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_Energy).

More generally, a Dirichlet form is a [Markovian](wikipedia://en/306422) [closed](wikipedia://en/64713671) [symmetric form](wikipedia://en/1803375) on an [*L*<sup>2</sup>-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L2-space).[^o4] In particular, a Dirichlet form on a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)$ is a bilinear function

$$\mathcal{E}: D\times D \to \mathbb{R}$$

such that

1. $D$ is a [dense subset](wikipedia://en/23523594) of $L^2(\mu)$.
1. $\mathcal{E}$ is symmetric, that is $\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\mathcal{E}(v,u)$ for every $u,v \in D$.
1. $\mathcal{E}(u,u) \geq 0$ for every $u \in D$.
1. The set $D$ equipped with the inner product defined by $(u,v)_{\mathcal{E}} := (u,v)_{L^2(\mu)} + \mathcal{E}(u,v)$ is a real Hilbert space.
1. For every $u \in D$ we have that $u_* = \min (\max(u, 0) , 1) \in D$ and $\mathcal{E}(u_*,u_*)\leq \mathcal{E}(u,u)$.

In other words, a Dirichlet form is nothing but a non negative symmetric bilinear form defined on a dense subset of $L^2(X, \mu)$ such that 4) and 5) hold.

Alternatively, the quadratic form $u \mapsto \mathcal{E}(u,u)$ itself is known as the Dirichlet form and it is still denoted by $\mathcal{E}$, so $\mathcal{E}(u) := \mathcal{E}(u,u)$.

:::

## Harmonic functions {#Harmonic_functions}

Functions that minimize the energy given certain boundary conditions are called harmonic, and the associated Laplacian (weak or not) will be zero on the interior, as expected.

For example, let $\mathcal{E}$ be standard Dirichlet form defined for $u \in H^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$ as

$$\mathcal{E}(u) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} |\nabla u|^2\;dx$$

Then a [harmonic function](wikipedia://en/55762) in the standard sense, i.e. such that $\Delta u = 0$, will have $\mathcal{E}(u) = 0$ as can be seen with integration by parts.

As an alternative example, the standard graph Dirichlet form is given by:

$$\mathcal{E}_G(u, v) = \sum_{x \sim y} ((u(x) - u(y))(v(x) - v(y))$$

where $x \sim y$ means they are connected by an edge. Let a subset of the vertex set be chosen, and call it the boundary of the graph. Assign a Dirichlet boundary condition (choose real numbers for each boundary vertex). One can find a function that minimizes the graph energy, and it will be harmonic. In particular, it will satisfy the averaging property, which is embodied by the graph Laplacian, that is, if $u_G(x)$ is a graph harmonic then

$$\Delta_G u_G(x) = \sum_{y \sim x} (u_G(y) - u_G(x)) = 0$$

which is equivalent to the averaging property

$$u_G(x) = \frac{1}{|\{ y : y \sim x\}|}\sum_{y \sim x} u_G(y) .$$

Technically, such objects are studied in [abstract potential theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_potential_theory), based on the classical [Dirichlet's principle](wikipedia://en/1240011). The theory of Dirichlet forms originated in the work of (txt) on Dirichlet spaces.

## Integral kernels {#Integral_kernels}

Another example of a Dirichlet form is given by

$$\mathcal{E}(u) = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n} (u(y)-u(x))^2 k(x,y) \, dx \, dy$$

where $k: \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is some non-negative symmetric [integral kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_kernel).

If the kernel $k$ satisfies the bound $k(x,y) \leq \Lambda |x-y|^{-n-s}$, then the quadratic form is bounded in $\dot H^{s/2}$. If moreover, $\lambda |x-y|^{-n-s} \leq k(x,y)$, then the form is comparable to the norm in $\dot H^{s/2}$ squared and in that case the set $D \subset L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ defined above is given by $H^{s/2}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. Thus Dirichlet forms are natural generalizations of the [Dirichlet integrals](wikipedia://en/1235977)

$$\mathcal{E}(u) = \int (A\nabla u,\nabla u) \; dx,$$

where $A(x)$ is a positive symmetric matrix. The Euler-Lagrange equation of a Dirichlet form is a non-local analogue of an elliptic equations in divergence form. Equations of this type are studied using variational methods and they are expected to satisfy similar properties.[^o10][^o11][^o9]
