# Formal semantics (natural language)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Formal semantics"}

**Formal semantics** is the scientific study of [linguistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic) meaning through formal tools from [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). It is an interdisciplinary field, sometimes regarded as a subfield of both linguistics and [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language). Formal semanticists rely on diverse methods to analyze [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173). Many examine the meaning of a sentence by studying the [circumstances in which it would be true](wikipedia://en/45132). They describe these circumstances using [abstract mathematical models](wikipedia://en/19858) to represent entities and their features. The [principle of compositionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_compositionality) helps them link the meaning of expressions to [abstract objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) in these models. This principle asserts that the meaning of a compound expression is determined by the meanings of its parts.

:::

[Propositional](wikipedia://en/18154) and [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic) are [formal systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_systems) used to [analyze the semantic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_analysis_%28linguistics%29) of sentences. They introduce concepts like [singular terms](wikipedia://en/246918), [predicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29), [quantifiers](wikipedia://en/43507260), and [logical connectives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_connectives) to represent the [logical form](wikipedia://en/3331706) of natural language expressions. [Type theory](wikipedia://en/40282) is another approach utilized to describe sentences as nested [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_function) with precisely defined input and output types. Various theoretical frameworks build on these systems. [Possible world](wikipedia://en/780566) semantics and [situation semantics](wikipedia://en/13279065) evaluate truth across different hypothetical scenarios. [Dynamic semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_semantics) analyzes the meaning of a sentence as the information contribution it makes.

Using these and similar theoretical tools, formal semanticists investigate a wide range of linguistic phenomena. They study [quantificational expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantifier_%28linguistics%29), which indicate the quantity of something, like the sentence "all ravens are black". An influential proposal analyzes them as relations between two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691)the set of ravens and the set of black things in this example. Quantifiers are also used to examine the meaning of [definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_description) and [indefinite descriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_description), which denote specific entities, like the expression "the president of Kenya". Formal semanticists are also interested in [tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_tense) and [aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_aspect), which provide temporal information about events and circumstances. In addition to studying [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) about what is true, semantics also investigates other sentences types such as [questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Questions) and [imperatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_mood). Other investigated linguistic phenomena include [intensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensionality), [modality](wikipedia://en/2783063), [negation](wikipedia://en/161019), [plural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural) expressions, and the influence of [contextual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context) factors.

Formal semantics is relevant to various fields. In logic and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), formal semantics refers to the analysis of meaning in artificially constructed logical and [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages). In [cognitive science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science), some researchers rely on the insights of formal semantics to study the nature of the [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind). Formal semantics has its roots in the development of modern logic starting in the late 19th century. [Richard Montague](wikipedia://en/610182)'s work in the late 1960s and early 1970s was pivotal in applying these logical principles to natural language, inspiring many scholars to refine his insights and apply them to diverse linguistic phenomena.

## Definition {#Definition}

Formal semantics is a branch of [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics) and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) that studies linguistic [meaning](wikipedia://en/4102640) using formal methods. To analyze language in a precise and systematic manner, it incorporates ideas from [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), like the concepts of [truth conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_conditions), [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), and [compositionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compositionality).[^o4] Because of the prominence of these concepts, formal semantics is also referred to as *truth-conditional semantics* and *model-theoretic semantics*.[^o5]

The primary focus of formal semantics is the analysis of [natural languages](wikipedia://en/21173) such as [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language), [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit_language), [Quechua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quechua_language), and [American Sign Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Sign_Language).[^o6] They investigate diverse linguistic phenomena, including [reference](wikipedia://en/20110874), [quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantifier_%28linguistics%29), [plurality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural), [tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_tense), [aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_aspect), [vagueness](wikipedia://en/32369), [modality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_modality), [scope](wikipedia://en/65142269), [binding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_%28linguistics%29), [conditionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence), [questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Questions), and [imperatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_mood).[^o7]

Understood in a wide sense, formal semantics also includes the study of [artificial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_language) or constructed languages. This covers the formal languages used in the logical analysis of arguments, such as the language of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), and [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages) in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), such as [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++), [JavaScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JavaScript), and [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29).[^o8] Formal semantics is related to formal [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics) since both are subfields of [formal linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_linguistics). One key difference is that formal pragmatics centers on how language is used in communication rather than the problem of meaning in general.[^o9]

:::

## Methodology {#Methodology}

Formal semanticists rely on diverse [methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodology), conceptual tools, and background assumptions, which distinguish the field from other branches of semantics. Most of these principles originate in logic, mathematics, and the philosophy of language.[^o11] One key principle is that an adequate theory of meaning needs to accurately predict sentences' [truth conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_conditions). A sentence's truth conditions are the circumstances under which it would be true. For example, the sentence "Tina is tall and happy" is true if Tina has the property of being tall and also the property of being happy; these are its truth conditions. This principle reflects the idea that understanding a sentence requires knowing how it relates to reality and under which circumstances it would be appropriate to use it.[^o12]

Formal semanticists test their theories using the linguistic [intuitions](wikipedia://en/154170) of [competent speakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_competence).[^o13] A key source of data comes from judgments concerning [entailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entailment), a relation between sentencescalled *[premises](wikipedia://en/7993337)* and *conclusions*in which [truth](wikipedia://en/29920) is preserved. For instance, the sentence "Tina is tall and happy" entails the sentence "Tina is tall" because the truth of the first sentence guarantees the truth of the second. One aspect of understanding the meaning of a sentence is comprehending what it does and does not entail.[^o14] The study of entailment relations helps formal semanticists build new theories and verify whether existing theories accurately reflect the intuitions of native speakers.[^o13]

*Image: Model theory.svg.* Formal semantics constructs abstract models that map natural language expressions to individual objects and sets of objects.[^o15]

One major goal of research in formal semantics is to uncover and explain the range of variation across human languages.[^o16] As part of this [typological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_typology) work, researchers have sought to uncover principles that apply to all languages known as *semantic [universals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_universal)*. A proposed universal is that languages only allow [determiners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determiner) which have the property of being [conservative](wikipedia://en/68498620), indicating that the human language faculty cannot [lexicalize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicalization) many quantifiers that are possible in principle.[^o17] Crosslinguistic research has also been used to inform theoretical issues within semantics. For example, early research on the semantics of English sparked debate over whether tense markers are quantificational or referential. Subsequent work on [Javanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javanese_language), [Atayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atayal_language), [St’átʼimcets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillooet_language), and [Tlingit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlingit_language) has been taken as evidence that natural languages actually make both interpretations available.[^o18] Similarly, evidence from [sign languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_language) has been used to support certain analyses of [coreference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coreference) which are hard to test in spoken languages because the crucial elements are [covert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covert_%28linguistics%29).[^o19] In order to collect data on languages that they themselves do not speak, formal semanticists carry out [fieldwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fieldwork).[^o20]

Formal semanticists typically analyze language by proposing a system of [model theoretic interpretation](wikipedia://en/19858). In such a system, a sentence is evaluated relative to a mathematical structure called a *model* that maps each expression to an [abstract object](wikipedia://en/19453961). Models rely on [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) and introduce abstract objects for all entities in this situation. For example, a model of a situation where Tina is tall and happy may include an abstract object representing Tina and two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of objectsone for all tall entities and another for all happy entities. Different models introduce different objects and group them into different sets while interpretation functions link natural language expressions to these objects and sets. Using this approach, semanticists can precisely calculate the truth values of sentences. For example, the sentence "Tina is tall" is true in models where the abstract object corresponding to the name "Tina" is in the set corresponding to the predicate "tall". In this way, model theoretic interpretation makes it possible to formally capture truth conditions and entailments.[^o21]

*Image: Pauline Jacobson 1.jpg.* [Pauline Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Jacobson) suggested direct compositionality as a methodological principle assuming a close link between syntax and semantics.[^o22]

The [principle of compositionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_compositionality) is another key methodological assumption for analyzing the meaning of natural language sentences and linking them to abstract models. It states that the meaning of a compound expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. According to this principle, if a person knows the meanings of the name "Tina", the verb "is", and the adjective "happy", they can understand the sentence "Tina is happy" even if they have never heard this specific combination of words before. The principle of compositionality explains how language users can comprehend an infinite number of sentences based on their knowledge of a finite number of words and rules.[^o23]

Semantic formalisms differ in whether they adopt the method of *direct interpretation* or *indirect interpretation*. In direct interpretation, the semantic interpretation rules map natural language expressions directly to their denotations, whereas indirect interpretation maps them to expressions of some formal language. For example, in direct interpretation the name "Barack Obama" would be mapped to the man himself, while a system of indirect interpretation would map it to a logical constant "o" which would then be mapped to the man himself. In this way, direct interpretation can be seen as providing a model theory for natural languages themselves, while indirect interpretation provides a system of [logic translation](wikipedia://en/73032003) and then relies on the model theory of the formal language. These two methods are in principle equivalent, but are often chosen based on practical or expository considerations.[^o24] Formal semanticists sometimes limit their studies to particular fragments of whatever language they are studying to isolate core phenomena and simplify analysis.[^o25]

## Formal systems and theories {#Formal_systems_and_theories}

### Propositional and predicate logic {#Propositional_and_predicate_logic}

*Image: Wismar Marienkirche Bronzebüste Gottlob Frege (01-1).JPG.* [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) developed predicate logic and used it to analyze natural language arguments.[^o28]

Formal semanticists often represent truth conditions using propositional or predicate logic. Propositional logic employs letters like $A$ and $B$ to represent simple [statements](wikipedia://en/81094). These can be combined to form complex formulas using [logical connectives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_connectives) such as $\land$ (and), $\lor$ (or), and $\to$ (if...then). For example, using the method of indirect interpretation, the sentence "Alice is happy and Bob is rich" can be translated into propositional logic with the formula $A \land B$, where $A$ stands for "Alice is happy" and $B$ stands for "Bob is rich". Of key interest to semantic analysis is that the truth value of these compound statements is [directly determined by the truth values](wikipedia://en/604707) of the simple statements. For instance, the formula $A \land B$ is only true if both $A$ and $B$ are true; otherwise, it is false.[^o29]

Predicate logic extends propositional logic using symbols for [singular terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_terms), [predicates](wikipedia://en/285109), and [quantifiers](wikipedia://en/43507260). Singular terms refer to specific entities, whereas predicates represent properties of and relations between entities. For instance, the sentence "Alice is happy" can be translated as the formula $H(a)$, where $a$ is a logical constant and $H$ is a predicate. Quantifiers express that a certain condition applies to some or all entities. For example, the sentence "Someone is happy" can be represented with the formula $\exists x H(x)$, where the [existential quantifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_quantifier) $\exists$ indicates that happiness applies at least to one person. Similarly, the idea that everyone is happy can be expressed through the formula $\forall x H(x)$, where $\forall$ is the [universal quantifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_quantifier).[^o30]

There are different ways how natural language sentences can be [translated into predicate logic](wikipedia://en/73032003). A common approach interprets verbs as predicates. [Intransitive verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intransitive_verbs), like "sleeps" and "dances", have a subject but no objects and are interpreted as one-place predicates. [Transitive verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_verbs), like "loves" and "gives", have one or more objects and are interpreted as predicates with two or more places. For example, the sentence "Bob loves Alice" can be formalized as $L(b,a)$, using the two-place predicate $L$. Typically, not every word in natural language sentences has a direct counterpart symbol in the logic translation, and in some cases, the pattern of the logical formula differs significantly from the surface structure of the natural language sentence. For example, sentences like "all cats are animals" are usually translated as $\forall x (C(x) \to A(x))$ (for all entities, if the entity is a cat then the entity is an animal) even though the expression "if...then" ($\to$) is not present in the original sentence.[^o31]

Many early approaches to formal semantics, such as the works of [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), [Rudolf Carnap](wikipedia://en/57987), and [Donald Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Davidson_%28philosopher%29), relied primarily on predicate logic.[^o32]

### Lambda calculus {#Lambda_calculus}

*Image: Type theory parse tree.svg.* Type-theoretic analysis of the sentence "Alice likes Bob". The blue letters indicate the type of the expression. The word "Bob" is first applied to the word "likes", resulting in a function to which the word "Alice" is then applied.[^o34]

Formal semantics employs the [typed lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/366208) to analyze the denotations of [parts of sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_constituent). Using the typed lambda calculus, one can formalize the denotation of the sentence "Tina loves Mary" with the formula $L(t,m)$, just like in predicate logic. However, the lambda calculus introduces additional formulas to represent the denotations of specific parts of sentences, such as the [verb phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verb_phrase) "loves Mary". In the lambda calculus, this could be formalized as $\lambda x . L(x,m)$, where the variable $x$ is a placeholder that can be filled by the denotation of whatever subject this verb phrase is combined with. The prefix $\lambda$ binds the variable $x$, notating the fact that this expression needs to be combined with an additional argument in order to express a complete proposition. This denotation can be combined with its subject argument using the [β-reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Β-reduction), a rule for simplifying expressions with bound variables and arguments.[^o35]

A crucial idea is that expressions belong to different types, which describe how the expressions can be used and combined with other expressions. Type theory, typically in the form of the typed lambda calculus, provides a formalism for this endeavor. It begins by defining a small number of basic types, which can be fused to create new types.[^o36]

According to a common approach, there are only two basic types: entities ($e$) and truth values ($t$). Entities are the denotations of names and similar noun phrases, while truth values are the denotations of declarative sentences. All other types are constructed from these two types as [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) that have entities, truth values, or other functions as inputs and outputs. This way, a sentence is analyzed as a complex function made up of several internal functions. When all functions are evaluated, the output is a truth value. Simple intransitive verbs without objects are functions that take an entity as input and produce a truth value as output. The type of this function is written as $\langle e, t \rangle$, where the first letter indicates the input type and the second letter the output type. According to this approach, the sentence "Alice sleeps" is analyzed as a function that takes the entity "Alice" as input to produce a truth value. Transitive verbs with one object, such as the verb "likes", are complex or nested functions. They take an entity as input and output a second function, which itself requires an entity as input to produce a truth value, formalized as $\langle e, \langle e, t \rangle \rangle$. This way, the sentence "Alice likes Bob" corresponds to a nested function to which two entities are [applied](wikipedia://en/20576545). Similar types of analyses are provided for all relevant expressions, including logical connectives and quantifiers.[^o37]

### Others {#Others}

[Possible worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_worlds) are another central concept used in the analysis of linguistic meaning. A possible world is a complete and consistent version of how everything could have been, similar to a hypothetical alternative universe. For instance, the [dinosaurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinosaurs) were wiped out in the actual world but there are possible worlds where they survived. Possible worlds have various applications in formal semantics, usually to study expressions or aspects of meaning that are difficult to explain when referring only to entities of the actual world. They include [modal statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_modality) about what is possible or necessary and descriptions of the contents of [mental states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_states), such as what people [believe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief) and [desire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desire). Possible worlds are also used to explain how two expressions can have different meanings even though they refer to the same entity, such as the expressions "the morning star" and "the evening star", which both refer to the planet [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus). One way to include possible worlds in the model-theoretic formalism is to define a set of all possible worlds as one additional component of a model. The interpretation of the meanings of different expressions is then modified to account for this change. For example, to explain that a sentence may be true in one possible world and false in another, one can interpret its meaning not directly as a truth value but as a function from a possible world to a truth value.[^o39]

[Situation semantics](wikipedia://en/13279065) is a theory closely related to possible world semantics. Situations, like possible worlds, present possible circumstances. However, unlike possible worlds, they do not encompass a whole universe but only capture specific parts or fragments of possible worlds. This modification reflects the observation that many statements are context-dependent and aim to describe the speaker's specific circumstances rather than the world at large. For example, the sentence "every student sings" is false when interpreted as an assertion about the universe as a whole. However, speakers may use this sentence in the context of a limited situation, such as a specific high school musical, in which it can be true.[^o40]

[Dynamic semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_semantics) interprets language usage as a dynamic process in which information is continually updated against the background of an existing context. It rejects static approaches that associate a given expression with a fixed meaning. Instead, this theory argues that meaning depends on the information that is already present in the context, understanding the meaning of a sentence as the change in information it produces. This view reflects the idea that sentences are usually not interpreted in isolation but form part of a larger discourse, to which they contribute in some way.[^o41] For example, update semanticsone form of dynamic semanticsdefines an information state as the set of all possible worlds compatible with the current information, reflecting the idea that the information is incomplete and cannot determine which of these worlds is the right one. Sentences introducing new information update the information state by excluding some possible worlds, thereby decreasing uncertainty.[^o42]

## Studied linguistic phenomena {#Studied_linguistic_phenomena}

### Quantifiers {#Quantifiers}

Quantifiers are expressions that indicate the quantity of something. In predicate logic, the most basic quantifiers only provide information about whether a condition applies to all or some entities, as seen in sentences like "all ravens are black" and "some students smoke". Formal semanticists use the concept of generalized quantifiers to extend this basic framework to a broad range of quantificational expressions in natural language that usually provide more detailed information. They include diverse expressions such as "most", "few", "twelve", and "fewer than ten".[^o45]

Most quantificational expressions can be interpreted as relations between two sets. For instance, the sentence "all ravens are black" conveys the idea that the set of ravens is a subset of the set of black entities. Similarly, the sentence "fewer than ten books were sold" asserts that the set of books and the set of sold items have fewer than ten elements [in common](wikipedia://en/41780044).[^o46] In English, quantifiers are often expressed with a [determiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determiner), such as "all" and "few", indicating the relation between the sets, followed by a [noun phrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun_phrase) and a [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28grammar%29) to describe the involved sets.[^o47]

Quantifiers can be divided into proportional and cardinal quantifiers based on the relation between the sets. Proportional quantifiers, such as "all" and "most", indicate the relative overlap of the first set with the second set. For them, the order of the sets matters. For instance, the sentences "all ravens are black" and "all black things are ravens" have different meanings even though they refer to the same sets. Cardinal quantifiers, such as "four" and "no", provide information about the absolute number of overlapping entities, independent of relative proportion. For them, the order of the sets does not matter, as exemplified by the sentences "no rose is black" and "no black thing is a rose".[^o48]

Typically, the [domain](wikipedia://en/229180) of natural language quantifiers is implicitly limited to a certain range of entities relevant to the discussed issue. For example, in the context of a specific kindergarten, the domain of the sentence "all children are sleeping" is limited to the children attending this kindergarten.[^o49]

The [scope](wikipedia://en/65142269) of a quantifier is the part of the sentence to which it applies. Some natural language sentences have scope [ambiguity](wikipedia://en/677), resulting in competing interpretations of the scope of quantifiers. Depending on how the scope is interpreted, the sentence "Some man loves every woman" can mean either "there is a man such that he loves all women" or "for every woman there is at least one man who loves her".[^o50]

### Descriptions and names {#Descriptions_and_names}

*Image: Honourable\_Bertrand\_Russell.jpg.* [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) analyzed the meaning of [definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_description) and [indefinite descriptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_description) using [existential quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_quantifier).[^o52]

Definite and indefinite descriptions are phrases that denote a specific entity or group of entities within a given context. Definite descriptions in English typically use the [definite article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_article) "the", followed by a noun phrase, such as "the president of Kenya". However, they can also take other forms, such as "her husband" or "John's bicycle". Indefinite descriptions are usually expressed with the [indefinite articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_articles) "a" and "an", as in "a lazy coworker" and "an old friend".[^o53] Definite descriptions typically point to a unique entity and assume that the listener is familiar with the referent. Indefinite descriptions usually allow the description to apply to more than one entity and introduce the entity without presupposing prior knowledge.[^o54]

Diverse theories about the correct analysis of definite and indefinite descriptions have been proposed. An influential early view, suggested by [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163), interprets them using [existential quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_quantifier). It proposes that indefinite descriptions like "a man ran" have the logical form $\exists x (Man(x) \land Ran(x))$. Definite descriptions have a similar form, with the difference that the description is unique, meaning that the first predicate only applies to a single entity.[^o52] A central motivation for Russell's approach was to solve semantic puzzles that arise from definite descriptions that do not refer to any particular entity. For example, France currently has no king so it is not clear how to determine the truth value of the sentence "the present king of France is bald". According to Russell's analysis, the sentence is false since no unique entity exists to which the predicates "present king of France" and "bald" apply.[^o55]

The problem of [names](wikipedia://en/164226) is closely related to that of definite descriptions because both expressions aim to refer to a particular entity. According to [Millian](wikipedia://en/15626) theories, names refer directly without any descriptive information of the denoted entity. This view is opposed by description theories, which argue that names carry implicit descriptive contents that help interpreters identify their referents. One view understands names as implicit definite descriptions, proposing that the descriptive content of the name "[Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates)" may include information like "the teacher of [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)".[^o56]

### Tense, aspect, and events {#Tense,_aspect,_and_events}

[Tense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_tense) and aspect provide temporal information about events and circumstances. Tense indicates whether something happened in the past, present, or future, offering a reference point to place events within a [timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline) relative to the time of the [utterance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utterance). Aspect conveys additional information about how events unfold in time, like the distinction between completed, ongoing, and repetitive events. In English, both tense and aspect can be expressed through [verb forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_conjugation). On the level of tense the sentence "I ate" indicates the past, whereas the sentence "I will eat" indicates the future. On the level of aspect, the sentence "I ate" indicates a completed action, whereas the sentence "I was eating" indicates an ongoing action.[^o58]

Formal semanticists employ diverse conceptual tools to describe tense, such as different types of [temporal logic](wikipedia://en/321481) as extensions of predicate logic. One approach includes a set of times in the mathematical model to interpret temporal statements. Some models conceptualize time as a series of [instances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant), while others introduce intervals as the basic units of time. The difference is that intervals have a duration and can overlap, whereas instances are discrete time points that do not intersect. One form of temporal logic introduces tense operators to indicate the time a sentence describes, like the operator $P$ for past events and the operator $F$ for future events. This way, the formula $P \ Dance(naomi)$ expresses that Naomi danced in the past, while $F \ Dance(naomi)$ asserts that she will dance in the future.[^o59] The semantic analysis of aspect is divided into [grammatical aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_aspect), expressed through verb forms, and [lexical aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_aspect), which covers the inherent temporal characteristics of different verbs.[^o60]

An influential approach to the semantic role of [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_%28philosophy%29) was proposed by [Donald Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Davidson_%28philosopher%29). Using predicate logic, it represents events as singular terms and translates action sentences into logical formulas about events, even if the original sentences contain no explicit reference to events. For example, it translates the sentence "Jones buttered the toast slowly with a knife" as $\exists e Butter(Jones, the \ toast, e) \land Slowly(e) \land With(e, a \ knife)$ (literally: there was an event, which was a buttering of the toast by Jones, was slow, and involved a knife). One motivation for this approach is to provide a systematic method for translating [adverbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adverbs), like "slowly", and other [adjuncts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjunct_%28grammar%29) into logical formulas.[^o61]

### Intensionality, modality, and propositional attitudes {#Intensionality,_modality,_and_propositional_attitudes}

Semanticists often distinguish two aspects of meaning: [extension](wikipedia://en/39095) and [intension](wikipedia://en/14570).[^o63] Extension is the entity or group of entities to which an expression refers, while intension is the inherent [concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept) or underlying idea it conveys. For example, the expressions "the morning star" and "the evening star" have the same extension, as both refer to the planet Venus. However, their meanings differ on the level of intension since they present the planet in different ways by evoking distinct concepts.[^o64]

Extensionality and intensionality are characteristics of sentences. A sentence is extensional if expressions with the same extension can be substituted without changing the sentence's truth value. For example, the sentence "the morning star is a planet" remains true if the expression "the morning star" is replaced with the expression "the evening star". Intensional sentences, by contrast, are not only sensitive to extensions but also to intensions, meaning that extensionally equivalent expressions cannot be freely replaced. For instance, Ann may not be aware that the morning star is identical to the evening star. In this case, the sentence "Ann knows that the morning star is the morning star" is true while the sentence "Ann knows that the morning star is the evening star" is false, indicating that the sentences are intensional.[^o65]

Intensionality is present in various linguistic expressions. For instance, modal expressions, such as "may", "can", and "must", usually introduce intensional contexts. They express what is possible or necessary, describing how the world could or could not have been rather than how it actually is. A common approach to the analysis of modal expressions is the use of the modal operators $\Diamond$ and $\Box$ to modify the meaning of sentences and represent what is possible and necessary. For example, if the formula $P$ stands for the statement "it is raining", then the formula $\Diamond P$ stands for the statement "it is possible that it is raining". To interpret the meaning of modal statements, formal semanticists often rely on the concept of possible worlds. According to this approach, a sentence is possibly true if it is true in at least one possible world, whereas it is necessarily true if it is true in all possible worlds.[^o66]

Propositional attitude reportsanother example of intensionalitydiscuss [mental states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_states) of individuals. They often use verbs like "believes", "doubts", and "wants", followed by a that-clause describing the content of the attitude, like the sentence "Kyrie believes that the Earth is flat". The use of possible worlds is also common for the analysis of propositional attitudes. For example, the content of a propositional attitude can be understood as the set of all possible worlds in which it is true, such as all possible worlds with a flat Earth in the mentioned example.[^o67] The meaning of propositional attitude reports containing definite or indefinite descriptions is often ambiguous. This ambiguity arises from the interpretation of the description, which can be subjective or objective. For example, if Jasper wants a drink from his butler but is unaware that his butler poisoned his wife, then the sentence "Jasper wants a drink from the poisoner of his wife" is ambiguous. According to the objective interpretationcalled [*de re* interpretation](wikipedia://en/530256)the sentence is true since the butler is in fact the poisoner. Conversely, the subjective interpretationcalled *de dicto* interpretationrenders it false since Jasper does not want drinks from poisoners.[^o68]

### Questions and imperatives {#Questions_and_imperatives}

The main focus of formal semantics is on statements, which aim to describe reality and are either true or false depending on whether they succeed. However, this analysis does not cover all types of sentences. Specific frameworks have been proposed for the analysis of other sentence types, such as questions and imperatives.[^o70]

Various theories analyze the meaning of questions in terms of possible answers, replacing the concept of truth conditions, common in the analysis of statements, with the related notion of answerhood conditions. One approach, initially formulated by [Charles Leonard Hamblin](wikipedia://en/19501790), interprets answerhood conditions as the set of statements that qualify as answers to a question. For instance, the sentences "Marco called" and "Don called" qualify as answers to the question "Who called?", but the sentence "I like ice cream" does not. A common distinction is between [yes-no questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yes-no_questions), which only ask for confirmation, and [open-ended questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-ended_question), which seek more detailed information. Additional considerations include the distinctions between true and false answers, and between complete and partial answers, depending on whether the response contains all the requested information. On the symbolic level, questions can be expressed using $?$ as an operator to indicate the subject of the question. For example, the question "Who called?" can be formalized as $? x Called(x)$, whereas the question "Did anyone call?" takes the form $? \exists x Called(x)$.[^o71]

Imperative sentences usually express commands or instructions, like the sentence "Close the door!". Unlike declarative and interrogative sentences, which generally convey or request information, the primary goal of imperatives is to influence the behavior of the listener. As a result, imperatives have no or at least no obvious truth conditions. Other difficulties in the analysis of imperative sentences are that they usually lack an explicit [subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject_%28grammar%29) and that they can express various other meanings besides commands, such as advice, invitations, or permissions. Formal semanticists study the meaning of imperatives by examining how they interact with other linguistic phenomena. These include cases in which one imperative entails another imperative, the negation of an imperative, and conditional imperatives as well as [conjunctions](wikipedia://en/18152) and [disjunctions](wikipedia://en/7962) of several imperatives.[^o72]

### Other phenomena {#Other_phenomena}

Diverse other linguistic phenomena are studied in formal semantics. [Negation](wikipedia://en/161019) is typically understood as an operation that inverts the meaning of an expression. In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), it is expressed through the operator $\lnot$ as in $\lnot Sleeps(mia)$, indicating that Mia is not sleeping. This operator inverts the truth value of a statement: if $Sleeps(mia)$ is false then $\lnot Sleeps(mia)$ is true. In natural language, negative particles and quantifiers, such as "not" and "no", are often used to indicate negation. These expressions can occur in different positions within sentences to negate either the full sentence or specific parts of it. The scope of a negation operator is the part of the sentence that it affects, which can sometimes be ambiguous. For example, the sentence "all doctors have no car" can mean that not every doctor has a car, that not a single doctor has a car, or that no individual car is collectively owned by all doctors.[^o74]

[Plural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural) expressions refer to multiple objects, such as the terms "children" and "apples". Formal semanticists typically interpret them as denoting some kind of plural object, such as the set of individuals belonging to the group in question. They distinguish between distributive and collective uses depending on whether the predicate applies to each individual separately or to the group as a whole. Some sentences are ambiguous and allow for both interpretations. For example, the sentence "two boys pushed a car" can mean that there were two cars and each boy pushed one (distributive) or that there was one car that both boys pushed together (collective).[^o75]

Formal semanticists also examine expressions whose meaning depends on [contextual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context) factors. They include [indexical](wikipedia://en/1029182) or [deictic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deictic) expressions, which refer to some aspect of the situation of the text. Examples are the [pronouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pronouns) "I" and "you", which refer to the speaker and the addressee, as well as the adverbs "today" and "over there", which refer to temporal and spatial aspects of the situation. [Anaphoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaphora_%28linguistics%29) expressions are another type of context-dependent expression. They refer to terms or phrases used earlier in the text, called [antecedents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antecedent_%28grammar%29). In the passage "Peter woke up. He switched on the light." the word "he" is an anaphoric expression with the word "Peter" as its antecedent. This grammatical association is known as [binding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_%28linguistics%29) and depends on the context since the word "he" would refer to someone else if the preceding sentence had a different antecedent.[^o76] Other linguistic phenomena studied by formal semanticists include [presupposition](wikipedia://en/1965727), [conditionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence), [thematic roles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thematic_relation), spatial expressions, [adjectives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjectives), and adverbs.[^o77]

While much work focuses on the study of English sentences, formal semantics is not limited to the English language and includes cross-linguistic analysis.[^o78] For example, there are diverse methods of expressing grammatical aspect across distinct languages, including [prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefixes), verb endings, [auxiliary verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_verbs), or combinations of several methods. They impact semantic analysis by influencing the hierarchical structure of sentences.[^o79] Similar problems of cross-linguistic analysis are also found for other linguistic phenomena, including quantification and indefinite descriptions.[^o80]

## Interfaces {#Interfaces}

In formal semantics, interfaces are points of interaction between semantics and other branches of linguistic inquiry. They concern how diverse factors, such as grammar and the speaker intention, influence meaning.[^o82]

### Syntax-semantics interface {#Syntax-semantics_interface}

*Image: ParseTree.svg.* A [syntax tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parse_tree) in which the sentence *S* is made up of a noun phrase *NP* and a verb phrase *VP*, which is itself made up of smaller [constituents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constituent_%28linguistics%29).

The meaning of a sentence depends in part on its syntactic structure. For instance, the sentences "John saw Mary" and "Mary saw John" contain the same words but have different meanings because they differ in their syntactic structure. Thus, theories of compositional semantics will provide interpretation rules that map [syntactic trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parse_tree) to their meanings. The nature of these rules depends in part on which model of syntax is assumed. Many theories of semantics assume a [generative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_linguistics) model of syntax, such as [government and binding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_and_binding_theory) or [minimalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimalist_program), while other common approaches include [categorial grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorial_grammar) and [HPSG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HPSG).[^o84]

In such theories, the interpretation system often uses rules such as the following. These rules specify how to start with a syntactic node $\alpha$ and calculate its denotation, written $[\![\alpha]\!]$. These rules are applied [recursively](wikipedia://en/25407).[^o85]

- **Terminal Node:** If $\alpha$ is a [syntactic node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Node_%28computer_science%29) with no daughters, then $[\![\alpha]\!]$ is specified in the [lexicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicon).
- **Nonbranching Node:** If $\alpha$ is a node whose sole daughter is $\beta$, then $[\![\alpha]\!]=[\![\beta]\!]$.
- **Function Application:** If $\alpha$ is a node with daughters $\beta$ and $\gamma$ where $[\![\beta]\!]$ is a function whose domain contains $[\![\gamma]\!]$, then $[\![\alpha]\!]=[\![\beta]\!]([\![\gamma]\!])$.

A major debate in the syntax-semantics interface concerns the proper treatment of apparent mismatches between form and meaning, particularly related to [scope](wikipedia://en/65142269). One common approach posits a level of grammatical structure called [logical form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_form_%28linguistics%29). In this view, sentences do not have a unique syntactic form, but rather a phonological form, which gets pronounced, and a separate logical form, which undergoes semantic interpretation. In these approaches, apparent mismatches between form and meaning can be explained using syntactic mechanisms such as [movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic_movement). This is in contrast to approaches such as direct compositionality, which assume that the surface grammatical form is the only grammatical form and that mismatches should be explained using other semantic mechanisms such as [type shifting](wikipedia://en/66672458). Empirical evidence informing this debate includes [ACD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antecedent-contained_deletion), scope [islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wh-movement#Extraction_islands), and data from languages such as [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language) which has been argued to "wear its \[logical form\] on its sleeve".[^o86]

### Semantics-prosody interface {#Semantics-prosody_interface}

In many languages, semantic meaning is influenced by [prosodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosody_%28linguistics%29) features such as [intonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_%28linguistics%29) and [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28linguistics%29). In particular, [focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_%28linguistics%29) can affect the truth conditions of a sentence. For instance, in a context where Hannah gave Matt many gifts, the a-sentence below can be true while the b-sentence would be false. On the other hand, in a context where Hannah gave everyone a book, the b-sentence could be true while the a-sentence would be false.[^o88]

(1) a. Hannah only gave MATT a book. b. Hannah only gave Matt a BOOK.

To explain focus-sensitive phenomena, linguists commonly adopt enriched systems such as [alternative semantics](wikipedia://en/66422858) or [structured semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structured_proposition) which provide a notion of a *focus alternative*.[^o89] Work at the semantics-prosody interface also addresses the meaning contributions of particular intonational tunes such as [rising declaratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rising_declarative).[^o90]

### Semantics-pragmatics interface {#Semantics-pragmatics_interface}

[Pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics) is the study of the parts of linguistic meaning which arise not through compositional semantics, but rather through reasoning about the speaker's intentions. For instance, the sentence "Steve invited Anne or Mary" typically conveys (i) that Steve invited one or the other and (ii) that he did not invite both. The first of these inferences is usually regarded as a semantic entailment, since it cannot be denied without contradicting the sentence. However, because the inference in (ii) can be denied without a contradiction, it is often regarded as a weaker pragmatic inference called an [implicature](wikipedia://en/393671).[^o92]

Traditionally, pragmatics has been viewed as a process of enriching the meanings computed by the semantics. On this view, the output of the semantics is taken to be part of the input to the pragmatics. For instance, the disjunctive example above is traditionally analyzed as semantically denoting an [inclusive disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusive_disjunction) $A \lor M$ whose truth conditions are compatible with Steve having invited both. Then the implicature that he didn't invite both is derived by applying pragmatic reasoning to the question of why the speaker didn't say the more informative alternative "Steve invited Anne and Mary".[^o93]

## In various fields {#In_various_fields}

### Formal logic {#Formal_logic}

Formal logic studies the laws of [deductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/61093), focusing on entailment relations between [premises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premises) and conclusions rather than linguistic meaning in general. It investigates [rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference), such as *[modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900)*, which describe the logical structure of deductively [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) arguments.[^o96] Formal logicians develop artificial languages, like the language of predicate logic, to avoid the ambiguities of natural language and give precise descriptions of the laws of logic.[^o97] Formal semantics plays a central role in this endeavor for applying these laws to natural language arguments. It helps logicians discern the [logical form](wikipedia://en/3331706) of everyday arguments, serving as a crucial step in [translating](wikipedia://en/73032003) them into [logical formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_formula).[^o98]

Another key overlap between formal semantics and formal logic concerns the meaning of artificial logical languages. The [semantics of logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_of_logic) examines the construction of mathematical models of formal languages, similar to the models used by formal semanticists to study natural language. These models typically include abstract objects to represent individuals and sets. The relation to formulas is established through an [interpretation function](wikipedia://en/14511671) that maps symbols to the abstract objects they denote.[^o99] A key aspect of this interface is the contrast between [syntactic](wikipedia://en/37531624#Syntactic_consequence) and [semantic entailment](wikipedia://en/37531624#Semantic_consequence). A premise syntactically entails a conclusion if the conclusion can be deduced using rules of inference. A premise semantically entails a conclusion if the conclusion is true in every possible model where the premise is true.[^o100]

### Computer science {#Computer_science}

[Computational semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_semantics) is an interdisciplinary field at the intersection of computer science and formal semantics. It studies how computational processes can be utilized to deal with linguistic meaning. A primary focus is the analysis of natural language sentences through computer-based methods to discern their logical structure, understand their content, and extract information. This form of inquiry has various applications in areas of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence), such as [automated reasoning](wikipedia://en/2884728), [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), and [machine translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_translation). Difficulties in this process come from the ambiguity, vagueness, and context dependence of natural language expressions.[^o102]

Another intersection concerns the analysis of the meaning of [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages). A programming language is an artificial language designed to give instructions or describe computations to be performed by computers. A [formal semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_%28computer_science%29) of a programming language is a mathematical model of how it works. Its goal is to help computer scientists understand, analyze, and verify program behavior.[^o103] Static semantics describes the process of [compilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler) or how a [human-readable programming language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_programming_language) is translated into [binary machine code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_code).[^o104] Dynamic semantics examines [run-time behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_%28computing%29) or what happens during the execution of instructions.[^o105] The main approaches to dynamic semantics are [denotational](wikipedia://en/55275), [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/518199), and [operational semantics](wikipedia://en/270062). Denotational semantics describes the effects of code elements, axiomatic semantics examines the conditions before and after code execution, and operational semantics interprets code execution as a series of [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_%28computer_science%29) transitions.[^o106]

### Cognitive science {#Cognitive_science}

Cognitive science studies the [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind) by focusing on how it represents and transforms information. It is an interdisciplinary field that integrates research from diverse areas, ranging from [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) and [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience) to [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), artificial intelligence, and linguistics.[^o108] Some researchers emphasize the central role of language in understanding the human mind and rely on formal semantics to provide an abstract model for analyzing how linguistic meaning is constructed and interpreted.[^o109]

Formal semantics is also relevant to [cognitive neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_neuroscience), which seeks to explain the biological processes underlying [cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognition). One approach uses [brain imaging techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroimaging) to visualize brain activity and employs mathematical models to link this data to cognitive processes. Insights from formal semantics can refine these models and help formulate testable predictions. For instance, researchers can examine semantic cognition by presenting a person with semantic variations of a sentence and measuring the differences in brain responses.[^o110]

## History {#History}

*Image: Alfred Tarski 1968 (as-is, cropped) (cropped).jpg.* [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) developed a rigorous [theory of truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski's_theory_of_truth) in formal languages.[^o113]

Formal semantics has its roots in the development of modern logic in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) laid the foundations of predicate logic and examined how this logical system can be used to analyze natural language arguments. He engaged in this analysis using a small number of basic concepts of formal semantics, such as singular terms, predicates, quantifiers, and logical connectives. Frege also formulated the principle of compositionality and introduced the distinction between sense and reference.[^o28]

Following Frege's work, [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) developed a rigorous [theory of truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski's_theory_of_truth) in formal languages starting in the 1930s. He provided a precise analysis of truth conditions and clarified the concept of [logical consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624). His work formed a cornerstone of model theory.[^o113] [Rudolf Carnap](wikipedia://en/57987) synthesized and generalized many of Frege's and Tarski's ideas. To overcome problems associated with extensional definitions of meaning, Carnap pioneered the study of [intensional](wikipedia://en/14570) semantics, defining intensions as functions from possible worlds to denotations.[^o114]

[Donald Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Davidson_%28philosopher%29) was influenced by Tarski's approach and emphasized the role of truth conditions as a key component of semantic theory and the analysis of sentence meaning. He also proposed an event-based formalism to translate action sentences into predicate logic.[^o115] [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky)'s work on [generative grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar) inspired [Jerrold Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerrold_Katz) and [Jerry Fodor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Fodor) to explore the relation between syntactic rules and semantic content through the principle of compositionality.[^o116]

Many of these contributions prepared the work of [Richard Montague](wikipedia://en/610182), usually considered the main founding figure of formal semantics. One of his key achievements, starting in the late 1960s and early 1970s, was the development of [a systematic formalism](wikipedia://en/709308) for analyzing significant portions of the English language using tools from formal logic. This stood in contrast to many earlier approaches, which addressed some aspects of natural language but were skeptical of broader applications and had their main focus on the analysis of formal languages. Relying on [type theory](wikipedia://en/40282) and the principle of compositionality, Montague analyzed complex natural language expressions as nested functions with precisely defined input and output types.[^o117] This development happened against the background of the "[linguistic wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_wars)"a debate between proponents of [generative semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_semantics) and interpretive semantics about whether syntax and semantics are deeply integrated or independent aspects of language. Montague's approach aimed to provide a unified perspective by explaining the relationship between syntactic and semantic rules.[^o118] His system also covers intensional sentences such as modal expressions and propositional attitude reports through the concept of possible worlds.[^o119]

*Image: Barbara\_partee.jpg.* [Barbara Partee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Partee) integrated Montague's insights into linguistic theory.[^o120]

In the following decades, Montague's work influenced many scholars, who sought to refine or modify his insights and apply them to diverse linguistic phenomena.[^o121] [Barbara Partee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Partee) was instrumental in explaining and popularizing Montague's ideas, helping formal semantics grow into a subfield of [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics) by integrating Montague's insights into linguistic theory.[^o120] In response, various theorists focused on the relation between syntax and semantics, proposing diverse grammatical theories to explain the interface, such as [generalized phrase structure grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_phrase_structure_grammar) and [head-driven phrase structure grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head-driven_phrase_structure_grammar). They also include the contributions of [Irene Heim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irene_Heim) and [Angelika Kratzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angelika_Kratzer) to the semantics of generative grammar.[^o122] A parallel development was a rising interest in [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics), which examines how the use of an expression affects its meaning, encompassing topics like context dependence, presupposition, and [indexicality](wikipedia://en/1029182).[^o123]

The work of [Robert Stalnaker](wikipedia://en/4050095) and [David Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lewis_%28philosopher%29) prepared the development of [dynamic semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_semantics), which analyzes the meaning of a sentence as the information contribution it makes. Their theories inspired later developments by [Hans Kamp](wikipedia://en/17054475), Irene Heim, [Jeroen Groenendijk](wikipedia://en/15752422), and [Martin Stokhof](wikipedia://en/3888996).[^o124] Stalnaker and Lewis, together with [Saul Kripke](wikipedia://en/48039), also made influential contributions to possible world semantics.[^o125] [Jon Barwise](wikipedia://en/912858) and [John Perry](wikipedia://en/2637941) proposed situation semantics as another influential framework. It incorporates many insights from possible world semantics but takes a more fine-grained approach, analyzing meaning in terms of situations rather than possible worlds.[^o126] Both [David Kaplan](wikipedia://en/1479115) and [Pauline Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Jacobson) made various contributions to the study of context-sensitive expressions, such as deictic and anaphoric terms. Jacobson also explored the principle of direct compositionality, which suggests a particularly close link between syntax and semantics.[^o127]
