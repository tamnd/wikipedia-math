# Commutativity of conjunction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commutativity of conjunction"}

In [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus), the **commutativity of conjunction** is a [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [argument form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_form) and truth-functional [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335). It is considered to be a law of [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868). It is the principle that the conjuncts of a [logical conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) may switch places with each other, while preserving the [truth-value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth-value) of the resulting proposition.[^o2]

:::

## Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

*Commutativity of conjunction* can be expressed in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation as:

$$(P \land Q) \vdash (Q \land P)$$

and

$$(Q \land P) \vdash (P \land Q)$$

where $\vdash$ is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) symbol meaning that $(Q \land P)$ is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) of $(P \land Q)$, in the one case, and $(P \land Q)$ is a syntactic consequence of $(Q \land P)$ in the other, in some [logical system](wikipedia://en/396102);

or in [rule form](wikipedia://en/252311):

$$\frac{P \land Q}{\therefore Q \land P}$$

and

$$\frac{Q \land P}{\therefore P \land Q}$$

where the rule is that wherever an instance of "$(P \land Q)$" appears on a line of a proof, it can be replaced with "$(Q \land P)$" and wherever an instance of "$(Q \land P)$" appears on a line of a proof, it can be replaced with "$(P \land Q)$";

or as the statement of a truth-functional tautology or [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of propositional logic:

$$(P \land Q) \to (Q \land P)$$

and

$$(Q \land P) \to (P \land Q)$$

where $P$ and $Q$ are [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094) expressed in some formal system.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Generalized principle"}

## Generalized principle {#Generalized_principle}

For any propositions H<sub>1</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>, ... H<sub>*n*</sub>, and permutation σ(n) of the numbers 1 through n, it is the case that:

H<sub>1</sub> $\land$ H<sub>2</sub> $\land$ ... $\land$ H<sub>n</sub>

is equivalent to

H<sub>σ(1)</sub> $\land$ H<sub>σ(2)</sub> $\land$ H<sub>σ(n)</sub>.

For example, if H<sub>1</sub> is

*It is raining*

H<sub>2</sub> is

*[Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) is mortal*

and H<sub>3</sub> is

*2+2=4*

then

*It is raining and Socrates is mortal and 2+2=4*

is equivalent to

*Socrates is mortal and 2+2=4 and it is raining*

and the other orderings of the predicates.

:::
