# Holonomic function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Holonomic function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), a **holonomic function** is a smooth [function of several variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_of_several_variables) that is a solution of a system of [linear homogeneous differential equations](wikipedia://en/379868) with polynomial coefficients and satisfies a suitable dimension condition in terms of [*D*-modules](wikipedia://en/3440755) theory. More precisely, a holonomic function is an element of a [holonomic module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holonomic_module) of smooth functions. Holonomic functions can also be described as **differentiably finite functions**, also known as ***D*-finite functions**. When a power series in the variables is the Taylor expansion of a holonomic function, the sequence of its coefficients, in one or several indices, is also called *holonomic*. **Holonomic sequences** are also called ***P*-recursive sequences**: they are defined recursively by multivariate recurrences satisfied by the whole sequence and by suitable specializations of it. The situation simplifies in the univariate case: any univariate sequence that satisfies a linear homogeneous [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) with polynomial coefficients, or equivalently a linear homogeneous difference equation with polynomial coefficients, is holonomic.[^o2]

:::

## Holonomic functions and sequences in one variable {#Holonomic_functions_and_sequences_in_one_variable}

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $\mathbb{K}$ be a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 0 (for example, $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$).

A function $f = f(x)$ is called *$D$-finite* (or *holonomic*) if there exist polynomials $0 \neq a_r(x), a_{r-1}(x), \ldots, a_0(x) \in \mathbb{K}[x]$ such that

$$a_r(x) f^{(r)}(x) + a_{r-1}(x) f^{(r-1)}(x) + \cdots + a_1(x) f'(x) + a_0(x) f(x) = 0$$

holds for all $x$. This can also be written as $A f = 0$ where

$$A = \sum_{k=0}^r a_k D_x^k$$

and $D_x$ is the [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) that maps $f(x)$ to $f'(x)$. $A$ is called an *annihilating operator* of $f$ (the annihilating operators of $f$ form an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) in the ring $\mathbb{K}[x][D_x]$, called the *annihilator* of $f$). The quantity $r$ is called the *order* of the annihilating operator. By extension, the holonomic function $f$ is said to be of order $r$ when an annihilating operator of such order exists.

A sequence $c = c_0, c_1, \ldots$ is called *$P$-recursive* (or *holonomic*) if there exist polynomials $a_r(n), a_{r-1}(n), \ldots, a_0(n) \in \mathbb{K}[n]$ such that

$$a_r(n) c_{n+r} + a_{r-1}(n) c_{n+r-1} + \cdots + a_0(n) c_n = 0$$

holds for all $n$. This can also be written as $A c = 0$ where

$$A = \sum_{k=0}^r a_k S_n$$

and $S_n$ the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) that maps $c_0, c_1, \ldots$ to $c_1, c_2, \ldots$. $A$ is called an *annihilating operator* of $c$ (the annihilating operators of $c$ form an ideal in the ring $\mathbb{K}[n][S_n]$, called the *annihilator* of $c$). The quantity $r$ is called the *order* of the annihilating operator. By extension, the holonomic sequence $c$ is said to be of order $r$ when an annihilating operator of such order exists.

Holonomic functions are precisely the [generating functions](wikipedia://en/160993) of holonomic sequences: if $f(x)$ is holonomic, then the coefficients $c_n$ in the power series expansion

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n x^n$$

form a holonomic sequence. Conversely, for a given holonomic sequence $c_n$, the function defined by the above sum is holonomic (this is true in the sense of [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012), even if the sum has a zero [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476)).

:::

### Closure properties {#Closure_properties}

Holonomic functions (or sequences) satisfy several [closure properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_property). In particular, holonomic functions (or sequences) form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). They are not closed under division, however, and therefore do not form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603).

If $\textstyle f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n x^n$ and $\textstyle g(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} g_n x^n$ are holonomic functions, then the following functions are also holonomic:

- $h(x) = \alpha f(x) + \beta g(x)$, where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are constants
- $h(x) = f(x) g(x)$ (the [Cauchy product](wikipedia://en/863791) of the sequences)
- $h(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} f_n g_n x^n$ (the Hadamard product of the sequences)
- $$h(x) = \int_0^x f(t) \,\mathrm{d}t$$
- $$h(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\sum_{k=0}^n f_k\right) x^n$$
- $h(x) = f(a(x))$, where $a(x)$ is any [algebraic function](wikipedia://en/974169). However, $a(f(x))$ is generally not holonomic.

A crucial property of holonomic functions is that the closure properties are effective: given annihilating operators for $f$ and $g$, an annihilating operator for $h$ as defined using any of the above operations can be computed explicitly.

### Examples of holonomic functions and sequences {#Examples_of_holonomic_functions_and_sequences}

Examples of holonomic functions include:

- all [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169), including [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) and [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210)
- the [sine and cosine](wikipedia://en/30367) functions (but *not* tangent, cotangent, secant, or cosecant)
- the [hyperbolic sine and cosine](wikipedia://en/56567) functions (but *not* hyperbolic tangent, cotangent, secant, or cosecant)
- [exponential functions](wikipedia://en/9678) and [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860) (to any base)
- the [generalized hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/361184) ${}_pF_q(a_1,\ldots,a_p, b_1, \ldots, b_q, x)$, considered as a function of $x$ with all the parameters $a_i$, $b_i$ held fixed
- the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111) $\operatorname{erf}(x)$
- the [Bessel functions](wikipedia://en/4700) $J_n(x)$, $Y_n(x)$, $I_n(x)$, $K_n(x)$
- the [Airy functions](wikipedia://en/406896) $\operatorname{Ai}(x)$, $\operatorname{Bi}(x)$

The class of holonomic functions is a strict superset of the class of hypergeometric functions. Examples of special functions that are holonomic but not hypergeometric include the [Heun functions](wikipedia://en/2070045).

Examples of holonomic sequences include:

- the sequence of [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number) $F_n$, and more generally, all [constant-recursive sequences](wikipedia://en/49700795)
- the sequence of [factorials](wikipedia://en/10606) $n!$
- the sequence of [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668) $\textstyle{n \choose k}$ (as functions of either $n$ or $k$)
- the sequence of [harmonic numbers](wikipedia://en/214729) $\textstyle H_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k}$, and more generally $\textstyle H_{n,m} = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{1}{k^m}$ for any integer $m$
- the sequence of [Catalan numbers](wikipedia://en/216488)
- the sequence of [Motzkin numbers](wikipedia://en/853141)
- the enumeration of [derangements](wikipedia://en/162132).

Hypergeometric functions, Bessel functions, and classical [orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718), in addition to being holonomic functions of their variable, are also holonomic sequences with respect to their parameters. For example, the Bessel functions $J_n$ and $Y_n$ satisfy the second-order linear recurrence $x (f_{n+1} + f_{n-1}) = 2 n f_n$.

### Examples of nonholonomic functions and sequences {#Examples_of_nonholonomic_functions_and_sequences}

Examples of nonholonomic functions include:

- the function $\tfrac{x}{e^x-1}$[^o9]
- the function $\tan x + \sec x$[^o10]
- the quotient of two holonomic functions is generally not holonomic.

Examples of nonholonomic sequences include:

- the [Bernoulli numbers](wikipedia://en/4964)
- the numbers of [alternating permutations](wikipedia://en/4813617)[^o11]
- the numbers of [integer partitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29)[^o10]
- the numbers $\log n$[^o10]
- the numbers $n^{\alpha}$ where $\alpha \not\in \mathbb{Z}$[^o10]
- the [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666)[^o10]
- the enumerations of *irreducible and connected permutations*.[^o12]

## Algorithms and software {#Algorithms_and_software}

Holonomic functions are a powerful tool in [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805). A holonomic function or sequence can be represented by a finite amount of data, namely an annihilating operator and a finite set of initial values, and the closure properties allow carrying out operations such as equality testing, summation and integration in an algorithmic fashion. In recent years, these techniques have allowed giving automated proofs of a large number of special function and combinatorial identities.

Moreover, there exist fast algorithms for evaluating holonomic functions to arbitrary precision at any point in the complex plane, and for numerically computing any entry in a holonomic sequence.

Software for working with holonomic functions includes:

- The *HolonomicFunctions* <http://www.risc.jku.at/research/combinat/software/HolonomicFunctions/> package for [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica), developed by Christoph Koutschan, which supports computing closure properties and proving identities for univariate and multivariate holonomic functions
- The *algolib* <http://algo.inria.fr/libraries/> library for [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29), which includes the following packages:
  - *gfun*, developed by Bruno Salvy, Paul Zimmermann and Eithne Murray, for univariate closure properties and proving <http://perso.ens-lyon.fr/bruno.salvy/?page_id=48>
  - *mgfun*, developed by Frédéric Chyzak, for multivariate closure properties and proving <http://algo.inria.fr/chyzak/mgfun.html>
  - *numgfun*, developed by Marc Mezzarobba, for numerical evaluation
