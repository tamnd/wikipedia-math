# Riesz mean

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Riesz mean"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Riesz mean** is a certain [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of the terms in a [series](wikipedia://en/15287). They were introduced by [Marcel Riesz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Riesz) in 1911 as an improvement over the [Cesàro mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesàro_mean). The Riesz mean should not be confused with the [Bochner–Riesz mean](wikipedia://en/20966805) or the [Strong–Riesz mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong–Riesz_mean).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a series $\{s_n\}$, the Riesz mean of the series is defined by

$$s^\delta(\lambda) = \sum_{n\le \lambda} \left(1-\frac{n}{\lambda}\right)^\delta s_n$$

Sometimes, a generalized Riesz mean is defined as

$$R_n = \frac{1}{\lambda_n} \sum_{k=0}^n (\lambda_k-\lambda_{k-1})^\delta s_k$$

Here, the $\lambda_n$ are a sequence with $\lambda_n\to\infty$ and with $\lambda_{n+1}/\lambda_n\to 1$ as $n\to\infty$. Other than this, the $\lambda_n$ are taken as arbitrary.

Riesz means are often used to explore the [summability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summability) of sequences; typical summability theorems discuss the case of $s_n = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k$ for some sequence $\{a_k\}$. Typically, a sequence is summable when the limit $\lim_{n\to\infty} R_n$ exists, or the limit $\lim_{\delta\to 1,\lambda\to\infty}s^\delta(\lambda)$ exists, although the precise summability theorems in question often impose additional conditions.

:::

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

Let $a_n=1$ for all $n$. Then

$$\sum_{n\le \lambda} \left(1-\frac{n}{\lambda}\right)^\delta = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \frac{\Gamma(1+\delta)\Gamma(s)}{\Gamma(1+\delta+s)} \zeta(s) \lambda^s \, ds = \frac{\lambda}{1+\delta} + \sum_n b_n \lambda^{-n}.$$

Here, one must take $c>1$; $\Gamma(s)$ is the [Gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) and $\zeta(s)$ is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). The [power series](wikipedia://en/59958)

$$\sum_n b_n \lambda^{-n}$$

can be shown to be convergent for $\lambda > 1$. Note that the integral is of the form of an inverse [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297).

Another interesting case connected with [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) arises by taking $a_n=\Lambda(n)$ where $\Lambda(n)$ is the [Von Mangoldt function](wikipedia://en/1928862). Then

$$\sum_{n\le \lambda} \left(1-\frac{n}{\lambda}\right)^\delta \Lambda(n) = - \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \frac{\Gamma(1+\delta)\Gamma(s)}{\Gamma(1+\delta+s)} \frac{\zeta^\prime(s)}{\zeta(s)} \lambda^s \, ds = \frac{\lambda}{1+\delta} + \sum_\rho \frac {\Gamma(1+\delta)\Gamma(\rho)}{\Gamma(1+\delta+\rho)} +\sum_n c_n \lambda^{-n}.$$

Again, one must take *c* > 1. The sum over *ρ* is the sum over the zeroes of the Riemann zeta function, and

$$\sum_n c_n \lambda^{-n} \,$$

is convergent for *λ* > 1.

The integrals that occur here are similar to the [Nörlund–Rice integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nörlund–Rice_integral); very roughly, they can be connected to that integral via [Perron's formula](wikipedia://en/4928530).

:::
