# Neighbourhood system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Neighbourhood system"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **neighbourhood system**, **complete system of neighbourhoods**,[^o2] or **neighbourhood filter** $\mathcal{N}(x)$ for a point $x$ in a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is the collection of all [neighbourhoods](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $x.$

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

**Neighbourhood of a point or set**

An open neighbourhood of a point (or [subset](wikipedia://en/27631)[^o19]) $x$ in a topological space $X$ is any [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) $U$ of $X$ that contains $x.$ A neighbourhood of $x$ in $X$ is any subset $N \subseteq X$ that contains some open neighbourhood of $x$; explicitly, $N$ is a neighbourhood of $x$ in $X$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) there exists some open subset $U$ with $x \in U \subseteq N$.[^n1][^n2] Equivalently, a neighborhood of $x$ is any set that contains $x$ in its [topological interior](wikipedia://en/55610).

Importantly, a "neighbourhood" does not have to be an open set; those neighbourhoods that also happen to be open sets are known as "open neighbourhoods."[^o20] Similarly, a neighbourhood that is also a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) (respectively, [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), etc.) set is called a closed neighbourhood (respectively, compact neighbourhood, connected neighbourhood, etc.). There are many other types of neighbourhoods that are used in topology and related fields like [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). The family of all neighbourhoods having a certain "useful" property often forms a neighbourhood basis, although many times, these neighbourhoods are not necessarily open. [Locally compact spaces](wikipedia://en/48632), for example, are those spaces that, at every point, have a neighbourhood basis consisting entirely of compact sets.

**Neighbourhood filter**

The neighbourhood system for a point (or [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) subset) $x$ is a [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549) called the neighbourhood filter for $x.$ The neighbourhood filter for a point $x \in X$ is the same as the neighbourhood filter of the [singleton set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) $\{x\}.$

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Fundamental system of neighborhoods"}

### Neighbourhood basis {#Neighbourhood_basis}

A neighbourhood basis or local basis (or neighbourhood base or local base) for a point $x$ is a [filter base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_base) of the neighbourhood filter; this means that it is a subset

$$\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{N}(x)$$

such that for all $V \in \mathcal{N}(x),$ there exists some $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $B \subseteq V.$[^n2] Here, $\mathcal{N}(x)$ denotes the set of all neighbourhoods of $x$. That is, for any neighbourhood $V$ we can find a neighbourhood $B$ in the neighbourhood basis that is contained in $V.$

Equivalently, $\mathcal{B}$ is a local basis at $x$ if and only if the neighbourhood filter $\mathcal{N}$ can be recovered from $\mathcal{B}$ in the sense that the following equality holds:[^o10]

$$\mathcal{N}(x) = \left\{ V \subseteq X ~:~ B \subseteq V \text{ for some } B \in \mathcal{B} \right\}\!\!\;.$$

A family $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{N}(x)$ is a neighbourhood basis for $x$ if and only if $\mathcal{B}$ is a [cofinal subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinal_set) of $\left(\mathcal{N}(x), \supseteq\right)$ with respect to the [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) $\supseteq$ (importantly, this partial order is the [superset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superset) relation and not the [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) relation).

:::

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Neighborhood subbase"}

### Neighbourhood subbasis {#Neighbourhood_subbasis}

A neighbourhood subbasis at $x$ is a family $\mathcal{S}$ of subsets of $X,$ each of which contains $x,$ such that the collection of all possible finite [intersections](wikipedia://en/23476429) of elements of $\mathcal{S}$ forms a neighbourhood basis at $x.$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

If $\mathbb{R}$ has its usual [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) then the neighborhoods of $0$ are all those subsets $N \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ for which there exists some [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $r > 0$ such that $(-r, r) \subseteq N.$ For example, all of the following sets are neighborhoods of $0$ in $\mathbb{R}$:

$$(-2, 2), \; [-2,2], \; [-2, \infty), \; [-2, 2) \cup \{10\}, \; [-2, 2] \cup \mathbb{Q}, \; \mathbb{R}$$

but none of the following sets are neighborhoods of $0$:

$$\{0\}, \; \mathbb{Q}, \; (0,2), \; [0, 2), \; [0, 2) \cup \mathbb{Q}, \; (-2, 2) \setminus \left\{1, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \ldots\right\}$$

where $\mathbb{Q}$ denotes the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024).

If $U$ is an open subset of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ then for every $u \in U,$ $U$ is a neighborhood of $u$ in $X.$ More generally, if $N \subseteq X$ is any set and $\operatorname{int}_X N$ denotes the [topological interior](wikipedia://en/55610) of $N$ in $X,$ then $N$ is a neighborhood (in $X$) of every point $x \in \operatorname{int}_X N$ and moreover, $N$ is not a neighborhood of any other point. Said differently, $N$ is a neighborhood of a point $x \in X$ if and only if $x \in \operatorname{int}_X N.$

**Neighbourhood bases**

In any topological space, the neighbourhood system for a point is also a neighbourhood basis for the point. The set of all open neighbourhoods at a point forms a neighbourhood basis at that point. For any point $x$ in a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), the sequence of [open balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) around $x$ with radius $1/n$ form a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) neighbourhood basis $\mathcal{B} = \left\{B_{1/n} : n = 1,2,3,\dots \right\}$. This means every metric space is [first-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-countable).

Given a space $X$ with the [indiscrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_topology) the neighbourhood system for any point $x$ only contains the whole space, $\mathcal{N}(x) = \{X\}$.

In the [weak topology](wikipedia://en/33662) on the space of measures on a space $E,$ a neighbourhood base about $\nu$ is given by

$$\left\{\mu \in \mathcal{M}(E) : \left|\mu f_i - \nu f_i\right| < r_i, \, i = 1,\dots,n\right\}$$

where $f_i$ are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) bounded functions from $E$ to the real numbers and $r_1, \dots, r_n$ are positive real numbers.

**Seminormed spaces and topological groups**

In a [seminormed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminormed_space), that is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) induced by a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067), all neighbourhood systems can be constructed by [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) of the neighbourhood system for the origin,

$$\mathcal{N}(x) = \mathcal{N}(0) + x.$$

This is because, by assumption, vector addition is separately continuous in the induced topology. Therefore, the topology is determined by its neighbourhood system at the origin. More generally, this remains true whenever the space is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) or the topology is defined by a [pseudometric](wikipedia://en/68503).

## Properties {#Properties}

Suppose $u \in U \subseteq X$ and let $\mathcal{N}$ be a neighbourhood basis for $u$ in $X.$ Make $\mathcal{N}$ into a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165) by [partially ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) it by superset inclusion $\,\supseteq.$ Then $U$ is not a neighborhood of $u$ in $X$ if and only if there exists an $\mathcal{N}$-indexed [net](wikipedia://en/22170) $\left(x_N\right)_{N \in \mathcal{N}}$ in $X \setminus U$ such that $x_N \in N \setminus U$ for every $N \in \mathcal{N}$ (which implies that $\left(x_N\right)_{N \in \mathcal{N}} \to u$ in $X$).
