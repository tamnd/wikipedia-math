# Frobenius group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frobenius group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Frobenius group** is a [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Transitivity_properties) [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) on a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742), such that no non-trivial element fixes more than one point and some non-trivial element fixes a point. They are named after [F. G. Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048).

:::

## Structure {#Structure}

Suppose *G* is a Frobenius group consisting of permutations of a set *X*. A [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *H* of *G* fixing a point of *X* is called a **Frobenius complement**. The identity element together with all elements not in any conjugate of *H* form a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) called the **Frobenius kernel** *K*. (This is a theorem due to (Frobenius 1901); there is still no proof of this theorem that does not use [character theory](wikipedia://en/691741), although see [^o4].) The Frobenius group *G* is the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of *K* and *H*:

$$G=K\rtimes H.$$

Both the Frobenius kernel and the Frobenius complement have very restricted structures. (txt) proved that the Frobenius kernel *K* is a [nilpotent group](wikipedia://en/144569). If *H* has even order then *K* is abelian. The Frobenius complement *H* has the property that every subgroup whose order is the product of 2 primes is cyclic; this implies that its [Sylow subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_subgroup) are [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) or [generalized quaternion](wikipedia://en/153130) groups. Any group such that all Sylow subgroups are cyclic is called a [Z-group](wikipedia://en/15757039#Groups_whose_Sylow_subgroups_are_cyclic), and in particular must be a [metacyclic group](wikipedia://en/7515561): this means it is the extension of two cyclic groups. If a Frobenius complement *H* is not solvable then [Zassenhaus](wikipedia://en/1376825) showed that it has a normal subgroup of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 1 or 2 that is the product of SL(2,5) and a metacyclic group of order coprime to 30. In particular, if a Frobenius complement coincides with its derived subgroup, then it is isomorphic with SL(2,5). If a Frobenius complement *H* is solvable then it has a normal metacyclic subgroup such that the quotient is a subgroup of the symmetric group on 4 points. A finite group is a Frobenius complement if and only if it has a faithful, finite-dimensional representation over a finite field in which non-identity group elements correspond to linear transformations without nonzero fixed points.

The Frobenius kernel *K* is uniquely determined by *G* as it is the [Fitting subgroup](wikipedia://en/1416042), and the Frobenius complement is uniquely determined up to conjugacy by the [Schur-Zassenhaus theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schur-Zassenhaus_theorem). In particular a finite group *G* is a Frobenius group in at most one way.

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Fano plane.svg.* The Fano plane

- The smallest example is the symmetric group on 3 points, with 6 elements. The Frobenius kernel *K* has order 3, and the complement *H* has order 2.
- For every [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) *F<sub>q</sub>* with *q* (> 2) elements, the group of invertible [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) $x \mapsto ax+b$, $a\ne 0$ acting naturally on *F<sub>q</sub>* is a Frobenius group. The preceding example corresponds to the case *F<sub>3</sub>*, the field with three elements.
- Another example is provided by the subgroup of order 21 of the [collineation group](wikipedia://en/4072976) of the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404) generated by a 3-fold symmetry σ fixing a point and a cyclic permutation τ of all 7 points, satisfying στ = τ<sup>2</sup>σ. Identifying *F*<sub>8</sub><sup>×</sup> with the Fano plane, σ can be taken to be the restriction of the [Frobenius automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_automorphism) σ(*x*) = *x*<sup>2</sup> of *F*<sub>8</sub> and τ to be multiplication by any element not 0 or 1 (i.e. a generator of the [cyclic multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/11615#Applications) of *F*<sub>8</sub>). This Frobenius group acts [simply transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) on the 21 [flags](wikipedia://en/9352497) in the Fano plane, i.e. lines with marked points.
- The [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of order 2*n* with *n* odd is a Frobenius group with complement of order 2. More generally if *K* is any abelian group of odd order and *H* has order 2 and acts on *K* by inversion, then the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) *K.H* is a Frobenius group.
- Many further examples can be generated by the following constructions. If we replace the Frobenius complement of a Frobenius group by a non-trivial subgroup we get another Frobenius group. If we have two Frobenius groups *K*<sub>1</sub>.*H* and *K*<sub>2</sub>.*H* then (*K*<sub>1</sub> × *K*<sub>2</sub>).*H* is also a Frobenius group.
- If *K* is the non-abelian group of order 7<sup>3</sup> with exponent 7, and *H* is the cyclic group of order 3, then there is a Frobenius group *G* that is an extension *K.H* of *H* by *K*. This gives an example of a Frobenius group with non-abelian kernel. This was the first example of Frobenius group with nonabelian kernel (it was constructed by Otto Schmidt).
- If *H* is the group *SL*<sub>2</sub>(*F*<sub>5</sub>) of order 120, it acts fixed point freely on a 2-dimensional vector space *K* over the field with 11 elements. The extension *K.H* is the smallest example of a non-[solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) Frobenius group.
- The subgroup of a [Zassenhaus group](wikipedia://en/1724820) fixing a point is a Frobenius group.
- Frobenius groups whose Fitting subgroup has arbitrarily large nilpotency class were constructed by Ito: Let *q* be a prime power, *d* a positive integer, and *p* a prime divisor of *q* −1 with *d* ≤ *p*. Fix some field *F* of order *q* and some element *z* of this field of order *p*. The Frobenius complement *H* is the cyclic subgroup generated by the diagonal matrix whose *i,i**th entry is*****z*<sup>*i*</sup>. The Frobenius kernel*K*is the Sylow*q*\-subgroup of GL(*d*,*q*) consisting of upper triangular matrices with ones on the diagonal. The kernel*K*has nilpotency class*d*−1, and the semidirect product*KH*is a Frobenius group.***

:::

## Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

The irreducible complex representations of a Frobenius group *G* can be read off from those of *H* and *K*. There are two types of [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of *G*:

- Any irreducible representation *R* of *H* gives an irreducible representation of *G* using the quotient map from *G* to *H*. These give the irreducible representations of *G* with *K* in their kernel.
- If *S* is any *non-trivial* irreducible representation of *K*, then the corresponding [induced representation](wikipedia://en/357452) of *G* is also irreducible. These give the irreducible representations of *G* with *K* not in their kernel.

## Alternative definitions {#Alternative_definitions}

There are a number of group theoretical properties which are interesting on their own right, but which happen to be equivalent to the group possessing a permutation representation that makes it a Frobenius group.

- *G* is a Frobenius group if and only if *G* has a proper, nonidentity subgroup *H* such that *H* ∩ *H*<sup>*g*</sup> is the identity subgroup for every *g* ∈ *G* − *H*, *i.e.* *H* is a [malnormal subgroup](wikipedia://en/3593817) of *G*.

This definition is then generalized to the study of trivial intersection sets which allowed the results on Frobenius groups used in the classification of [CA groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CA_group) to be extended to the results on [CN groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CN_group) and finally the [odd order theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_order_theorem#An_outline_of_the_proof).

Assuming that $G = K\rtimes H$ is the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of the normal subgroup *K* and complement *H*, then the following restrictions on [centralizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) are equivalent to *G* being a Frobenius group with Frobenius complement *H*:

- The [centralizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) C<sub>*G*</sub>(*k*) is a subgroup of K for every nonidentity *k* in *K*.
- C<sub>*H*</sub>(*k*) = 1 for every nonidentity *k* in *K*.
- C<sub>*G*</sub>(*h*) ≤ H for every nonidentity *h* in H.
