# Ira Gessel

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ira Gessel"}

**Ira Martin Gessel** (born 9 April 1951)[^o2] is an American mathematician, known for his work in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170). He is a long-time faculty member at [Brandeis University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandeis_University) and resides in [Arlington, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arlington,_Massachusetts).

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Gessel studied at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) graduating *[magna cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_cum_laude)* in 1973. There, he became a [Putnam Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putnam_Fellow) in 1972, alongside [Arthur Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Rubin) and [David Vogan](wikipedia://en/10229758).[^o4]

He received his Ph.D. at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT) and was the first student of [Richard P. Stanley](wikipedia://en/1633397). He was then a postdoctoral fellow at the [IBM Watson Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Watson_Research_Center) and MIT. He then joined Brandeis University faculty in 1984. He was promoted to P professor of mathematics and computer science in 1990, became a chair in 1996–98, and [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus) in 2015.

Gessel is a prolific contributor to [enumerative](wikipedia://en/3925533) and [algebraic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/14835582). He is credited with the invention of [quasisymmetric functions](wikipedia://en/29661763) in 1984[^o5] and foundational work on the [Lagrange inversion theorem](wikipedia://en/94158). As of 2017, Gessel was an advisor of 27 Ph.D. students.

Gessel was elected a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) in the inaugural class of 2012. Since 2015, he is an associate editor of the *[Digital Library of Mathematical Functions](wikipedia://en/12647205)*.[^o6]

## Gessel's lattice path conjecture {#Gessel's_lattice_path_conjecture}

Gessel has made significant contributions to an area in combinatorics known as lattice walks, which usually take place on the integer lattice and are sometimes confined to the upper right quadrant. An excursion is a lattice walk which starts at the origin and returns to the origin. A lattice excursion in the upper right quadrant with four possible steps, up, down, northeast, and southwest, is now known as a Gessel excursion.

By 2001 Gessel had noted empirically, and conjectured, that the number of Gessel excursions with 2n steps admit a simple hypergeometric closed form. This closed form counting function equation became known as Gessel's lattice path conjecture. A computer aided proof of Gessel's conjecture by [Manuel Kauers](wikipedia://en/40133395), [Christoph Koutschan](wikipedia://en/48644256), and [Doron Zeilberger](wikipedia://en/1724825), was published in 2009.[^o9]

The 2022 [David P. Robbins Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_P._Robbins_Prize) of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) was awarded to Alin Bostan, Irina Kurkova, and Kilian Raschel, for their 2017 paper "A human proof of Gessel's lattice path conjecture."[^o10]

:::

## Political activism {#Political_activism}

In 1970, while a senior in high school, Ira Gessel and his brother Michael Gessel started a [grassroots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassroots) political organization to [end pay toilets in America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_to_End_Pay_Toilets_in_America).[^o12] The movement was largely successful and was disbanded in 1976.
