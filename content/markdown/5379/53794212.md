# Kempe's universality theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kempe's universality theorem"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), **Kempe's universality theorem** states that any [bounded subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_subset) of an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) may be traced out by the motion of one of the joints in a suitably chosen [linkage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linkage_%28mechanical%29).[^o2] It is named for British mathematician [Alfred B. Kempe](wikipedia://en/9092767), who in 1876 published his article *On a General Method of describing Plane Curves of the nth degree by Linkwork,*[^o3] which showed that for any arbitrary algebraic plane curve, a linkage can be constructed that draws the curve. However, Kempe's proof was flawed and the first complete proof was provided in 2002 based on his ideas.[^o4][^o5]

:::

This theorem has been popularized by describing it as saying, "One can design a linkage which will sign your name!"[^o6]

Kempe recognized that his results demonstrate the existence of a drawing linkage but it would not be practical. He states

> It is hardly necessary to add, that this method would not be practically useful on account of the complexity of the linkwork employed, a necessary consequence of the perfect generality of the demonstration.[^o3]

He then calls for the "mathematical artist" to find simpler ways to achieve this result:

> The method has, however, an interest, as showing that there *is* a way of drawing any given case; and the variety of methods of expressing particular functions that have already been discovered renders it in the highest degree probable that in every case a simpler method can be found. There is still, however, a wide field open to the mathematical artist to discover the simplest linkworks that will describe particular curves.[^o3]

A series of animations demonstrating the linkwork that results from Kempe's universality theorem are available for the [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), self-intersecting [cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_plane_curve), smooth [elliptic](wikipedia://en/10225) cubic and the [trifolium](wikipedia://en/77387454) curves.[^o7]

## Simpler drawing linkages {#Simpler_drawing_linkages}

Several approaches have been taken to simplify the drawing linkages that result from Kempe's universality theorem. Some of the complexity arises from the linkages Kempe used to perform addition and subtraction of two angles, the multiplication of an angle by a constant, and translation of the rotation of a link in one location to a rotation of a second link at another location. Kempe called these linkages additor, reversor, multiplicator and translator linkages, respectively. The drawing linkage can be simplified by using [bevel gear differentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28mechanical_device%29) to add and subtract angles, [gear trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gear_train) to multiply angles and [belt or cable drives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_%28mechanical%29) to translate rotation angles.[^o9]

Another source of complexity is the generality of Kempe's application to all algebraic curves. By focusing on parameterized algebraic curves, [dual quaternion algebra](wikipedia://en/8730871) can be used to factor the motion polynomial and obtain a drawing linkage.[^o10] This has been extended to provide movement of the end-effector, but again for parameterized curves.[^o11]

Specializing the curves to those defined by [trigonometric polynomials](wikipedia://en/591568) has provided another way to obtain simpler drawing linkages.[^o12] [Bézier curves](wikipedia://en/4664) can be written in the form of [trigonometric polynomials](wikipedia://en/591568) therefore a linkage system can be designed that draws any curve that is approximated by a sequence of Bézier curves.[^o13]
