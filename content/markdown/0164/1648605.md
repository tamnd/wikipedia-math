# Unimodular lattice

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unimodular lattice"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and mathematical [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a **unimodular lattice** is an integral [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) of [determinant](wikipedia://en/469246#Dividing_space_according_to_a_lattice) 1 or −1. For a lattice in *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), this is equivalent to requiring that the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of any [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) for the lattice be 1.

:::

The [*E*<sub>8</sub> lattice](wikipedia://en/1800359) and the [Leech lattice](wikipedia://en/344919) are two famous examples.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

- A **lattice** is a [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) of finite [rank](wikipedia://en/247296) with a [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) (·, ·).
- The lattice is **integral** if (·,·) takes [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) values.
- The **dimension** of a lattice is the same as its [rank](wikipedia://en/326454) (as a **Z**-[module](wikipedia://en/276410)).
- The **norm** of a lattice element *a* is (*a*, *a*).
- A lattice is **positive definite** if the norm of all nonzero elements is positive.
- The **determinant** of a lattice is the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [Gram matrix](wikipedia://en/987959), a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with entries (*a<sub>i</sub>*, *a<sub>j</sub>*), where the elements *a<sub>i</sub>* form a basis for the lattice.
- An integral lattice is **unimodular** if its determinant is 1 or −1.
- A unimodular lattice is **even** or **type II** if all norms are even, otherwise **odd** or **type I**.
- The **minimum** of a positive definite lattice is the lowest nonzero norm.
- Lattices are often embedded in a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with a symmetric bilinear form. The lattice is **positive definite**, **Lorentzian**, and so on if its vector space is.
- The **signature** of a lattice is the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28quadratic_form%29) of the form on the vector space.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The three most important examples of unimodular lattices are:

- The lattice **Z** (the set of all [integers](wikipedia://en/14563)), in one dimension.
- The [*E*<sub>8</sub> lattice](wikipedia://en/1800359), an even 8-dimensional lattice.
- The [Leech lattice](wikipedia://en/344919), the 24-dimensional even unimodular lattice with no roots.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

An integral lattice is unimodular [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its [dual lattice](wikipedia://en/1395200) is integral. Unimodular lattices are equal to their dual lattices, and for this reason, unimodular lattices are also known as self-dual.

Given a pair (*m*,*n*) of nonnegative integers, an even unimodular lattice of signature (*m*,*n*) exists if and only if *m*−*n* is divisible by 8, but an odd unimodular lattice of signature (*m*,*n*) always exists. In particular, even unimodular definite lattices only exist in dimension divisible by 8. Examples in all admissible signatures are given by the *II<sub>m,n</sub>* and *I<sub>m,n</sub>* constructions, respectively.

The [theta function](wikipedia://en/449745) of a unimodular positive definite lattice is a [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000) whose weight is one half the rank. If the lattice is even, the form has [level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_of_a_modular_form) 1, and if the lattice is odd the form has Γ<sub>0</sub>(4) structure (i.e., it is a modular form of level 4). Due to the dimension bound on spaces of modular forms, the minimum norm of a nonzero vector of an even unimodular lattice is no greater than ⎣*n*/24⎦ + 1. An even unimodular lattice that achieves this bound is called extremal. Extremal even unimodular lattices are known in relevant dimensions up to 80,[^o8] and their non-existence has been [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) for dimensions above 163,264.[^o9]

:::

## Classification {#Classification}

For indefinite lattices, the classification is easy to describe. Write **R**<sup>*m*,*n*</sup> for the *m* + *n* [dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) vector space **R**<sup>*m*+*n*</sup> with the inner product of (*a*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *a*<sub>*m*+*n*</sub>) and (*b*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *b*<sub>*m*+*n*</sub>) given by

$$a_1 b_1 + \cdots + a_m b_m - a_{m+1} b_{m+1} - \cdots - a_{m+n} b_{m+n}. \,$$

In **R**<sup>*m*,*n*</sup> there is one odd indefinite unimodular lattice up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), denoted by

*I*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub>,

which is given by all vectors (*a*<sub>1</sub>,...,*a*<sub>*m*+*n*</sub>) in **R**<sup>*m*,*n*</sup> with all the *a*<sub>*i*</sub> integers.

There are no indefinite even unimodular lattices unless

*m* − *n* is divisible by 8,

in which case there is a unique example up to isomorphism, denoted by

*II*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub>.

This is given by all vectors (*a*<sub>1</sub>,...,*a*<sub>*m*+*n*</sub>) in **R**<sup>*m*,*n*</sup> such that either all the *a<sub>i</sub>* are integers or they are all integers plus 1/2, and their sum is even. The lattice *II*<sub>8,0</sub> is the same as the *E*<sub>8</sub> lattice.

Positive definite unimodular lattices have been classified up to dimension 25. There is a unique example *I*<sub>*n*,0</sub> in each dimension *n* less than 8, and two examples (*I*<sub>8,0</sub> and *II*<sub>8,0</sub>) in dimension 8. The number of lattices increases moderately up to dimension 25 (where there are 665 of them), but beyond dimension 25 the [Smith-Minkowski-Siegel mass formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith-Minkowski-Siegel_mass_formula) implies that the number increases very rapidly with the dimension; for example, there are more than 80,000,000,000,000,000 in dimension 32.

In some sense unimodular lattices up to dimension 9 are controlled by *E*<sub>8</sub>, and up to dimension 25 they are controlled by the Leech lattice, and this accounts for their unusually good behavior in these dimensions. For example, the [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109) of the norm-2 vectors of unimodular lattices in dimension up to 25 can be naturally identified with a configuration of vectors in the Leech lattice. The wild increase in numbers beyond 25 dimensions might be attributed to the fact that these lattices are no longer controlled by the Leech lattice.

Even positive definite unimodular lattice exist only in dimensions divisible by 8. There is one in dimension 8 (the *E*<sub>8</sub> lattice), two in dimension 16 (*E*<sub>8</sub><sup>2</sup> and *II*<sub>16,0</sub>), and 24 in dimension 24, called the [Niemeier lattices](wikipedia://en/1652072) (examples: the [Leech lattice](wikipedia://en/344919), *II*<sub>24,0</sub>, *II*<sub>16,0</sub> + *II*<sub>8,0</sub>, *II*<sub>8,0</sub><sup>3</sup>). Beyond 24 dimensions the number increases very rapidly; in 32 dimensions there are more than a billion of them.

Unimodular lattices with no *roots* (vectors of norm 1 or 2) have been classified up to dimension 29. There are none of dimension less than 23 (other than the zero lattice!). There is one in dimension 23 (called the **short Leech lattice**), two in dimension 24 (the Leech lattice and the **odd Leech lattice**), and (Bacher Venkov 2001) showed that there are 0, 1, 3, 38 in dimensions 25, 26, 27, 28, respectively. Beyond this the number increases very rapidly; there are 10092 in dimension 29 (Allombert Chenevier 2025). In sufficiently high dimensions most unimodular lattices have no roots.

The only non-zero example of even positive definite unimodular lattices with no roots in dimension less than 32 is the Leech lattice in dimension 24. In dimension 32 there are more than ten million examples, and above dimension 32 the number increases very rapidly.

## Applications {#Applications}

The second [cohomology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group) of a closed [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented) topological [4-manifold](wikipedia://en/1362795) is a unimodular lattice. [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970) showed that this lattice almost determines the [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470): there is a unique such manifold for each even unimodular lattice, and exactly two for each odd unimodular lattice. In particular if we take the lattice to be 0, this implies the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798) for 4-dimensional topological manifolds. [Donaldson's theorem](wikipedia://en/2180754) states that if the manifold is [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) and the lattice is positive definite, then it must be a sum of copies of **Z**, so most of these manifolds have no [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238). One such example is the [$E_8$ manifold](wikipedia://en/6091657).

:::
