# C0-semigroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="C0-semigroup"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), a ***C*<sub>0</sub>-semigroup**, also known as a **strongly continuous one-parameter semigroup**, is a generalization of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678). Just as exponential functions provide solutions of scalar [linear](wikipedia://en/379868) [constant coefficient](wikipedia://en/379868#Homogeneous_equation_with_constant_coefficients) [ordinary](wikipedia://en/32742753) [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309), strongly continuous semigroups provide solutions of linear constant coefficient ordinary differential equations in [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). Such differential equations in Banach spaces arise from e.g. [delay differential equations](wikipedia://en/3941333) and [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564).

:::

Formally, a strongly continuous semigroup is a representation of the [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) $(\mathbb R_{\ge 0}, +)$ on some Banach space $X$ that is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29) in the [strong operator topology](wikipedia://en/458699).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A **strongly continuous semigroup** on a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $X$ is a map $T : \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \to L(X)$ (where $L(X)$ is the space of [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961) on $X$) such that

1. $T(0) = I$, (the [identity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_operator) on $X$)
1. $$\forall t,s \ge 0 : \ T(t + s) = T(t) T(s)$$
1. $\forall x_0 \in X: \ \|T(t) x_0 - x_0\| \to 0$, as $t\downarrow 0$.

The first two axioms are [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923), and state that $T$ is a representation of the semigroup ${(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0},+)}$; the last is [topological](wikipedia://en/29954), and states that the map $T$ is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29) in the [strong operator topology](wikipedia://en/458699).

:::

## Infinitesimal generator {#Infinitesimal_generator}

The **infinitesimal generator** $A$ of a strongly continuous semigroup $T$ is defined by

$$A\,x = \lim_{t\downarrow0} \frac{(T(t)- I)\,x}{t}$$

whenever the limit exists. The [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of $A$, $D(A)$, is the set of $x \in X$ for which this limit does exist; $D(A)$ is a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) and $A$ is [linear](wikipedia://en/18102) on this domain.[^o5] The operator $A$ is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_operator), although not necessarily [bounded](wikipedia://en/455961), and the domain is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $X$.[^o6]

The strongly continuous semigroup $T$ with generator $A$ is often denoted by the symbol $e^{At}$ (or, equivalently, $\exp(At)$). This notation is compatible with the notation for [matrix exponentials](wikipedia://en/672731), and for functions of an operator defined via [functional calculus](wikipedia://en/965348) (for example, via the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495)).

## Uniformly continuous semigroup {#Uniformly_continuous_semigroup}

A uniformly continuous semigroup is a strongly continuous semigroup $T$ such that

$$\lim_{t \to 0^+} \| T(t) - I \| = 0$$

holds. In this case, the infinitesimal generator $A$ of $T$ is bounded and we have

$$\mathcal{D}(A)=X$$

and

$$T(t) = e^{At}:=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{A^k}{k!}t^k.$$

[Conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), any bounded operator

$$A \colon X \to X$$

is the infinitesimal generator of a uniformly continuous semigroup given by

$$T(t) := e^{At}.$$

Thus, a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $A$ is the infinitesimal generator of a uniformly continuous semigroup if and only if $A$ is a bounded linear operator.[^o8] If $X$ is a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) Banach space, then any strongly continuous semigroup is a uniformly continuous semigroup. For a strongly continuous semigroup which is not a uniformly continuous semigroup the infinitesimal generator $A$ is not bounded. In this case, $e^{At}$ does not need to converge.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Multiplication semigroup {#Multiplication_semigroup}

Consider the Banach space $C_0(\mathbb{R}):=\{f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{C} \text{ continuous}: \forall \epsilon >0 ~\exists c>0 \text{ such that } \vert f(x) \vert \leq \epsilon ~ \forall x\in \mathbb{R} \setminus [-c,c] \}$ endowed with the [sup norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sup_norm) $\Vert f\Vert := \text{sup}_{x\in \mathbb {R}}\vert f(x) \vert$. Let $q: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ be a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) with $\text{sup}_{s\in \mathbb{R}}\text{Re}(q(s))<\infty$. The operator $M_qf:=q\cdot f$ with domain $D(M_q):=\{f\in C_0(\mathbb{R}): q\cdot f \in C_0(\mathbb{R}) \}$ is a closed [densely defined operator](wikipedia://en/13510193) and generates the multiplication semigroup $(T_q(t))_{t\geq 0}$ where $T_q(t)f:= \mathrm{e}^{qt}f.$ Multiplication operators can be viewed as the infinite dimensional generalisation of [diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_matrices) and a lot of the properties of $M_q$ can be derived by properties of $q$. For example $M_q$ is bounded on $C_0(\mathbb{R)}$ if and only if $q$ is [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509).[^o12]

### Translation semigroup {#Translation_semigroup}

Let $C_{ub}(\mathbb{R})$ be the space of bounded, [uniformly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_continuous) functions on $\mathbb{R}$ endowed with the sup norm. The (left) translation semigroup $(T_l(t))_{t\geq 0}$ is given by $T_l(t)f(s):=f(s+t), \quad s,t\in \mathbb{R}$.

Its generator is the derivative $Af:=f'$ with domain $D(A):=\{f\in C_{ub}(\mathbb{R}): f \text{ differentiable with }f'\in C_{ub}(\mathbb{R})\}$.[^o14]

:::

## Abstract Cauchy problems {#Abstract_Cauchy_problems}

Consider the abstract [Cauchy problem](wikipedia://en/1725027):

$$u'(t)=Au(t),\quad u(0)=x,$$

where $A$ is a [closed operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_operator) on a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $X$ and $x \in X$. There are two concepts of solution of this problem:

- a [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable) function $u : [0, \infty) \to X$ is called a **classical solution** of the Cauchy problem if $u(t) \in D(A)$ for all $t > 0$ and it satisfies the [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383),
- a continuous function $u : [0, \infty) \to X$ is called a **mild solution** of the Cauchy problem if

$$\int_0^t u(s)\,ds\in D(A)\text{ and }A \int_0^t u(s)\,ds=u(t)-x.$$

Any classical solution is a mild solution. A mild solution is a classical solution if and only if it is continuously differentiable.[^o16]

The following [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) connects abstract Cauchy problems and strongly continuous semigroups.

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem:[^o18]** Let $A$ be a closed operator on a Banach space $X$. The following assertions are equivalent:

1. for all $x \in X$ there exists a unique mild solution of the abstract Cauchy problem,
1. the operator $A$ generates a strongly continuous semigroup,
1. the [resolvent set](wikipedia://en/8721698) of $A$ is [nonempty](wikipedia://en/9566) and for all $x \in D(A)$ there exists a unique classical solution of the Cauchy problem.

When these assertions hold, the solution of the Cauchy problem is given by $u(t) \in T(t)x$ with $T$ the strongly continuous semigroup generated by $A$.

:::

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Generation theorems"}

## Generation theorems {#Generation_theorems}

In connection with Cauchy problems, usually a linear operator $A$ is given and the question is whether this is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup. Theorems which answer this question are called **generation theorems**. A complete characterization of operators that generate exponentially bounded strongly continuous semigroups is given by the [Hille–Yosida theorem](wikipedia://en/1578212). Of more practical importance are however the much easier to verify conditions given by the [Lumer–Phillips theorem](wikipedia://en/12281541).

For families of [unitary operators](wikipedia://en/225999) on a Hilbert space that are not just semigroups but groups, i.e. are defined for negative values of $t$ as well, [Stone's theorem on one-parameter unitary groups](wikipedia://en/893698) describes their generators as precisely the [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408). This has applications in the [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) of a [quantum mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanical) system.

:::

## Special classes of semigroups {#Special_classes_of_semigroups}

### Uniformly continuous semigroups {#Uniformly_continuous_semigroups}

The strongly continuous semigroup $T$ is called **uniformly continuous** if the map $t \to T(t)$ is continuous from $[0,\infty)$ to $L(X)$.

The generator of a uniformly continuous semigroup is a [bounded operator](wikipedia://en/455961).

### Analytic semigroups {#Analytic_semigroups}

::: {#o25 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Contraction semi-group"}

### Contraction semigroups {#Contraction_semigroups}

A *C*<sub>0</sub>-semigroup $(T(t))_{t \ge 0}$ is called a **quasicontraction semigroup** if there is a constant $\omega$ such that $\|T(t)\| \le \exp(\omega t)$ for all $t \ge 0$. It is called a **contraction semigroup** if $\|T(t)\| \le 1$ for all $t \ge 0$.[^o26]

:::

### Differentiable semigroups {#Differentiable_semigroups}

A strongly continuous semigroup $T$ is called **eventually differentiable** if there exists a $t_0 > 0$ such that $T(t_0)X \subset D(A)$ (equivalently: $T(t)X \subset D(A)$ for all $t \ge t_0$) and $T$ is **immediately differentiable** if $T(t)X \subset D(A)$ for all $t > 0$.

Every analytic semigroup is immediately differentiable.

An equivalent characterization in terms of Cauchy problems is the following: the strongly continuous semigroup generated by $A$ is eventually differentiable if and only if there exists a $t_1 \ge 0$ such that for all $x \in X$ the solution $u$ of the abstract Cauchy problem is differentiable on $(t_1, \infty)$. The semigroup is immediately differentiable if $t_1$ can be chosen to be zero.

### Compact semigroups {#Compact_semigroups}

A strongly continuous semigroup $T$ is called **eventually compact** if there exists a $t_0 > 0$ such that $T(t_0)$ is a [compact operator](wikipedia://en/577441) (equivalently[^o29] if $T(t)$ is a compact operator for all $t \ge t_0$) . The semigroup is called **immediately compact** if $T(t)$ is a compact operator for all $t > 0$.

### Norm continuous semigroups {#Norm_continuous_semigroups}

A strongly continuous semigroup is called **eventually norm continuous** if there exists a $t_0 \ge 0$ such that the map $t \mapsto T(t)$ is continuous from $(t_0, \infty)$ to $L(X)$. The semigroup is called **immediately norm continuous** if $t_0$ can be chosen to be zero.

Note that for an immediately norm continuous semigroup the map $t \mapsto T(t)$ may not be continuous in $t=0$ (that would make the semigroup uniformly continuous).

Analytic semigroups, (eventually) differentiable semigroups and (eventually) compact semigroups are all eventually norm continuous.[^o31]

## Stability {#Stability}

::: {#o34 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Exponentially stable semigroup"}

### Exponential stability {#Exponential_stability}

The **growth bound** of a semigroup $T$ is the constant

$$\omega_0 = \inf_{t>0} \frac{1}{t} \log \| T(t) \|.$$

It is so called as this number is also the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\omega$ such that there exists a constant $M > 0$ with

$$\|T(t)\| \leq Me^{\omega t}$$

for all $t \ge 0$.

The following are equivalent:[^o35]

1. There exist $M, \omega > 0$ such that for all $t \ge 0$: $\|T(t)\|\leq M{\rm e}^{-\omega t},$
1. The growth bound is negative: $\omega_0 < 0$,
1. The semigroup converges to zero in the [uniform operator topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_operator_topology): $\lim_{t\to\infty}\|T(t)\|=0$,
1. There exists a $t_0 > 0$ such that $\|T(t_0)\|<1$,
1. There exists a $t_1 > 0$ such that the [spectral radius](wikipedia://en/386169) of $T(t_1)$ is strictly smaller than 1,
1. There exists a $p \in [1, \infty)$ such that for all $x \in X$: $\int_0^\infty\|T(t)x\|^p\,dt<\infty$,
1. For all $p \in [1, \infty)$ and all $x \in X$: $\int_0^\infty\|T(t)x\|^p\,dt<\infty.$

A semigroup that satisfies these equivalent conditions is called **exponentially stable** or **uniformly stable** (either of the first three of the above statements is taken as the definition in certain parts of the literature). That the $L^p$ conditions are equivalent to exponential stability is called the **Datko-Pazy theorem**.

In case $X$ is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) there is another condition that is equivalent to exponential stability in terms of the [resolvent operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolvent_operator) of the generator:[^o36] all $\lambda$ with positive [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) belong to the resolvent set of $A$ and the resolvent operator is uniformly bounded on the right half plane, i.e. $(\lambda I - A)^{-1}$ belongs to the [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780) $H^\infty(\mathbb{C}_+;L(X))$. This is called the **Gearhart-Pruss theorem**.

The **spectral bound** of an operator *A* is the constant

$$s(A) := \sup\{{\rm Re}\,\lambda:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\},$$

with the convention that $s(A) = -\infty$ if the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of *A* is empty.

The growth bound of a semigroup and the spectral bound of its generator are related by[^o37] $s(A) \le \omega_0(T)$. There are examples[^o38] where $s(A) < \omega_0(T)$. If $s(A) = \omega_0(T)$, then $T$ is said to satisfy the **spectral determined growth condition**. Eventually norm-continuous semigroups satisfy the spectral determined growth condition.[^o39] This gives another equivalent characterization of exponential stability for these semigroups:

- An eventually norm-continuous semigroup is exponentially stable if and only if $s(A) < 0$.

Note that eventually compact, eventually differentiable, analytic and uniformly continuous semigroups are eventually norm-continuous so that the spectral determined growth condition holds in particular for those semigroups.

:::

### Strong stability {#Strong_stability}

A strongly continuous semigroup $T$ is called **strongly stable** or **asymptotically stable** if for all $x \in X$: $\lim_{t\to\infty}\|T(t)x\| = 0$.

Exponential stability implies strong stability, but the converse is not generally true if $X$ is infinite-dimensional (it is true for $X$ finite-dimensional).

The following sufficient condition for strong stability is called the **Arendt–Batty–Lyubich–Phong theorem**:[^o41][^o42] Assume that

1. $T$ is bounded: there exists a $M \ge 1$ such that $\|T(t)\|\leq M$,
1. $A^*$ has not [point spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_spectrum) on the imaginary axis, and
1. The spectrum of $A$ located on the imaginary axis is countable.

Then $T$ is strongly stable.

If $X$ is [reflexive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_Banach_space) then the conditions simplify: if $T$ is bounded, $A$ has no [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) on the imaginary axis and the spectrum of $A$ located on the imaginary axis is countable, then $T$ is strongly stable.
