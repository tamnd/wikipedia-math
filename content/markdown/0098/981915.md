# Singular point of an algebraic variety

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Singular point of an algebraic variety"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **singular point** of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $V$ is a point $P$ that is 'special' (so, singular), in the geometric sense that at this point the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at the variety may not be regularly defined. In case of varieties defined over the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), this notion generalizes the notion of [local non-flatness](wikipedia://en/12941444). A point of an algebraic variety that is not singular is said to be **regular**. An algebraic variety that has no singular point is said to be **non-singular** or **smooth**. The concept is generalized to [smooth schemes](wikipedia://en/35033819) in the modern language of [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory).

:::

*Image: Newtonsche Knoten.png.* The [plane algebraic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_algebraic_curve) (a [cubic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_curve)) of equation $y^{2} - x^{2}(x + 1) = 0$ crosses itself at the origin $(0, 0)$. The origin is a [double point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_point) of this curve. It is *singular* because a single [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) may not be correctly defined there.

## Definition {#Definition}

A [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) defined by an [implicit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation)

$$F(x,y)=0,$$

where $F$ is a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) is said to be *singular* at a point if the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of $F$ has [order](wikipedia://en/59958#Order_of_a_power_series) at least $2$ at this point.

The reason for this is that, in [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), the tangent at the point $(x_{0}, y_{0})$ of such a curve is defined by the equation

$$(x-x_0)F'_x(x_0,y_0) + (y-y_0)F'_y(x_0,y_0)=0,$$

whose left-hand side is the term of degree one of the Taylor expansion. Thus, if this term is zero, the tangent may not be defined in the standard way, either because it does not exist or a special definition must be provided.

In general for a [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101)

$$F(x,y,z,\ldots) = 0$$

the **singular points** are those at which all the [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) simultaneously vanish. A general [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $V$ being defined as the common zeros of several [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), the condition on a point $P$ of $V$ to be a singular point is that the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) of the first-order partial derivatives of the polynomials has a [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_of_a_matrix) at $P$ that is lower than the rank at other points of the variety.

Points of $V$ that are not singular are called **non-singular** or **regular**. It is always true that almost all points are non-singular, in the sense that the non-singular points form a set that is both [open](wikipedia://en/39358) and [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in the variety (for the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744), as well as for the usual topology, in the case of varieties defined over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)).[^o4]

In case of a real variety (that is the set of the points with real coordinates of a variety defined by polynomials with real coefficients), the variety is a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) near every regular point. But a real variety may be a manifold and have singular points. For example the equation $y^{3} + 2x^{2}y - x^{4} = 0$ defines a real [analytic manifold](wikipedia://en/3183798) but has a singular point at the origin.[^o5] This may be explained by saying that the curve has two [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) [branches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branch_%28complex_analysis%29) that cut the real branch at the origin.

:::

## Singular points of smooth mappings {#Singular_points_of_smooth_mappings}

As the notion of singular points is a purely local property, the above definition can be extended to cover the wider class of [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) mappings (functions from $M$ to $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ where all derivatives exist). Analysis of these singular points can be reduced to the algebraic variety case by considering the [jets](wikipedia://en/1521924) of the mapping. The $k$th jet is the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of the mapping truncated at degree $k$ and deleting the [constant term](wikipedia://en/79861).

## Nodes {#Nodes}

Singular point of a curve In [classical algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/35326250), certain special singular points were also called **nodes**. A node is a singular point where the [Hessian matrix](wikipedia://en/412108) is non-singular; this implies that the singular point has multiplicity two and the [tangent cone](wikipedia://en/1416915) is not singular outside its vertex.
