# Discourse analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discourse analysis"}

Subfields

**Discourse analysis** (**DA**), or **discourse studies**, is an approach to the analysis of written, spoken, or sign language, including any significant [semiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotic) event.

:::

The objects of discourse analysis ([discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse), writing, conversation, communicative [event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_interactionism)) are variously defined in terms of coherent sequences of [sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28linguistics%29), [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094), [speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_acts), or [turns-at-talk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation_Analysis#Turn-taking_organization). Contrary to much of traditional linguistics, discourse analysts not only study language use 'beyond the sentence boundary' but also prefer to analyze 'naturally occurring' language use, not invented examples.[^o2] [Text linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_linguistics) is a closely related field. The essential difference between discourse analysis and text linguistics is that discourse analysis aims at revealing [socio-psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_psychology_%28sociology%29) characteristics of a person/persons rather than text structure.[^o3]

Discourse analysis has been taken up in a variety of disciplines in the [humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities) and [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science), including [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), education, [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology), [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology), [social work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_work), [cognitive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychology), [social psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_psychology), [area studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_studies), [cultural studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_studies), [international relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_relations), [human geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_geography), [environmental studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_studies), [communication studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_studies), [biblical studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_studies), [public relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_relations), [argumentation studies](wikipedia://en/1317383), and [translation studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_studies), each of which is subject to its own assumptions, dimensions of analysis, and [methodologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodology).

## History {#History}

section

### Early use of the term {#Early_use_of_the_term}

There is ongoing discussion about whether Austria-born [Leo Spitzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Spitzer)'s *Stilstudien* (*Style Studies*) of 1928 is the earliest example of *discourse analysis* (DA). [Michel Foucault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Foucault) translated it into French.[^o7] However, the term first came into general use following the publication of a series of papers by [Zellig Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zellig_Harris) from 1952[^o8] reporting on work from which he developed [transformational grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformational_grammar) in the late 1930s. Formally equivalent relations among the sentences of a coherent discourse are made explicit by using sentence transformations to put the text in a canonical form. Words and sentences with equivalent information then appear in the same column of an array.

This work progressed over the next four decades (see references) into a science of [sublanguage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublanguage) analysis (Kittredge & Lehrberger 1982), culminating in a demonstration of the informational structures in texts of a sublanguage of science, that of immunology (Harris et al. 1989),[^o9] and a fully articulated theory of linguistic informational content (Harris 1991).[^o9] During this time, however, most linguists ignored such developments in favor of a succession of elaborate theories of sentence-level syntax and semantics.[^o10]

In January 1953, a linguist working for the American Bible Society, James A. Lauriault (*alt.* Loriot), needed to find answers to some fundamental errors in translating Quechua, in the Cuzco area of Peru. Following Harris's 1952 publications, he worked over the meaning and placement of each word in a collection of Quechua legends with a native speaker of Quechua and was able to formulate discourse rules that transcended the simple sentence structure. He then applied the process to Shipibo, another language of Eastern Peru. He taught the theory at the Summer Institute of Linguistics[^o11] in Norman, Oklahoma, in the summers of 1956 and 1957 and entered the University of Pennsylvania[^o12] to study with Harris in the interim year. He tried to publish a paper,[^o13]*Shipibo Paragraph Structure*, but it was delayed until 1970 (Loriot & Hollenbach 1970). In the meantime, [Kenneth Lee Pike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Lee_Pike), a professor at the University of Michigan,[^o14] taught the theory, and one of his students, [Robert E. Longacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Longacre), developed it in his writings. Harris's methodology disclosing the correlation of form with meaning was developed into a system for the computer-aided analysis of natural language by a team led by [Naomi Sager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naomi_Sager) at [NYU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NYU), which has been applied to a number of sublanguage domains, most notably to medical informatics.

### In the humanities {#In_the_humanities}

In the late 1960s and 1970s, and without reference to this prior work, a variety of other approaches to a new cross-discipline of DA began to develop in most of the humanities and social sciences concurrently with, and related to, other disciplines. These include [semiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics), [psycholinguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psycholinguistics), [sociolinguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociolinguistics), and [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics). Many of these approaches, especially those influenced by the social sciences, favor a more dynamic study of oral talk-in-interaction. An example is "[conversational analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation_analysis)" (CA),[^o16] which was influenced by the sociologist [Harold Garfinkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Garfinkel),[^o17] the founder of [ethnomethodology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnomethodology).

## Perspectives {#Perspectives}

The following are some of the specific theoretical perspectives and analytical approaches used in linguistic discourse analysis:

- [Applied linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_linguistics), an interdisciplinary perspective on linguistic analysis[^o19]
- [Cognitive neuroscience of discourse comprehension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aron_K._Barbey#Cognitive_neuroscience_of_human_intelligence)[^o20][^o21]
- [Cognitive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychology), studying the production and comprehension of discourse
- [Conversation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation_analysis)
- [Critical discourse analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_discourse_analysis)
- [Discursive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discursive_psychology)
- [Emergent grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_grammar)
- [Ethnography of communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnography)
- [Functional grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_theories_of_grammar)
- [Interactional sociolinguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactional_sociolinguistics)
- [Mediated stylistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediated_stylistics)
- [Pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics)
- [Response based therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Response_based_therapy) (counselling)
- [Rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447)
- [Stylistics (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stylistics_%28linguistics%29)
- [Sublanguage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublanguage) analysis
- [Tagmemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagmemics)
- [Text linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_linguistics)
- [Variation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variation_analysis)

Although these approaches emphasize different aspects of language use, they all view language as social interaction and are concerned with the social contexts in which discourse is embedded.

Often a distinction is made between 'local' structures of discourse (such as relations among sentences, propositions, and turns) and 'global' structures, such as overall topics and the schematic organization of discourses and conversations. For instance, many types of discourse begin with some kind of global 'summary', in titles, headlines, leads, abstracts, and so on.

## Topics of interest {#Topics_of_interest}

Topics of discourse analysis include:[^o23]

- The various levels or dimensions of discourse, such as sounds ([intonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_%28linguistics%29), etc.), [gestures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestures), [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860), the [lexicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicon), [style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stylistics_%28linguistics%29), [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447), [meanings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28linguistic%29), [speech acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_acts), moves, [strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategies), turns, and other aspects of [interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_interaction)
- [Genres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genres) of discourse (various types of discourse in politics, the media, education, science, business, etc.)
- The relations between discourse and the [emergence of syntactic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_grammar)
- The relations between text (discourse) and [context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context_%28language_use%29)
- The relations between discourse and [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28sociology%29)
- The relations between discourse and interaction
- The relations between discourse and [cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognition) and [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory)
- [Lexical density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_density)
- Ecocultural discourse studies consider human relationships with their wider ecologies. As Milstein writes, “discourses not only serve to structure, oppress, and transform human lives, but so, too, do discourses serve to symbolically and materially construct, constrain, and change the more than human world”.[^o24]

::: {#o26 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Political discourse"}

### Political discourse {#Political_discourse}

**Political discourse** is the text and talk of professional politicians or political institutions, such as presidents and prime ministers and other members of government, parliament or political parties, both at the local, national and international levels, includes both the speaker and the audience.[^o27]

Political discourse analysis is a field of discourse analysis which focuses on discourse in political forums (such as debates, speeches, and hearings) as the phenomenon of interest. [Policy analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_analysis) requires discourse analysis to be effective from the [post-positivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-positivist) perspective.[^o28][^o29]

Political discourse is the formal exchange of reasoned views as to which of several alternative courses of action should be taken to solve a societal problem.[^o30]

:::

### Corporate discourse {#Corporate_discourse}

**Corporate discourse** can be broadly defined as the language used by corporations. It encompasses a set of messages that a corporation sends out to the world (the general public, the customers and other corporations) and the messages it uses to communicate within its own structures (the employees and other stakeholders).[^o32]
