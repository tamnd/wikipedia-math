# Mertens' theorems

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mertens' theorems"}

In [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513), **Mertens' theorems** are three 1874 results related to the density of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) proved by [Franz Mertens](wikipedia://en/41999).[^o2]

:::

In the following, let $p\le n$ mean all primes not exceeding *n*.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="First theorem"}

## First theorem {#First_theorem}

**Mertens' first theorem** is that

$$\sum_{p \le n} \frac{\log p}{p} - \log n$$

does not exceed 2 in [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) for any $n\ge 2$. ()

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Second theorem"}

## Second theorem {#Second_theorem}

**Mertens' second theorem** is

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{p\le n}\frac1p -\log\log n-M\right) =0,$$

*Image: Mertens second theorem.png.* Comparison between $\sum_{p\le n}{\frac{1}{p}}$ to $\log\log n + M$, where $M$ is the [Meissel-Mertens constant](wikipedia://en/435212).

where *M* is the [Meissel–Mertens constant](wikipedia://en/435212) (). More precisely, Mertens[^o2] proves that the expression under the limit does not in absolute value exceed

$$\frac 4{\log(n+1)} +\frac 2{n\log n}$$

for any $n\ge 2$.

::: {#o8 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

The main step in the proof of Mertens' second theorem is

$$\begin{aligned} O(n)+n\log n=\log n! =\sum_{p^k\le n} \lfloor n/p^k\rfloor\log p \\ = \sum_{p^k\le n} \left(\frac{n}{p^k}+O(1)\right)\log p= n \sum_{p^k\le n}\frac{\log p}{p^k}\ + O(n) \end{aligned}$$

where the last equality needs $\sum_{p^k\le n}\log p =O(n)$ which follows from $\sum_{p\in (n,2n]}\log p\le \log{2n\choose n}=O(n)$.

Thus, we have proved that

$$\sum_{p^k\le n}\frac{\log p}{p^k}=\log n+O(1).$$

Since the sum over prime powers with $k \ge 2$ converges, this implies

$$\sum_{p\le n}\frac{\log p}{p}=\log n+O(1).$$

A [partial summation](wikipedia://en/246188) yields

$$\sum_{p\le n} \frac1{p} = \log\log n+M+O(1/\log n).$$

:::

### Changes in sign {#Changes_in_sign}

*Image: Mertens second theorem oscillations.png.* Oscillations of the difference between $\sum_{p\le n}{\frac{1}{p}}$ to $\log \log n + M$

In a paper [^o10] on the growth rate of the [sum-of-divisors function](wikipedia://en/407249#Approximate_growth_rate) published in 1983, Guy Robin proved that in Mertens' second theorem the difference

$$\sum_{p\le n}\frac1p -\log\log n-M$$

changes sign infinitely often, and that in Mertens' third theorem the difference

$$\log n\prod_{p\le n}\left(1-\frac1p\right)-e^{-\gamma}$$

changes sign infinitely often. Robin's results are analogous to [Littlewood](wikipedia://en/158371)'s [famous theorem](wikipedia://en/250178#Skewes's_numbers) that the difference π(*x*) − li(*x*) changes sign infinitely often. No analog of the [Skewes number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skewes_number) (an upper bound on the first [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *x* for which π(*x*) > li(*x*)) is known in the case of Mertens' second and third theorems.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Relation to the prime number theorem"}

### Relation to the prime number theorem {#Relation_to_the_prime_number_theorem}

Regarding this asymptotic formula Mertens refers in his paper to "two curious formula of Legendre",[^o2] the first one being Mertens' second theorem's prototype (and the second one being Mertens' third theorem's prototype: see the very first lines of the paper). He recalls that it is contained in Legendre's third edition of his "Théorie des nombres" (1830; it is in fact already mentioned in the second edition, 1808), and also that a more elaborate version was proved by [Chebyshev](wikipedia://en/149568) in 1851.[^o13] Note that, already in 1737, [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) knew the asymptotic behaviour of this sum.

Mertens diplomatically describes his proof as more precise and rigorous. In reality none of the previous proofs are acceptable by modern standards: Euler's computations involve the infinity (and the hyperbolic [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of infinity, and the logarithm of the logarithm of infinity!); Legendre's argument is [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic); and Chebyshev's proof, although perfectly sound, makes use of the Legendre-Gauss conjecture, which was not proved until 1896 and became better known as the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692).

Mertens' proof does not appeal to any unproved hypothesis (in 1874), and only to elementary [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478). It comes 22 years before the first proof of the prime number theorem which, by contrast, relies on a careful analysis of the behavior of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) as a function of a complex variable. Mertens' proof is in that respect remarkable. Indeed, with [modern notation](wikipedia://en/44578) it yields

$$\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p=\log\log x+M+O(1/\log x)$$

whereas the prime number theorem (in its simplest form, without error estimate), can be shown to imply [^o14]

$$\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p=\log\log x+M+o(1/\log x).$$

In 1909 [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230), by using the best version of the prime number theorem then at his disposition, proved[^o15] that

$$\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p=\log\log x+M+O(e^{-(\log x)^{1/14}})$$

holds; in particular the error term is smaller than $1/(\log x)^k$ for any fixed [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *k*. A simple [summation by parts](wikipedia://en/246188) exploiting the [strongest form known](wikipedia://en/23692#Prime-counting_function_in_terms_of_the_logarithmic_integral) of the prime number theorem improves this to

$$\sum_{p\le x}\frac1p=\log\log x+M+O(e^{-c(\log x)^{3/5}(\log\log x)^{-1/5}})$$

for some $c > 0$.

Similarly a partial summation shows that $\sum_{p\le x} \frac{\log p}{p} = \log x+ C+o(1)$ is implied by the PNT.

:::

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Third theorem"}

## Third theorem {#Third_theorem}

**Mertens' third theorem** is

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\log n\prod_{p\le n}\left(1-\frac1p\right)=e^{-\gamma} \approx 0.561459483566885,$$

where $\gamma$ is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant) ().

### Relation to sieve theory {#Relation_to_sieve_theory}

An estimate of the probability of $X$ ($X \gg n$) having no factor $\le n$ is given by

$$\prod_{p\le n}\left(1-\frac1p\right)$$

This is closely related to Mertens' third theorem which gives an asymptotic approximation of

$$P(p \nmid X\ \forall p \le n) = \frac{1}{e^\gamma \log n }$$

:::
