# Exhaustivity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Exhaustivity"}

In [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), **exhaustivity** is the phenomenon where a [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) can be strengthened with the [negation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_negation) of certain alternatives. For example, in response to the question "Which students got an A?", the utterance "Ava got an A" has an **exhaustive interpretation** when it conveys that no other students got an A. It has a **non-exhaustive interpretation** when it merely conveys that Ava was among the students who got an A.

:::

Exhaustivity is a major topic in the linguistic subfields of [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_%28linguistics%29) and [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics). Research on the topic aims to explain when and why expressions receive exhaustive interpretations. Particular factors include [focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_%28linguistics%29), [disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction), [questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Questions),[^n1] and [polarity items](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarity_items). A major theoretical issue is whether exhaustivity is a semantic [entailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entailment) encoded in the [grammar](wikipedia://en/12569), a pragmatic [implicature](wikipedia://en/393671) arising from [Gricean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gricean) [social cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_cognition), or some combination of the two.[^n2]
