# Linear response function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear response function"}

A **linear response function** describes the input-output relationship of a [signal transducer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_transducer), such as a radio turning [electromagnetic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave) into music or a [neuron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuron) turning [synaptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapse) input into a response. Because of its many applications in [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) there exist alternative names for specific linear response functions such as [susceptibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_susceptibility), [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825) or [impedance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_impedance); see also [transfer function](wikipedia://en/31146). The concept of a [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) or [fundamental solution](wikipedia://en/1820858) of an [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) is closely related.

:::

## Mathematical definition {#Mathematical_definition}

Denote the input of a system by $h(t)$ (e.g. a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force)), and the response of the system by $x(t)$ (e.g. a position). Generally, the value of $x(t)$ will depend not only on the present value of $h(t)$, but also on past values. Approximately $x(t)$ is a weighted sum of the previous values of $h(t')$, with the weights given by the linear response function $\chi(t-t')$:

$$x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^t dt'\, \chi(t-t') h(t') + \cdots\,.$$

The explicit term on the right-hand side is the [leading order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading-order) term of a [Volterra expansion](wikipedia://en/5811728) for the full nonlinear response. If the system in question is highly non-linear, higher order terms in the expansion, denoted by the dots, become important and the signal transducer cannot adequately be described just by its linear response function.

The complex-valued [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) $\tilde{\chi}(\omega)$ of the linear response function is very useful as it describes the output of the system if the input is a sine wave $h(t) = h_0 \sin(\omega t)$ with frequency $\omega$. The output reads

$$x(t) = \left|\tilde{\chi}(\omega)\right| h_0 \sin(\omega t+\arg\tilde{\chi}(\omega))\,,$$

with [amplitude gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplifier) $|\tilde{\chi}(\omega)|$ and [phase shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_shift) $\arg\tilde{\chi}(\omega)$.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider a [damped harmonic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damped_harmonic_oscillator) with input given by an external driving force $h(t)$,

$$\ddot{x}(t)+\gamma \dot{x}(t)+\omega_0^2 x(t) = h(t).$$

The complex-valued [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of the linear response function is given by

$$\tilde{\chi}(\omega) = \frac{\tilde{x}(\omega)}{\tilde{h}(\omega)} = \frac{1}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}.$$

The amplitude gain is given by the magnitude of the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $\tilde\chi (\omega ),$ and the phase shift by the arctan of the imaginary part of the function divided by the real one.

From this representation, we see that for small $\gamma$ the Fourier transform $\tilde{\chi}(\omega)$ of the linear response function yields a pronounced maximum ("[Resonance](wikipedia://en/41660)") at the frequency $\omega\approx\omega_0$. The linear response function for a harmonic oscillator is mathematically identical to that of an [RLC circuit](wikipedia://en/27174683). The width of the maximum, $\Delta\omega ,$ typically is much smaller than $\omega_0 ,$ so that the [Quality factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_factor) $Q:=\omega_0 /\Delta\omega$ can be extremely large.

## Kubo formula {#Kubo_formula}

The exposition of linear response theory, in the context of [quantum statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_statistics), can be found in a paper by [Ryogo Kubo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryogo_Kubo).[^o7] This defines particularly the [Kubo formula](wikipedia://en/37099898), which considers the general case that the "force" $h(t)$ is a perturbation of the basic operator of the system, the [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_function), $\hat H_0 \to \hat{H}_0 -h(t')\hat{B}(t')$ where $\hat B$ corresponds to a measurable quantity as input, while the output $x(t)$ is the perturbation of the thermal expectation of another measurable quantity $\hat A(t)$. The Kubo formula then defines the quantum-statistical calculation of the [susceptibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susceptibility_%28disambiguation%29) $\chi ( t -t' )$ by a general formula involving only the mentioned operators.

As a consequence of the principle of [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality_%28physics%29) the complex-valued function $\tilde{\chi }(\omega )$ has poles only in the lower half-plane. This leads to the [Kramers–Kronig relations](wikipedia://en/2481686), which relates the real and the imaginary parts of $\tilde{\chi }(\omega )$ by integration. The simplest example is once more the [damped harmonic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damped_harmonic_oscillator).[^o8]

:::

## Nonequilibrium linear response formula {#Nonequilibrium_linear_response_formula}

Linear response theory has versions for nonequilibrium processes for open systems where there is no detailed balance but a steady driving or agitation is applied. A small perturbation of these driven or active systems gives rise to a response in violation with the equilibrium expressions. A possible methodology proceeds via path-space ensembles where the probabilities of trajectories are evaluated; see e.g.[^o11] The resulting response formulae have an entropic part (similar to the detailed balance case) and a frenetic part. The latter involves the correlation of the (excess) [frenesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frenesy_%28physics%29) (due to the perturbation) with the observable. In detailed balance, the two contributions merge and reproduce the Kubo and [Green-Kubo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green-Kubo) formulae. Out of detailed balance, the frenetic contribution is responsible for the possibility of negative heat capacities and mobilities, and they do not longer measure a fluctuation, e.g. in terms of a variance of the energy or of the current.
