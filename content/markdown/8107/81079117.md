# Pi prime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pi prime"}

In mathematics, a **pi prime** is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) appearing in the decimal expansion of the constant [pi](wikipedia://en/23601).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\pi_n$ be the [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) formed by the first $n$ digits of [pi](wikipedia://en/23601). We call $\pi_n$ a *pi prime* when this value is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). The first four pi primes are

- $$\pi_1=3$$
- $$\pi_2=31$$
- $$\pi_6=314159$$
- $$\pi_{38}=31415926535897932384626433832795028841.$$

In 2026, Robert Baillie proved that $\pi_{16208}=3141592653...7943936307$ is the fifth pi prime.[^o6] [^o7]

:::

## History {#History}

In 1979, Robert Baillie and Marvin Wunderlich found that $\pi_{38}$ is prime. [^o9]

In 2001, Ed T. Prothro found that the first 16208 digits of pi form a [probable prime](wikipedia://en/160560), and that after $\pi_{38}$, all such numbers with fewer than 16208 digits are [composite numbers](wikipedia://en/82289).

In 2006, Eric Weisstein showed that the next two possible pi primes have 47577 and 78073 digits. In 2016, A. Bondrescu showed that, after 78073, the next possible pi prime has 613373 digits.[^o11] As of 2026, it has not been proven that any of these three numbers are primes.

A heuristic argument suggests that the sequence of pi primes is infinite.[^o5]

::: {#o13 .definition cue=heading name="Alternative definitions"}

## Alternative definitions {#Alternative_definitions}

One alternative definition considers only the decimal portion of pi, i.e. digits after the starting 3. In this definition we define $\pi_k$ to be the integer formed by the first $k$ digits of the decimal part of [pi](wikipedia://en/23601). Using this definition, $\pi_k$ is prime when $k = 5, 12, 281, 547$.[^o14]

:::
