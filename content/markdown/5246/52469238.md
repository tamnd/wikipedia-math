# Loosemore–Hanby index

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Loosemore–Hanby index"}

The **Loosemore–Hanby index** measures [disproportionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_representation) of [electoral systems](wikipedia://en/29066482), how much the principle of [one person, one vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_person,_one_vote) is violated.[^o2] It computes the absolute difference between votes cast and seats obtained using the formula:

:::

$LH=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n|v_i-s_i|$, where $v_i$ is the vote share and $s_i$ the seat share of party $i$ such that $\Sigma_i v_i = \Sigma_i s_i = 1$, and $n$ is the overall number of parties.[^o3]

This index is minimized by the [largest remainder (LR) method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_remainder_method) with the [Hare quota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hare_quota). Any apportionment method that minimizes it will always apportion identically to LR-Hare. Other methods, including the widely used [divisor methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_average_method) such as the [Webster/Sainte-Laguë method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Webster/Sainte-Laguë_method) or the [D'Hondt method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method) minimize the [Sainte-Laguë index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sainte-Laguë_Index) instead.

The index is named after John Loosemore and Victor J. Hanby, who first published the formula in 1971 in a paper entitled "The Theoretical Limits of Maximum Distortion: Some Analytic Expressions for Electoral Systems". Along with [Douglas W. Rae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_W._Rae)'s, the formula is one of the two most cited disproportionality indices.[^o4] Whereas the **Rae index** measures the average [deviation](wikipedia://en/9252911), the Loosemore–Hanby index measures the total deviation. [Michael Gallagher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Gallagher_%28academic%29) used [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) to develop the [Gallagher index](wikipedia://en/1743713), which takes a middle ground between the Rae and Loosemore–Hanby indices.[^o5]

The LH index is related to the [Schutz index](wikipedia://en/3247678) of inequality, which is defined as

$$S = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |e_i - a_i|,$$

where $e_i$ is the expected share of individual $i$ and $a_i$ her allocated share. Under the LH index, parties take the place of individuals, vote shares replace expectation shares, and seat shares allocation shares. The LH index is also related to the [dissimilarity index of segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissimilarity_index). All three indexes are special cases of the more general $\Delta$ index of dissimilarity.[^o6] The LH index is related to the amount of [wasted vote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasted_vote), which only measures the difference between votes cast and seats obtained for parties which did not obtain any seats.

The complement of the LH index is called **Party Total Representativity**,[^o7] also called **Rose index** R. The Rose index is typically expressed in % and can be calculated by subtracting the LH index from 1:[^o8]

$$R=1-LH=1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n|v_i-s_i|.$$
