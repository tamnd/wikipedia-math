# Conway criterion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conway criterion"}

*Image: Octagon Prototile.svg.* Prototile Octagon satisfying the Conway criterion. Sections AB and ED are shown in red, and the remaining segments are shown in color with a dot on the point of centrosymmetry.

*Image: Octagon Prototile Tessellation.svg.* A tessellation of the above prototile meeting the Conway criterion.

In the mathematical theory of [tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellations), the **Conway criterion**, named for the English mathematician [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807), is a [sufficient](wikipedia://en/169319) rule for when a [prototile](wikipedia://en/39372) will tile the plane. It consists of the following requirements:[^o2] The tile must be a [closed topological disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_disc#Topological_balls) with six consecutive points A, B, C, D, E, and F on the boundary such that:

:::

- the boundary part from A to B is [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) to the boundary part from E to D by a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) T where T(A) = E and T(B) = D.
- each of the boundary parts BC, CD, EF, and FA is [centrosymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrosymmetry)—that is, each one is congruent to itself when rotated by 180-degrees around its midpoint.
- some of the six points may coincide but at least three of them must be distinct.[^o3]

Any prototile satisfying Conway's criterion admits a [periodic tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_tiling) of the plane—and does so using only 180-degree rotations.[^o2] The Conway criterion is a sufficient condition to prove that a [prototile](wikipedia://en/39372) tiles the plane but not a necessary one. There are tiles that fail the criterion and still tile the plane.[^o4]

Every Conway tile is foldable into either an [isotetrahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotetrahedron) or a rectangle [dihedron](wikipedia://en/6109457) and conversely, every net of an isotetrahedron or rectangle dihedron is a Conway tile.[^o12][^o4]

## History {#History}

The Conway criterion applies to any shape that is a closed disk—if the boundary of such a shape satisfies the criterion, then it will tile the plane. Although the graphic artist [M.C. Escher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.C._Escher) never articulated the criterion, he discovered it in the mid-1920s. One of his earliest tessellations, later numbered 1 by him, illustrates his understanding of the conditions in the criterion. Six of his earliest tessellations all satisfy the criterion. In 1963 the German mathematician [Heinrich Heesch](wikipedia://en/1156248) described the five types of tiles that satisfy the criterion. He shows each type with notation that identifies the edges of a tile as one travels around the boundary: CCC, CCCC, TCTC, TCTCC, TCCTCC, where C means a centrosymmetric edge, and T means a translated edge.[^o6]

Conway was likely inspired by [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435)'s July 1975 column in [Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American) that discussed which convex polygons can tile the plane.[^o7] In August 1975, Gardner revealed that Conway had discovered his criterion while trying to find an efficient way to determine which of the 108 [heptominoes](wikipedia://en/2034821) tile the plane.[^o8]

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Isohedral tiling p6-3.svg.* Example tessellation based on a Type 1 hexagonal tile.

In its simplest form, the criterion simply states that any hexagon with a pair of opposite sides that are parallel and congruent will tessellate the plane.[^o11] In Gardner's article, this is called a type 1 hexagon.[^o8] This is also true of [parallelograms](wikipedia://en/133496). But the translations that match the opposite edges of these tiles are the composition of two 180° rotations—about the midpoints of two adjacent edges in the case of a hexagonal [parallelogon](wikipedia://en/15302229), and about the midpoint of an edge and one of its vertices in the case of a parallelogram. When a tile that satisfies the Conway Criterion is rotated 180° about the midpoint of a centrosymmetric edge, it creates either a generalized parallelogram or a generalized hexagonal parallelogon (these have opposite edges congruent and parallel), so the doubled tile can tile the plane by translations.[^o12] The translations are the composition of 180° rotations just as in the case of the straight-edge hexagonal parallelogon or parallelograms.[^o13]

*Image: Non-Conway tiling nonomino 1.jpg.* A tiling nonomino that does not satisfy the Conway criterion.

*Image: No Tile Heptominoes.png.* The four heptominoes incapable of tiling the plane, including the one heptomino with a hole.

The Conway criterion is surprisingly powerful—especially when applied to [polyforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyforms). With the exception of four [heptominoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heptominoes), all [polyominoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyominoes) up through order 7 either satisfy the Conway criterion or two copies can form a patch which satisfies the criterion.[^o14] Except for two nonominoes, all tiling polyominoes up to size 9 form a patch of at least one tile satisfying it, with higher-size exceptions more frequent.
