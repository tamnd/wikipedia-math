# Isotropic quadratic form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isotropic quadratic form"}

In mathematics, a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *F* is said to be **isotropic** if there is a non-zero vector on which the form evaluates to zero; otherwise, it is **anisotropic**. More explicitly, if *q* is a quadratic form on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* over *F*, then a non-zero vector *v* in *V* is said to be **isotropic** if *q*(*v*) = 0. A quadratic form is isotropic if and only if there exists a non-zero isotropic vector (or [null vector](wikipedia://en/1453977)) for that quadratic form.

:::

Suppose that (*V*, *q*) is [quadratic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_space) and *W* is a [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of *V*. Then *W* is called an **isotropic subspace** of *V* if *some* vector in it is isotropic, a **totally isotropic subspace** if *all* vectors in it are isotropic, and an **anisotropic subspace** if it does not contain *any* (non-zero) isotropic vectors. The **isotropy index** of a quadratic space is the maximum of the dimensions of the totally isotropic subspaces.[^o7]

Over the real numbers, more generally in the case where *F* is a [real closed field](wikipedia://en/1066093) (so that the signature is defined), if the quadratic form is non-degenerate and has the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28quadratic_form%29) (*a*, *b*), then its isotropy index is the minimum of *a* and *b*. An anisotropic quadratic form over a real closed field is always [definite](wikipedia://en/20437320). An important example of an isotropic form over the reals occurs in [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546).

## Hyperbolic plane {#Hyperbolic_plane}

*Image: Drini-conjugatehyperbolas.svg.* *F*= real numbers, quadratic form *r*, isotropic elements are red, *r* = 1 blue, *r* = −1 green, [conjugate hyperbolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola)

Let *F* be a field of [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28field%29) not 2 and *V* = *F*<sup>2</sup>. If we consider the general element (*x*, *y*) of *V*, then the quadratic forms *q* = *xy* and *r* = *x*<sup>2</sup> − *y*<sup>2</sup> are equivalent since there is a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) on *V* that makes *q* look like *r*, and vice versa. Evidently, (*V*, *q*) and (*V*, *r*) are isotropic. This example is called the **hyperbolic plane** in the theory of [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478). A common instance has *F* = [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) in which case {*x* ∈ *V* : *q*(*x*) = nonzero constant} and {*x* ∈ *V* : *r*(*x*) = nonzero constant} are [hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052). In particular, {*x* ∈ *V* : *r*(*x*) = 1} is the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122). The notation $\langle$1$\rangle$ ⊕ $\langle$−1$\rangle$ has been used by Milnor and Husemoller[^o7] for the hyperbolic plane as the signs of the terms of the [bivariate polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) *r* are exhibited.

The affine hyperbolic plane was described by [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) as a quadratic space with basis {*M*, *N*} satisfying *M*<sup>2</sup> = *N*<sup>2</sup> = 0, *NM* = 1, where the products represent the quadratic form.[^o4]

Through the [polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965#Symmetric_bilinear_forms) the quadratic form is related to a [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) *B*(*u*, *v*) = $\tfrac{1}{4}$(*q*(*u* + *v*) − *q*(*u* − *v*)).

Two vectors *u* and *v* are [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) when *B*(*u*, *v*) = 0. In the case of the hyperbolic plane, such *u* and *v* are [hyperbolic-orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic-orthogonal).

:::

## Split quadratic space {#Split_quadratic_space}

A space with quadratic form is **split** (or **metabolic**) if there is a subspace which is equal to its own [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833); equivalently, the index of isotropy is equal to half the dimension.[^o7] The hyperbolic plane is an example, and over a field of characteristic not equal to 2, every split space is a direct sum of hyperbolic planes.[^o7]

:::

## Relation with classification of quadratic forms {#Relation_with_classification_of_quadratic_forms}

From the point of view of classification of quadratic forms, spaces with anisotropic quadratic forms are the basic building blocks for quadratic spaces of arbitrary dimensions. For a general field *F*, classification of anisotropic quadratic forms is a nontrivial problem. By contrast, the isotropic forms are usually much easier to handle. By [Witt's decomposition theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witt's_decomposition_theorem), every [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) over a field is an [orthogonal direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_direct_sum) of a split space and a space with definite quadratic form.[^o7]

## Field theory {#Field_theory}

- If *F* is an [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed) field, for example, the field of [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers), and (*V*, *q*) is a quadratic space of dimension at least two, then it is isotropic.
- If *F* is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) and (*V*, *q*) is a quadratic space of dimension at least three, then it is isotropic (this is a consequence of the [Chevalley–Warning theorem](wikipedia://en/2934314#Statement_of_the_theorems)).
- If *F* is the field *Q*<sub>*p*</sub> of [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) and (*V*, *q*) is a quadratic space of dimension at least five, then it is isotropic.
