# LF-space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="LF-space"}

article In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an ***LF*-space**, also written **(*LF*)-space**, is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) *X* that is a locally convex [inductive limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_limit) of a countable inductive system $(X_n, i_{nm})$ of [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801).[^n1] This means that *X* is a [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of a direct system $(X_n, i_{nm})$ in the category of [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex) [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) and each $X_n$ is a Fréchet space. The name *LF* stands for **L**imit of **F**réchet spaces.

:::

If each of the bonding maps $i_{nm}$ is an embedding of TVSs then the *LF*-space is called a **strict *LF*-space**. This means that the subspace topology induced on $X_{n}$ by $X_{n+1}$ is identical to the original topology on $X_{n}$.[^n1][^o4] Some authors (e.g. Schaefer) define the term "*LF*-space" to mean "strict *LF*-space," so when reading mathematical literature, it is recommended to always check how *LF*-space is defined.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Inductive/final/direct limit topology {#Inductive/final/direct_limit_topology}

Throughout, it is assumed that

- $\mathcal{C}$ is either the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) or some subcategory of the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVSs);
  - If all objects in the category have an algebraic structure, then all morphisms are assumed to be homomorphisms for that algebraic structure.
- $I$ is a non-empty [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165);
- $X_{\bullet} = ( X_{i} )_{i \in I}$ is a family of objects in $\mathcal{C}$ where $(X_{i}, \tau_{X_{i}})$ is a topological space for every index $i$;
  - To avoid potential confusion, $\tau_{X_{i}}$ should ***not*** be called $X_{i}$'s "initial topology" since the term "[initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123)" already has a well-known definition. The topology $\tau_{X_{i}}$ is called the **original** topology on $X_{i}$ or $X_{i}$'s **given topology**.
- $X$ is a set (and if objects in $\mathcal{C}$ also have algebraic structures, then $X$ is automatically assumed to have whatever algebraic structure is needed);
- $f_{\bullet} = ( f_{i} )_{i \in I}$ is a family of maps where for each index $i$, the map has prototype $f_{i} : (X_{i}, \tau_{X_{i}}) \to X$. If all objects in the category have an algebraic structure, then these maps are also assumed to be homomorphisms for that algebraic structure.

If it exists, then the **[final topology](wikipedia://en/1819736) on $X$ in $\mathcal{C}$**, also called the **colimit** or **inductive topology** in $\mathcal{C}$, and denoted by $\tau_{f_{\bullet}}$ or $\tau_{f}$, is the [finest topology](wikipedia://en/393069) on $X$ such that

1. $(X, \tau_{f})$ is an object in $\mathcal{C}$, and
1. for every index $i$, the map $f_{i} : (X_{i}, \tau_{X_{i}}) \to (X, \tau_{f})$ is a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) morphism in $\mathcal{C}$.

In the category of topological spaces, the final topology always exists and moreover, a subset $U \subseteq X$ is open (resp. closed) in $(X, \tau_{f})$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $f_{i}^{-\,1} (U)$ is open (resp. closed) in $(X_{i}, \tau_{X_{i}})$ for every index $i$.

However, the final topology may not exist in the category of [Hausdorff topological spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) due to the requirement that $(X, \tau_{X_{f}})$ belong to the original category (i.e. belong to the category of Hausdorff topological spaces).[^n2]

### Direct systems {#Direct_systems}

Suppose that $(I, \le)$ is a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165) and that for all indices $i \le j$ there are (continuous) morphisms in $\mathcal{C}$

$$f_{i}^{j} : X_{i} \to X_{j}$$

such that if $i = j$ then $f_{i}^{j}$ is the identity map on $X_{i}$ and if $i \le j \le k$ then the following **compatibility condition** is satisfied:

$$f_{i}^{k} = f_{j}^{k} \circ f_{i}^{j},$$

where this means that the composition

$$X_{i} \xrightarrow{f_i^j} X_{j} \xrightarrow{f_j^k} X_{k} \;\;\;\; \text{ is equal to } \;\;\;\; X_{i} \xrightarrow{f_i^k} X_{k}.$$

If the above conditions are satisfied then the triple formed by the collections of these objects, morphisms, and the indexing set

$$\left( X_{\bullet}, \left \{f_{i}^{j} \;:\; i, j \in I \;\text{ and }\; i \le j \right \}, I \right)$$

is known as a **[direct system](wikipedia://en/211566)** in the category $\mathcal{C}$ that is [**directed**](wikipedia://en/9165) (or **indexed**) by $I$. Since the indexing set $I$ is a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165), the direct system is said to be **directed**.[^n3] The maps $f_{i}^{j}$ are called the **bonding**, **connecting**, or **linking** **maps** of the system.

If the indexing set $I$ is understood then $I$ is often omitted from the above tuple (i.e. not written); the same is true for the bonding maps if they are understood. Consequently, one often sees written "$X_{\bullet}$ is a direct system" where "$X_{\bullet}$" actually represents a triple with the bonding maps and indexing set either defined elsewhere (e.g. canonical bonding maps, such as natural inclusions) or else the bonding maps are merely assumed to exist but there is no need to assign symbols to them (e.g. the bonding maps are not needed to state a theorem).

### Direct limit of a direct system {#Direct_limit_of_a_direct_system}

For the construction of a direct limit of a general inductive system, please see the article: [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566).

**Direct limits of injective systems**

If each of the bonding maps $f_{i}^{j}$ is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) then the system is called **injective**.[^n3]

**Assumptions**: In the case where the direct system is injective, it is often assumed without loss of generality that for all indices $i \le j$, each $X_{i}$ is a vector subspace of $X_{j}$ (in particular, $X_{i}$ is identified with the range of $f_{i}^{j}$) and that the bonding map $f_{i}^{j}$ is the natural inclusion $\operatorname{In}^{j}_{i} : X_{i} \to X_{j}$ (i.e. defined by $x \mapsto x$) so that the subspace topology on $X_{i}$ induced by $X_{j}$ is [weaker (i.e. coarser)](wikipedia://en/393069) than the original (i.e. given) topology on $X_{i}$.

In this case, also take

$$X := i \in I X_{i}.$$

The limit maps are then the natural inclusions $\operatorname{In}_{i} : X_{i} \to X$. The direct limit topology on $X$ is the final topology induced by these inclusion maps.

If the $X_{i}$'s have an algebraic structure, say addition for example, then for any $x, y \in X$, we pick any index $i$ such that $x, y \in X_{i}$ and then define their sum using by using the addition operator of $X_{i}$. That is, $x + y := x +_{i} y$, where $+_{i}$ is the addition operator of $X_{i}$. This sum is independent of the index $i$ that is chosen.

In the category of locally convex topological vector spaces, the topology on the direct limit $X$ of an injective directed inductive limit of locally convex spaces can be described by specifying that an [absolutely convex](wikipedia://en/1734292) subset $U$ of $X$ is a neighborhood of $0$ if and only if $U \cap X_{i}$ is an absolutely convex neighborhood of $0$ in $X_{i}$ for every index $i$.[^n3]

**Direct limits in Top**

Direct limits of directed direct systems always exist in the categories of sets, topological spaces, groups, and [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) TVSs. In the category of topological spaces, if every bonding map $f_{i}^{j}$ is/is a [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_map) (resp. [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective_map), [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective_map), [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660), [topological embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding), [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29)) then so is every $f_{i} : X_{i} \to X$.[^n2]

#### Problem with direct limits {#Problem_with_direct_limits}

Direct limits in the categories of topological spaces, topological vector spaces (TVSs), and Hausdorff locally convex TVSs are "poorly behaved".[^n3] For instance, the direct limit of a sequence (i.e. indexed by the natural numbers) of locally convex [nuclear](wikipedia://en/2702957) [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801) may fail to be Hausdorff (in which case the direct limit does not exist in the category of Hausdorff TVSs). For this reason, only certain "well-behaved" direct systems are usually studied in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). Such systems include *LF*-spaces.[^n3] However, non-Hausdorff locally convex inductive limits do occur in natural questions of analysis.[^n3]

### Strict inductive limit {#Strict_inductive_limit}

If each of the bonding maps $f_{i}^{j}$ is an embedding of TVSs onto proper vector subspaces and if the system is directed by $\mathbb{N}$ with its natural ordering, then the resulting limit is called a **strict** (**countable**) **direct limit**. In such a situation we may assume without loss of generality that each $X_{i}$ is a vector subspace of $X_{i+1}$ and that the subspace topology induced on $X_{i}$ by $X_{i+1}$ is identical to the original topology on $X_{i}$.[^n1]

## Properties {#Properties}

An inductive limit in the category of locally convex TVSs of a family of [bornological](wikipedia://en/1804457) (resp. [barrelled](wikipedia://en/1733592), [quasi-barrelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-barrelled_space)) spaces has this same property.[^n4]

### LF-spaces {#LF-spaces}

Every LF-space is a [meager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meager_set) subset of itself.[^n5] The strict inductive limit of a sequence of complete locally convex spaces (such as Fréchet spaces) is necessarily complete. In particular, every LF-space is complete.[^n6] Every *LF*-space is [barrelled](wikipedia://en/1733592) and [bornological](wikipedia://en/1804457), which together with completeness implies that every LF-space is [ultrabornological](wikipedia://en/63904912). An LF-space that is the inductive limit of a countable sequence of separable spaces is separable.[^n7] [LF spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LF_space) are [distinguished](wikipedia://en/64266856) and their strong duals are [bornological](wikipedia://en/1804457) and [barrelled](wikipedia://en/1733592) (a result due to [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042)).

If $X$ is the strict inductive limit of an increasing sequence of [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) $X_{n}$ then a subset $B$ of $X$ is bounded in $X$ if and only if there exists some $n$ such that $B$ is a bounded subset of $X_{n}$.[^n6]

A linear map from an LF-space into another TVS is continuous if and only if it is [sequentially continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequentially_continuous).[^n8] A linear map from an LF-space $X$ into a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) $Y$ is continuous if and only if its graph is closed in $X \times Y$.[^n9] Every [bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_map) linear operator from an LF-space into another TVS is continuous.[^n10]

If $X$ is an LF-space defined by a sequence $\left( X_i \right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ then the strong dual space $X^{\prime}_{b}$ of $X$ is a Fréchet space if and only if all $X_{i}$ are [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable).[^n11] Thus the strong dual space of an LF-space is a Fréchet space if and only if it is an [LB-space](wikipedia://en/63848931).

## Examples {#Examples}

### Space of smooth compactly supported functions {#Space_of_smooth_compactly_supported_functions}

A typical example of an *LF*-space is, $C^\infty_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$, the space of all infinitely differentiable functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with compact support. The *LF*-space structure is obtained by considering a sequence of compact sets $K_1 \subset K_2 \subset \ldots \subset K_i \subset \ldots \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ with $\bigcup_i K_i = \mathbb{R}^n$ and for all i, $K_i$ is a subset of the interior of $K_{i+1}$. Such a sequence could be the balls of radius *i* centered at the origin. The space $C_c^\infty(K_i)$ of infinitely differentiable functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with compact support contained in $K_i$ has a natural [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) structure and $C^\infty_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$ inherits its *LF*-space structure as described above. The *LF*-space topology does not depend on the particular sequence of compact sets $K_i$.

With this *LF*-space structure, $C^\infty_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is known as the space of test functions, of fundamental importance in the [theory of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).

### Direct limit of finite-dimensional spaces {#Direct_limit_of_finite-dimensional_spaces}

Suppose that for every positive integer $n$, $X_{n} := \mathbb{R}^{n}$ and for $m < n$, consider *X*<sub>*m*</sub> as a vector subspace of $X_{n}$ via the canonical embedding $X_{m} \to X_{n}$ defined by $x := (x_{1}, ..., x_{m}) \mapsto (x_{1}, ..., x_{m}, 0, ..., 0)$. Denote the resulting LF-space by $X$. Since any TVS topology on $X$ makes continuous the inclusions of the *X*<sub>*m*</sub>'s into $X$, the latter space has the maximum among all TVS topologies on an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space with countable [Hamel dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_dimension). It is a LC topology, associated with the family of all seminorms on $X$. Also, the TVS inductive limit topology of $X$ coincides with the topological inductive limit; that is, the direct limit of the finite dimensional spaces $X_{n}$ in the category TOP and in the category TVS coincide. The continuous dual space $X^{\prime}$ of $X$ is equal to the [algebraic dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_dual_space) of $X$, that is the space of all real valued sequences $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ and the weak topology on $X^{\prime}$ is equal to the [strong topology](wikipedia://en/589726) on $X^{\prime}$ (i.e. $X^{\prime}_{\sigma} = X^{\prime}_{b}$).[^n12] In fact, it is the unique LC topology on $X^{\prime}$ whose topological dual space is X.
