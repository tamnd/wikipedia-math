# Radial basis function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Radial basis function"}

In mathematics a **radial basis function** (**RBF**) is a [real-valued function](wikipedia://en/22284647) $\varphi$ whose value depends only on the distance between the input and some [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738), either the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432), so that $\varphi(\mathbf{x}) = \hat\varphi(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)$, or some other fixed point $\mathbf{c}$, called a *center*, so that $\varphi(\mathbf{x}) = \hat\varphi(\left\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}\right\|)$. Any function $\varphi$ that satisfies the property $\varphi(\mathbf{x}) = \hat\varphi(\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|)$ is a [radial function](wikipedia://en/25242779). The distance is usually [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932), although other [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_function) are sometimes used. They are often used as a collection $\{ \varphi_k \}_k$ which forms a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for some [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) of interest, hence the name.

:::

Sums of radial basis functions are typically used to [approximate given functions](wikipedia://en/336897). This approximation process can also be interpreted as a simple kind of [neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network); this was the context in which they were originally applied to [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), in work by [David Broomhead](wikipedia://en/43419676) and David Lowe in 1988,[^o2][^o3] which stemmed from [Michael J. D. Powell](wikipedia://en/16082618)'s seminal research from 1977.[^o4][^o5][^o6] RBFs are also used as a [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_basis_function_kernel) in [support vector classification](wikipedia://en/65309).[^o7] The technique has proven effective and flexible enough that radial basis functions are now applied in a variety of engineering applications.[^o8][^o9]

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A radial function is a function $\varphi:[0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$. When paired with a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\|\cdot\|:V \to [0,\infty)$ on a vector space, a function of the form $\varphi_\mathbf{c} = \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}\|)$ is said to be a **radial kernel** centered at $\mathbf{c} \in V$. A radial function and the associated radial kernels are said to be radial basis functions if, for any [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of nodes $\{\mathbf{x}_k\}_{k=1}^n \subseteq V$, all of the following conditions are true: The kernels $\varphi_{\mathbf{x}_1}, \varphi_{\mathbf{x}_2}, \dots, \varphi_{\mathbf{x}_n}$ are linearly independent (for example $\varphi(r)=r^2$ in $V=\mathbb{R}$ is not a radial basis function); The kernels $\varphi_{\mathbf{x}_1}, \varphi_{\mathbf{x}_2}, \dots, \varphi_{\mathbf{x}_n}$ form a basis for a [Haar Space](wikipedia://en/51716359), meaning that the [interpolation matrix](wikipedia://en/60415297) (given below) is non-[singular](wikipedia://en/245528).[^o12][^o13]

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{bmatrix} \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_1\|) & \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_1\|) & \dots & \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{x}_1\|) \\ \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2\|) & \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_2\|) & \dots & \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{x}_2\|) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_n\|) & \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_n\|) & \dots & \varphi(\|\mathbf{x}_n - \mathbf{x}_n\|) \\ \end{bmatrix} \tag{1}$$

:::

## Approximation {#Approximation}

Radial basis functions are typically used to build up [function approximations](wikipedia://en/336897) of the form

::: {#o29 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \, \varphi(\left\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i\right\|), \tag{10}$$

:::

where the approximating function $y(\mathbf{x})$ is represented as a sum of $N$ radial basis functions, each associated with a different center $\mathbf{x}_i$, and weighted by an appropriate coefficient $w_i.$ The weights $w_i$ can be estimated using the matrix methods of [linear least squares](wikipedia://en/2407860), because the approximating function is *linear* in the weights $w_i$.

Approximation schemes of this kind have been particularly used in [time series prediction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series_prediction) and [control](wikipedia://en/7039) of [nonlinear systems](wikipedia://en/146103) exhibiting sufficiently simple [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295) behaviour and 3D reconstruction in [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics) (for example, [hierarchical RBF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchical_RBF) and [Pose Space Deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pose_Space_Deformation)).

## RBF Network {#RBF_Network}

*Image: Unnormalized radial basis functions.svg.* Two unnormalized Gaussian radial basis functions in one input dimension. The basis function centers are located at $x_1 = 0.75$ and $x_2 = 3.25$.

The sum

::: {#o31 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$y(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \, \varphi(\left\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i\right\|), \tag{11}$$

:::

can also be interpreted as a rather simple single-layer type of [artificial neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network) called a [radial basis function network](wikipedia://en/9651443), with the radial basis functions taking on the role of the activation functions of the network. It can be shown that any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) on a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) interval can in principle be interpolated with arbitrary accuracy by a sum of this form, if a sufficiently large number $N$ of radial basis functions is used.

The approximant $y(\mathbf{x})$ is differentiable with respect to the weights $w_i$. The weights could thus be learned using any of the standard iterative methods for neural networks.

Using radial basis functions in this manner yields a reasonable interpolation approach provided that the fitting set has been chosen such that it covers the entire range systematically (equidistant data points are ideal). However, without a polynomial term that is orthogonal to the radial basis functions, estimates outside the fitting set tend to perform poorly.

## RBFs for PDEs {#RBFs_for_PDEs}

Radial basis functions are used to approximate functions and so can be used to discretize and numerically solve Partial Differential Equations (PDEs). This was first done in 1990 by E. J. Kansa who developed the first RBF based numerical method. It is called the [Kansa method](wikipedia://en/35705190) and was used to solve the elliptic [Poisson equation](wikipedia://en/193735) and the linear [advection-diffusion equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advection-diffusion_equation). The function values at points $\mathbf{x}$ in the domain are approximated by the linear combination of RBFs:

::: {#o33 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$u(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i \, \varphi(\left\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i\right\|),\quad \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d \tag{12}$$

:::

The derivatives are approximated as such:

::: {#o34 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$\frac{\partial^n u(\textbf{x})}{\partial x^n} = \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i \, \frac{\partial^n}{\partial x^n}\varphi(\left\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i\right\|),\quad \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d \tag{13}$$

:::

where $N$ are the number of points in the discretized domain, $d$ the dimension of the domain and $\lambda$ the scalar coefficients that are unchanged by the differential operator.[^o35]

Different numerical methods based on Radial Basis Functions were developed thereafter. Some methods are the RBF-FD method,[^o36][^o37] the RBF-QR method[^o38] and the RBF-PUM method.[^o39]
