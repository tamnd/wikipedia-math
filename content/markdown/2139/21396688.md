# Unisolvent functions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unisolvent functions"}

In mathematics, a set of *n* [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) *f*<sub>1</sub>, *f*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*n*</sub> is **unisolvent** (meaning "uniquely solvable") on a [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) Ω if the [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28mathematics%29)

:::

$$\begin{bmatrix}f_1(x_1) \\ f_1(x_2) \\ \vdots \\ f_1(x_n)\end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix}f_2(x_1) \\ f_2(x_2) \\ \vdots \\ f_2(x_n)\end{bmatrix}, \dots, \begin{bmatrix}f_n(x_1) \\ f_n(x_2) \\ \vdots \\ f_n(x_n)\end{bmatrix}$$

are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) for any choice of *n* distinct points *x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub> ... *x*<sub>*n*</sub> in Ω. Equivalently, the collection is unisolvent if the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) *F* with entries *f*<sub>*i*</sub>(*x*<sub>*j*</sub>) has a nonzero [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468): det(*F*) ≠ 0 for any choice of distinct *x*<sub>*j*</sub>'s in Ω. Unisolvency is a property of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), not just particular sets of functions. That is, a vector space of functions of dimension *n* is unisolvent if given any [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) (equivalently, a linearly independent set of *n* functions), the basis is unisolvent (as a set of functions). This is because any two bases are related by an [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122) (the change of basis matrix), so one basis is unisolvent [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) any other basis is unisolvent.

Unisolvent systems of functions are widely used in [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) since they guarantee a unique solution to the interpolation problem. The set of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) of degree at most $d$ (which form a vector space of dimension $d + 1$) are unisolvent by the [unisolvence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unisolvence_theorem).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- 1, *x*, *x*<sup>2</sup> is unisolvent on any interval by the unisolvence theorem
- 1, *x*<sup>2</sup> is unisolvent on \[0, 1\], but not unisolvent on \[−1, 1\]
- 1, cos(*x*), cos(2*x*), ..., cos(*nx*), sin(*x*), sin(2*x*), ..., sin(*nx*) is unisolvent on \[−*π*, *π*\]
- Unisolvent functions are used in [linear inverse problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_inverse_problem).

:::

## Unisolvence in the finite element method {#Unisolvence_in_the_finite_element_method}

When using "simple" functions to approximate an unknown function, such as in the [finite element method](wikipedia://en/18233581), it is useful to consider a set of functionals $\{f_i\}_{i=1}^n$ that act on a finite dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V_h$ of functions, usually polynomials. Often, the functionals are given by evaluation at points in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) or some subset of it.[^o5][^o6]

For example, let

$$V_h = \left\{ p(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n p_k x^k \right\}$$

be the space of univariate polynomials of degree $n$ or less, and let $f_k(p) := f\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\right)$ for $0\leq k \leq n$ be defined by evaluation at $n+1$ equidistant points on the unit interval $[0,1]$. In this context, the unisolvence of $V_h$ with respect to $\{f_k\}_{k=1}^n$ means that $\{f_k\}_{k=1}^n$ is a basis for $V_h^*$, the dual space of $V_h$. Equivalently, and perhaps more intuitively, unisolvence here means that given any set of values $\{c_k\}_{k=1}^n$, there exists a unique polynomial $q(x) \in V_h$ such that $f_k(q) = q( \tfrac{k}{n} ) = c_k$. Results of this type are widely applied in [polynomial interpolation](wikipedia://en/203565); given any function on $\phi \in C([0,1])$, by letting $c_k = \phi( \tfrac{k}{n})$, we can find a polynomial $q\in V_h$ that interpolates $\phi$ at each of the $n+1$ points: . $\phi(\tfrac{k}{n}) = q(\tfrac{k}{n}), \ \forall k \in \{0,1,..,n\}$

## Dimensions {#Dimensions}

Systems of unisolvent functions are much more common in 1 dimension than in higher dimensions. In dimension *d* = 2 and higher (Ω ⊂ **R**<sup>*d*</sup>), the functions *f*<sub>1</sub>, *f*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*n*</sub> cannot be unisolvent on Ω if there exists a single [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) on which they are all continuous. To see this, consider moving points *x*<sub>1</sub> and *x*<sub>2</sub> along continuous paths in the open set until they have switched positions, such that *x*<sub>1</sub> and *x*<sub>2</sub> never intersect each other or any of the other *x*<sub>*i*</sub>. The determinant of the resulting system (with *x*<sub>1</sub> and *x*<sub>2</sub> swapped) is the negative of the determinant of the initial system. Since the functions *f*<sub>*i*</sub> are continuous, the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884) implies that some intermediate configuration has determinant zero, hence the functions cannot be unisolvent.
