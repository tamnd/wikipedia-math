# Symmetric bilinear form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symmetric bilinear form"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **symmetric bilinear form** on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) is a [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365) from two copies of the vector space to the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) such that the order of the two vectors does not affect the value of the map. In other words, it is a [bilinear](wikipedia://en/449477) function $B$ that maps every pair $(u,v)$ of elements of the vector space $V$ to the underlying field such that $B(u,v)=B(v,u)$ for every $u$ and $v$ in $V$. They are also referred to more briefly as just **symmetric forms** when "bilinear" is understood.

:::

Symmetric bilinear forms on finite-dimensional vector spaces precisely correspond to [symmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/126474) given a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for $V$. Among bilinear forms, the symmetric ones are important because they are the ones for which the vector space admits a particularly simple kind of basis known as an [orthogonal basis](wikipedia://en/755400) (at least when the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of the field is not 2).

Given a symmetric bilinear form $B$, the function $q(x)=B(x,x)$ is the associated [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) on the vector space. Moreover, if the characteristic of the field is not 2, $B$ is the unique symmetric bilinear form associated with $q$.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let *V* be a vector space of dimension *n* over a field *K*. A [map](wikipedia://en/185427) $B : V\times V\rightarrow K$ is a symmetric bilinear form on the space if:

- $$B(u,v)=B(v,u)\ \quad \forall u,v \in V$$
- $$B(u+v,w)=B(u,w)+B(v,w)\ \quad \forall u,v,w \in V$$
- $$B(\lambda v,w)=\lambda B(v,w)\ \quad \forall \lambda \in K,\forall v,w \in V$$

The last two axioms only establish linearity in the first argument, but the first axiom (symmetry) then immediately implies linearity in the second argument as well.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Let $V=\mathbb{R}^n$, the $n$ dimensional [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083). Then the standard [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) is a symmetric bilinear form, $B(x,y)=x\cdot y$. The matrix corresponding to this bilinear form (see below) on a [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

Let $V$ be any vector space (including possibly infinite-dimensional), and assume $T$ is a linear function from $V$ to the field (a [linear form](wikipedia://en/214137)). Then the function defined by $B(x,y)=T(x)T(y)$ is a symmetric bilinear form.

Let $V$ be the vector space of continuous single-variable real functions. For $f,g \in V$ one can define $\textstyle B(f,g)=\int_0^1 f(t)g(t) dt$. By the properties of [definite integrals](wikipedia://en/15532), this defines a symmetric bilinear form on $V$. This is an example of a symmetric bilinear form which is not associated to any symmetric matrix (since the vector space is infinite-dimensional).

:::

## Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

Let $\mathcal{C}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}$ be a basis for $V$. Define the $n\times n$ matrix $A$ by $A_{ij}=B(e_{i},e_{j})$. The matrix $A$ is a [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) exactly due to symmetry of the bilinear form. If we let the $n\times 1$ matrix $x$ represent the vector $v$ with respect to this basis, and similarly let the $n\times 1$ matrix $y$ represent the vector $w$, then $B(v,w)$ is given by:

$$x^\mathsf{T} A y=y^\mathsf{T} A x.$$

Suppose $\mathcal{C}'$ is another basis for $V$, with $\begin{bmatrix}e'_{1} & \cdots & e'_{n}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}e_{1} & \cdots & e_{n}\end{bmatrix}S$, where $S$ is an invertible $n\times n$ matrix. Then the new matrix representation for the symmetric bilinear form is given by

$$A' =S^\mathsf{T} A S .$$

## Orthogonality and singularity {#Orthogonality_and_singularity}

Two vectors $v$ and $w$ are defined to be orthogonal with respect to the bilinear form $B$ if $B(v,w)=0$, which, for a symmetric bilinear form, is equivalent to $B(w,v)=0$.

The **radical** of a bilinear form $B$ is the set of vectors orthogonal with every vector in $V$. That this is a subspace of $V$ follows from the linearity of $B$ in each of its arguments. When working with a matrix representation $A$ with respect to a certain basis, $v$, represented by $x$, is in the radical if and only if

$$A x = 0 \Longleftrightarrow x^\mathsf{T} A = 0 .$$

The matrix $A$ is singular [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the radical is nontrivial.

If $W$ is a subset of $V$, then its *[orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833)* $W^{\perp}$ is the set of all vectors in $V$ that are orthogonal to every vector in $W$; it is a subspace of $V$. When $B$ is non-degenerate, the radical of $B$ is trivial and the dimension of $W^{\perp}$ is $\dim(W^{\perp})=\dim(V)-\dim(W)$.

## Orthogonal basis {#Orthogonal_basis}

A basis $C=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}$ is orthogonal with respect to *B* if and only if:

$$B(e_{i},e_{j}) = 0\ \forall i \neq j.$$

When the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of the field is not two, *V* always has an orthogonal basis. This can be proven by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881).

A basis *C* is orthogonal if and only if the matrix representation *A* is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080).

::: {#o10 .statement cue=named-heading name="Signature and Sylvester's law of inertia"}

### Signature and Sylvester's law of inertia {#Signature_and_Sylvester's_law_of_inertia}

In a more general form, [Sylvester's law of inertia](wikipedia://en/1127460) says that, when working over an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430), the numbers of diagonal elements in the diagonalized form of a matrix that are positive, negative and zero respectively are independent of the chosen orthogonal basis. These three numbers form the *[signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28quadratic_form%29)* of the bilinear form.

:::

### Real case {#Real_case}

When working in a space over the reals, one can go a bit a further. Let $C=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}$ be an orthogonal basis.

We define a new basis $C'=\{e'_1,\ldots,e'_n\}$

$$e'_i = \begin{cases} e_i & \text{if } B(e_i,e_i)=0 \\ \frac{e_i}{\sqrt{B(e_i,e_i)}} & \text{if } B(e_i,e_i) >0\\ \frac{e_i}{\sqrt{-B(e_i,e_i)}}& \text{if } B(e_i,e_i) <0 \end{cases}$$

Now, the new matrix representation *A* will be a diagonal matrix with only 0, 1 and −1 on the diagonal. Zeroes will appear if and only if the radical is nontrivial.

### Complex case {#Complex_case}

When working in a space over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), one can go further as well and it is even easier. Let $C=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$ be an orthogonal basis.

We define a new basis $C'=\{e'_1,\ldots,e'_n\}$ :

$$e'_i = \begin{cases} e_i & \text{if }\; B(e_i,e_i)=0 \\ e_i/\sqrt{B(e_i,e_i)} & \text{if }\; B(e_i,e_i) \neq 0\\ \end{cases}$$

Now the new matrix representation *A* will be a diagonal matrix with only 0 and 1 on the diagonal. Zeroes will appear if and only if the radical is nontrivial.

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Orthogonal polarity"}

## Orthogonal polarities {#Orthogonal_polarities}

Let *B* be a symmetric bilinear form with a trivial radical on the space *V* over the field *K* with [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) not 2. One can now define a map from D(*V*), the set of all subspaces of *V*, to itself:

$$\alpha:D(V)\rightarrow D(V) :W\mapsto W^{\perp}.$$

This map is an **orthogonal polarity** on the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) PG(*W*). Conversely, one can prove all orthogonal polarities are induced in this way, and that two symmetric bilinear forms with trivial radical induce the same polarity if and only if they are equal up to [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373).

:::
