# Mathematical structure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematical structure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **structure** on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) (or on some sets) refers to providing or endowing it (or them) with certain additional features (e.g. an [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245), [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558), [metric](wikipedia://en/20018), or [topology](wikipedia://en/30450)). Τhe additional features are attached or related to the set (or to the sets), so as to provide it (or them) with some additional meaning or significance.

:::

A partial list of possible structures is [measures](wikipedia://en/19873), [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) ([groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), etc.), [topologies](wikipedia://en/30450), [metric structures](wikipedia://en/20018) ([geometries](wikipedia://en/18973446)), [orders](wikipedia://en/361924), [graphs](wikipedia://en/12401), [events](wikipedia://en/34812675), [differential structures](wikipedia://en/725272), [categories](wikipedia://en/53759), [setoids](wikipedia://en/769434), and [equivalence relations](wikipedia://en/9259).

Sometimes, a set is endowed with more than one feature simultaneously, which allows to study the interaction between the different structures more richly. For example, an ordering imposes a rigid form, shape, or topology on a set, and if a set has both a topology feature and a group feature, such that these two features are compatible in a certain way, then the structure becomes a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315).[^o2]

A [map](wikipedia://en/516931) between two similarly-structured sets that preserves their structure is known as a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891), and such maps are of special interest in many fields of mathematics. Examples include [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564), which preserve [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364); [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122), which preserve topological structures; and [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206), which preserve differential structures. Morphisms that can be inverted, known as [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828), allow to describe when sets endowed with the same kind of structure have the same properties from the point of view of that structure, in which case they are called isomorphic.

## History {#History}

In 1939, the French group with the pseudonym "[Nicolas Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394)" saw structures as the root of mathematics. They first mentioned them in their "Fascicule" of *Theory of Sets* and expanded it into Chapter IV of the 1957 edition.[^o4] They identified **three *mother structures*: algebraic, topological, and order**.[^o4][^o5]

## Example: the real numbers {#Example:_the_real_numbers}

The set of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) has several standard structures:

- An order: each number is either less than or greater than any other number.
- Algebraic structure: there are operations of addition and multiplication, the first of which makes it into a [group](wikipedia://en/41890) and the pair of which together make it into a [field](wikipedia://en/10603).
- A measure: [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172) of the real line have a specific [length](wikipedia://en/18542), which can be extended to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on many of its [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631).
- A metric: there is a notion of [distance](wikipedia://en/20018) between points.
- A geometry: it is equipped with a [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) and is [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_space).
- A topology: there is a notion of [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358).

There are interfaces among these:

- Its order and, independently, its metric structure induce its topology.
- Its order and algebraic structure make it into an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430).
- Its algebraic structure and topology make it into a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), a type of [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315).
