# Bapat–Beg theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bapat–Beg theorem"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **Bapat–Beg theorem** gives the [joint probability distribution](wikipedia://en/879637) of [order statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_statistics) of [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_%28probability%29) but not necessarily [identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identically_distributed) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) in terms of the [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) of the random variables. [Ravindra Bapat](wikipedia://en/52284032) and M.I. Beg published the theorem in 1989,[^o2] though they did not offer a proof. A simple proof was offered by Hande in 1994.[^o3]

:::

Often, all elements of the [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) are obtained from the same population and thus have the same [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). The Bapat–Beg theorem describes the order statistics when each element of the sample is obtained from a different [statistical population](wikipedia://en/27585) and therefore has its own [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543).[^o2]

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $X_1,X_2,\ldots, X_n$ be independent real valued random variables with [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) respectively $F_1(x),F_2(x),\ldots,F_n(x)$. Write $X_{(1)},X_{(2)},\ldots, X_{(n)}$ for the order statistics. Then the joint probability distribution of the $n_1, n_2\ldots, n_k$ order statistics (with $n_1<n_2<\cdots < n_k$ and $x_1<x_2<\cdots< x_k$) is

$$\begin{aligned} F_{X_{(n_1)},\ldots, X_{(n_k)}}(x_1,\ldots,x_k) & = \Pr ( X_{(n_1)}\leq x_1 \land X_{(n_2)}\leq x_2 \land\cdots\land X_{(n_k)} \leq x_k) \\ & = \sum_{i_k=n_k}^n \cdots\sum_{i_2=n_2}^{i_3} \sum _{i_1=n_1}^{i_2}\frac{P_{i_1,\ldots,i_k} (x_1,\ldots ,x_k)}{i_1! (i_2-i_1)! \cdots (n-i_k)!}, \end{aligned}$$

where

$$\begin{aligned} P_{i_1,\ldots,i_k}(x_1,\ldots,x_k) = \operatorname{per} \begin{bmatrix} F_1(x_1) \cdots F_1(x_1) & F_1(x_2)-F_1(x_1) \cdots F_1(x_2)-F_1(x_1) & \cdots & 1-F_1(x_k) \cdots 1-F_1(x_k) \\ F_2(x_1) \cdots F_2(x_1) & F_2(x_2)-F_2(x_1) \cdots F_2(x_2)-F_2(x_1) & \cdots & 1-F_2(x_k) \cdots 1-F_1(x_k )\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ \underbrace{F_n(x_1) \cdots F_n(x_1) }_{i_1} & \underbrace{F_n(x_2)-F_n(x_1) \cdots F_n(x_2)-F_n(x_1)}_{i_2-i_1} & \cdots & \underbrace{1-F_n(x_k) \cdots 1-F_n(x_k) }_{n-i_k} \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned}$$

is the [permanent](wikipedia://en/215889) of the given [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464). (The figures under the braces show the number of columns.)[^o2]

### Independent identically distributed case {#Independent_identically_distributed_case}

In the case when the variables $X_1,X_2,\ldots, X_n$ are [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed) with [cumulative probability distribution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulative_probability_distribution_function) $F_i=F$ for all *i* the theorem reduces to

$$\begin{aligned} F_{X_{(n_1)},\ldots, X_{(n_k)}}(x_1,\ldots,x_k) = \sum_{i_k=n_k}^n \cdots \sum_{i_2=n_2}^{i_3} \sum_{i_1=n_1}^{i_2} n! \frac{F(x_1)^{i_1}}{i_1!} \frac{(1-F(x_k))^{n-i_k}}{(n-i_k)!} \prod\limits_{j=2}^k \frac{\left[ F(x_j) -F(x_{j-1}) \right]^{i_j-i_{j-1}} }{(i_j-i_{j-1})!}. \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Remarks {#Remarks}

- No assumption of continuity of the cumulative distribution functions is needed.[^o3]
- If the inequalities *x*<sub>1</sub> \< *x*<sub>2</sub> \< ... \< *x*<sub>*k*</sub> are not imposed, some of the inequalities "may be redundant and the probability can be evaluated after making the necessary reduction."[^o2]

## Complexity {#Complexity}

Glueck and co-authors note that the Bapat‒Beg formula is computationally intractable, because it involves an exponential number of permanents of the size of the number of random variables.[^o9] However, when the random variables have only two possible distributions, the complexity can be reduced to $O(m^{2k})$.[^o9] Thus, in the case of two populations, the complexity is polynomial in $m$ for any fixed number of statistics $k$.
