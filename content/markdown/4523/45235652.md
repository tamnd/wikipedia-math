# Straight-line program

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Straight-line program"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), a **straight-line program** is, informally, a [program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program) that does not contain any loop or any test, and is formed by a sequence of steps that apply each an [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) to previously computed elements.

:::

This article is devoted to the case where the allowed operations are the operations of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), that is multiplication and inversion. More specifically a **straight-line program** (**SLP**) for a finite group *G* = ⟨*S*⟩ is a finite sequence *L* of elements of *G* such that every element of *L* either belongs to *S*, is the inverse of a preceding element, or the product of two preceding elements. An SLP *L* is said to *compute* a group element *g* ∈ *G* if *g* ∈ *L*, where *g* is encoded by a word in *S* and its inverses.

Intuitively, an SLP computing some *g* ∈ *G* is an *efficient* way of storing *g* as a group word over *S*; observe that if *g* is constructed in *i* steps, the word length of *g* may be exponential in *i*, but the length of the corresponding SLP is linear in *i*. This has important applications in [computational group theory](wikipedia://en/618584), by using SLPs to efficiently encode group elements as words over a given generating set.

Straight-line programs were introduced by Babai and Szemerédi in 1984[^o2] as a tool for studying the computational complexity of certain matrix group properties. Babai and Szemerédi prove that every element of a finite group *G* has an SLP of length *O*(log<sup>2</sup>|*G*|) in every generating set.

An efficient solution to the *constructive membership problem* is crucial to many group-theoretic algorithms. It can be stated in terms of SLPs as follows. Given a finite group *G* = ⟨*S*⟩ and *g* ∈ *G*, find a straight-line program computing *g* over *S*. The constructive membership problem is often studied in the setting of [black box groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_box_group). The elements are encoded by bit strings of a fixed length. Three *oracles* are provided for the group-theoretic functions of multiplication, inversion, and checking for equality with the identity. A *black box algorithm* is one which uses only these oracles. Hence, straight-line programs for black box groups are black box algorithms.

Explicit straight-line programs are given for a wealth of finite simple groups in the online [ATLAS of Finite Groups](wikipedia://en/22659407).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Informal definition {#Informal_definition}

Let *G* be a finite group and let *S* be a subset of *G*. A sequence *L* = (*g*<sub>1</sub>,...,*g*<sub>*m*</sub>) of elements of *G* is a *straight-line program* over *S* if each *g*<sub>*i*</sub> can be obtained by one of the following three rules:

1. *g*<sub>*i*</sub> ∈ *S*
1. *g*<sub>*i*</sub> = *g*<sub>*j*</sub> $\cdot$ *g*<sub>*k*</sub> for some *j*,*k* \< *i*
1. *g*<sub>*i*</sub> = *g*${}^{-1}_{j}$ for some *j* \< *i*.

The straight-line *cost* *c*(*g*|*S*) of an element *g* ∈ *G* is the length of a shortest straight-line program over *S* computing *g*. The cost is infinite if *g* is not in the subgroup generated by *S*.

A straight-line program is similar to a derivation in predicate logic. The elements of *S* correspond to axioms and the group operations correspond to the rules of inference.

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

### Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let *G* be a finite group and let *S* be a subset of *G*. A *straight-line program* of length *m* over *S* computing some *g* ∈ *G* is a sequence of expressions (*w*<sub>1</sub>,...,*w*<sub>*m*</sub>) such that for each *i*, *w*<sub>*i*</sub> is a symbol for some element of *S*, or *w*<sub>*i*</sub> = (*w*<sub>*j*</sub>,-1) for some *j* \< *i*, or *w*<sub>*i*</sub> = (*w*<sub>*j*</sub>,*w*<sub>*k*</sub>) for some *j*,*k* \< *i*, such that *w*<sub>*m*</sub> takes upon the value *g* when evaluated in *G* in the obvious manner.

The original definition appearing in [^o8] requires that *G* =⟨*S*⟩. The definition presented above is a common generalisation of this.

From a computational perspective, the formal definition of a straight-line program has some advantages. Firstly, a sequence of abstract expressions requires less memory than terms over the generating set. Secondly, it allows straight-line programs to be constructed in one representation of *G* and evaluated in another. This is an important feature of some algorithms.[^o8]

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

*Short descriptions of finite groups*. Straight-line programs can be used to study compression of finite groups via [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983). They provide a tool to construct "short" sentences describing *G* (i.e. much shorter than |*G*|). In more detail, SLPs are used to prove that every finite [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) has a first-order description of length *O*(log|*G*|), and every finite group *G* has a first-order description of length *O*(log<sup>3</sup>|*G*|).[^o13]

*Straight-line programs computing generating sets for maximal subgroups of finite simple groups*. The online ATLAS of Finite Group Representations[^o14] provides abstract straight-line programs for computing generating sets of maximal subgroups for many finite simple groups.

## Reachability theorem {#Reachability_theorem}

The reachability theorem states that, given a finite group *G* generated by *S*, each *g* ∈ *G* has a maximum cost of $(1 + \lg G)^{2}$. This can be understood as a bound on how hard it is to generate a group element from the generators.

Here the function lg(*x*) is an integer-valued version of the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) function: for *k*≥1 let lg(*k*) = max{*r* : 2<sup>r</sup> ≤ *k*}.

The idea of the proof is to construct a set *Z* = {*z*<sub>1</sub>,...,*z*<sub>*s*</sub>} that will work as a new generating set (*s* will be defined during the process). It is usually larger than *S*, but any element of *G* can be expressed as a word of length at most $2Z$ over *Z*. The set *Z* is constructed by inductively defining an increasing sequence of sets *K*(*i*).

Let *K*(*i*) = {*z*<sub>1</sub><sup>*α*<sub>1</sub></sup>·*z*<sub>2</sub><sup>*α*<sub>2</sub></sup>·...·*z*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>*α*<sub>*i*</sub></sup> : *α*<sub>*j*</sub> ∈ {0,1}}, where *z*<sub>*i*</sub> is the group element added to *Z* at the *i*-th step. Let *c*(*i*) denote the length of a shortest straight-line program that contains *Z*(*i*) = {*z*<sub>1</sub>,...,*z*<sub>*i*</sub>}. Let *K*(0) = {1<sub>*G*</sub>} and *c*(0)=0. We define the set *Z* recursively:

- If *K*(*i*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*i*) = *G*, declare *s* to take upon the value *i* and stop.
- Else, choose some *z*<sub>*i*+1</sub> ∈ *G*\\*K*(*i*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*i*) (which is non-empty) that minimises the "cost increase" *c*(*i*+1) − *c*(*i*).

By this process, *Z* is defined in a way so that any *g* ∈ *G* can be written as an element of *K*(*i*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*i*), effectively making it easier to generate from *Z*.

We now need to verify the following claim to ensure that the process terminates within lg(|*G*|) many steps:

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/claim cue=math-theorem name="Claim 1"}

**Claim 1.**

If *i* \< *s* then $K(i+1) = 2K(i)$.

:::

::: {#o19 .proof cue=math-proof}

It is immediate that $K(i+1) \le 2K(i)$. Now suppose for a contradiction that $K(i+1) < 2K(i)$. By the pigeonhole principle there are *k*<sub>1</sub>,*k*<sub>2</sub> ∈ *K*(*i*+1) with *k*<sub>1</sub> = *z*<sub>1</sub><sup>*α*<sub>1</sub></sup>·*z*<sub>2</sub><sup>*α*<sub>2</sub></sup>·...·*z*<sub>*i*+1</sub><sup>*α*<sub>*i*+1</sub></sup> = *z*<sub>1</sub><sup>*β*<sub>1</sub></sup>·*z*<sub>2</sub><sup>*β*<sub>2</sub></sup>·...·*z*<sub>*i*+1</sub><sup>*β*<sub>*i*+1</sub></sup> = *k*<sub>2</sub> for some *α*<sub>*j*</sub>,*β*<sub>*j*</sub> ∈ {0,1}. Let *r* be the largest integer such that *α*<sub>*r*</sub> ≠ *β*<sub>*r*</sub>. Assume WLOG that *α*<sub>*r*</sub> = 1. It follows that *z*<sub>*r*</sub> = *z*<sub>*p*</sub><sup>−*α*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>·*z*<sub>*p*-1</sub><sup>−*α*<sub>*p*-1</sub></sup>·...·*z*<sub>1</sub><sup>−*α*<sub>1</sub></sup>·*z*<sub>1</sub><sup>*β*<sub>1</sub></sup>·*z*<sub>2</sub><sup>*β*<sub>2</sub></sup>·...·*z*<sub>*q*</sub><sup>*β*<sub>*q*</sub></sup>, with *p*,*q* \< *r*. Hence *z*<sub>*r*</sub> ∈ *K*(*r*−1)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*r* − 1), a contradiction.

:::

The next claim is used to show that the cost of every group element is within the required bound.

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/claim cue=math-theorem name="Claim 2"}

**Claim 2.**

$$c(i) \le i^{2} - i.$$

:::

::: {#o21 .proof cue=math-proof}

Since *c*(0)=0 it suffices to show that *c*(*i*+1) - *c*(*i*) ≤ 2*i*. The [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of *G* is connected and if *i* \< *s*, *K*(*i*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*i*) ≠ *G*, then there is an element of the form $g_{1}\cdot g_{2} \in G \backslash K(i)^{-1}K(i)$ with *g*<sub>1</sub> ∈ *K*(*i*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*i*) and *g*<sub>2</sub> ∈ *S*.

:::

It takes at most 2*i* steps to generate *g*<sub>1</sub> ∈ *K*(*i*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*i*). There is no point in generating the element of maximum length, since it is the identity. Hence $2i -1$ steps suffice. To generate *g*<sub>1</sub>·*g*<sub>2</sub> ∈ *G*\\*K*(*i*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*i*), 2*i* steps are sufficient.

We now finish the theorem. Since *K*(*s*)<sup>−1</sup>*K*(*s*) = *G*, any *g* ∈ *G* can be written in the form *k*${}^{-1}_{1}$·*k*<sub>2</sub> with *k*${}^{-1}_{1}$,*k*<sub>2</sub> ∈ *K*(*s*). By Corollary 2, we need at most $s^{2} - s$ steps to generate *Z*(*s*) = *Z*, and no more than $2s - 1$ steps to generate *g* from *Z*(*s*).

Therefore $c(g|S) \le s^{2} + s - 1 \le \lg^{2}G + \lg G - 1 \le (1 + \lg G)^{2}$.

:::
