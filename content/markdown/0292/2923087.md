# Otakar Borůvka

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Otakar Borůvka"}

**Otakar Borůvka** (10 May 1899 – 22 July 1995) was a [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechs) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). He is best known for his work in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401).[^o5][^o3]

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Borůvka was born in [Uherský Ostroh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uherský_Ostroh), a town in [Moravia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravia), [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary) (today in the [Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic)), the son of a school headmaster.[^o3] He attended the grammar school in [Uherské Hradiště](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uherské_Hradiště) beginning in 1910. In 1916, influenced by the ongoing [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I), he moved to the military school (Realschule) in [Hranice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hranice_%28Přerov_District%29), and later he enrolled into the [Imperial and Royal Technical Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_and_Royal_Technical_Military_Academy) in [Mödling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mödling) near [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866).[^o5][^o3]

When the war ended, Borůvka returned to Uherské Hradiště, finished his studies in 1918 at the Gymnasium there, and became a student at the [Imperial Czech Technical University of Franz Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brno_University_of_Technology), in [Brno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brno), initially studying [civil engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering).[^o5][^o3] In 1920, [Masaryk University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masaryk_University) opened in Brno, and Borůvka also began taking courses there.[^o5] He became an assistant to [Mathias Lerch](wikipedia://en/4436105) at Masaryk in 1921, but Lerch died in 1922; his position at Masaryk was taken by [Eduard Čech](wikipedia://en/859830), whom Borůvka also assisted, earning his doctorate in 1923.[^o6]

At Čech's suggestion, Borůvka visited [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989) from 1926 to 1927.[^o5][^o3] He earned his [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation) from Masaryk University in 1927, and (turning down an offer from the [University of Zagreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zagreb)) he became a docent there in 1928.[^o5][^o3] He continued to travel abroad through the late 1920s and early 1930s, to Cartan in Paris again as well as to [Wilhelm Blaschke](wikipedia://en/728998) in [Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburg).[^o5][^o3] He was promoted to assistant professor at Masaryk in 1934, given a chair in 1940, and made an ordinary professor in 1946.[^o5][^o3]

In 1965, he founded the new journal *[Archivum Mathematicum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archivum_Mathematicum)*, and in 1969, he became a founding member of the Institute of Mathematics (Czechoslovak Academy of Sciences) of the [Czechoslovak Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovak_Academy_of_Sciences), splitting his time between the Institute and his professorship at Masaryk.[^o3]

## Contributions {#Contributions}

The problem of designing efficient [electric distribution networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_distribution_network) had been suggested to Borůvka by his friend Jindřich Saxel, an employee of the West Moravian Power Company, during World War I. In his 1926 paper *O jistém problému minimálním* (English *On a certain minimal problem*),[^o8] Borůvka solved this problem by modeling it mathematically as a [minimum spanning tree](wikipedia://en/41795) problem, and described the first known [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for finding the [minimum spanning tree](wikipedia://en/41795) of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) (the set of cities to be connected by the network, together with their distances).[^o5] Now called [Borůvka's algorithm](wikipedia://en/197253), his method works by repeatedly adding a connections between each subtree of the minimum spanning tree found so far and its nearest neighboring subtree.[^o9] The same algorithm has been rediscovered repeatedly.[^o10][^o11][^o12] It is more suitable for [distributed](wikipedia://en/6183392) and [parallel computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_computation) than many other minimum spanning tree algorithms, can achieve [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) complexity on [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) and more generally in [minor](wikipedia://en/353042)-closed graph families,[^o13] and plays a central role in the randomized [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) algorithm of (Karger Klein Tarjan 1995).[^o15]

From 1924 to 1935, Borůvka's primary interest was in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625). His work in this area concerned analytic correspondences between [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350), [normal curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of high-dimensional surfaces, and [Frenet formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frenet_formula) for curves in high-dimensional spaces.[^o3]

Beginning in the 1930s, Borůvka's interests shifted to [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), and in particular the theory of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447). He was also one of the first to study a generalization of groups, called by him "groupoids" but now more commonly referred to as [magmas](wikipedia://en/141916).[^o3] A textbook by him on groups and groupoids, originally published in Czech in 1944, went through several expansions, and translations, including an English edition in 1976.[^o5]

Following the war, Borůvka shifted gears again, from algebra to the theory of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309). He published several research papers on this subject, as well as a monograph on second-order differential equations which he published in 1971.[^o5]

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

Borůvka became a corresponding member of the [Czechoslovak Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovak_Academy_of_Sciences) at its creation in 1953, and an ordinary member in 1965. In 1969, [Comenius University in Bratislava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comenius_University_in_Bratislava) gave him an honorary doctorate, and in 1994 he received a second honorary doctorate from [Masaryk University in Brno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masaryk_University_in_Brno).[^o5][^o17]

He has also been given medals by the [Free University of Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_libre_de_Bruxelles), the [University of Liège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Liège), [Jagiellonian University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagiellonian_University), Comenius University, [Palacký University of Olomouc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palacký_University_of_Olomouc), [Jan Evangelista Purkyně University in Ústí nad Labem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Evangelista_Purkyně_University_in_Ústí_nad_Labem), the [German Academy of Sciences at Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Academy_of_Sciences_at_Berlin), the [Russian Academy of Sciences#Academy of Sciences of the USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences#Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_USSR), and the Czechoslovak Academy of Sciences.[^o18]
