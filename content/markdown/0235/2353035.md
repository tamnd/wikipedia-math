# Eisenstein integer

Eulerian integer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eisenstein integer"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Eisenstein integers** (named after [Gotthold Eisenstein](wikipedia://en/1452482)), occasionally also known[^o20] as **Eulerian integers** (after [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)), are the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) of the form

:::

$$z = a + b\omega ,$$

where $a$ and $b$ are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and

$$\omega = \frac{-1 + i\sqrt 3}{2} = e^{i2\pi/3}$$

is a [primitive](wikipedia://en/171950#General_definition) (hence non-real) [cube root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_root_of_unity).

*Image: Eisenstein integer lattice.png.* Eisenstein integers as the points of a certain triangular lattice in the complex plane

The Eisenstein integers form a [triangular lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_lattice) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), in contrast with the [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers), which form a [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853) in the complex plane. The Eisenstein integers are a [countably infinite set](wikipedia://en/6026).

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The Eisenstein integers form a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ of [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) in the [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) $\mathbf{Q}(\omega)$ – the third [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462). To see that the Eisenstein integers are algebraic integers note that each $z = a + b\omega$ is a root of the [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730)

$$z^2 - (2a - b)\;\!z + \left(a^2 - ab + b^2\right)~.$$

In particular, $\omega$ satisfies the equation

$$\omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0~.$$

The product of two Eisenstein integers $a + b\omega$ and $c + d\omega$ is given explicitly by

$$(a + b\;\!\omega) \;\! (c + d\;\!\omega)=(ac - bd) + (bc + ad - bd)\;\!\omega~.$$

The 2-norm of an Eisenstein integer is just its [squared modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squared_modulus), and is given by

$${\left|a + b\;\!\omega\right|}^2 \,= \, {(a - \tfrac{1}{2} b)}^2 + \tfrac{3}{4} b^2 \, = \, a^2 - ab + b^2~,$$

which is clearly a positive ordinary (rational) integer.

Also, the [complex conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) of $\omega$ satisfies

$$\bar\omega = \omega^2~.$$

The [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) in this ring is the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) formed by the sixth [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) in the complex plane: $\{\pm1, \pm\omega, \pm\omega^{2}\}$, the Eisenstein integers of norm $1$.

:::

## Euclidean domain {#Euclidean_domain}

The ring of Eisenstein integers forms a [Euclidean domain](wikipedia://en/10376) whose norm $N$ is given by the square modulus, as above:

$$N(a+b\,\omega) = a^2 - a b + b^2.$$

A [division algorithm](wikipedia://en/3336479), applied to any dividend $\alpha$ and divisor $\beta \ne 0$, gives a quotient $\kappa$ and a remainder $\rho$ smaller than the divisor, satisfying:

$$\alpha = \kappa \beta +\rho \ \ \text{ with }\ \ N(\rho) < N(\beta).$$

Here, $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\kappa$, $\rho$ are all Eisenstein integers. This algorithm implies the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377), which proves [Euclid's lemma](wikipedia://en/826617) and the [unique factorization](wikipedia://en/11556) of Eisenstein integers into Eisenstein primes.

One division algorithm is as follows. First perform the division in the field of complex numbers, and write the quotient in terms of $\omega$:

$$\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\ =\ \tfrac{1}{\ |\beta|^2}\alpha\overline{\beta} \ =\ a+bi \ =\ a+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}b+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}b\omega,$$

for rational $a, b \in \mathbf{Q}$. Then obtain the Eisenstein integer quotient by rounding the rational coefficients to the nearest integer:

$$\kappa = \left\lfloor a+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}b\right\rceil + \left\lfloor \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}b\right\rceil\omega \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \rho = {\alpha} - \kappa\beta.$$

Here $\lfloor x\rceil$ may denote any of the standard [rounding](wikipedia://en/169945)-to-integer functions.

The reason this satisfies $N(\rho) < N(\beta)$, while the analogous procedure fails for most other [quadratic integer](wikipedia://en/21834785) rings, is as follows. A [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) for the ideal $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]\beta = \mathbf{Z}\beta + \mathbf{Z}\omega\beta$, acting by translations on the complex plane, is the 60°–120° rhombus with vertices $0$, $\beta$, $\omega\beta$, $\beta + \omega\beta$. Any Eisenstein integer $\alpha$ lies inside one of the translates of this [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496), and the quotient $\kappa$ is one of its vertices. The remainder is the square distance from $\alpha$ to this vertex, but the maximum possible distance in our algorithm is only $\tfrac{\sqrt3}2 |\beta|$, so $|\rho| \leq \tfrac{\sqrt3}2 |\beta|< |\beta|$. (The size of $\rho$ could be slightly decreased by taking $\kappa$ to be the closest corner.)

## Eisenstein primes {#Eisenstein_primes}

*Image: EisensteinPrimes-01.svg.* Small Eisenstein primes. Those on the green axes are associate to a natural prime of the form $3n + 2$. All others have an absolute value equal to 3 or square root of a natural prime of the form $3n + 1$.

*Image: Eisenstein primes.svg.* Eisenstein primes in a larger range

If $x$ and $y$ are Eisenstein integers, we say that $x$ divides $y$ if there is some Eisenstein integer $z$ such that $y = zx$. A non-unit Eisenstein integer $x$ is said to be an Eisenstein prime if its only non-unit divisors are of the form $ux$, where $u$ is any of the six units. They are the corresponding concept to the [Gaussian primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_prime) in the Gaussian integers.

There are two types of Eisenstein prime.

- an ordinary [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) (or *rational prime*) which is congruent to $2 \bmod 3$ is also an Eisenstein prime.
- $3$ and each rational prime congruent to $1 \bmod 3$ are equal to the norm $x^{2} - xy + y^{2}$ of an Eisenstein integer $x + \omega y$. Thus, such a prime may be factored as $(x + \omega y)(x + \omega^{2}y)$, and these factors are Eisenstein primes: they are precisely the Eisenstein integers whose norm is a rational prime.

In the second type, factors of $3$, $1-\omega$ and $1-\omega^2$ are [associates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associate_%28ring_theory%29): $1-\omega=(-\omega)(1-\omega^2)$, so it is regarded as a special type in some books.[^o8]

The first few Eisenstein primes of the form $3n - 1$ are:

[2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_%28number%29), [5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_%28number%29), [11](wikipedia://en/208156), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [23](wikipedia://en/362203), [29](wikipedia://en/362212), [41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/41_%28number%29), [47](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/47_%28number%29), [53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53_%28number%29), [59](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/59_%28number%29), [71](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/71_%28number%29), [83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/83_%28number%29), [89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/89_%28number%29), [101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101_%28number%29), ... .

Natural primes that are congruent to $0$ or $1$ modulo $3$ are *not* Eisenstein primes:[^o9] they admit nontrivial factorizations in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$. For example:

$$3 = -(1 + 2\omega)^{2}$$

$$7 = (3 + \omega)(2 - \omega).$$

In general, if a natural prime $p$ is $1$ modulo $3$ and can therefore be written as $p = a^{2} - ab + b^{2}$, then it factorizes over $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ as

$$p = (a + b\omega)((a - b) - b\omega).$$

Some non-real Eisenstein primes are

$2 + \omega$, $3 + \omega$, $4 + \omega$, $5 + 2\omega$, $6 + \omega$, $7 + \omega$, $7 + 3\omega$.

Up to conjugacy and unit multiples, the primes listed above, together with $2$ and $5$, are all the Eisenstein primes of [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) not exceeding $7$.

, the largest known real Eisenstein prime is the [12th-largest known prime](wikipedia://en/1669332) $10223 \times 2^{31172165} + 1$, discovered by Péter Szabolcs and [PrimeGrid](wikipedia://en/3255603).[^o10]

## Eisenstein series {#Eisenstein_series}

The sum of the reciprocals of all Eisenstein integers excluding $0$ raised to the fourth power is $0$:[^o12]

$$\sum_{z\in\mathbf{E}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{z^4}=G_4\left(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\right)=0$$

so $e^{2\pi i/3}$ is a root of [j-invariant](wikipedia://en/451988). In general $G_k\left(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\right)=0$ if and only if $k\not\equiv 0 \pmod 6$.[^o13]

The sum of the reciprocals of all Eisenstein integers excluding $0$ raised to the sixth power can be expressed in terms of the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316):

$$\sum_{z\in\mathbf{E}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{z^6}=G_6\left(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}}\right)=\frac{\Gamma (1/3)^{18}}{8960\pi^6} \approx 5.86303$$

where $\mathbf{E}$ are the Eisenstein integers and $G_{6}$ is the [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458) of weight 6.[^o14]

## Quotient of $\mathbf{C}$ by the Eisenstein integers {#Quotient_of_by_the_Eisenstein_integers}

The [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) of the complex plane $\mathbf{C}$ by the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) containing all Eisenstein integers is a [complex torus](wikipedia://en/3055042) of real dimension $2$. This is one of two tori with maximal [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) among all such complex tori.[^o16] This torus can be obtained by identifying each of the three pairs of opposite edges of a regular hexagon.

*Image: Wrapped hexagon topology.png.* Identifying each of the three pairs of opposite edges of a regular hexagon.[^o17]

The other maximally symmetric torus is the quotient of the complex plane by the additive lattice of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integers), and can be obtained by identifying each of the two pairs of opposite sides of a square fundamental domain, such as $[0, 1] \times [0, 1]$.
