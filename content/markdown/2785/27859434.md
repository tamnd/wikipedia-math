# Fugit

::: {#o1 .table cue=table}

| **Calculation of fugit:** For Fugit — where $n$ is the number of time-steps in the tree; $t$ is the time to option expiry; and $i$ is the current time-step — the calculation is as follows:[^o10]<sup>; see also</sup> [^o6] (1) set the fugit of all nodes at the end of the tree equal to $i = n$ (2) work backwards recursively: if the option should be exercised at a node, set the fugit at that node equal to its period if the option should not be exercised at a node, set the fugit to the risk-neutral expected fugit over the next period. (3) the number calculated in this fashion at the beginning of the first period ($i = 0$) is the current fugit. Finally, to annualize the fugit, multiply the resulting value by $t / n$. |
|---|

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Fugit"}

In [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), **fugit** is the expected (or optimal) date to exercise an [American-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_style#American_and_European_options) or [Bermudan option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermudan_option). It is useful for [hedging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedge_%28finance%29) purposes here; see [Greeks (finance)](wikipedia://en/250251) and Optimal stopping#Option trading. The term was first introduced by Mark Garman in an article "Semper tempus fugit" published in 1989.[^o3] The [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) term "tempus fugit" means "time flies"[^o4] and Garman suggested the name because "time flies especially when you're having fun managing your book of American options".

:::

## Details {#Details}

Fugit provides an estimate of when an option would be exercised, which is then a useful indication for the maturity to use when hedging American or Bermudan products with [European options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_option).[^o6] Fugit is thus used for the hedging of [convertible bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convertible_bond), equity linked convertible notes, and any putable or callable [exotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_derivatives) coupon notes. Although see [^o7] and [^o8] for qualifications here. Fugit is also useful in estimating "the (risk-neutral) expected life of the option"[^o9] for [Employee stock options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employee_stock_option#Valuation) (note the brackets).

Fugit is calculated as "the expected time to exercise of American options",[^o3] and is also described as the "[risk-neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk-neutral) expected life of the option"[^o10] The computation requires a [binomial tree](wikipedia://en/250074) — although a [Finite difference approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_difference_methods_for_option_pricing) would also apply[^o6] — where, a second quantity, additional to option price, is required at each node of the tree;[^o11] see methodology aside. Note that fugit is not always a unique value.[^o7]

[Nassim Taleb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nassim_Taleb) proposes a "rho fudge", as a “shortcut method... to find the right duration (i.e., expected time to termination) for an American option”.[^o12] Taleb terms this result “Omega” as opposed to fugit. The formula is

$$\text{Omega} = \text{Nominal Duration} \times \frac{\rho_2 \text{ of an American option}}{\rho_2 \text{ of a European option}}$$

Here, $\rho_{2}$ refers to sensitivity to dividends or the foreign interest rate, as opposed to [the more usual rho](wikipedia://en/250251#Rho) which measures sensitivity to (local) interest rates; the latter is sometimes used, however.[^o13] Taleb notes that this approach was widely applied, already in the 1980s, preceding Garman.[^o14]
