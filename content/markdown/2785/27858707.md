# Ralph S. Phillips

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Ralph S. Phillips"}

**Ralph Saul Phillips** (23 June 1913 – 23 November 1998) was an American mathematician and academic known for his contributions to [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), [scattering theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_theory), and [servomechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servomechanism). He served as a professor of mathematics at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University). He made major contributions to acoustical scattering theory in collaboration with [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365), proving remarkable results on local energy decay and the connections between poles of the [scattering matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) and the analytic properties of the [resolvent](wikipedia://en/3378540). With Lax, he coauthored the widely referred book on [scattering theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_theory) titled *Scattering Theory for Automorphic Functions*. Phillips received the 1997 [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) for Lifetime Achievement.[^o4]

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Phillips was born in [Oakland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland,_California) on 23 June 1913. He received his bachelor's degree from the [University of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) at [Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles) (UCLA) in 1935 and his Ph.D. from the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) in 1939 under the direction of Theophil H. Hildebrandt.[^o2]

From 1939 until 1942 he was a member of the Institute for Advanced Study in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), an instructor at the [University of Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Washington), and an instructor at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University). It was at the University of Washington he met his future wife, Jean.[^o6] During the war he led a research group at the Radiation Laboratory at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology), the facility where much of the theoretical and practical work on radar technology was done. This work led to his book Theory of Servomechanisms,[^o7] which for many years was the standard text in the subject. After the war he returned to mathematics, joining as an assistant professor at the [Courant Institute of Mathematical Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courant_Institute_of_Mathematical_Sciences). He moved to the [University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California) the next year and returned to UCLA in 1958. In 1960 he joined [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) and remained there until his death in 1998. He was the Robert Grimmett Professor of mathematics at Stanford. Philips's work (with [A. Lubotzky](wikipedia://en/22754130) and [P. Sarnak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Sarnak)) on [Ramanujan graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan_graphs) had a huge impact on [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science).
