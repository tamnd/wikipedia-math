# Four-dimensional space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Four-dimensional space"}

**Four-dimensional (4D) space** is the mathematical extension of the concept of [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) (3D). Three-dimensional space is the simplest possible abstraction of the observation that one needs only three numbers, called *[dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398)*, to describe the [sizes](wikipedia://en/43537463) or [locations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location) of objects in the everyday world. This concept of ordinary space is called [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) because it corresponds to [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)['s geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), which was originally abstracted from the spatial experiences of everyday life.

:::

Single locations in Euclidean 4D space can be given as [vectors](wikipedia://en/32370) or *[4-tuples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-tuples)*, i.e., as ordered lists of numbers such as $(x, y, z, w)$. For example, the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of a rectangular box is found by measuring and multiplying its length, width, and height (often labeled as $x$, $y$, and $z$). It is only when such locations are linked together into more complicated shapes that the full richness and geometric complexity of 4D spaces emerge. A hint of that complexity can be seen in the accompanying 2D animation of one of the simplest possible [regular 4D objects](wikipedia://en/18801624), the [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112), which is [analogous](wikipedia://en/39783) to the 3D [cube](wikipedia://en/6285).

## History {#History}

The idea of making [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) the fourth dimension began with [Jean le Rond d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d'Alembert)'s "Dimensions," published in 1754 in the *[Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopédie,_ou_dictionnaire_raisonné_des_sciences,_des_arts_et_des_métiers)*.[^o3][^o4] That [physical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) can be viewed as occurring also in time was posited by [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793) in 1755 and published in 1788 in his Mécanique analytique.[^o5]

Mathematics of 4D commenced in the nineteenth century.[^o6] 3D form rotation onto its mirror-image is possible in 4D space was realized by [August Ferdinand Möbius](wikipedia://en/37875)[^n1] in *Der barycentrische Calcul* published 1827.[^o8] An arithmetic of four spatial dimensions, called [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), was defined by [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1843. Soon after, [tessarines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessarine) and [coquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquaternion) were introduced as other four-dimensional [algebras over **R**](wikipedia://en/191788). Higher-dimensional non-Euclidean spaces were put on a firm footing by [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980)'s 1854 [thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitationsschrift), Über die Hypothesen welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen, in which he considered a "point" to be any sequence of coordinates $(x_{1}, ..., x_{n})$.

Euclidean spaces of more than three dimensions were first described in 1852, when [Ludwig Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408) generalized Euclidean geometry to spaces of dimension *n*, using both synthetic and algebraic methods. He discovered all of the regular polytopes (higher-dimensional analogues of the [Platonic solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_solids)) that exist in Euclidean spaces of any dimension, including [six found in 4-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/18801624).[^n2] Schläfli's work was only published posthumously in 1901, and remained largely unknown until publication of [H.S.M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328)'s *[Regular Polytopes](wikipedia://en/8629753)* in 1947. During that interval many others also discovered higher-dimensional Euclidean space. One of the first popular expositors of the fourth dimension was [Charles Howard Hinton](wikipedia://en/562977), starting in 1880 with his essay "What is the Fourth Dimension?", published in the [Dublin University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_University) magazine, in which he explained the concept of a "[four-dimensional cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-dimensional_cube)" with a step-by-step generalization of the properties of lines, squares, and cubes.[^o10] He coined the terms *[tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)*, *ana* and *kata* in his book *[A New Era of Thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_New_Era_of_Thought)* and introduced a method for visualizing the fourth dimension using cubes in the book *Fourth Dimension*.[^o11][^o12] 1886, [Victor Schlegel](wikipedia://en/34212179) described[^o13] his method of visualizing [four-dimensional objects](wikipedia://en/24979) with [Schlegel diagrams](wikipedia://en/7201595).

[Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537)'s 1908 paper[^o14] consolidating the role of time as the fourth dimension of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) provided the geometric basis for Einstein's theories of special and general relativity.[^o15] The geometry of spacetime, being [non-Euclidean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean), is profoundly different from that explored by Schläfli and popularised by Hinton. Hinton's ideas inspired a fantasy about a "Church of the Fourth Dimension" featured by [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) in his January 1962 "[Mathematical Games column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Games_column)" in *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)*. 1967, The [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) of W R Hamilton was the source of the science of [vector analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_analysis) in three dimensions as recounted by [Michael J. Crowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_J._Crowe) in *[A History of Vector Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_History_of_Vector_Analysis)*. The study of [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) required Riemann's mathematics which is quite different from that of four-dimensional Euclidean space, and so developed along quite different lines. This separation was less clear in the popular imagination, with works of fiction and philosophy blurring the distinction, so in 1973 [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) felt compelled to write: Little, if anything, is gained by representing the fourth Euclidean dimension as *time*. In fact, this idea, so attractively developed by [H. G. Wells in *The Time Machine*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Time_Machine), has led such authors as [John William Dunne (*An Experiment with Time*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Experiment_with_Time) into a serious misconception of the theory of Relativity. Minkowski's geometry of space-time is *not* Euclidean, and consequently has no connection with the present investigation.

## Vectors {#Vectors}

Mathematically, a four-dimensional space is a [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) that needs four parameters to specify a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) in it. For example, a general point might have position [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) $\mathbf{a}$, equal to

$$\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3 \\ a_4 \end{pmatrix}.$$

This can be written in terms of the four [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) vectors $(\mathbf{e}_{1}, \mathbf{e}_{2}, \mathbf{e}_{3}, \mathbf{e}_{4})$, given by

$$\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}; \mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}; \mathbf{e}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}; \mathbf{e}_4 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},$$

so the [general vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_vector_space) $\mathbf{a}$ is

$$\mathbf{a} = a_1\mathbf{e}_1 + a_2\mathbf{e}_2 + a_3\mathbf{e}_3 + a_4\mathbf{e}_4.$$

Vectors add, subtract and scale as in three dimensions.

The [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of Euclidean three-dimensional space generalizes to four dimensions as

$$\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 + a_4 b_4.$$

It can be used to calculate the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) or [length](wikipedia://en/53932) of a vector,

$$\left| \mathbf{a} \right| = \sqrt{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a} } = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2 + a_4^2},$$

and calculate or define the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between two non-zero vectors as

$$\theta = \arccos{\frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\left|\mathbf{a}\right| \left|\mathbf{b}\right|}}.$$

Minkowski spacetime is four-dimensional space with geometry defined by a non-degenerate [pairing](wikipedia://en/1191490) different from the dot product:

$$\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 - a_4 b_4.$$

As an example, the distance squared between the points $(0,0,0,0)$ and $(1,1,1,0)$ is 3 in both the Euclidean and Minkowskian 4-spaces, while the distance squared between $(0,0,0,0)$ and $(1,1,1,1)$ is 4 in Euclidean space and 2 in Minkowski space; increasing $b_{4}$ decreases the metric distance. This leads to many of the well-known apparent "paradoxes" of relativity.

The [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) is not defined in four dimensions. Instead, the [exterior product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product) is used for some applications, and is defined as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{a} \wedge \mathbf{b} = (a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)\mathbf{e}_{12} + (a_1b_3 - a_3b_1)\mathbf{e}_{13} + (a_1b_4 - a_4b_1)\mathbf{e}_{14} + (a_2b_3 - a_3b_2)\mathbf{e}_{23} \\ + (a_2b_4 - a_4b_2)\mathbf{e}_{24} + (a_3b_4 - a_4b_3)\mathbf{e}_{34}. \end{aligned}$$

This is [bivector](wikipedia://en/884040) valued, with bivectors in four dimensions forming a [six-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-dimensional_space) linear space with basis $(\mathbf{e}_{12}, \mathbf{e}_{13}, \mathbf{e}_{14}, \mathbf{e}_{23}, \mathbf{e}_{24}, \mathbf{e}_{34})$. They can be used to generate rotations in four dimensions.

## Orthogonality and vocabulary {#Orthogonality_and_vocabulary}

In the familiar three-dimensional space of daily life, there are three [coordinate axes](wikipedia://en/81931)—usually labeled $x$, $y$, and $z$—with each axis [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) (i.e. perpendicular) to the other two. The six cardinal directions in this space can be called *up*, *down*, *east*, *west*, *north*, and *south*. Positions along these axes can be called *altitude*, *longitude*, and *latitude*. Lengths measured along these axes can be called *height*, *width*, and *depth*.

Comparatively, four-dimensional space has an extra coordinate axis, orthogonal to the other three, which is usually labeled $w$. To describe the two additional cardinal directions, [Charles Howard Hinton](wikipedia://en/562977) coined the terms *ana* and *kata*, from the Greek words meaning "up toward" and "down from", respectively.[^o11]

As mentioned, Hermann Minkowski exploited the idea of four dimensions to discuss cosmology including the finite [velocity of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_of_light). In appending a time dimension to three-dimensional space, he specified an alternative perpendicularity, [hyperbolic orthogonality](wikipedia://en/958449). This notion provides his four-dimensional space with a modified [simultaneity](wikipedia://en/3406142) appropriate to electromagnetic relations in his cosmos. Minkowski's world overcame problems associated with the traditional [absolute space and time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_space_and_time) cosmology previously used in a universe of three space dimensions and one time dimension.

## Geometry {#Geometry}

Just as in three dimensions there are [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) made of two dimensional [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621), in four dimensions there are polychora made of polyhedra. In three dimensions, there are 5 regular polyhedra known as the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905). In three dimensions, a circle may be [extruded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrude) to form a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29). In four dimensions, there are several different cylinder-like objects. A sphere may be extruded to obtain a spherical cylinder (a cylinder with spherical "caps", known as a [spherinder](wikipedia://en/39661691)), and a cylinder may be extruded to obtain a cylindrical prism (a cubinder).[^o22] The [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two circles may be taken to obtain a [duocylinder](wikipedia://en/2114445). All three can "roll" in four-dimensional space, each with its properties.

In three dimensions, curves can form [knots](wikipedia://en/366808) but surfaces cannot (unless they are self-intersecting). In four dimensions, however, knots made using curves can be trivially untied by displacing them in the fourth direction—but 2D surfaces can form non-trivial, non-self-intersecting knots in 4D space.[^o23] Because these surfaces are two-dimensional, they can form much more complex knots than strings in 3D space can. The [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412) is an example of such a knotted surface. Another such surface is the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325).

### Hypersphere {#Hypersphere}

The set of points in [Euclidean 4-space](wikipedia://en/9697) having the same distance $R$ from a fixed point $P_{0}$ forms a [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) known as a [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792). The hyper-volume of the enclosed space is:

$$\mathbf V = \begin{matrix} \frac{1}{2} \end{matrix} \pi^2 R^4$$

This is part of the [Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric](wikipedia://en/343031) in [General relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) where $R$ is substituted by function $R(t)$ with $t$ meaning the cosmological age of the universe. Growing or shrinking $R$ with time means expanding or collapsing universe, depending on the mass density inside.[^o25]

## Four-dimensional perception in humans {#Four-dimensional_perception_in_humans}

Research using [virtual reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_reality) finds that humans, despite living in a three-dimensional world, can, without special practice, make spatial judgments about line segments embedded in four-dimensional space, based on their length (one-dimensional) and the angle (two-dimensional) between them.[^o27] The researchers noted that "the participants in our study had minimal practice in these tasks, and it remains an open question whether it is possible to obtain more sustainable, definitive, and richer 4D representations with increased perceptual experience in 4D virtual environments".[^o27] In another study,[^o28] the ability of humans to orient themselves in 2D, 3D, and 4D mazes has been tested. Each maze consisted of four path segments of random length and connected with orthogonal random bends, but without branches or loops (i.e. actually [labyrinths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labyrinth)). The graphical interface was based on John McIntosh's free 4D Maze game.[^o29] The participating persons had to navigate through the path and finally estimate the linear direction back to the starting point. The researchers found that some of the participants were able to mentally integrate their path after some practice in 4D (the lower-dimensional cases were for comparison and for the participants to learn the method).

However, a 2020 review underlined how these studies are composed of a small subject sample and mainly of college students. It also pointed out other issues that future research has to resolve: elimination of [artifacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artifact_%28error%29) (these could be caused, for example, by strategies to resolve the required task that don't use 4D representation/4D reasoning and feedback given by researchers to speed up the adaptation process) and analysis on inter-subject variability (if 4D perception is possible, its acquisition could be limited to a subset of humans, to a specific [critical period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_period), or to people's attention or motivation). Furthermore, it is undetermined if there is a more appropriate way to project the 4-dimension (because there are no restrictions on how the 4-dimension can be projected). Researchers also hypothesized that human acquisition of 4D perception could result in the activation of brain visual areas and [entorhinal cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entorhinal_cortex). If so they suggest that it could be used as a strong indicator of 4D space perception acquisition. Authors also suggested using a variety of different [neural network architectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network) (with different *[a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori)* assumptions) to understand which ones are or are not able to learn.[^o30]

## Dimensional analogy {#Dimensional_analogy}

*Image: Tesseract net Crooked House.svg.* A net of a tesseract

To understand the nature of four-dimensional space, a device called *dimensional analogy* is commonly employed. Dimensional analogy is the study of how ($n - 1$) dimensions relate to $n$ dimensions, and then inferring how $n$ dimensions would relate to ($n + 1$) dimensions.[^o32]

The dimensional analogy was used by [Edwin Abbott Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Abbott_Abbott) in the book *[Flatland](wikipedia://en/75214)*, which narrates a story about a square that lives in a two-dimensional world, like the surface of a piece of paper. From the perspective of this square, a three-dimensional being has seemingly god-like powers, such as ability to remove objects from a safe without breaking it open (by moving them across the third dimension), to see everything that from the two-dimensional perspective is enclosed behind walls, and to remain completely invisible by standing a few inches away in the third dimension.

By applying dimensional analogy, one can infer that a four-dimensional being would be capable of similar feats from the three-dimensional perspective. [Rudy Rucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudy_Rucker) illustrates this in his novel *[Spaceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceland_%28novel%29)*, in which the protagonist encounters four-dimensional beings who demonstrate such powers.

### Cross-sections {#Cross-sections}

As a three-dimensional object passes through a two-dimensional plane, two-dimensional beings in this plane would only observe a [cross-section](wikipedia://en/1337370) of the three-dimensional object within this plane. For example, if a sphere passed through a sheet of paper, beings in the paper would see first a single point. A circle gradually grows larger, until it reaches the diameter of the sphere, and then gets smaller again, until it shrinks to a point and disappears. The 2D beings would not see a circle in the same way as three-dimensional beings do; rather, they only see a [one-dimensional](wikipedia://en/25584215) projection of the circle on their 1D "retina". Similarly, if a four-dimensional object passed through a three-dimensional (hyper) surface, one could observe a three-dimensional cross-section of the four-dimensional object. For example, a [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) would appear first as a point, then as a growing sphere (until it reaches the "hyperdiameter" of the hypersphere), with the sphere then shrinking to a single point and then disappearing.[^o34] This means of visualizing aspects of the fourth dimension was used in the novel *Flatland* and also in several works of [Charles Howard Hinton](wikipedia://en/562977).[^o11] And, in the same way, three-dimensional beings (such as humans with a 2D retina) can see all the sides and the insides of a 2D shape simultaneously, a 4D being could see all faces and the inside of a 3D shape at once with their 3D retina.

### Projections {#Projections}

A useful application of dimensional analogy in visualizing higher dimensions is in [projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_projection). A projection is a way of representing an *n*-dimensional object in $n - 1$ dimensions. For instance, computer screens are two-dimensional, and all the photographs of three-dimensional people, places, and things are represented in two dimensions by projecting the objects onto a flat surface. By doing this, the dimension orthogonal to the screen (*depth*) is removed and replaced with indirect information. The [retina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retina) of the [eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_eye) is also a two-dimensional [array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Array_data_structure) of [receptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensory_receptor) but the [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain) can perceive the nature of three-dimensional objects by inference from indirect information (such as shading, [foreshortening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreshortening), [binocular vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binocular_vision), etc.). [Artists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artist) often use [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405) to give an illusion of three-dimensional depth to two-dimensional pictures. The *shadow*, cast by a fictitious grid model of a rotating tesseract on a plane surface, as shown in the figures, is also the result of projections.

Similarly, objects in the fourth dimension can be mathematically projected to the familiar three dimensions, where they can be more conveniently examined. In this case, the 'retina' of the four-dimensional eye is a three-dimensional array of receptors. A hypothetical being with such an eye would perceive the nature of four-dimensional objects by inferring four-dimensional depth from indirect information in the three-dimensional images in its retina.

The perspective projection of three-dimensional objects into the retina of the eye introduces artifacts such as foreshortening, which the brain interprets as depth in the third dimension. In the same way, perspective projection from four dimensions produces similar foreshortening effects. By applying dimensional analogy, one may infer four-dimensional "depth" from these effects.

### Shadows {#Shadows}

A concept closely related to projection is the casting of shadows.

*Image: Schlegel wireframe 8-cell.png.*

If a light is shone on a three-dimensional object, a two-dimensional shadow is cast. By dimensional analogy, light shone on a two-dimensional object in a two-dimensional world would cast a one-dimensional shadow, and light on a one-dimensional object in a one-dimensional world would cast a zero-dimensional shadow, that is, a point of non-light. Going the other way, one may infer that light shining on a four-dimensional object in a four-dimensional world would cast a three-dimensional shadow.

If the wireframe of a cube is lit from above, the resulting shadow on a flat two-dimensional surface is a square within a square with the corresponding corners connected. Similarly, if the wireframe of a tesseract were lit from "above" (in the fourth dimension), its shadow would be that of a three-dimensional cube within another three-dimensional cube suspended in midair (a "flat" surface from a four-dimensional perspective). (Note that, technically, the visual representation shown here is a two-dimensional image of the three-dimensional shadow of the four-dimensional wireframe figure.)

### Bounding regions {#Bounding_regions}

The dimensional analogy also helps in inferring basic properties of objects in higher dimensions, such as the [bounding region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounding_region). For example, two-dimensional objects are bounded by one-dimensional boundaries: a square is bounded by four edges. Three-dimensional objects are bounded by two-dimensional surfaces: a cube is bounded by 6 square faces.

By applying dimensional analogy, one may infer that a four-dimensional cube, known as a *[tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112)*, is bounded by three-dimensional volumes. And indeed, this is the case: mathematics shows that the tesseract is bounded by 8 cubes. Knowing this is key to understanding how to interpret a three-dimensional projection of the tesseract. The boundaries of the tesseract project to *volumes* in the image, not merely two-dimensional surfaces.

### Hypervolume {#Hypervolume}

The **4-volume** or [hypervolume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypervolume) in 4D can be calculated in closed form for simple geometrical figures, such as the tesseract (*s*<sup>4</sup>, for side length *s*) and the [4-ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-ball_%28mathematics%29) ($\pi^2 r^4 /2$ for radius *r*).

Reasoning by analogy from familiar lower dimensions can be an excellent intuitive guide, but care must be exercised not to accept results that are not more rigorously tested. For example, consider the formulas for the area enclosed by a circle in two dimensions ($A = \pi r^2$) and the volume enclosed by a sphere in three dimensions ($V = \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3$). One might guess that the volume enclosed by the sphere in four-dimensional space is a rational multiple of $\pi r^4$, but the correct volume is $\frac{\pi^2}{2} r^4$.[^n3] The [volume of an *n*-ball](wikipedia://en/21067650) in an arbitrary dimension *n* is computable from a [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) connecting dimension $n$ to dimension $n - 2$.

### In literature {#In_literature}

[Science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction) texts often mention the concept of "dimension" when referring to [parallel or alternate universes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_universes_in_fiction) or other imagined [planes of existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28esotericism%29). This usage is derived from the idea that to travel to parallel/alternate universes/planes of existence one must travel in a direction/dimension besides the standard ones. In effect, the other universes/planes are just a small distance away from this universe, but the distance is in a fourth (or higher) spatial (or non-spatial) dimension, not the standard ones.

One of the most heralded science fiction stories regarding true geometric dimensionality, and often recommended as a starting point for those just starting to investigate such matters, is the 1884 novella *[Flatland](wikipedia://en/75214)* by Edwin A. Abbott. Isaac Asimov, in his foreword to the Signet Classics 1984 edition, described *Flatland* as "The best introduction one can find into the manner of perceiving dimensions."

The idea of other dimensions was incorporated into many early science fiction stories, appearing prominently, for example, in [Miles J. Breuer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_J._Breuer)'s *The Appendix and the Spectacles* (1928) and [Murray Leinster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Leinster)'s *The Fifth-Dimension Catapult* (1931); and appeared irregularly in science fiction by the 1940s. Classic stories involving other dimensions include [Robert A. Heinlein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_A._Heinlein)'s *[—And He Built a Crooked House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/—And_He_Built_a_Crooked_House)* (1941), in which a California architect designs a house based on a three-dimensional projection of a tesseract; [Alan E. Nourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_E._Nourse)'s *Tiger by the Tail* and *The Universe Between* (both 1951); and *[The Ifth of Oofth](https://archive.org/stream/galaxymagazine-1957-04/Galaxy_1957_04#page/n59/mode/2up)* (1957) by [Walter Tevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Tevis). Another reference is [Madeleine L'Engle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madeleine_L'Engle)'s novel *[A Wrinkle In Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Wrinkle_In_Time)* (1962), which uses the fifth dimension as a way of "tesseracting the universe" or "folding" space to move across it quickly. The fourth and fifth dimensions are also key components of the book *[The Boy Who Reversed Himself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boy_Who_Reversed_Himself)* (1986) by [William Sleator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sleator).

### In philosophy {#In_philosophy}

[Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) wrote in 1783: "That everywhere space (which is not itself the boundary of another space) has three dimensions and that space, in general, cannot have more dimensions is based on the proposition that not more than three lines can intersect at right angles in one point. This proposition cannot at all be shown from concepts, but rests immediately on intuition and indeed on pure intuition *a priori* because it is apodictically (demonstrably) certain."[^o45]

"Space has Four Dimensions" is a short story published in 1846 by German philosopher and [experimental psychologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_psychology) [Gustav Fechner](wikipedia://en/203499) under the [pseudonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudonym) "Dr. Mises". The protagonist in the tale is a shadow who is aware of and able to communicate with other shadows, but who is trapped on a two-dimensional surface. According to Fechner, this "shadow-man" would conceive of the third dimension as being one of time.[^o46] The story bears a strong similarity to the "[Allegory of the Cave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory_of_the_Cave)" presented in [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s *[The Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Republic_%28Plato%29)* (c. 380 BC).

Simon Newcomb wrote an article for the *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* in 1898 entitled "The Philosophy of Hyperspace".[^o47] [Linda Dalrymple Henderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linda_Dalrymple_Henderson) coined the term "hyperspace philosophy", used to describe writing that uses higher dimensions to explore [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) themes, in her 1983 thesis about the fourth dimension in early-twentieth-century art.[^o48] Examples of "hyperspace philosophers" include [Charles Howard Hinton](wikipedia://en/562977), the first writer, in 1888, to use the word "tesseract";[^o49] and the Russian [esotericist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_esotericism) [P. D. Ouspensky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._D._Ouspensky).
