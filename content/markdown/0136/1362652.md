# Kleinian group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kleinian group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Kleinian group** is a [discrete subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_subgroup) of the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of orientation-preserving [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space) $\mathbf{H}^{3}$. The latter, identifiable with [$\operatorname{PSL}(2,\,\mathbf{C})$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSL%282,C%29), is the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of the 2 by 2 [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) of [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) 1 by their [center](wikipedia://en/7125), which consists of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) and its product by $-1$. $\operatorname{PSL}(2,\,\mathbf{C})$ has a natural representation as orientation-preserving [conformal transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_transformation) of the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), and as orientation-preserving [conformal transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_transformation) of the open [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) $B^{3}$ in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$. The group of [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493) is also related as the non-orientation-preserving isometry group of $\mathbf{H}^{3}$, $\operatorname{PGL}(2,\,\mathbf{C})$. So, a Kleinian group can be regarded as a discrete subgroup [acting](wikipedia://en/12781) on one of these spaces.

:::

## History {#History}

The theory of general Kleinian groups was founded by Felix Klein[^o3] and Henri Poincaré,[^o4] who named them after [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887). The special case of [Schottky groups](wikipedia://en/5871034) had been studied a few years earlier, in 1877, by [Friedrich Schottky](wikipedia://en/5872505).

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

One modern definition of Kleinian group is as a group which acts on the [3-ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-ball) $B^3$ as a [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) of [hyperbolic isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_isometry). [Hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space) has a natural boundary; in the [ball model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_model), this can be identified with the 2-sphere. We call it the ***sphere at infinity***, and denote it by $S^2_\infty$. A hyperbolic isometry extends to a [conformal homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/50627) of the sphere at infinity (and conversely, every conformal homeomorphism on the sphere at infinity extends uniquely to a hyperbolic isometry on the ball by [Poincaré extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_extension)).[^o7] It is a standard result from complex analysis that conformal homeomorphisms on the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) are exactly the [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493), which can further be identified as elements of the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) PGL(2,**C**).[^o7] Thus, a Kleinian group can also be defined as a subgroup Γ of PGL(2,**C**). Classically, a Kleinian group was required to act properly discontinuously on a non-empty open subset of the Riemann sphere, but modern usage allows any discrete subgroup.

When Γ is isomorphic to the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) $\pi_1$ of a [hyperbolic 3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1237700), then the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) **H**<sup>3</sup>/Γ becomes a [Kleinian model](wikipedia://en/1589114) of the manifold. Many authors use the terms *Kleinian model* and *Kleinian group* interchangeably, letting the one stand for the other.

Discreteness implies points in the interior of hyperbolic 3-space have finite [stabilizers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_%28group_theory%29), and discrete [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) under the group Γ. On the other hand, the orbit Γ*p* of a point *p* will typically [accumulate](wikipedia://en/226505) on the boundary of the [closed ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_ball) $\bar{B}^3$.

*Image: Apollonian gasket.svg.* An [Apollonian gasket](wikipedia://en/1049256) is an example of a limit set of a Kleinian group

The set of [accumulation points](wikipedia://en/226505) of Γ*p* in $S^2_\infty$ is called the ***limit set*** of Γ, and usually denoted $\Lambda(\Gamma)$. The complement $\Omega(\Gamma)=S^2_\infty - \Lambda(\Gamma)$ is called the **domain of discontinuity** or the **ordinary set** or the **regular set**. [Ahlfors' finiteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahlfors'_finiteness_theorem) implies that if the group is finitely generated then $\Omega(\Gamma)/\Gamma$ is a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) orbifold of finite type.

The unit ball *B*<sup>3</sup> with its conformal structure is the [Poincaré model](wikipedia://en/461942) of [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space). When we think of it metrically, with metric

$$ds^2= \frac{4 \, \left| dx \right|^2 }{\left( 1-|x|^2 \right)^2}$$

it is a model of 3-dimensional hyperbolic space **H**<sup>3</sup>. The set of conformal self-maps of *B*<sup>3</sup> becomes the set of [isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometries) (i.e. distance-preserving maps) of **H**<sup>3</sup> under this identification. Such maps restrict to conformal self-maps of $S^2_\infty$, which are [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493). There are isomorphisms

$$\operatorname{Mob}(S^2_\infty) \cong \operatorname{Conf}(B^3) \cong \operatorname{Isom}(\mathbf{H}^3).$$

The [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of these groups consisting of [orientation-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation-preserving) transformations are all isomorphic to the projective matrix group: PSL(2,**C**) via the usual identification of the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) with the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line) **P**<sup>1</sup>(**C**).

### Variations {#Variations}

There are some variations of the definition of a Kleinian group: sometimes Kleinian groups are allowed to be subgroups of PSL(2, **C**).2 (that is, of PSL(2, **C**) extended by complex conjugations), in other words to have orientation reversing elements, and sometimes they are assumed to be [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042), and sometimes they are required to act properly discontinuously on a non-empty open subset of the Riemann sphere.

:::

:::

## Types {#Types}

- A Kleinian group is said to be of **finite type** if its region of discontinuity has a finite number of orbits of components under the group action, and the quotient of each component by its stabilizer is a compact Riemann surface with finitely many points removed, and the covering is ramified at finitely many points.
- A Kleinian group is called **finitely generated** if it has a finite number of generators. The [Ahlfors finiteness theorem](wikipedia://en/30979615) says that such a group is of finite type.
- A Kleinian group Γ has **finite covolume** if **H**<sup>3</sup>/Γ has finite volume. Any Kleinian group of finite covolume is finitely generated.
- A Kleinian group is called **[geometrically finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrically_finite)** if it has a fundamental polyhedron (in hyperbolic 3-space) with finitely many sides. Ahlfors showed that if the limit set is not the whole Riemann sphere then it has measure 0.
- A Kleinian group Γ is called **arithmetic** if it is commensurable with the group norm 1 elements of an order of quaternion algebra *A* ramified at all real places over a number field *k* with exactly one complex place. Arithmetic Kleinian groups have finite covolume.
- A Kleinian group Γ is called **cocompact** if **H**<sup>3</sup>/Γ is compact, or equivalently SL(2, **C**)/Γ is compact. Cocompact Kleinian groups have finite covolume.
- A Kleinian group is called **topologically tame** if it is finitely generated and its hyperbolic manifold is homeomorphic to the interior of a compact manifold with boundary.
- A Kleinian group is called **geometrically tame** if its ends are either geometrically finite or simply degenerate.[^o11]
- A Kleinian group is said to be of **type 1** if the limit set is the whole Riemann sphere, and of **type 2** otherwise.

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [Maskit slice](wikipedia://en/30984876) through the [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609) of Kleinian groups

### Bianchi groups {#Bianchi_groups}

A [Bianchi group](wikipedia://en/3238478) is a Kleinian group of the form PSL(2, *O*<sub>*d*</sub>), where $\mathcal{O}_d$ is the ring of integers of the [imaginary quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_quadratic_field) $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$ for d a positive [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525).

### Elementary and reducible Kleinian groups {#Elementary_and_reducible_Kleinian_groups}

A Kleinian group is called elementary if its limit set is finite, in which case the limit set has 0, 1, or 2 points. Examples of elementary Kleinian groups include finite Kleinian groups (with empty limit set) and infinite cyclic Kleinian groups.

A Kleinian group is called reducible if all elements have a common fixed point on the Riemann sphere. Reducible Kleinian groups are elementary, but some elementary finite Kleinian groups are not reducible.

### Fuchsian groups {#Fuchsian_groups}

Any [Fuchsian group](wikipedia://en/471365) (a discrete subgroup of PSL(2, **R**)) is a Kleinian group, and conversely any Kleinian group preserving the real line (in its action on the Riemann sphere) is a Fuchsian group. More generally, every Kleinian group preserving a circle or straight line in the Riemann sphere is conjugate to a Fuchsian group.

### Koebe groups {#Koebe_groups}

- A **factor** of a Kleinian group *G* is a subgroup *H* maximal subject to the following properties:
  - *H* has a simply connected invariant component *D*
  - A conjugate of an element *h* of *H* by a conformal bijection is parabolic or elliptic if and only if *h* is.
  - Any parabolic element of *G* fixing a boundary point of *D* is in *H*.
- A Kleinian group is called a **Koebe group** if all its factors are elementary or Fuchsian.

### Quasi-Fuchsian groups {#Quasi-Fuchsian_groups}

*Image: Kleinian group limit set on sphere.svg.* Limit set of a quasi-Fuchsian group

A Kleinian group that preserves a [Jordan curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve) is called a **[quasi-Fuchsian group](wikipedia://en/30960521)**. When the Jordan curve is a circle or a straight line these are just conjugate to Fuchsian groups under conformal transformations. Finitely generated quasi-Fuchsian groups are conjugate to Fuchsian groups under quasi-conformal transformations. The limit set is contained in the invariant Jordan curve, and if it is equal to the Jordan curve the group is said to be of **the first kind**, and otherwise it is said to be of **the second kind**.

### Schottky groups {#Schottky_groups}

Let *C*<sub>i</sub> be the boundary circles of a finite collection of disjoint closed disks. The group generated by [inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_inversion) in each circle has limit set a [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172), and the quotient **H**<sup>3</sup>/*G* is a [mirror orbifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_orbifold) with underlying space a ball. It is [double covered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_cover_%28topology%29) by a [handlebody](wikipedia://en/864472); the corresponding [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 2 subgroup is a Kleinian group called a [Schottky group](wikipedia://en/5871034).

### Crystallographic groups {#Crystallographic_groups}

Let *T* be a [periodic](wikipedia://en/10779) [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) of hyperbolic 3-space. The group of symmetries of the tessellation is a Kleinian group.

### Fundamental groups of hyperbolic 3-manifolds {#Fundamental_groups_of_hyperbolic_3-manifolds}

The fundamental group of any oriented hyperbolic 3-manifold is a Kleinian group. There are many examples of these, such as the complement of a figure 8 knot or the [Seifert–Weber space](wikipedia://en/1233773). Conversely if a Kleinian group has no nontrivial torsion elements then it is the fundamental group of a hyperbolic 3-manifold.

### Degenerate Kleinian groups {#Degenerate_Kleinian_groups}

A Kleinian group is called degenerate if it is not elementary and its limit set is simply connected. Such groups can be constructed by taking a suitable limit of quasi-Fuchsian groups such that one of the two components of the regular points contracts down to the empty set; these groups are called **singly degenerate**. If both components of the regular set contract down to the empty set, then the limit set becomes a space-filling curve and the group is called **doubly degenerate**. The existence of degenerate Kleinian groups was first shown indirectly by Bers,[^o23] and the first explicit example was found by Jørgensen. (Cannon Thurston 2007)[^o25] gave examples of doubly degenerate groups and space-filling curves associated to [pseudo-Anosov maps](wikipedia://en/2327355).
