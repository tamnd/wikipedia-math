# Normalization (statistics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Normalization"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and applications of statistics, **normalization** can have a range of meanings.[^o30] In the simplest cases, **normalization of ratings** means adjusting values measured on different scales to a notionally common scale, often prior to averaging. In more complicated cases, normalization may refer to more sophisticated adjustments where the intention is to bring the entire [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) of adjusted values into alignment. In the case of **normalization of scores** in educational assessment, there may be an intention to align distributions to a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). A different approach to normalization of probability distributions is [quantile normalization](wikipedia://en/27698750), where the [quantiles](wikipedia://en/25184) of the different measures are brought into alignment.

:::

In another usage in statistics, normalization refers to the creation of shifted and scaled versions of statistics, where the intention is that these **normalized values** allow the comparison of corresponding normalized values for different datasets in a way that eliminates the effects of certain gross influences, as in an [anomaly time series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomaly_time_series). Some types of normalization involve only a rescaling, to arrive at values relative to some size variable. In terms of [levels of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels_of_measurement), such ratios only make sense for *ratio* measurements (where ratios of measurements are meaningful), not *interval* measurements (where only distances are meaningful, but not ratios).

In theoretical statistics, parametric normalization can often lead to [pivotal quantities](wikipedia://en/6081205) – functions whose [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) does not depend on the parameters – and to [ancillary statistics](wikipedia://en/670398) – pivotal quantities that can be computed from observations, without knowing parameters.

## History {#History}

### Standard score (Z-score) {#Standard_score_(Z-score)}

The concept of normalization emerged alongside the study of the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) by [Abraham De Moivre](wikipedia://en/57327), [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783), and [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) from the 18th to the 19th century. As the name “standard” refers to the particular normal distribution with expectation zero and standard deviation one, that is, the [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution), normalization, in this case, “standardization”, was then used to refer to the rescaling of any [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) or [data set](wikipedia://en/8495) to have mean zero and standard deviation one.[^o4]

While the study of normal distribution structured the process of standardization, the result of this process, also known as the [Z-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-score), given by the difference between sample value and [population mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_mean) divided by [population standard deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_standard_deviation) and measuring the number of standard deviations of a value from its population mean,[^o5] was not formalized and popularized until [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) and [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695) elaborated the concept as part of the broader framework of [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577) and [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing)[^o6][^o7] in the early 20th century.

### Student’s t-Statistic {#Student’s_t-Statistic}

[William Sealy Gosset](wikipedia://en/105651) initiated the adjustment of normal distribution and standard score on small sample size. Educated in Chemistry and Mathematics at Winchester and Oxford, Gosset was employed by [Guinness Brewery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinness_Brewery), the biggest brewer in [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland) back then, and was tasked with precise [quality control](wikipedia://en/41613). It was through small-sample experiments that Gosset discovered that the distribution of the means using small-scaled samples slightly deviated from the distribution of the means using large-scaled samples – the normal distribution – and appeared “taller and narrower” in comparison.[^o9] This finding was later published in a Guinness internal report titled *The application of the “Law of Error” to the work of the brewery* and was sent to [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695) for further discussion, which later yielded a formal publishment titled *The probable error of a mean* in the year of 1908.[^o10] Under Guinness Brewery’s privacy restrictions, Gosset published the paper under the pseudonym “Student”. Gosset’s work was later enhanced and transformed by [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) to the form that is used today,[^o11] and was, alongside the names “[Student’s t distribution](wikipedia://en/105375)” – referring to the adjusted normal distribution Gosset proposed, and “[Student’s t-statistic](wikipedia://en/22474343)” – referring to the [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975) used in measuring the departure of the estimated value of a [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065) from its hypothesized value divided by its [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520), popularized through Fisher’s publishment titled *Applications of “Student’s” distribution*.[^o9]

### Feature Scaling {#Feature_Scaling}

The rise of [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) and [multivariate statistics](wikipedia://en/19384) in mid-20th century necessitated normalization to process data with different units, hatching [feature scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_scaling) – a method used to rescale data to a fixed range – like [min-max scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_scaling) and [robust scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_scaling). This modern normalization process especially targeting large-scaled data became more formalized in fields including [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), [pattern recognition](wikipedia://en/126706), and [neural networks](wikipedia://en/21523) in late 20th century.[^o13][^o14]

### Batch Normalization {#Batch_Normalization}

Batch normalization was proposed by Sergey Ioffe and Christian Szegedy in 2015 to enhance the efficiency of training in [neural networks](wikipedia://en/21523).[^o16]

## Examples {#Examples}

There are different types of normalizations in statistics – nondimensional ratios of errors, residuals, means and [standard deviations](wikipedia://en/27590), which are hence [scale invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariant) – some of which may be summarized as follows. Note that in terms of [levels of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levels_of_measurement), these ratios only make sense for *ratio* measurements (where ratios of measurements are meaningful), not *interval* measurements (where only distances are meaningful, but not ratios). See also .

::: {#o19 .table cue=table}

| Name | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| [Standard score](wikipedia://en/221722) | $\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$ | Normalizing errors when population parameters are known. Works well for populations that are [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462)[^o21] |
| [Student's t-statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student's_t-statistic) | $\frac{\widehat\beta - \beta_0}{\operatorname{s.e.}(\widehat\beta)}$ | the departure of the estimated value of a parameter from its hypothesized value, normalized by its standard error. |
| [Studentized residual](wikipedia://en/609197) | $\frac{\hat \varepsilon_i}{\hat \sigma_i} = \frac{X_i - \hat \mu_i}{\hat \sigma_i}$ | Normalizing residuals when parameters are estimated, particularly across different data points in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997). |
| [Standardized moment](wikipedia://en/202713) | $\frac{\mu_k}{\sigma^k}$ | Normalizing moments, using the standard deviation $\sigma$ as a measure of scale. |
| [Coefficient of variation](wikipedia://en/1012687) | $\frac{\sigma}{\mu}$ | Normalizing dispersion, using the mean $\mu$ as a measure of scale, particularly for positive distribution such as the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) and [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144). |
| [Min-max feature scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_scaling#Rescaling_%28min-max_normalization%29) | $X' = \frac{X - X_{\min}}{X_{\max} - X_{\min}}$ | [Feature scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_scaling) is used to bring all values into the range \[0,1\]. This is also called unity-based normalization. This can be generalized to restrict the range of values in the dataset between any arbitrary points $a$ and $b$, using for example $X' = a + \frac{\left(X-X_{\min}\right)\left(b-a\right)}{X_{\max} - X_{\min}}$. |

:::

Note that some other ratios, such as the [variance-to-mean ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance-to-mean_ratio) $\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{\mu}\right)$, are also done for normalization, but are not nondimensional: the units do not cancel, and thus the ratio has units, and is not scale-invariant.

:::

## Other types {#Other_types}

Other non-dimensional normalizations that can be used with no assumptions on the distribution include:

- Assignment of [percentiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percentiles). This is common on standardized tests. See also [quantile normalization](wikipedia://en/27698750).
- Normalization by adding and/or multiplying by constants so values fall between 0 and 1. This is used for [probability density functions](wikipedia://en/43487), with applications in fields such as quantum mechanics in assigning probabilities to $\lvert \psi\rvert^{2}$.
