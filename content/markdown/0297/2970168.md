# Einstein relation (kinetic theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Einstein relation"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) (specifically, the [kinetic theory of gases](wikipedia://en/64204)), the **Einstein relation** is a previously unexpected connection revealed independently by [William Sutherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sutherland_%28physicist%29) in 1904,[^o2][^o3][^o4] [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) in 1905,[^o5] and by [Marian Smoluchowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_Smoluchowski) in 1906[^o6] in their works on [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436). The more general form of the equation in the classical case is[^o7]

:::

$$D = \mu \, k_\text{B} T,$$

where

- $D$ is the [diffusion coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_diffusivity);
- $\mu$ is the "mobility", or the ratio of the particle's [terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_velocity) [drift velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_velocity) to an applied [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force), $\mu = v_{d}/F$;
- $k_{B}$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant);
- $T$ is the [absolute temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_temperature).

This equation is an early example of a [fluctuation-dissipation relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluctuation_dissipation_theorem).[^o8] Note that the equation above describes the classical case and should be modified when quantum effects are relevant.

Two frequently used important special forms of the relation are:

- **Einstein–Smoluchowski equation**, for diffusion of [charged](wikipedia://en/9804) particles:[^o9] $$D = \frac{\mu_q \, k_\text{B} T}{q}$$
- **Stokes–Einstein–Sutherland equation**, for diffusion of spherical particles through a liquid with low [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856): $$D = \frac{k_\text{B} T}{6\pi\,\eta\,r}$$

Here

- $q$ is the [electrical charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_charge) of a particle;
- $\mu_{q}$ is the [electrical mobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_mobility) of the charged particle;
- $\eta$ is the dynamic [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754);
- $r$ is the [Stokes radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes_radius) of the spherical particle.

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

### Electrical mobility equation (classical case) {#Electrical_mobility_equation_(classical_case)}

For a particle with [electrical charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_charge) $q$, its [electrical mobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_mobility) $\mu_{q}$ is related to its generalized mobility $\mu$ by the equation $\mu = \mu_{q}/q$. The parameter $\mu_{q}$ is the ratio of the particle's terminal [drift velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_velocity) to an applied [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092). Hence, the equation in the case of a charged particle is given as

$$D = \frac{\mu_q \, k_\text{B} T}{q},$$

where

- $D$ is the diffusion coefficient ($\mathrm{m^2 s^{-1}}$).
- $\mu_q$ is the [electrical mobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_mobility) ($\mathrm{m^2 V^{-1} s^{-1}}$).
- $q$ is the [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) of particle (C, coulombs)
- $T$ is the electron temperature or ion temperature in plasma (K).[^o13]

If the temperature is given in [volts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt), which is more common for plasma:

$$D = \frac{\mu_q \, T}{Z},$$

where

- $Z$ is the [charge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_number) of particle (unitless)
- $T$ is electron temperature or ion temperature in plasma (V).

### Electrical mobility equation (quantum case) {#Electrical_mobility_equation_(quantum_case)}

For the case of [Fermi gas](wikipedia://en/181983) or a [Fermi liquid](wikipedia://en/182445), relevant for the electron mobility in normal metals like in the [free electron model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_electron_model), Einstein relation should be modified:

$$D = \frac{\mu_q \, E_{\mathrm F}}{q},$$

where $E_{\mathrm F}$ is [Fermi energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_energy).

### Stokes–Einstein–Sutherland equation {#Stokes–Einstein–Sutherland_equation}

In the limit of low [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856), the mobility *μ* is the inverse of the drag coefficient $\zeta$. A damping constant $\gamma = \zeta / m$ is frequently used for the inverse momentum relaxation time (time needed for the inertia momentum to become negligible compared to the random momenta) of the diffusive object. For spherical particles of radius *r*, [Stokes' law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes'_law) gives

$$\zeta = 6 \pi \, \eta \, r,$$

where $\eta$ is the [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) of the medium. Thus the Einstein–Smoluchowski relation results into the Stokes–Einstein–Sutherland relation

$$D = \frac{k_\text{B} T}{6\pi\,\eta\,r}.$$

This has been applied for many years to estimating the self-diffusion coefficient in liquids, and a version consistent with isomorph theory has been confirmed by computer simulations of the [Lennard-Jones](wikipedia://en/227686) system.[^o16]

In the case of [rotational diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_diffusion), the friction is $\zeta_\text{r} = 8 \pi \eta r^3$, and the rotational diffusion constant $D_\text{r}$ is

$$D_\text{r} = \frac{k_\text{B} T}{8\pi\,\eta\,r^3}.$$

This is sometimes referred to as the Stokes–Einstein–Debye relation.

### Semiconductor {#Semiconductor}

In a [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) with an arbitrary [density of states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_states), i.e. a relation of the form $p = p(\varphi)$ between the density of holes or electrons $p$ and the corresponding [quasi Fermi level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi_Fermi_level) (or [electrochemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_potential)) $\varphi$, the Einstein relation is[^o18][^o19]

$$D = \frac{\mu_q p}{q \frac{dp}{d\varphi}},$$

where $\mu_q$ is the [electrical mobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_mobility) (see for a proof of this relation). An example assuming a [parabolic dispersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_states#Parabolic_dispersion) relation for the density of states and the [Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics](wikipedia://en/170167), which is often used to describe [inorganic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_compound) [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor) materials, one can compute (see [density of states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_states#Density_of_states_and_distribution_functions)):

$$p(\varphi) = N_0 e^{\frac{q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}},$$

where $N_0$ is the total density of available energy states, which gives the simplified relation:

$$D = \mu_q \frac{k_\text{B} T}{q}.$$

### Nernst–Einstein equation {#Nernst–Einstein_equation}

By replacing the diffusivities in the expressions of electric ionic mobilities of the cations and anions from the expressions of the [equivalent conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_conductivity) of an electrolyte the Nernst–Einstein equation is derived:

$$\Lambda_e = \frac{z_i^2 F^2}{RT}(D_+ + D_-).$$

were *R* is the [gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant).

:::

## Proof of the general case {#Proof_of_the_general_case}

The proof of the Einstein relation can be found in many references, for example see the work of [Ryogo Kubo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryogo_Kubo).[^o23] The following derives it from the steady state of the [convection-diffusion equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection-diffusion_equation#Velocity_in_response_to_a_force) with a velocity proportional to a conservative force.

Suppose some fixed, external [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) $U$ generates a [conservative force](wikipedia://en/44158) $F(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla U(\mathbf{x})$ (for example, an electric force) on a particle located at a given position $\mathbf{x}$. We assume that the particle would respond by moving with velocity $v(\mathbf{x})=\mu(\mathbf{x}) F(\mathbf{x})$ (see [Drag (physics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29)). Now assume that there are a large number of such particles, with local concentration $\rho(\mathbf{x})$ as a function of the position. After some time, equilibrium will be established: particles will pile up around the areas with lowest potential energy $U$, but still will be spread out to some extent because of [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion). At equilibrium, there is no net flow of particles: the tendency of particles to get pulled towards lower $U$, called the *drift current*, perfectly balances the tendency of particles to spread out due to diffusion, called the *diffusion current*.

The net flux of particles due to the drift current is

$$\mathbf{J}_\mathrm{drift}(\mathbf{x}) = \mu(\mathbf{x}) F(\mathbf{x}) \rho(\mathbf{x}) = -\rho(\mathbf{x}) \mu(\mathbf{x}) \nabla U(\mathbf{x}),$$

i.e., the number of particles flowing past a given position equals the particle concentration times the average velocity.

The flow of particles due to the diffusion current is, by [Fick's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fick's_law),

$$\mathbf{J}_\mathrm{diffusion}(\mathbf{x})=-D(\mathbf{x}) \nabla \rho(\mathbf{x}),$$

where the minus sign means that particles flow from higher to lower concentration.

Now consider the equilibrium condition. First, there is no net flow, i.e. $\mathbf{J}_\mathrm{drift} + \mathbf{J}_\mathrm{diffusion} = 0$. Second, for non-interacting point particles, the equilibrium density $\rho$ is solely a function of the local potential energy $U$, i.e. if two locations have the same $U$ then they will also have the same $\rho$ (e.g. see [Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell-Boltzmann_statistics) as discussed below.) That means, applying the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113),

$$\nabla\rho = \frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d} U} \nabla U.$$

Therefore, at equilibrium:

$$0 = \mathbf{J}_\mathrm{drift} + \mathbf{J}_\mathrm{diffusion} = -\mu \rho \nabla U - D \nabla \rho = \left(-\mu \rho - D \frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d} U}\right)\nabla U.$$

As this expression holds at every position $\mathbf{x}$, it implies the general form of the Einstein relation:

$$D = -\mu \frac{\rho}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d} U}}.$$

The relation between $\rho$ and $U$ for [classical particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics) can be modeled through [Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell-Boltzmann_statistics)

$$\rho(\mathbf{x}) = A e^{-\frac{U(\mathbf{x})}{k_\text{B} T}},$$

where $A$ is a constant related to the total number of particles. Therefore

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d} U} = -\frac{1}{k_\text{B} T}\rho.$$

Under this assumption, plugging this equation into the general Einstein relation gives:

$$D = -\mu \frac{\rho}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d} U}} = \mu k_\text{B} T,$$

which corresponds to the classical Einstein relation.
