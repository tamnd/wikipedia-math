# Mary Ellen Rudin

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mary Ellen Rudin"}

**Mary Ellen Rudin** (December 7, 1924 – March 18, 2013)[^o3] was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) known for her work in [set-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set-theoretic) [topology](wikipedia://en/29954).[^o4] In 2013, [Elsevier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elsevier) established the [Mary Ellen Rudin Young Researcher Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Ellen_Rudin_Young_Researcher_Award), which is awarded annually to a young researcher, mainly in fields adjacent to [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649).[^o5]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Mary Ellen (Estill) Rudin was born in [Hillsboro, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillsboro,_Texas) to Joe Jefferson Estill and Irene (Shook) Estill. Her mother Irene was an English teacher before marriage, and her father Joe was a civil engineer. The family moved with her father's work, but spent a great deal of Mary Ellen's childhood around [Leakey, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leakey,_Texas).[^o7] She had one sibling, a younger brother. Both of Rudin's maternal grandmothers had attended [Mary Sharp College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Sharp_College) near their hometown of [Winchester, Tennessee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winchester,_Tennessee). Rudin remarks on this legacy and how much her family valued education in an interview.[^o7]

She attended the [University of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin), completing her B.A. in 1944 after just three years before moving into the graduate program in mathematics under [Robert Lee Moore](wikipedia://en/1077133). Her graduate thesis presented a counterexample to one of "Moore's axioms". She completed her Ph.D. in 1949.

During her time as an undergraduate, she was a member of the [Phi Mu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Mu) Women's Fraternity,[^o9] and was elected to the [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa) society.[^o10]

In 1953, she married mathematician [Walter Rudin](wikipedia://en/5491766), whom she met while teaching at [Duke University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_University). They had four children.

## Career {#Career}

At the beginning of her career, Rudin taught at Duke University and the [University of Rochester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rochester).[^o12] She took a position as lecturer at the [University of Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin_at_Madison) in 1959, and was appointed Professor of Mathematics in 1971. After her retirement in 1991, she continued to serve as a Professor Emerita. She was the first [Grace Chisholm Young](wikipedia://en/239997) Professor of Mathematics and also held the Hilidale Professorship.[^o7][^o12]

She was an Invited Speaker of the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in 1974 in Vancouver.[^o13] She served as vice-president of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822), 1980–1981. In 1984 she was selected to be a [Noether Lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether_Lecturer). She was an honorary member of the [Hungarian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Academy_of_Sciences) (1995). In 2012 she became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o14]

Rudin is best known in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) for her constructions of counterexamples to well-known conjectures. In 1958, she found an [unshellable](wikipedia://en/32457161) [triangulation](wikipedia://en/1440970) of the [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606).[^o15] Most famously, Rudin was the first to construct a [Dowker space](wikipedia://en/12037102),[^o16] which she did in 1971, thus disproving a conjecture of [Clifford Hugh Dowker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Hugh_Dowker) that had stood, and helped drive topological research, for more than twenty years. Her example fueled the search for "small" [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC) Dowker spaces. She, Keiko Chiba, and C. Przymusiński also proved the first [Morita conjecture](wikipedia://en/12034549) and a restricted version of the second.[^o17] Her last major result was a proof of [Nikiel's conjecture](wikipedia://en/60682233).[^o18] Early proofs that every [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact) were somewhat involved, but Rudin provided an elementary one.[^o19]

"Reading the articles of Mary Ellen Rudin, studying them until there is no mystery takes hours and hours; but those hours are rewarded, the student obtains power to which few have access. They are not hard to read, they are just hard mathematics, that's all." (Steve Watson[^o20])

## Later life {#Later_life}

Rudin resided in [Madison, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madison,_Wisconsin), in the [Rudin House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Rudin_House), a home designed by architect [Frank Lloyd Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Lloyd_Wright). She died aged 88 on March 18, 2013.

## Mary Ellen Rudin Young Researcher Award {#Mary_Ellen_Rudin_Young_Researcher_Award}

The **Mary Ellen Rudin Young Researcher Award** is an annual award given to young researchers in [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649) and its related fields.[^o27] It was established in 2013 by [Elsevier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elsevier) on behalf of the journal *[Topology and its Applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_and_its_Applications)* and consists of US\$15,000 that must be used by the awardee in the following way: US\$5,000 for three major conferences in topology, US\$5,000 for visiting a research center, and US\$5,000, which can be used freely and is regarded as a cash prize.

The prize was named after Rudin. Rudin gave her permission to use her name for the award but died before the first prize was awarded.[^o28][^o29]
