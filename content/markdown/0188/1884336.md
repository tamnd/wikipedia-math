# Wigner quasiprobability distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wigner quasiprobability distribution"}

*Image: Wignerfunction catstate even 2.0.png.* Wigner function of a so-called [cat state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cat_state)

The **Wigner quasiprobability distribution**, also called the **Wigner function** or the **Wigner–Ville distribution**, after [Eugene Wigner](wikipedia://en/10101) and [Jean-André Ville](wikipedia://en/56194638), is a [quasiprobability distribution](wikipedia://en/4518807), similar to the [Margenau-Hill quasiprobability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margenau-Hill_quasiprobability_distribution) and the Kirkwood–Dirac quasiprobability distribution. It was introduced by Eugene Wigner in 1932[^o2] to study [quantum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum) corrections to classical [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481). The goal was to link the [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) that appears in the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) to a probability distribution in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).

:::

It is a [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) for all spatial [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) functions of a given quantum-mechanical wavefunction $\psi(x)$. Thus, it maps[^o3] on the quantum [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) in the map between real phase-space functions and [Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian) operators introduced by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) in 1927,[^o4] in a context related to [representation theory](wikipedia://en/12695) in mathematics (see [Weyl quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_quantization)). In effect, it is the [Wigner–Weyl transform](wikipedia://en/4277086) of the density matrix, so the realization of that operator in phase space.

It has applications in [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry), [quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_optics), and classical [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics). Via the [Wigner distribution function](wikipedia://en/4110803) in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), it has further applications in diverse fields such as [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), [seismology](wikipedia://en/28935), [time–frequency analysis for music signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time–frequency_analysis_for_music_signals), [spectrograms](wikipedia://en/263317) in [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) and speech processing, and [engine design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall_plot).

## Relation to classical mechanics {#Relation_to_classical_mechanics}

A classical particle has a definite position and momentum, and hence it is represented by a point in phase space. Given a collection ([ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_ensemble_%28mathematical_physics%29)) of particles, the probability of finding a particle at a certain position in phase space is specified by a probability distribution, the Liouville density. This strict interpretation fails for a quantum particle, due to the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883). Instead, the above quasiprobability Wigner distribution plays an analogous role, but does not satisfy all the properties of a conventional probability distribution; and, conversely, satisfies boundedness properties unavailable to classical distributions.

For instance, the Wigner distribution can and normally does take on negative values for states which have no classical model—and is a convenient indicator of quantum-mechanical interference. (See below for a characterization of pure states whose Wigner functions are non-negative.) Smoothing the Wigner distribution through a filter of size larger than $\text{ħ}$ (e.g., convolving with a phase-space Gaussian, a [Weierstrass transform](wikipedia://en/17467553), to yield the Husimi representation, below), results in a positive-semidefinite function, i.e., it may be thought to have been coarsened to a semi-classical one.

Regions of such negative value are provable (by convolving them with a small Gaussian) to be "small": they cannot extend to compact regions larger than a few $\text{ħ}$, and hence disappear in the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit). They are shielded by the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883), which does not allow precise location within phase-space regions smaller than $\text{ħ}$, and thus renders such "[negative probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_probability)" less paradoxical.

## Definition and meaning {#Definition_and_meaning}

The Wigner distribution $W(x,p)$ of a pure state is defined as

where $\psi$ is the wavefunction, and $x$ and $p$ are position and momentum, but could be any conjugate variable pair (e.g. real and imaginary parts of the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) or frequency and time of a signal). Note that it may have support in $x$ even in regions where $\psi$ has no support in $x$ ("beats").

It is symmetric in $x$ and $p$:

$$W(x, p) = \frac{1}{\pi\hbar} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \varphi^*(p + q) \varphi(p - q) e^{-2ixq/\hbar} \,dq,$$

where $\varphi$ is the normalized momentum-space wave function, proportional to the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of $\psi$.

In 3D,

$$W(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{p}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3} \int \psi^*{\!\left(\mathbf{r} + \tfrac{\hbar}{2}\mathbf{s}\right)} \psi{\left(\mathbf{r} - \tfrac{\hbar}{2} \mathbf{s}\right)} e^{i\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{s}} \,d^3 s.$$

In the general case, which includes mixed states, it is the Wigner transform of the [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844):

$$W(x, p) = \frac{1}{\pi\hbar} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \langle x - y| \hat{\rho} |x + y \rangle e^{2ipy/\hbar} \,dy,$$

where ⟨*x*|*ψ*⟩ = $\psi(x)$. This Wigner transformation (or map) is the inverse of the [Weyl transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_quantization), which maps phase-space functions to [Hilbert-space](wikipedia://en/20598932) operators, in [Weyl quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_quantization). Thus, the Wigner function is the cornerstone of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).

In second quantization the phase-space operators are $x=(a+a^\dagger)/\sqrt{2}$ and $p=-i(a-a^\dagger)/\sqrt{2}$ where the Wigner function is

$$W(x,p) = \frac{1}{\pi\hbar} \mathrm{Tr}[\rho D(\alpha) \Pi D^\dagger(\alpha)]$$

for [parity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29) $\Pi=e^{i\pi a^\dagger a}$ and [displacement operator](wikipedia://en/5122241) $D(\alpha)=e^{\alpha a^\dagger - \alpha^* a}$ with $\alpha=(x+ip)/\sqrt{2}$.

In 1949, [José Enrique Moyal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Enrique_Moyal) elucidated how the Wigner function provides the integration measure (analogous to a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487)) in phase space, to yield [expectation values](wikipedia://en/9653) from phase-space [c-number](wikipedia://en/13004986) functions $g(x, p)$ uniquely associated to suitably ordered operators $\text{Ĝ}$ through Weyl's transform (see [Wigner–Weyl transform](wikipedia://en/4277086) and property 7 below), in a manner evocative of classical [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542).

Specifically, an operator's $\text{Ĝ}$ expectation value is a "phase-space average" of the Wigner transform of that operator:

$$\langle \hat{G} \rangle = \int dx\,dp\, W(x, p) g(x, p).$$

## Mathematical properties {#Mathematical_properties}

*Image: Number state Wigner function.png.* Wigner function for number states a) *n* = 0, b) *n* = 1, and c) *n* = 19. Marginal distributions for *x* and *p* are recovered by integrating over *p* and *x* respectively.

1. $W(x, p)$ is a real-valued function.
1. The $x$ and $p$ probability distributions are given by the [marginals](wikipedia://en/506077): $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\, W(x, p) = \langle x|\hat{\rho}|x \rangle.$$ $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty dx\, W(x, p) = \langle p|\hat{\rho}|p \rangle.$$ If the system can be described by a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state), one gets $\int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\, W(x, p) = |\psi(x)|^2$ and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dx\, W(x, p) = |\varphi(p)|^2.$ $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty dx \int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\, W(x, p) = \operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}).$$ Typically the trace of the density matrix $\hat{\rho}$ is equal to 1.
1. *W*(*x*, *p*) has the following reflection symmetries:
   - Time symmetry: $\psi(x) \to \psi(x)^* \Rightarrow W(x, p) \to W(x, -p).$
   - Space symmetry: $\psi(x) \to \psi(-x) \Rightarrow W(x, p) \to W(-x, -p).$
1. *W*(*x*, *p*) is [Galilei-covariant](wikipedia://en/295183): $$\psi(x) \to \psi(x + y) \Rightarrow W(x, p) \to W(x + y, p).$$ It is not [Lorentz-covariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz-covariant).
1. The equation of motion for each point in the phase space is classical in the absence of forces: $$\frac{\partial W(x, p)}{\partial t} = \frac{-p}{m} \frac{\partial W(x, p)}{\partial x}.$$ In fact, it is classical even in the presence of harmonic forces.
1. State overlap is calculated as $$|\langle \psi|\theta \rangle|^2 = 2\pi\hbar \int_{-\infty}^\infty dx \int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\, W_\psi(x, p) W_\theta(x, p).$$
1. Operator expectation values (averages) are calculated as phase-space averages of the respective Wigner transforms: $$g(x, p) \equiv \int_{-\infty}^\infty dy\, \left\langle x - \frac{y}{2} \right| \hat{G} \left| x + \frac{y}{2} \right\rangle e^{ipy/\hbar},$$ $$\langle \psi|\hat{G}|\psi\rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho} \hat{G}) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty dx \int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\, W(x, p) g(x, p).$$
1. For $W(x, p)$ to represent physical (positive) density matrices, it must satisfy $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty dx\, \int_{-\infty}^\infty dp\, W(x, p) W_\theta(x, p) \ge 0$$ for all pure states $\lvert \theta\rangle$.
1. By virtue of the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128), for a pure state, it is constrained to be bounded: $$-\frac 2 h \leq W(x, p) \leq \frac 2 h.$$ This bound disappears in the classical limit, *ħ* → 0. In this limit, *W*(*x*, *p*) reduces to the probability density in coordinate space *x*, usually highly localized, multiplied by [δ-functions](wikipedia://en/37021) in momentum: the classical limit is "spiky". Thus, this quantum-mechanical bound precludes a Wigner function which is a perfectly localized δ-function in phase space, as a reflection of the uncertainty principle.[^o9]
1. The Wigner transformation is simply the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of the [antidiagonals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antidiagonal) of the density matrix, when that matrix is expressed in a position basis.[^o10]

## Examples {#Examples}

Let $|m\rangle \equiv \frac{a^{\dagger m}}{\sqrt{m!}} |0\rangle$ be the $m$-th [Fock state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fock_state) of a [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719). Groenewold (1946) discovered its associated Wigner function, in dimensionless variables:

$$W_{|m\rangle}(x, p) = \frac{(-1)^m}{\pi} e^{-(x^2 + p^2)} L_m\big(2(p^2 + x^2)\big),$$

where $L_m(x)$ denotes the $m$-th [Laguerre polynomial](wikipedia://en/943917).

This may follow from the expression for the static eigenstate wavefunctions,

$$u_m(x) = \pi^{-1/4} H_m(x) e^{-x^2/2},$$

where $H_m$ is the $m$-th [Hermite polynomial](wikipedia://en/195984). From the above definition of the Wigner function, upon a change of integration variables,

$$W_{|m\rangle}(x, p) = \frac{(-1)^m}{\pi^{3/2} 2^m m!} e^{-x^2 - p^2} \int_{-\infty}^\infty d\zeta\, e^{-\zeta^2} H_m(\zeta - ip + x) H_m(\zeta - ip - x).$$

The expression then follows from the integral relation between Hermite and Laguerre polynomials.[^o13]

:::

## Evolution equation {#Evolution_equation}

The **Wigner transformation** is a general invertible transformation of an operator $\text{Ĝ}$ on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) to a function $g(x, p)$ on [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) and is given by

$$g(x, p) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty ds\, e^{ips/\hbar} \left\langle x - \tfrac s2\right| \hat G \left|x + \tfrac s2\right\rangle.$$

Hermitian operators map to real functions. The inverse of this transformation, from phase space to Hilbert space, is called the **[Weyl transformation](wikipedia://en/4277086)**:

$$\langle x | \hat G | y \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{dp}{h} e^{ip(x - y)/\hbar} g{\left(\frac{x + y}{2}, p\right)}$$

(not to be confused with the distinct [Weyl transformation in differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_transformation)).

The **Wigner function** $W(x, p)$ discussed here is thus seen to be the Wigner transform of the [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) operator $\rho\text{̂}$. Thus the trace of an operator with the density matrix Wigner-transforms to the equivalent phase-space integral overlap of $g(x, p)$ with the Wigner function.

The Wigner transform of the [von Neumann evolution equation](wikipedia://en/62844) of the density matrix in the [Schrödinger picture](wikipedia://en/419200) is **Moyal's evolution equation** for the Wigner function:

where $H(x, p)$ is the Hamiltonian, and {{⋅, ⋅}} is the [Moyal bracket](wikipedia://en/8875186). In the classical limit, $\text{ħ} \to 0$, the Moyal bracket reduces to the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340), while this evolution equation reduces to the [Liouville equation](wikipedia://en/312301#Poisson_bracket) of classical statistical mechanics.

Formally, the classical Liouville equation can be solved in terms of the phase-space particle trajectories which are solutions of the classical Hamilton equations. This technique of solving partial differential equations is known as the [method of characteristics](wikipedia://en/751933). This method transfers to quantum systems, where the characteristics' "trajectories" now determine the evolution of Wigner functions. The solution of the Moyal evolution equation for the Wigner function is represented formally as

$$W(x, p, t) = W\big(\star\big(x_{-t}(x, p), p_{-t}(x, p)\big), 0\big),$$

where $x_t(x, p)$ and $p_t(x, p)$ are the characteristic trajectories subject to the [quantum Hamilton equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_quantum_characteristics) with initial conditions $x_{t=0}(x, p) = x$ and $p_{t=0}(x, p) = p$, and where [$\star$-product](wikipedia://en/1946702) composition is understood for all argument functions.

Since $\star$-composition of functions is *thoroughly nonlocal* (the "quantum probability fluid" diffuses, as observed by Moyal), vestiges of local trajectories in quantum systems are barely discernible in the evolution of the Wigner distribution function. In the integral representation of $\star$-products, successive operations by them have been adapted to a phase-space path integral, to solve the evolution equation for the Wigner function[^o16] (see also [^o17][^o18][^o19]). This non-local feature of Moyal time evolution[^o20] is illustrated in the gallery below, for Hamiltonians more complex than the harmonic oscillator. In the classical limit, the trajectory nature of the time evolution of Wigner functions becomes more and more distinct. At *ħ* = 0, the characteristics' trajectories reduce to the classical trajectories of particles in phase space.

## Classical limit {#Classical_limit}

The Wigner function allows one to study the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit), offering a comparison of the classical and [quantum dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_dynamics) in phase space.[^o23][^o24]

It has been suggested that the Wigner function approach can be viewed as a quantum analogy to the operatorial formulation of classical mechanics introduced in 1932 by [Bernard Koopman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Koopman) and [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942): the time evolution of the Wigner function approaches, in the limit *ħ* → 0, the time evolution of the [Koopman–von Neumann wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koopman–von_Neumann_wavefunction) of a classical particle.[^o25]

Moments of the Wigner function generate symmetrized operator averages, in contrast to the normal order and antinormal order generated by the [Glauber–Sudarshan P representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glauber–Sudarshan_P_representation) and [Husimi Q representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husimi_Q_representation) respectively. The Wigner representation is thus very well suited for making semi-classical approximations in quantum optics[^o26] and field theory of Bose-Einstein condensates where high mode occupation approaches a semiclassical limit.[^o27]

## Positivity of the function {#Positivity_of_the_function}

As already noted, the Wigner function of quantum state typically takes some negative values. Indeed, for a pure state in one variable, if $W(x, p) \ge 0$ for all $x$ and $p$, then the wave function must have the form

$$\psi(x) = e^{-ax^2+bx+c}$$

for some complex numbers $a, b, c$ with $\operatorname{Re}(a) > 0$ (Hudson's theorem[^o29]). Note that $a$ is allowed to be complex. In other words, it is a one-dimensional [gaussian wave packet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_packet#quantum_mechanics). Thus, pure states with non-negative Wigner functions are not necessarily minimum-uncertainty states in the sense of the [Heisenberg uncertainty formula](wikipedia://en/31883); rather, they give equality in the [Schrödinger uncertainty formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robertson–Schrodinger_relation), which includes an anticommutator term in addition to the commutator term. (With careful definition of the respective variances, all pure-state Wigner functions lead to Heisenberg's inequality all the same.)

In higher dimensions, the characterization of pure states with non-negative Wigner functions is similar; the wave function must have the form

$$\psi(x) = e^{-(x,Ax)+b\cdot x+c},$$

where $A$ is a symmetric complex matrix whose real part is positive-definite, $b$ is a complex vector, and $c$ is a complex number.[^o30] The Wigner function of any such state is a Gaussian distribution on phase space.

Francisco Soto and Pierre Claverie[^o30] give an elegant proof of this characterization, using the [Segal–Bargmann transform](wikipedia://en/40731477#The_Segal–Bargmann_transform). The reasoning is as follows. The [Husimi Q function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husimi_Q_representation) of $\psi$ may be computed as the squared magnitude of the Segal–Bargmann transform of $\psi$, multiplied by a Gaussian. Meanwhile, the Husimi Q function is the convolution of the Wigner function with a Gaussian. If the Wigner function of $\psi$ is non-negative everywhere on phase space, then the Husimi Q function will be strictly positive everywhere on phase space. Thus, the Segal–Bargmann transform $F(x + ip)$ of $\psi$ will be nowhere zero. Thus, by a standard result from complex analysis, we have

$$F(x + ip) = e^{g(x+ip)}$$

for some [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) $g$. But in order for $F$ to belong to the [Segal–Bargmann space](wikipedia://en/40731477)—that is, for $F$ to be square-integrable with respect to a Gaussian measure—$g$ must have at most quadratic growth at infinity. From this, elementary complex analysis can be used to show that $g$ must actually be a quadratic polynomial. Thus, we obtain an explicit form of the Segal–Bargmann transform of any pure state whose Wigner function is non-negative. We can then invert the Segal–Bargmann transform to obtain the claimed form of the position wave function.

There does not appear to be any simple characterization of [mixed states](wikipedia://en/62844) with non-negative Wigner functions.

## Interpretations of quantum mechanics {#Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics}

It has been shown that the Wigner quasiprobability distribution function can be regarded as an $\text{ħ}$-[deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) of another phase-space distribution function that describes an ensemble of [de Broglie–Bohm](wikipedia://en/54717) causal trajectories.[^o32] [Basil Hiley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Hiley) has shown that the quasi-probability distribution may be understood as the [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) re-expressed in terms of a mean position and momentum of a "cell" in phase space, and the de Broglie–Bohm interpretation allows one to describe the dynamics of the centers of such "cells".[^o33][^o34]

There is a close connection between the description of quantum states in terms of the Wigner function and a method of quantum states reconstruction in terms of [mutually unbiased bases](wikipedia://en/20191641).[^o35]

## Use outside quantum mechanics {#Use_outside_quantum_mechanics}

*Image: Chirpedpulse.jpg.* A contour plot of the Wigner–Ville distribution for a [chirped](wikipedia://en/65635) pulse of light. The plot makes it obvious that the frequency is a linear function of time.

- In the modelling of optical systems such as telescopes or fibre telecommunications devices, the Wigner function is used to bridge the gap between simple [ray tracing](wikipedia://en/17003295) and the full wave analysis of the system. Here $p/\text{ħ}$ is replaced with $k = |k| \sin \theta \approx |k|\theta$ in the small-angle (paraxial) approximation. In this context, the Wigner function is the closest one can get to describing the system in terms of rays at position $x$ and angle $\theta$ while still including the effects of interference.[^o37] If it becomes negative at any point, then simple ray tracing will not suffice to model the system. That is to say, negative values of this function are a symptom of the [Gabor limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabor_limit) of the classical light signal and *not* of quantum features of light associated with $\text{ħ}$.
- In [signal analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_analysis), a time-varying electrical signal, mechanical vibration, or sound wave are represented by a [Wigner function](wikipedia://en/4110803). Here, $x$ is replaced with the time, and $p/\text{ħ}$ is replaced with the angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$, where $f$ is the regular frequency.
- In [ultrafast optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrafast_optics), short laser pulses are characterized with the Wigner function using the same $f$ and $t$ substitutions as above. Pulse defects such as [chirp](wikipedia://en/65635) (the change in frequency with time) can be visualized with the Wigner function. See adjacent figure.
- In quantum optics, $x$ and $p/\text{ħ}$ are replaced with the $X$ and $P$ quadratures, the real and imaginary components of the electric field (see [coherent state](wikipedia://en/277213)).

## Other related quasiprobability distributions {#Other_related_quasiprobability_distributions}

The Wigner distribution was the first quasiprobability distribution to be formulated, but many more followed, formally equivalent and transformable to and from it (see [Transformation between distributions in time–frequency analysis](wikipedia://en/25848944)). As in the case of coordinate systems, on account of varying properties, several such have with various advantages for specific applications:

- [Glauber P representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glauber_P_representation)
- [Husimi Q representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husimi_Q_representation)

Nevertheless, in some sense, the Wigner distribution holds a privileged position among all these distributions, since it is the *only one* whose requisite star-product drops out (integrates out by parts to effective unity) in the evaluation of expectation values, as illustrated above, and so *can* be visualized as a quasiprobability measure analogous to the classical ones.

## History {#History}

section The Wigner function was independently derived several times in different contexts. It was introduced by Eugene Wigner in 1932.[^o2] Eugene Wigner was unaware that even within the context of quantum theory, it had been introduced a couple of years before by [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg) and [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742),[^o41][^o42] albeit purely formally: these two missed its significance, and that of its negative values, as they merely considered it as an approximation to the full quantum description of a system such as the atom.

It was later rederived by Jean Ville in 1948 as a quadratic (in signal) [representation of the local time-frequency energy of a signal](wikipedia://en/4110803),[^o43] effectively a [spectrogram](wikipedia://en/263317).

In 1949, [José Enrique Moyal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Enrique_Moyal), who had derived it independently, recognized it as the [quantum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum) moment-generating functional,[^o44] and thus as the basis of an elegant encoding of all quantum expectation values, and hence quantum mechanics, in phase space (see [Phase-space formulation](wikipedia://en/36053570)).

In most of his correspondence with Moyal in the mid-1940s, Dirac was unaware that Moyal's quantum-moment generating function was effectively the Wigner function, and it was Moyal who finally brought it to his attention.[^o45]
