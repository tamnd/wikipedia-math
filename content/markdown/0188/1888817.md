# Central force

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Central force"}

*Image: Centralkraft.png.* A diagram of Central forces

In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), a **central force** on an object is a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_%28physics%29) that is directed towards or away from a point called **center of force**.

$$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = F( \mathbf{r} ) {\hat{\mathbf{r}}}$$

where $\mathbf{F}$ is a force vector, $F$ is a scalar valued force function (whose absolute value gives the magnitude of the force and is positive if the force is outward and negative if the force is inward), $\mathbf{r}$ is the [position vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_vector), $\|\mathbf{r}\|$ is its length, and $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf r / \|\mathbf r\|$ is the corresponding [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053).[^o2]p. 93

:::

Not all central force fields are [conservative](wikipedia://en/44158) or [spherically symmetric](wikipedia://en/2217599#Spherical_symmetry). However, a central force is conservative if and only if it is spherically symmetric or rotationally invariant.[^o2]p. 133–38 Examples of spherically symmetric central forces include the [Coulomb force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_force) and the force of gravity.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Central forces that are conservative can always be expressed as the negative [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of a [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703):

$$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) = - \mathbf{\nabla} V(\mathbf{r}) \; \text{, where } V(\mathbf{r}) = \int_{|\mathbf{r}|}^{+\infty} F(r)\,\mathrm{d}r$$

(the upper bound of integration is arbitrary, as the potential is defined up to an additive constant).

In a conservative field, the total [mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy) ([kinetic](wikipedia://en/17327) and potential) is conserved:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2} m |\mathbf{\dot{r}}|^2 + \tfrac{1}{2} I |\boldsymbol{\omega}|^2 + V(\mathbf{r}) = \text{constant}$$

(where $\dot{r}$ denotes the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of $r$ with respect to time, that is the [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903), $I$ denotes [moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700) of that body and $\omega$ denotes [angular velocity](wikipedia://en/65927)), and in a central force field, so is the [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839):

$$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{\dot{r}} = \text{constant}$$

because the [torque](wikipedia://en/30400) exerted by the force is zero. As a consequence, the body moves on the plane perpendicular to the angular momentum vector and containing the origin, and obeys [Kepler's second law](wikipedia://en/17553). (If the angular momentum is zero, the body moves along the line joining it with the origin.)

It can also be shown that an object that moves under the influence of *any* central force obeys Kepler's second law. However, the first and third laws depend on the inverse-square nature of [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) and do not hold in general for other central forces.

As a consequence of being conservative, these specific central force fields are irrotational, that is, its [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) is zero, *except at the origin*:

$$\nabla\times\mathbf{F} (\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{0} .$$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Gravitational force and [Coulomb force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_force) are two familiar examples with $F( \mathbf{r} )$ being [proportional to $1/r^{2}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law) only. An object in such a force field with negative $F( \mathbf{r} )$ (corresponding to an attractive force) obeys [Kepler's laws of planetary motion](wikipedia://en/17553).

The force field of a spatial [harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/13899) is central with $F( \mathbf{r} )$ proportional to *r* only and negative.

By [Bertrand's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand's_theorem), these two, $F( \mathbf{r} ) = -k/r^2$and $F( \mathbf{r} ) = -kr$, are the only possible central force fields where all bounded orbits are stable closed orbits. However, there exist other force fields, which have some closed orbits.
