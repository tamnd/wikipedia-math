# Fourier–Bros–Iagolnitzer transform

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fourier–Bros–Iagolnitzer transform"}

substantially different definitions in the literature

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **FBI transform** or **Fourier–Bros–Iagolnitzer transform** is a generalization of the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) developed by the French [mathematical physicists](wikipedia://en/173416) Jacques Bros and Daniel Iagolnitzer in order to characterise the [local analyticity](wikipedia://en/61478) of functions (or [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29)) on **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. The transform provides an alternative approach to analytic [wave front sets](wikipedia://en/1904007) of distributions, developed independently by the Japanese mathematicians [Mikio Sato](wikipedia://en/373336), [Masaki Kashiwara](wikipedia://en/4376209) and Takahiro Kawai in their approach to [microlocal analysis](wikipedia://en/6108841). It can also be used to prove the analyticity of solutions of analytic [elliptic partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/662787) as well as a version of the classical uniqueness theorem, strengthening the [Cauchy–Kowalevski theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy–Kowalevski_theorem), due to the Swedish mathematician [Erik Albert Holmgren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Albert_Holmgren) (1872–1943).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of a [Schwartz function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_function) *f* in *S*(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>) is defined by

$$({\mathcal F}f)(t) = (2\pi)^{-n / 2} \int_{{\mathbf R}^n}f(x) e^{-ix \cdot t}\, dx.$$

The **FBI transform** of *f* is defined for *a* ≥ 0 by

$$({\mathcal F}_a f)(t,y) = (2\pi)^{-n / 2} \int_{{\mathbf R}^n}f(x)e^{-a |x-y|^2/2} e^{-ix \cdot t}\, dx.$$

Thus, when *a* = 0, it essentially coincides with the Fourier transform.

The same formulas can be used to define the Fourier and FBI transforms of [tempered distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Tempered_distributions_and_Fourier_transform) in *S**(**R**<sup>*n*</sup>).***

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Inversion formula"}

## Inversion formula {#Inversion_formula}

The [Fourier inversion formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_inversion_formula)

$$f(x)= {\mathcal F}^2 f(-x)$$

allows a function *f* to be recovered from its Fourier transform.

In particular

$$f(x)= (2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{{\mathbf R}^n} e^{i t \cdot x}{\mathcal F}(f)(t) \, dt$$

Similarly, at a positive value of *a*, *f*(0) can be recovered from the FBI transform of *f*(*x*) by the inversion formula

$$f(x)= (2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{{\mathbf R}^n} e^{i t \cdot x}e^{a|x-y|^2/2}{\mathcal F}_a (f)(t,y) \, dt$$

:::

::: {#o7 .statement cue=named-heading name="Criterion for local analyticity"}

## Criterion for local analyticity {#Criterion_for_local_analyticity}

Bros and Iagolnitzer showed that a distribution *f* is locally equal to a [real analytic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_analytic_function) at *y*, in the direction *ξ* if and only if its FBI transform satisfies an inequality of the form

$$|({\mathcal F}_{|\xi|}f)(\xi,y)| \le C e^{-\varepsilon |\xi|},$$

for *\|ξ|* sufficiently large.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Holmgren's uniqueness theorem"}

## Holmgren's uniqueness theorem {#Holmgren's_uniqueness_theorem}

A simple consequence of the Bros and Iagolnitzer characterisation of local analyticity is the following regularity result of [Lars Hörmander](wikipedia://en/500958) and [Mikio Sato](wikipedia://en/373336).[^o10]

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.** Let *P* be an [elliptic partial differential operator](wikipedia://en/662787) with analytic coefficients defined on an open subset *X* of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. If *Pf* is analytic in *X*, then so too is *f*.

When "analytic" is replaced by "smooth" in this theorem, the result is just [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544)'s classical lemma on [elliptic regularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoellipticity), usually proved using [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964).[^o12] It is a special case of more general results involving the analytic [wave front set](wikipedia://en/1904007) (see below), which imply Holmgren's classical strengthening of the [Cauchy–Kowalevski theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy–Kowalevski_theorem) on linear [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) with real analytic coefficients. In modern language, Holmgren's uniqueness theorem states that any distributional solution of such a system of equations must be analytic and therefore unique, by the Cauchy–Kowalevski theorem.

:::

:::

## The analytic wave front set {#The_analytic_wave_front_set}

The **[analytic wave front set](wikipedia://en/1904007)** or **singular spectrum** WF<sub>A</sub>(*f*) of a [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) *f* (or more generally of a [hyperfunction](wikipedia://en/1678626)) can be defined in terms of the FBI transform[^o14] as the complement of the conical set of points (*x*, λ ξ) (λ > 0) such that the FBI transform satisfies the Bros–Iagolnitzer inequality

$$|({\mathcal F}_{|\xi|}f)(\xi,y)| \le C e^{-\varepsilon |\xi|},$$

for *y* the point at which one would like to test for analyticity, and |*ξ*| sufficiently large and pointing in the direction one would like to look for the wave front, that is, the direction at which the singularity at *y*, if it exists, propagates. J.M. Bony[^o10][^o14] proved that this definition coincided with other definitions introduced independently by Sato, Kashiwara and Kawai and by Hörmander. If *P* is an *m*th order linear differential operator having analytic coefficients

$$P =\sum_{|\alpha|\le m} a_\alpha(x) D^\alpha,$$

with ***principal symbol***

$$\sigma_P(x,\xi) = \sum_{|\alpha|= m} a_\alpha(x) \xi^\alpha,$$

and ***characteristic variety***

$${\rm char}\, P =\{(x,\xi): \xi\ne0, \, \sigma_P(x,\xi) =0\},$$

then

- $$\operatorname{WF}_A(Pf) \subseteq \operatorname{WF}_A(f)$$
- $$\operatorname{WF}_A(f) \subseteq \operatorname{WF}_A(Pf) \cup \operatorname{char} P.$$

In particular, when *P* is elliptic, char *P* = ø, so that

WF<sub>A</sub>(*Pf*) = WF<sub>A</sub>(*f*).

This is a strengthening of the analytic version of elliptic regularity mentioned above.
