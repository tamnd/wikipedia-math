# Pentagram map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pentagram map"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **pentagram map** is a [discrete dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087#Discrete_dynamical_system) acting on [polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygons) in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). It defines a new polygon whose vertices are obtained as the intersection points of the shortest [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) of the initial polygon. This is a [projectively](wikipedia://en/382667) [equivariant](wikipedia://en/422711) procedure, hence it [descends](wikipedia://en/237213) to the [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609) of polygons and defines another dynamical system (which is also referred to as the pentagram map). It was first introduced by [Richard Schwartz](wikipedia://en/1213887) in 1992.[^n1]

:::

The pentagram map on the moduli space is famous for its [complete integrability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_integrable) and its link with [cluster algebras](wikipedia://en/27038423).[^n2]

It admits many generalizations in [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135) and other settings.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

### Informal definition {#Informal_definition}

#### On polygons {#On_polygons}

*Image: Pentagram\_pentagon\_nolabel\_big.svg.* The pentagram map applied on a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014).

Initially, the pentagram map was defined for [convex polygons](wikipedia://en/660019) (with at least five sides) on the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). Given such a polygon $P$ with $n$ sides, one can draw the "shortest [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251)", meaning the [segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) whose endpoints are a [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) and one of its second neighbors (as in the figure). The intersections of the shortest diagonals are then taken as the vertices of a new $n$-gon $T(P)$; this new polygon is the output of the pentagram map.[^n3]

The same construction can be done on [non-convex polygons](wikipedia://en/605156), but there are several complications. First, some consecutive short diagonals may not intersect, so one must extend the segments to [lines](wikipedia://en/946975). Second, the image $T(P)$ can fail to be a new $n$-gon because some consecutive vertices could coincide. However, this [generically](wikipedia://en/12886758) doesn't happen.[^n4] Finally, it is possible that two diagonals are [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) and don't intersect on the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). This is resolved by extending the Euclidean plane to the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325) by the addition of a [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142), where the [intersection point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_point) lies (see the figure in the next section). Hence, the pentagram map is defined for generic polygons in the real projective plane.[^n5]

More generally, the construction of the pentagram map is well defined whenever the concepts of lines and their intersections make sense. This is encompassed by the notion of a general [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350), of which the real projective plane is one example; but the pentagram map can also be considered over other [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), for instance the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), which give the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155).[^n6]

#### On the moduli space of polygons {#On_the_moduli_space_of_polygons}

Since the pentagram map is constructed by drawing lines and marking their intersections, it [commutes](wikipedia://en/294390) with any transformation that sends lines to lines. Such maps are called [projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformations). Hence, polygons can be identified [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [projective transformations](wikipedia://en/23912960#Projectivity). This identification gives the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) (technically called a [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609)) of [classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of polygons.

The pentagram map on polygons induces another dynamical system on the moduli space,[^n7] whose behavior differs quite a lot from the initial one. The dynamic is trivial for the classes of pentagons and hexagons, but this is no longer the case for polygons with more vertices.

### Historical elements {#Historical_elements}

The pentagram map for general polygons was introduced in Schwartz, but the simplest case is the one of [pentagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentagons), hence the name "[pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544)".[^n8] Their study goes back to Clebsch,[^n9] Kasner[^n10] and Motzkin.[^n11]

The pentagram map interacts with some classical configuration theorems of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). It provides results analogous to the ones of [Pascal's theorem](wikipedia://en/699966) and [Brianchon's theorem](wikipedia://en/1554065).[^n12] Some specific configurations make [Desargues's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desargues'_theorem) and [Poncelet's porism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poncelet's_porism) appear.[^n13]

## Definitions and first properties {#Definitions_and_first_properties}

### Definition of the map {#Definition_of_the_map}

*Image: Pentagram\_pentagon\_label\_big.svg.* The pentagram map on a convex pentagon, with vertices labeled.

*Image: Pentagram\_on\_nonconvex\_pentagon.svg.* The pentagram map applied on a [self-intersecting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-intersecting_polygon) (in particular, non-convex) pentagon. The vertex $w_2$ is on the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142), because it is the [intersection of two parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_point).

Let $n\geq 5$ be an integer. A polygon $P$ with $n$ sides, or $n$-gon, is a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) of [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ lying in some [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) $\mathbb P ^2$, where the indices are understood [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $n$. The [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251) of the space of $n$-gons is $2n$.[^n14]

Suppose that the vertices are in sufficiently [general position](wikipedia://en/383736), meaning that no consecutive triple of points are [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581).[^n15] Taking the intersection of two consecutive "shortest" [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) defines a new point

$$w_k := \overline{v_{k-1} v_{k+1}} \cap \overline{v_{k} v_{k+2}}.$$

This procedure defines a new $n$-gon $T(P)=(w_1,\dots,w_n)$.[^n16]

The labeling of the indices of $T(P)$ is not [canonical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical). In most papers, a choice is made at the beginning of the paper and the formulas are tuned accordingly.[^n17]

The pentagram map on polygons is a [birational map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_map) $T:(\mathbb P^2)^n$⇢$(\mathbb P^2)^n$. Indeed, each [coordinate](wikipedia://en/243316) of $w_k$ is given as a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) of the coordinates of $v_{k-1},\dots,v_{k+2}$, since it is defined as the intersection of lines passing by them. Moreover, the [inverse map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_map) is given by taking the intersections $\overline{w_{k-2} w_{k-1}} \cap \overline{w_{k} w_{k+1}}$, which is rational for the same reason.[^n18]

### Moduli space {#Moduli_space}

The pentagram map is defined by taking [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) and intersections of them. The biggest [group](wikipedia://en/19447) which maps lines to lines is the one of [projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformations), denoted by $\mathbb P \mathrm{GL}_{3}$. Such a transformation $M$ [acts](wikipedia://en/12781) on a polygon $P$ by sending it to $M \cdot P:=(Mv_1,\dots,Mv_n)$. The pentagram map [commutes](wikipedia://en/294390) with this action, and thereby induces another [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) on the [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609) of projective [equivalence classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_classes) of polygons, whose [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251) is $2n-8$.[^n7]

### Twisted polygons {#Twisted_polygons}

*Image: Twisted\_heptagon.svg.* An example of twisted [heptagon](wikipedia://en/365349) on the real plane.

The pentagram map naturally generalizes to the larger space of twisted polygons (see the example of heptagon in the figure). For any integer $n\geq5$, a twisted $n$-gon $P$ is the data of:

- a [bi-infinite sequence](wikipedia://en/27838#Indexing) of points $(v_k)_{k\in\mathbb Z}$ in the projective plane (called the vertices),
- a [projective transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation) $M \in \mathbb P \mathrm{GL}_3$ (called the [monodromy](wikipedia://en/251250)),

such that for any $k \in \mathbb Z$, the property $v_{k+n}=Mv_k$ is satisfied. The dimension of the space of twisted $n$-gons is $2n+8$.[^n19]

When $M$ is the [identity](wikipedia://en/14962), this gives back the initial definition of polygons (which are said to be closed). The space of closed $n$-gons is of [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) $8$ in the space of twisted ones.[^n20]

The action of projective transformations over the space of closed polygons generalizes to the space of twisted ones (the monodromy is changed by [conjugation](wikipedia://en/341149)). This provides again a moduli space, of dimension $2n$.[^n21]

## Collapsing of convex polygons {#Collapsing_of_convex_polygons}

### Exponential shrinking {#Exponential_shrinking}

*Image: Pentagram\_map\_convex\_heptagon\_iterate.svg.* The pentagram map iterated on a convex [heptagon](wikipedia://en/365349), exhibiting the convergence.

Let $P$ be a closed [strictly convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019#Strictly_convex_polygon) lying on the real plane. One of the first results proved by Richard Schwartz it that its iterates under the pentagram map shrink [exponentially fast](wikipedia://en/191933) to a point, as illustrated in the figure. This follows from two facts.

1. The image of a strictly convex polygon is contained in its [interior](wikipedia://en/55610), and is also strictly convex.[^n22]
1. There exists a constant $0< \eta_P<1$, depending on $P$, such that for any $N \in \mathbb N$, the diameters of the iterates verify the inequality $\operatorname{diam}(T^N(P))\leq\eta_P^N \operatorname{diam}(P).$[^n23]

Hence, by [Cantor's intersection theorem](wikipedia://en/28046776#Variant_in_complete_metric_spaces), the sequence of polygons collapses toward a point.[^n24]

The behavior on the moduli space is very different, since the dynamics is [recurrent](wikipedia://en/5505578).[^n25] It is even a [quasiperiodic motion](wikipedia://en/1590390),[^n26] as discussed in [the section about integrability](wikipedia://en/26179254#Complete_integrability).

### Coordinates of the limit point {#Coordinates_of_the_limit_point}

The limit point coordinates were given in Glick. They satisfy some [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) 3 [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation), whose coefficients are [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in the coordinates of the vertices of the starting polygon. The proof relies on the fact that the limit point must be an [eigenline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenline) of a certain [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) of $\mathbb R^3$.[^n27]

This operator was reinterpreted in Aboud as the [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) monodromy of the polygon. The [scaling symmetry](wikipedia://en/26179254#The_scaling_symmetry) is used to [deform](wikipedia://en/679351) a closed polygon $P$ into a family of twisted ones **$(P_z)_{z\in \mathbb C^*}$** with monodromy $M_z$. The infinitesimal monodromy is defined to be:[^n28]

$$\left.\frac{dM_z}{dz}\right|_{z=1}.$$

::: {#o44 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

### Generalization {#Generalization}

The collapsing of polygons may also happen in some [generalization of the pentagram map](wikipedia://en/26179254#Generalizations), when considering some specific configurations of polygons in the real plane. The coordinates of the collapse point are given by a formula analogous to the one for the original pentagram map.[^n29]

:::

## Periodic orbits on the moduli space {#Periodic_orbits_on_the_moduli_space}

For some configurations of closed polygons, the iterate of the pentagram map will send $P$ to a projectively equivalent polygon (up to some shift of the indices). This means that, on the moduli space, the orbit of the class of $P$ is [periodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit).

### Pentagons and hexagons {#Pentagons_and_hexagons}

*Image: Penta\_hexagon.svg.* The outward hexagon is projectively equivalent to the inward one, with respect to their labeling.

The following two facts are proved by checking [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) equalities, so they are true for polygons in any [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) (not just the [real one](wikipedia://en/411325)).[^n30]

The pentagram map $T$ is the identity on the moduli space of [pentagons](wikipedia://en/20741014).[^n31][^n32][^n33] The second iterate $T^2$ is the identity on the space of labeled [hexagons](wikipedia://en/59733), up to a shift of labeling (see the figure).[^n34] This phenomenon doesn't generalize to generic polygons with at least seven sides, for which the motion is [quasiperiodic](wikipedia://en/1590390).[^n35]

::: {#o55 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

#### Generalization {#Generalization}

The result about pentagons and hexagons generalizes to some [higher pentagram maps](wikipedia://en/26179254#Generalizations) in $\mathbb P ^k$, for polygons with $k+3$ or $2k+2$ sides. The proof uses a generalization of the [Gale transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gale_transform).[^n36]

:::

### Poncelet polygons {#Poncelet_polygons}

A polygon is said to be Poncelet if it is [inscribed](wikipedia://en/4470203) in a [conic](wikipedia://en/19008673) and circumscribed about another one.[^n37] For a convex Poncelet $n$-gon $P$ lying on the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325), the polygon $T^2(P)$ is projectively equivalent to $P$.[^n38]

In fact, when $n$ is odd, the converse is also true.[^n39] However, this converse statement is no longer true when the polygons are considered over the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155) since there are explicit counterexamples.[^n40]

## Coordinates for the moduli space {#Coordinates_for_the_moduli_space}

The moduli space can be described by different [coordinate systems](wikipedia://en/81931). The following ones give simple expressions for the dynamics, as presented in the next section.

### Corner coordinates {#Corner_coordinates}

*Image: Corner\_coordinates\_big.svg.* The geometric construction of the points defining the corner invariants.

Define the [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) of four [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581) points to be

$$[a,b,c,d]=\frac{(a-b)(c-d)}{(a-c)(b-d)}.$$

The corner invariants are a system of coordinates on the space of twisted polygons, constructed by taking intersections as in the figure.[^n41] The left and right invariants are respectively defined as the following cross-ratios:

$$x_k:=[v_{k-2},v_{k-1},\overline{v_{k-2}v_{k-1}}\cap\overline{v_{k}v_{k+1}},\overline{v_{k-2}v_{k-1}}\cap\overline{v_{k+1}v_{k+2}}],$$

$$y_k:=[\overline{v_{k+1}v_{k+2}}\cap\overline{v_{k-2}v_{k-1}}, \overline{v_{k+1}v_{k+2}}\cap\overline{v_{k-1}v_{k}},v_{k+1},v_{k+2}].$$

Since the cross-ratio is [projective invariant](wikipedia://en/401767#Projective_geometry), the sequences $(x_k)_{k \in \mathbb Z}$ and $(y_k)_{k \in \mathbb Z}$ associated to a twisted $n$-gon are $n$-periodic.[^n42]

The corner invariants are elements of $\mathbb{P}^1\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\}$, and they realize an [isomorphism of varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_of_varieties) between the moduli space of twisted $n$-gons and $(\mathbb{P}^1\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\})^{2n}$.[^n43]

### ab-coordinates {#ab-coordinates}

There is a second set of coordinates for the moduli space of twisted $n$-gons defined over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$ satisfying $\mathrm{SL}_3(F)\cong \mathbb P\mathrm{GL}_3(F)$,[^n44] and such that $n$ is not divisible by $3$.[^n45]

The vertices $v_k$ in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) $\mathbb P^2(F)$ can be [lifted](wikipedia://en/10504570) to [vectors](wikipedia://en/32370) $V_k$ in the [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) $F^3$ so that each consecutive triple of vectors spans a [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975) having [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) equal to $1$. This leads to the relation defining the $ab$-coordinates:[^n46]

$$V_{k+3} = a_k V_{k+2} + b_k V_{k+1} + V_k.$$

This bring out an analogy between twisted polygons and solutions of third order linear [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations), normalized to have unit [Wronskian](wikipedia://en/302846).[^n47]

They are linked to the corner coordinates by:[^n48]

$$x_k=\frac{a_{k-2}}{b_{k-2}b_{k-1}},$$

$$y_k=-\frac{b_{k-1}}{a_{k-2}a_{k-1}}.$$

::: {#o74 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas on the moduli space"}

## Formulas on the moduli space {#Formulas_on_the_moduli_space}

### As a birational map {#As_a_birational_map}

The pentagram map is a [birational map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_map) on the moduli space, because it can be decomposed as the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two [birational](wikipedia://en/382733) [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450).[^n49] The corner invariants change in the following way:[^n50]

$$x_k'=x_k\frac{1-x_{k-1} y_{k-1}}{1-x_{k+1}y_{k+1}},$$

$$y_k'=y_{k+1}\frac{1-x_{k+2} y_{k+2}}{1-x_k y_k}.$$

### The scaling symmetry {#The_scaling_symmetry}

The [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) $F\smallsetminus\{0\}$ [acts](wikipedia://en/754851) on the moduli space in the following way:

$$R_s\cdot(x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n)=(sx_1,\dots,sx_n,s^{-1}y_1,\dots,s^{-1}y_n),$$

where $R$ is called the scaling action and $s$ is the scaling parameter. This action commutes with the pentagram map on the moduli space (as presented in the previous formulas). This property is called the scaling symmetry, and is instrumental in proving the [complete integrability](wikipedia://en/26179254#Complete_integrability) of the dynamics.[^n51]

:::

## Invariant structures {#Invariant_structures}

### Monodromy invariants {#Monodromy_invariants}

The monodromy invariants, introduced in Schwartz, are a collection of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on the [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609) that are invariant under the pentagram map.[^n52] The simplest examples of them are

$$O_n= x_1x_2\cdots x_{n}, \quad E_n = y_1y_2\cdots y_n.$$

The other monodromy invariants can be retrieved through different points of view: through the [scaling symmetry](wikipedia://en/26179254#The_scaling_symmetry), as [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) objects, or as some [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468).[^n53] The one involving scaling symmetry is presented here.

Let $M\in \mathrm{GL}_3$ be a [lift](wikipedia://en/10504570) of the monodromy of a twisted $n$-gon. The quantities

$$\Omega_1=\frac{\operatorname{trace}^3(M)}{\det(M)}, \quad \Omega_2=\frac{\operatorname{trace}^3(M^{-1})}{\det(M^{-1})},$$

are independent of the choice of lift and are invariant under [conjugation](wikipedia://en/341149), so they are well defined for the projective class of the polygon. They are invariant under the pentagram map, since the monodromy matrix doesn't change.[^n54] Now, the quantities

$$\tilde{\Omega}_1=O_n^2E_n\Omega_1, \quad \tilde{\Omega}_2=O_nE_n^2\Omega_2,$$

have the same properties, but turn out to be polynomials in the corner invariants. They can be written as[^n54]

$$\tilde{\Omega}_1=\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}O_k\biggr)^3, \quad \tilde{\Omega}_2=\biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}E_k\biggr)^3,$$

where each $O_k$ and $E_k$ are [homogeneous polynomials](wikipedia://en/1698977) respectively of weight $k$ and $-k$,[^n55] meaning they change under the [rescaling action](wikipedia://en/26179254#The_scaling_symmetry) on variables by[^n54]

$$R_s(O_k)= s^k O_k, \quad R_s(E_k)= s^{-k} E_k.$$

The quantities $O_1,\dots,O_{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor},O_n, E_1,\dots,E_{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor},E_n,$ are unchanged by the dynamics, and are called the monodromy invariants. Moreover, they are [algebraically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_independent).[^n52]

#### Polygons on conics {#Polygons_on_conics}

Whenever $P$ is [inscribed](wikipedia://en/4470203) in a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673), one has $O_k(P)=E_k(P)$ for all $k$.[^n56] Moreover, if $P$ is circumscribed about another conic, then its monodromy invariants are characterized by the pair of conics.[^n57] For such odd-gons, the translation on the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) is restricted to the [Prym variety](wikipedia://en/4116892) (which is a half-dimensional torus in the Jacobian).[^n58]

### Poisson bracket {#Poisson_bracket}

An invariant [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) on the space of twisted polygons was found in Ovsienko. The monodromy invariants [commute](wikipedia://en/294340#Constants_of_motion) with respect to it:

$$\{O_i,O_j\}=\{O_i,E_j\}=\{E_i,E_j\}=0$$

for all $i,j$.[^n59]

The Poisson bracket is defined in terms of the corner coordinates by:

$$\begin{aligned} \{x_i,x_{i\pm1}\} &= \mp x_i x_{i+1}, \\ \{y_i,y_{i\pm 1}\} &= \mp y_i y_{i+1}, \\ \end{aligned}$$

and

$$\{x_i,x_j\} = \{y_i,y_j\} = \{x_i,y_j\} = 0$$

for all other $i,j.$[^n60]

### The spectral curve {#The_spectral_curve}

Let $\zeta$ be an element of the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) and $P_\zeta$ be the polygon obtained by applying the [rescaling action](wikipedia://en/26179254#The_scaling_symmetry) $R_\zeta$ on $P$. A [Lax matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax_matrix) $\hat{T}(\zeta) \in \mathrm{GL}_3$ is a lift of the monodromy of $P_\zeta$ satisfying a [zero-curvature equation](wikipedia://en/5527029#Zero-curvature_equation).[^n61] Then, the spectral function is the [bivariate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bivariate_polynomial) [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268)

$$Q(\lambda,\zeta) := \det(\lambda\operatorname{Id}-\hat{T}(\zeta)),$$

or some renormalization of it. The [spectral curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_curve) is the [projective completion](wikipedia://en/320469#projective_completion) of the [affine curve](wikipedia://en/253260) defined by the equation $Q(\lambda,\zeta)=0$.[^n62] It is invariant under the pentagram map, and the monodromy invariants appear as the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) of $Q$.[^n63] Its [geometric genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) is $n-1$ if $n$ is odd, and $n-2$ if $n$ is even.[^n64]

It was first introduced in (Soloviev 2013) for his proof of [algebro-geometric integrability](wikipedia://en/26179254#Algebro-geometric_integrability).[^n65]

## Complete integrability {#Complete_integrability}

The pentagram map on the moduli space has been proved to be a [completely integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_integrable) [discrete dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_dynamical_system), both in the [Arnold-Liouville](wikipedia://en/2573213#Hamiltonian_systems_and_Liouville_integrability) and the [algebro-geometric](wikipedia://en/2573213#Complete_integrability_over_the_complex_numbers) senses. In any case, this means that the moduli space is [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) [foliated](wikipedia://en/371227) by [flat tori](wikipedia://en/74800#Flat_torus) (or in the algebraic setting, [Abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/247152)), where the motion is a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682). This [generically](wikipedia://en/12886758) induces a [quasiperiodic motion](wikipedia://en/1590390) on the corresponding torus.[^n26]

### Arnold–Liouville integrability {#Arnold–Liouville_integrability}

The proof of the integrability of the pentagram map on a real twisted polygon was achieved in Ovsienko. This is done by noticing that the monodromy invariants $O_n$ and $E_n$ are [Casimir invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimir_invariant) for the Poisson bracket, meaning (in this context) that

$$\{O_n,f\}=\{E_n,f\} = 0$$

for every [smooth function](wikipedia://en/1408000) $f$.[^n59] When $n$ is even, this is also true for the monodromy invariants $O_{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor }$ and $E_{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor }$.[^n59]

This allows to consider the Casimir [level set](wikipedia://en/559622), where each Casimir invariant has a specified value. Because of [Sard's theorem](wikipedia://en/914901), any generic level set is a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold).[^n66] This family of level sets forms a [foliation](wikipedia://en/371227) in [symplectic leaves](wikipedia://en/882902#Symplectic_leaves), on which the Poisson bracket gives rise to a [symplectic form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_form).[^n67]

Each of these symplectic leaves has an iso-monodromy [foliation](wikipedia://en/371227), namely, a decomposition into the common level sets of the remaining monodromy functions. By using again [Sard's theorem](wikipedia://en/914901), they are generically [Lagrangian manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356#Lagrangian_submanifolds).[^n68] Moreover, they are compact.[^n69] Since the monodromy invariants Poisson-commute and there are enough of them, the discrete [Liouville–Arnold theorem](wikipedia://en/40071018) can be applied to prove that the level sets are [flat tori](wikipedia://en/74800#Flat_torus) over which the dynamics is a translation.[^n70]

### Algebro-geometric integrability {#Algebro-geometric_integrability}

In Soloviev, it was shown that the pentagram map admits a [Lax representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax_representation) with a spectral parameter, which allows to prove its algebro-geometric integrability. This means that the space of polygons (either twisted or closed) is parametrized by its spectral data, consisting of [its spectral curve](wikipedia://en/26179254#The_spectral_curve), with marked points and a [divisor](wikipedia://en/1693055) given by a [Floquet](wikipedia://en/2987843)–[Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch's_theorem) equation. This gives an embedding to the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) through the [Abel–Jacobi map](wikipedia://en/10756143), where the motion is expressed in terms of translation.[^n71] The previously defined Poisson bracket is also retrieved.[^n72]

This integrability was generalized in Weinreich from the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) to any [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) different from 2. The translation on a torus is replaced by a translation on an [Abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) (in fact, a Jacobian variety again).[^n73]

### Dimension of the invariant manifold {#Dimension_of_the_invariant_manifold}

For twisted $n$-gons, the [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) of the invariant tori (or Jacobian varieties) is[^n74]

$$\begin{cases} n-1 & \text{when }n \text{ is odd,}\\ n-2 & \text{when }n \text{ is even.} \end{cases}$$

Moreover, when $n$ is even, there are two isomorphic Jacobians on which the iterates of the pentagram map alternate. But on each of them, the second iterate is a translation.[^n73]

### For closed polygons {#For_closed_polygons}

There is no Poisson structure on the space of closed polygons.[^n75] Nevertheless, the one from twisted polygons can be used to prove integrability.[^n76]

Algebro-geometric integrability holds for closed polygons in a same manner as for the twisted ones.[^n77] However, Arnold-Liouville integrability is proved for real closed polygons only when they are convex. This is done by restricting the [Hamiltonian vector fields](wikipedia://en/1818270) of monodromy functions to smaller dimensional tori, and showing that enough of them are still independent.[^n78]

In both situation, the dimension of the invariant manifolds decreases by $3$ for closed $n$-gons (compared to the twisted case), and is equal to[^n77][^n79]

$$\begin{cases} n-4 & \text{when }n \text{ is odd,}\\ n-5 & \text{when }n \text{ is even.} \end{cases}$$

## Connections to other topics {#Connections_to_other_topics}

### The Boussinesq equation {#The_Boussinesq_equation}

The [continuous limit](wikipedia://en/330017) of a convex polygon is a parametrized [convex curve](wikipedia://en/29747989) in the plane. When the time parameter is suitably chosen, the continuous limit of the pentagram map is the [Boussinesq equation](wikipedia://en/14993993). This [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) models water waves under some conditions, which is a classical example of [integrable system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable).[^n80]

Here is a description of the geometric action of the Boussinesq equation. Given a [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex) curve $C:\mathbb R\to \mathbb R^2$ and real numbers $x$ and $t$, consider the [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) connecting $C(x-t)$ to $C(x+t)$. The [envelope](wikipedia://en/880235) of all these chords is a new curve $C_t(x)$. When $t$ is extremely small, the curve $C_t(x)$ is a good model for the time $t$ evolution of the original curve $C_0(x)$ under the Boussinesq equation. This construction is also similar to the pentagram map. Moreover, the pentagram invariant bracket is a discretization of an invariant Poisson bracket associated to the Boussinesq equation.[^n81]

### Cluster algebras {#Cluster_algebras}

The pentagram map[^n82] and some of its generalizations[^n83][^n84] are identified as special cases of discrete dynamical systems powered by [cluster algebras](wikipedia://en/27038423). Using the results from Goncharov, this provides a link with the [Poisson–Lie groups](wikipedia://en/4257207), [dimer models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimer_model) and other so-called cluster-integrable systems.[^n85][^n86] These methods allow to retrieve the Poisson bracket and Hamiltonians used to prove complete integrability[^n87] and provide [Lax representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax_representation).[^n88]

### Octahedron recurrence {#Octahedron_recurrence}

Using a method to compute [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468) called [Dodgson condensation](wikipedia://en/1740225), Schwartz proves that the pentagram map satisfies a property called the "octahedron recurrence".[^n89] This property turns out to be shared by other dynamical systems defined by geometric constructions similar to one of the pentagram map.[^n90] It is also shared by higher dimensional pentagram maps defined through cluster algebras mutations, referred as $T$-systems.[^n91]

### Singularity theory {#Singularity_theory}

The pentagram map exhibits a property called singularity confinement, which is a typical phenomenon in [integrable systems](wikipedia://en/2573213).[^n92] It states that if a polygon $P$ is [singular](wikipedia://en/981915) for the pentagram map $T$, then there exists an integer $m$ such that $P$ not singular for the iterate map $T^m$.[^n93]

Moreover, the pentagram map (along with some of its generalizations and other discrete dynamical systems) exhibit the Devron property. This means that if a polygon $P$ is singular for some iterate of the pentagram map $T^m$, then it will also be singular for some iterate of the inverse map $T^{-m'}$.[^n94]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The definition of polygons still makes sense in any [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb P^d$ under the action of the [projective group](wikipedia://en/382667) $\mathbb P \mathrm{GL}_{d+1}$, and even in other spaces with their associated groups.

The pentagram map can be generalized in many ways, and some of them are presented here. Not all of them are integrable.[^n95] Some are [discretizations](wikipedia://en/330017) of [PDEs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDEs) from the [KdV hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KdV_hierarchy), seen as higher dimensional version of [Boussinesq](wikipedia://en/14993993) or [KP](wikipedia://en/4211219) equations.[^n96][^n97] The description of all generalized pentagram maps in terms of [cluster algebras](wikipedia://en/27038423) is still an open question.[^n2]

### Polygons in general positions {#Polygons_in_general_positions}

Let $d \geq 2$ and $P$ be a twisted polygon of $\mathbb P^d$ in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736).

#### Short diagonal pentagram maps {#Short_diagonal_pentagram_maps}

The $k$-th short diagonal hyperplane $H_k^{sh}$ is uniquely defined by passing through the vertices $v_k,v_{k+2},\dots,v_{k+2d-2}$. [Generically](wikipedia://en/12886758#In_algebraic_geometry), the intersection of $d$ consecutive [hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/99862) uniquely defines a new point

$$T_{sh}v_k:=H_k^{sh}\cap H_{k+1}^{sh}\cap \dots \cap H_{k+d-1}^{sh}.$$

Doing this for every vertex defines a new twisted polygon. This map, denoted by $T_{sh}$, is again projectively equivariant.[^n98]

#### Generalized pentagram maps {#Generalized_pentagram_maps}

The previous procedure can be generalized. Let $I=(i_1,\dots,i_{d-1}),~J=(j_1,\dots,j_{d-1})$ be two sets of integers, respectively called the jump tuple and the intersection tuple. Define $H_k^I$ to be the unique hyperplane passing through the vertices $v_k,v_{k+i_1},\dots,v_{k+i_1+\dots+i_{d-1}}$. A new point is given by the intersection

$$T_{I,J}v_k:=H_k^I \cap H_{k+j_1}^I \cap \dots \cap H_{k+j_1+\dots +j_{d-1}}^I.$$

The map $T_{I,J}$ is called a generalized pentagram map.[^n95] The original pentagram map is recovered by considering$d=2,~I=(2),~J=(1)$.

Integrability can be numerically tested by picking a random polygon $P$ with [rational coordinates](wikipedia://en/2960200) and studying the growth rate of the [height](wikipedia://en/3141855) of its iterates. This is called the [diophantine integrability](wikipedia://en/2573213#Diophantine_integrability) test, and some generalized pentagram maps don't seem to pass it.[^n99] However, it is conjectured that the maps $T_{I,I}$ are integrable for any $I$.[^n100]

Some of these maps are [discretizations](wikipedia://en/330017) of higher dimensional counterpart of the [Boussinesq equation](wikipedia://en/14993993) in the [KdV hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KdV_hierarchy).[^n101][^n102]

#### Dented pentagram maps {#Dented_pentagram_maps}

Fix an integer $m\in \{1,\dots ,d-1\}$. Consider the jump tuple $I_m:=(1,\dots,1,2,1,\dots,1)$, where the $2$ is at the $m$-th place, and the intersection tuple $J:=(1,\dots,1)$. The dented pentagram map is $T_m :=T_{I_m,J}$. They are proved to be integrable.[^n103]

For an integer $p \geq 2$, the deep dented pentagram map (of depth $p$) $T_m^p$ is the same map as before, but the number $2$ in the definition of $I_m$ is replaced by $p$. This kind of pentagram maps are integrable too.[^n104]

### Corrugated polygons {#Corrugated_polygons}

A twisted polygon $P$ lying in $\mathbb P^d$ is said to be corrugated if for any $k\in \mathbb Z$, the vertices $v_k,v_{k+1},v_{k+d},v_{k+d+1}$ span a projective two-dimensional plane. Such polygons are not in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736). A new point is defined by

$$T_\text{cor}v_k:=\overline{v_k v_{k+d}}\cap \overline{v_{k+1} v_{k+d+1}}.$$

The map $T_\text{cor}$ yields a new corrugated polygon. This dynamics is [completely Liouville-integrable](wikipedia://en/2573213#Hamiltonian_systems_and_Liouville_integrability).[^n105]

In fact, they can be retrieved as some dented pentagram map applied on corrugated polygons.[^n106]

### Grassmannian polygons {#Grassmannian_polygons}

Let $d \geq 3, m \geq 1$ be integers. The pentagram map can also be generalized to the [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810) space $\mathrm{Gr}(m,md)$, which consists of $m$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [linear subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) of an $md$-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). When $m=1$, the linear subspaces are [lines](wikipedia://en/32370#vector_line), which retrieves the definition of [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb P^d$.[^n107]

A point $v\in\operatorname{Gr}(m,md)$ is represented by an $md \times m$ matrix $X_v$ such that its columns form a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of $v$. Consider the [action](wikipedia://en/12781) of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\mathrm{GL}_{md}$ by multiplication on the left of $X_v$. This defines an action on the Grassmannian, even though it is not [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_action). Hence, the polygons of $\mathrm{Gr}(m,md)$ and their moduli spaces are defined as before, after the change of underlying group.[^n107]

Depending on the parity of $d$, one can define linear subspaces spanned by some $X_{v_k}$'s such that taking their intersection generically defines a new point $v\in\mathrm{Gr}(m,md)$.[^n108] This generalization of the pentagram map is integrable in a [noncommutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative) sense.[^n109]

### Over rings {#Over_rings}

The pentagram map admits a generalization by considering [projective planes](wikipedia://en/242135#Generalizations) over [stably finite rings](wikipedia://en/26021350), instead of [fields](wikipedia://en/10603). In particular, this retrieves the pentagram map over Grassmannians. Again, it admits a [Lax representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax_representation).[^n110]
