# Schwartz kernel theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schwartz kernel theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Schwartz kernel theorem** is a foundational result in the theory of [generalized functions](wikipedia://en/1580246), published by [Laurent Schwartz](wikipedia://en/317485) in 1952. It states, in broad terms, that the generalized functions introduced by Schwartz ([Schwartz distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_distribution)) have a two-variable theory that includes all reasonable [bilinear forms](wikipedia://en/449477) on the space $\mathcal{D}$ of [test functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Definitions_of_test_functions_and_distributions). The space $\mathcal{D}$ itself consists of [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) of [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

Let $X$ and $Y$ be open sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Every [distribution](wikipedia://en/51955) $k \in \mathcal{D}'(X \times Y)$ defines a [continuous linear map](wikipedia://en/1802169) $K \colon \mathcal{D}(Y) \to \mathcal{D}'(X)$ such that

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\left \langle k, u \otimes v \right \rangle = \left \langle Kv , u \right \rangle \tag{1}$$

:::

for every $u \in \mathcal{D}(X), v \in \mathcal{D}(Y)$. Conversely, for every such continuous linear map $K$, there exists one and only one distribution $k \in \mathcal{D}'(X \times Y)$ such that ((1)) holds. The distribution $k$ is called the kernel of the map $K$, in reference to the kernel of an [integral transform](wikipedia://en/430912). In line with this, one sometimes writes the linear map $K$ informally as

$$Kv = \int_{Y} k(\cdot,y) v(y) d y$$

so that

$$\langle Kv,u \rangle = \int_{X} \int_{Y} k(x,y) v(y) u(x) d y d x.$$

:::

## Integral kernels {#Integral_kernels}

The traditional [kernel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_function) $K(x,y)$ of two variables of the theory of [integral operators](wikipedia://en/2649421) having been expanded in scope to include their generalized function analogues, which are allowed to be more singular in a serious way, a large class of operators from $\mathcal{D}$ to its [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) $\mathcal{D}'$ of distributions can be constructed. The point of the theorem is to assert that the extended class of operators can be characterised abstractly, as containing all operators subject to a minimum continuity condition. A bilinear form on $\mathcal{D}$ arises by pairing the image distribution with a test function.

A simple example is that the natural embedding of the test function space $\mathcal{D}$ into $\mathcal{D}'$ - sending every test function $f$ to its [corresponding distribution](wikipedia://en/51955#Functions_as_distributions) $[f]$ - corresponds to the delta distribution

$$\delta(x-y)$$

concentrated at the diagonal of the underlined Euclidean space, in terms of the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021) $\delta$. While this is at most an observation, it shows how the distribution theory adds to the scope. Integral operators are not so 'singular'; another way to put it is that for $K$ a continuous kernel, only [compact operators](wikipedia://en/577441) are created on a space such as the continuous functions on $[0,1]$. The operator $I$ is far from compact, and its kernel is intuitively speaking approximated by functions on $[0,1]\times[0,1]$ with a spike along the diagonal $x=y$ and vanishing elsewhere.

This result implies that the formation of distributions has a major property of 'closure' within the traditional domain of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). It was interpreted (comment of [Jean Dieudonné](wikipedia://en/1070084)) as a strong verification of the suitability of the Schwartz theory of distributions to mathematical analysis more widely seen. In his *[Éléments d'analyse](wikipedia://en/53092613)* volume 7, p. 3 he notes that the theorem includes [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) on the same footing as integral operators, and concludes that it is perhaps the most important modern result of functional analysis. He goes on immediately to qualify that statement, saying that the setting is too 'vast' for differential operators, because of the property of monotonicity with respect to the [support of a function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_of_a_function), which is evident for differentiation. Even monotonicity with respect to [singular support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_support) is not characteristic of the general case; its consideration leads in the direction of the contemporary theory of [pseudo-differential operators](wikipedia://en/973479).

## Smooth manifolds {#Smooth_manifolds}

[Dieudonné](wikipedia://en/1070084) proves a version of the Schwartz result valid for [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), and additional supporting results, in sections 23.9 to 23.12 of that [book](wikipedia://en/53092613).

## Generalization to nuclear spaces {#Generalization_to_nuclear_spaces}

Much of the theory of [nuclear spaces](wikipedia://en/2702957) was developed by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) while investigating the Schwartz kernel theorem and published in (Grothendieck 1955). We have the following generalization of the theorem.

**Schwartz kernel theorem**:[^n1] Suppose that *X* is [nuclear](wikipedia://en/2702957), *Y* is locally convex, and *v* is a continuous bilinear form on $X \times Y$. Then *v* originates from a space of the form $X^{\prime}_{A^{\prime}} \widehat{\otimes}_{\epsilon} Y^{\prime}_{B^{\prime}}$ where $A^{\prime}$ and $B^{\prime}$ are suitable equicontinuous subsets of $X^{\prime}$ and $Y^{\prime}$. Equivalently, *v* is of the form,

$v(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \lambda_i \left\langle x, x_i^{\prime} \right\rangle \left\langle y, y_i^{\prime} \right\rangle$ for all $(x, y) \in X \times Y$

where $\left( \lambda_i \right) \in l^1$ and each of $\{ x^{\prime}_1, x^{\prime}_2, \ldots \}$ and $\{ y^{\prime}_1, y^{\prime}_2, \ldots \}$ are equicontinuous. Furthermore, these sequences can be taken to be null sequences (i.e. converging to 0) in $X^{\prime}_{A^{\prime}}$ and $Y^{\prime}_{B^{\prime}}$, respectively.
