# Metallic Metals Act

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Metallic Metals Act"}

The **Metallic Metals Act** was a fictional piece of legislation included in a 1947 American opinion survey conducted by Sam Gill and published in the March 14, 1947, issue of *Tide* magazine. When given four possible replies, 70% of respondents claimed to have an opinion on the act. It has become a classic example of the risks of meaningless responses to [closed-ended questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-ended_question) and prompted the study of the pseudo-opinion phenomenon.

:::

## The question {#The_question}

Respondents were asked this question and were given four possible answers: "Which of the following statements most closely coincides with your opinion of the Metallic Metals Act?"[^o11]

- It would be a good move on the part of the [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)
- It would be a good thing, but should be [left to the individual states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/States'_rights)
- It's alright for foreign countries, but should not be required here
- It is of no value at all

## Initial publication and reaction {#Initial_publication_and_reaction}

Sam Gill was a Marketing Research Director for Sherman & Marquette, Inc when he included a question about the fictional Metallic Metals Act in a survey. He reported on the results in the March 14, 1947, issue of *Tide* magazine in an article titled "How Do You Stand on Sin?", saying that 70% of respondents claimed to have an opinion on the topic.[^o4][^o6] Gill also asked respondents if they favored [incest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest), an unfamiliar term to most people at the time, and one third supported it.[^o11] The article did not include any information on the size or make-up of the [sample population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29), nor how much pressure the interviewer applied to receive a response.[^o5]

A similar study by Eugene Hartley in 1946 asked college students how connected they felt to students of various nationalities. His questionnaire included three imaginary nationalities, but a majority of students did not question them.[^o11] Together, these two studies are the earliest publicized examples of opinion surveys on fake subjects, a phenomenon known as a **pseudo-opinion**.[^o10][^o8] At the time, the results of both studies amused [laymen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laymen) but were not immediately taken seriously in the field of public opinion because most professionals felt the studies were ridiculous and reflected negatively on their field.[^o6] One exception, Stanley L. Payne, wrote about Gill's study in the 1951 *[The Public Opinion Quarterly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Public_Opinion_Quarterly)* journal article "Thoughts About Meaningless Questions" and called for further investigation into this type of [non-sampling error](wikipedia://en/21987680).[^o6]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

Despite Payne's call to action, pseudo-opinions remained largely unstudied until the 1980s,[^o8] but in 1970 Philip Converse postulated that answering "don't know" is seen by respondents as an admission of "mental incapacity".[^o9] In 1981, researchers Howard Schuman and Stanley Presser were unable to locate documentation for Gill's study and concluded it should be taken as an [anecdote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anecdote) rather than a true study.[^o10] Their research found that pseudo-opinions are a significant source of error but not as prevalent as Hartley and Gill's studies suggested.[^o11]

The Metallic Metals Act is considered a classic example of pseudo-opinions and difficulties with close-ended survey questions[^o4][^o12] and continues to be supported by later studies.[^o13] By 1991, it had become standard practice to include a false question in opinion surveys to gauge the degree of pseudo-opinions.[^o14] A study by the University of Cincinnati found 20 to 40 percent of Americans will provide pseudo-opinions because of social pressure, using context clues to select an answer they believe will please the questioner.[^o15] This has occasionally provided a source for jokes on [talk shows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talk_show) and comedy shows who air interviews to mock the respondents.[^o5] Other studies have shown the phenomenon is not limited to the United States.[^o16] In a 2019 opinion piece written for *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)*, [Richard Seymour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Seymour_%2821st-century_writer%29) speculated that most opinion polls represent only what respondents heard most recently in the news media.[^o17]
