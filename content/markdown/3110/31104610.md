# Apollonian network

*Image: Apollonian-network.svg.* An Apollonian network

*Image: Goldner-Harary graph.svg.* The [Goldner–Harary graph](wikipedia://en/24674758), a non-Hamiltonian Apollonian network

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Apollonian network"}

In [combinatorial mathematics](wikipedia://en/5170), an **Apollonian network** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) formed by a process of recursively subdividing a triangle into three smaller triangles. Apollonian networks may equivalently be defined as the [planar](wikipedia://en/24314) [3-trees](wikipedia://en/31104438), the maximal planar chordal graphs, the [uniquely 4-colorable](wikipedia://en/676304) planar graphs, and the graphs of [stacked polytopes](wikipedia://en/44760854). They are named after [Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242), who studied a related circle-packing construction.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An Apollonian network may be formed, starting from a single triangle [embedded](wikipedia://en/8149170) in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), by repeatedly selecting a triangular face of the embedding, adding a new vertex inside the face, and connecting the new vertex to each vertex of the face containing it. In this way, the triangle containing the new vertex is subdivided into three smaller triangles, which may in turn be subdivided in the same way.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816) on three and four vertices, $K_{3}$ and $K_{4}$, are both Apollonian networks. $K_{3}$ is formed by starting with a triangle and not performing any subdivisions, while $K_{4}$ is formed by making a single subdivision before stopping.

The [Goldner–Harary graph](wikipedia://en/24674758) is an Apollonian network that forms the smallest [non-Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) maximal planar graph.[^o6] Another more complicated Apollonian network was used by (Nishizeki 1980) to provide an example of a [1-tough](wikipedia://en/8839340) non-Hamiltonian maximal planar graph.[^n1]

:::

## Graph-theoretic characterizations {#Graph-theoretic_characterizations}

As well as being defined by recursive subdivision of triangles, the Apollonian networks have several other equivalent mathematical characterizations. They are the [chordal](wikipedia://en/744165) maximal planar graphs, the chordal [polyhedral graphs](wikipedia://en/24656270), and the planar [3-trees](wikipedia://en/31104438). They are the [uniquely 4-colorable](wikipedia://en/676304) planar graphs, and the planar graphs with a unique [Schnyder wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schnyder_wood) decomposition into three trees. They are the maximal planar graphs with treewidth three, a class of graphs that can be characterized by their [forbidden minors](wikipedia://en/13547826) or by their reducibility under [Y-Δ transforms](wikipedia://en/268145). They are the maximal planar graphs with [degeneracy](wikipedia://en/27545816) three. They are also the planar graphs on a given number of vertices that have the largest possible number of triangles, the largest possible number of tetrahedral subgraphs, the largest possible number of cliques, and the largest possible number of pieces after decomposing by separating triangles.

### Chordality {#Chordality}

Apollonian networks are examples of [maximal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_element) [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), graphs to which no additional edges can be added without destroying planarity, or equivalently graphs that can be drawn in the plane so that every face (including the outer face) is a triangle. They are also [chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/744165), graphs in which every cycle of four or more vertices has a diagonal edge connecting two non-consecutive cycle vertices, and the order in which vertices are added in the subdivision process that forms an Apollonian network is an elimination ordering as a chordal graph. This forms an alternative characterization of the Apollonian networks: they are exactly the chordal maximal planar graphs or equivalently the chordal [polyhedral graphs](wikipedia://en/24656270).[^o11]

In an Apollonian network, every [maximal clique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_clique) is a complete graph on four vertices, formed by choosing any vertex and its three earlier neighbors. Every minimal [clique separator](wikipedia://en/17598460) (a clique that partitions the graph into two disconnected subgraphs) is one of the subdivided triangles. A chordal graph in which all maximal cliques and all minimal clique separators have the same size is a [$k$-tree](wikipedia://en/31104438), and Apollonian networks are examples of 3-trees. Not every 3-tree is planar, but the planar 3-trees are exactly the Apollonian networks.

### Unique colorability {#Unique_colorability}

Every Apollonian network is also a [uniquely 4-colorable graph](wikipedia://en/676304). Because it is a planar graph, the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949) implies that it has a [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) with only four colors, but once the three colors of the initial triangle are selected, there is only one possible choice for the color of each successive vertex, so up to permutation of the set of colors it has exactly one 4-coloring. It is more difficult to prove, but also true, that every uniquely 4-colorable planar graph is an Apollonian network. Therefore, Apollonian networks may also be characterized as the uniquely 4-colorable planar graphs.[^n2] Apollonian networks also provide examples of planar graphs having as few $k$-colorings as possible for $k > 4$.[^o14]

The Apollonian networks are also exactly the maximal planar graphs that (once an exterior face is fixed) have a unique [Schnyder wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schnyder_wood), a partition of the edges of the graph into three interleaved trees rooted at the three vertices of the exterior face.[^o15]

### Treewidth {#Treewidth}

The Apollonian networks do not form a family of graphs that is closed under the operation of taking [graph minors](wikipedia://en/353042), as removing edges but not vertices from an Apollonian network produces a graph that is not an Apollonian network. However, the planar [partial 3-trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_k-tree), subgraphs of Apollonian networks, are minor-closed. Therefore, according to the [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769), they can be characterized by a finite number of [forbidden minors](wikipedia://en/13547826). The minimal forbidden minors for the planar partial 3-trees are the four minimal graphs among the forbidden minors for the planar graphs and the partial 3-trees: the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{5}$, the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) $K_{3,3}$, the graph of the [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), and the graph of the [pentagonal prism](wikipedia://en/2859659). The Apollonian graphs are the maximal graphs that do not have any of these four graphs as a minor.[^o17]

A [Y-Δ transform](wikipedia://en/268145), an operation that replaces a degree-three vertex in a graph by a triangle connecting its neighbors, is sufficient (together with the removal of parallel edges) to reduce any Apollonian network to a single triangle, and more generally the planar graphs that can be reduced to a single edge by Y-Δ transforms, removal of parallel edges, removal of degree-one vertices, and compression of degree-two vertices are exactly the planar partial 3-trees. The dual graphs of the planar partial 3-trees form another minor-closed graph family and are exactly the planar graphs that can be reduced to a single edge by Δ-Y transforms, removal of parallel edges, removal of degree-one vertices, and compression of degree-two vertices.[^o18]

### Degeneracy {#Degeneracy}

In every subgraph of an Apollonian network, the most recently added vertex has [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) at most three, so Apollonian networks have [degeneracy](wikipedia://en/27545816) three. The order in which the vertices are added to create the network is therefore a degeneracy ordering, and the Apollonian networks coincide with the 3-degenerate maximal planar graphs.

### Extremality {#Extremality}

Another characterization of the Apollonian networks involves their [connectivity](wikipedia://en/1614492). Any maximal planar graph may be decomposed into 4-vertex-connected maximal planar subgraphs by splitting it along its separating triangles (triangles that are not faces of the graph): given any non-facial triangle: one can form two smaller maximal planar graphs, one consisting of the part inside the triangle and the other consisting of the part outside the triangle. The maximal planar graphs without separating triangles that may be formed by repeated splits of this type are sometimes called blocks, although that name has also been used for the [biconnected components](wikipedia://en/4842394) of a graph that is not itself biconnected. An Apollonian network is a maximal planar graph in which all of the blocks are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to the complete graph $K_{4}$.

In [extremal graph theory](wikipedia://en/529568), Apollonian networks are also exactly the $n$-vertex planar graphs in which the number of blocks achieves its maximum, $n - 3$, and the planar graphs in which the number of triangles achieves its maximum, $3n - 8$. Since each $K_{4}$ subgraph of a planar graph must be a block, these are also the planar graphs in which the number of $K_{4}$ subgraphs achieves its maximum, $n - 3$, and the graphs in which the number of [cliques](wikipedia://en/524466) of any type achieves its maximum, $8n - 16$.[^o21]

## Geometric realizations {#Geometric_realizations}

### Construction from circle packings {#Construction_from_circle_packings}

*Image: Apollonian gasket.svg.* An example of an Apollonian gasket

*Image: Circle packing theorem K5 minus edge example.svg.* Construction of an Apollonian network from a circle packing

Apollonian networks are named after [Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242), who studied the [Problem of Apollonius](wikipedia://en/2642185) of constructing a circle tangent to three other circles. One method of constructing Apollonian networks is to start with three mutually-tangent circles and then repeatedly inscribe another circle within the gap formed by three previously-drawn circles. The fractal collection of circles produced in this way is called an [Apollonian gasket](wikipedia://en/1049256).

If the process of producing an Apollonian gasket is stopped early, with only a finite set of circles, then the graph that has one vertex for each circle and one edge for each pair of tangent circles is an Apollonian network.[^o24] The existence of a set of tangent circles whose tangencies represent a given Apollonian network forms a simple instance of the [Koebe–Andreev–Thurston circle-packing theorem](wikipedia://en/16648043), which states that any planar graph can be represented by tangent circles in the same way.[^o25]

### Polyhedra {#Polyhedra}

*Image: Triakistetrahedron.svg.* The [triakis tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/722548), a polyhedral realization of an 8-vertex Apollonian network

Apollonian networks are planar [3-connected graphs](wikipedia://en/7566175) and therefore, by [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817), can always be represented as the graphs of convex [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470). The convex polyhedron representing an Apollonian network is a 3-dimensional [stacked polytope](wikipedia://en/44760854). Such a polytope can be obtained from a tetrahedron by repeatedly gluing additional tetrahedra one at a time onto its triangular faces. Therefore, Apollonian networks may also be defined as the graphs of stacked 3d polytopes.[^o27] It is possible to find a representation of any Apollonian network as convex 3d polyhedron in which all of the coordinates are integers of polynomial size, better than what is known for other planar graphs.[^o28]

### Triangle meshes {#Triangle_meshes}

The recursive subdivision of triangles into three smaller triangles was investigated as an [image segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segmentation_%28image_processing%29) technique in [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision) by (Elcock Gargantini Walsh 1987); in this context, they called it the **ternary scalene triangle decomposition**. They observed that, by placing each new vertex at the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of its enclosing triangle, the triangulation could be chosen in such a way that all triangles have equal areas, although they do not all have the same shape.[^n3] More generally, Apollonian networks may be drawn in the plane with any prescribed area in each face; if the areas are [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), so are all of the vertex coordinates.[^o32]

It is also possible to carry out the process of subdividing a triangle to form an Apollonian network in such a way that, at every step, the edge lengths are rational numbers; it is an open problem whether every planar graph has a drawing with this property.[^o33] It is possible in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) to find a drawing of a planar 3-tree with integer coordinates minimizing the area of the [bounding box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounding_box) of the drawing, and to test whether a given planar 3-tree may be drawn with its vertices on a given set of points.[^o34]

## Properties and applications {#Properties_and_applications}

### Matching-free graphs {#Matching-free_graphs}

(Plummer 1992) used Apollonian networks to construct an infinite family of maximal planar graphs with an even number of vertices but with no [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031). Plummer's graphs are formed in two stages. In the first stage, starting from a triangle $abc$, one repeatedly subdivides the triangular face of the subdivision that contains edge $bc$: the result is a graph consisting of a path from $a$ to the final subdivision vertex together with an edge from each path vertex to each of $b$ and $c$. In the second stage, each of the triangular faces of the resulting planar graph is subdivided one more time. If the path from $a$ to the final subdivision vertex of the first stage has even length, then the number of vertices in the overall graph is also even. However, approximately 2/3 of the vertices are the ones inserted in the second stage; these form an [independent set](wikipedia://en/524501), and cannot be matched to each other, nor are there enough vertices outside the independent set to find matches for all of them.[^n4]

Although Apollonian networks themselves may not have perfect matchings, the [planar dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_dual) graphs of Apollonian networks are [3-regular graphs](wikipedia://en/980508) with no [cut edges](wikipedia://en/1305071), so by a theorem of (Petersen 1891) they are guaranteed to have at least one perfect matching. However, in this case more is known: the duals of Apollonian networks always have an exponential number of perfect matchings.[^o41] [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938) and [Michael D. Plummer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_D._Plummer) conjectured that a similar exponential lower bound holds more generally for every 3-regular graph without cut edges, a result that was later proven.

::: {#o43 .statement cue=named-heading name="Power law graphs"}

### Power law graphs {#Power_law_graphs}

(Andrade Herrmann Andrade da Silva 2005) studied [power laws](wikipedia://en/24522) in the [degree sequences](wikipedia://en/1374448) of a special case of networks of this type, formed by subdividing all triangles the same number of times. They used these networks to model packings of space by particles of varying sizes. Based on their work, other authors introduced random Apollonian networks, formed by repeatedly choosing a random face to subdivide, and they showed that these also obey power laws in their degree distribution [^o45] and have small average distances.[^o46] [Alan M. Frieze](wikipedia://en/22466549) and Charalampos E. Tsourakakis analyzed the highest degrees and the eigenvalues of random Apollonian networks.[^o47] Andrade et al. also observed that their networks satisfy the [small world effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_world_effect), that all vertices are within a small distance of each other. Based on numerical evidence they estimated the average distance between randomly selected pairs of vertices in an $n$-vertex network of this type to be proportional to $(\log n)^{3/4}$, but later researchers showed that the average distance is actually proportional to $\log n$.[^o48]

:::

### Angle distribution {#Angle_distribution}

(Butler Graham 2010) observed that if each new vertex is placed at the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) of its triangle, so that the edges to the new vertex [bisect the angles](wikipedia://en/152547#Angle_bisector) of the triangle, then the set of triples of angles of triangles in the subdivision, when reinterpreted as triples of [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinate_system_%28mathematics%29) of points in an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285), converges in shape to the [Sierpinski triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_triangle) as the number of levels of subdivision grows.[^n5]

### Hamiltonicity {#Hamiltonicity}

(Takeo 1960) claimed erroneously that all Apollonian networks have [Hamiltonian cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle); however, the [Goldner–Harary graph](wikipedia://en/24674758) provides a counterexample. If an Apollonian network has [toughness](wikipedia://en/8839340) greater than one (meaning that removing any set of vertices from the graph leaves a smaller number of connected components than the number of removed vertices) then it necessarily has a Hamiltonian cycle, but there exist non-Hamiltonian Apollonian networks whose toughness is equal to one.[^o54]

::: {#o56 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Enumeration"}

### Enumeration {#Enumeration}

The [combinatorial enumeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_enumeration) problem of counting Apollonian triangulations was studied by (Takeo 1960), who showed that they have the simple [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) $f(x)$ described by the equation $f(x) = 1 + x(f(x))^{3}$. In this generating function, the term of degree $n$ counts the number of Apollonian networks with a fixed outer triangle and $n + 3$ vertices. Thus, the numbers of Apollonian networks (with a fixed outer triangle) on 3, 4, 5, ... vertices are:

1, 1, 3, 12, 55, 273, 1428, 7752, 43263, 246675, ... ,

a sequence that also counts [ternary trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternary_tree) and dissections of convex polygons into odd-sided polygons. For instance, there are 12 6-vertex Apollonian networks: three formed by subdividing the outer triangle once and then subdividing two of the resulting triangles, and nine formed by subdividing the outer triangle once, subdividing one of its triangles, and then subdividing one of the resulting smaller triangles.

## History {#History}

(Birkhoff 1930) is an early paper that uses a dual form of Apollonian networks, the planar maps formed by repeatedly placing new regions at the vertices of simpler maps, as a class of examples of planar maps with few colorings.

Geometric structures closely related to Apollonian networks have been studied in [polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519) since at least the early 1960s, when they were used by (Grünbaum 1963) to describe graphs that can be realized as the graph of a polytope in only one way, without dimensional or combinatorial ambiguities, and by (Moon Moser 1963) to find [simplicial polytopes](wikipedia://en/8853373) with no long paths. In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the close connection between planarity and treewidth goes back to (Robertson Seymour 1984), who showed that every minor-closed family of graphs either has bounded treewidth or contains all of the planar graphs. Planar 3-trees, as a class of graphs, were explicitly considered by (Hakimi Schmeichel 1979), (Alon Caro 1984), (Patil 1986), and many authors since them.

The name "Apollonian network" was given by (Andrade Herrmann Andrade da Silva 2005) to the networks they studied in which the level of subdivision of triangles is uniform across the network; these networks correspond geometrically to a type of stacked polyhedron called a [Kleetope](wikipedia://en/31165912).[^o67] Other authors applied the same name more broadly to planar 3-trees in their work generalizing the model of Andrade et al. to random Apollonian networks.[^o46] The triangulations generated in this way have also been named "stacked triangulations"[^o68] or "stack-triangulations".[^o69]
