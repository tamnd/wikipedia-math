# Wiener algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wiener algebra"}

In mathematics, the **Wiener algebra**, named after [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185) and usually denoted by $A(\mathbf{T})$, is the space of [absolutely convergent](wikipedia://en/61532) [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). Here $\mathbf{T}$ denotes the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177).

:::

## Banach algebra structure {#Banach_algebra_structure}

The norm of a function $f \in A(\mathbf{T})$ is given by

$$\|f\|=\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty |\hat{f}(n)|,\,$$

where

$$\hat{f}(n)= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(t)e^{-int} \, dt$$

is the $n$th Fourier coefficient of $f$. The Wiener algebra $A(\mathbf{T})$ is closed under pointwise multiplication of functions. Indeed,

$$\begin{aligned} f(t)g(t) & = \sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}} \hat{f}(m)e^{imt}\,\cdot\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} \hat{g}(n)e^{int} \\ & = \sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}} \hat{f}(m)\hat{g}(n)e^{i(m+n)t} \\ & = \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} \left\{ \sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} \hat{f}(n-m)\hat{g}(m) \right\}e^{int} ,\qquad f,g\in A(\mathbb{T}); \end{aligned}$$

therefore

$$\|f g\| = \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} \left| \sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} \hat{f}(n-m)\hat{g}(m) \right| \leq \sum_{m} |\hat{f}(m)| \sum_n |\hat{g}(n)| = \|f\| \, \|g\|.\,$$

Thus the Wiener algebra is a commutative unitary [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665). Also, $A(\mathbf{T})$ is isomorphic to the Banach algebra $l_{1}(\mathbf{Z})$, with the isomorphism given by the Fourier transform.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The sum of an absolutely convergent Fourier series is continuous, so

$$A(\mathbb{T})\subset C(\mathbb{T})$$

where $C(\mathbf{T})$ is the ring of continuous functions on the unit circle.

On the other hand an [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252), together with the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128) and [Parseval's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parseval's_formula), shows that

$$C^1(\mathbb{T})\subset A(\mathbb{T}).\,$$

More generally,

$$\mathrm{Lip}_\alpha(\mathbb{T})\subset A(\mathbb{T})\subset C(\mathbb{T})$$

for $\alpha>1/2$ (see (Katznelson 2004)).

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Wiener's 1/f theorem"}

## Wiener's 1/*f* theorem {#Wiener's_1/f_theorem}

(txt) proved that if $f$ has absolutely convergent Fourier series and is never zero, then its reciprocal $1/f$ also has an absolutely convergent Fourier series. Many other proofs have appeared since then, including an elementary one by (txt).

(txt) used the theory of Banach algebras that he developed to show that the maximal ideals of $A(\mathbf{T})$ are of the form

$$M_x = \left\{ f \in A(\mathbb{T}) \, \mid \, f(x) = 0 \right\}, \quad x \in \mathbb{T}~,$$

which is equivalent to Wiener's theorem.

:::
