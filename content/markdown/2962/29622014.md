# Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel statistics"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test** (**CMH**) is a test used in the analysis of [stratified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratification_%28clinical_trials%29) or [matched](wikipedia://en/23389623) [categorical data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorical_data). It allows an investigator to test the association between a binary predictor or treatment and a binary outcome such as case or control status while taking into account the stratification.[^o2] Unlike the [McNemar test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McNemar_test), which can only handle pairs, the CMH test handles arbitrary strata sizes. It is named after [William G. Cochran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_G._Cochran), [Nathan Mantel](wikipedia://en/6184748) and [William Haenszel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Haenszel).[^o3][^o4] Extensions of this test to a categorical response and/or to several groups are commonly called Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel statistics.[^o5] It is often used in [observational studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_studies) in which random assignment of subjects to different treatments cannot be controlled but [confounding](wikipedia://en/3105999) covariates can be measured.

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

We consider a binary outcome variable such as case status (e.g. lung cancer) and a binary predictor such as treatment status (e.g. smoking). The observations are grouped in strata. The stratified data are summarized in a series of 2 × 2 [contingency tables](wikipedia://en/935515), one for each stratum. The *i*-th such contingency table is:

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

|  | Treatment | No treatment | Row total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Case | *A<sub>i</sub>* | *B<sub>i</sub>* | *N*<sub>1*i*</sub> |
| Controls | *C<sub>i</sub>* | *D<sub>i</sub>* | *N*<sub>2*i*</sub> |
| Column total | *M*<sub>1*i*</sub> | *M*<sub>2*i*</sub> | *T<sub>i</sub>* |

:::

The common [odds-ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odds-ratio) of the *K* contingency tables is defined as:

$$R = {{\sum_{i=1}^K \frac{A_i D_i}{T_i}} \over {\sum_{i=1}^K {B_i C_i \over T_i}}},$$

The null hypothesis is that there is no association between the treatment and the outcome. More precisely, the null hypothesis is $H_0: R=1$ and the alternative hypothesis is $H_1: R\ne 1$. The test statistic is:

$$\xi_\text{CMH} = \frac{ \left[ \sum_{i=1}^K \left(A_i - \frac{N_{1i} M_{1i}}{T_i}\right) \right]^2}{\sum_{i=1}^K {N_{1i}N_{2i}M_{1i}M_{2i} \over T_i^2(T_i-1)}}.$$

It follows a [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) asymptotically with 1 degree of freedom under the null hypothesis.[^o2]

:::

## Subset stability {#Subset_stability}

The standard odds- or [risk ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_ratio) of all strata could be calculated, giving risk ratios $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$, where $n$ is the number of strata. Let $R$ be the aggregate risk ratio if the stratification were eliminated and all data points placed in a single stratum.

One generally expects the risk of an event unconditional on the stratification to be bounded between the highest and lowest risk within the strata (or identically with [odds ratios](wikipedia://en/406880)). It is easy to construct examples where this is not the case, and $R$ is larger or smaller than all of $r_i$ for $i\in 1,\dots, n$. This is comparable but not identical to [Simpson's paradox](wikipedia://en/46096), and as with Simpson's paradox, it is difficult to interpret the statistic and decide policy based upon it.

Define a statistic to be *subset stable* iff $R$ is bounded between $\min(r_i)$ and $\max(r_i)$, and a *well-behaved* statistic as being [infinitely differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) and not dependent on the order of the strata; then the CMH statistic is the unique well-behaved statistic satisfying subset stability.[^o10]

## Related tests {#Related_tests}

- The [McNemar test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McNemar_test) can only handle pairs. The CMH test is a generalization of the [McNemar test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McNemar_test) as their test statistics are identical when each stratum shows a pair.[^o12]
- [Conditional logistic regression](wikipedia://en/45447625) is more general than the CMH test as it can handle continuous variable and perform multivariate analysis. When the CMH test can be applied, the CMH test statistic and the [score test](wikipedia://en/1599160) statistic of the [conditional logistic regression](wikipedia://en/45447625) are identical.[^o13]
- [Breslow–Day test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breslow–Day_test) for homogeneous association. The CMH test supposes that the effect of the treatment is homogeneous in all strata. The Breslow-Day test allows to test this assumption. This is not a concern if the strata are small e.g. pairs.
