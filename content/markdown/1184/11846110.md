# Jayanta Bhatta

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jayanta Bhatta"}

**Jayanta Bhatta** (c. 820 CE – c. 900 CE[^o3]) was a poet, teacher, logician, and an advisor to King [Sankaravarman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankaravarman) of [Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir). He was a philosopher of the [Nyaya](wikipedia://en/59941) school of [Hindu philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_philosophy).[^o4][^o5] He authored three works on [Nyāya](wikipedia://en/59941) philosophy: one of which is not known, an allegorical drama, and a commentary on [Pāṇinian grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pāṇini), He refutes the arguments given by [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism) philosophers like [Dharmakirti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmakirti) and [Dignāga](wikipedia://en/1280290) against the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas) and establishes the authority of the [Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas).[^o3]

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Jayanta was born into a wealthy [Gaur Brahmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaur_Brahmins) family.[^o8][^o5] He was a [child prodigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_prodigy), composing a commentary to [Panini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pāṇini)'s *[Ashtadhyayi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashtadhyayi)* and earned the name Nava-Vrittikara, or new commentator.[^o10] Later in life, he mastered various *[shastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shastra)* and *[agama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agama_%28Hinduism%29)*, distinguished himself in scholarly debates, and passed his knowledge on to his students.

Jayanta's birth year, lifespan, dates of his written works are a subject of scholarly debates. His philosophical work *Nyāyamañjarī* as well as his drama *[Āgamaḍambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Āgamaḍambara)*, refer to King [Sankaravarman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankaravarman) (883 – 902 CE) as a contemporary.

*Kādambarikathāsāra*, a work written by Jayanta's son Abhinanda, mentions that Jayanta's great grandfather was a minister of king [Lalitaditya Muktapida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalitaditya_Muktapida), who was a ruler of the Karkota dynasty in the second quarter of the 8th century CE. Several attempts to specify his life span are based on references to his work by other authors and references to contemporary events and individuals in his work. They range from the middle of the 8th century CE to the start of the 10th century CE. However, most reliable estimates place him around the 9th century CE.

### Lineage {#Lineage}

*Kādambarikathāsāra* provides some information about Jayanta's lineage. It says his ancestor Shakti was a [Gaur Brahmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaur_Brahmins) and a direct [patriline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriline) descendant of [Bharadwaja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharadwaja) gotra from the [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal), who lived in Darvabhisara, near the border of [Kashmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir). His son was named Mitra, and his grandson was Saktisvämin (Shaktisvamin).[^o8][^o9]

Saktisvämin, the great grandfather of Jayanta, was a minister of Kashmir [Lalitaditya Muktapida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalitaditya_Muktapida) of the Karkota dynasty (c. 724 – 761 CE). Jayanta mentions in *Nyayamanjari* that his grandfather obtained a village named Gauramulaka, believed to have been located north of the modern town of [Rajouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajouri), from King Muktapida. Saktisvämin had a son named Chandra, Jayanta's father.[^o10]

## Career {#Career}

The *[Agamadambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamadambara)* provides details about Bhatta's political career. He was an adviser to Kashmiri king [Sankaravarman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sankaravarman). In his position, he played a role in banishing the Nilambara (Black-Blankets) sect from Kashmir. Commenting on [Tantric literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantric_sex), he argued that the Nilambara sect promoted "immoral teachings". Jayanta claimed the Nilambara "wear simply one blue garment, and then as a group engages in unconstrained public sex". He argued that this practice was "unnecessary" and threatened the fundamental values of society.[^o12]

## Works {#Works}

### Philosophical works {#Philosophical_works}

Jayanta wrote three known treatises on [Nyaya](wikipedia://en/59941) philosophy, of which two survive. His first, the *Nyayamanjari* (A Cluster of Flowers of the Nyaya tree) is a commentary on Nyaya-aphorisms that serves as a critique of the theories of rival philosophical systems like the [Mīmānsādarśana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mīmāṃsā).

His second, the *Nyayakalika* (A Bud of the Nyaya tree) is an overview of the basic tenets of the [Nyāya Sūtras](wikipedia://en/1884117), a foundational text of the Nyaya school. His third work, *Pallava* (probably *Nyayapallava*, A Twig of the Nyaya tree) though quoted in *Syadvadaratnakara*, has not survived.[^o3][^o10]

Jayanta mentions in *Nyayamanjari* that he wrote this treatise during his confinement in a cave by the king.[^o15] This treatise is unique because it is an independent work, not a commentary of an earlier work.

Secondly, according to Jayanta, the purpose of Nyaya is to protect the authority of the *[Vedas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedas)*, Hinduism's oldest scriptures, whereas earlier Nyaya scholars considered Nyaya to be an *[Anvikshiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anviksiki)* (scientific study) providing true knowledge about the real nature of the objects of cognition.

#### Nyayamanjari {#Nyayamanjari}

Jayanta, in his Nyayamanjari, identifies ignorance (*[moha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohā)*), attachment (*[raga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raga_%28Sanskrit_term%29)*), and antipathy ([dvesha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dvesha)) as three distinct defects (*dosas*) that drive human actions:[^o17]

- *moha*: further includes *dosas* such as *[pramada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pramāda)* (carelessness), *mada* (sense of false superiority)
- *raga*: further includes *dosas* such as *[kama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kama)*, *[trsna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trsna)* (desire)
- *dvesha*: further includes *dosas* such as *droha* (harming others), *[irsya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Īrṣyā)* (jealousy)

Jayanta considers *moha* as the worst defect arguing that it gives rise to *raga* and *dvesha*. He further argues that all defects stem from false knowledge and are resolved by right knowledge, however, each defect is psychologically distinct.[^o17]

### Literary works {#Literary_works}

His major literary work is *[Āgamaḍambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Āgamaḍambara)*, a Sanskrit play in four acts. The hero of his quasi-philosophical drama is a young graduate of the [Mimansa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimansa) school, who wants to defeat all opponents of Vedas through reasoning.[^o10]

## Philosophy {#Philosophy}

### God {#God}

The discussion of God's Existence is found in part 1 of *Nyaya Manjuri*. Jayanta adheres to a realist viewpoint of God and the world and defends the possibility of reasoned arguments favoring God as a realistic and adequate cause of the world.[^o21]

### Criticism of Lokayats {#Criticism_of_Lokayats}

Jayanta Bhatta criticized the [Lokayata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charvaka) school of philosophy for not developing a Lokayata culture. He said, "The Lokayata is not an [Agama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agama_%28Hinduism%29). viz. not a guide to cultural living, not a system of do's and dont's; hence it is nothing but irresponsible wrangling."[^o23]

## English translations {#English_translations}

The [Clay Sanskrit Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_Sanskrit_Library) published a translation of *[Āgamaḍambara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Āgamaḍambara)* by Csaba Dezső under the title of *Much Ado about Religion*.
