# Quantities of information

*Image: Entropy-mutual-information-relative-entropy-relation-diagram.svg.* A simple [information diagram](wikipedia://en/18372173) showing additive and subtractive relationships among [Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693)'s basic quantities of information for correlated variables $X$ and $Y$. The area contained by both circles is the [joint entropy](wikipedia://en/910967) $\mathrm{H}(X,Y)$. The circle on the left (red and violet) is the [individual entropy](wikipedia://en/15445) $\mathrm{H}(X)$, with the red being the [conditional entropy](wikipedia://en/908548) $\mathrm{H}(X|Y)$. The circle on the right (blue and violet) is $\mathrm{H}(Y)$, with the blue being $\mathrm{H}(Y|X)$. The violet is the [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282) $\operatorname{I}(X;Y)$. Positive areas may represent negative quantities. [^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Quantities of information"}

The [mathematical theory of information](wikipedia://en/14773) is based on [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), and measures information with several **quantities of information**, where *[quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantity_%28science%29)* is a property that can be quantified by measurement.

:::

## Units {#Units}

The choice of [logarithmic base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_base) in the following formulae determines the *[units of information](wikipedia://en/18075548)* being used. The most common [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement) of [information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy) is the [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit), or more correctly the [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_%28unit%29),[^o4] based on the [binary logarithm](wikipedia://en/249992). Although *bit* is more frequently used in place of *shannon*, its name is not distinguished from the bit as used in data processing to refer to a binary value or stream regardless of its entropy (information content). Other units include the [nat](wikipedia://en/3070481), based on the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), and the [hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciban), based on the base 10 or [common logarithm](wikipedia://en/174482).

## Self-information {#Self-information}

Shannon derived a measure of information content called the **[self-information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-information)** or **"surprisal"** of a message $m$:

$$\operatorname{I}(m) = \log \left( \frac{1}{p(m)} \right) = - \log( p(m) ) \,$$

where $p(m) = \mathrm{Pr}(M=m)$ is the probability that message $m$ is chosen from all possible choices in the message space $M$.[^o6] The base of the logarithm only affects a scaling factor and, consequently, the units in which the measured information content is expressed. If the logarithm is base 2, the measure of information is expressed in units of [shannons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_%28unit%29) or more often simply "bits" (a [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) in other contexts is rather defined as a "binary digit", whose [average information content](wikipedia://en/5275277) is at most 1 shannon).

Information from a source is gained by a recipient only if the recipient did not already have that information to begin with. Messages that convey information over a certain (P=1) event (or one which is *known* with certainty, for instance, through a back-channel) provide no information, as the above equation indicates. Infrequently occurring messages contain more information than more frequently occurring messages.

It can also be shown that a compound message of two (or more) unrelated messages would have a quantity of information that is the sum of the measures of information of each message individually. That can be derived using this definition by considering a compound message $m\&n$ providing information regarding the values of two random variables M and N using a message which is the concatenation of the elementary messages *m* and *n*, each of whose information content are given by $\operatorname{I}(m)$ and $\operatorname{I}(n)$ respectively. If the messages *m* and *n* each depend only on M and N, and the processes M and N are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence), then since $P(m\&n) = P(m) P(n)$ (the definition of statistical independence) it is clear from the above definition that $\operatorname{I}(m\&n) = \operatorname{I}(m) + \operatorname{I}(n)$.

An example: The weather forecast broadcast is: "Tonight's forecast: Dark. Continued darkness until widely scattered light in the morning." This message contains almost no information. However, a forecast of a snowstorm would certainly contain information since such does not happen every evening. There would be an even greater amount of information in an accurate forecast of snow for a warm location, such as [Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami). The amount of information in a forecast of snow for a location where it never snows (impossible event) is the highest (infinity).

## Entropy {#Entropy}

The **[entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy)** of a discrete message space $M$ is a measure of the amount of **uncertainty** one has about which message will be chosen. It is defined as the [average](wikipedia://en/9653) self-information of a message $m$ from that message space:[^o8]

$$\mathrm{H}(M) = \mathbb{E} \left[\operatorname{I}(M)\right] = \sum_{m \in M} p(m) \operatorname{I}(m) = -\sum_{m \in M} p(m) \log p(m)$$

where

$\mathbb{E} [ \cdot ]$ denotes the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) operation.

An important property of entropy is that it is maximized when all the messages in the message space are equiprobable (e.g. $p(m) = 1/|M|$). In this case $\mathrm{H}(M) = \log |M|$.

Sometimes the function $\mathrm{H}$ is expressed in terms of the probabilities of the distribution:

$\mathrm{H}(p_1, p_2, \ldots , p_k) = -\sum_{i=1}^k p_i \log p_i,$ where each $p_i \geq 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^k p_i = 1$

An important special case of this is the **[binary entropy function](wikipedia://en/5275277)**:

$$\mathrm{H}_\mbox{b}(p) = \mathrm{H}(p, 1-p) = - p \log p - (1-p)\log (1-p)\,$$

## Joint entropy {#Joint_entropy}

The **[joint entropy](wikipedia://en/910967)** of two discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ is defined as the entropy of the [joint distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution) of $X$ and $Y$:[^o8]

$$\mathrm{H}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}_{X,Y} \left[-\log p(x,y)\right] = - \sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \log p(x, y) \,$$

If $X$ and $Y$ are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence), then the joint entropy is simply the sum of their individual entropies.

(Note: The joint entropy should not be confused with the [cross entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_entropy), despite similar notations.)

## Conditional entropy (equivocation) {#Conditional_entropy_(equivocation)}

Given a particular value of a random variable $Y$, the conditional entropy of $X$ given $Y=y$ is defined as:[^o8]

$$\mathrm{H}(X|y) = \mathbb{E}_{\left[X|Y \right]} [-\log p(x|y)] = -\sum_{x \in X} p(x|y) \log p(x|y)$$

where $p(x|y) = \frac{p(x,y)}{p(y)}$ is the [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) of $x$ given $y$.

The **[conditional entropy](wikipedia://en/908548)** of $X$ given $Y$, also called the **equivocation**[^o8] of $X$ about $Y$ is then given by:

$$\mathrm{H}(X|Y) = \mathbb{E}_Y \left[\mathrm{H}\left(X|y \right)\right] = -\sum_{y \in Y} p(y) \sum_{x \in X} p(x|y) \log p(x|y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log \frac{p(y)}{p(x,y)}.$$

This uses the [conditional expectation](wikipedia://en/435099) from probability theory.

A basic property of the conditional entropy is that:

$$\mathrm{H}(X|Y) = \mathrm{H}(X,Y) - \mathrm{H}(Y) .\,$$

## Kullback–Leibler divergence (information gain) {#Kullback–Leibler_divergence_(information_gain)}

The **[Kullback–Leibler divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence)** (or **information divergence**, **information gain**, or **relative entropy**) is a way of comparing two distributions, a "true" [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) $p$, and an arbitrary probability distribution $q$.[^o12] If we compress data in a manner that assumes $q$ is the distribution underlying some data, when, in reality, $p$ is the correct distribution, Kullback–Leibler divergence is the number of average additional bits per datum necessary for compression, or, mathematically,

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p(X) \| q(X) \bigr) = \sum_{x \in X} p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.$$

It is in some sense the "distance" from $q$ to $p$, although it is not a true [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) due to its not being symmetric.

## Mutual information (transinformation) {#Mutual_information_(transinformation)}

It turns out that one of the most useful and important measures of information is the **[mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282)**, or **transinformation**. This is a measure of how much information can be obtained about one [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) by observing another. The mutual information of $X$ relative to $Y$ (which represents conceptually the average amount of information about $X$ that can be gained by observing $Y$) is given by:[^o8]

$$\operatorname{I}(X;Y) = \sum_{y\in Y} p(y)\sum_{x\in X} {p(x|y) \log \frac{p(x|y)}{p(x)}} = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\, p(y)}.$$

A basic property of the mutual information is that:

$$\operatorname{I}(X;Y) = \mathrm{H}(X) - \mathrm{H}(X|Y).\,$$

That is, knowing $Y$, we can save an average of $\operatorname{I}(X; Y)$ bits in encoding $X$ compared to not knowing $Y$. Mutual information is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/22510359):

$$\operatorname{I}(X;Y) = \operatorname{I}(Y;X) = \mathrm{H}(X) + \mathrm{H}(Y) - \mathrm{H}(X,Y).\,$$

Mutual information can be expressed as the average [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) (information gain) of the [posterior probability distribution](wikipedia://en/357672) of $X$ given the value of $Y$ to the [prior distribution](wikipedia://en/472877) on $X$:

$$\operatorname{I}(X;Y) = \mathbb{E}_{p(y)} \left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl( p(X|Y=y) \| p(X) \bigr)\right].$$

In other words, this is a measure of how much, on the average, the probability distribution on $X$ will change if we are given the value of $Y$. This is often recalculated as the divergence from the product of the marginal distributions to the actual joint distribution:

$$\operatorname{I}(X; Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p(X,Y) \| p(X)p(Y) \bigr).$$

Mutual information is closely related to the [log-likelihood ratio test](wikipedia://en/45035) in the context of contingency tables and the [multinomial distribution](wikipedia://en/1045553) and to [Pearson's χ<sup>2</sup> test](wikipedia://en/226676): mutual information can be considered a statistic for assessing independence between a pair of variables, and has a well-specified [asymptotic distribution](wikipedia://en/3001287).

## Differential entropy {#Differential_entropy}

The basic measures of discrete entropy have been extended by analogy to [continuous](wikipedia://en/23543#Continuous_probability_distribution) spaces by replacing sums with integrals and [probability mass functions](wikipedia://en/154725) with [probability density functions](wikipedia://en/43487). Although, in both cases, mutual information expresses the number of bits of information common to the two sources in question, the analogy does *not* imply identical properties; for example, differential entropy may be negative. [^o8]

The differential analogies of entropy, joint entropy, conditional entropy, and mutual information are defined as follows:

$$h(X) = -\int_X f(x) \log f(x) \,dx$$

$$h(X,Y) = -\int_Y \int_X f(x,y) \log f(x,y) \,dx \,dy$$

$$h(X|y) = -\int_X f(x|y) \log f(x|y) \,dx$$

$$h(X|Y) = \int_Y \int_X f(x,y) \log \frac{f(y)}{f(x,y)} \,dx \,dy$$

$$\operatorname{I}(X;Y) = \int_Y \int_X f(x,y) \log \frac{f(x,y)}{f(x)f(y)} \,dx \,dy$$

where $f(x,y)$ is the joint density function, $f(x)$ and $f(y)$ are the marginal distributions, and $f(x|y)$ is the conditional distribution.
