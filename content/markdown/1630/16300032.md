# Discrete Fourier transform over a ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete Fourier transform over a ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **discrete Fourier transform over a ring** generalizes the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) (DFT), of a function whose values are commonly [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), over an arbitrary [ring](wikipedia://en/48404).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $R$ be any [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), let $n\geq 1$ be an integer, and let $\alpha \in R$ be a [principal](wikipedia://en/14593201) *n*th root of unity, defined by:[^o4]

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{aligned} & \alpha^n = 1 \\ & \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \alpha^{jk} = 0 \text{ for } 1 \leq k < n \end{aligned} \tag{1}$$

:::

The discrete Fourier transform maps an [*n*-tuple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-tuple) $(v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1})$ of elements of $R$ to another *n*-tuple $(f_0,\ldots,f_{n-1})$ of elements of $R$ according to the following formula:

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$f_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} v_j\alpha^{jk}. \tag{2}$$

:::

By convention, the tuple $(v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1})$ is said to be in the *time domain* and the index $j$ is called *time*. The tuple $(f_0,\ldots,f_{n-1})$ is said to be in the *frequency domain* and the index $k$ is called *frequency*. The tuple $(f_0,\ldots,f_{n-1})$ is also called the *[spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_spectrum)* of $(v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1})$. This terminology derives from the applications of Fourier transforms in [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324).

If $R$ is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) (which includes [fields](wikipedia://en/10603)), it is sufficient to choose $\alpha$ as a [primitive *n*th root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity), which replaces the condition ((1)) by:[^o4]

$\alpha^{k} \ne 1$ for $1 \leq k < n$

::: {#o7 .proof cue=math-proof}

Take $\beta = \alpha^k$ with $1 \leq k < n$. Since $\alpha^n=1$, $\beta^n=(\alpha^n)^k=1$, giving:

$\beta^n-1 = (\beta-1)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \beta^j\right) = 0$ where the sum matches ((1)). Since $\alpha$ is a primitive root of unity, $\beta - 1 \ne 0$. Since $R$ is an integral domain, the sum must be zero. ∎

:::

Another simple condition applies in the case where *n* is a power of two: ((1)) may be replaced by $\alpha^{n/2} = -1$.[^o4]

:::

## Inverse {#Inverse}

The inverse of the discrete Fourier transform is given as:

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$v_j = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f_k\alpha^{-jk}. \tag{3}$$

:::

where $1/n$ is the multiplicative inverse of $n$ in $R$ (if this inverse does not exist, the DFT cannot be inverted).

::: {#o10 .proof cue=math-proof}

Substituting ((2)) into the right-hand-side of ((3)), we get

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f_k\alpha^{-jk} \\ = {} & \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j'=0}^{n-1} v_{j'}\alpha^{j'k}\alpha^{-jk} \\ = {} & \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j'=0}^{n-1} v_{j'} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha^{(j'-j)k}. \end{aligned}$$

This is exactly equal to $v_j$, because $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha^{(j'-j)k}=0$ when $j'\neq j$ (by ((1)) with $k=j'-j$), and $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\alpha^{(j'-j)k}=n$ when $j'=j$. ∎

:::

## Matrix formulation {#Matrix_formulation}

Since the discrete Fourier transform is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), it can be described by [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). In matrix notation, the discrete Fourier transform is expressed as follows:

$$\begin{bmatrix}f_0\\f_1\\\vdots\\f_{n-1}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1&1&1&\cdots &1 \\ 1&\alpha&\alpha^2&\cdots&\alpha^{n-1} \\ 1&\alpha^2&\alpha^4&\cdots&\alpha^{2(n-1)}\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 1&\alpha^{n-1}&\alpha^{2(n-1)}&\cdots&\alpha^{(n-1)(n-1)}\\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}v_0\\v_1\\\vdots\\v_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.$$

The matrix for this transformation is called the [DFT matrix](wikipedia://en/1090930).

Similarly, the matrix notation for the inverse Fourier transform is

$$\begin{bmatrix}v_0\\v_1\\\vdots\\v_{n-1}\end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{n}\begin{bmatrix} 1&1&1&\cdots &1 \\ 1&\alpha^{-1}&\alpha^{-2}&\cdots&\alpha^{-(n-1)} \\ 1&\alpha^{-2}&\alpha^{-4}&\cdots&\alpha^{-2(n-1)}\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 1&\alpha^{-(n-1)}&\alpha^{-2(n-1)}&\cdots&\alpha^{-(n-1)(n-1)} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}f_0\\f_1\\\vdots\\f_{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.$$

## Polynomial formulation {#Polynomial_formulation}

Sometimes it is convenient to identify an $n$-tuple $(v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1})$ with a formal polynomial

$$p_v(x) = v_0 + v_1x + v_2x^2 + \cdots + v_{n-1}x^{n-1}. \,$$

By writing out the summation in the definition of the discrete Fourier transform ((2)), we obtain:

$$f_k = v_0 + v_1\alpha^{k} + v_2\alpha^{2k} + \cdots + v_{n-1}\alpha^{(n-1)k}. \,$$

This means that $f_k$ is just the value of the polynomial $p_v(x)$ for $x=\alpha^k$, i.e.,

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$f_k = p_v(\alpha^k).\, \tag{4}$$

:::

The Fourier transform can therefore be seen to relate the *coefficients* and the *values* of a polynomial: the coefficients are in the time-domain, and the values are in the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346). Here, of course, it is important that the polynomial is evaluated at the $n$th roots of unity, which are exactly the powers of $\alpha$.

Similarly, the definition of the inverse Fourier transform ((3)) can be written:

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$v_j = \frac{1}{n}(f_0 + f_1\alpha^{-j} + f_2\alpha^{-2j} + \cdots + f_{n-1}\alpha^{-(n-1)j}). \tag{5}$$

:::

With

$$p_f(x) = f_0 + f_1x + f_2x^2 + \cdots + f_{n-1}x^{n-1},$$

this means that

$$v_j = \frac{1}{n}p_f(\alpha^{-j}).$$

We can summarize this as follows: if the *values* of $p_v(x)$ are the *coefficients* of $p_f(x)$, then the *values* of $p_f(x)$ are the *coefficients* of $p_v(x)$, up to a scalar factor and reordering.[^o15]

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

### Complex numbers {#Complex_numbers}

If $F={\mathbb C}$ is the field of complex numbers, then the $n$th roots of unity can be visualized as points on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). In this case, one usually takes

$$\alpha=e^{\frac{-2\pi i}{n}},$$

which yields the usual formula for the [complex discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811):

$$f_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} v_j e^{\frac{-2\pi i}{n}jk}.$$

Over the complex numbers, it is often customary to normalize the formulas for the DFT and inverse DFT by using the scalar factor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ in both formulas, rather than $1$ in the formula for the DFT and $\frac{1}{n}$ in the formula for the inverse DFT. With this normalization, the DFT matrix is then unitary. Note that $\sqrt{n}$ does not make sense in an arbitrary field.

### Finite fields {#Finite_fields}

If $F=\mathrm{GF}(q)$ is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), where $q$ is a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) power, then the existence of a primitive $n$th root automatically implies that $n$ [divides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divides) $q-1$, because the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) of each element must divide the size of the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of $F$, which is $q-1$. This in particular ensures that $n=\underbrace{1+1+\cdots+1}_{n\ \rm times}$ is invertible, so that the notation $\frac{1}{n}$ in ((3)) makes sense.

An application of the discrete Fourier transform over $\mathrm{GF}(q)$ is the reduction of [Reed–Solomon codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed–Solomon_code) to [BCH codes](wikipedia://en/40779) in [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869). Such transform can be carried out efficiently with proper fast algorithms, for example, [cyclotomic fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/41435967).

#### Polynomial formulation without n<sup>th</sup> root {#Polynomial_formulation_without_nth_root}

Suppose $F=\mathrm{GF}(p)$. If $p\nmid n$, it may be the case that $n \nmid p-1$. This means we cannot find an $n^{th}$ root of unity in $F$. We may view the Fourier transform as an isomorphism $\mathrm{F}[C_n]=\mathrm{F}[x]/(x^n-1) \cong \bigoplus_i \mathrm{F}[x]/(P_i(x))$ for some polynomials $P_i(x)$, in accordance with [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007). The map is given by the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713), and the inverse is given by applying [Bézout's identity](wikipedia://en/4947) for polynomials.[^o21]

$x^n-1=\prod_{d|n} \Phi_d(x)$, a product of cyclotomic polynomials. Factoring $\Phi_d(x)$ in $F[x]$ is equivalent to factoring the [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) $(p)$ in $\mathrm{Z}[\zeta] = \mathrm{Z}[x]/(\Phi_d(x))$. We obtain $g$ polynomials $P_1 \ldots P_g$ of degree $f$ where $fg = \varphi(d)$ and $f$ is the order of $p \text{ mod } d$.

As above, we may extend the base field to $\mathrm{GF}(q)$ in order to find a primitive root, i.e. a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) for $x^n-1$. Now $x^n-1 = \prod_k (x-\alpha^{k})$, so an element $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} v_j x^j \in F[x]/(x^n-1)$ maps to $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} v_j x^j \mod (x-\alpha^k) \equiv \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} v_j (\alpha^k)^j$ for each $k$.

#### When p divides n {#When_p_divides_n}

When $p|n$, we may still define an $F_p$-linear isomorphism as above. Note that $(x^n-1)=(x^m-1)^{p^s}$ where $n=m p^s$ and $p \nmid m$. We apply the above factorization to $x^m-1$, and now obtain the decomposition $F[x]/(x^n-1) \cong \bigoplus_i F[x]/(P_i(x)^{p^s})$. The modules occurring are now indecomposable rather than irreducible.

#### Order of the DFT matrix {#Order_of_the_DFT_matrix}

Suppose $p \nmid n$ so we have an $n^{th}$ root of unity $\alpha$. Let $A$ be the above DFT matrix, a [Vandermonde matrix](wikipedia://en/348641) with entries $A_{ij}=\alpha^{ij}$ for $0 \le i,j < n$. Recall that $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \alpha^{(k-l)j} = n\delta_{k,l}$ since if $k=l$, then every entry is 1. If $k \ne l$, then we have a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) with common ratio $\alpha^{k-l}$, so we obtain $\frac{1-\alpha^{n(k-l)}}{1-\alpha^{k-l}}$. Since $\alpha^n=1$ the numerator is zero, but $k-l \ne 0$ so the denominator is nonzero.

First computing the square, $(A^2)_{ik} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \alpha^{j(i+k)} = n\delta_{i,-k}$. Computing $A^4=(A^2)^2$ similarly and simplifying the deltas, we obtain $(A^4)_{ik}=n^2\delta_{i,k}$. Thus, $A^4=n^2 I_n$ and the order is $4\cdot \text{ord}(n^2)$.

#### Normalizing the DFT matrix {#Normalizing_the_DFT_matrix}

In order to align with the complex case and ensure the matrix is order 4 exactly, we can normalize the above DFT matrix $A$ with $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$. Note that though $\sqrt{n}$ may not exist in the splitting field $F_q$ of $x^n-1$, we may form a quadratic extension $F_{q^2} \cong F_q[x]/(x^2-n)$ in which the square root exists. We may then set $U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}A$, and $U^4=I_n$.

#### Unitarity {#Unitarity}

Suppose $p \nmid n$. One can ask whether the DFT matrix is [unitary over a finite field](wikipedia://en/173993#finite_fields). If the matrix entries are over $F_q$, then one must ensure $q$ is a perfect square or extend to $F_{q^2}$ in order to define the order two automorphism $x \mapsto x^q$. Consider the above DFT matrix $A_{ij}=\alpha^{ij}$. Note that $A$ is symmetric. Conjugating and transposing, we obtain $A_{ij}^{*}=\alpha^{qji}$.

$$(AA^*)_{ik} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\alpha^{j(i+qk)} = n\delta_{i,-qk}$$

by a similar geometric series argument as above. We may remove the $n$ by normalizing so that $U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}A$ and $(UU^*)_{ik} = \delta_{i,-qk}$. Thus $U$ is unitary iff $q \equiv -1 \,(\text{mod} \, n)$. Recall that since we have an $n^{th}$ root of unity, $n|q^2-1$. This means that $q^2 - 1 \equiv (q+1)(q-1) \equiv 0 \,(\text{mod} \, n)$. Note if $q$ was not a perfect square to begin with, then $n|q-1$ and so $q \equiv 1 \, (\text{mod} \, n)$.

For example, when $p=3, n=5$ we need to extend to $q^2=3^4$ to get a 5th root of unity. $q=9 \equiv -1 \, (\text{mod} \, 5)$.

For a nonexample, when $p=3, n=8$ we extend to $F_{3^2}$ to get an 8th root of unity. $q^2=9$, so $q \equiv 3 \,(\text{mod} \, 8)$, and in this case $q+1 \not\equiv 0$ and $q-1 \not\equiv 0$. $UU^*$ is a square root of the identity, so $U$ is not unitary.

#### Eigenvalues of the DFT matrix {#Eigenvalues_of_the_DFT_matrix}

When $p \nmid n$, we have an $n^{th}$ root of unity $\alpha$ in the splitting field $F_q \cong F_p[x]/(x^n-1)$. Note that the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of the above DFT matrix may not split over $F_q$. The DFT matrix is order 4. We may need to go to a further extension $F_{q'}$, the splitting extension of the characteristic polynomial of the DFT matrix, which at least contains fourth roots of unity. If $a$ is a generator of the multiplicative group of $F_{q'}$, then the eigenvalues are $\{\pm 1,\pm a^{(q'-1)/4}\}$, in exact analogy with the complex case. They occur with some nonnegative multiplicity.

### Number-theoretic transform {#Number-theoretic_transform}

The **number-theoretic transform (NTT)**[^o29] is obtained by specializing the discrete Fourier transform to $F={\mathbb Z}/p$, the [integers modulo a prime $p$](wikipedia://en/20087). This is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), and primitive $n$th roots of unity exist whenever $n$ divides $p-1$, so we have $p=\xi n+1$ for a positive integer $\xi$. Specifically, let $\omega$ be a primitive $(p-1)$th root of unity, then an $n$th root of unity $\alpha$ can be found by letting $\alpha=\omega^{\xi}$.

e.g. for $p=5$, $\alpha = 2$

$$\begin{aligned}2^{1}&=2 \pmod 5\\2^{2}&=4 \pmod 5\\2^{3}&=3 \pmod 5\\2^{4}&=1 \pmod 5\end{aligned}$$

when $N=4$

$$\begin{bmatrix} F(0) \\ F(1) \\ F(2) \\ F(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} f(0) \\ f(1) \\ f(2) \\ f(3) \end{bmatrix}$$

The number theoretic transform may be meaningful in the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $\mathbb{Z}/m$, even when the modulus $m$ is not prime, provided a principal root of order $n$ exists. Special cases of the number theoretic transform such as the [Fermat Number](wikipedia://en/91127) Transform ($m = 2^{k}+1$), used by the [Schönhage–Strassen algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schönhage–Strassen_algorithm), or Mersenne Number Transform[^o30] ($m = 2^{k} - 1$) use a composite modulus.

In general, if $m = \prod_i p_i^{e_i}$, then one may find an [$n^{th}$ root of unity mod $m$](wikipedia://en/30949769) by finding primitive $n^{th}$ roots of unity $g_i$ mod $p_i^{e_i}$, yielding a tuple $g=\left(g_i\right)_i \in \prod_i \left(\mathbb{Z}/p_i^{e_i}\mathbb{Z}\right)^\ast$. The preimage of $g$ under the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) isomorphism is an $n^{th}$ root of unity $\alpha$ such that $\alpha^{n/2} = -1 \mod m$. This ensures that the above summation conditions are satisfied. We must have that $n|\varphi(p_i^{e_i})$ for each $i$, where $\varphi$ is the [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452).[^o31]

[Fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) can be adapted to NTT and implemented with only integer operations.[^o32] Some choices of *m* such as the [Solinas prime](wikipedia://en/24257463) $2^{64}-2^{32}+1$ are even easier to calculate on computers as they require no division operation for reduction.[^o33]

:::

### Discrete weighted transform {#Discrete_weighted_transform}

The **discrete weighted transform (DWT)** is a variation on the discrete Fourier transform over arbitrary rings involving [weighting](wikipedia://en/245523) the input before transforming it by multiplying elementwise by a weight vector, then weighting the result by another vector.[^o36] The [Irrational base discrete weighted transform](wikipedia://en/662181) is a special case of this.

## Properties {#Properties}

Most of the important attributes of the [complex DFT](wikipedia://en/8811), including the inverse transform, the [convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/53268), and most [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT) algorithms, depend only on the property that the kernel of the transform is a principal root of unity. These properties also hold, with identical proofs, over arbitrary rings. In the case of fields, this analogy can be formalized by the [field with one element](wikipedia://en/13798040), considering any field with a primitive *n*th root of unity as an algebra over the extension field $\mathbf{F}_{1^n}.$

In particular, the applicability of $O(n \log n)$ [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) algorithms to compute the NTT, combined with the convolution theorem, mean that the [number-theoretic transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number-theoretic_transform) gives an efficient way to compute exact [convolutions](wikipedia://en/7519) of integer sequences. While the complex DFT can perform the same task, it is susceptible to [round-off error](wikipedia://en/432450) in finite-precision [floating point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_point) arithmetic; the NTT has no round-off because it deals purely with fixed-size integers that can be exactly represented.

:::

## Fast algorithms {#Fast_algorithms}

For the implementation of a "fast" algorithm (similar to how [FFT](wikipedia://en/11512) computes the [DFT](wikipedia://en/8811)), it is often desirable that the transform length is also highly composite, e.g., a [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948). However, there are specialized fast Fourier transform algorithms for finite fields, such as Wang and Zhu's algorithm,[^o40] that are efficient regardless of the transform length factors.
