# Fréchet–Urysohn space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fréchet–Urysohn space"}

In the field of [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_%28mathematics%29), a **Fréchet–Urysohn space** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ with the property that for every subset $S \subseteq X$ the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of $S$ in $X$ is identical to the *sequential* closure of $S$ in $X.$ Fréchet–Urysohn spaces are a special type of [sequential space](wikipedia://en/4668395).

:::

The property is named after [Maurice Fréchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Fréchet) and [Pavel Urysohn](wikipedia://en/23770).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let $(X, \tau)$ be a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). The **sequential closure** of $S$ in $(X, \tau)$ is the set:

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} \operatorname{scl} S :&= [ S]_{\operatorname{seq}} := \left\{ x \in X ~:~ \text{ there exists a sequence } s_{\bullet} = \left(s_i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty} \subseteq S \text{ in } S \text{ such that } s_{\bullet} \to x \text{ in } (X, \tau) \right\} \end{alignedat}$$

where $\operatorname{scl}_X S$ or $\operatorname{scl}_{(X, \tau)} S$ may be written if clarity is needed.

A topological space $(X, \tau)$ is said to be a **Fréchet–Urysohn space** if

$$\operatorname{cl}_X S = \operatorname{scl}_X S$$

for every subset $S \subseteq X,$ where $\operatorname{cl}_X S$ denotes the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of $S$ in $(X, \tau).$

### Sequentially open/closed sets {#Sequentially_open/closed_sets}

Suppose that $S \subseteq X$ is any subset of $X.$ A sequence $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ is **eventually in $S$** if there exists a [positive integer](wikipedia://en/21474) $N$ such that $x_i \in S$ for all indices $i \geq N.$

The set $S$ is called **sequentially open** if every sequence $\left(x_i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ in $X$ that converges to a point of $S$ is eventually in $S$; Typically, if $X$ is understood then $\operatorname{scl} S$ is written in place of $\operatorname{scl}_X S.$

The set $S$ is called **sequentially closed** if $S = \operatorname{scl}_X S,$ or equivalently, if whenever $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ is a sequence in $S$ converging to $x,$ then $x$ must also be in $S.$ The [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of a sequentially [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) is a sequentially [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279), and vice versa.

Let

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} \operatorname{SeqOpen} (X, \tau) :&= \left\{ S \subseteq X ~:~ S \text{ is sequentially open in } (X, \tau) \right\} \\ &= \left\{ S \subseteq X ~:~ S = \operatorname{SeqInt}_{(X, \tau)} S \right\} \\ \end{alignedat}$$

denote the set of all sequentially open subsets of $(X, \tau),$ where this may be denoted by $\operatorname{SeqOpen} X$ is the topology $\tau$ is understood. The set $\operatorname{SeqOpen} (X, \tau)$ is a [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_%28structure%29) on $X$ that is [finer](wikipedia://en/393069) than the original topology $\tau.$ Every open (resp. closed) subset of $X$ is sequentially open (resp. sequentially closed), which implies that

$$\tau \subseteq \operatorname{SeqOpen} (X, \tau).$$

### Strong Fréchet–Urysohn space {#Strong_Fréchet–Urysohn_space}

A topological space $X$ is a **strong Fréchet–Urysohn space** if for every point $x \in X$ and every sequence $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ of subsets of the space $X$ such that $x \in \bigcap_n \overline{A_n},$ there exist a sequence $\left( a_i \right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ in $X$ such that $a_i \in A_i$ for every $i \in \mathbb{N}$ and $\left( a_i \right)_{i=1}^{\infty} \to x$ in $(X, \tau).$ The above properties can be expressed as [selection principles](wikipedia://en/50398478).

### Contrast to sequential spaces {#Contrast_to_sequential_spaces}

Every open subset of $X$ is sequentially open and every closed set is sequentially closed. However, the converses are in general not true. The spaces for which the converses are true are called **[sequential spaces](wikipedia://en/4668395)**; that is, a sequential space is a topological space in which every sequentially open subset is necessarily open, or equivalently, it is a space in which every sequentially closed subset is necessarily closed. Every Fréchet-Urysohn space is a sequential space but there are sequential spaces that are not Fréchet-Urysohn spaces.

Sequential spaces (respectively, Fréchet-Urysohn spaces) can be viewed/interpreted as exactly those spaces $X$ where for any single given subset $S \subseteq X,$ knowledge of which sequences in $X$ converge to which point(s) of $X$ (and which do not) is sufficient to **determine whether or not** $S$ is closed in $X$ (respectively, is sufficient to **determine the closure** of $S$ in $X$).[^o7] Thus sequential spaces are those spaces $X$ for which sequences in $X$ can be used as a "test" to determine whether or not any given subset is open (or equivalently, closed) in $X$; or said differently, sequential spaces are those spaces whose topologies can be completely characterized in terms of sequence convergence. In any space that is not sequential, there exists a subset for which this "test" gives a "[false positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_positive)."[^o8]

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

If $(X, \tau)$ is a topological space then the following are equivalent:

$X$ is a Fréchet–Urysohn space. Definition: $\operatorname{scl}_X S ~=~ \operatorname{cl}_X S$ for every subset $S \subseteq X.$ $\operatorname{scl}_X S ~\supseteq~ \operatorname{cl}_X S$ for every subset $S \subseteq X.$

This statement is equivalent to the definition above because $\operatorname{scl}_X S ~\subseteq~ \operatorname{cl}_X S$ always holds for every $S \subseteq X.$ Every subspace of $X$ is a [sequential space](wikipedia://en/4668395). For any subset $S \subseteq X$ that is not closed in $X$ and **for every** $x \in \left( \operatorname{cl}_X S \right) \setminus S,$ there exists a sequence in $S$ that converges to $x.$

Contrast this condition to the following characterization of a [sequential space](wikipedia://en/4668395):

For any subset $S \subseteq X$ that is not closed in $X,$ **there exists** some $x \in \left( \operatorname{cl}_X S \right) \setminus S$ for which there exists a sequence in $S$ that converges to $x.$[^o11]

This characterization implies that every Fréchet–Urysohn space is a sequential space.

The characterization below shows that from among Hausdorff sequential spaces, Fréchet–Urysohn spaces are exactly those for which a "[cofinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinal_set) convergent diagonal sequence" can always be found, similar to the [diagonal principal](wikipedia://en/2123123#Diagonal_principal) that is used to [characterize topologies in terms of convergent nets](wikipedia://en/2123123#Convergent_net_characterization). In the following characterization, all convergence is assumed to take place in $(X, \tau).$

If $(X, \tau)$ is a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [sequential space](wikipedia://en/4668395) then $X$ is a Fréchet–Urysohn space if and only if the following condition holds: If $\left(x_l\right)_{l=1}^{\infty}$ is a sequence in $X$ that converge to some $x \in X$ and if for every $l \in \mathbb{N},$ $\left(x_l^i\right)_{i=1}^{\infty}$ is a sequence in $X$ that converges to $x_l,$ where these hypotheses can be summarized by the following diagram

$$\begin{alignedat}{11} &x_1^1 ~\;~ &x_1^2 ~\;~ &x_1^3 ~\;~ &x_1^4 ~\;~ &x_1^5 ~~ &\ldots ~~ &x_1^i ~~ \ldots ~~ &\to ~~ &x_1 \\[1.2ex] &x_2^1 ~\;~ &x_2^2 ~\;~ &x_2^3 ~\;~ &x_2^4 ~\;~ &x_2^5 ~~ &\ldots ~~ &x_2^i ~~ \ldots ~~ &\to ~~ &x_2 \\[1.2ex] &x_3^1 ~\;~ &x_3^2 ~\;~ &x_3^3 ~\;~ &x_3^4 ~\;~ &x_3^5 ~~ &\ldots ~~ &x_3^i ~~ \ldots ~~ &\to ~~ &x_3 \\[1.2ex] &x_4^1 ~\;~ &x_4^2 ~\;~ &x_4^3 ~\;~ &x_4^4 ~\;~ &x_4^5 ~~ &\ldots ~~ &x_4^i ~~ \ldots ~~ &\to ~~ &x_4 \\[0.5ex] & & &\;\,\vdots & & & &\;\,\vdots & &\;\,\vdots \\[0.5ex] &x_l^1 ~\;~ &x_l^2 ~\;~ &x_l^3 ~\;~ &x_l^4 ~\;~ &x_l^5 ~~ &\ldots ~~ &x_l^i ~~ \ldots ~~ &\to ~~ &x_l \\[0.5ex] & & &\;\,\vdots & & & &\;\,\vdots & &\;\,\vdots \\ & & & & & & & & &\,\downarrow \\ & & & & & & & & ~~ &\;x \\ \end{alignedat}$$

then there exist strictly increasing maps $\iota, \lambda : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ such that $\left(x_{\lambda(n)}^{\iota(n)}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty} \to x.$

(It suffices to consider only sequences $\left(x_l\right)_{l=1}^{\infty}$ with infinite ranges (i.e. $\left\{x_l : l \in \mathbb{N}\right\}$ is infinite) because if it is finite then Hausdorffness implies that it is necessarily eventually constant with value $x,$ in which case the existence of the maps $\iota, \lambda : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ with the desired properties is readily verified for this special case (even if $(X, \tau)$ is not a Fréchet–Urysohn space).

## Properties {#Properties}

Every subspace of a Fréchet–Urysohn space is Fréchet–Urysohn.[^o14]

Every Fréchet–Urysohn space is a sequential space although the opposite implication is not true in general.[^o15][^o16]

If a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $(X, \tau)$ is a Fréchet-Urysohn space then $\tau$ is equal to the [final topology](wikipedia://en/1819736) on $X$ induced by the set $\operatorname{Arc}\left([0, 1]; X\right)$ of all [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28topology%29) in $(X, \tau),$ which by definition are continuous [paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_%28mathematics%29) $[0, 1] \to (X, \tau)$ that are also [topological embeddings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding).

## Examples {#Examples}

Every [first-countable space](wikipedia://en/904940) is a Fréchet–Urysohn space. Consequently, every [second-countable space](wikipedia://en/901459), every [metrizable space](wikipedia://en/19738), and every [pseudometrizable space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudometrizable_space) is a Fréchet–Urysohn space. It also follows that every topological space $(X, \tau)$ on a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) $X$ is a Fréchet–Urysohn space.

### Metrizable continuous dual spaces {#Metrizable_continuous_dual_spaces}

A [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) $X$ (for example, a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801)) is a [normable space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) if and only if its [strong dual space](wikipedia://en/63951372) $X^{\prime}_b$ is a Fréchet–Urysohn space,[^o20] or equivalently, if and only if $X^{\prime}_b$ is a normable space.[^n1]

### Sequential spaces that are not Fréchet–Urysohn {#Sequential_spaces_that_are_not_Fréchet–Urysohn}

**Direct limit of finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces**

**The [space of finite real sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_finite_real_sequences)** $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ is a Hausdorff sequential space that is not Fréchet–Urysohn. For every integer $n \geq 1,$ identify $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the set $\mathbb{R}^n \times \{ \left( 0, 0, 0, \ldots \right)\} = \left\{ \left( x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, 0, \ldots \right) ~:~x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R} \right\},$ where the latter is a subset of the [space of sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_sequences) of real numbers $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}};$ explicitly, the elements $\left( x_1, \ldots, x_n \right) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\left( x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, 0, \ldots \right)$ are identified together. In particular, $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be identified as a subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ and more generally, as a subset $\mathbb{R}^n \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n+k}$ for any integer $k \geq 0.$ Let

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} \mathbb{R}^{\infty} := \left\{ \left( x_1, x_2, \ldots \right) \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}} ~:~ \text{ all but finitely many } x_i \text{ are equal to } 0 \right\} = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathbb{R}^n. \end{alignedat}$$

Give $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ its usual topology $\tau,$ in which a subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ is open (resp. closed) if and only if for every integer $n \geq 1,$ the set $S \cap \mathbb{R}^n = \left\{ \left( x_1, \ldots, x_n \right) ~:~ \left( x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, \ldots \right) \in S \right\}$ is an open (resp. closed) subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (with it usual [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085)). If $v \in \mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ and $v_{\bullet}$ is a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ then $v_{\bullet} \to v$ in $\left( \mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau \right)$ if and only if there exists some integer $n \geq 1$ such that both $v$ and $v_{\bullet}$ are contained in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $v_{\bullet} \to v$ in $\mathbb{R}^n.$ From these facts, it follows that $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau\right)$ is a sequential space. For every integer $n \geq 1,$ let $B_n$ denote the open ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$ of radius $1/n$ (in the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm)) centered at the origin. Let $S := \mathbb{R}^{\infty} \,\setminus\, \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n.$ Then the closure of $S$ is $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau\right)$ is all of $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ but the origin $(0, 0, 0, \ldots)$ of $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ does not belong to the sequential closure of $S$ in $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau\right).$ In fact, it can be shown that

$$\mathbb{R}^{\infty} = \operatorname{cl}_{\mathbb{R}^{\infty}} S ~\neq~ \operatorname{scl}_{\mathbb{R}^{\infty}} S = \mathbb{R}^{\infty} \setminus \{(0, 0, 0, \ldots)\}.$$

This proves that $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau\right)$ is not a Fréchet–Urysohn space.

**Montel DF-spaces**

Every infinite-dimensional [Montel](wikipedia://en/1796319) [DF-space](wikipedia://en/63703274) is a sequential space but not a Fréchet–Urysohn space.

**The [Schwartz space](wikipedia://en/4277567) $\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^n\right)$ and the space of [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) $C^{\infty}(U)$**

The following extensively used spaces are prominent examples of sequential spaces that are not Fréchet–Urysohn spaces. Let $\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^n\right)$ denote the [Schwartz space](wikipedia://en/4277567) and let $C^{\infty}(U)$ denote the space of smooth functions on an open subset $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n,$ where both of these spaces have their usual [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) topologies, as defined in the article about [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29). Both $\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^n\right)$ and $C^{\infty}(U),$ as well as the [strong dual spaces](wikipedia://en/63951372) of both these of spaces, are [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) [nuclear](wikipedia://en/2702957) [Montel](wikipedia://en/1796319) [ultrabornological](wikipedia://en/63904912) spaces, which implies that all four of these locally convex spaces are also [paracompact](wikipedia://en/48631)[^o23] [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091) [barrelled spaces](wikipedia://en/1733592). The strong dual spaces of both $\mathcal{S}\left(\mathbb{R}^n\right)$ and $C^{\infty}(U)$ are sequential spaces but neither one of these duals is a [Fréchet-Urysohn space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fréchet-Urysohn_space).[^o24][^o25]
