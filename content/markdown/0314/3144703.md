# Riemann–Roch theorem for surfaces

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Riemann–Roch theorem for surfaces"}

**Riemann–Roch theorem for surfaces.**

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Riemann–Roch theorem for surfaces"}

In mathematics, the **Riemann–Roch theorem for surfaces** describes the dimension of linear systems on an [algebraic surface](wikipedia://en/648311). The classical form of it was first given by (txt), after preliminary versions of it were found by (txt) and (txt). The [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466)-theoretic version is due to [Hirzebruch](wikipedia://en/987495).

:::

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

One form of the Riemann–Roch theorem states that if $D$ is a divisor on a non-singular projective surface then

$$\chi(D) = \chi(0) +\tfrac{1}{2} D . (D - K) \,$$

where $\chi$ is the [holomorphic Euler characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory), the dot $.$ is the [intersection number](wikipedia://en/384327), and $K$ is the canonical divisor. The constant $\chi(0)$ is the holomorphic Euler characteristic of the trivial bundle, and is equal to $1 + p_a$, where $p_a$ is the [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) of the surface. For comparison, the Riemann–Roch theorem for a curve states that $\chi(D) = \chi(0) + \operatorname{deg}(D)$.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Noether's formula"}

## Noether's formula {#Noether's_formula}

[Noether's](wikipedia://en/2482337) formula states that

$$\chi = \frac{c_1^2+c_2}{12} = \frac{(K.K)+e}{12}$$

where χ=χ(0) is the holomorphic Euler characteristic, *c*<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> = (*K*.*K*) is a [Chern number](wikipedia://en/294349#Chern_number) and the self-intersection number of the canonical class *K*, and *e* = *c*<sub>2</sub> is the topological Euler characteristic. It can be used to replace the term χ(0) in the Riemann–Roch theorem with topological terms; this gives the [Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/2892412) for surfaces.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Relation to the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem"}

## Relation to the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem {#Relation_to_the_Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch_theorem}

For surfaces, the [Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/2892412) is essentially the Riemann–Roch theorem for surfaces combined with the Noether formula. To see this, recall that for each divisor *D* on a surface there is an [invertible sheaf](wikipedia://en/581859) *L* = O(*D*) such that the [linear system](wikipedia://en/722503) of *D* is more or less the space of sections of *L*. For surfaces the [Todd class](wikipedia://en/1568489) is $1 + c_1(X) / 2 + (c_1(X)^2 + c_2(X)) / 12$, and the Chern character of the sheaf *L* is just $1 + c_1(L) + c_1(L)^2 / 2$, so the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem states that

$$\begin{aligned} \chi(D) &= h^0(L) - h^1(L) + h^2(L)\\ &= \frac{1}{2} c_1(L)^2 + \frac{1}{2} c_1(L) \, c_1(X) + \frac{1}{12} \left(c_1(X)^2 + c_2(X)\right) \end{aligned}$$

Fortunately this can be written in a clearer form as follows. First putting *D* = 0 shows that

$\chi(0) = \frac{1}{12}\left(c_1(X)^2 + c_2(X)\right)$ (Noether's formula)

For invertible sheaves (line bundles) the second Chern class vanishes. The products of second cohomology classes can be identified with intersection numbers in the [Picard group](wikipedia://en/1850134), and we get a more classical version of Riemann Roch for surfaces:

$$\chi(D) = \chi(0) + \frac{1}{2}(D.D - D.K)$$

If we want, we can use [Serre duality](wikipedia://en/359175) to express *h*<sup>2</sup>(O(*D*)) as *h*<sup>0</sup>(O(*K* − *D*)), but unlike the case of curves there is in general no easy way to write the *h*<sup>1</sup>(O(*D*)) term in a form not involving [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) (although in practice it often vanishes).

:::

## Early versions {#Early_versions}

The earliest forms of the Riemann–Roch theorem for surfaces were often stated as an inequality rather than an equality, because there was no direct geometric description of first cohomology groups. A typical example is given by (Zariski 1995, p. 78), which states that

$$r\ge n-\pi+p_a+1-i$$

where

- *r* is the dimension of the complete linear system |*D*| of a divisor *D* (so *r* = *h*<sup>0</sup>(O(*D*)) −1)
- *n* is the **virtual degree** of *D*, given by the self-intersection number (*D*.*D*)
- π is the **virtual genus** of *D*, equal to 1 + (D.D + K.D)/2
- *p*<sub>*a*</sub> is the **arithmetic genus** χ(O<sub>*F*</sub>) − 1 of the surface
- *i* is the **index of speciality** of *D*, equal to dim *H*<sup>0</sup>(O(*K* − *D*)) (which by Serre duality is the same as dim *H*<sup>2</sup>(O(D))).

The difference between the two sides of this inequality was called the **superabundance** *s* of the divisor *D*. Comparing this inequality with the sheaf-theoretic version of the Riemann–Roch theorem shows that the superabundance of *D* is given by *s* = dim *H*<sup>1</sup>(O(*D*)). The divisor *D* was called **regular** if *i* = *s* = 0 (or in other words if all higher cohomology groups of O(*D*) vanish) and **superabundant** if *s* > 0.
