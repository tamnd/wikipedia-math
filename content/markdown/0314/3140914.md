# Symmetric product of an algebraic curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symmetric product of an algebraic curve"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the *n*-fold **symmetric product** of an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) *C* is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of the *n*-fold [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095)

:::

*C* × *C* × ... × *C*

or *C*<sup>*n*</sup> by the [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) *S*<sub>*n*</sub> on *n* letters permuting the factors. It exists as a [smooth](wikipedia://en/35033819) [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) denoted by Σ<sup>*n*</sup>*C*. If *C* is a [compact Riemann surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Riemann_surface), Σ<sup>*n*</sup>*C* is therefore a [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742). Its interest in relation to the classical geometry of curves is that its points correspond to [effective divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_divisor) on *C* of degree *n*, that is, [formal sums](wikipedia://en/498958) of points with non-negative integer coefficients.

For *C* the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) (say the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) $\mathbb{C}$ ∪ {∞} ≈ *S*<sup>*2*</sup>), its nth symmetric product Σ<sup>*n*</sup>*C* can be identified with [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb{CP}^n$ of dimension *n*.

If *G* has [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g* ≥ 1 then the Σ<sup>*n*</sup>*C* are closely related to the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) *J* of *C*. More accurately for *n* taking values up to *g* they form a sequence of approximations to *J* from below: their images in *J* under addition on *J* (see [theta-divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theta-divisor)) have dimension *n* and fill up *J*, with some identifications caused by [special divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_divisor).

For *g* = *n* we have Σ<sup>*g*</sup>*C* actually [birationally equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birationally_equivalent) to *J*; the Jacobian is a [blowing down](wikipedia://en/1817790) of the symmetric product. That means that at the level of [function fields](wikipedia://en/2783843) it is possible to construct *J* by taking [linearly disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_disjoint) copies of the function field of *C*, and within their [compositum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compositum) taking the [fixed subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_subfield) of the symmetric group. This is the source of [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019)'s technique of constructing *J* as an [abstract variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_variety) from 'birational data'. Other ways of constructing *J*, for example as a [Picard variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard_variety), are preferred now[^o2] but this does mean that for any rational function *F* on *C*

*F*(*x*<sub>1</sub>) + ... + *F*(*x*<sub>*g*</sub>)

makes sense as a rational function on *J*, for the *x*<sub>*i*</sub> staying away from the poles of *F*.

For *n* > *g* the mapping from Σ<sup>*n*</sup>*C* to *J* by addition fibers it over *J*; when *n* is large enough (around twice *g*) this becomes a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) bundle (the **Picard bundle**). It has been studied in detail, for example by Kempf and Mukai.

## Betti numbers and the Euler characteristic of the symmetric product {#Betti_numbers_and_the_Euler_characteristic_of_the_symmetric_product}

Let *C* be a smooth projective curve of genus *g* over the complex numbers **C**. The [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262) *b*<sub>*i*</sub>(Σ<sup>*n*</sup>C) of the symmetric products Σ<sup>*n*</sup>C for all n = 0, 1, 2, ... are given by the [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993)

$$\sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} \sum_{i = 0}^{2n} b_i( \Sigma^n C ) y^n u^{i-n} = \frac{ (1 + y)^{2g} }{ (1 - u y) (1 - u^{-1} y) }$$

and their Euler characteristics *e*(Σ<sup>*n*</sup>C) are given by the generating function

$$\sum_{n = 0}^{\infty} e( \Sigma^n C ) p^n = (1 - p)^{2g - 2} .$$

Here we have set *u* = -1 and *y* = -*p* in the previous formula.
