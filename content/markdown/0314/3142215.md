# Jacobi sum

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jacobi sum"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Jacobi sum** is a type of [character sum](wikipedia://en/2986034) formed with [Dirichlet characters](wikipedia://en/174515). Simple examples would be Jacobi sums *J*(*χ*, *ψ*) for Dirichlet characters *χ*, *ψ* modulo a prime number *p*, defined by

:::

$$J(\chi,\psi) = \sum \chi(a) \psi(1 - a) \,,$$

where the summation runs over all [residues](wikipedia://en/502897) *a* = 2, 3, ..., *p* − 1 mod *p* (for which neither *a* nor 1 − *a* is 0). Jacobi sums are the analogues for [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) of the [beta function](wikipedia://en/245660). Such sums were introduced by [C. G. J. Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._G._J._Jacobi) early in the nineteenth century in connection with the theory of [cyclotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomy). Jacobi sums *J* can be factored generically into products of powers of [Gauss sums](wikipedia://en/6378204) *g*. For example, when the character *χψ* is nontrivial,

$$J(\chi, \psi) = \frac{g(\chi)g(\psi)}{g(\chi\psi)}\,,$$

analogous to the formula for the beta function in terms of [gamma functions](wikipedia://en/12316). Since the nontrivial Gauss sums *g* have absolute value *p*<sup>$\tfrac{1}{2}$</sup>, it follows that *J*(*χ*, *ψ*) also has absolute value *p*<sup>$\tfrac{1}{2}$</sup> when the characters *χψ*, *χ*, *ψ* are nontrivial. Jacobi sums *J* lie in smaller [cyclotomic fields](wikipedia://en/30872462) than do the nontrivial Gauss sums *g*. The summands of *J*(*χ*, *ψ*) for example involve no *p*th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950), but rather involve just values which lie in the cyclotomic field of (*p* − 1)th roots of unity. Like Gauss sums, Jacobi sums have known [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) factorisations in their cyclotomic fields; see [Stickelberger's theorem](wikipedia://en/2202290).

When *χ* is the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567),

$$J(\chi, \chi) = -\chi(-1) = (-1)^\frac{p+1}{2} \,.$$

In general the values of Jacobi sums occur in relation with the [local zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function) of [diagonal forms](wikipedia://en/3028504). The result on the Legendre symbol amounts to the formula *p* + 1 for the number of points on a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) that is a [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) over the field of *p* elements. A paper of [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) from 1949 very much revived the subject. Indeed, through the [Hasse–Davenport relation](wikipedia://en/14619729) of the late 20th century, the formal properties of powers of Gauss sums had become current once more.

As well as pointing out the possibility of writing down local zeta-functions for diagonal hypersurfaces by means of general Jacobi sums, Weil (1952) demonstrated the properties of Jacobi sums as [Hecke characters](wikipedia://en/3149443). This was to become important once the [complex multiplication of abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/1138222) became established. The Hecke characters in question were exactly those one needs to express the [Hasse–Weil *L*-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasse–Weil_L-function) of the [Fermat curves](wikipedia://en/897529), for example. The exact conductors of these characters, a question Weil had left open, were determined in later work.
