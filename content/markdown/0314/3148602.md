# Intersection theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Intersection theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **intersection theory** is one of the main branches of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), where it gives information about the [intersection](wikipedia://en/41780044) of two [subvarieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subvariety_%28algebraic_geometry%29) of a given variety.[^n1] The theory for varieties is older, with roots in [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217) on curves and [elimination theory](wikipedia://en/245978). On the other hand, the topological theory more quickly reached a definitive form.

:::

There is yet an ongoing development of intersection theory. Currently the main focus is on: virtual fundamental cycles, quantum intersection rings, [Gromov–Witten theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gromov–Witten_theory) and the extension of intersection theory from [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) to [stacks](wikipedia://en/13185596).[^n2]

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Intersection form"}

## Topological intersection form {#Topological_intersection_form}

For a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [oriented manifold](wikipedia://en/187446) $M$ of [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_of_a_manifold) $2n$ the **intersection form** is defined on the $n$-th [cohomology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group) (what is usually called the 'middle dimension') by the evaluation of the [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684) on the [fundamental class](wikipedia://en/669440) $[M]$ in $H_{2n}(M,\partial M)$. Stated precisely, there is a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477)

$$\lambda_M \colon H^n(M,\partial M) \times H^n(M,\partial M)\to \mathbf{Z}$$

given by

$$\lambda_M(a,b)=\langle a\smile b,[M]\rangle \in \mathbf{Z}$$

with

$$\lambda_M(a,b)=(-1)^n\lambda_M(b,a) \in \mathbf{Z}.$$

This is a [symmetric form](wikipedia://en/1803375) for $n$ even (so $2n = 4k$ [doubly even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_even)), in which case the [signature](wikipedia://en/1152311) of $M$ is defined to be the signature of the form, and an [alternating form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_form) for $n$ odd (so $2n = 4k + 2$ is [singly even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singly_even)). These can be referred to uniformly as [ε-symmetric forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ε-symmetric_form), where $\varepsilon = (-1)^{n} = \pm1$ respectively for symmetric and skew-symmetric forms. It is possible in some circumstances to refine this form to an [$\varepsilon$-quadratic form](wikipedia://en/13979549), though this requires additional data such as a [framing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framed_manifold) of the tangent bundle. It is possible to drop the orientability condition and work with $\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}$ coefficients instead.

These forms are important [topological invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant). For example, a theorem of [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970) states that [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [4-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1362795) are (almost) determined by [their intersection forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_form_%284-manifold%29) up to [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660).

By [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732), it turns out that there is a way to think of this geometrically. If possible, choose representative $n$-dimensional submanifolds $A$, $B$ for the Poincaré duals of $a$ and $b$. Then $\lambda_{M}\,(a, b)$ is the [oriented intersection number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_intersection_number) of $A$ and $B$, which is well-defined because since dimensions of $A$ and $B$ sum to the total dimension of $M$ they generically intersect at isolated points. This explains the terminology *intersection form*.

:::

## Intersection theory in algebraic geometry {#Intersection_theory_in_algebraic_geometry}

[William Fulton](wikipedia://en/9642149) in *Intersection Theory* (1984) writes

> ... if $A$ and $B$ are subvarieties of a non-singular variety $X$, the intersection product $A \cdot B$ should be an equivalence class of algebraic cycles closely related to the geometry of how $A \cap B$, $A$ and $B$ are situated in $X$. Two extreme cases have been most familiar. If the intersection is *proper*, i.e. $\dim(A \cap B) = \dim A + \dim B - \dim X$, then $A \cdot B$ is a linear combination of the irreducible components of $A \cap B$, with coefficients the intersection multiplicities. At the other extreme, if $A = B$ is a non-singular subvariety, the self-intersection formula says that $A \cdot B$ is represented by the top [Chern class](wikipedia://en/294349) of the [normal bundle](wikipedia://en/2370200) of $A$ in $X$.

To give a definition, in the general case, of the **intersection multiplicity** was the major concern of [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019)'s 1946 book *Foundations of Algebraic Geometry*. Work in the 1920s of [B. L. van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390) had already addressed the question; in the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424) the ideas were well known, but foundational questions were not addressed in the same spirit.

### Moving cycles {#Moving_cycles}

A well-working machinery of intersecting [algebraic cycles](wikipedia://en/9520954) $V$ and $W$ requires more than taking just the set-theoretic intersection $V \cap W$ of the cycles in question. If the two cycles are in "good position" then the *intersection product*, denoted $V \cdot W$, should consist of the set-theoretic intersection of the two subvarieties. However cycles may be in bad position, e.g. two parallel lines in the plane, or a plane containing a line (intersecting in 3-space). In both cases the intersection should be a point, because, again, if one cycle is moved, this would be the intersection. The intersection of two cycles $V$ and $W$ is called *proper* if the [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) of the (set-theoretic) intersection $V \cap W$ is the sum of the codimensions of $V$ and $W$, respectively, i.e. the "expected" value.

Therefore, the concept of *moving cycles* using appropriate [equivalence relations on algebraic cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_relations_on_algebraic_cycles) is used. The equivalence must be broad enough that given any two cycles $V$ and $W$, there are equivalent cycles $V'$ and $W'$ such that the intersection $V' \cap W'$ is proper. Of course, on the other hand, for a second equivalent $V''$ and $W''$, $V' \cap W'$ needs to be equivalent to $V'' \cap W''$.

For the purposes of intersection theory, *rational equivalence* is the most important one. Briefly, two $r$-dimensional cycles on a variety $X$ are rationally equivalent if there is a rational function $\,f\,$ on a $(r + 1)$-dimensional subvariety $Y$, i.e. an element of the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) $k(Y)$ or equivalently a function $\,f\, : Y \to \mathbf{P}^{1}$, such that $V - W = \,f\,^{-1}(0) - \,f\,^{-1}(\infty)$, where $\,f\,^{-1}(\cdot)$ is counted with multiplicities. Rational equivalence accomplishes the needs sketched above.

### Intersection multiplicities {#Intersection_multiplicities}

*Image: intersection number.png.* Intersection of lines and parabola

The guiding principle in the definition of [intersection multiplicities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_multiplicity) of cycles is continuity in a certain sense. Consider the following elementary example: the intersection of a parabola $y = x^{2}$ and an axis $y = 0$ should be $2 \cdot (0, 0)$, because if one of the cycles moves (yet in an undefined sense), there are precisely two intersection points which both converge to $(0, 0)$ when the cycles approach the depicted position. (The picture is misleading insofar as the apparently empty intersection of the parabola and the line $y = -3$ is empty, because only the real solutions of the equations are depicted).

The first fully satisfactory definition of intersection multiplicities was given by [Serre](wikipedia://en/205483): Let the ambient variety $X$ be smooth (or all local rings [regular](wikipedia://en/506959)). Further let $V$ and $W$ be two (irreducible reduced closed) subvarieties, such that their intersection is proper. The construction is local, therefore the varieties may be represented by two ideals $I$ and $J$ in the coordinate ring of $X$. Let $Z$ be an irreducible component of the set-theoretic intersection $V \cap W$ and $z$ its [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978). The multiplicity of $Z$ in the intersection product $V \cdot W$ is defined by

$$\mu(Z; V, W) := \sum^\infty_{i=0} (-1)^i \text{length}_{\mathcal O_{X, z}} \text{Tor}_i^{\mathcal O_{X, z}} (\mathcal O_{X, z}/I, \mathcal O_{X, z}/J),$$

the alternating sum over the [length](wikipedia://en/816070) over the local ring of $X$ in $z$ of [torsion](wikipedia://en/856347) groups of the factor rings corresponding to the subvarieties. This expression is sometimes referred to as *Serre's Tor-formula*.

Remarks:

- The first summand, the length of

$$\left ( \mathcal O_{X, z}/I \right ) \otimes_{\mathcal O_{X, z}} \left (\mathcal O_{X, z}/J \right ) = \mathcal O_{Z, z}$$

is the "naive" guess of the multiplicity; however, as Serre shows, it is not sufficient.

- The sum is finite, because the regular local ring $\mathcal O_{X, z}$ has finite Tor-dimension.
- If the intersection of $V$ and $W$ is not proper, the above multiplicity will be zero. If it is proper, it is strictly positive. (Both statements are not obvious from the definition).
- Using a [spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/803894) argument, it can be shown that $\mu(Z; V, W) = \mu(Z; W, V)$.

:::

### The Chow ring {#The_Chow_ring}

The [Chow ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chow_ring) is the group of algebraic cycles modulo [rational equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_relations_on_algebraic_cycles) together with the following commutative *intersection product*:

$$V \cdot W := \sum_{i} \mu(Z_i; V, W)Z_i$$

whenever *V* and *W* meet properly, where $V \cap W = \cup_i Z_i$ is the decomposition of the set-theoretic intersection into irreducible components.

### Self-intersection {#Self-intersection}

Given two subvarieties $V$ and $W$, one can take their intersection $V \cap W$, but it is also possible, though more subtle, to define the *self*-intersection of a single subvariety.

Given, for instance, a curve $C$ on a surface $S$, its intersection with itself (as sets) is just itself: $C \cap C = C$. This is clearly correct, but on the other hand unsatisfactory: given any two *distinct* curves on a surface (with no component in common), they intersect in some set of points, which for instance one can count, obtaining an *intersection number*, and we may wish to do the same for a given curve: the analogy is that intersecting distinct curves is like multiplying two numbers: $xy$, while self-intersection is like squaring a single number: $x^{2}$. Formally, the analogy is stated as a [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) (multiplication) and a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) (squaring).

A geometric solution to this is to intersect the curve $C$ not with itself, but with a slightly pushed off version of itself. In the plane, this just means translating the curve $C$ in some direction, but in general one talks about taking a curve $C'$ that is [linearly equivalent](wikipedia://en/1186886) to $C$, and counting the intersection $C \cdot C'$, thus obtaining an intersection number, denoted $C \cdot C$. Note that *unlike* for distinct curves $C$ and $D$, the *actual points of intersection* are not defined, because they depend on a choice of $C'$, but the “self intersection points of $C''$ can be interpreted as $k$ [generic points](wikipedia://en/3043978) on $C$, where $k = C \cdot C$. More properly, the self-intersection point of $C$ is *the* generic point of $C$, taken with multiplicity $C \cdot C$.

Alternatively, one can “solve” (or motivate) this problem algebraically by dualizing, and looking at the class of $[C] \cup [C]$ – this both gives a number, and raises the question of a geometric interpretation. Note that passing to cohomology *classes* is analogous to replacing a curve by a linear system.

Note that the self-intersection number can be negative, as the example below illustrates.

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

Consider a line $L$ in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) $\mathbf{P}^{2}$: it has self-intersection number 1 since all other lines cross it once: one can push $L$ off to $L'$, and $L \cdot L' = 1$ (for any choice) of $L'$, hence $L \cdot L = 1$. In terms of intersection forms, we say the plane has one of type $x^{2}$ (there is only one class of lines, and they all intersect with each other).

Note that on the [*affine* plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), one might push off $L$ to a parallel line, so (thinking geometrically) the number of intersection points depends on the choice of push-off. One says that “the affine plane does not have a good intersection theory”, and intersection theory on non-projective varieties is much more difficult.

A line on a $\mathbf{P}^{1} \times \mathbf{P}^{1}$ (which can also be interpreted as the non-singular [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570) $Q$ in $\mathbf{P}^{3}$) has self-intersection $0$, since a line can be moved off itself. (It is a [ruled surface](wikipedia://en/705158).) In terms of intersection forms, we say $\mathbf{P}^{1} \times \mathbf{P}^{1}$ has one of type $xy$ – there are two basic classes of lines, which intersect each other in one point ($xy$), but have zero self-intersection (no $x^{2}$ or $y^{2}$ terms).

:::

#### Blow-ups {#Blow-ups}

A key example of self-intersection numbers is the exceptional curve of a blow-up, which is a central operation in [birational geometry](wikipedia://en/382733). Given an [algebraic surface](wikipedia://en/648311) $S$, [blowing up](wikipedia://en/1817790) at a point creates a curve $C$. This curve $C$ is recognisable by its [genus](wikipedia://en/61891#Algebraic_geometry), which is $0$, and its self-intersection number, which is $-1$. (This is not obvious.) Note that as a corollary, $\mathbf{P}^{2}$ and $\mathbf{P}^{1} \times \mathbf{P}^{1}$ are [minimal surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_model_%28birational_geometry%29) (they are not blow-ups), since they do not have any curves with negative self-intersection. In fact, [Castelnuovo](wikipedia://en/1558891)’s [contraction theorem](wikipedia://en/45626463) states the converse: every $(-1)$-curve is the exceptional curve of some blow-up (it can be “blown down”).
