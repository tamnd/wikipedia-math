# Glossary of arithmetic and diophantine geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Glossary of arithmetic and diophantine geometry"}

This is a glossary of **arithmetic and diophantine geometry** in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), areas growing out of the traditional study of [Diophantine equations](wikipedia://en/9109) to encompass large parts of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). Much of the theory is in the form of proposed [conjectures](wikipedia://en/6138), which can be related at various levels of generality.

:::

[Diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/502205) in general is the study of [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties) *V* over fields *K* that are finitely generated over their [prime fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field)—including as of special interest [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) and [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615)—and over [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375). Of those, only the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) are [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed); over any other *K* the existence of points of *V* with coordinates in *K* is something to be proved and studied as an extra topic, even knowing the geometry of *V*.

[Arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177) can be more generally defined as the study of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) of finite type over the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of the ring of [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers). Arithmetic geometry has also been defined as the application of the techniques of algebraic geometry to problems in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).[^o3]

See also the glossary of number theory terms at [Glossary of number theory](wikipedia://en/77416429).

## A {#A}

::: {#o5 .entry cue=term name="abc conjecture"}

**abc conjecture**

The [abc conjecture](wikipedia://en/174108) of [Masser](wikipedia://en/2172777) and [Oesterlé](wikipedia://en/1604631) attempts to state as much as possible about repeated prime factors in an equation *a* + *b* = *c*. For example 3 + 125 = 128 but the prime powers here are exceptional.

:::

::: {#o6 .entry cue=term name="Arakelov class group"}

**Arakelov class group**

The *Arakelov class group* is the analogue of the [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907) or [divisor class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor_class_group) for [Arakelov divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arakelov_divisor).[^o7]

:::

::: {#o8 .entry cue=term name="Arakelov divisor"}

**Arakelov divisor**

An *Arakelov divisor* (or *replete divisor*[^o99]) on a global field is an extension of the concept of [divisor](wikipedia://en/1693055) or [fractional ideal](wikipedia://en/460700). It is a formal linear combination of [places](wikipedia://en/347049) of the field with [finite places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_place) having integer coefficients and the [infinite places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_place) having real coefficients.[^o7][^o9][^o10]

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=term name="Arakelov height"}

**Arakelov height**

The [Arakelov height](wikipedia://en/3141855#Arakelov_height) on a projective space over the field of algebraic numbers is a global [height function](wikipedia://en/3141855) with local contributions coming from [Fubini–Study metrics](wikipedia://en/2574486) on the [Archimedean fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_field) and the usual metric on the [non-Archimedean fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Archimedean_field).[^o12][^o13]

:::

::: {#o14 .entry cue=term name="Arakelov theory"}

**Arakelov theory**

[Arakelov theory](wikipedia://en/16364229) is an approach to arithmetic geometry that explicitly includes the 'infinite primes'.

:::

::: {#o15 .entry cue=term name="Arithmetic of abelian varieties"}

**Arithmetic of abelian varieties**

*See main article [arithmetic of abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/384577)*

:::

::: {#o16 .entry cue=term name="Artin L-functions"}

**Artin L-functions**

[Artin L-functions](wikipedia://en/2774171) are defined for quite general [Galois representations](wikipedia://en/486929). The introduction of [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) in the 1960s meant that [Hasse–Weil L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasse–Weil_L-function) could be regarded as Artin L-functions for the Galois representations on [l-adic cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-adic_cohomology) groups.

:::

## B {#B}

::: {#o18 .entry cue=term name="Bad reduction"}

**Bad reduction**

See *good reduction*.

:::

::: {#o19 .entry cue=term name="Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture"}

**Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture**

The [Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture](wikipedia://en/435161) on [elliptic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curves) postulates a connection between the [rank of an elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/51244926) and the order of pole of its Hasse–Weil L-function. It has been an important landmark in Diophantine geometry since the mid-1960s, with results such as the [Coates–Wiles theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coates–Wiles_theorem), [Gross–Zagier theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross–Zagier_theorem) and [Kolyvagin's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolyvagin's_theorem).[^o20]

:::

## C {#C}

::: {#o22 .entry cue=term name="Canonical height"}

**Canonical height**

The canonical height on an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) is a height function that is a distinguished [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478). See [Néron–Tate height](wikipedia://en/9236652).

:::

::: {#o23 .entry cue=term name="Chabauty's method"}

**Chabauty's method**

**Chabauty's method**, based on *p*-adic analytic functions, is a special application but capable of proving cases of the [Mordell conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell_conjecture) for curves whose Jacobian's rank is less than its dimension. It developed ideas from [Thoralf Skolem](wikipedia://en/453765)'s method for an [algebraic torus](wikipedia://en/504697). (Other older methods for Diophantine problems include [Runge's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runge's_method).)

:::

::: {#o24 .entry cue=term name="Coates–Wiles theorem"}

**Coates–Wiles theorem**

The *Coates–Wiles theorem* states that an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) with [complex multiplication](wikipedia://en/537183) by an [imaginary quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_quadratic_field) of [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_numbers_of_imaginary_quadratic_fields) 1 and positive [rank](wikipedia://en/244541) has [L-function](wikipedia://en/245283) with a zero at *s* = 1. This is a special case of the [Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture](wikipedia://en/435161).[^o25]

:::

::: {#o26 .entry cue=term name="Crystalline cohomology"}

**Crystalline cohomology**

[Crystalline cohomology](wikipedia://en/10220067) is a p-adic cohomology theory in [characteristic p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_p), introduced by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) to fill the gap left by [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) which is deficient in using mod *p* coefficients in this case. It is one of a number of theories deriving in some way from [Dwork's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwork's_method), and has applications outside purely arithmetical questions.

:::

## D {#D}

::: {#o28 .entry cue=term name="Diagonal forms"}

**Diagonal forms**

[Diagonal forms](wikipedia://en/3028504) are some of the simplest [projective varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_varieties) to study from an arithmetic point of view (including the [Fermat varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_varieties)). Their [local zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function) are computed in terms of [Jacobi sums](wikipedia://en/3142215). [Waring's problem](wikipedia://en/33569) is the most classical case.

:::

::: {#o29 .entry cue=term name="Diophantine dimension"}

**Diophantine dimension**

The *Diophantine dimension* of a field is the smallest natural number *k*, if it exists, such that the field of is class C<sub>*k*</sub>: that is, such that any homogeneous polynomial of degree *d* in *N* variables has a non-trivial zero whenever *N* > *d*<sup>*k*</sup>. [Algebraically closed fields](wikipedia://en/1018) are of Diophantine dimension 0; [quasi-algebraically closed fields](wikipedia://en/2934213) of dimension 1.[^o30]

:::

::: {#o31 .entry cue=term name="Discriminant of a point"}

**Discriminant of a point**

The *discriminant of a point* refers to two related concepts relative to a point *P* on an algebraic variety *V* defined over a number field *K*: the *geometric (logarithmic) discriminant*[^o32] *d*(*P*) and the *arithmetic discriminant*, defined by Vojta.[^o33] The difference between the two may be compared to the difference between the [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) of a [singular curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_curve) and the [geometric genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) of the [desingularisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desingularisation).[^o33] The arithmetic genus is larger than the geometric genus, and the height of a point may be bounded in terms of the arithmetic genus. Obtaining similar bounds involving the geometric genus would have significant consequences.[^o33]

:::

::: {#o34 .entry cue=term name="Dwork's method"}

**Dwork's method**

[Bernard Dwork](wikipedia://en/1678313) used distinctive methods of [p-adic analysis](wikipedia://en/291327), p-adic [algebraic differential equations](wikipedia://en/9501827), [Koszul complexes](wikipedia://en/519992) and other techniques that have not all been absorbed into general theories such as [crystalline cohomology](wikipedia://en/10220067). He first proved the [rationality](wikipedia://en/361210) of local zeta-functions, the initial advance in the direction of the [Weil conjectures](wikipedia://en/244705).

:::

## E {#E}

::: {#o36 .entry cue=term name="Étale cohomology"}

**Étale cohomology**

The search for a Weil cohomology (q.v.) was at least partially fulfilled in the [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) theory of [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) and [Michael Artin](wikipedia://en/1519821). It provided a proof of the [functional equation](wikipedia://en/595708) for the [local zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function), and was basic in the formulation of the Tate conjecture (q.v.) and numerous other theories.

:::

## F {#F}

::: {#o38 .entry cue=term name="Faltings height"}

**Faltings height**

The [Faltings height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faltings_height) of an elliptic curve or abelian variety defined over a number field is a measure of its complexity introduced by [Faltings](wikipedia://en/862226) in his proof of the [Mordell conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell_conjecture).[^o39][^o40]

:::

::: {#o41 .entry cue=term name="Fermat's Last Theorem"}

**Fermat's Last Theorem**

[Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953), the most celebrated conjecture of Diophantine geometry, was proved by [Andrew Wiles](wikipedia://en/2027) and [Richard Taylor](wikipedia://en/646931).

:::

::: {#o42 .entry cue=term name="Flat cohomology"}

**Flat cohomology**

[Flat cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_cohomology) is, for the school of Grothendieck, one terminal point of development. It has the disadvantage of being quite hard to compute with. The reason that the [flat topology](wikipedia://en/10538912) has been considered the 'right' foundational [topos](wikipedia://en/21391464) for [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory) goes back to the fact of [faithfully-flat descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithfully-flat_descent), the discovery of Grothendieck that the [representable functors](wikipedia://en/844461) are sheaves for it (i.e. a very general [gluing axiom](wikipedia://en/1204294) holds).

:::

::: {#o43 .entry cue=term name="Function field analogy"}

**Function field analogy**

It was realised in the nineteenth century that the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of a number field has analogies with the affine [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of an algebraic curve or compact Riemann surface, with a point or more removed corresponding to the 'infinite places' of a number field. This idea is more precisely encoded in the theory that [global fields](wikipedia://en/288358) should all be treated on the same basis. The idea goes further. Thus [elliptic surfaces](wikipedia://en/3073462) over the complex numbers, also, have some quite strict analogies with [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) over number fields.

:::

## G {#G}

::: {#o45 .entry cue=term name="Geometric class field theory"}

**[Geometric class field theory](wikipedia://en/51692308)**

The extension of [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695)-style results on [abelian coverings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_covering) to varieties of dimension at least two is often called *geometric* class field theory.

:::

::: {#o46 .entry cue=term name="Good reduction"}

**Good reduction**

Fundamental to [local analysis](wikipedia://en/312867) in arithmetic problems is to *reduce* *[modulo](wikipedia://en/20087)* all prime numbers *p* or, more generally, [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928). In the typical situation this presents little difficulty for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) *p*; for example [denominators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) of fractions are tricky, in that reduction modulo a prime in the denominator looks like [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663), but that rules out only finitely many *p* per fraction. With a little extra sophistication, [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) allow clearing of denominators by multiplying by a common scalar. For a given, single point one can do this and not leave a common factor *p*. However [singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763) enters: a [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities) point may become a [singular point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) on reduction modulo *p*, because the [Zariski tangent space](wikipedia://en/510207) can become larger when linear terms reduce to 0 (the geometric formulation shows it is not the fault of a single set of coordinates). *Good reduction* refers to the reduced variety having the same properties as the original, for example, an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) having the same [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_of_a_curve), or a [smooth variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_variety) remaining smooth. In general there will be a finite set *S* of primes for a given variety *V*, assumed smooth, such that there is otherwise a smooth reduced *V*<sub>*p*</sub> over **Z**/*p***Z**. For [abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_varieties), good reduction is connected with [ramification](wikipedia://en/384644) in the field of [division points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_point) by the [Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich criterion](wikipedia://en/35128259). The theory is subtle, in the sense that the freedom to change variables to try to improve matters is rather unobvious: see [Néron model](wikipedia://en/9190726), [potential good reduction](wikipedia://en/35945175), [Tate curve](wikipedia://en/35189927), [semistable abelian variety](wikipedia://en/898483), [semistable elliptic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semistable_elliptic_curve), [Serre–Tate theorem](wikipedia://en/35189754).[^o47]

:::

::: {#o48 .entry cue=term name="Grothendieck–Katz conjecture"}

**Grothendieck–Katz conjecture**

The [Grothendieck–Katz p-curvature conjecture](wikipedia://en/3135542) applies reduction modulo primes to [algebraic differential equations](wikipedia://en/9501827), to derive information on [algebraic function](wikipedia://en/974169) solutions. The initial result of this type was [Eisenstein's theorem](wikipedia://en/1946047).

:::

## H {#H}

::: {#o50 .entry cue=term name="Hasse principle"}

**Hasse principle**

The [Hasse principle](wikipedia://en/489707) states that solubility for a [global field](wikipedia://en/288358) is the same as solubility in all relevant [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375). One of the main objectives of Diophantine geometry is to classify cases where the Hasse principle holds. Generally that is for a large number of variables, when the degree of an equation is held fixed. The Hasse principle is often associated with the success of the [Hardy–Littlewood circle method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Littlewood_circle_method). When the circle method works, it can provide extra, quantitative information such as asymptotic number of solutions. Reducing the number of variables makes the circle method harder; therefore failures of the Hasse principle, for example for [cubic forms](wikipedia://en/6680683) in small numbers of variables (and in particular for [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) as [cubic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_curve)) are at a general level connected with the limitations of the analytic approach.

:::

::: {#o51 .entry cue=term name="Hasse–Weil L-function"}

**Hasse–Weil L-function**

A [Hasse–Weil L-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasse–Weil_L-function), sometimes called a *global* L-function, is an [Euler product](wikipedia://en/398540) formed from local zeta-functions. The properties of such [L-functions](wikipedia://en/245283) remain largely in the realm of conjecture, with the proof of the [Taniyama–Shimura conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taniyama–Shimura_conjecture) being a breakthrough. The [Langlands philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langlands_philosophy) is largely complementary to the theory of global L-functions.

:::

::: {#o52 .entry cue=term name="Height function"}

**Height function**

A [height function](wikipedia://en/3141855) in Diophantine geometry quantifies the size of solutions to Diophantine equations.[^o53]

:::

::: {#o54 .entry cue=term name="Hilbertian fields"}

**Hilbertian fields**

A [Hilbertian field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbertian_field) *K* is one for which the [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135) over *K* are not [thin sets](wikipedia://en/3143411) in the sense of [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483). This is a geometric take on [Hilbert's irreducibility theorem](wikipedia://en/1951153) which shows the rational numbers are Hilbertian. Results are applied to the [inverse Galois problem](wikipedia://en/739166). Thin sets (the French word is *mince*) are in some sense analogous to the [meagre sets](wikipedia://en/226975) (French *maigre*) of the [Baire category theorem](wikipedia://en/55227).

:::

## I {#I}

::: {#o56 .entry cue=term name="Igusa zeta-function"}

**Igusa zeta-function**

An [Igusa zeta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igusa_zeta-function), named for [Jun-ichi Igusa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jun-ichi_Igusa), is a [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) counting numbers of points on an algebraic variety modulo high powers *p*<sup>*n*</sup> of a fixed prime number *p*. General [rationality theorems](wikipedia://en/361210) are now known, drawing on methods of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636).[^o57]

:::

::: {#o58 .entry cue=term name="Infinite descent"}

**Infinite descent**

[Infinite descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_descent) was [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966)'s classical method for Diophantine equations. It became one half of the standard proof of the Mordell–Weil theorem, with the other being an argument with height functions (q.v.). Descent is something like division by two in a group of [principal homogeneous spaces](wikipedia://en/363441) (often called 'descents', when written out by equations); in more modern terms in a [Galois cohomology](wikipedia://en/1111315) group which is to be proved finite. See [Selmer group](wikipedia://en/1970862).

:::

::: {#o59 .entry cue=term name="Iwasawa theory"}

**Iwasawa theory**

[Iwasawa theory](wikipedia://en/396150) builds up from the [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513) and [Stickelberger's theorem](wikipedia://en/2202290) as a theory of [ideal class groups](wikipedia://en/150907) as [Galois modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_module) and [p-adic L-functions](wikipedia://en/22059815) (with roots in [Kummer congruence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kummer_congruence) on [Bernoulli numbers](wikipedia://en/4964)). In its early days in the late 1960s it was called *[Iwasawa's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenkichi_Iwasawa) analogue of the Jacobian*. The analogy was with the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) *J* of a curve *C* over a finite field *F* (*qua* Picard variety), where the finite field has [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) added to make finite field extensions *F* The local zeta-function (q.v.) of *C* can be recovered from the points *J*(*F*) as Galois module. In the same way, Iwasawa added *p*<sup>*n*</sup>-power roots of unity for fixed *p* and with *n* → ∞, for his analogue, to a number field *K*, and considered the [inverse limit](wikipedia://en/15109) of class groups, finding a *p*-adic L-function earlier introduced by Kubota and Leopoldt.

:::

## K {#K}

::: {#o61 .entry cue=term name="K-theory"}

**K-theory**

[Algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) is on one hand a quite general theory with an [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) flavour, and, on the other hand, implicated in some formulations of arithmetic conjectures. See for example [Birch–Tate conjecture](wikipedia://en/7774402), [Lichtenbaum conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lichtenbaum_conjecture).

:::

## L {#L}

::: {#o63 .entry cue=term name="Lang conjecture"}

**Lang conjecture**

[Enrico Bombieri](wikipedia://en/10475) (dimension 2), [Serge Lang](wikipedia://en/872991) and [Paul Vojta](wikipedia://en/1802312) (integral points case) and Piotr Blass have conjectured that algebraic varieties of [general type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_type) do not have [Zariski dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_dense) subsets of *K*-rational points, for *K* a finitely-generated field. This circle of ideas includes the understanding of *analytic hyperbolicity* and the Lang conjectures on that, and the Vojta conjectures. An *analytically hyperbolic algebraic variety* *V* over the complex numbers is one such that no [holomorphic mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_mapping) from the whole [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) to it exists, that is not constant. Examples include [compact Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Riemann_surface) of genus *g* > 1. Lang conjectured that *V* is analytically hyperbolic if and only if all subvarieties are of general type.[^o106]

:::

::: {#o64 .entry cue=term name="Linear torus"}

**Linear torus**

A *linear torus* is a [geometrically](wikipedia://en/62303294) irreducible Zariski-closed subgroup of an affine torus (product of multiplicative groups).[^o65]

:::

::: {#o66 .entry cue=term name="Local zeta-function"}

**Local zeta-function**

A [local zeta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function) is a [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) for the number of points on an algebraic variety *V* over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) *F*, over the finite [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634) of *F*. According to the Weil conjectures (q.v.) these functions, for [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities) varieties, exhibit properties closely analogous to the [Riemann zeta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta-function), including the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125).

:::

## M {#M}

::: {#o68 .entry cue=term name="Manin–Mumford conjecture"}

**Manin–Mumford conjecture**

The [Manin–Mumford conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manin–Mumford_conjecture), now proved by [Michel Raynaud](wikipedia://en/4760928), states that a curve *C* in its [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) *J* can only contain a finite number of points that are of finite order in *J*, unless *C* = *J*.[^o69][^o70]

:::

::: {#o71 .entry cue=term name="Mordell conjecture"}

**Mordell conjecture**

The [Mordell conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell_conjecture) is now [Faltings' theorem](wikipedia://en/172199), and states that a curve of genus at least two has only finitely many rational points. The [Uniformity conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformity_conjecture) states that there should be a uniform bound on the number of such points, depending only on the genus and the field of definition.

:::

::: {#o72 .entry cue=term name="Mordell–Lang conjecture"}

**Mordell–Lang conjecture**

The Mordell–Lang conjecture, now proved by [McQuillan](wikipedia://en/39463065) following work of Laurent, [Raynaud](wikipedia://en/4760928), Hindry, [Vojta](wikipedia://en/1802312), and [Faltings](wikipedia://en/862226), is a conjecture of [Lang](wikipedia://en/872991) unifying the Mordell conjecture and [Manin–Mumford conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manin–Mumford_conjecture) in an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) or [semiabelian variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiabelian_variety).[^o73][^o74]

:::

::: {#o75 .entry cue=term name="Mordell–Weil theorem"}

**Mordell–Weil theorem**

The [Mordell–Weil theorem](wikipedia://en/23087727) is a foundational result stating that for an abelian variety *A* over a number field *K* the group *A*(*K*) is a [finitely-generated abelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely-generated_abelian_group). This was proved initially for number fields *K*, but extends to all finitely-generated fields.

:::

::: {#o76 .entry cue=term name="Mordellic variety"}

**Mordellic variety**

A [Mordellic variety](wikipedia://en/35795816) is an algebraic variety which has only finitely many points in any finitely generated field.[^o77]

:::

## N {#N}

::: {#o79 .entry cue=term name="Naive height"}

**Naive height**

The [naive height](wikipedia://en/3141855#Naive_height) or classical height of a vector of rational numbers is the maximum absolute value of the vector of coprime integers obtained by multiplying through by a [lowest common denominator](wikipedia://en/378200). This may be used to define height on a point in projective space over **Q**, or of a polynomial, regarded as a vector of coefficients, or of an algebraic number, from the height of its minimal polynomial.[^o80]

:::

::: {#o81 .entry cue=term name="Néron symbol"}

**Néron symbol**

The *Néron symbol* is a bimultiplicative pairing between divisors and [algebraic cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_cycles) on an [Abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) used in Néron's formulation of the [Néron–Tate height](wikipedia://en/9236652) as a sum of local contributions.[^o82][^o83][^o84] The global Néron symbol, which is the sum of the local symbols, is just the negative of the height pairing.[^o85]

:::

::: {#o86 .entry cue=term name="Néron–Tate height"}

**Néron–Tate height**

The [Néron–Tate height](wikipedia://en/9236652) (also often referred to as the [canonical height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_height)) on an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) *A* is a height function (q.v.) that is essentially intrinsic, and an exact [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478), rather than approximately quadratic with respect to the addition on *A* as provided by the general theory of heights. It can be defined from a general height by a limiting process; there are also formulae, in the sense that it is a sum of local contributions.[^o85]

:::

::: {#o87 .entry cue=term name="Nevanlinna invariant"}

**Nevanlinna invariant**

The *[Nevanlinna invariant](wikipedia://en/35799819)* of an [ample divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ample_divisor) *D* on a [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_variety) [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) *X* is a real number which describes the rate of growth of the number of rational points on the variety with respect to the embedding defined by the divisor.[^o88] It has similar formal properties to the abscissa of convergence of the [height zeta function](wikipedia://en/35800065) and it is conjectured that they are essentially the same.[^o89]

:::

## O {#O}

::: {#o91 .entry cue=term name="Ordinary reduction"}

**Ordinary reduction**

An Abelian variety *A* of dimension *d* has *ordinary reduction* at a prime *p* if it has [good reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_reduction) at *p* and in addition the *p*-torsion has rank *d*.[^o92]

:::

## Q {#Q}

::: {#o94 .entry cue=term name="Quasi-algebraic closure"}

**Quasi-algebraic closure**

The topic of [quasi-algebraic closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-algebraic_closure), i.e. solubility guaranteed by a number of variables polynomial in the degree of an equation, grew out of studies of the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) and the [Chevalley–Warning theorem](wikipedia://en/2934314). It stalled in the face of [counterexamples](wikipedia://en/143151); but see [Ax–Kochen theorem](wikipedia://en/5911859) from [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636).

:::

## R {#R}

::: {#o96 .entry cue=term name="Reduction modulo a prime number or ideal"}

**Reduction *modulo* a prime number or ideal**

See *good reduction*.

:::

::: {#o97 .entry cue=term name="Replete ideal"}

**Replete ideal**

A *replete ideal* in a number field *K* is a formal product of a [fractional ideal](wikipedia://en/460700) of *K* and a vector of positive real numbers with components indexed by the infinite places of *K*.[^o98] A *replete divisor* is an [Arakelov divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arakelov_divisor).[^o99]

:::

## S {#S}

::: {#o101 .entry cue=term name="Sato–Tate conjecture"}

**Sato–Tate conjecture**

The [Sato–Tate conjecture](wikipedia://en/597837) describes the distribution of [Frobenius elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_element) in the [Tate modules](wikipedia://en/3094921) of the [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) obtained from reducing a given elliptic curve over the rationals. [Mikio Sato](wikipedia://en/373336) and, independently, [John Tate](wikipedia://en/245933)[^o102] suggested it around 1960. It is a prototype for [Galois representations](wikipedia://en/486929) in general.

:::

::: {#o103 .entry cue=term name="Skolem's method"}

**Skolem's method**

See *Chabauty's method*.

:::

::: {#o104 .entry cue=term name="Special set"}

**Special set**

The *special set* in an algebraic variety is the subset in which one might expect to find many rational points. The precise definition varies according to context. One definition is the [Zariski closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_closure) of the union of images of algebraic groups under non-trivial rational maps; alternatively one may take images of abelian varieties;[^o105] another definition is the union of all subvarieties that are not of general type.[^o106] For abelian varieties the definition would be the union of all translates of proper abelian subvarieties.[^o107] For a complex variety, the *holomorphic special set* is the Zariski closure of the images of all non-constant holomorphic maps from **C**. Lang conjectured that the analytic and algebraic special sets are equal.[^o108]

:::

::: {#o109 .entry cue=term name="Subspace theorem"}

**Subspace theorem**

Schmidt's **[subspace theorem](wikipedia://en/8830237)** shows that points of small height in projective space lie in a finite number of hyperplanes. A quantitative form of the theorem, in which the number of subspaces containing all solutions, was also obtained by Schmidt, and the theorem was generalised by Schlickewei (1977) to allow more general [absolute values](wikipedia://en/5042667) on [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field). The theorem may be used to obtain results on [Diophantine equations](wikipedia://en/9109) such as [Siegel's theorem on integral points](wikipedia://en/3989092) and solution of the [S-unit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-unit_equation).[^o110]

:::

## T {#T}

::: {#o112 .entry cue=term name="Tamagawa numbers"}

**Tamagawa numbers**

The direct [Tamagawa number](wikipedia://en/1168653) definition works well only for [linear algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/395885). There the [Weil conjecture on Tamagawa numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_conjecture_on_Tamagawa_numbers) was eventually proved. For abelian varieties, and in particular the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (q.v.), the Tamagawa number approach to a [local–global principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local–global_principle) fails on a direct attempt, though it has had heuristic value over many years. Now a sophisticated [equivariant Tamagawa number conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariant_Tamagawa_number_conjecture) is a major research problem.

:::

::: {#o113 .entry cue=term name="Tate conjecture"}

**Tate conjecture**

The [Tate conjecture](wikipedia://en/3124804) ([John Tate](wikipedia://en/245933), 1963) provided an analogue to the [Hodge conjecture](wikipedia://en/244518), also on [algebraic cycles](wikipedia://en/9520954), but well within arithmetic geometry. It also gave, for [elliptic surfaces](wikipedia://en/3073462), an analogue of the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (q.v.), leading quickly to a clarification of the latter and a recognition of its importance.

:::

::: {#o114 .entry cue=term name="Tate curve"}

**Tate curve**

The [Tate curve](wikipedia://en/35189927) is a particular elliptic curve over the [p-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) introduced by John Tate to study bad reduction (see *good reduction*).

:::

::: {#o115 .entry cue=term name="Tsen rank"}

**Tsen rank**

The [Tsen rank](wikipedia://en/36376453) of a field, named for [C. C. Tsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._C._Tsen) who introduced their study in 1936,[^o116] is the smallest natural number *i*, if it exists, such that the field is of class T<sub>*i*</sub>: that is, such that any system of polynomials with no constant term of degree *d<sub>j</sub>* in *n* variables has a non-trivial zero whenever *n* > Σ *d*<sub>*j*</sub><sup>*i*</sup>. Algebraically closed fields are of Tsen rank zero. The Tsen rank is greater or equal to the [Diophantine dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_dimension) but it is not known if they are equal except in the case of rank zero.[^o117]

:::

## U {#U}

::: {#o119 .entry cue=term name="Uniformity conjecture"}

**Uniformity conjecture**

The [uniformity conjecture](wikipedia://en/66851658) states that for any number field *K* and *g* > 2, there is a uniform bound *B*(*g*,*K*) on the number of *K*-rational points on any curve of genus *g*. The conjecture would follow from the [Bombieri–Lang conjecture](wikipedia://en/3142105).[^o120]

:::

::: {#o121 .entry cue=term name="Unlikely intersection"}

**Unlikely intersection**

An *unlikely intersection* is an algebraic subgroup intersecting a subvariety of a torus or abelian variety in a set of unusually large dimension, such as is involved in the [Mordell–Lang conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell–Lang_conjecture).[^o122]

:::

## V {#V}

::: {#o124 .entry cue=term name="Vojta conjecture"}

**Vojta conjecture**

The [Vojta conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vojta_conjecture) is a complex of conjectures by [Paul Vojta](wikipedia://en/1802312), making analogies between [Diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558) and [Nevanlinna theory](wikipedia://en/393135).

:::

## W {#W}

::: {#o126 .entry cue=term name="Weights"}

**Weights**

The [yoga of weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoga_of_weights) is a formulation by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) of analogies between [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590) and [l-adic cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-adic_cohomology).[^o127]

:::

::: {#o128 .entry cue=term name="Weil cohomology"}

**Weil cohomology**

The initial idea, later somewhat modified, for proving the Weil conjectures (q.v.), was to construct a [cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_theory) applying to algebraic varieties over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) that would both be as good as [singular homology](wikipedia://en/431041) at detecting topological structure, and have [Frobenius mappings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_mapping) acting in such a way that the [Lefschetz fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/582530) could be applied to the counting in [local zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function). For later history see [motive (algebraic geometry)](wikipedia://en/430680), [motivic cohomology](wikipedia://en/809314).

:::

::: {#o129 .entry cue=term name="Weil conjectures"}

**Weil conjectures**

The [Weil conjectures](wikipedia://en/244705) were three highly influential conjectures of [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019), made public around 1949, on local zeta-functions. The proof was completed in 1973. Those being proved, there remain extensions of the [Chevalley–Warning theorem](wikipedia://en/2934314) congruence, which comes from an elementary method, and [improvements of Weil bounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improvements_of_Weil_bounds), e.g. better estimates for curves of the number of points than come from Weil's basic theorem of 1940. The latter turn out to be of interest for [Algebraic geometry codes](wikipedia://en/1882683).

:::

::: {#o130 .entry cue=term name="Weil distributions on algebraic varieties"}

**Weil distributions on algebraic varieties**

André Weil proposed a theory in the 1920s and 1930s on [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) decomposition of algebraic numbers in coordinates of points on algebraic varieties. It has remained somewhat under-developed.

:::

::: {#o131 .entry cue=term name="Weil function"}

**Weil function**

A *Weil function* on an algebraic variety is a real-valued function defined off some [Cartier divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartier_divisor) which generalises the concept of [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) in [Arakelov theory](wikipedia://en/16364229).[^o132] They are used in the construction of the local components of the [Néron–Tate height](wikipedia://en/9236652).[^o133]

:::

::: {#o134 .entry cue=term name="Weil height machine"}

**Weil height machine**

The *Weil height machine* is an effective procedure for assigning a height function to any divisor on smooth projective variety over a number field (or to [Cartier divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartier_divisor) on non-smooth varieties).[^o135]

:::
