# Thin set (Serre)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thin set"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **thin set in the sense of Serre**, named after [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483), is a certain kind of subset constructed in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) over a given [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*, by allowed operations that are in a definite sense 'unlikely'. The two fundamental ones are: solving a polynomial equation that may or may not be the case; solving within *K* a polynomial that does not always factorise. One is also allowed to take finite unions.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

More precisely, let *V* be an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) over *K* (assumptions here are: *V* is an [irreducible set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_set), a [quasi-projective variety](wikipedia://en/1321785), and *K* has [characteristic zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_zero)). A **type I thin** set is a subset of *V*(*K*) that is not [Zariski-dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski-dense). That means it lies in an [algebraic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_set) that is a finite union of algebraic varieties of dimension lower than *d*, the [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251) of *V*. A **type II thin set** is an image of an [algebraic morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_morphism) (essentially a polynomial mapping) φ, applied to the *K*-points of some other *d*-dimensional algebraic variety *V*′, that maps essentially onto *V* as a [ramified covering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramified_covering) with degree *e* > 1. Saying this more technically, a thin set of type II is any subset of

φ(*V*′(*K*))

where *V*′ satisfies the same assumptions as *V* and φ is [generically surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generically_surjective) from the geometer's point of view. At the level of [function fields](wikipedia://en/2783843) we therefore have

\[*K*(*V*): *K*(*V*′)\] = *e* > 1.

While a typical point *v* of *V* is φ(*u*) with *u* in *V*′, from *v* lying in *V*(*K*) we can conclude typically only that the coordinates of *u* come from solving a degree *e* equation over *K*. The whole object of the theory of thin sets is then to understand that the solubility in question is a rare event. This reformulates in more geometric terms the classical [Hilbert irreducibility theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_irreducibility_theorem).

A **thin set**, in general, is a subset of a finite union of thin sets of types I and II .

The terminology *thin* may be justified by the fact that if *A* is a thin subset of the line over **Q** then the number of points of *A* of height at most *H* is ≪ *H*: the number of integral points of height at most *H* is $O\left({H^{1/2}}\right)$, and this result is best possible.[^o4]

A result of S. D. Cohen, based on the [large sieve method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_sieve_method), extends this result, counting points by [height function](wikipedia://en/3141855) and showing, in a strong sense, that a thin set contains a low proportion of them (this is discussed at length in Serre's *Lectures on the Mordell-Weil theorem*). Let *A* be a thin set in affine *n*-space over **Q** and let *N*(*H*) denote the number of integral points of naive height at most *H*. Then[^o5]

$$N(H) = O\left({H^{n-1/2} \log H}\right) .$$

## Hilbertian fields {#Hilbertian_fields}

A **Hilbertian variety** *V* over *K* is one for which *V*(*K*) is *not* thin: this is a [birational invariant](wikipedia://en/2605247) of *V*.[^o8] A **Hilbertian field** *K* is one for which there exists a Hilbertian variety of positive dimension over *K*:[^o8] the term was introduced by Lang in 1962.[^o9] If *K* is Hilbertian then the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) over *K* is Hilbertian, so this may be taken as the definition.[^o14][^o10]

The rational number field **Q** is Hilbertian, because [Hilbert's irreducibility theorem](wikipedia://en/1951153) has as a corollary that the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578) over **Q** is Hilbertian: indeed, any [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) is Hilbertian, again by the Hilbert irreducibility theorem.[^o14][^o11] More generally a finite degree extension of a Hilbertian field is Hilbertian[^o12] and any finitely generated infinite field is Hilbertian.[^o10]

There are several results on the permanence criteria of Hilbertian fields. Notably Hilbertianity is preserved under finite separable extensions[^o13] and abelian extensions. If *N* is a Galois extension of a Hilbertian field, then although *N* need not be Hilbertian itself, Weissauer's results asserts that any proper finite extension of *N* is Hilbertian. The most general result in this direction is [Haran's diamond theorem](wikipedia://en/24506903). A discussion on these results and more appears in Fried-Jarden's *Field Arithmetic*.

Being Hilbertian is at the other end of the scale from being [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed): the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) have all sets thin, for example. They, with the other [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375) ([real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), [p-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423)) are *not* Hilbertian.[^o14]

## WWA property {#WWA_property}

The **WWA property** (weak 'weak approximation', *sic*) for a variety *V* over a number field is [weak approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_approximation_theorem) (cf. [approximation in algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/3087377)), for finite sets of places of *K* avoiding some given finite set. For example take *K* = **Q**: it is required that *V*(**Q**) be dense in

Π *V*(**Q**<sub>*p*</sub>)

for all products over finite sets of prime numbers *p*, not including any of some set {*p*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *p*<sub>*M*</sub>} given once and for all. Ekedahl has proved that WWA for *V* implies *V* is Hilbertian.[^o17] In fact [Colliot-Thélène](wikipedia://en/15256996) conjectures WWA holds for any [unirational variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unirational_variety), which is therefore a stronger statement. This conjecture would imply a positive answer to the [inverse Galois problem](wikipedia://en/739166).[^o17]
