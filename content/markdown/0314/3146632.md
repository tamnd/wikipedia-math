# Bland–Altman plot

*Image: Bland-Altman Plot.svg.* Bland–Altman plot example

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bland–Altman plot"}

A **Bland–Altman plot** (or **Bland–Altman difference plot**) in [analytical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemistry) or [biomedicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedicine) is a method of [data plotting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_plot) used in analyzing the agreement between two different [assays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assay). It was popularised in [medical statistics](wikipedia://en/11082896) by [J. Martin Bland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Martin_Bland) and [Douglas G. Altman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Altman).[^o2][^o3] In other fields, it is also called a **Tukey mean-difference plot**, named after [John Tukey](wikipedia://en/415974).[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

Consider a [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) consisting of $n$ observations (for example, objects of unknown volume). Both assays (for example, different methods of volume measurement) are performed on each sample, resulting in $2n$ data points. Each of the $n$ samples is then represented on the graph by assigning the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of the two measurements as the $x$-value, and the difference between the two values as the $y$-value.

The [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) of a given sample $S$ with values of $S_1$ and $S_2$ determined by the two assays is

$$S(x,y)=\left( \frac{S_1+S_2}{2}, S_1-S_2 \right).$$

For comparing the dissimilarities between the two sets of samples independently from their mean values, it is more appropriate to look at the ratio of the pairs of measurements.[^o7] Log transformation (base 2) of the measurements before the analysis will enable the standard approach to be used; so the plot will be given by the following equation:

$$S(x,y)=\left( \frac{\log_2 S_1 +\log_2 S_2}{2}, \log_2 S_1 -\log_2 S_2 \right).$$

This version of the plot is used in [MA plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MA_plot).

:::

## Interpretation {#Interpretation}

Interpretation of a Bland-Altman plot is contingent on the construction of the plot and data at hand. Variations to the default plot have introduced throughout the years and each should be interpreted accordingly.[^o9]

### Original construction {#Original_construction}

The original plot displays a [scatter plot](wikipedia://en/412544) of differences between individual data points. The differences should be of the new reference system minus a [gold standard](wikipedia://en/723000).[^o3] An average of the differences is plotted horizontally with limits of agreement plotted parallel to this mean difference line. The limits of agreement represent a [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) for which most of the differences lie between systems. The mean difference represents a general bias between the two systems; a positive mean difference indicates the reference system generally produces larger values relative to the golden standard, and a negative mean difference indicating the reference system generally produces lower values than the verified system.[^o3] A mean difference close to 0 indicates agreement between two systems, though the limits of agreement illustrate more nuance.

### Limits of agreement {#Limits_of_agreement}

Since the limits of agreement are by-default contingent on the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) of the data, the distribution of the differences must follow a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). In the event that the distribution of differences are not normal, limits of agreement not contingent on normal distribution may be used instead. Bland and Altman's follow up paper on the topic explains that [percentile](wikipedia://en/354907) of differences are a suitable replacement in such cases.[^o7]

In any case, the limits of agreement more accurately illustrate the agreement between systems as opposed to just the mean difference. A novel reference system is said to be an appropriate substitute for a golden standard system if the limits of agreement are within a predetermined threshold. The threshold depends extensively on the magnitude of the data, the nature of the systems, and the contexts in which they are to be used.[^o12]

The 95% limits of agreement can be unreliable estimates of the population parameters especially for small sample sizes so, when comparing methods or assessing [repeatability](wikipedia://en/986651), it is important to calculate confidence intervals for 95% limits of agreement. This can be done by Bland and Altman's approximate method [^o3] or by more precise methods.[^o13]

### Sample size and power estimation {#Sample_size_and_power_estimation}

Determining an adequate sample size is a key consideration in Bland–Altman analysis, as it influences the precision of the estimated limits of agreement and the statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences between measurement methods. Historically, there has been limited formal guidance on how to perform power or sample size calculations for Bland–Altman studies. Early recommendations by Martin Bland suggested estimating sample size from the expected width of the confidence interval for the limits of agreement, an approach that does not explicitly account for Type II error and may yield insufficient sample sizes for typical study designs.[^o15]

A more rigorous approach was later introduced by Lu *et al.* (2016), who proposed a statistical framework for assessing power and determining sample size based on the distribution of measurement differences and predefined limits of clinical agreement.[^o16] Their method explicitly incorporates Type II error control and provides more accurate estimates of required sample sizes for studies targeting a given statistical power, typically 80%. Simulation studies in that work demonstrated good performance of the method under practical conditions; however, the authors did not provide publicly available software to implement the approach.

Several software packages now include implementations of the Lu *et al.* methodology. The commercial [MedCalc](https://www.medcalc.org/) statistical software provides sample size and power estimation tools for Bland–Altman analyses.[^o17] In addition, an open-source implementation is available in the R package *[blandPower](https://github.com/nfultz/blandPower)*, which provides functions to estimate power curves, determine required sample sizes, and visualize confidence interval widths as a function of sample size. The *blandPower* package was developed to promote reproducibility and accessibility of power and sample size calculations for method comparison studies using the Bland–Altman framework.

### Visualization variations {#Visualization_variations}

In the case that the differences grow proportionally to the magnitude of the data, then the data is said to have a 'proportional bias'. There are many methods for visualizing the plot and subsequent analysis to accommodate for it.[^o19]

Firstly, a [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) could illustrate any relevant trends. If the distribution of differences are equal at all points around the regression the data is said to be [homoscedastic](wikipedia://en/70780067) and the trend is a simple proportional bias. Inversely, if the data has wider spread at different magnitudes of the data, then the differences are said to be [heteroscedastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heteroscedastic), which has further implications. Statistical tests such as the [Breusch–Pagan test](wikipedia://en/2941510) or the [White test](wikipedia://en/4780627) can provide statistical indicators of heteroscedasticity.

*Image: Bland-altman plot of three clinicians' ratings of burn size, using two different methods.png.* Bland–Altman plot showing likely proportional bias

One typical example of a plot with heteroscedastic data is one whose [variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variation_%28statistics%29) of differences grows proportional to the magnitude of the data, visualized as an expanding 'v' shape.[^o19] In such cases, it may be suitable to visualize the [proportion](wikipedia://en/81863) of data points between systems as opposed to the raw differences.[^o20] Similarly, the plot of differences could be visualized [logarithmically](wikipedia://en/164055).[^o19] In either case, the relationship between the two systems illustrates a multiplicative relationship as opposed to linear one. This also indicates that the magnitude of the data correlates with variations of accuracy for the systems.

## Application {#Application}

One primary application of the Bland-Altman plot is to compare two clinical measurements that produce [continuous output](wikipedia://en/46561894).[^o23] It can be used to compare a new reference system, technique, or method with a verified [gold standard](wikipedia://en/723000), but a gold standard does not imply it to be without error.[^o7]

In order for the plot to be used to verify a reference system, a threshold is typically predetermined for which the limits of agreement must fall under. The value for the threshold is contingent on a myriad of contexts in which the systems and data exist within.[^o12]

The ability to verify a reference system lends the plot to a broad applicability and prominence across many fields. Over the years, it has gained prominence in [Optometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optometry), [nutritional science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutritional_science), [radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiology), [environmental sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_science), [surgery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgery), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine), [veterinary medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veterinary_medicine), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), and [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), to name a few.[^o12][^o24][^o25][^o26][^o27][^o28] Many recommendations and scholarly articles have also been published in efforts of polishing the technique, the underlying statistical construction, and validity of the plot.[^o29][^o30]

See [Analyse-it](wikipedia://en/9402181), [MedCalc](wikipedia://en/4294204), [NCSS](wikipedia://en/15049034), [GraphPad Prism](wikipedia://en/21572326#GraphPad_Prism), [R](wikipedia://en/376707), [StatsDirect](wikipedia://en/42010769), or [JASP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JASP) for software providing Bland–Altman plots.

## Notes {#Notes}

A similar method was proposed in 1981 by Eksborg.[^o33] This method was based on [Deming regression](wikipedia://en/251549)—a method introduced by Adcock in 1878. It has to be stressed that Eksborg' auggestion is absolutely not "a similar method2. Actually, Eksborg wrote "I suggest that a plot of the relative values found by the two methods being compared (method 1/method 2) vs the mean values found by the methods (or,alternatively, vs the values given by the reference method) illustrates far better the correlation between values obtained by the two methods.". Then to illustrae the advantages of the proposed plot he compared with the Deming's regression it compared the results from four simulated situations: 1. Constant precision (5% for both methods). 2. Constant precision within the range of high analytical values (5% from 400 to 1500 units for both methods); at lower analytical values the precision is decreasing with the decreasing analytical value. 3. Constant precision (5% for both methods) with the presence of a constant error ("blank"). 4. Constant precision (5% for both methods) with the presence of a constant error. This proposal does not make uncorrelated as the differences and means approach of Bland and Altman, two otherwise much correlated variables from the two measurement methods. So, this proposal is supported only by particular simulation cases without any sound methodological statistical background and has to be discharged.

Bland and Altman's Lancet paper [^o3] was number 29 in a list of the top 100 most-cited papers of all time with over 23,000 citations.[^o34]
