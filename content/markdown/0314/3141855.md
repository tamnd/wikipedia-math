# Height function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Height function"}

A **height function** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that quantifies the complexity of mathematical objects. In [Diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/502205), height functions quantify the size of solutions to [Diophantine equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_equations) and are typically functions from a set of points on [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) (or a set of algebraic varieties) to the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers).[^o2]

:::

For instance, the *classical* or *naive height* over the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) is typically defined to be the maximum of the numerators and denominators of the coordinates (e.g. $7$ for the coordinates $(3/7, 1/2)$), but in a [logarithmic scale](wikipedia://en/164055).

## Significance {#Significance}

Height functions allow mathematicians to count objects, such as [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200), that are otherwise infinite in quantity. For instance, the set of rational numbers of naive height (the maximum of the numerator and denominator when [expressed in lowest terms](wikipedia://en/14822)) below any given constant is finite despite the set of rational numbers being infinite.[^o4] In this sense, height functions can be used to prove [asymptotic results](wikipedia://en/641995) such as [Baker's theorem](wikipedia://en/28751817) in [transcendental number theory](wikipedia://en/1157698) which was proved by (txt).

In other cases, height functions can distinguish some objects based on their complexity. For instance, the [subspace theorem](wikipedia://en/8830237) proved by (txt) demonstrates that points of small height (i.e. small complexity) in [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) lie in a finite number of [hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/99862) and generalizes [Siegel's theorem on integral points](wikipedia://en/3989092) and solution of the [S-unit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-unit_equation).[^o7]

Height functions were crucial to the proofs of the [Mordell–Weil theorem](wikipedia://en/23087727) and [Faltings' theorem](wikipedia://en/172199) by (txt) and (txt) respectively. Several outstanding unsolved problems about the heights of rational points on algebraic varieties, such as the [Manin conjecture](wikipedia://en/41912043) and [Vojta's conjecture](wikipedia://en/34682455), have far-reaching implications for problems in [Diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558), [Diophantine equations](wikipedia://en/9109), [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177), and [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636).[^o10][^o11]

## History {#History}

An early form of height function was proposed by [Giambattista Benedetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giambattista_Benedetti) (c. 1563), who argued that the [consonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonance) of a [musical interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) could be measured by the product of its numerator and denominator (in reduced form); see Giambattista Benedetti.

Heights in Diophantine geometry were initially developed by [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) and [Douglas Northcott](wikipedia://en/9368121) beginning in the 1920s.[^o13] Innovations in 1960s were the [Néron–Tate height](wikipedia://en/9236652) and the realization that heights were linked to projective representations in much the same way that [ample line bundles](wikipedia://en/1186896) are in other parts of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). In the 1970s, [Suren Arakelov](wikipedia://en/10705349) developed Arakelov heights in [Arakelov theory](wikipedia://en/16364229).[^o14] In 1983, Faltings developed his theory of Faltings heights in his proof of Faltings' theorem.[^o15]

## Height functions in Diophantine geometry {#Height_functions_in_Diophantine_geometry}

::: {#o18 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Classical height"}

### Naive height {#Naive_height}

*Classical* or *naive height* is defined in terms of ordinary absolute value on [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316). It is typically a logarithmic scale and therefore can be viewed as being proportional to the "algebraic complexity" or number of [bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) needed to store a point.[^o4] It is typically defined to be the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of the maximum absolute value of the vector of coprime integers obtained by multiplying through by a [lowest common denominator](wikipedia://en/378200). This may be used to define height on a point in projective space over **Q**, or of a polynomial, regarded as a vector of coefficients, or of an algebraic number, from the height of its minimal polynomial.[^o19]

The naive height of a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) *x* = *p*/*q* (in lowest terms) is

- multiplicative height $H(p/q) = \max\{|p|,|q|\}$
- logarithmic height: $h(p/q) = \log H (p/q)$[^o20]

Therefore, the naive multiplicative and logarithmic heights of $4/10$ are $5$ and $\log(5)$, for example.

The naive height *H* of an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) *E* given by $y^{2} = x^{3} + Ax + B$ is defined to be $H(E) = \log \max(4|A|^{3}, 27|B|^{2})$.

:::

### Néron–Tate height {#Néron–Tate_height}

The *Néron–Tate height*, or *canonical height*, is a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) on the [Mordell–Weil group](wikipedia://en/5841813) of [rational points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_points) of an abelian variety defined over a [global field](wikipedia://en/288358). It is named after [André Néron](wikipedia://en/9175241), who first defined it as a sum of local heights,[^o22] and [John Tate](wikipedia://en/245933), who defined it globally in an unpublished work.[^o23]

### Weil height {#Weil_height}

Let *X* be a [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) over a number field *K*. Let *L* be a line bundle on *X*. One defines the *Weil height* on *X* with respect to *L* as follows.

First, suppose that *L* is [very ample](wikipedia://en/1186896). A choice of basis of the space $\Gamma(X,L)$ of global sections defines a morphism *ϕ* from *X* to projective space, and for all points *p* on *X*, one defines $h_L(p) := h(\phi(p))$, where *h* is the naive height on projective space.[^o25][^o26] For fixed *X* and *L*, choosing a different basis of global sections changes $h_L$, but only by a bounded function of *p*. Thus $h_L$ is well-defined up to addition of a function that is *O(1)*.

In general, one can write *L* as the difference of two very ample line bundles *L<sub>1</sub>* and *L<sub>2</sub>* on *X* and define $h_{L} := h_{L_1} - h_{L_2},$ which again is well-defined up to *O(1)*.[^o25][^o26]

::: {#o28 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Arakelov height"}

#### Arakelov height {#Arakelov_height}

The *Arakelov height* on a projective space over the field of algebraic numbers is a global height function with local contributions coming from [Fubini–Study metrics](wikipedia://en/2574486) on the [Archimedean fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_field) and the usual metric on the [non-Archimedean fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Archimedean_field).[^o29][^o30] It is the usual Weil height equipped with a different metric.[^o31]

:::

::: {#o33 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Faltings height"}

### Faltings height {#Faltings_height}

The *Faltings height* of an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) defined over a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) is a measure of its arithmetic complexity. It is defined in terms of the height of a [metrized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) [line bundle](wikipedia://en/361366). It was introduced by (txt) in his proof of the [Mordell conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell_conjecture).

:::

## Height functions in algebra {#Height_functions_in_algebra}

::: {#o37 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Height of a polynomial"}

### Height of a polynomial {#Height_of_a_polynomial}

For a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) *P* of degree *n* given by

$$P = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_n x^n ,$$

the **height** *H*(*P*) is defined to be the maximum of the magnitudes of its coefficients:[^o38]

$$H(P) = \underset{i}{\max} \,|a_i|.$$

One could similarly define the **length** *L*(*P*) as the sum of the magnitudes of the coefficients:

$$L(P) = \sum_{i=0}^n |a_i|.$$

#### Relation to Mahler measure {#Relation_to_Mahler_measure}

The [Mahler measure](wikipedia://en/1088425) *M*(*P*) of *P* is also a measure of the complexity of *P*.[^o40] The three functions *H*(*P*), *L*(*P*) and *M*(*P*) are related by the [inequalities](wikipedia://en/89489)

$$\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}^{-1} H(P) \le M(P) \le H(P) \sqrt{n+1} ;$$

$$L(p) \le 2^n M(p) \le 2^n L(p) ;$$

$$H(p) \le L(p) \le (n+1) H(p)$$

where $\scriptstyle \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$ is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668).

:::

## Height functions in automorphic forms {#Height_functions_in_automorphic_forms}

One of the conditions in the definition of an [automorphic form](wikipedia://en/782146) on the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) of an [adelic algebraic group](wikipedia://en/1168608) is *moderate growth*, which is an asymptotic condition on the growth of a height function on the general linear group viewed as an [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468).[^o42]

## Other height functions {#Other_height_functions}

The height of an irreducible [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) *x* = *p*/*q*, *q* > 0 is $|p|+q$ (this function is used for constructing a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$).[^o44]
