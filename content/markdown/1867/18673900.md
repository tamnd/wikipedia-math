# Riesz transform

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Riesz transform"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) theory of [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147), the **Riesz transforms** are a family of generalizations of the [Hilbert transform](wikipedia://en/574024) to [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697) of dimension *d* > 1. They are a type of [singular integral](wikipedia://en/12791220) [operator](wikipedia://en/22213), meaning that they are given by a [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of one function with another function having a singularity at the origin. Specifically, the Riesz transforms of a complex-valued function ƒ on **R**<sup>*d*</sup> are defined by

::: {#o2 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$R_jf(x) = c_d\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbf{R}^d\backslash B_\epsilon(x)}\frac{(x_j-t_j)f(t)}{|x-t|^{d+1}}\,dt \tag{1}$$

:::

for *j* = 1,2,...,*d*. The constant *c*<sub>*d*</sub> is a dimensional normalization given by

:::

$$c_d = \frac{1}{\pi\omega_{d-1}} = \frac{\Gamma[(d+1)/2]}{\pi^{(d+1)/2}}.$$

where ω<sub>*d*−1</sub> is the [volume of the unit (*d* − 1)-ball](wikipedia://en/21067650). The limit is written in various ways, often as a [principal value](wikipedia://en/408195), or as a [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) with the [tempered distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Tempered_distributions_and_Fourier_transform)

$$K(x) = \frac{1}{\pi\omega_{d-1}} \, p.v. \frac{x_j}{|x|^{d+1}}.$$

The Riesz transforms arises in the study of differentiability properties of harmonic potentials in [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342) and [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147). In particular, they arise in the proof of the Calderón-Zygmund inequality (Gilbarg Trudinger 1983, §9.4).

## Multiplier properties {#Multiplier_properties}

The Riesz transforms are given by a [Fourier multiplier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_multiplier). Indeed, the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of *R*<sub>*j*</sub>ƒ is given by

$$\mathcal{F}(R_jf)(x) = -i\frac{x_j}{|x|}(\mathcal{F}f)(x).$$

In this form, the Riesz transforms are seen to be generalizations of the [Hilbert transform](wikipedia://en/574024). The kernel is a [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) which is [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/622844) of degree zero. A particular consequence of this last observation is that the Riesz transform defines a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) from *L*<sup>2</sup>(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>) to itself.[^o5]

This homogeneity property can also be stated more directly without the aid of the Fourier transform. If σ<sub>*s*</sub> is the [dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation) on **R**<sup>*d*</sup> by the scalar *s*, that is σ<sub>*s*</sub>*x* = *sx*, then σ<sub>*s*</sub> defines an action on functions via [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995):

$$\sigma_s^* f = f\circ\sigma_s.$$

The Riesz transforms commute with σ<sub>*s*</sub>:

$$\sigma_s^* (R_jf) = R_j(\sigma_x^*f).$$

Similarly, the Riesz transforms commute with translations. Let τ<sub>*a*</sub> be the translation on **R**<sup>*d*</sup> along the vector *a*; that is, τ<sub>*a*</sub>(*x*) = *x* + *a*. Then

$$\tau_a^* (R_jf) = R_j(\tau_a^*f).$$

For the final property, it is convenient to regard the Riesz transforms as a single [vectorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) entity *R*ƒ = (*R*<sub>1</sub>ƒ,...,*R*<sub>*d*</sub>ƒ). Consider a [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) ρ in **R**<sup>*d*</sup>. The rotation acts on spatial variables, and thus on functions via pullback. But it also can act on the spatial vector *R*ƒ. The final transformation property asserts that the Riesz transform is [equivariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariant) with respect to these two actions; that is,

$$\rho^* R_j [(\rho^{-1})^*f] = \sum_{k=1}^d \rho_{jk} R_kf.$$

These three properties in fact characterize the Riesz transform in the following sense. Let *T*=(*T*<sub>*1*</sub>,...,*T*<sub>*d*</sub>) be a *d*-tuple of bounded linear operators from *L*<sup>2</sup>(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>) to *L*<sup>2</sup>(**R**<sup>*d*</sup>) such that

- *T* commutes with all dilations and translations.
- *T* is equivariant with respect to rotations.

Then, for some constant *c*, *T* = *cR*.

## Relationship with the Laplacian {#Relationship_with_the_Laplacian}

Formally, the Riesz transforms can be identified with [fractional derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_derivative) via

$$R f = -\,\nabla (-\Delta)^{-1/2} f,$$

that is, $R_j = -\,\partial_j (-\Delta)^{-1/2}$.

In particular, one has the operator identity

$$\partial_i \partial_j = -\,R_i R_j \Delta,$$

valid as Fourier multipliers on $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d)$ and, by extension, on the space of [tempered distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempered_distributions) $\mathcal{S}'$.

For [Schwartz functions](wikipedia://en/4277567) $u$, this implies

$$R_i R_j(\Delta u) = -\,\tfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}.$$

The situation becomes subtler for general tempered distributions. If $\Delta u \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^d)$, then $u$ is determined modulo [affine functions](wikipedia://en/38449), which have vanishing second derivatives, and the above identity holds in $L^2$ without correction.

More generally, if $u$ is such that $\Delta u$ has only tempered growth but not integrability (for instance, if $u$ is a quadratic polynomial), then additional polynomial terms may appear:

$$\tfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} = -\,R_i R_j (\Delta u) + P_{ij}(x),$$

where $P_{ij}$ is a polynomial depending on $u$. This reflects the fact that inversion of the Laplacian is only well-defined on tempered distributions modulo polynomials.
