# Conformal gravity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conformal gravity"}

**Conformal gravity** refers to gravity theories that are invariant under [conformal transformations](wikipedia://en/50627) in the [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243) sense; more accurately, they are invariant under [Weyl transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_transformation) $g_{ab}\rightarrow\Omega^2(x)g_{ab}$ where $g_{ab}$ is the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) and $\Omega(x)$ is a function on [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758).

:::

## Weyl-squared theories {#Weyl-squared_theories}

The simplest theory in this category has the square of the [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848) as the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/40752010)

$$\mathcal{S}=\int \, \mathrm{d}^4x \, \sqrt{-g \;} \, C_{abcd}\,C^{abcd}~,$$

where $\; C_{abcd} \;$ is the Weyl tensor. This is to be contrasted with the usual [Einstein–Hilbert action](wikipedia://en/855776) where the Lagrangian is just the [Ricci scalar](wikipedia://en/285622). The equation of motion upon varying the metric is called the [Bach tensor](wikipedia://en/5784784),

$$2\,\partial_a\,\partial_d\,{{C^a}_{bc}}^d ~~+~~ R_{ad} \, {{C^a}_{bc}}^d ~=~ 0~,$$

where $\; R_{ab} \;$ is the [Ricci tensor](wikipedia://en/276582). Conformally flat metrics are solutions of this equation.

Since these theories lead to [fourth-order equations](wikipedia://en/308152) for the fluctuations around a fixed background, they are not manifestly unitary. It has therefore been generally believed that they could not be consistently quantized. This is now disputed.[^o3]

## Four-derivative theories {#Four-derivative_theories}

Conformal gravity is an example of a 4-[derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) theory. This means that each term in the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) can contain up to four derivatives. There are pros and cons of 4-derivative theories. The pros are that the quantized version of the theory is more convergent and [renormalisable](wikipedia://en/291453). The cons are that there may be issues with [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality). A simpler example of a 4-derivative wave equation is the scalar 4-derivative wave equation:

$$\operatorname{\Box}^2 \Phi =0$$

The solution for this in a central field of force is:

$$\Phi(r)= 1 - \frac{2m}{r} +ar +br^2$$

The first two terms are the same as a normal wave equation. Because this equation is a simpler approximation to conformal gravity, m corresponds to the mass of the central source. The last two terms are unique to 4-derivative wave equations. It has been suggested that small values be assigned to them to account for the [galactic acceleration constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerating_expansion_of_the_universe) (also known as [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter)) and the [dark energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy) constant.[^o5] The solution equivalent to the [Schwarzschild solution](wikipedia://en/264606) in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) for a spherical source for conformal gravity has a metric with:

$$\varphi(r) = g^{00} = (1-6bc)^\frac{1}{2} - \frac{2b}{r} + c r + \frac{d}{3} r^2$$

to show the difference between general relativity. 6bc is very small, and so can be ignored. The additional parameters have been argued to explain [galactic rotation curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactic_rotation_curve) without invoking dark matter.[^o6]

The main issue with conformal gravity theories, as well as any theory with higher derivatives, is the typical presence of [ghosts](wikipedia://en/671768#General_ghosts_in_theoretical_physics), which point to instabilities of the [quantum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum) version of the theory, although there might be a solution to the ghost problem.[^o7]

An alternative approach is to consider the gravitational constant as a [symmetry broken](wikipedia://en/297466) [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879), in which case you would consider a small correction to [Newtonian gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation) like this (where we consider $\varepsilon$ to be a small correction):

$$\operatorname \Box \Phi + \varepsilon^2 \operatorname{\Box}^2 \Phi = 0$$

in which case the general solution is the same as the Newtonian case except there can be an additional term:

$$\Phi = 1 - \frac{2m}{r} \left( 1 + \alpha \sin\left(\frac r \varepsilon +\beta\right) \right)$$

where there is an additional component varying [sinusoidally](wikipedia://en/324749) over space. The wavelength of this variation could be quite large, such as an atomic width. Thus there appear to be several stable potentials around a gravitational force in this model.

## Conformal unification to the Standard Model {#Conformal_unification_to_the_Standard_Model}

By adding a suitable gravitational term to the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) action in [curved](wikipedia://en/2557627) [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), the theory develops a local conformal (Weyl) invariance. The conformal gauge is fixed by choosing a reference mass scale based on the gravitational constant. This approach generates the masses for the [vector bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_boson) and matter fields similar to the [Higgs mechanism](wikipedia://en/644550) without traditional spontaneous symmetry breaking.[^o9]
