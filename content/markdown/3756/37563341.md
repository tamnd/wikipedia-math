# Resolvent (Galois theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Resolvent"}

In [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), a discipline within the field of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **resolvent** for a [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) *G* is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) whose [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) depend polynomially on the coefficients of a given polynomial *p* and has, roughly speaking, a [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of *p* is included in *G*. More exactly, if the Galois group is included in *G*, then the resolvent has a rational root, and the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) is true if the rational root is a [simple root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_root_%28polynomial%29). Resolvents were introduced by [Joseph Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) and systematically used by [Évariste Galois](wikipedia://en/9815). Nowadays they are still a fundamental tool to compute [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673). The simplest examples of resolvents are

:::

- $X^2-\Delta$ where $\Delta$ is the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607), which is a resolvent for the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726). In the case of a [cubic equation](wikipedia://en/180787), this resolvent is sometimes called the **quadratic resolvent**; its roots appear explicitly in the formulas for the roots of a cubic equation.
- The [cubic resolvent](wikipedia://en/25975323) of a [quartic equation](wikipedia://en/308152), which is a resolvent for the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of 8 elements.
- The [Cayley resolvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintic_function#Solvable_quintics) is a resolvent for the maximal [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) Galois group in degree five. It is a polynomial of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) 6.

These three resolvents have the property of being *always separable*, which means that, if they have a [multiple root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_root), then the polynomial *p* is not [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725). It is not known if there is an always separable resolvent for every group of permutations.

For every equation the roots may be expressed in terms of [radicals](wikipedia://en/235029) and of a root of a resolvent for a solvable group, because the Galois group of the equation over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) generated by this root is solvable.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $n$ be a positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), which will be the degree of the equation that we will consider, and $(X_{1}, ..., X_{n})$ an ordered list of [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29). According to [Vieta's formulas](wikipedia://en/714050) this defines the *generic* monic polynomial of degree $n$

$$F(X)=X^n+\sum_{i=1}^n (-1)^i E_i X^{n-i} = \prod_{i=1}^n (X-X_i),$$

where $E_{i}$ is the $i$ th [elementary symmetric polynomial](wikipedia://en/1440695).

The [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$ [acts](wikipedia://en/12781) on the $X_{i}$ by permuting them, and this induces an action on the polynomials in the $X_{i}$. The [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_%28group_theory%29) of a given polynomial under this action is generally trivial, but some polynomials have a bigger stabilizer. For example, the stabilizer of an elementary symmetric polynomial is the whole [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $S_{n}$. If the stabilizer is non-trivial, the polynomial is fixed by some non-trivial [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $G$; it is said to be an *invariant* of $G$. Conversely, given a subgroup $G$ of $S_{n}$, an invariant of $G$ is a **resolvent invariant** for $G$ if it is not an invariant of any bigger subgroup of $S_{n}$.[^o4]

Finding invariants for a given subgroup $G$ of $S_{n}$ is relatively easy; one can sum the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of a [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) under the action of $S_{n}$. However, it may occur that the resulting polynomial is an invariant for a larger group. For example, consider the case of the subgroup $G$ of $S_{4}$ of order 4, consisting of $(12)(34)$, $(13)(24)$, $(14)(23)$ and the identity (for the notation, see [Permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634)). The monomial $X_{1}X_{2}$ gives the invariant $2(X_{1}X_{2} + X_{3}X_{4})$. It is not a resolvent invariant for $G$, because being invariant by $(12)$, it is in fact a resolvent invariant for the larger dihedral subgroup $D_{4}$: $\langle(12), (1324)\rangle$, and is used to define the [resolvent cubic](wikipedia://en/25975323) of the [quartic equation](wikipedia://en/251591).

If $P$ is a resolvent invariant for a group $G$ of [index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_%28group_theory%29) $m$ inside $S_{n}$, then its orbit under $S_{n}$ has order $m$. Let $P_{1}, ..., P_{m}$ be the elements of this orbit. Then the polynomial

$$R_G=\prod_{i=1}^m (Y-P_i)$$

is invariant under $S_{n}$. Thus, when expanded, its coefficients are polynomials in the $X_{i}$ that are invariant under the action of the symmetry group and thus may be expressed as polynomials in the elementary symmetric polynomials. In other words, $R_{G}$ is an [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) in $Y$ whose coefficients are polynomial in the coefficients of $F$. Having the resolvent invariant as a root, it is called a **resolvent** (sometimes **resolvent equation**).

Consider now an irreducible polynomial

$$f(X)=X^n+\sum_{i=1}^n a_i X^{n-i} = \prod_{i=1}^n (X-x_i),$$

with coefficients in a given field $K$ (typically the [field of rationals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_of_rationals)) and roots $x_{i}$ in an [algebraically closed field extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed_extension). Substituting the $X_{i}$ by the $x_{i}$ and the coefficients of $F$ by those of $f$ in the above, we get a polynomial $R_G^{(f)}(Y)$, also called *resolvent* or *specialized resolvent* in case of ambiguity). If the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of $f$ is contained in $G$, the specialization of the resolvent invariant is invariant by $G$ and is thus a root of $R_G^{(f)}(Y)$ that belongs to $K$ (is rational on $K$). Conversely, if $R_G^{(f)}(Y)$ has a rational root, which is not a multiple root, the Galois group of $f$ is contained in $G$.

:::

## Terminology {#Terminology}

There are some variants in the terminology.

- Depending on the authors or on the context, *resolvent* may refer to *resolvent invariant* instead of to *resolvent equation*.
- A **Galois resolvent** is a resolvent such that the resolvent invariant is linear in the roots.
- The **Lagrange resolvent** may refer to the linear polynomial $$\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} X_i \omega^i$$ where $\omega$ is a [primitive *n*th root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_nth_root_of_unity). It is the resolvent invariant of a Galois resolvent for the identity group.
- A **relative resolvent** is defined similarly as a resolvent, but considering only the action of the elements of a given subgroup $H$ of $S_{n}$, having the property that, if a relative resolvent for a subgroup $G$ of $H$ has a rational simple root and the Galois group of $f$ is contained in $H$, then the Galois group of $f$ is contained in $G$. In this context, a usual resolvent is called an **absolute resolvent**.

:::

::: {#o8 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Resolvent method"}

## Resolvent method {#Resolvent_method}

The Galois group of a polynomial of degree $n$ is $S_n$ or a [proper subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subgroup) of it. If a polynomial is separable and irreducible, then the corresponding Galois group is a transitive subgroup.

Transitive subgroups of $S_n$ form a directed graph: one group can be a subgroup of several groups. One resolvent can tell if the Galois group of a polynomial is a (not necessarily proper) subgroup of given group. The resolvent method is just a systematic way to check groups one by one until only one group is possible. This does not mean that every group must be checked: every resolvent can cancel out many possible groups. For example, for degree five polynomials there is never need for a resolvent of $D_5$: resolvents for $A_5$ and $M_{20}$ give desired information.

One way is to begin from maximal (transitive) subgroups until the right one is found and then continue with maximal subgroups of that.
