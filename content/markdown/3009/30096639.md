# Sufficient dimension reduction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sufficient dimension reduction"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **sufficient dimension reduction (SDR)** is a paradigm for analyzing data that combines the ideas of [dimension reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_reduction) with the concept of [sufficiency](wikipedia://en/140841).

:::

Dimension reduction has long been a primary goal of [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997). Given a response variable *y* and a *p*-dimensional predictor vector $\textbf{x}$, regression analysis aims to study the distribution of $y\mid\textbf{x}$, the [conditional distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_distribution) of $y$ given $\textbf{x}$. A **dimension reduction** is a function $R(\textbf{x})$ that maps $\textbf{x}$ to a subset of $\mathbb{R}^k$, *k* \< *p*, thereby reducing the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of $\textbf{x}$.[^o2] For example, $R(\textbf{x})$ may be one or more [linear combinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_combinations) of $\textbf{x}$.

A dimension reduction $R(\textbf{x})$ is said to be **sufficient** if the distribution of $y\mid R(\textbf{x})$ is the same as that of $y\mid\textbf{x}$. In other words, no information about the regression is lost in reducing the dimension of $\textbf{x}$ if the reduction is sufficient.[^o2]

## Graphical motivation {#Graphical_motivation}

In a regression setting, it is often useful to summarize the distribution of $y\mid\textbf{x}$ graphically. For instance, one may consider a [scatterplot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scatterplot) of $y$ versus one or more of the predictors or a linear combination of the predictors. A scatterplot that contains all available regression information is called a **sufficient summary plot**.

When $\textbf{x}$ is high-dimensional, particularly when $p\geq 3$, it becomes increasingly challenging to construct and visually interpret sufficiency summary plots without reducing the data. Even three-dimensional scatter plots must be viewed via a [computer program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program), and the third dimension can only be visualized by rotating the coordinate axes. However, if there exists a sufficient dimension reduction $R(\textbf{x})$ with small enough dimension, a sufficient summary plot of $y$ versus $R(\textbf{x})$ may be constructed and visually interpreted with relative ease.

Hence sufficient dimension reduction allows for graphical intuition about the distribution of $y\mid\textbf{x}$, which might not have otherwise been available for high-dimensional data.

Most graphical methodology focuses primarily on dimension reduction involving linear combinations of $\textbf{x}$. The rest of this article deals only with such reductions.

## Dimension reduction subspace {#Dimension_reduction_subspace}

Suppose $R(\textbf{x}) = A^T\textbf{x}$ is a sufficient dimension reduction, where $A$ is a $p\times k$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) $k\leq p$. Then the regression information for $y\mid\textbf{x}$ can be inferred by studying the distribution of $y\mid A^T\textbf{x}$, and the plot of $y$ versus $A^T\textbf{x}$ is a sufficient summary plot.

[Without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000), only the [space](wikipedia://en/32370) [spanned](wikipedia://en/56353) by the columns of $A$ need be considered. Let $\eta$ be a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for the [column space](wikipedia://en/97848) of $A$, and let the space spanned by $\eta$ be denoted by $\mathcal{S}(\eta)$. It follows from the definition of a sufficient dimension reduction that

$$F_{y\mid x} = F_{y\mid\eta^Tx},$$

where $F$ denotes the appropriate [distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793). Another way to express this property is

$$y\perp\!\!\!\perp\textbf{x}\mid\eta^T\textbf{x},$$

or $y$ is [conditionally independent](wikipedia://en/801135) of $\textbf{x}$, given $\eta^T\textbf{x}$. Then the subspace $\mathcal{S}(\eta)$ is defined to be a **dimension reduction subspace (DRS)**.[^o5]

### Structural dimensionality {#Structural_dimensionality}

For a regression $y\mid\textbf{x}$, the **structural dimension**, $d$, is the smallest number of distinct linear combinations of $\textbf{x}$ necessary to preserve the conditional distribution of $y\mid\textbf{x}$. In other words, the smallest dimension reduction that is still sufficient maps $\textbf{x}$ to a subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$. The corresponding DRS will be *d*-dimensional.[^o5]

### Minimum dimension reduction subspace {#Minimum_dimension_reduction_subspace}

A subspace $\mathcal{S}$ is said to be a **minimum DRS** for $y\mid\textbf{x}$ if it is a DRS and its dimension is less than or equal to that of all other DRSs for $y\mid\textbf{x}$. A minimum DRS $\mathcal{S}$ is not necessarily unique, but its dimension is equal to the structural dimension $d$ of $y\mid\textbf{x}$, by definition.[^o5]

If $\mathcal{S}$ has basis $\eta$ and is a minimum DRS, then a plot of *y* versus $\eta^T\textbf{x}$ is a **minimal sufficient summary plot**, and it is (*d* + 1)-dimensional.

## Central subspace {#Central_subspace}

If a subspace $\mathcal{S}$ is a DRS for $y\mid\textbf{x}$, and if $\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{S}_\text{drs}$ for all other DRSs $\mathcal{S}_\text{drs}$, then it is a **central dimension reduction subspace**, or simply a **central subspace**, and it is denoted by $\mathcal{S}_{y\mid x}$. In other words, a central subspace for $y\mid\textbf{x}$ exists [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the intersection $\bigcap\mathcal{S}_\text{drs}$ of all dimension reduction subspaces is also a dimension reduction subspace, and that intersection is the central subspace $\mathcal{S}_{y\mid x}$.[^o5]

The central subspace $\mathcal{S}_{y\mid x}$ does not necessarily exist because the intersection $\bigcap\mathcal{S}_\text{drs}$ is not necessarily a DRS. However, if $\mathcal{S}_{y\mid x}$ *does* exist, then it is also the unique minimum dimension reduction subspace.[^o5]

### Existence of the central subspace {#Existence_of_the_central_subspace}

While the existence of the central subspace $\mathcal{S}_{y\mid x}$ is not guaranteed in every regression situation, there are some rather broad conditions under which its existence follows directly. For example, consider the following proposition from Cook (1998):

Let $\mathcal{S}_1$ and $\mathcal{S}_2$ be dimension reduction subspaces for $y\mid\textbf{x}$. If $\textbf{x}$ has [density](wikipedia://en/43487) $f(a) > 0$ for all $a\in\Omega_x$ and $f(a) = 0$ everywhere else, where $\Omega_x$ is [convex](wikipedia://en/6292), then the intersection $\mathcal{S}_1\cap\mathcal{S}_2$ is also a dimension reduction subspace.

It follows from this proposition that the central subspace $\mathcal{S}_{y\mid x}$ exists for such $\textbf{x}$.[^o5]

## Methods for dimension reduction {#Methods_for_dimension_reduction}

There are many existing methods for dimension reduction, both graphical and numeric. For example, **[sliced inverse regression](wikipedia://en/25444200) (SIR)** and **sliced average variance estimation (SAVE)** were introduced in the 1990s and continue to be widely used.[^o11] Although SIR was originally designed to estimate an *effective dimension [reducing subspace](wikipedia://en/69116423)*, it is now understood that it estimates only the central subspace, which is generally different.

More recent methods for dimension reduction include [likelihood](wikipedia://en/44968)-based sufficient dimension reduction,[^o12] estimating the central subspace based on the inverse third [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) (or *k*th moment),[^o13] estimating the central solution space,[^o14] graphical regression,[^o5] envelope model, and the principal [support vector machine](wikipedia://en/65309).[^o15] For more details on these and other methods, consult the statistical literature.

[Principal components analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_components_analysis) **(PCA)** and similar methods for dimension reduction are not based on the sufficiency principle.

### Example: linear regression {#Example:_linear_regression}

Consider the regression model

$$y = \alpha + \beta^T\textbf{x} + \varepsilon,\text{ where }\varepsilon\perp\!\!\!\perp\textbf{x}.$$

Note that the distribution of $y\mid\textbf{x}$ is the same as the distribution of $y\mid\beta^T\textbf{x}$. Hence, the span of $\beta$ is a dimension reduction subspace. Also, $\beta^T\textbf{x}$ is 1-dimensional (unless $\beta=\textbf{0}$), so the structural dimension of this regression is $d=1$.

The [OLS](wikipedia://en/1651906) estimate $\hat{\beta}$ of $\beta$ is [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569), and so the span of $\hat{\beta}$ is a consistent estimator of $\mathcal{S}_{y\mid x}$. The plot of $y$ versus $\hat{\beta}^T\textbf{x}$ is a sufficient summary plot for this regression.
