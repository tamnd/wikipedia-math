# History of randomness

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="History of randomness"}

*Image: Pompeii - Osteria della Via di Mercurio - Dice Players.jpg.* Ancient [fresco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresco) of dice players in [Pompei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompei)

In ancient **history**, the concepts of chance and **[randomness](wikipedia://en/19196523)** were intertwined with that of fate. Many ancient peoples threw dice to determine fate, and this later evolved into [games of chance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Games_of_chance). At the same time, most ancient cultures used various methods of [divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination) to attempt to circumvent randomness and fate.[^n1][^n2] Beyond [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion) and [games of chance](wikipedia://en/53481), randomness has been attested for [sortition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sortition) since at least ancient [Athenian democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athenian_democracy) in the form of a [kleroterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleroterion).[^n3]

:::

The formalization of odds and chance was perhaps earliest done by the Chinese 3,000 years ago. The [Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy) discussed randomness at length, but only in non-quantitative forms. It was only in the sixteenth century that Italian mathematicians began to formalize the odds associated with various games of chance. The invention of modern [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) had a positive impact on the formal study of randomness. In the 19th century the concept of [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) was introduced in physics.

The early part of the twentieth century saw a rapid growth in the formal analysis of randomness, and mathematical foundations for probability were introduced, leading to its [axiomatization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatization) in 1933. At the same time, the advent of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) changed the scientific perspective on [determinacy](wikipedia://en/2596030). In the mid to late 20th-century, ideas of [algorithmic information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_information_theory) introduced new dimensions to the field via the concept of [algorithmic randomness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_randomness).

Although randomness had often been viewed as an obstacle and a nuisance for many centuries, in the twentieth century computer scientists began to realize that the *deliberate* introduction of randomness into computations can be an effective tool for designing better algorithms. In some cases, such [randomized algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_algorithms) are able to outperform the best deterministic methods.

## Antiquity to the Middle Ages {#Antiquity_to_the_Middle_Ages}

*Image: Fortuna or Fortune.jpg.* Depiction of Roman goddess [Fortuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortuna) who determined fate, by [Hans Beham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Sebald_Beham), 1541

Pre-Christian people along the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean) threw dice to determine fate, and this later evolved into games of chance.[^n4] There is also evidence of games of chance played by ancient Egyptians, Hindus and Chinese, dating back to 2100 BC.[^n5] The Chinese used dice before the Europeans, and have a long history of playing games of chance.[^n6]

Over 3,000 years ago, the problems concerned with the tossing of several coins were considered in the [I Ching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Changes), one of the oldest Chinese mathematical texts, that probably dates to 1150 BC. The two principal elements [yin and yang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yin_and_yang) were combined in the I Ching in various forms to produce *Heads and Tails* permutations of the type HH, TH, HT, etc. and the Chinese seem to have been aware of [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497) long before the Europeans formalized it in the 17th century.[^n7] However, Western philosophy focused on the non-mathematical aspects of chance and randomness until the 16th century.

The development of the concept of chance throughout history has been very gradual. Historians have wondered why progress in the field of randomness was so slow, given that humans have encountered chance since antiquity. [Deborah J. Bennett](wikipedia://en/67026360) suggests that ordinary people face an inherent difficulty in understanding randomness, although the concept is often taken as being obvious and self-evident. She cites studies by [Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman) and [Tversky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amos_Tversky); these concluded that statistical principles are not learned from everyday experience because people do not attend to the detail necessary to gain such knowledge.[^n8]

The Greek philosophers were the earliest Western thinkers to address chance and randomness. Around 400 BC, [Democritus](wikipedia://en/8211) presented a view of the world as governed by the unambiguous laws of order and considered randomness as a subjective concept that only originated from the inability of humans to understand the nature of events. He used the example of two men who would send their servants to bring water at the same time to cause them to meet. The servants, unaware of the plan, would view the meeting as random.[^n9]

[Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) saw chance and necessity as opposite forces. He argued that nature had rich and constant patterns that could not be the result of chance alone, but that these patterns never displayed the machine-like uniformity of necessary determinism. He viewed randomness as a genuine and widespread part of the world, but as subordinate to necessity and order.[^n10] Aristotle classified events into three types: *certain* events that happen necessarily; *probable* events that happen in most cases; and *unknowable* events that happen by pure chance. He considered the outcome of games of chance as unknowable.[^n11]

Around 300 BC [Epicurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurus) proposed the concept that randomness exists by itself, independent of human knowledge. He believed that in the atomic world, atoms would *swerve* at random along their paths, bringing about randomness at higher levels.[^n12]

*Image: HoteiMusashi.jpg.* [Hotei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budai), the deity of fortune observing a [cock fight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cock_fight) in a 16th-century Japanese print

For several centuries thereafter, the idea of chance continued to be intertwined with fate. Divination was practiced in many cultures, using diverse methods. The Chinese analyzed the cracks in turtle shells, while the Germans, who according to [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus) had the highest regards for lots and omens, utilized strips of bark.[^n13] In the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire), chance was personified by the Goddess [Fortuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortuna). The Romans would partake in games of chance to simulate what Fortuna would have decided. In 49 BC, [Julius Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar) allegedly decided on his fateful decision to cross the [Rubicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubicon) after throwing dice.[^n14]

Aristotle's classification of events into the three classes: *certain*, *probable* and *unknowable* was adopted by Roman philosophers, but they had to reconcile it with deterministic [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) teachings in which even events unknowable to man were considered to be predetermined by God. About 960 Bishop [Wibold of Cambrai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wibold_%28bishop_of_Cambrai%29) correctly enumerated the 56 different outcomes (without permutations) of playing with three dice. No reference to playing cards has been found in Europe before 1350. The Church preached against card playing, and card games spread much more slowly than games based on dice.[^n15] The Christian Church specifically forbade [divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination); and wherever Christianity went, divination lost most of its old-time power.[^n16][^n17]

Over the centuries, many Christian scholars wrestled with the conflict between the belief in [free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will) and its implied randomness, and the idea that God knows everything that happens. Saints [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine) and [Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquinas) tried to reach an accommodation between foreknowledge and free will, but [Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther) argued against randomness and took the position that God's omniscience renders human actions unavoidable and determined.[^n18] In the 13th century, [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas) viewed randomness not as the result of a single cause, but of several causes coming together by chance. While he believed in the existence of randomness, he rejected it as an explanation of the end-directedness of nature, for he saw too many patterns in nature to have been obtained by chance.[^n19]

The Greeks and Romans had not noticed the magnitudes of the relative frequencies of the games of chance. For centuries, chance was discussed in Europe with no mathematical foundation and it was only in the 16th century that Italian mathematicians began to discuss the outcomes of games of chance as ratios.[^n20][^n21][^n22] In his 1565 *Liber de Lude Aleae* (a gambler's manual published after his death) [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145) wrote one of the first formal tracts to analyze the odds of winning at various games.[^n23]

## 17th–19th centuries {#17th–19th_centuries}

*Image: Pascal Pajou Louvre RF2981.jpg.* Statue of [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068), [Louvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musée_du_Louvre)

Around 1620 [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo) wrote a paper called *On a discovery concerning dice* that used an early probabilistic model to address specific questions.[^n24] In 1654, prompted by [Chevalier de Méré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalier_de_Méré)'s interest in gambling, [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068) corresponded with [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966), and much of the groundwork for probability theory was laid. [Pascal's Wager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal's_Wager) was noted for its early use of the concept of [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), and the first formal use of [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216). The work of Pascal and Fermat influenced [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281)'s work on the [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus), which in turn provided further momentum for the formal analysis of probability and randomness.

The first known suggestion for viewing randomness in terms of complexity was made by [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) in an obscure 17th-century document discovered after his death. Leibniz asked how one could know if a set of points on a piece of paper were selected at random (e.g. by splattering ink) or not. Given that for any set of finite points there is always a mathematical equation that can describe the points, (e.g. by [Lagrangian interpolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_interpolation)) the question focuses on the way the points are expressed mathematically. Leibniz viewed the points as random if the function describing them had to be extremely complex. Three centuries later, the same concept was formalized as [algorithmic randomness](wikipedia://en/4257548) by [A. N. Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._N._Kolmogorov) and [Gregory Chaitin](wikipedia://en/12308) as the minimal length of a computer program needed to describe a finite string as random.[^n25]

*[The Doctrine of Chances](wikipedia://en/161715)*, the first textbook on [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) was published in 1718 and the field continued to grow thereafter.[^n26] The [frequency theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_probability) approach to probability was first developed by [Robert Ellis](wikipedia://en/5881929) and [John Venn](wikipedia://en/251482) late in the 19th century.

*Image: Vouet, Simon - The Fortune Teller.jpg.* *The Fortune Teller* by [Vouet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vouet), 1617

While the mathematical elite was making progress in understanding randomness from the 17th to the 19th century, the public at large continued to rely on practices such as [fortune telling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_telling) in the hope of taming chance. Fortunes were told in a multitude of ways both in the Orient (where fortune telling was later termed an addiction) and in Europe by Romanis and others.[^n27][^n28] English practices such as the reading of eggs dropped in a glass were exported to Puritan communities in North America.[^n29]

"I know of scarcely anything so apt to impress the imagination as the wonderful form of cosmic order expressed by the "Law of Frequency of Error." The law would have been personified by the Greeks and deified, if they had known of it. It reigns with serenity and in complete self-effacement amidst the wildest confusion. The huger the mob, and the greater the apparent anarchy, the more perfect is its sway. It is the supreme law of Unreason. Whenever a large sample of chaotic elements are taken in hand and marshalled in the order of their magnitude, an unsuspected and most beautiful form of regularity proves to have been latent all along. The tops of the marshalled row form a flowing curve of invariable proportions; and each element, as it is sorted into place, finds, as it were, a pre-ordained niche, accurately adapted to fit it."

The term [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891), which is now a key element in the study of randomness, was coined by [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius) in 1865 as he studied heat engines in the context of the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/133017). Clausius was the first to state "entropy always increases".[^n30]

From the time of [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) until about 1890, it was generally believed that if one knows the initial state of a system with great accuracy, and if all the forces acting on the system can be formulated with equal accuracy, it would be possible, in principle, to make predictions of the state of the universe for an infinitely long time. The limits to such predictions in physical systems became clear as early as 1893 when [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) showed that in the [three-body problem](wikipedia://en/1208420) in astronomy, small changes to the initial state could result in large changes in trajectories during the numerical integration of the equations.[^n31]

During the 19th century, as probability theory was formalized and better understood, the attitude towards "randomness as nuisance" began to be questioned. [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe) wrote:

> The tissue of the world is built from necessities and randomness; the intellect of men places itself between both and can control them; it considers the necessity and the reason of its existence; it knows how randomness can be managed, controlled, and used.

The words of Goethe proved prophetic, when in the 20th century [randomized algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_algorithms) were discovered as powerful tools.[^n32] By the end of the 19th century, Newton's model of a mechanical universe was fading away as the statistical view of the collision of molecules in gases was studied by [Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) and [Boltzmann](wikipedia://en/544255).[^n33] Boltzmann's equation *S* = *k* log<sub>*e*</sub> *W* (inscribed on his tombstone) first related [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) with [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860).

## 20th century {#20th_century}

*Image: Antony Gormley Quantum Cloud 2000.jpg.* [Antony Gormley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_Gormley)'s *[Quantum Cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Cloud)* sculpture in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London) was designed by a computer using a [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451) algorithm.

During the 20th century, the five main [interpretations of probability](wikipedia://en/23538) theory (e.g., *classical*, *logical*, *frequency*, *propensity* and *subjective*) became better understood, were discussed, compared and contrasted.[^n34] A significant number of application areas were developed in this century, from finance to physics. In 1900 [Louis Bachelier](wikipedia://en/1062753) applied [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) to evaluate [stock options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_options), effectively launching the fields of [financial mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_mathematics) and [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes).

[Émile Borel](wikipedia://en/356161) was one of the first mathematicians to formally address randomness in 1909, and introduced [normal numbers](wikipedia://en/204277).[^n35] In 1919 [Richard von Mises](wikipedia://en/821517) gave the first definition of algorithmic randomness via the [impossibility of a gambling system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impossibility_of_a_gambling_system). He advanced the frequency theory of randomness in terms of what he called *the collective*, i.e. a [random sequence](wikipedia://en/48256).[^n36] Von Mises regarded the randomness of a collective as an empirical law, established by experience. He related the "disorder" or randomness of a collective to the lack of success of attempted gambling systems. This approach led him to suggest a definition of randomness that was later refined and made mathematically rigorous by [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731) by using [computable functions](wikipedia://en/1139338) in 1940.[^n37] Von Mises likened the principle of the *impossibility of a gambling system* to the principle of the [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088), a law that cannot be proven, but has held true in repeated experiments.[^n38]

Von Mises never totally formalized his rules for sub-sequence selection, but in his 1940 paper "On the concept of random sequence", [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731) suggested that the functions used for place settings in the formalism of von Mises be computable functions rather than arbitrary functions of the initial segments of the sequence, appealing to the [Church–Turing thesis](wikipedia://en/6854) on effectiveness.[^n39][^n40]

The advent of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) in the early 20th century and the formulation of the [Heisenberg uncertainty principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisenberg_uncertainty_principle) in 1927 saw the end to the Newtonian mindset among physicists regarding the *determinacy of nature*. In quantum mechanics, there is not even a way to consider all observable elements in a system as random variables *at once*, since many observables do not commute.[^n41]

*Image: Cafe Central Wenen.jpg.* [Café Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Café_Central), one of the early meeting places of the [Vienna circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_circle)

By the early 1940s, the frequency theory approach to probability was well accepted within the [Vienna circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_circle), but in the 1950s [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) proposed the [propensity theory](wikipedia://en/11351089).[^n42][^n43] Given that the frequency approach cannot deal with "a single toss" of a coin, and can only address large ensembles or collectives, the single-case probabilities were treated as propensities or chances. The concept of propensity was also driven by the desire to handle single-case probability settings in quantum mechanics, e.g. the probability of decay of a specific atom at a specific moment. In more general terms, the frequency approach can not deal with the probability of the death of a *specific person* given that the death can not be repeated multiple times for that person. [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) echoed the same sentiment as Aristotle in viewing randomness as subordinate to order when he wrote that "the concept of chance is not opposed to the concept of law" in nature, provided one considers the laws of chance.[^n44]

[Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693)'s development of [Information theory](wikipedia://en/14773) in 1948 gave rise to the *[entropy](wikipedia://en/15445) view* of randomness. In this view, randomness is the opposite of *determinism* in a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895). Hence if a stochastic system has entropy zero it has no randomness and any increase in entropy increases randomness. Shannon's formulation defaults to [Boltzmann](wikipedia://en/544255)'s 19th century formulation of entropy in case all probabilities are equal.[^n45][^n46] Entropy is now widely used in diverse fields of science from [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) to [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry).[^n47]

[Martingales](wikipedia://en/274536) for the study of chance and betting strategies were introduced by [Paul Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462) in the 1930s and were formalized by [Joseph L. Doob](wikipedia://en/1547599) in the 1950s.[^n48] The application of [random walk hypothesis](wikipedia://en/2009882) in [financial theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_theory) was first proposed by [Maurice Kendall](wikipedia://en/589136) in 1953.[^n49] It was later promoted by [Eugene Fama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Fama) and [Burton Malkiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burton_Malkiel).

Random strings were first studied in the 1960s by [A. N. Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._N._Kolmogorov) (who had provided the first axiomatic definition of probability theory in 1933),[^n50] [Chaitin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaitin) and [Martin-Löf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin-Löf).[^n51] The [algorithmic randomness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_randomness) of a string was defined as the minimum size of a program (e.g. in bits) executed on a [universal computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_computer) that yields the string. [Chaitin's Omega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaitin's_Omega) number later related randomness and the halting probability for programs.[^n52]

In 1964, [Benoît Mandelbrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benoît_Mandelbrot) suggested that most statistical models approached only a first stage of dealing with indeterminism, and that they ignored many aspects of real world turbulence.[^n53][^n54] In his 1997 he defined *[seven states of randomness](wikipedia://en/29329597)* ranging from "mild to wild", with traditional randomness being at the mild end of the scale.[^n55]

Despite mathematical advances, reliance on other methods of dealing with chance, such as fortune telling and [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) continued in the 20th century. The government of [Myanmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myanmar) reportedly shaped 20th century economic policy based on fortune telling and planned the move of the capital of the country based on the advice of astrologers.[^n56][^n57][^n58] [White House Chief of Staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_House_Chief_of_Staff) [Donald Regan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Regan) criticized the involvement of astrologer [Joan Quigley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Quigley) in decisions made during [Ronald Reagan's presidency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_of_Ronald_Reagan) in the 1980s.[^n59][^n60][^n61] Quigley claims to have been the White House astrologer for seven years.[^n62]

During the 20th century, limits in dealing with randomness were better understood. The best-known example of both theoretical and operational limits on predictability is weather forecasting, simply because models have been used in the field since the 1950s. Predictions of weather and climate are necessarily uncertain. Observations of weather and climate are uncertain and incomplete, and the models into which the data are fed are uncertain.[^n63] In 1961, [Edward Lorenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lorenz) noticed that a very small change to the initial data submitted to a computer program for weather simulation could result in a completely different weather scenario. This later became known as the [butterfly effect](wikipedia://en/4024), often paraphrased as the question: "*Does the flap of a butterfly’s wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?*".[^n64] A key example of serious practical limits on predictability is in geology, where the ability to [predict earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake_prediction) either on an individual or on a statistical basis remains a remote prospect.[^n65]

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, [computer scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) began to realize that the *deliberate* introduction of randomness into computations can be an effective tool for designing better algorithms. In some cases, such [randomized algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_algorithms) outperform the best deterministic methods.[^n32]
