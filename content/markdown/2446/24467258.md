# Precision (statistics)

Accuracy and precision

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Precision"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **precision matrix** or **concentration matrix** is the [matrix inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inverse) of the [covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/191752) or dispersion matrix, $P = \Sigma^{-1}$.[^o2][^o3][^o4] For [univariate distributions](wikipedia://en/9253219), the precision matrix degenerates into a [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) **precision**, defined as the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344), $p = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}$.[^o5]

:::

Other [summary statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_statistic) of [statistical dispersion](wikipedia://en/23773247) also called *precision* (or *imprecision*[^o6][^o7]) include the reciprocal of the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), $p = \frac{1}{\sigma}$;[^o4] the standard deviation itself and the [relative standard deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_standard_deviation);[^o8] as well as the [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520)[^o9] and the [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) (or its half-width, the [margin of error](wikipedia://en/277379)).[^o10]

## Usage {#Usage}

One particular use of the precision matrix is in the context of [Bayesian analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_analysis) of the [multivariate normal distribution](wikipedia://en/50347): for example, Bernardo & Smith prefer to parameterise the multivariate normal distribution in terms of the precision matrix, rather than the covariance matrix, because of certain simplifications that then arise.[^o12] For instance, if both the [prior](wikipedia://en/472877) and the [likelihood](wikipedia://en/44968) have [Gaussian](wikipedia://en/245552) form, and the precision matrix of both of these exist (because their covariance matrix is full rank and thus invertible), then the precision matrix of the [posterior](wikipedia://en/357672) will simply be the sum of the precision matrices of the prior and the likelihood.

As the inverse of a [Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/189682), the precision matrix of real-valued random variables, if it exists, is [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) and symmetrical.

Another reason the precision matrix may be useful is that if two dimensions $i$ and $j$ of a multivariate normal are [conditionally independent](wikipedia://en/801135), then the $ij$ and $ji$ elements of the precision matrix are $0$. This means that precision matrices tend to be sparse when many of the dimensions are conditionally independent, which can lead to computational efficiencies when working with them. It also means that precision matrices are closely related to the idea of [partial correlation](wikipedia://en/8771567).

The precision matrix plays a central role in [generalized least squares](wikipedia://en/4504135), compared to [ordinary least squares](wikipedia://en/1651906), where $P$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), and to [weighted least squares](wikipedia://en/2407860), where $P$ is diagonal (the [weight matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight_matrix)).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The term *precision* in this sense ("mensura praecisionis observationum") first appeared in the works of [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) (1809) "*Theoria motus corporum coelestium in sectionibus conicis solem ambientium*" (page 212). Gauss's definition differs from the modern one by a factor of $\sqrt2$. He writes, for the density function of a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) with precision $h$ (reciprocal of standard deviation),

$$\varphi\Delta = \frac h {\sqrt \pi}\, e^{-hh\Delta\Delta} .$$

where $h h = h^2$ (see [modern exponential notation](wikipedia://en/99491#Modern_exponential_notation)). Later Whittaker & Robinson (1924) "*Calculus of observations*" called this quantity *the modulus (of precision)*, but this term has dropped out of use.[^o14]
