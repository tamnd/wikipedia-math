# Polar sine

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polar sine"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **polar sine** generalizes the [sine function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_function) of [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) to the [vertex angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_angle) of a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471). It is denoted by **psin**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### $n$ vectors in $n$-dimensional space {#vectors_in_-dimensional_space}

*Image: 3dvol.svg.* The interpretations of [3D](wikipedia://en/3054853) [volumes](wikipedia://en/32498) for **left:** a [parallelepiped](wikipedia://en/23975) (Ω in polar sine definition) and **right:** a [cuboid](wikipedia://en/156802) (Π in definition). The interpretation is similar in higher dimensions.

Let $\mathbf{v}_{1}, ..., \mathbf{v}_{n}$ ($n \ge 1$) be non-zero [Euclidean vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) in [$n$-dimensional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-dimensional_space) ($\mathbf{R}^{n}$) that are directed from a [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a [parallelotope](wikipedia://en/23975#Parallelotope), forming the edges of the parallelotope. The polar sine of the vertex angle is:

$$\operatorname{psin}(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n) = \frac{\Omega}{\Pi},$$

where the numerator is the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468)

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega & = \det\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} v_{11} & v_{21} & \cdots & v_{n1} \\ v_{12} & v_{22} & \cdots & v_{n2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ v_{1n} & v_{2n} & \cdots & v_{nn} \\ \end{vmatrix} \end{aligned}\,,$$

which equals the [signed hypervolume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_volume) of the parallelotope with vector edges[^o5]

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_1 &= (v_{11}, v_{12}, \dots, v_{1n})^T \\ \mathbf{v}_2 &= (v_{21}, v_{22}, \dots, v_{2n})^T \\ & \,\,\,\vdots \\ \mathbf{v}_n &= (v_{n1}, v_{n2}, \dots, v_{nn})^T\,, \\ \end{aligned}$$

and where the denominator is the $n$-fold [product](wikipedia://en/20845#Capital_Pi_notation)

$$\Pi = \prod_{i=1}^n \|\mathbf{v}_i\|$$

of the [magnitudes](wikipedia://en/990534) of the vectors, which equals the hypervolume of the $n$-dimensional [hyperrectangle](wikipedia://en/1688550) with edges equal to the magnitudes of the vectors $||\mathbf{v}_{1}||$, $||\mathbf{v}_{2}||$, ... $||\mathbf{v}_{n}||$ rather than the vectors themselves. Also see Ericksson.[^o6]

The parallelotope is like a "squashed hyperrectangle", so it has less hypervolume than the hyperrectangle, meaning (see image for the 3d case):

$$|\Omega| \leq \Pi \implies \frac{|\Omega|}{\Pi} \leq 1 \implies -1 \leq \operatorname{psin}(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n) \leq 1\,,$$

as for the ordinary sine, with either bound being reached only in the case that all vectors are mutually [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal).

In the case $n = 2$, the polar sine is the ordinary [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) of the angle that is swept out if the first vector is rotated counterclockwise to the position of the second vector.

### In higher dimensions {#In_higher_dimensions}

A non-negative version of the polar sine that works in any $m$-dimensional space can be defined using the [Gram determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram_determinant). It is a ratio where the denominator is as described above. The numerator is

$$|\Omega| = \sqrt{\det \left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix}^T \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix} \right)} \,,$$

where the superscript T indicates [matrix transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_transposition). This can be nonzero only if $m \ge n$. In the case $m = n$, this is equivalent to the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the definition given previously. In the degenerate case $m < n$, the determinant will be of a [singular](wikipedia://en/245528) $n \times n$ matrix, giving $\Omega = 0$ and $\operatorname{psin} = 0$, because it is not possible to have $n$ linearly independent vectors in $m$-dimensional space when $m < n$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Interchange of vectors {#Interchange_of_vectors}

The polar sine changes sign whenever two vectors are interchanged, due to the antisymmetry of [row-exchanging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_operations) in the determinant; however, its absolute value will remain unchanged.

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega & = \det\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_i & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_j & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix} \\ & = -\!\det\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_j & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_i & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix} \\ & = -\Omega \end{aligned}$$

### Invariance under scalar multiplication of vectors {#Invariance_under_scalar_multiplication_of_vectors}

The polar sine does not change if all of the vectors $\mathbf{v}_{1}, ..., \mathbf{v}_{n}$ are [scalar-multiplied](wikipedia://en/244373) by positive constants $c_{i}$, due to [factorization](wikipedia://en/82341)

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{psin}(c_1 \mathbf{v}_1,\dots, c_n \mathbf{v}_n) & = \frac{\det\begin{bmatrix}c_1\mathbf{v}_1 & c_2\mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & c_n\mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix}}{\prod_{i=1}^n \|c_i \mathbf{v}_i\|} \\[6pt] & = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n c_i}{\prod_{i=1}^n |c_i|} \cdot \frac{\det\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_1 & \mathbf{v}_2 & \cdots & \mathbf{v}_n \end{bmatrix}}{\prod_{i=1}^n \|\mathbf{v}_i\|} \\[6pt] & = \operatorname{psin}(\mathbf{v}_1,\dots, \mathbf{v}_n). \end{aligned}$$

If an [odd number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) of these constants are instead negative, then the sign of the polar sine will change; however, its absolute value will remain unchanged.

### Vanishes with linear dependencies {#Vanishes_with_linear_dependencies}

If the vectors are not [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent), the polar sine will be zero. This will always be so in the [degenerate case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_case) that the number of dimensions $m$ is strictly less than the number of vectors $n$.

### Relationship to pairwise cosines {#Relationship_to_pairwise_cosines}

The [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) of the angle between two non-zero vectors is given by

$$\cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2}{\|\mathbf{v}_1\| \|\mathbf{v}_2\|}\,$$

using the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093). Comparison of this expression to the definition of the absolute value of the polar sine as given above gives:

$$\left|\operatorname{psin}(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_n)\right|^2 = \det\!\left[\begin{matrix} 1 & \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) & \cdots & \cos(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_n) \\ \cos(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_1) & 1 & \cdots & \cos(\mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \cos(\mathbf{v}_n, \mathbf{v}_1) & \cos(\mathbf{v}_n, \mathbf{v}_2) & \cdots & 1 \\ \end{matrix}\right].$$

In particular, for $n = 2$, this is equivalent to

$$\sin^2(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2) = 1 - \cos^2(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2)\,,$$

which is the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).
