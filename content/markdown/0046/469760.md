# Metric map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Metric map"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), a **metric map** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) between metric spaces that does not increase any distance. These maps are the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) in the [category of metric spaces](wikipedia://en/535617), **Met**. Such functions are always [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122). They are also called [Lipschitz functions](wikipedia://en/51731) with [Lipschitz constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_constant) 1, **nonexpansive maps**, **nonexpanding maps**, **weak contractions**, or **short maps**.

:::

Specifically, suppose that $X$ and $Y$ are metric spaces and $f$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) from $X$ to $Y$. Thus we have a metric map when, for any points $x$ and $y$ in $X$,

$$d_{Y}(f(x),f(y)) \leq d_{X}(x,y) . \!$$

Here $d_X$ and $d_Y$ denote the metrics on $X$ and $Y$ respectively.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Consider the metric space $[0,1/2]$ with the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric). Then the function $f(x)=x^2$ is a metric map, since for $x\ne y$, $|f(x)-f(y)|=|x+y||x-y|<|x-y|$. In this example the Lipschitz constant is 1, that implies a metric map.

:::

## Category of metric maps {#Category_of_metric_maps}

The [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two metric maps is another metric map, and the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) $\mathrm{id}_M\colon M \rightarrow M$ on a metric space $M$ is a metric map, which is also the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for function composition. Thus metric spaces together with metric maps form a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) **[Met](wikipedia://en/535617)**. **Met** is a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) of the category of metric spaces and Lipschitz functions. A map between metric spaces is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) if and only if it is a [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) metric map whose [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) is also a metric map. Thus the [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) in **Met** are precisely the isometries.

## Multivalued version {#Multivalued_version}

A mapping $T\colon X\to \mathcal{N}(X)$ from a metric space $X$ to the family of nonempty subsets of $X$ is said to be Lipschitz if there exists $L\geq 0$ such that

$$H(Tx,Ty)\leq L d(x,y),$$

for all $x,y\in X$, where $H$ is the [Hausdorff distance](wikipedia://en/429296). When $L=1$, $T$ is called *nonexpansive*, and when $L<1$, $T$ is called a [contraction](wikipedia://en/6239).
