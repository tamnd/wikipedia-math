# D'Alembert's paradox

*Image: Maurice Quentin de La Tour - Portrait de Jean Le Rond d'Alembert.jpg.* Jean le Rond d'Alembert (1717-1783)

*Image: Drag coefficient on a sphere vs. Reynolds number - main trends.svg.* From [experiments](wikipedia://en/59861) it is known that there is always – except in case of [superfluidity](wikipedia://en/35607283) – a drag force for a body placed in a steady fluid onflow. The figure shows the [drag coefficient](wikipedia://en/172291) *C*<sub>d</sub> for a sphere as a function of [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856) *Re*, as obtained from laboratory experiments. The dark line is for a sphere with a smooth surface, while the lighter line is for the case of a rough surface. The numbers along the line indicate several flow regimes and associated changes in the drag coefficient: •2: attached flow ([Stokes flow](wikipedia://en/2047298)) and [steady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady_flow) [separated flow](wikipedia://en/4871070), •3: separated unsteady flow, having a [laminar flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminar_flow) [boundary layer](wikipedia://en/224301) upstream of the separation, and producing a [vortex street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex_street), •4: separated unsteady flow with a laminar boundary layer at the upstream side, before flow separation, with downstream of the sphere a chaotic [turbulent](wikipedia://en/154664) [wake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_%28physics%29), •5: post-critical separated flow, with a turbulent boundary layer.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="D'Alembert's paradox"}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), **d'Alembert's paradox** (or the **hydrodynamic paradox**) is a paradox discovered in 1752 by French mathematician [Jean le Rond d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d'Alembert).[^o2] D'Alembert proved that – for [incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477) and [inviscid](wikipedia://en/5877457) [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) – the [drag force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29) is zero on a body moving with constant [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) relative to (and simultaneously through) the [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid).[^o3] Zero drag is in direct contradiction to the observation of substantial drag on bodies moving relative to and at the same time through a fluid, such as air and water; especially at high velocities corresponding with high [Reynolds numbers](wikipedia://en/23868856). It is a particular example of the [reversibility paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversibility_paradox).[^o4]

:::

D’Alembert, working on a 1749 Prize Problem of the [Berlin Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences) on flow drag, concluded: It seems to me that the theory (potential flow), developed in all possible rigor, gives, at least in several cases, a strictly vanishing resistance, a singular paradox which I leave to future Geometers \[i.e. mathematicians - the two terms were used interchangeably at that time\] to elucidate. A [physical paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_paradox) indicates flaws in the theory.

Fluid mechanics was thus discredited by engineers from the start, which resulted in an unfortunate split – between the field of [hydraulics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulics), observing phenomena which could not be explained, and theoretical [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988) explaining phenomena which could not be observed – in the words of the Chemistry Nobel Laureate [Sir Cyril Hinshelwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_Norman_Hinshelwood).[^o5]

According to [scientific consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus), the occurrence of the paradox is due to the neglected effects of [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754). In conjunction with scientific experiments, there were huge advances in the theory of viscous fluid friction during the 19th century. With respect to the paradox, this culminated in the discovery and description of thin [boundary layers](wikipedia://en/224301) by [Ludwig Prandtl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Prandtl) in 1904. Even at very high Reynolds numbers, the thin boundary layers remain as a result of viscous forces. These viscous forces cause [friction drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_friction) on streamlined objects, and for [bluff bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluff_body) the additional result is [flow separation](wikipedia://en/4871070) and a low-pressure [wake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_%28physics%29) behind the object, leading to [form drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_drag).[^o6][^o7][^o8][^o9]

The general view in the fluid mechanics community is that, from a practical point of view, the paradox is solved along the lines suggested by Prandtl.[^o6][^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11] A formal mathematical proof is lacking, and difficult to provide, as in so many other fluid-flow problems involving the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395) (which are used to describe viscous flow).

## Viscous friction: Saint-Venant, Navier and Stokes {#Viscous_friction:_Saint-Venant,_Navier_and_Stokes}

First steps towards solving the paradox were made by [Adhémar Barré de Saint-Venant](wikipedia://en/5878555), who modelled [viscous](wikipedia://en/18963754) fluid friction. Saint-Venant states in 1847:[^o13]

Soon after, in 1851, [George Gabriel Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gabriel_Stokes) calculated the drag on a sphere in [Stokes flow](wikipedia://en/2047298), known as [Stokes' law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes'_law).[^o14] Stokes flow is the low Reynolds-number limit of the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395) describing the motion of a viscous liquid.[^o15]

However, when the flow problem is put into a [non-dimensional form](wikipedia://en/8267), the viscous Navier–Stokes equations converge for increasing Reynolds numbers towards the inviscid [Euler equations](wikipedia://en/396022), suggesting that the flow should converge towards the inviscid solutions of [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) theory – having the zero drag of the d'Alembert paradox. Of this, there is no evidence found in experimental measurements of drag and flow visualisations.[^o16] In the second half of the 19th century this again raised questions concerning the applicability of fluid mechanics.

## Inviscid separated flow: Kirchhoff and Rayleigh {#Inviscid_separated_flow:_Kirchhoff_and_Rayleigh}

*Image: Cavity flow plate.svg.* Steady and separated incompressible potential flow around a plate in two dimensions,[^o18] with a constant pressure along the two free streamlines separating from the plate edges.

In the second half of the 19th century, focus shifted again towards using [inviscid flow](wikipedia://en/5877457) theory for the description of fluid drag—assuming that viscosity becomes less important at high Reynolds numbers. The model proposed by [Kirchhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff)[^o19] and [Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Strutt,_3rd_Baron_Rayleigh)[^o20] was based on the free-streamline theory of [Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_von_Helmholtz)[^o21] and consists of a steady [wake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_%28physics%29) behind the body. Assumptions applied to the wake region include: flow velocities equal to the body velocity, and a constant pressure. This wake region is separated from the potential flow outside the body and wake by [vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex) sheets with discontinuous jumps in the [tangential](wikipedia://en/31482) velocity across the interface.[^o22][^o23] In order to have a non-zero drag on the body, the wake region must extend to infinity. This condition is indeed fulfilled for the Kirchhoff flow perpendicular to a plate. The theory correctly states the drag force to be proportional to the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of the velocity.[^o24] In first instance, the theory could only be applied to flows separating at sharp edges. Later, in 1907, it was extended by [Levi-Civita](wikipedia://en/499180) to flows separating from a smooth curved boundary.[^o25]

It was readily known that such steady flows are not stable, since the vortex sheets develop so-called [Kelvin–Helmholtz instabilities](wikipedia://en/567946).[^o23] But this steady-flow model was studied further in the hope it still could give a reasonable estimate of drag. Rayleigh asks *"... whether the calculations of resistance are materially affected by this circumstance as the pressures experienced must be nearly independent of what happens at some distance in the rear of the obstacle, where the instability would first begin to manifest itself."*[^o20]

However, fundamental objections arose against this approach: [Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin) observed that if a plate is moving with constant velocity through the fluid (at rest far from the plate, except for the wake) the velocity in the wake is equal to that of the plate. The infinite extent of the wake—widening with the distance from the plate, as obtained from the theory—results in an infinite kinetic energy in the wake, which must be rejected on physical grounds.[^o24][^o26] Moreover, the observed pressure differences between front and back of the plate, and resulting drag forces, are much larger than predicted: for a flat plate perpendicular to the flow the predicted [drag coefficient](wikipedia://en/172291) is *C*<sub>D</sub>=0.88, while in experiments *C*<sub>D</sub>=2.0 is found. This is mainly due to suction at the wake side of the plate, induced by the unsteady flow in the real wake (as opposed to the theory which assumes a constant flow velocity equal to the plate's velocity).[^o27]

So, this theory is found to be unsatisfactory as an explanation of drag on a body moving through a fluid. Although it can be applied to so-called [cavity flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavity_flow) where, instead of a wake filled with fluid, a vacuum cavity is assumed to exist behind the body.[^o23][^o24][^o28]

## Thin boundary layers: Prandtl {#Thin_boundary_layers:_Prandtl}

*Image: Flow pressure with friction.svg.* Pressure distribution for the flow around a circular cylinder. The dashed blue line is the pressure distribution according to [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) theory, resulting in d'Alembert's paradox. The solid blue line is the mean pressure distribution as found in experiments at high [Reynolds numbers](wikipedia://en/23868856). The pressure is the radial distance from the cylinder surface; a positive pressure (overpressure) is inside the cylinder, towards the centre, while a negative pressure (underpressure) is drawn outside the cylinder.

The German physicist [Ludwig Prandtl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Prandtl) suggested in 1904 that the effects of a thin viscous [boundary layer](wikipedia://en/224301) possibly could be the source of substantial drag.[^o30] Prandtl put forward the idea that, at high velocities and high Reynolds numbers, a [no-slip boundary condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-slip_boundary_condition) causes a strong variation of the flow speeds over a thin layer near the wall of the body. This leads to the generation of [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010) and viscous [dissipation](wikipedia://en/164572) of [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) in the boundary layer. The energy dissipation, which is lacking in the inviscid theories, results for bluff bodies in [separation](wikipedia://en/4871070) of the flow. The low pressure in the [wake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_%28physics%29) region causes [form drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_drag), and this can be larger than the friction drag due to the viscous [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) at the wall.[^o16]

Evidence that Prandtl's scenario occurs for bluff bodies in flows of high Reynolds numbers can be seen in impulsively started flows around a cylinder. Initially the flow resembles potential flow, after which the flow separates near the rear [stagnation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagnation_point). Thereafter, the separation points move upstream, resulting in a low-pressure region of separated flow.[^o16]

Prandtl made the hypothesis that the viscous effects are important in thin layers – called boundary layers – adjacent to solid boundaries, and that [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) has no role of importance outside. The [boundary-layer thickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary-layer_thickness) becomes smaller when the viscosity reduces. The full problem of viscous flow, described by the non-linear [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395), is in general not mathematically solvable. However, using his hypothesis (and backed up by experiments) Prandtl was able to derive an approximate model for the flow inside the boundary layer, called *boundary-layer theory*; while the flow outside the boundary layer could be treated using [inviscid flow](wikipedia://en/5877457) theory. Boundary-layer theory is amenable to the [method of matched asymptotic expansions](wikipedia://en/4265892) for deriving approximate solutions. In the simplest case of a flat plate parallel to the incoming flow, boundary-layer theory results in (friction) drag, whereas all inviscid flow theories will predict zero drag. Importantly for [aeronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeronautics), Prandtl's theory can be applied directly to streamlined bodies like [airfoils](wikipedia://en/290053) where, in addition to surface-friction drag, there is also form drag. Form drag is due to the effect of the boundary layer and thin wake on the [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) distribution around the airfoil.[^o8][^o31]

## Open questions {#Open_questions}

To verify, as Prandtl suggested, that a vanishingly small cause (vanishingly small viscosity for increasing Reynolds number) has a large effect – substantial drag — may be very difficult.

The mathematician [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916) in the opening chapter of his book *Hydrodynamics* from 1950,[^o34] addresses a number of paradoxes of fluid mechanics (including d'Alembert's paradox) and expresses a clear doubt in their official resolutions:

"*Moreover, I think that to attribute them all to the neglect of viscosity is an unwarranted oversimplification The root lies deeper, in lack of precisely that deductive rigor whose importance is so commonly minimized by physicists and engineers.*"[^o35]

In particular, on d'Alembert's paradox, he considers another possible route to the creation of drag: instability of the potential flow solutions to the [Euler equations](wikipedia://en/396022). Birkhoff states:

"*In any case, the preceding paragraphs make it clear that the theory of non-viscous flows is incomplete. Indeed, the reasoning leading to the concept of a "steady flow" is inconclusive; there is no rigorous justification for the elimination of time as an independent variable. Thus though Dirichlet flows (potential solutions) and other steady flows are mathematically possible, there is no reason to suppose that any steady flow is stable.*"[^o36]

In his 1951 review[^o37] of Birkhoff's book, the mathematician [James J. Stoker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_J._Stoker) sharply criticizes the first chapter of the book:

"*The reviewer found it difficult to understand for what class of readers the first chapter was written. For readers that are acquainted with hydrodynamics the majority of the cases cited as paradoxes belong either to the category of mistakes long since rectified, or in the category of discrepancies between theory and experiments the reasons for which are also well understood. On the other hand, the uninitiated would be very likely to get the wrong ideas about some of the important and useful achievements in hydrodynamics from reading this chapter.*"

In the second and revised edition of Birkhoff's *Hydrodynamics* in 1960, the above two statements no longer appear.[^o38]

The importance and usefulness of the achievements, made on the subject of the d'Alembert paradox, are reviewed by [Keith Stewartson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Stewartson) thirty years later. His long 1981 survey article starts with:[^o10]

"*Since classical inviscid theory leads to the patently absurd conclusion that the resistance experienced by a rigid body moving through a fluid with uniform velocity is zero, great efforts have been made during the last hundred or so years to propose alternate theories and to explain how a vanishingly small frictional force in the fluid can nevertheless have a significant effect on the flow properties. The methods used are a combination of experimental observation, computation often on a very large scale, and analysis of the structure of the asymptotic form of the solution as the friction tends to zero. This three-pronged attack has achieved considerable success, especially during the last ten years, so that now the paradox may be regarded as largely resolved.*"

For many paradoxes in physics, their resolution often lies in transcending the available theory.[^o39] In the case of d'Alembert's paradox, the essential mechanism for its resolution was provided by Prandtl through the discovery and modelling of thin viscous [boundary layers](wikipedia://en/224301) – which are non-vanishing at high [Reynolds numbers](wikipedia://en/23868856).[^o30]

## Proof of zero drag in steady potential flow {#Proof_of_zero_drag_in_steady_potential_flow}

*Image: Potential cylinder.svg.* [Streamlines](wikipedia://en/166086) for the potential flow around a [circular](wikipedia://en/6220) cylinder in a uniform onflow.

### Potential flow {#Potential_flow}

The three main assumptions in the derivation of d'Alembert's paradox is that the [steady flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady_flow) is [incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477), [inviscid](wikipedia://en/18963754) and [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational).[^o43] An inviscid fluid is described by the [Euler equations](wikipedia://en/396022), which together with the other two conditions read

$$\begin{aligned} & \mathbf{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 && \text{(incompressibility)} \\ & \mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{u}=0 && \text{(irrotational)} \\ & \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathbf{u} + \left(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{\nabla}\right) \mathbf{u} = - \frac{1}{\rho} \mathbf{\nabla} p && \text{(Euler equation)} \end{aligned}$$

where ***u*** denotes the [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) of the fluid, *p* the [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure), *ρ* the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density), and **∇** is the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) operator.

We have the second term in the Euler equation as:

$$\left(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{\nabla}\right) \mathbf{u} = \tfrac12 \mathbf{\nabla} \left(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}\right) - \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{\nabla} \times \mathbf{u} = \tfrac12 \mathbf{\nabla} \left(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}\right) \qquad (1)$$

where the first equality is a [vector calculus identity](wikipedia://en/3114930) and the second equality uses that the flow is irrotational. Furthermore, for every irrotational flow, there exists a [velocity potential](wikipedia://en/2448955) *φ* such that **u** = ∇*φ*. Substituting this all in the equation for momentum conservation yields

$$\mathbf{\nabla} \left( \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12 \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} + \frac p\rho \right) = \mathbf{0}.$$

Thus, the quantity between brackets must be constant (any *t*-dependence can be eliminated by redefining *φ*). Assuming that the fluid is at rest at infinity and that the pressure is defined to be zero there, this constant is zero, and thus

$$\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12 \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} + \frac p\rho = 0, \qquad (2)$$

which is the [Bernoulli equation](wikipedia://en/64219) for unsteady potential flow.

### Zero drag {#Zero_drag}

Now, suppose that a body moves with constant velocity **v** through the fluid, which is at rest infinitely far away. Then the velocity field of the fluid has to follow the body, so it is of the form $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{v} t, 0)$, where **x** is the spatial coordinate vector, and thus:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \left( \mathbf{v} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} \right) \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}.$$

Since **u** = ∇*φ*, this can be integrated with respect to **x**:

$$\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t} = -\mathbf{v} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} \varphi + R(t) = -\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u} + R(t).$$

The force **F** that the fluid exerts on the body is given by the [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867)

$$\mathbf{F} = - \int_A p\, \mathbf{n}\; \mathrm{d} S$$

where *A* denotes the body surface and ***n*** the [normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector) on the body surface. But it follows from (2) that

$$p = - \rho \left( \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12 \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} \right) = \rho \left( \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u} - \tfrac12 \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} - R(t) \right),$$

thus

$$\mathbf{F} = - \int_A p\, \mathbf{n}\; \mathrm{d} S = \rho \int_A \left(\tfrac12 \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{u}\right) \mathbf{n}\; \mathrm{d} S,$$

with the contribution of *R*(*t*) to the integral being equal to zero.

At this point, it becomes more convenient to work in the [vector components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_component). The *k*th component of this equation reads

$$F_k = \rho \int_A \sum_i (\tfrac12 u_i^2 - u_i v_i) n_k \, \mathrm{d} S. \qquad (3)$$

Let *V* be the volume occupied by the fluid. The [divergence theorem](wikipedia://en/151864) says that

$$\frac12 \int_A \sum_i u_i^2 n_k \, \mathrm{d} S = - \frac12 \int_V \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k} \left( \sum_i u_i^2 \right) \,\mathrm{d} V.$$

The right-hand side is an integral over an infinite volume, so this needs some justification, which can be provided by appealing to potential theory to show that the velocity ***u*** must fall off as *r*<sup>−3</sup> – corresponding to a [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole) potential field in case of a three-dimensional body of finite extent – where *r* is the distance to the centre of the body. The integrand in the volume integral can be rewritten as follows:

$$\frac12 \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k} \left( \sum_i u_i^2 \right) = \sum_i u_i \frac{\partial u_k}{\partial x_i} = \sum_i \frac{\partial(u_iu_k)}{\partial x_i}$$

where first equality (1) and then the incompressibility of the flow are used. Substituting this back into the volume integral and another application of the divergence theorem again. This yields

$$- \frac12 \int_V \frac{\partial}{\partial x_k} \left( \sum_i u_i^2 \right) \,\mathrm{d} V = -\int_V \sum_i \frac{\partial(u_iu_k)}{\partial x_i} \,\mathrm{d} V = \int_A u_k \sum_i u_i n_i \,\mathrm{d} S.$$

Substituting this in (3), we find that

$$F_k = \rho \int_A \sum_i (u_k u_i n_i - v_i u_i n_k) \, \mathrm{d} S.$$

The fluid cannot penetrate the body and thus **n** · **u** = **n** · **v** on the body surface. So $\sum_i n_i\, v_i = \sum_i n_i\, u_i$ and

$$F_k = \rho \int_A \sum_i (u_k v_i n_i - v_i u_i n_k) \, \mathrm{d} S.$$

Finally, the drag is the force in the direction in which the body moves, so

$$\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf F = \sum_k v_k F_k = 0.$$

Hence the drag vanishes. This is d'Alembert's paradox.
