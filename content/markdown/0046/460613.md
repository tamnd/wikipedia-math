# Ring of integers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ring of integers"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **ring of integers** of an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) $K$ (also sometimes called the **number ring** corresponding to number field *$K$*)[^o2] is the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of all [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) contained in $K$.[^n1] An algebraic integer is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203): $x^n+c_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+c_0$.[^n2] This ring is often denoted by $O_K$ or $\mathcal O_K$. Since any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) belongs to $K$ and is an [integral element](wikipedia://en/9478630) of $K$, the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is always a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of $O_K$.

:::

The ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ is the simplest possible ring of integers. Namely, $\mathbb{Z}=O_{\mathbb{Q}}$ where $\mathbb{Q}$ is the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024).[^n3] And indeed, in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) the elements of $\mathbb{Z}$ are often called the "rational integers" because of this.

The next simplest example is the ring of [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, consisting of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) whose [real and imaginary parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_and_imaginary_parts) are integers. It is the ring of integers in the number field $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ of [Gaussian rationals](wikipedia://en/318052), consisting of complex numbers whose real and imaginary parts are rational numbers. Like the rational integers, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a [Euclidean domain](wikipedia://en/10376).

The ring of integers of an algebraic number field is the unique maximal [order](wikipedia://en/1114171) in the field. It is always a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132).[^n4]

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The ring of integers $O_{K}$ is a [finitely-generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely-generated_module) $\mathbb Z$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410). Indeed, it is a [free](wikipedia://en/326454) $\mathbb Z$-module, and thus has an **integral basis**, that is a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $b_{1},\,..., b_{n} \in O_{K}$ of the $\mathbb Q$-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $K$ such that each element $x$ in $O_{K}$ can be uniquely represented as

$$x=\sum_{i=1}^na_ib_i,$$

with $a_i \in \mathbb Z$.[^o9] The [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_of_a_free_module) $n$ of $O_{K}$ as a free $\mathbb Z$-module is equal to the [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) of $K$ over $\mathbb Q$.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Computational tool {#Computational_tool}

A useful tool for computing the integral closure of the ring of integers in an algebraic field $K / \mathbb Q$ is the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/3022963). If $K$ is of degree $n$ over $\mathbb Q$, and $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n \in \mathcal{O}_K$ form a basis of $K$ over $\mathbb Q$, set $d = \Delta_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n)$. Then, $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a [submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule) of the $\mathbb Z$-module spanned by $\alpha_1/d,\ldots,\alpha_n/d$.[^o13] <sup>pg. 33</sup> In fact, if $d$ is square-free, then $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$ forms an integral basis for $\mathcal{O}_K$.[^o13] <sup>pg. 35</sup>

### Cyclotomic extensions {#Cyclotomic_extensions}

If $p$ is a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666), $\zeta$ is a $p$th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) and $K = \mathbb Q(\zeta)$ is the corresponding [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462), then an integral basis of $\mathcal{O}_K=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ is given by $(1,\,\zeta,\,\zeta^{\,2},\,...,\,\zeta^{\,p-2})$.[^n5]

### Quadratic extensions {#Quadratic_extensions}

If $d$ is a [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525) and $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}\,)$ is the corresponding [quadratic field](wikipedia://en/454781), then $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a ring of [quadratic integers](wikipedia://en/21834785) and its integral basis is given by $\left(1, \frac{1 + \sqrt{d}}{2}\right)$ if $d \equiv 1 (\bmod 4)$ and by $(1, \sqrt{d})$ if $d \equiv 2,\,3\pmod{4}$.[^n6] This can be found by computing the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) of an arbitrary element $a + b\sqrt{d} \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ where $a,b \in \mathbb{Q}$.

:::

## Multiplicative structure {#Multiplicative_structure}

In a ring of integers, every element has a factorization into [irreducible elements](wikipedia://en/60693), but the ring need not have the property of [unique factorization](wikipedia://en/60546): for example, in the ring of integers $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, the element 6 has two essentially different factorizations into irreducibles:[^n4][^o20]

$$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}).$$

A ring of integers is always a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132), and so has unique factorization of [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) into [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928).[^n7]

The [units](wikipedia://en/289666) of a ring of integers $O_{K}$ form a [finitely generated abelian group](wikipedia://en/58527) by [Dirichlet's unit theorem](wikipedia://en/460227). The [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) consists of the [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) of $K$. A set of torsion-free generators is called a set of *[fundamental units](wikipedia://en/3995927)*.[^n8]

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

One defines the ring of integers of a [non-archimedean local field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-archimedean_local_field) $F$ as the set of all elements of $F$ with absolute value $\le\,1$; this is a ring because of the strong triangle inequality.[^n9] If $F$ is the completion of an algebraic number field, its ring of integers is the completion of the latter's ring of integers. The ring of integers of an algebraic number field may be characterised as the elements which are integers in every non-archimedean completion.[^n3]

For example, the [$p$-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer) $\mathbb Z_p$ are the ring of integers of the [$p$-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) $\mathbb Q_p$.

:::
