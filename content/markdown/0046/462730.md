# Inscribed angle

*Image: Inscribed Angle.png.* The inscribed angle β subtended by arc AB at point P located on the circumference of the circle.

*Image: Inscribed angles2.svg.* The inscribed angle $\theta$ circle. solid red Solid black solid lime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inscribed angle"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **inscribed angle** is the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) formed in the interior of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) when two [chords](wikipedia://en/297947) intersect on the circle. It can also be defined as the angle [subtended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtend) at a point on the circle by two given points on the circle.

:::

The **inscribed angle theorem**, sometimes called the **central angle theorem**, relates the [measure](wikipedia://en/1196#Measuring_angles) of an inscribed angle to that of the [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) intercepting the same [arc](wikipedia://en/5578920). Its oldest appearance is in Propositions 20–21 in Book 3 of [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107).

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

### Statement {#Statement}

*Image: ArcCapable.gif.* For fixed points $A$ and $B$, the set of points *M* in the plane, for which the angle $\angle AMB$ is equal to *α*, is an arc of a circle. The measure of $\angle AOB$, where $O$ is the center of the circle, is $2\alpha$.

The inscribed angle theorem states that an angle $\theta$ inscribed in a circle is half of the central angle $2\theta$ that [intercepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercepted_arc) the same [arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) on the circle. Therefore, the angle does not change as its [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) is moved to different positions on the same arc of the circle.

:::

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

#### Inscribed angles where one chord is a diameter {#Inscribed_angles_where_one_chord_is_a_diameter}

*Image: InscribedAngle 1ChordDiam.svg.* Case: One chord is a diameter

Let $O$ be the center of a circle, as in the diagram at right. Choose two points on the circle, and call them $V$ and $A$. Designate point $B$ to be [diametrically opposite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diametrically_opposite) point $V$. Draw chord $VB$, a diameter containing point $O$. Draw chord $VA$. Angle $\angle BVA$ is an inscribed angle that intercepts arc $AB$; denote it as $\psi$. Draw line $OA$. Angle $\angle BOA$ is a [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) that also intercepts arc $AB$; denote it as $\theta$.

Lines $OV$ and $OA$ are both [radii](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the circle, so they have equal lengths. Therefore, triangle $\triangle VOA$ is [isosceles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosceles), so angle $\angle BVA$ and angle $\angle VAO$ are equal.

Angles $\angle BOA$ and $\angle AOV$ are [supplementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_angle), summing to a [straight angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_angle) (180°), so angle $\angle AOV$ measures $180^{\circ} - \theta$.

The three angles of triangle $\triangle VOA$ [must sum to $180^{\circ}$](wikipedia://en/5407025):

$$(180^\circ - \theta) + \psi + \psi = 180^\circ.$$

Adding $\theta - 180^\circ$ to both sides yields

$$2\psi = \theta.$$

#### Inscribed angles with the center of the circle in their interior {#Inscribed_angles_with_the_center_of_the_circle_in_their_interior}

*Image: Circle-angles-21add-inscribed.svg.* Case: Center interior to angle solid blue solid green solid red

Given a circle whose center is point $O$, choose three points $V, C, D$ on the circle. Draw lines $VC$ and $VD$: angle $\angle DVC$ is an inscribed angle. Now draw line $OV$ and extend it past point $O$ so that it intersects the circle at point $E$. Angle $\angle DVC$ intercepts arc $DC$ on the circle.

Suppose this arc includes point $E$ within it. Point $E$ is diametrically opposite to point $V$. Angles $\angle DVE, \angle EVC$ are also inscribed angles, but both of these angles have one side which passes through the center of the circle, therefore the theorem from the above Part 1 can be applied to them.

Therefore,

$$\angle DVC = \angle DVE + \angle EVC.$$

then let

$$\begin{aligned} \psi_0 &= \angle DVC, \\ \psi_1 &= \angle DVE, \\ \psi_2 &= \angle EVC, \end{aligned}$$

so that

$$\psi_0 = \psi_1 + \psi_2. \qquad \qquad (1)$$

Draw lines $OC$ and $OD$. Angle $\angle DOC$ is a central angle, but so are angles $\angle DOE$ and $\angle EOC$, and

$$\angle DOC = \angle DOE + \angle EOC.$$

Let

$$\begin{aligned} \theta_0 &= \angle DOC, \\ \theta_1 &= \angle DOE, \\ \theta_2 &= \angle EOC, \end{aligned}$$

so that

$$\theta_0 = \theta_1 + \theta_2. \qquad \qquad (2)$$

From Part One we know that $\theta_1 = 2 \psi_1$ and that $\theta_2 = 2 \psi_2$. Combining these results with equation (2) yields

$$\theta_0 = 2 \psi_1 + 2 \psi_2 = 2(\psi_1 + \psi_2)$$

therefore, by equation (1),

$$\theta_0 = 2 \psi_0.$$

#### Inscribed angles with the center of the circle in their exterior {#Inscribed_angles_with_the_center_of_the_circle_in_their_exterior}

*Image: InscribedAngle CenterCircleExtV2.svg.* Case: Center exterior to angle solid orange solid green solid blue

The previous case can be extended to cover the case where the measure of the inscribed angle is the *difference* between two inscribed angles as discussed in the first part of this proof.

Given a circle whose center is point $O$, choose three points $V, C, D$ on the circle. Draw lines $VC$ and $VD$: angle $\angle DVC$ is an inscribed angle. Now draw line $OV$ and extend it past point $O$ so that it intersects the circle at point $E$. Angle $\angle DVC$ intercepts arc $DC$ on the circle.

Suppose this arc does not include point $E$ within it. Point $E$ is diametrically opposite to point $V$. Angles $\angle EVD, \angle EVC$ are also inscribed angles, but both of these angles have one side which passes through the center of the circle, therefore the theorem from the above Part 1 can be applied to them.

Therefore,

$$\angle DVC = \angle EVC - \angle EVD.$$

then let

$$\begin{aligned} \psi_0 &= \angle DVC, \\ \psi_1 &= \angle EVD, \\ \psi_2 &= \angle EVC, \end{aligned}$$

so that

$$\psi_0 = \psi_2 - \psi_1. \qquad \qquad (3)$$

Draw lines $OC$ and $OD$. Angle $\angle DOC$ is a central angle, but so are angles $\angle EOD$ and $\angle EOC$, and

$$\angle DOC = \angle EOC - \angle EOD.$$

Let

$$\begin{aligned} \theta_0 &= \angle DOC, \\ \theta_1 &= \angle EOD, \\ \theta_2 &= \angle EOC, \end{aligned}$$

so that

$$\theta_0 = \theta_2 - \theta_1. \qquad \qquad (4)$$

From Part One we know that $\theta_1 = 2 \psi_1$ and that $\theta_2 = 2 \psi_2$. Combining these results with equation (4) yields

$$\theta_0 = 2 \psi_2 - 2 \psi_1$$

therefore, by equation (3),

$$\theta_0 = 2 \psi_0.$$

*Image: Animated gif of proof of the inscribed angle theorem.gif.* Animated gif of proof of the inscribed angle theorem. The large triangle that is inscribed in the circle gets subdivided into three smaller triangles, all of which are isosceles because their upper two sides are radii of the circle. Inside each isosceles triangle the pair of base angles are equal to each other, and are half of 180° minus the apex angle at the circle's center. Adding up these isosceles base angles yields the theorem, namely that the inscribed angle, $\psi$, is half the central angle, $\theta$.

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary"}

### Corollary {#Corollary}

*Image: Inscribed angle theorem4.svg.* The angle $\theta$ between a chord and a tangent is half the arc belonging to the chord.

By a similar argument, the angle between a [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) and the [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) line at one of its intersection points equals half of the central angle subtended by the chord. See also [Tangent lines to circles](wikipedia://en/19491492).

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: cyclic\_quadrilateral\_supplementary\_angles\_visual\_proof.svg.* [Proof without words](wikipedia://en/18045517) using the inscribed angle theorem that opposite angles of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) are supplementary:2𝜃 + 2𝜙 = 360° ∴ 𝜃 + 𝜙 = 180°

The inscribed angle [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) is used in many proofs of elementary [Euclidean geometry of the plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry_of_the_plane). A special case of the theorem is [Thales's theorem](wikipedia://en/267471), which states that the angle subtended by a [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) is always 90°, i.e., a right angle. As a consequence of the theorem, opposite angles of [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131) sum to 180°; conversely, any quadrilateral for which this is true can be inscribed in a circle. As another example, the inscribed angle theorem is the basis for several theorems related to the [power of a point](wikipedia://en/2094044) with respect to a circle. Further, it allows one to prove that when two chords intersect in a circle, the products of the lengths of their pieces are equal.

:::
