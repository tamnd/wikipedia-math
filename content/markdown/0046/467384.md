# Shift operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Shift operator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and in particular [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **shift operator**, also known as the **translation operator**, is an [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) that takes a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $x \mapsto f(x)$ to its **translation** $x \mapsto f(x + a)$. In [time series analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series_analysis), the shift operator is called the *[lag operator](wikipedia://en/1481533)*.

:::

Shift operators are examples of [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), important for their simplicity and natural occurrence. The shift operator action on [functions of a real variable](wikipedia://en/1268958) plays an important role in [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147), for example, it appears in the definitions of [almost periodic functions](wikipedia://en/405512#Uniform_or_Bohr_or_Bochner_almost_periodic_functions), [positive-definite functions](wikipedia://en/649616), [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921), and [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519).[^o3] Shifts of sequences (functions of an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) variable) appear in diverse areas such as [Hardy spaces](wikipedia://en/314780), the theory of [abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/247152), and the theory of [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263), for which the [baker's map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker's_map) is an explicit representation. The notion of [triangulated category](wikipedia://en/1679700) is a [categorified](wikipedia://en/1596969) analogue of the shift operator.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Functions of a real variable {#Functions_of_a_real_variable}

The shift operator $T^{t}$ (where $t \in \mathbb{R}$) takes a function $f$ on $\mathbb{R}$ to its translation $f_{t}$,

$$T^t f(x) = f_t(x) = f(x+t)~.$$

A practical [operational calculus](wikipedia://en/8950551) representation of the linear operator $T^{t}$ in terms of the plain derivative $\tfrac{d}{dx}$ was introduced by [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange),

which may be interpreted operationally through its formal [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) in $t$; and whose action on the monomial $x^{n}$ is evident by the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677), and hence on *all series in* $x$, and so all functions $f(x)$ as above.[^o8] This, then, is a formal encoding of the Taylor expansion in Heaviside's calculus.

The operator thus provides the prototype[^o9] for Lie's celebrated [advective flow for Abelian groups](wikipedia://en/1448702#Lie's_data_transport_equation),

$$\exp\left(t \beta(x) \frac{d}{dx}\right) f(x) = \exp\left(t \frac{d}{dh}\right) F(h) = F(h+t) = f\left(h^{-1}(h(x)+t)\right),$$

where the canonical coordinates $h$ ([Abel functions](wikipedia://en/5834526)) are defined such that

$$h'(x)\equiv \frac 1 {\beta(x)} ~, \qquad f(x)\equiv F(h(x)).$$

For example, it easily follows that $\beta (x)=x$ yields scaling,

$$\exp\left(t x \frac{d}{dx}\right) f(x) = f(e^t x) ,$$

hence $\exp\left(i\pi x \tfrac{d}{dx}\right) f(x) = f(-x)$ (parity); likewise, $\beta (x)=x^2$ yields[^o10]

$$\exp\left(t x^2 \frac{d}{dx}\right) f(x) = f \left(\frac{x}{1-tx}\right),$$

$\beta (x)= \tfrac{1}{x}$ yields

$$\exp\left(\frac{t} {x} \frac{d}{dx}\right) f(x) = f \left(\sqrt{x^2+2t} \right) ,$$

$\beta (x)=e^x$ yields

$$\exp\left (t e^x \frac d {dx} \right ) f(x) = f\left (\ln \left (\frac{1}{e^{-x} - t} \right ) \right ) ,$$

etc.

The [initial condition](wikipedia://en/1186804) of the flow and the group property completely determine the entire Lie flow, providing a solution to the translation functional equation[^o11]

$$f_t(f_\tau (x))=f_{t+\tau} (x) .$$

### Sequences {#Sequences}

The **left shift** operator acts on one-sided [infinite sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence) of numbers by

$$S^*: (a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots) \mapsto (a_2, a_3, a_4, \ldots)$$

and on two-sided infinite sequences by

$$T: (a_k)_{k\,=\,-\infty}^\infty \mapsto (a_{k+1})_{k\,=\,-\infty}^\infty.$$

The **right shift** operator acts on one-sided [infinite sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence) of numbers by

$$S: (a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots) \mapsto (0, a_1, a_2, \ldots)$$

and on two-sided infinite sequences by

$$T^{-1}:(a_k)_{k\,=\,-\infty}^\infty \mapsto (a_{k-1})_{k\,=\,-\infty}^\infty.$$

The right and left shift operators acting on two-sided infinite sequences are called ***bilateral*** shifts. A finite-dimensional analog is given by the [shift matrices](wikipedia://en/7864525).

### Abelian groups {#Abelian_groups}

In general, as illustrated above, if $F$ is a function on an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) $G$, and $h$ is an element of $G$, the shift operator $T^{g}$ maps $F$ to[^o11][^o14]

$$F_g(h) = F(h+g).$$

## Properties of the shift operator {#Properties_of_the_shift_operator}

The shift operator acting on real- or complex-valued functions or sequences is a linear operator which preserves most of the standard [norms](wikipedia://en/990534) which appear in functional analysis. Therefore, it is usually a [continuous operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_operator) with norm one.

### Derivation via Taylor series {#Derivation_via_Taylor_series}

The operator may be derived by a simple distribution of linear operators. Given an [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478), [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000) function $f$ evaluated at some $x$, its translation by some distance $t$ may be written

$$f(x+t) = f(x) + tf'(x) + \frac{t^2}{2!}f''(x) + \frac{t^3}{3!}f'''(x) + \ldots$$

Interpreting a power of the differential operator as a composition and thus its order, one can distribute terms, rewriting this expression as

$$f(x+t) = \left(\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\left(t\frac{d}{dx}\right)^k}{k!}\right)f(x) = e^{t\frac{d}{dx}}f(x)$$

by the definition of the power series representation of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678#Power_series).

### Action on Hilbert spaces {#Action_on_Hilbert_spaces}

The shift operator acting on two-sided sequences is a [unitary operator](wikipedia://en/225999) on $\ell_2(\mathbb{Z}).$ The shift operator acting on functions of a real variable is a unitary operator on $L_2(\mathbb{R}).$

In both cases, the (left) shift operator satisfies the following commutation relation with the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247):

$$\mathcal{F} T^t = M^t \mathcal{F},$$

where $M^{t}$ is the [multiplication operator](wikipedia://en/976642) by $\exp(itx)$. Therefore, the spectrum of $T^{t}$ is the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).

The one-sided shift $S$ acting on $\ell_2(\mathbb{N})$ is a proper [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) with [range](wikipedia://en/275991) equal to all [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) which vanish in the first [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate). The operator $S$ is a [compression](wikipedia://en/681190) of $T^{-1}$, in the sense that

$$T^{-1}y = Sx \text{ for each } x \in \ell^2(\mathbb{N}),$$

where $y$ is the vector in $\ell_2(\mathbb{Z})$ with $y_{i} = x_{i}$ for $i \ge 0$ and $y_{i} = 0$ for $i < 0$. This observation is at the heart of the construction of many [unitary dilations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_dilation) of isometries.

The [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of $S$ is the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738). The shift $S$ is one example of a [Fredholm operator](wikipedia://en/279722); it has Fredholm index −1.

## Generalization {#Generalization}

[Jean Delsarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Delsarte) introduced the notion of **generalized shift operator** (also called **generalized displacement operator**); it was further developed by [Boris Levitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Levitan).[^o3]

A family of operators $\{L^x\}_{x \in X}$ acting on a space $\Phi$ of functions from a set $X$ to $\mathbb{C}$ is called a family of generalized shift operators if the following properties hold:

1. [Associativity](wikipedia://en/1335): let $(R^y f)(x) = (L^x f)(y).$ Then $L^x R^y = R^y L^x.$
1. There exists $e$ in $X$ such that $L^{e}$ is the [identity operator](wikipedia://en/15069).

In this case, the set $X$ is called a [hypergroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergroup).
