# Fractional ideal

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fractional ideal"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in particular [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), the concept of **fractional ideal** is introduced in the context of [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462) and is particularly fruitful in the study of [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132). In some sense, fractional ideals of an integral domain are like [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) where [denominators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) are allowed. In contexts where fractional ideals and ordinary [ring ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_ideal) are both under discussion, the latter are sometimes termed ***integral ideals*** for clarity.

:::

## Definition and basic results {#Definition_and_basic_results}

Let $R$ be an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), and let $K = \operatorname{Frac}R$ be its [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271).

A **fractional ideal** of $R$ is an $R$-[submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule) $I$ of $K$ such that there exists a non-zero $r \in R$ such that $rI\subseteq R$. Equivalently, $I \subseteq K$ is a fractional ideal of $R$ if $I = r^{-1}J$, where $r$ is a non-zero element of $R$ and $J$ is an ideal of $R$. The element $r$ can be thought of as clearing out the denominators in $I$, hence the name fractional ideal.

The **principal fractional ideals** are those $R$-submodules of $K$ generated by a single nonzero element of $K$. A fractional ideal $I$ is contained in $R$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is an (integral) ideal of $R$.

A fractional ideal $I$ is called **invertible** if there is another fractional ideal $J$ such that

$$IJ = R$$

where

$$IJ = \{ a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \cdots + a_n b_n : a_i \in I, b_j \in J, n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0} \}$$

is the **product** of the two fractional ideals.

In this case, the fractional ideal $J$ is uniquely determined and equal to the generalized [ideal quotient](wikipedia://en/980388)

$$(R :_{K} I) = \{ x \in K : xI \subseteq R \}.$$

The set of invertible fractional ideals forms a [commutative group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_group) with respect to the above product, where the identity is the [unit ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ideal) $(1) = R$ itself. This group is called the **group of fractional ideals** of $R$. The principal fractional ideals form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011). A (nonzero) fractional ideal is invertible if and only if it is [projective](wikipedia://en/364488) as an $R$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410). Geometrically, this means an invertible fractional ideal can be interpreted as a rank 1 [vector bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundle_%28algebraic_geometry%29) over the [affine scheme](wikipedia://en/37291) $\text{Spec}(R)$.

Every [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) *R*-submodule of *K* is a fractional ideal and if $R$ is [noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129), then these are all the fractional ideals of $R$.

## Dedekind domains {#Dedekind_domains}

In [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132), the situation is much simpler. In particular, every non-zero fractional ideal is invertible. In fact, this property characterizes Dedekind domains:

An integral domain is a Dedekind domain if and only if every non-zero fractional ideal is invertible.

The set of fractional ideals over a Dedekind domain $R$ is denoted $\text{Div}(R)$.

Its [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of fractional ideals by the subgroup of principal fractional ideals is an important invariant of a Dedekind domain called the [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907).

## Number fields {#Number_fields}

For the special case of a [number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) $K$ (such as a [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462)) there is an associated [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) denoted $\mathcal{O}_K$ called the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of $K$. For example, $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}\,)} = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}\,]$ for $d$ [square-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squarefree_integer) and [congruent](wikipedia://en/20087) to $2,3 \text{ }(\text{mod } 4)$. The key property of these rings $\mathcal{O}_K$ is they are Dedekind domains. Hence the theory of fractional ideals can be described for the rings of integers of number fields. In fact, [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695) is the study of such groups of class rings.

### Associated structures {#Associated_structures}

For the ring of integers[^o6]<sup>pg 2</sup> $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field, the group of fractional ideals forms a group denoted $\mathcal{I}_K$ and the subgroup of principal fractional ideals is denoted $\mathcal{P}_K$. The **[ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907)** is the group of fractional ideals modulo the principal fractional ideals, so

$$\mathcal{C}_K := \mathcal{I}_K/\mathcal{P}_K$$

and its class number $h_K$ is the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) of the group, $h_K = |\mathcal{C}_K|$. In some ways, the class number is a measure for how "far" the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ is from being a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) (UFD). This is because $h_K = 1$ if and only if $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a UFD.

#### Exact sequence for ideal class groups {#Exact_sequence_for_ideal_class_groups}

There is an [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215)

$$0 \to \mathcal{O}_K^* \to K^* \to \mathcal{I}_K \to \mathcal{C}_K \to 0$$

associated to every number field.

### Structure theorem for fractional ideals {#Structure_theorem_for_fractional_ideals}

One of the important structure theorems for fractional ideals of a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) states that every fractional ideal $I$ decomposes uniquely up to ordering as

$$I = (\mathfrak{p}_1\ldots\mathfrak{p}_n)(\mathfrak{q}_1\ldots\mathfrak{q}_m)^{-1}$$

for [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928)

$$\mathfrak{p}_i,\mathfrak{q}_j \in \text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_K).$$

in the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) of $\mathcal{O}_K$. For example,

$\frac{2}{5}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(i)}$ factors as $(1+i)(1-i)((1+2i)(1-2i))^{-1}$

Another useful structure theorem is that integral fractional ideals are generated by up to 2 elements. We call a fractional ideal which is a subset of $\mathcal{O}_K$ *integral*.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- $\frac{5}{4}\mathbb{Z}$ is a fractional ideal over $\mathbb{Z}$
- For $K = \mathbb{Q}(i)$ the ideal $(5)$ splits in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(i)} = \mathbb{Z}[i]$ as $(2-i)(2+i)$
- For $K=\mathbb{Q}_{\zeta_3}$ we have the factorization $(3) = (2\zeta_3 + 1)^2$. This is because if we multiply it out, we get

$$\begin{aligned} (2\zeta_3 + 1)^2 &= 4\zeta_3^2 + 4\zeta_3 + 1 \\ &= 4(\zeta_3^2 + \zeta_3) + 1 \end{aligned}$$

Since $\zeta_3$ satisfies $\zeta_3^2 + \zeta_3 =-1$, our factorization makes sense.

- For $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-23})$ we can multiply the fractional ideals

$I = \left(2, \frac12\sqrt{-23} - \frac12\right)$ and $J=\left(4,\frac12\sqrt{-23} + \frac32\right)$

to get the ideal

$$IJ=\left(\frac12\sqrt{-23}+\frac32\right).$$

:::

## Divisorial ideal {#Divisorial_ideal}

Let $\tilde I$ denote the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all principal fractional ideals containing a nonzero fractional ideal $I$.

Equivalently,

$$\tilde I = (R : (R : I)),$$

where as above

$$(R : I) = \{ x \in K : xI \subseteq R \}.$$

If $\tilde I = I$ then *I* is called **divisorial**.[^o14] In other words, a divisorial ideal is a nonzero intersection of some nonempty set of fractional principal ideals.

If *I* is divisorial and *J* is a nonzero fractional ideal, then (*I* : *J*) is divisorial.

Let *R* be a [local](wikipedia://en/153391) [Krull domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_domain) (e.g., a [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209) local domain). Then *R* is a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633) if and only if the [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of *R* is divisorial.[^o15]

An integral domain that satisfies the [ascending chain conditions](wikipedia://en/3189) on divisorial ideals is called a [Mori domain](wikipedia://en/35957282).[^n1]
