# Standard basis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Standard basis"}

Canonical basis

*Image: 3D Vector.svg.* Every vector $\mathbf{a}$ in three dimensions is a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of the standard basis vectors $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **standard basis** (also called **natural basis** or **[canonical basis](wikipedia://en/1028158)**) of a [coordinate vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_vector_space) (such as $\mathbb{R}^n$ or $\mathbb{C}^n$) is the set of vectors, each of whose components are all zero, except one that equals 1.[^n1] For example, in the case of the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) $\mathbb{R}^2$ formed by the pairs $(x, y)$ of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the standard basis is formed by the vectors

$$\mathbf{e}_x = (1,0),\quad \mathbf{e}_y = (0,1).$$

Similarly, the standard basis for the [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) $\mathbb{R}^3$ is formed by vectors

$$\mathbf{e}_x = (1,0,0),\quad \mathbf{e}_y = (0,1,0),\quad \mathbf{e}_z=(0,0,1).$$

Here the vector $\mathbf{e}_{x}$ points in the $x$ direction, the vector $\mathbf{e}_{y}$ points in the $y$ direction, and the vector $\mathbf{e}_{z}$ points in the $z$ direction. There are several common [notations](wikipedia://en/277184) for standard-basis vectors, including $\mathbf{e}_{x}, \mathbf{e}_{y}, \mathbf{e}_{z}$, $\mathbf{e}_{1}, \mathbf{e}_{2}, \mathbf{e}_{3}$, $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$, and $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{z}$. These vectors are sometimes written with a [hat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex) to emphasize their status as [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) (**standard unit vectors**).

:::

These vectors are a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) in the sense that any other vector can be expressed uniquely as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of these.[^n2] For example, every vector $\mathbf{v}$ in three-dimensional space can be written uniquely as

$$v_x\,\mathbf{e}_x + v_y\,\mathbf{e}_y + v_z\,\mathbf{e}_z,$$

the [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) $v_{x}$, $v_{y}$, $v_{z}$ being the [scalar components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_component) of the vector $\mathbf{v}$.

In the $n$-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) Euclidean space $\mathbb R^n$, the standard basis consists of $n$ distinct vectors

$$\{ \mathbf{e}_i : 1\leq i\leq n\},$$

where $\mathbf{e}_{i}$ denotes the vector with a 1 in the $i$th [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) and 0's elsewhere.

Standard bases can be defined for other [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), whose definition involves [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), such as [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) and [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859). In both cases, the standard basis consists of the elements of the space such that all coefficients but one are 0 and the non-zero one is 1. For polynomials, the standard basis thus consists of the [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) and is commonly called [monomial basis](wikipedia://en/976666). For matrices $M_{m\times n}$, the standard basis consists of the $m\times n$–matrices with exactly one non-zero entry, which is 1. For example, the standard basis for 2×2 matrices is formed by the 4 matrices

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{e}_{11} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, & \mathbf{e}_{12} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\ \mathbf{e}_{21} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, & \mathbf{e}_{22} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

By definition, the standard basis is a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) [unit vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_vectors). In other words, it is an [ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_basis) and [orthonormal](wikipedia://en/195063) basis.

However, an ordered orthonormal basis is not necessarily a standard basis. For instance the two vectors representing a 30° rotation of the 2D standard basis described above, i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} v_1 &= \left( {\sqrt 3 \over 2} , {1 \over 2} \right) \\ v_2 &= \left( {1 \over 2} , {-\sqrt 3 \over 2} \right) \end{aligned}$$

are also orthogonal unit vectors, but they are not aligned with the axes of the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), so the basis with these vectors does not meet the definition of standard basis.

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

There is a *standard* basis also for the ring of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in $n$ indeterminates over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), namely the [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416).

All of the preceding are special cases of the [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339)

$${(e_i)}_{i\in I}= ( (\delta_{ij} )_{j \in I} )_{i \in I}$$

where $I$ is any set and $\delta_{ij}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890), equal to zero whenever $i \ne j$ and equal to 1 if $i = j$. This family is the *canonical* basis of the $R$-module ([free module](wikipedia://en/326454))

$$R^{(I)}$$

of all families

$$f=(f_i)$$

from $I$ into a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$, which are [zero except for a finite number of indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_support), if we interpret 1 as $1_{R}$, the [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in $R$.[^n3]

:::

## Other usages {#Other_usages}

The existence of other 'standard' bases has become a topic of interest in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), beginning with work of [Hodge](wikipedia://en/384430) from 1943 on [Grassmannians](wikipedia://en/373810). It is now a part of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) called *standard monomial theory*. The idea of standard basis in the [universal enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551) of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) is established by the [Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem](wikipedia://en/762696).

[Gröbner bases](wikipedia://en/357328) are also sometimes called standard bases.

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the standard basis vectors for a given Euclidean space are sometimes referred to as the [versors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Versor_%28physics%29) of the axes of the corresponding Cartesian coordinate system.
