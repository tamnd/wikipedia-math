# Blaise de Vigenère

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Blaise de Vigenère"}

**Blaise de Vigenère** (; 5 April 1523 – 19 February 1596)[^o3] was a French [diplomat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomat), [cryptographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographer), [translator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translator) and [alchemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alchemy).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Vigenère was born into a respectable family in the village of [Saint-Pourçain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Pourçain-sur-Sioule) in [Bourbonnais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbonnais). When he was 12, his father, Jehan (modern spelling [Jean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_%28male_given_name%29)) de Vigenère, arranged for him to have a classical education in Paris. Registered at the university at 14, he quit after three years without a known degree.[^o5]

From 1539 to around 1545, he worked under Gilbert Bayard, a first secretary to [King Francis I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Francis_I), who had fiefs in Bourbonnais.[^o5]

In 1545, he accompanied the French envoy Louis Adhémar de Monteil, Count of Grignan, to the [Diet of Worms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diet_of_Worms) as a junior secretary.[^o6][^o7] After the diet's rupture, he traveled in Europe.[^o8]

In 1547, he quit the court and entered the service of the [House of Nevers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counts_and_dukes_of_Nevers). He would remain associated with it until at least a year before his death in 1596. At first he was secretary to [François I, Duke of Nevers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_I,_Duke_of_Nevers), a position he held until the deaths of the duke and [his son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_II,_Duke_of_Nevers) in 1562. A letter of July 1593 reveals he was also secretary to [Louis de Gonzague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_de_Gonzague,_Duke_of_Nevers) (who became Duke of Nevers by his marriage to François I's daughter [Henriette of Cleves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henriette_of_Cleves) in 1565) and tutored [Louis' son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_Gonzaga,_Duke_of_Mantua) (born 1580).[^o9]

In 1549, he took his first trip to Italy, in particular to Rome. It is not known who his protector was on the trip,[^o10] which lasted for three to four years,[^o11] but one of his biographers, Maurice Sarazin, has suggested it may have been [Cardinal Tournon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Tournon), a celebrated diplomat and friend of the arts.[^o12] He would return to Rome again in 1566 for another three years. During his stays, he examined all the ancient buildings and expanded his knowledge of antiquity.[^o11] In his 1586 book, *Traicté des chiffres ou secretes manières d'escrire*, he wrote:

> in Rome, I did all that was possible — talking to learned men versed in Roman antiquity, visiting and revisiting the marble reliefs, bronzes, medals and ancient cameos from which one might draw knowledge and instruction — but I couldn't restore anything. [^o13]

After the death of [François II, Duke of Nevers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_II,_Duke_of_Nevers), in 1562, Vigenère resumed his studies. He received lessons from [Adrianus Turnebus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrianus_Turnebus) and [Jean Dorat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Daurat) and learned Greek and Hebrew.[^o8]

In 1566, the queen mother, [Catherine de Médicis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_de_Médicis), sent Vigenère to Rome,[^o14] where he was secretary under Juste de Tournon, the ambassador of her son [King Charles IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_IX_of_France).[^o11] In December of that year she sent a letter to Tournon, requesting that Vigenère respond to an overture made by the secretary of the elderly king of Poland, [Sigismond Auguste Jagellon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigismond_Auguste_Jagellon), who had no children. Apparently the secretary had proposed the Polish king name her son Henri (the Duke of Anjou and future [King Henry III of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Henry_III_of_France)), as his successor. She specified Vigenère should interview the Polish diplomat verbally, verifying the proposal's authenticity, then facilitate the idea, all without revealing he had been authorized to do so by her.[^o15] Nine years later, Vigenère wrote in detail about this incident in his book *La description du Royaume de Poloigne*, without naming himself or revealing that he could have held secrets.[^o16]

During this later stay in Italy, Vigenère also visited other Italian cities, notably [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) and [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence).[^o14]

In 1570, at age 47, Vigenère retired from traveling and settled in Paris to devote himself to writing.[^o11] He donated his 1,000 livres a year income to the poor in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989). He married Marie Varé on 24 July 1570.[^o17]

Vigenère was a [Christian Kabbalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Kabbalah) who studied Hebrew.[^o18][^o19] He was a student of well-known [Christian Hebraists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Hebraist) [Gilbert Génébrard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_Génébrard) and [Guy Le Fèvre de la Boderie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Lefèvre_de_la_Boderie).[^o20][^o21][^o22]

He died of throat cancer in 1596 and is buried in the [Saint-Étienne-du-Mont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Étienne-du-Mont) church.[^o23]

## Vigenère cipher {#Vigenère_cipher}

On Vigenère's trips to Italy he read books about cryptography and came in contact with cryptologists. [Giovan Battista Bellaso](wikipedia://en/15928585) described a method of encryption in his 1553 book *La cifra del. Sig. Giovan Battista Belaso*, published in Venice in 1553,[^o25] which in the 19th century was misattributed to Vigenère and became widely known as the "[Vigenère cipher](wikipedia://en/69509)".[^o26] In 1567 and 1568, Vigenère created a different, stronger [autokey cipher](wikipedia://en/157792), which he published in 1586 in his book *Traicté des chiffres ou secrètes manières d'escrire* (*Treatise on Ciphers or Secret Ways of Writing*).[^o27][^o28] It differs from Bellaso's in several ways:

- Bellaso used a "reciprocal table" of five alphabets; Vigenère used ten;
- Bellaso's cipher was based on the first letter of the word; Vigenère used a letter agreed upon before communication.

## Works {#Works}

After his retirement, Vigenère composed and translated over 20 books, including:

- 1573: *Les Chroniques et annales de Poloigne*. Paris: Jean Richer. Available on [Gallica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallica).
- 1573: *La description du royaume de Poloigne, & pays adjacens : avec les statuts, constitutions, mœurs, & façon de faire d'iceux*. Paris: Jean Richer. [Digital copy](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/421850) available at [Utrecht University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utrecht_University).
- 1576: *[La somptueuse et magnifique entrée du roi Henri III en la cité de Mantoue](http://architectura.cesr.univ-tours.fr/Traite/Images/INHA-8R531Index.asp)*. Paris: Nicolas Chesneau. (Includes a description of contemporary [Mantua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantua).)
- 1578: *Traicté des Cometes ou estoilles chevelues, apparoissantes extraordinairement au ciel, avec leurs causes et effects, par Bl. de Vigere*. Available on Gallica.[^o30]
- 1582: *Les Commentaires de César, des guerres de la Gaule. Mis en françois par Blaise de Vigenère, Secretaire de la Chambre du Roy. Avec quelques annotations dessus*.
- 1583: *Les [Décades qui se trouvent de Tite-Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ab_Urbe_condita_libri), mises en langue francoise avec des annotations & figures pour l'intelligence de l'antiquité romaine, plus une description particulière des lieux : & une chronologie generale des principaux potentats de la terre*. Paris: Abel L'Angelier, 1583 and 1606.
- 1584: *Les commentaires de Cesar, des Guerres de la Gaule. Mise en francois par Blaise de Vigenere. Bourbonnois : revues et corrigez par luy-mesme en cette derniere edition. Avec quelques annotations dessus*. 1584.
- 1585: *L'histoire de Geoffroy de Villehardouyn, mareschal de Champagne & de Romenie : de la conqueste de Constantinople par les barons Franc̦ois associez aux Venitiens, l'an 1204 d'vn costé en son vieil langage, & de l'autre en vn plus moderne & intelligible*. Paris: Abel Langelier, 1585. Translation of *[De la Conquête de Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_la_Conquête_de_Constantinople)* from the original old French.
- 1586: *Traicté des chiffres ou secrètes manières d'escrire*. Available on [Gallica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallica).[^o31]
- 1588: *[Le psaultier de David torne en prose mesuree, ou vers libres. Par Blaise de Vigenère, Bourbonnois](http://mediatheques.agglo-moulins.fr/agglo-moulins.fr/recherche/viewnotice/clef/LEPSAULTIERDEDAVIDTORNEENPROSE--BLAISEDEVIGENERE--ALANGELIER-1588-100#/page/7) (2016-03-07). <https://web.archive.org/web/20160307114611/http://mediatheques.agglo-moulins.fr/agglo-moulins.fr/recherche/viewnotice/clef/lepsaultierdedavidtorneenprose--blaisedevigenere--alangelier-1588-100#/page/7>*.
