# Discrete group

*Image: Number-line.svg.* The integers with their usual topology are a discrete subgroup of the real numbers.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) *G* is called a **discrete group** if there is no [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) in it (i.e., for each element in *G*, there is a neighborhood which only contains that element). Equivalently, the group *G* is discrete if and only if its [identity](wikipedia://en/14962) is [isolated](wikipedia://en/390557).[^n1]

:::

A [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *H* of a topological group *G* is a **discrete subgroup** if *H* is discrete when endowed with the [subspace topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_topology) from *G*. In other words there is a neighbourhood of the identity in *G* containing no other element of *H*. For example, the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), **Z**, form a discrete subgroup of the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), **R** (with the standard [metric topology](wikipedia://en/20018)), but the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), **Q**, do not.

Any group can be endowed with the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology), making it a discrete topological group. Since every map from a discrete space is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29), the topological homomorphisms between discrete groups are exactly the [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) between the underlying groups. Hence, there is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/489935) between the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092) and the category of discrete groups. Discrete groups can therefore be identified with their underlying (non-topological) groups.

There are some occasions when a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) or [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) is usefully endowed with the discrete topology, 'against nature'. This happens for example in the theory of the [Bohr compactification](wikipedia://en/634780), and in [group cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) theory of Lie groups.

A discrete [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694) is an isometry group such that for every point of the metric space the set of images of the point under the isometries is a [discrete set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_set). A discrete [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) is a symmetry group that is a discrete isometry group.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Since topological groups are [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325), one need only look at a single point to determine if the topological group is discrete. In particular, a topological group is discrete only if the [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) containing the identity is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358).

A discrete group is the same thing as a zero-dimensional [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) ([uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) discrete groups are not [second-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-countable), so authors who require Lie groups to have this property do not regard these groups as Lie groups). The [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787) of a discrete group is just the [trivial subgroup](wikipedia://en/193059) while the [group of components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_components) is isomorphic to the group itself.

Since the only [Hausdorff topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausdorff_topology) on a finite set is the discrete one, a finite Hausdorff topological group must necessarily be discrete. It follows that every finite subgroup of a Hausdorff group is discrete.

A discrete subgroup *H* of *G* is **cocompact** if there is a [compact subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_subset) *K* of *G* such that *HK* = *G*.

Discrete [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) play an important role in the theory of [covering groups](wikipedia://en/1378699) and [locally isomorphic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_isomorphic_groups). A discrete normal subgroup of a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) group *G* necessarily lies in the [center](wikipedia://en/7125) of *G* and is therefore [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974).

*Other properties*:

- every discrete group is [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected)
- every subgroup of a discrete group is discrete.
- every [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) of a discrete group is discrete.
- the product of a finite number of discrete groups is discrete.
- a discrete group is [compact](wikipedia://en/750326) if and only if it is finite.
- every discrete group is [locally compact](wikipedia://en/1651204).
- every discrete subgroup of a Hausdorff group is closed.
- every discrete subgroup of a compact Hausdorff group is finite.

## Examples {#Examples}

- [Frieze groups](wikipedia://en/375503) and [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214) are discrete subgroups of the [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694) of the Euclidean plane. Wallpaper groups are cocompact, but Frieze groups are not.
- A [crystallographic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_group) usually means a cocompact, discrete subgroup of the isometries of some Euclidean space. Sometimes, however, a [crystallographic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_group) can be a cocompact discrete subgroup of a nilpotent or [solvable Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvable_Lie_group).
- Every [triangle group](wikipedia://en/2069342) *T* is a discrete subgroup of the isometry group of the sphere (when *T* is finite), the Euclidean plane (when *T* has a **Z** + **Z** subgroup of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581)), or the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/318742).
- [Fuchsian groups](wikipedia://en/471365) are, by definition, discrete subgroups of the isometry group of the hyperbolic plane.
  - A Fuchsian group that preserves orientation and acts on the upper half-plane model of the hyperbolic plane is a discrete subgroup of the Lie group PSL(2,**R**), the group of orientation preserving isometries of the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) model of the hyperbolic plane.
  - A Fuchsian group is sometimes considered as a special case of a [Kleinian group](wikipedia://en/1362652), by embedding the hyperbolic plane isometrically into three-dimensional hyperbolic space and extending the group action on the plane to the whole space.
  - The [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) PSL(2,**Z**) is thought of as a discrete subgroup of PSL(2,**R**). The modular group is a lattice in PSL(2,**R**), but it is not cocompact.
- [Kleinian groups](wikipedia://en/1362652) are, by definition, discrete subgroups of the isometry group of [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space). These include [quasi-Fuchsian groups](wikipedia://en/30960521).
  - A Kleinian group that preserves orientation and acts on the upper half space model of hyperbolic 3-space is a discrete subgroup of the Lie group PSL(2,**C**), the group of orientation preserving isometries of the [upper half-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_half-space) model of hyperbolic 3-space.
- A [lattice](wikipedia://en/15782871) in a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) is a discrete subgroup such that the [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) of the quotient space is finite.
