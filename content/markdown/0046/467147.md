# Radiative forcing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Radiative forcing"}

*Image: Physical Drivers of climate change.svg.* The assessment of radiative forcing and [climate sensitivity](wikipedia://en/1765281) shows which physical parameters are contributing to temperature changes.[^o2] Parameters shown with orange bars lead to a temperature increase (due to positive radiative forcings), whereas parameters shown with blue bars lead to a temperature decrease (due to negative radiative forcing).

**Radiative forcing** (or **climate forcing**[^o3]) is a concept used to quantify a change to the [balance of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_energy_budget) flowing through a planetary atmosphere. Various factors contribute to this change in energy balance, such as concentrations of [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas) and [aerosols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerosol), and changes in [surface albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albedo) and [solar irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance). In more technical terms, it is defined as "the change in the net, downward minus upward, [radiative flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_flux) (expressed in W/m<sup>2</sup>) due to a change in an external driver of climate change."[^o7] These external drivers are distinguished from [feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedback) and [variability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change) that are internal to the [climate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_system), and that further influence the direction and magnitude of [imbalance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_energy_imbalance). Radiative forcing on Earth is meaningfully evaluated at the [tropopause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropopause) and at the [stratopause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratopause). It is quantified in units of [watts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt) per square [meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter), and often summarized as an average over the total surface area of the globe.

:::

A planet in radiative equilibrium with its parent star and the rest of space can be characterized by net zero radiative forcing and by a [planetary equilibrium temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_equilibrium_temperature).[^o4]

Radiative forcing is not *a thing* in the sense that a single instrument can independently measure it. Rather it is a scientific concept and entity whose strength can be estimated from more fundamental [physics principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outline_of_physics#General_concepts). Scientists use measurements of changes in atmospheric parameters to calculate the radiative forcing.[^o5]

The IPCC summarized the current [scientific consensus about radiative forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change) changes as follows: "Human-caused radiative forcing of 2.72 W/m<sup>2</sup> in 2019 relative to 1750 has warmed the climate system. This warming is mainly due to increased GHG concentrations, partly reduced by cooling due to increased aerosol concentrations".[^o2]

The atmospheric burden of greenhouse gases due to human activity has grown especially rapidly during the last several decades (since about year 1950). For [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide), the 50% increase (*C/C<sub>0</sub>* = 1.5) realized as of year 2020 since 1750 corresponds to a cumulative radiative forcing change (ΔF) of +2.17 W/m<sup>2</sup>.[^o94] Assuming no change in the emissions growth path, a doubling of concentrations (*C/C<sub>0</sub>* = 2) within the next several decades would correspond to a cumulative radiative forcing change (ΔF) of +3.71 W/m<sup>2</sup>.

Radiative forcing can be a useful way to compare the growing warming influence of different anthropogenic greenhouse gases over time. The radiative forcing of long-lived and well-mixed greenhouse gases have been increasing in earth's atmosphere since the industrial revolution.[^o94] Carbon dioxide has the biggest impact on total forcing, while [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane) and [chlorofluorocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbons) (CFCs) play smaller roles as time goes on.[^o94] The five major greenhouse gases ([water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor), carbon dioxide, methane, [nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide) and [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone)) account for about 96% of the direct radiative forcing by long-lived greenhouse gas increases since 1750. The remaining 4% is contributed by the 15 minor [halogenated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogenated) gases.

## Definition and fundamentals {#Definition_and_fundamentals}

*Radiative forcing* is defined in the [IPCC Sixth Assessment Report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_Sixth_Assessment_Report) as follows: "The change in the net, downward minus upward, radiative flux (expressed in W/m<sup>2</sup>) due to a change in an external driver of climate change, such as a change in the concentration of carbon dioxide (CO<sub>2</sub>), the concentration of volcanic aerosols or the output of the Sun."[^o7]

There are some different types of radiative forcing as defined in the literature:[^o7]

- *Stratospherically adjusted radiative forcing: "*when all tropospheric properties held fixed at their unperturbed values, and after allowing for stratospheric temperatures, if perturbed, to readjust to radiative-dynamical equilibrium."
- *Instantaneous radiative forcing:* "if no change in stratospheric temperature is accounted for".
- *Effective radiative forcing*: "once both stratospheric and tropospheric adjustments are accounted for".

The radiation balance of the Earth (i.e. the balance between absorbed and radiated energy) determines the [average global temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_global_temperature). This balance is also called [Earth's energy balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_energy_budget). Changes to this balance occur due to factors such as the intensity of [solar energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_energy), reflectivity of clouds or gases, absorption by various [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas) or surfaces and heat emission by various materials. Any such alteration is a *radiative forcing*, which along with its [climate feedbacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedback), ultimately changes the balance. This happens continuously as sunlight hits the surface of Earth, clouds and aerosols form, the concentrations of atmospheric gases vary and seasons alter the [groundcover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundcover).

*Positive radiative forcing* means Earth receives more incoming energy from sunlight than it radiates to space. This net gain of energy will cause [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming). Conversely, *negative radiative forcing* means that Earth loses more energy to space than it receives from the Sun, which produces cooling ([global dimming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_dimming)).

### History {#History}

Transport of energy and matter in the Earth-atmosphere system is governed by the principles of [equilibrium thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_thermodynamics) and more generally [non-equilibrium thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/472429). During the first half of the 20th century, physicists developed a comprehensive description of [radiative transfer](wikipedia://en/2925371) that they began to apply to stellar and planetary atmospheres in [radiative equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative_equilibrium). Studies of [radiative-convective equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiative-convective_equilibrium) (RCE) followed and matured through the 1960s and 1970s. RCE models began to account for more complex material flows within the energy balance, such as those from a [water cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle), and thereby described observations better.

Another application of equilibrium models is that a [perturbation](wikipedia://en/102140) in the form of an [externally imposed intervention](wikipedia://en/39144241) can estimate a change in [state](wikipedia://en/2747182). The RCE work distilled this into a *forcing-feedback framework* for change, and produced [climate sensitivity](wikipedia://en/1765281) results agreeing with those from [GCMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_circulation_model). This [conceptual framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_framework) asserts that a homogeneous disturbance (effectively imposed onto the top-of-atmosphere energy balance) will be met by slower responses (correlated more or less with changes in a planet's surface temperature) to bring the system to a new equilibrium state. *Radiative forcing* was a term used to describe these disturbances and gained widespread traction in the literature by the 1980s.[^o5]

### Related metrics {#Related_metrics}

The concept of radiative forcing has been evolving from the initial proposal, named nowadays *instantaneous radiative forcing* (IRF), to other proposals that aim to relate better the radiative imbalance with global warming (global surface mean temperature). For example, researchers explained in 2003 how the *adjusted troposphere and stratosphere forcing* can be used in [general circulation models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_circulation_model).[^o10]

The adjusted radiative forcing, in its different calculation methodologies, estimates the imbalance once the stratosphere temperatures has been modified to achieve a radiative equilibrium in the stratosphere (in the sense of zero radiative heating rates). This new methodology is not estimating any *adjustment* or *feedback* that could be produced on the troposphere (in addition to stratospheric temperature adjustments), for that goal another definition, named *effective radiative forcing* has been introduced.[^o11] In general the ERF is the recommendation of the CMIP6 radiative forcing analysis [^o12] although the stratospherically adjusted methodologies are still being applied in those cases where the adjustments and feedbacks on the troposphere are considered not critical, like in the well mixed greenhouse gases and ozone.[^o13][^o14] A methodology named *radiative kernel approach* allows to estimate the climate feedbacks within an offline calculation based on a linear approximation [^o15]

## Uses {#Uses}

*Image: ESSD Radiative Forcing 1750 to 2022.png.* An assessment of effective radiative forcings in 2022 using a baseline year of 1750.[^o18]

### Climate change attribution {#Climate_change_attribution}

Radiative forcing is used to quantify the strengths of different natural and man-made drivers of Earth's energy imbalance over time. The detailed physical mechanisms by which these drivers cause the planet to warm or cool are varied. Radiative forcing allows the contribution of any one driver to be compared against others.

Another metric called *effective radiative forcing* or ERF removes the effect of rapid adjustments (so-called "fast feedbacks") within the atmosphere that are unrelated to longer term surface temperature responses. ERF means that climate change drivers can be placed onto a more level playing field to enable comparison of their effects and a more consistent view of how [global surface temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surface_temperature) responds to various types of human forcing.[^o23]

### Climate sensitivity {#Climate_sensitivity}

Radiative forcing and climate feedbacks can be used together to estimate a subsequent change in steady-state (often denoted "equilibrium") surface temperature (Δ*T*<sub>s</sub>) via the equation:

$$\Delta T_s =~ \tilde{\lambda}~\Delta F$$

where $\tilde{\lambda}$ commonly denotes the [climate sensitivity](wikipedia://en/1765281) parameter, usually with units K/(W/m<sup>2</sup>), and Δ*F* is the radiative forcing in W/m<sup>2</sup>.[^o21] An estimate for $\tilde{\lambda}$ is obtained from the inverse of the [climate feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_feedback) parameter $\lambda$ having units (W/m<sup>2</sup>)/K. An estimated value of $\tilde{\lambda}\approx0.8$ gives an increase in global temperature of about 1.6 K above the 1750 reference temperature due to the increase in over that time (278 to 405 ppm, for a forcing of 2.0 W/m<sup>2</sup>), and predicts a further warming of 1.4 K above present temperatures if the mixing ratio in the atmosphere were to become double its pre-industrial value. Both of these calculations assume no other forcings.[^o22]

Historically, radiative forcing displays the best predictive capacity for specific types of forcing such as greenhouse gases. It is less effective for other anthropogenic influences like [soot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soot).[^o23]

:::

## Calculations and measurements {#Calculations_and_measurements}

### Atmospheric observation {#Atmospheric_observation}

Earth's global [radiation balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_balance) fluctuates as the planet rotates and orbits the Sun, and as global-scale thermal anomalies arise and dissipate within the terrestrial, oceanic and atmospheric systems (e.g. [ENSO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENSO)).[^o26] Consequently, the planet's 'instantaneous radiative forcing' (IRF) is also dynamic and naturally fluctuates between states of overall warming and cooling. The combination of [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848) and [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_system) processes that give rise to these natural variations will typically revert over periods lasting as long as a few years to produce a net-zero average IRF. Such fluctuations also mask the longer-term (decade-long) forcing trends due to human activities, and thus make direct observation of such trends challenging.[^o27]

*Image: NASA Earth Science Division Operating Missions.jpg.* NASA Earth Science Division Operating Missions[^o28]

Earth's radiation balance has been continuously monitored by NASA's [Clouds and the Earth's Radiant Energy System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clouds_and_the_Earth's_Radiant_Energy_System) (CERES) instruments since year 1998.[^o29][^o30] Each scan of the globe provides an estimate of the total (all-sky) instantaneous radiation balance. This data record captures both the natural fluctuations and human influences on IRF; including changes in greenhouse gases, aerosols, land surface, etc. The record also includes the lagging radiative responses to the radiative imbalances; occurring mainly by way of Earth system feedbacks in temperature, surface albedo, atmospheric water vapor and clouds.[^o31][^o32]

Researchers have used measurements from CERES, [AIRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_infrared_sounder), [CloudSat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CloudSat) and other satellite-based instruments within NASA's [Earth Observing System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_Observing_System) to parse out contributions by the natural fluctuations and system feedbacks. Removing these contributions within the multi-year data record allows observation of the anthropogenic trend in top-of-atmosphere (TOA) IRF. The data analysis has also been done in a way that is computationally efficient and independent of most related [modelling](wikipedia://en/47513) methods and results. Radiative forcing was thus directly observed to have risen by +0.53 W m<sup>−2</sup> (±0.11 W m<sup>−2</sup>) from years 2003 to 2018. About 20% of the increase was associated with a reduction in the atmospheric aerosol burden, and most of the remaining 80% was attributed to the rising burden of greenhouse gases.[^o27][^o33][^o34]

A rising trend in the radiative imbalance due to increasing global has been previously observed by ground-based instruments. For example, such measurements have been separately gathered under clear-sky conditions at two [Atmospheric Radiation Measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_Radiation_Measurement) (ARM) sites in Oklahoma and Alaska.[^o35] Each direct observation found that the associated radiative (infrared) heating experienced by surface dwellers rose by +0.2 W m<sup>−2</sup> (±0.07 W m<sup>−2</sup>) during the decade ending 2010.[^o36][^o37] In addition to its focus on [longwave radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outgoing_longwave_radiation) and the most influential forcing gas () only, this result is proportionally less than the TOA forcing due to its buffering by atmospheric absorption.

### Basic estimates {#Basic_estimates}

Radiative forcing can be evaluated for its dependence on different factors which are external to the climate system.[^o39] Basic estimates summarized in the following sections have been derived (assembled) in accordance with [first principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_principles) of the physics of matter and energy. Forcings (ΔF) are expressed as changes over the total surface of the planet and over a specified time interval. Estimates may be significant in the context of global climate forcing for times spanning decades or longer.[^o5] Gas forcing estimates presented in the IPCC's [AR6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AR6) report have been adjusted to include so-called "fast" feedbacks (positive or negative) which occur via atmospheric responses (i.e. *effective radiative forcing*).

## Forcing due to changes in atmospheric gases {#Forcing_due_to_changes_in_atmospheric_gases}

*Image: 1750- Radiative forcing - greenhouse gases and aerosols.svg.* Hansen *et al.* (2025) wrote that the IPCC had underestimated aerosols' cooling effect, causing it to also underestimate [climate sensitivity](wikipedia://en/1765281) (Earth's responsiveness to increases in greenhouse gas concentrations).[^o41] In what Hansen called a [Faustian bargain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deal_with_the_Devil), regulation of aerosols improved air quality, but aerosols' cooling effect became inadequate to temper the increasing warming effect of greenhouse gases—explaining unexpectedly large global warming in 2023–2024.[^o41]

For a well-mixed greenhouse gas, [radiative transfer codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_radiative_transfer_codes) that examine each [spectral line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line) for atmospheric conditions can be used to calculate the forcing ΔF as a function of a change in its concentration. These calculations may be simplified into an algebraic formulation that is specific to that gas.

### Carbon dioxide {#Carbon_dioxide}

*Image: ModtranRadiativeForcingDoubleCO2.png.* Radiative forcing for doubling , as calculated by radiative transfer code Modtran. Red lines are [Planck curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck's_law).

A simplified first-order approximation expression for [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) () is:[^o43]

$$\Delta F = 5.35 \times \ln {(C_0+\Delta C) \over C_0} ~~(\mathrm{W}~\mathrm{m}^{-2}) \,,$$

where *C*<sub>0</sub> is a reference concentration in parts per million (ppm) by volume and *ΔC* is the concentration change in ppm. For the purpose of some studies (e.g. climate sensitivity), *C*<sub>0</sub> is taken as the concentration prior to substantial anthropogenic changes and has a value of 278 ppm as estimated for the year 1750.

The atmospheric burden of greenhouse gases due to human activity has grown especially rapidly during the last several decades (since about year 1950). For carbon dioxide, the 50% increase (*C/C<sub>0</sub>* = 1.5) realized as of year 2020 since 1750 corresponds to a cumulative radiative forcing change (delta F) of +2.17 W/m<sup>2</sup>.[^o94] Assuming no change in the emissions growth path, a doubling of concentrations (*C/C<sub>0</sub>* = 2) within the next several decades would correspond to a cumulative radiative forcing change (delta F) of +3.71 W/m<sup>2</sup>.

The relationship between and radiative forcing is [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/164055) at concentrations up to around eight times the current value.[^o45] Constant concentration increases thus have a progressively smaller warming effect. However, the first-order approximation is inaccurate at higher concentrations and there is no saturation in the absorption of infrared radiation by .[^o46] Various mechanism behind the logarithmic scaling has been proposed but the spectrum distribution of the carbon dioxide seems to be essential,[^o47] particularly a broadening in the relevant 15-*μ*m band coming from a [Fermi resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_resonance#CO2) present in the molecule.[^o48][^o49][^o50]

### Other trace gases {#Other_trace_gases}

Somewhat different formulae apply for other trace greenhouse gases such as [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane) and N (square-root dependence) or [CFCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CFCs) (linear), with coefficients that may be found for example in the [IPCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_Panel_on_Climate_Change) reports.[^o52] A 2016 study suggests a significant revision to the methane IPCC formula.[^o53] Forcings by the most influential trace gases in Earth's atmosphere are included in the section describing recent growth trends, and in the [IPCC list of greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPCC_list_of_greenhouse_gases).

### Water vapor {#Water_vapor}

Water vapor is Earth's primary greenhouse gas currently responsible for about half of all atmospheric gas forcing. Its overall atmospheric concentration depends almost entirely on the average planetary temperature, and has the potential to increase by as much as 7% with every degree (°C) of temperature rise (see also: [Clausius–Clapeyron relation](wikipedia://en/1780425)).[^o55] Thus over long time scales, water vapor behaves as a system feedback that amplifies the radiative forcing driven by the growth of carbon dioxide and other trace gases.[^o56][^o57]

## Forcing due to changes in solar irradiance {#Forcing_due_to_changes_in_solar_irradiance}

### Variations in total solar irradiance (TSI) {#Variations_in_total_solar_irradiance_(TSI)}

The intensity of [solar irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance) including all wavelengths is the [Total Solar Irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_Solar_Irradiance) (TSI) and on average is the [solar constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_constant). It is equal to about 1361 W m<sup>−2</sup> at the distance of Earth's annual-mean orbital radius of one [astronomical unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit) and as measured at the top of the atmosphere.[^o60] Earth TSI varies with both solar activity and planetary orbital dynamics. Multiple satellite-based instruments including [ERB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nimbus_7), [ACRIM 1-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACRIMSAT), [VIRGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_and_Heliospheric_Observatory), and [TIM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Radiation_and_Climate_Experiment)[^o61][^o62] have continuously measured TSI with improving [accuracy and precision](wikipedia://en/41932) since 1978.[^o63]

Approximating Earth as a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), the cross-sectional area exposed to the Sun ($\pi r^2$) is equal to one quarter the area of the planet's surface ($4\pi r^2$). The globally and annually averaged amount of solar irradiance per square meter of Earth's atmospheric surface ($I_0$) is therefore equal to one quarter of TSI, and has a nearly constant value of $I_0=340~~\mathrm{W}~\mathrm{m}^{-2}$.

Earth follows an [elliptical orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_orbit) around the Sun, so that the TSI received at any instant fluctuates between about 1321 W m<sup>−2</sup> (at [aphelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphelion) in early July) and 1412 W m<sup>−2</sup> (at perihelion in early January), and thus by about ±3.4% over each year.[^o64] This change in irradiance has minor influences on Earth's seasonal weather patterns and its [climate zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_zone), which primarily result from the annual cycling in Earth's relative tilt direction.[^o65] Such repeating cycles contribute a net-zero forcing (by definition) in the context of decades-long climate changes.

### Sunspot activity {#Sunspot_activity}

*Image: Sunspot Numbers.png.* 400 year sunspot history, including the [Maunder Minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maunder_Minimum)

Average annual TSI varies between about 1360 W m<sup>−2</sup> and 1362 W m<sup>−2</sup> (±0.05%) over the course of a typical 11-year [sunspot activity cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot_cycle).[^o67] Sunspot observations have been recorded since about year 1600 and show evidence of lengthier oscillations (Gleissberg cycle, Devries/Seuss cycle, etc.) which modulate the 11-year cycle (Schwabe cycle). Despite such complex behavior, the amplitude of the 11-year cycle has been the most prominent variation throughout this long-term observation record.[^o68]

TSI variations associated with sunspots contribute a small but non-zero net forcing in the context of decadal climate changes.[^o63] Some research suggests they may have partly influenced climate shifts during the [Little Ice Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Ice_Age), along with concurrent changes in volcanic activity and deforestation.[^o69] Since the late 20th century, average TSI has trended slightly lower along with a downward trend in [sunspot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot) activity.[^o70]

### Milankovitch shifts {#Milankovitch_shifts}

Climate forcing caused by variations in solar irradiance have occurred during Milankovitch cycles, which span periods of about 40,000 to 100,000 years. Milankovitch cycles consist of long-duration cycles in Earth's orbital eccentricity (or [ellipticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipticity)), cycles in its orbital obliquity (or [axial tilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt)), and [precession](wikipedia://en/24714) of its relative tilt direction.[^o72] Among these, the 100,000 year cycle in eccentricity causes TSI to fluctuate by about ±0.2%.[^o73] Currently, Earth's eccentricity is nearing its least elliptic (most circular) causing average annual TSI to very slowly decrease.[^o72] Simulations also indicate that Earth's orbital dynamics will [remain stable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_of_the_solar_system) including these variations for least the next 10 million years.[^o74]

### Sun aging {#Sun_aging}

The Sun has consumed about half its hydrogen fuel since forming approximately 4.5 billion years ago.[^o76] TSI will continue to slowly increase during the aging process at a rate of about 1% each 100 million years. Such rate of change is far too small to be detectable within measurements and is insignificant on human timescales.

## Forcing due to changes in albedo and aerosols {#Forcing_due_to_changes_in_albedo_and_aerosols}

### Variations in Earth's albedo {#Variations_in_Earth's_albedo}

A fraction of incident solar radiation is reflected by clouds and aerosols, oceans and landforms, snow and ice, vegetation, and other natural and man-made surface features. The reflected fraction is known as Earth's [bond albedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_albedo) (R), is evaluated at the top of the atmosphere, and has an average annual global value of about 0.30 (30%). The overall fraction of solar power absorbed by Earth is then (1−R) or 0.70 (70%).[^o83]

Atmospheric components contribute about three-quarters of Earth albedo, and clouds alone are responsible for half. The major roles of clouds and water vapor are linked with the majority presence of liquid water covering [the planet's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust). Global patterns in cloud formation and circulation are highly complex, with couplings to ocean heat flows, and with [jet streams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_stream) assisting their rapid transport. Moreover, the albedos of Earth's northern and southern hemispheres have been observed to be essentially equal (within 0.2%). This is noteworthy since more than two-thirds of land and 85% of the human population are in the north.[^o84]

Multiple satellite-based instruments including [MODIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderate_Resolution_Imaging_Spectroradiometer), [VIIRs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_Infrared_Imaging_Radiometer_Suite), and [CERES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clouds_and_the_Earth's_Radiant_Energy_System) have continuously monitored Earth's albedo since 1998.[^o85] [Landsat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landsat_program) imagery, available since 1972, has also been used in some studies.[^o86] Measurement accuracy has improved and results have converged in recent years, enabling more confident assessment of the recent decadal forcing influence of planetary albedo.[^o84] Nevertheless, the existing data record is still too short to support longer-term predictions or to address other related questions.

Seasonal variations in planetary albedo can be understood as a set of system feedbacks that occur largely in response to the yearly cycling of Earth's relative tilt direction. Along with the atmospheric responses, most apparent to surface dwellers are the changes in vegetation, snow, and sea-ice coverage. Intra-annual variations of about ±0.02 (± 7%) around Earth's mean albedo have been observed throughout the course of a year, with maxima occurring twice per year near the time of each solar equinox.[^o84] This repeating cycle contributes net-zero forcing in the context of decades-long climate changes.

### Interannual variability {#Interannual_variability}

*Image: CERES Global albedo anomaly.png.* Measured global albedo anomaly from CERES (2000-2011).

Regional albedos change from year to year due to shifts arising from natural processes, human actions, and system feedbacks. For example, human acts of [deforestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestion) typically raise Earth's reflectivity while introducing [water storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_storage) and [irrigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrigation) to arid lands may lower it. Likewise considering feedbacks, [ice loss](wikipedia://en/21435575) in arctic regions decreases albedo while expanding [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification) at low to middle latitudes increases it.

During years 2000-2012, no overall trend in Earth's albedo was discernible within the 0.1% standard deviation of values measured by CERES.[^o84] Along with the hemispherical equivalence, some researchers interpret the remarkably small interannual differences as evidence that planetary albedo may currently be constrained by the action of complex system feedbacks. Nevertheless, historical evidence also suggests that infrequent events such as major [volcanic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_winter) can significantly perturb the planetary albedo for several years or longer.[^o88]

Satellite observations show that various Earth system feedbacks have stabilized planetary albedo despite recent natural and human-caused shifts.[^o85] On longer timescales, it is more uncertain whether the net forcing which results from such external changes will remain minor.

## Recent growth trends {#Recent_growth_trends}

The IPCC summarized the current [scientific consensus about radiative forcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_consensus_on_climate_change) changes as follows: "Human-caused radiative forcing of 2.72 \[1.96 to 3.48\] W/m<sup>2</sup> in 2019 relative to 1750 has warmed the climate system. This warming is mainly due to increased GHG concentrations, partly reduced by cooling due to increased aerosol concentrations".[^o2]

These data show that dominates the total forcing, with [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane) and [chlorofluorocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbons) (CFC) becoming relatively smaller contributors to the total forcing over time.[^o94] The five major greenhouse gases account for about 96% of the direct radiative forcing by long-lived greenhouse gas increases since 1750. The remaining 4% is contributed by the 15 minor [halogenated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halogenated) gases.

It might be observed that the total forcing for year 2016, 3.027 W m<sup>−2</sup>, together with the commonly accepted value of climate sensitivity parameter λ, 0.8 K /(W m<sup>−2</sup>), results in an increase in global temperature of 2.4 K, much greater than the observed increase, about 1.2 K.[^o97] Part of this difference is due to lag in the global temperature achieving steady state with the forcing. The remainder of the difference is due to negative aerosol forcing (compare [climate effects of particulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particulates#Climate_effects)), climate sensitivity being less than the commonly accepted value, or some combination thereof.[^o98]

The table also includes an "Annual Greenhouse Gas Index" (AGGI), which is defined as the ratio of the total direct radiative forcing due to long-lived greenhouse gases for any year for which adequate global measurements exist to that which was present in 1990.[^o94] 1990 was chosen because it is the baseline year for the [Kyoto Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Protocol). This index is a measure of the inter-annual changes in conditions that affect [carbon dioxide emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas_emissions) and uptake, methane and nitrous oxide sources and sinks, the decline in the atmospheric abundance of [ozone-depleting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_depletion) chemicals related to the [Montreal Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal_Protocol). and the increase in their substitutes (hydrogenated CFCs (HCFCs) and hydrofluorocarbons (HFC). Most of this increase is related to . For 2013, the AGGI was 1.34 (representing an increase in total direct radiative forcing of 34% since 1990). The increase in forcing alone since 1990 was about 46%. The decline in CFCs considerably tempered the increase in net radiative forcing.
