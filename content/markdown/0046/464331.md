# Y-intercept

*Image: Y-intercept.svg.* Graph $y=f(x)$ with the $x$-axis as the horizontal axis and the $y$-axis as the vertical axis. The $y$-intercept of $f(x)$ is indicated by the red dot at $(x=0, y=1)$.

*Image: X-intercept and Y-intercept.svg.* Graph of a function depicting its Y-intercept on the Y-axis, with coordinates $(0, b)$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Y-intercept"}

In [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), using the common convention that the horizontal axis represents a variable $x$ and the vertical axis represents a variable $y$, a **$y$-intercept** or **vertical intercept** is a point where the [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352) or [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) intersects the $y$-axis of the [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931).[^o2] As such, these points satisfy $x = 0$.

:::

## Using equations {#Using_equations}

If the curve in question is given as $y = f(x),$ the $y$-coordinate of the $y$-intercept is found by calculating $f(0)$. Functions which are undefined at $x = 0$ have no $y$-intercept.

If the function is [linear](wikipedia://en/152111) and is expressed in [slope-intercept form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope-intercept_form) as $f(x) = a + bx$, the constant term $a$ is the $y$-coordinate of the $y$-intercept.[^o4]

## Multiple $y$-intercepts {#Multiple_y-intercepts}

Some 2-dimensional mathematical relationships such as [circles](wikipedia://en/6220), [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277), and [hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052) can have more than one $y$-intercept. Because [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) associate $x$-values to no more than one $y$-value as part of their definition, they can have at most one $y$-intercept.

## $x$-intercepts {#x-intercepts}

Analogously, an [$x$-intercept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-intercept) is a point where the [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352) or [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) intersects with the $x$-axis. As such, these points satisfy $y = 0$. The zeros, or roots, of such a function or relation are the $x$-coordinates of these $x$-intercepts.[^o7]

Functions of the form $y = f(x)$ have at most one $y$-intercept, but may contain multiple $x$-intercepts. The $x$-intercepts of functions, if any exist, are often more difficult to locate than the $y$-intercept, as finding the $y$-intercept involves simply evaluating the function at $x = 0$.

## In higher dimensions {#In_higher_dimensions}

The notion may be extended for 3-dimensional space and higher dimensions, as well as for other coordinate axes, possibly with other names. For example, one may speak of the $I$-intercept of the [current–voltage characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current–voltage_characteristic) of, say, a [diode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diode). (In [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), $I$ is the symbol used for [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_%28electricity%29).)
