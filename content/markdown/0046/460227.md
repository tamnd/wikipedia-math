# Dirichlet's unit theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirichlet's unit theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Dirichlet's unit theorem** is a basic result in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) due to [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002).[^o2] It determines the [rank](wikipedia://en/244541) of the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) in the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $O_{K}$ of [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) of a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) $K$. The **regulator** is a positive real number that determines how "dense" the units are.

:::

Note that if $K$ is [Galois](wikipedia://en/457064) over $\mathbb{Q}$ then either $r_{1} = 0$ or $r_{2} = 0$.

Other ways of determining $r_{1}$ and $r_{2}$ are

- use the [primitive element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_element_%28field_theory%29) theorem to write $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, and then $r_{1}$ is the number of [conjugates](wikipedia://en/458467) of $\alpha$ that are real, $2r_{2}$ the number that are complex; in other words, if $f$ is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, then $r_{1}$ is the number of real roots and $2r_{2}$ is the number of non-real complex roots of $f$ (which come in complex conjugate pairs);
- write the [tensor product of fields](wikipedia://en/372240) $K \otimes_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{R}$ as a product of fields, there being $r_{1}$ copies of $\mathbb{R}$ and $r_{2}$ copies of $\mathbb{C}$.

As an example, if $K$ is a [quadratic field](wikipedia://en/454781), the rank is 1 if it is a real quadratic field, and 0 if an imaginary quadratic field. The theory for real quadratic fields is essentially the theory of [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877).

The rank is positive for all number fields besides $\mathbb{Q}$ and imaginary quadratic fields, which have rank 0. The 'size' of the units is measured in general by a [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) called the regulator. In principle a basis for the units can be effectively computed; in practice the calculations are quite involved when $n$ is large.

The torsion in the group of units is the set of all roots of unity of $K$, which form a finite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327). For a number field with at least one real embedding the torsion must therefore be only $\{1,-1$. There are number fields, for example most [imaginary quadratic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_quadratic_field), having no real embeddings which also have $\{1,-1$ for the torsion of its unit group.

Totally real fields are special with respect to units. If $L/K$ is a finite extension of number fields with degree greater than 1 and the units groups for the integers of $L$ and $K$ have the same rank then $K$ is totally real and $L$ is a totally complex quadratic extension. The converse holds too. (An example is $K$ equal to the rationals and $L$ equal to an imaginary quadratic field; both have unit rank 0.)

The theorem not only applies to the maximal order $O_{K}$ but to any order $O \subset O_{K}$.[^o3]

There is a generalisation of the unit theorem by [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118) (and later [Claude Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040)) to describe the structure of the group of *[$S$-units](wikipedia://en/18637484)*, determining the rank of the unit group in [localizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of rings of integers. Also, the [Galois module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_module) structure of $\mathbb{Q} \oplus O_{K, S} \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Q}$ has been determined.[^n1]

## The regulator {#The_regulator}

Suppose that *K* is a number field and $u_1, \dots, u_r$ are a set of generators for the unit group of *K* modulo roots of unity. There will be $r + 1$ Archimedean places of *K*, either real or complex. For $u\in K$, write $u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(r+1)}$ for the different embeddings into $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ and set $N_{j}$ to 1 or 2 if the corresponding embedding is real or complex respectively. Then the $r \times (r + 1)$ matrix

$$\left(N_j\log \left|u_i^{(j)}\right|\right)_{i=1,\dots,r,\; j=1,\dots,r+1}$$

has the property that the sum of any row is zero (because all units have norm 1, and the log of the norm is the sum of the entries in a row). This implies that the absolute value $R$ of the determinant of the submatrix formed by deleting one column is independent of the column. The number $R$ is called the **regulator** of the algebraic number field (it does not depend on the choice of generators $u_{i}$). It measures the "density" of the units: if the regulator is small, this means that there are "lots" of units.

The regulator has the following geometric interpretation. The map taking a unit $u$ to the vector with entries $N_j\log \left|u^{(j)}\right|$ has an image in the $r$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{r + 1}$ consisting of all vectors whose entries have sum 0, and by Dirichlet's unit theorem the image is a lattice in this subspace. The volume of a fundamental domain of this lattice is $R\sqrt{r + 1}$.

The regulator of an algebraic number field of degree greater than 2 is usually quite cumbersome to calculate, though there are now computer algebra packages that can do it in many cases. It is usually much easier to calculate the product $hR$ of the [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28number_theory%29) $h$ and the regulator using the [class number formula](wikipedia://en/3021101), and the main difficulty in calculating the class number of an algebraic number field is usually the calculation of the regulator.

If $\alpha$ denotes a root of $f(x)$, then a set of fundamental units is $\{\varepsilon_{1}, \varepsilon_{2}\}$, where $\varepsilon_{1} = \alpha^{2} + \alpha - 1$ and $\varepsilon_{2} = 2 - \alpha^{2}$. The area of the fundamental domain is approximately 0.910114, so the regulator of $K$ is approximately 0.525455.

- The regulator of an [imaginary quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_quadratic_field), or of the rational integers, is 1 (as the determinant of a $0 \times 0$ matrix is 1).
- The regulator of a [real quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_quadratic_field) is the logarithm of its [fundamental unit](wikipedia://en/3995927): for example, that of the [golden field](wikipedia://en/80262208) $\mathbb{Q}\bigl(\sqrt5~\!\bigr)$ is $\log \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \sqrt{5}~\!\bigr)$. This can be seen as follows. A fundamental unit is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) $\tfrac12\bigl(1 + \sqrt{5}~\!\bigr)$, and its images under the two embeddings into $\mathbb{R}$ are $\tfrac12\bigl(1 + \sqrt{5}~\!\bigr)$ and $\tfrac12\bigl(1 - \sqrt{5}~\!\bigr)$. So the $r \times (r + 1)$ matrix is $$\left[1\times\log\left|\frac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{2}\right|, \quad 1\times \log\left|\frac{-\sqrt{5} + 1}{2}\right|\ \right].$$
- The regulator of the [cyclic cubic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_cubic_field) $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, where $\alpha$ is a root of $x^{3} + x^{2} - 2x - 1$, is approximately 0.5255. A basis of the group of units modulo roots of unity is $\{\varepsilon_{1}, \varepsilon_{2}\}$ where $\varepsilon_{1} = \alpha^{2} + \alpha - 1$ and $\varepsilon_{2} = 2 - \alpha^{2}$.[^o8]

## Higher regulators {#Higher_regulators}

A 'higher' regulator refers to a construction for a function on an [algebraic $K$-group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_K-group) with index $n > 1$ that plays the same role as the classical regulator does for the group of units, which is a group $K_{1}$. A theory of such regulators has been in development, with work of [Armand Borel](wikipedia://en/365672) and others. Such higher regulators play a role, for example, in the [Beilinson conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beilinson_conjectures), and are expected to occur in evaluations of certain [$L$-functions](wikipedia://en/245283) at integer values of the argument.[^o10] See also [Beilinson regulator](wikipedia://en/39654854).

## Stark regulator {#Stark_regulator}

The formulation of [Stark's conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stark's_conjectures) led [Harold Stark](wikipedia://en/788807) to define what is now called the **Stark regulator**, similar to the classical regulator as a determinant of logarithms of units, attached to any [Artin representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_representation).[^o13][^o14]

## $p$-adic regulator {#-adic_regulator}

Let $K$ be a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) and for each [prime](wikipedia://en/347049) $P$ of $K$ above some fixed rational prime $p$, let $U_{P}$ denote the local units at $P$ and let $U_{1,P}$ denote the subgroup of principal units in $U_{P}$. Set

$$U_1 = \prod_{P|p} U_{1,P}.$$

Then let $E_{1}$ denote the set of global units $\varepsilon$ that map to $U_{1}$ via the diagonal embedding of the global units in $E$.

Since $E_{1}$ is a finite-[index](wikipedia://en/230581) subgroup of the global units, it is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) of rank $r_{1} + r_{2} - 1$. The **$p$-adic regulator** is the determinant of the matrix formed by the $p$-adic logarithms of the generators of this group. *[Leopoldt's conjecture](wikipedia://en/21829200)* states that this determinant is non-zero.[^o16][^o17]
