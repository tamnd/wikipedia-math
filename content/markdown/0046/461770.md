# Eddington number

*Image: Arthur\_Stanley\_Eddington.jpg.* [Arthur Stanley Eddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Stanley_Eddington) (1882–1944)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eddington number"}

In [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), the **Eddington number**, $N_{\operatorname{Edd}}$, is the number of [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) in the [observable universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe). Eddington originally calculated it as about \1.57 × 10<sup>79</sup>; current estimates make it approximately × 10<sup>80</sup>.[^o8]

:::

The term is named for British astrophysicist [Arthur Eddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Eddington), who in 1940 was the first to propose a value of $N_{\operatorname{Edd}}$ and to explain why this number might be important for [physical cosmology](wikipedia://en/5378) and the foundations of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939).

## History {#History}

Eddington argued that the value of the [fine-structure constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-structure_constant), *α*, could be obtained by pure deduction. He related *α* to the Eddington number, which was his estimate of the number of protons in the universe.[^o3] This led him in 1929 to conjecture that *α* was exactly 1/136.[^o4] He devised a "proof" that *N*<sub>Edd</sub> = 136 × 2<sup>256</sup>, or about \1.57 × 10<sup>79</sup>. Other physicists did not adopt this conjecture and did not accept his argument. It even led to a major journal publishing a joke article making fun of the idea.[^o5]

During a course of lectures that he delivered in 1938 as [Tarner Lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarner_Lectures) at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), Eddington averred that:

I believe there are 15 [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) in the universe and the same number of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron).[^o6]

This large number was soon named the "Eddington number".

Shortly thereafter, improved measurements of *α* yielded values closer to 1/137, whereupon Eddington changed his "proof" to show that *α* had to be exactly 1/137.[^o7]

Current estimates of *N*<sub>Edd</sub> point to a value of about × 10<sup>80</sup>.[^o8] These estimates assume that all matter can be taken to be [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) and require assumed values for the number and size of [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) and [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) in the universe.[^o9]
