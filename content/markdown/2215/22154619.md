# Hilbert metric

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hilbert metric"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hilbert metric**, also known as the **Hilbert projective metric**, is an explicitly defined [distance function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) on a bounded [convex subset](wikipedia://en/6292) of the *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. It was introduced by (txt) as a generalization of [Cayley's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley_metric) for the distance in the [Cayley–Klein model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein_model) of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), where the convex set is the *n*-dimensional open [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball). Hilbert's metric has been applied to [Perron–Frobenius theory](wikipedia://en/1540333) and to constructing [Gromov hyperbolic spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gromov_hyperbolic_space).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let Ω be a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) [open](wikipedia://en/39358) domain in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) that does not contain a line. Given two distinct points *A* and *B* of Ω, let *X* and *Y* be the points at which the straight line *AB* intersects the boundary of Ω, where the order of the points is *X*, *A*, *B*, *Y*. Then the **Hilbert distance** $d(A, B)$ is the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of the [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) of this quadruple of points:

$$d(A,B)=\log\left(\frac{|YA|}{|YB|}\frac{|XB|}{|XA|}\right).$$

The function *d* is extended to all pairs of points by letting $d(A, A) = 0$ and defines a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) on Ω. If one of the points *A* and *B* lies on the boundary of Ω then *d* can be formally defined to be +∞, corresponding to a limiting case of the above formula when one of the denominators is zero.

A variant of this construction arises for a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [convex cone](wikipedia://en/3558266) *K* in a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) *V* (possibly, infinite-dimensional). In addition, the cone *K* is assumed to be *pointed*, i.e. $K \cap (-K) = \{0\}$ and thus *K* determines a [partial order](wikipedia://en/3558266#Partial_order_defined_by_a_convex_cone) $\leq_K$ on *V*. Given any vectors *v* and *w* in $K \setminus\{0\}$, one first defines

$$M(v/w)=\inf\{\lambda:v\leq_K\lambda w\}, \quad m(v/w)=\sup\{\mu:\mu w \leq_K v\}.$$

The **Hilbert pseudometric** on $K \setminus\{0\}$ is then defined by the formula

$$d(v,w)=\log\frac{M(v/w)}{m(v/w)}.$$

It is invariant under the rescaling of *v* and *w* by positive constants and so descends to a metric on the space of rays of *K*, which is interpreted as the [projectivization](wikipedia://en/21861125) of *K* (in order for *d* to be finite, one needs to restrict to the interior of *K*). Moreover, if $K \subset \mathbf{R} \times V$ is the cone over a convex set Ω,

$$K=\{(t,tx): t\in\mathbb{R}, x\in\Omega \},$$

then the space of rays of *K* is canonically isomorphic to Ω. If *v* and *w* are vectors in rays in *K* corresponding to the points $A, B \in \Omega$ then these two formulas for *d* yield the same value of the distance.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- In the case where the domain Ω is a unit ball in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, the formula for *d* coincides with the expression for the distance between points in the [Cayley–Klein model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein_model) of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), up to a multiplicative constant.
- If the cone *K* is the positive [orthant](wikipedia://en/1283725) in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> then the induced metric on the projectivization of *K* is often called simply **Hilbert's projective metric**. This cone corresponds to a domain Ω which is a regular [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) of dimension *n* − 1.

:::

## Motivation and applications {#Motivation_and_applications}

- Hilbert introduced his metric in order to construct an axiomatic metric geometry in which there exist triangles *ABC* whose vertices *A*, *B*, *C* are not [collinear](wikipedia://en/946975), yet one of the sides is equal to the sum of the other two — it follows that the shortest path connecting two points is not unique in this geometry. In particular, this happens when the convex set Ω is a Euclidean [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) and the straight line extensions of the segments *AB*, *BC*, *AC* do not meet the interior of one of the sides of Ω.
- [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916) used Hilbert's metric and the [Banach contraction principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_contraction_principle) to rederive the [Perron–Frobenius theorem](wikipedia://en/1540333) in finite-dimensional linear algebra and its analogues for [integral operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_operators) with positive kernels. Birkhoff's ideas have been further developed and used to establish various nonlinear generalizations of the Perron-Frobenius theorem, which have found significant uses in computer science, mathematical biology, game theory, dynamical systems theory, and ergodic theory.
- Generalizing earlier results of Anders Karlsson and Guennadi Noskov, Yves Benoist determined a system of necessary and sufficient conditions for a bounded convex domain in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, endowed with its Hilbert metric, to be a [Gromov hyperbolic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gromov_hyperbolic_space).
- C. Vernicos and C. Walsh showed that balls in the Hilbert Metric and asymptotic balls are approximately equivalent up to constant factors.
- C. Vernicos and C. Walsh, then expanded upon by [David Mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Mount) and Ahmed Abdelkader, showed that balls in the Hilbert Metric and [Macbeath regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeath_regions) are approximately equivalent up to constant factors.
