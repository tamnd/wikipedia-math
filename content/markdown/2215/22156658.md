# True arithmetic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="True arithmetic"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), **true arithmetic** is the set of all true [first-order](wikipedia://en/10983) statements about the [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474).[^o2] This is the theory [associated](wikipedia://en/3512103#Theories_associated_with_a_structure) with the [standard model](wikipedia://en/25005#Models) of the [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005) in the [language](wikipedia://en/7788156) of the first-order Peano axioms. True arithmetic is occasionally called Skolem arithmetic, though this term usually refers to the [different theory of natural numbers with multiplication](wikipedia://en/6694711).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The [signature](wikipedia://en/7788156) of [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic) includes the addition, multiplication, and successor function symbols, the equality and less-than relation symbols, and a constant symbol for 0. The (well-formed) formulas of the **language of first-order arithmetic** are built up from these symbols together with the logical symbols in the usual manner of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983).

The [structure](wikipedia://en/4055928) $\mathcal{N}$ is defined to be a model of Peano arithmetic as follows.

- The [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180) is the set $\mathbb{N}$ of natural numbers,
- The symbol 0 is interpreted as the number 0,
- The function symbols are interpreted as the usual arithmetical operations on $\mathbb{N}$,
- The equality and less-than relation symbols are interpreted as the usual equality and order relation on $\mathbb{N}$.

This structure is known as the [**standard model**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonstandard_arithmetic) or [intended interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intended_interpretation) of first-order arithmetic.

A [sentence](wikipedia://en/2129527) in the language of first-order arithmetic is said to be true in $\mathcal{N}$ if it is true in the structure just defined. The notation $\mathcal{N} \models \varphi$ is used to indicate that the sentence $\varphi$ is true in $\mathcal{N}.$

**True arithmetic** is defined to be the set of all sentences in the language of first-order arithmetic that are true in $\mathcal{N}$, written Th($\mathcal{N}$). This set is, equivalently, the (complete) theory of the structure $\mathcal{N}$.[^o5]

:::

## Arithmetic undefinability {#Arithmetic_undefinability}

The central result on true arithmetic is the [undefinability theorem](wikipedia://en/583785) of [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) (1936). It states that the set Th($\mathcal{N}$) is not arithmetically definable. This means that there is no formula $\varphi(x)$ in the language of first-order arithmetic such that, for every sentence *θ* in this language,

$$\mathcal{N} \models \theta \quad \text{if and only if} \quad \mathcal{N} \models \varphi(\underline{\#(\theta)}).$$

Here $\underline{\#(\theta)}$ is the numeral of the canonical [Gödel number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel_number) of the sentence *θ*.

[Post's theorem](wikipedia://en/764468) is a sharper version of the undefinability theorem that shows a relationship between the definability of Th($\mathcal{N}$) and the [Turing degrees](wikipedia://en/764405), using the [arithmetical hierarchy](wikipedia://en/186475). For each natural number *n*, let Th<sub>*n*</sub>($\mathcal{N}$) be the subset of Th($\mathcal{N}$) consisting of only sentences that are $\Sigma^0_n$ or lower in the arithmetical hierarchy. Post's theorem shows that, for each *n*, Th<sub>*n*</sub>($\mathcal{N}$) is arithmetically definable, but only by a formula of complexity higher than $\Sigma^0_n$. Thus no single formula can define Th($\mathcal{N}$), because

$$\mbox{Th}(\mathcal{N}) = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \mbox{Th}_n(\mathcal{N})$$

but no single formula can define Th<sub>*n*</sub>($\mathcal{N}$) for arbitrarily large *n*.

## Computability properties {#Computability_properties}

As discussed above, Th($\mathcal{N}$) is not arithmetically definable, by Tarski's theorem. A corollary of Post's theorem establishes that the [Turing degree](wikipedia://en/764405) of Th($\mathcal{N}$) is **0**<sup>(ω)</sup>, and so Th($\mathcal{N}$) is not [decidable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decidable_set) nor [recursively enumerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable_set).

Th($\mathcal{N}$) is closely related to the theory Th($\mathcal{R}$) of the [recursively enumerable Turing degrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable_Turing_degree), in the signature of [partial orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order).[^o8] In particular, there are computable functions *S* and *T* such that:

- For each sentence *φ* in the signature of first-order arithmetic, *φ* is in Th($\mathcal{N}$) if and only if *S*(*φ*) is in Th($\mathcal{R}$).
- For each sentence *ψ* in the signature of partial orders, *ψ* is in Th($\mathcal{R}$) if and only if *T*(*ψ*) is in Th($\mathcal{N}$).

## Model-theoretic properties {#Model-theoretic_properties}

True arithmetic is an [unstable theory](wikipedia://en/15828771), and so has $2^\kappa$ models for each uncountable cardinal $\kappa$. As there are [continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901) many [types](wikipedia://en/3741564) over the empty set, true arithmetic also has $2^{\aleph_0}$ countable models. Since the theory is [complete](wikipedia://en/10086335), all of its models are [elementarily equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementarily_equivalent).

## True theory of second-order arithmetic {#True_theory_of_second-order_arithmetic}

The true theory of second-order arithmetic consists of all the sentences in the language of [second-order arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3542454) that are satisfied by the standard model of second-order arithmetic, whose first-order part is the structure $\mathcal{N}$ and whose second-order part consists of every subset of $\mathbb{N}$.

The true theory of first-order arithmetic, Th($\mathcal{N}$), is a subset of the true theory of second-order arithmetic, and Th($\mathcal{N}$) is definable in second-order arithmetic. However, the generalization of Post's theorem to the [analytical hierarchy](wikipedia://en/634233) shows that the true theory of second-order arithmetic is not definable by any single formula in second-order arithmetic.

(Simpson 1977) has shown that the true theory of second-order arithmetic is computably interpretable with the theory of the partial order of all [Turing degrees](wikipedia://en/764405), in the signature of partial orders, and *vice versa*.
