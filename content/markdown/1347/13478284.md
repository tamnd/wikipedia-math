# Differentiation of integrals

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differentiation of integrals"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the problem of **differentiation of integrals** is that of determining under what circumstances the [mean value](wikipedia://en/60167) [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of a suitable [function](wikipedia://en/185427) on a small [neighbourhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood_%28topology%29) of a point approximates the value of the function at that point. More formally, given a space *X* with a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) *μ* and a [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) *d*, one asks for what functions *f* : *X* → **R** does

$$\lim_{r \to 0} \frac1{\mu \big( B_{r} (x) \big)} \int_{B_{r} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \mu(y) = f(x)$$

for all (or at least *μ*-[almost all](wikipedia://en/1309)) *x* ∈ *X*? (Here, as in the rest of the article, *B*<sub>*r*</sub>(*x*) denotes the [open ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) in *X* with *d*-[radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) *r* and centre *x*.) This is a natural question to ask, especially in view of the heuristic construction of the [Riemann integral](wikipedia://en/26390), in which it is almost implicit that *f*(*x*) is a "good representative" for the values of *f* near *x*.

:::

## Theorems on the differentiation of integrals {#Theorems_on_the_differentiation_of_integrals}

### Lebesgue measure {#Lebesgue_measure}

One result on the differentiation of integrals is the [Lebesgue differentiation theorem](wikipedia://en/5766547), as proved by [Henri Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/49514) in 1910. Consider *n*-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) *λ*<sup>*n*</sup> on *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. Then, for any [locally integrable function](wikipedia://en/4258398) *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R**, one has

$$\lim_{r \to 0} \frac1{\lambda^{n} \big( B_{r} (x) \big)} \int_{B_{r} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \lambda^{n} (y) = f(x)$$

for *λ*<sup>*n*</sup>-almost all points *x* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. The measure zero set of "bad" points depends on the function *f*.

### Borel measures on R<sup>*n*</sup> {#Borel_measures_on_Rn}

The result for Lebesgue measure turns out to be a special case of the following result, which is based on the [Besicovitch covering theorem](wikipedia://en/17743903): if *μ* is any [locally finite](wikipedia://en/6621919) [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> and *f* : **R**<sup>*n*</sup> → **R** is locally integrable with respect to *μ*, then

$$\lim_{r \to 0} \frac1{\mu \big( B_{r} (x) \big)} \int_{B_{r} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \mu (y) = f(x)$$

for *μ*-almost all points *x* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.

### Gaussian measures {#Gaussian_measures}

The problem of the differentiation of integrals is much harder in an infinite-dimensional setting. Consider a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) (*H*, ⟨ , ⟩) equipped with a [Gaussian measure](wikipedia://en/6572909) *γ*. As stated in the article on the [Vitali covering theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitali_covering_theorem), the Vitali covering theorem fails for Gaussian measures on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Two results of David Preiss (1981 and 1983) show the kind of difficulties that one can expect to encounter in this setting:

- There is a Gaussian measure *γ* on a separable Hilbert space *H* and a Borel set *M* ⊆ *H* so that, for *γ*-almost all *x* ∈ *H*, $$\lim_{r \to 0} \frac{\gamma \big( M \cap B_{r} (x) \big)}{\gamma \big( B_{r} (x) \big)} = 1.$$
- There is a Gaussian measure *γ* on a separable Hilbert space *H* and a function *f* ∈ *L*<sup>1</sup>(*H*, *γ*; **R**) such that $$\lim_{r \to 0} \inf \left\{ \left. \frac1{\gamma \big( B_{s} (x) \big)} \int_{B_{s} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \gamma(y) \right| x \in H, 0 < s < r \right\} = + \infty.$$

However, there is some hope if one has good control over the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) of *γ*. Let the covariance operator of *γ* be *S* : *H* → *H* given by

$$\langle Sx, y \rangle = \int_{H} \langle x, z \rangle \langle y, z \rangle \, \mathrm{d} \gamma(z),$$

or, for some [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) (*e*<sub>*i*</sub>)<sub>*i*∈**N**</sub> of *H*,

$$Sx = \sum_{i \in \mathbf{N}} \sigma_{i}^{2} \langle x, e_{i} \rangle e_{i}.$$

In 1981, Preiss and Jaroslav Tišer showed that if there exists a constant 0 \< *q* \< 1 such that

$$\sigma_{i + 1}^{2} \leq q \sigma_{i}^{2},$$

then, for all *f* ∈ *L*<sup>1</sup>(*H*, *γ*; **R**),

$$\frac1{\mu \big( B_{r} (x) \big)} \int_{B_{r} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \mu(y) \xrightarrow[r \to 0]{\gamma} f(x),$$

where the convergence is [convergence in measure](wikipedia://en/7996261) with respect to *γ*. In 1988, Tišer showed that if

$$\sigma_{i + 1}^{2} \leq \frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{i^{\alpha}}$$

for some *α* > 5 ⁄ 2, then

$$\frac1{\mu \big( B_{r} (x) \big)} \int_{B_{r} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \mu(y) \xrightarrow[r \to 0]{} f(x),$$

for *γ*-almost all *x* and all *f* ∈ *L*<sup>*p*</sup>(*H*, *γ*; **R**), *p* > 1.

As of 2007, it is still an open question whether there exists an infinite-dimensional Gaussian measure *γ* on a separable Hilbert space *H* so that, for all *f* ∈ *L*<sup>1</sup>(*H*, *γ*; **R**),

$$\lim_{r \to 0} \frac{1}{\gamma \big( B_{r} (x) \big)} \int_{B_{r} (x)} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \gamma(y) = f(x)$$

for *γ*-almost all *x* ∈ *H*. However, it is conjectured that no such measure exists, since the *σ*<sub>*i*</sub> would have to decay very rapidly.
