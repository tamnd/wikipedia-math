# Edmund Harriss

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Edmund Harriss"}

**Edmund Orme Harriss** (born 1976 in [Worcester, UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcester,_UK)) is a British mathematician,[^o2] writer[^o29] and artist.[^o3] Since 2010 he has been at the Fulbright College of Arts & Sciences at The [University of Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arkansas) in [Fayetteville, Arkansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fayetteville,_Arkansas) where he is an Assistant Professor of Arts & Sciences (ARSC) and Mathematical Sciences (MASC). He does research in the Geometry of [Tilings and Patterns](wikipedia://en/321671),[^o15] a branch of [Convex](wikipedia://en/4165882) and [Discrete](wikipedia://en/386468) Geometry.[^o4] He is the discoverer of the spiral that bears his name.[^o5]

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Harriss earned a [Master of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/2108291) at the [University of Warwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Warwick) (2000) and then obtained his PhD at [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London) (2003) with the dissertation "On Canonical Substitution Tilings" under Jeroen Lamb.

Harriss has been a speaker at [FSCONS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FSCONS), a Nordic Free software conference.[^o8]

Harriss is active on [Numberphile](wikipedia://en/61716514) where he has given talks on [Heesch numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heesch_number), [Tribonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribonacci_number), the [Rauzy fractal](wikipedia://en/29344658) and the [plastic ratio](wikipedia://en/2148532).[^o9]

In May and June 2020 Harriss was a visiting fellow at The Institute for Advanced Study of [Aix-Marseille University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-Marseille_University) (IMéRA) where he studied the possibilities of visual and spatial models and animations to illustrate a wide variety of mathematical ideas.[^o10]

## Mathematical art {#Mathematical_art}

*Image: MetalCurvahedraBall.jpg.* Sculpture made from flat materials using the curvahedra invented by Harris[^o12]

The [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229) gives the relationship between the curvature of a surface and the amount of turning as you traverse the surface’s boundary.[^o13] Harriss used this theorem to invent shapes called Curvahedra which were then incorporated into sculpture.[^o14] Scientists at MIT are investigating ways in which curvahedra may have applications in construction.[^o16]

Art and mathematics are intertwined in Harris's work.[^o15] He uses public art to demonstrate deep mathematical ideas[^o16] and his academic work frequently involves the visualization of mathematics.[^o17] Mathematically themed sculptures by Harriss have been installed at [Oklahoma State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oklahoma_State_University–Stillwater),[^o18] at the University of Arkansas,[^o19][^o20] and at [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London).[^o15]

Combining his interest in art and mathematical tilings he is one of 24 mathematicians and artists who make up the [Mathemalchemy](wikipedia://en/70985345) Team.[^o21][^o22]

## Harriss Spiral {#Harriss_Spiral}

*Image: Golden and Harriss Spiral Story 4.pdf.* The Harriss Spiral

Harriss noticed that the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) is just one example of a more general idea: In how many ways can a rectangle be divided into squares and rectangles? The golden ratio results when a rectangle is divided into a one square and one similar rectangle. But by varying the number of squares and sub-rectangles, we arrive at what Harriss calls "proportion systems". The solutions in all cases are algebraic numbers and the golden ratio is just one of them.[^o24]

"The golden ratio is this incredibly well-explored corner of a whole city,” he said. “I wanted to give signposts to other locations in that city."[^o5]

Harriss investigated the next simplest case, dividing a rectangle into one square and *two* similar rectangles. The ratio that emerged in this case is the so-called [plastic ratio](wikipedia://en/2148532).[^o25] The [golden spiral](wikipedia://en/219370) is closely related to the first case, dissection into one square and one similar rectangle. Harriss applied the same idea to this second case and discovered a new [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) [spiral](wikipedia://en/61563) related to the [plastic ratio](wikipedia://en/2148532) and since named after him.[^o5]

### Books {#Books}

Harriss has published several books designed to spread joy in mathematics.[^o28] The sales of his colouring books run well beyond 100,000.[^o29][^o30]

- (2015) *Snowflake Seashell Star: Colouring Adventures in Numberland* with Alex Bellos [^o31]

- (2016) *Visions of the Universe: A Coloring Journey Through Math's Great Mysteries*, with Alex Bellos
