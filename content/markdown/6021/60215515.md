# Researcher degrees of freedom

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Researcher degrees of freedom"}

**Researcher degrees of freedom** is a concept referring to the inherent flexibility involved in the process of designing and conducting a [scientific experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_experiment), and in analyzing its results.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

The term reflects the fact that researchers can choose between multiple ways of collecting and analyzing data, and these decisions can be made either arbitrarily or because they, unlike other possible choices, produce a positive and [statistically significant](wikipedia://en/160995) result.[^o8] The researcher degrees of freedom has positives such as affording the ability to look at nature from different angles, allowing new discoveries and hypotheses to be generated.[^o3][^o4][^o5] However, researcher degrees of freedom can lead to [data dredging](wikipedia://en/1311951) and other questionable research practices where the different interpretations and analyses are taken for granted.[^o6][^o7] Their widespread use represents an inherent methodological limitation in [scientific research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_research), and contributes to an inflated rate of [false-positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False-positive) findings.[^o8] They can also lead to overestimated [effect sizes](wikipedia://en/437276).[^o9]

Though the concept of researcher degrees of freedom has mainly been discussed in the context of [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology), it can affect any scientific discipline.[^o8][^o10] Like [publication bias](wikipedia://en/511115), the existence of researcher degrees of freedom has the potential to lead to an inflated degree of [funnel plot](wikipedia://en/15233213) asymmetry.[^o11] It is also a potential explanation for [p-hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-hacking), as researchers have so many degrees of freedom to draw on, especially in the social and behavioral sciences. [Multiverse analysis](wikipedia://en/73722175) is a method that helps bring these degrees of freedom to light. Studies with smaller [sample sizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size) are more susceptible to the biasing influence of researcher degrees of freedom.[^o12]

::: {#o14 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Steegen et al. (2016) showed how, starting from a single raw data set, applying different reasonable data processing decisions can give rise to a multitude of processed data sets (called the data multiverse), often leading to different statistical results.[^o15] Wicherts et al. (2016) provided a list of 34 degrees of freedom (DFs) researchers have when conducting psychological research. The DFs listed span every stage of the research process, from formulating a [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) to the reporting of results. They include conducting exploratory, hypothesis-free research, which the authors note "...pervades many of the researcher DFs that we describe below in the later phases of the study." Other DFs listed in this paper include the creation of multiple manipulated [independent variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable) and the measurement of additional variables that may be selected for analysis later on.[^o9]

:::
