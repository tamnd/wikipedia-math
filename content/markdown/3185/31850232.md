# Principle of marginality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Principle of marginality"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **principle of marginality**, sometimes called **hierarchical principle**, is the fact that the average (or [main](wikipedia://en/7042478)) effects of variables in an analysis are [marginal](wikipedia://en/506077) to their [interaction effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interaction_effect)—that is, the main effect of one [explanatory variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explanatory_variable) captures the effect of that variable averaged over all values of a second explanatory variable whose value influences the first variable's effect.[^o2] The principle of marginality implies that, in general, it is wrong to test, estimate, or interpret main effects of [explanatory variables](wikipedia://en/437701) where the variables interact or, similarly, to model interaction effects but delete main effects that are marginal to them.[^o3] While such models are interpretable, they lack applicability, as they ignore the dependence of a variable's effect upon another variable's value.

:::

[Nelder](wikipedia://en/5865261)[^o4] and Venables[^o5] have argued strongly for the importance of this principle in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997).

## Regression form {#Regression_form}

If two independent continuous variables, say *x* and *z*, both influence a [dependent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable) *y*, and if the extent of the effect of each independent variable depends on the level of the other independent variable then the regression equation can be written as:

$$y_i=a+bx_i+cz_i+d(x_iz_i)+ e_i,$$

where *i* indexes observations, *a* is the intercept term, *b*, *c*, and *d* are [effect size](wikipedia://en/437276) parameters to be estimated, and *e* is the [error](wikipedia://en/461509) term.

If this is the correct model, then the omission of any of the right-side terms would be incorrect, resulting in misleading interpretation of the regression results.

With this model, the effect of *x* upon *y* is given by the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) of *y* with respect to *x*; this is $b+dz_i$, which depends on the specific value $z_i$ at which the partial derivative is being evaluated. Hence, the main effect of *x* – the effect averaged over all values of *z* – is meaningless as it depends on the design of the experiment (specifically on the relative frequencies of the various values of *z*) and not just on the underlying relationships. Hence:

- In the case of interaction, it is wrong to try to test, estimate, or interpret a "main effect" coefficient *b* or *c*, omitting the interaction term.[^o7]

In addition:

- In the case of interaction, it is wrong to not include *b* or *c*, because this will give incorrect estimates of the interaction.[^o8][^o9]
