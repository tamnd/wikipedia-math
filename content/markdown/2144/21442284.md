# Emil Grosswald

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Emil Grosswald"}

**Emil Grosswald** (December 15, 1912 – April 11, 1989) was a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who worked primarily in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).

:::

## Life and education {#Life_and_education}

*Image: Fred van der Blij.jpg.* Emil Grosswald (right) and [Fred van der Blij](wikipedia://en/39394032) in 1968.

Grosswald was born on December 15, 1912, in [Bucharest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucharest), [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania). He received a [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree) in both [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) from the [University of Bucharest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bucharest) in 1933, spent six months in Italy and then received a Diplôme from [École supérieure d'électricité](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supélec) in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989).[^o4]

Grosswald was [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews). When war broke out, he fled from Paris in June, 1940 to the [University of Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Montpellier), where he began doctoral studies in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). He fled at the end of 1941, through Spain and [Lisbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisbon) to [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba). He moved to [Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico) in 1946 and then to the United States in 1948. He received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D.) under [Hans Rademacher](wikipedia://en/1864916) from the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) in 1950.[^o5] He was visiting professor at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) in 1964–1965 and one of his books, *The Theory of Numbers,* was written that year.

He met his wife Elisabeth (Lissy) Rosenthal in Cuba, probably in 1941 or 1942. They were married in 1950 in [Saskatoon, Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saskatoon,_Canada), where he had his first teaching position after receiving his Ph.D. They spent two years at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), in 1951 and 1959. During their first stay, they met [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736), with whom Emil had a correspondence, later bequeathed to the University of Texas, and formed many friendships, among others with the physicist [Freeman Dyson](wikipedia://en/11397).

Emil and Lissy had two daughters, Blanche, who became a professor of Social Work at [Rutgers University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutgers_University) but died in 2003 at the age of 50, and Vivian, a professor of law at the [University of Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pittsburgh). Vivian was decorated in 2007 by the [Republic of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria) for her work as the United States appointee to the Austrian General Settlement Fund Committee for Nazi-era property compensation, and in 2013 by the government of France for her services in promotion of the French language and culture in the United States. Emil is the uncle of [Pamela Ronald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamela_Ronald), a member of the National Academy of Sciences, whose father Robert Ronald (né Rosenthal) describes the family's escape from the Nazis in his memoir, "Last Train to Freedom". The son of Lissy's second cousin ([Ernest Beutler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Beutler)) is 2011 [Nobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize) Laureate [Bruce Beutler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Beutler). Emil was also the nephew of the French composer [Marcel Mihalovici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Mihalovici), who arrived in Paris in the 1920s with [Georges Enesco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Enesco).

After Grosswald's death, the American Mathematical Society held a national meeting in his honor, and in 1991 a Festschrift was published in his honor: "A Tribute to Emil Grosswald: Number Theory and Related Analysis." Of his attitude towards mathematics, [Marvin Knopp](wikipedia://en/34193073) noted the following: "In Grosswald's world, mathematics is challenge demanding dedication and long hours of work; it is science combined with art, truth with beauty. It is passionate and eternal pursuit of excellence. It is humility in the face of a powerful and proud history. Above all, it is meaning, a reason to go on..."[^o6] Mark Sheingorn wrote: "He seemed to know everything – indeed it seems to me he must always have known everything. He was supportive, enthusiastic, but also demanding ... The depth of his love for mathematics inspired us all to strive to do better."[^o6]

Grosswald died on April 11, 1989, in [Narberth, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narberth,_Pennsylvania).[^o4]

## Career {#Career}

Grosswald's first three scientific papers, written while he was in [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba), were published under the pseudonym E. G. Garnea.[^o8] He published articles in English, German, French, Spanish, and Italian.

After receiving his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in 1950, Grosswald taught at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) until, towards the end of his life, he moved to [Temple University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_University) to help build its graduate department in mathematics. He also held positions at the [University of Saskatchewan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Saskatchewan) (1950), [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) (1951 and 1959), the [Technion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technion) (1980–1981), [Swarthmore College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarthmore_College) (1982), and the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) (Institut Marie Curie).

Grosswald completed some works of his teacher [Hans Rademacher](wikipedia://en/1864916), who died in 1969. Rademacher had prepared notes for an [Earle Raymond Hedrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earle_Raymond_Hedrick) Lecture in [Boulder, Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulder,_Colorado), in 1963 on [Dedekind sums](wikipedia://en/1122242), but fell ill, and Grosswald gave the lecture for him.[^o9] After Rademacher's death, Grosswald edited and completed the notes and published them in the [Carus Mathematical Monographs](wikipedia://en/2941952) series as *Dedekind Sums*.[^o10] He also edited for publication Rademacher's posthumous textbook *Topics in Analytic Number Theory*.[^o4] He published numerous other books and countless articles. Together with Franz Josef Schnitzer he proved the [Grosswald–Schnitzer theorem](wikipedia://en/80475043) on the existence of classes of modified [Zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta_function) that share the same non-trivial zeros as the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).

Grosswald was elected to the Board of Governors of the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) for 1965–1968.[^o11] [Temple University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_University)'s Mathematics Department annually sponsors the Emil Grosswald Memorial Lectures.[^o12]
