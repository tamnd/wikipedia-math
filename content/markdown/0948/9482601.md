# Liouville's theorem (differential algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Liouville's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Liouville's theorem**, originally formulated by French mathematician [Joseph Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810) in 1833 to 1841,[^o2][^o3][^o4] places an important restriction on [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) that can be expressed as [elementary functions](wikipedia://en/10412).

:::

The antiderivatives of certain [elementary functions](wikipedia://en/10412) cannot themselves be expressed as elementary functions. These are called [nonelementary antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/1520379). A standard example of such a function is $e^{-x^2},$ whose antiderivative is (with a multiplier of a constant) the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111), familiar in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685). Other examples include the functions $\frac{\sin (x)}{x}$ and $x^x.$

Liouville's theorem states that if an elementary function has an elementary antiderivative, then the antiderivative can be expressed only using logarithms and functions that are involved, in some sense, in the original elementary function. An example is the antiderivative of $\sec x$ is $\log|\sec x + \tan x|$, which uses only logarithms and [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367). More precisely, Liouville's theorem states that elementary antiderivatives, if they exist, are in the same [differential field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_field) as the function, plus possibly a finite number of applications of the logarithm function.

The Liouville theorem is a precursor to the [Risch algorithm](wikipedia://en/289450), which relies on the Liouville theorem to find any elementary antiderivative.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

For any [differential field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_field) $F,$ the **[Constants of a differential field](wikipedia://en/48779551)** of $F$ is the subfield

$$\operatorname{Con}(F) = \{ f \in F : D f = 0\}.$$

Given two differential fields $F$ and $G,$ $G$ is called a **logarithmic extension** of $F$ if $G$ is a [simple](wikipedia://en/4771726) [transcendental extension](wikipedia://en/287364) of $F$ (that is, $G = F(t)$ for some [transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_element) $t$) such that

$$D t = \frac{D s}{s} \quad \text{ for some } s \in F.$$

This has the form of a [logarithmic derivative](wikipedia://en/470327). Intuitively, one may think of $t$ as the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of some element $s$ of $F,$ in which case, this condition is analogous to the ordinary [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113). However, $F$ is not necessarily equipped with a unique logarithm; one might adjoin many "logarithm-like" extensions to $F.$ Similarly, an **exponential extension** is a simple transcendental extension that satisfies

$$\frac{D t}{t} = D s \quad \text{ for some } s \in F.$$

With the above caveat in mind, this element may be thought of as an exponential of an element $s$ of $F.$ Finally, $G$ is called an **elementary differential extension** of $F$ if there is a finite chain of [subfields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subfield_%28mathematics%29) from $F$ to $G$ where each [extension](wikipedia://en/11634) in the chain is either algebraic, logarithmic, or exponential.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Basic theorem"}

## Basic theorem {#Basic_theorem}

Suppose $F$ and $G$ are differential fields with $\operatorname{Con}(F) = \operatorname{Con}(G),$ and that $G$ is an elementary differential extension of $F.$ Suppose $f \in F$ and $g \in G$ satisfy $D g = f$ (in words, suppose that $G$ contains an antiderivative of $f$). Then there exist $c_1, \ldots, c_n \in \operatorname{Con}(F)$ and $f_1, \ldots, f_n, s \in F$ such that

$$f = c_1 \frac{D f_1}{f_1} + \dotsb + c_n \frac{D f_n}{f_n} + D s.$$

In other words, the only functions that have "elementary antiderivatives" (that is, antiderivatives living in, at worst, an elementary differential extension of $F$) are those with this form. Thus, on an intuitive level, the theorem states that the only elementary antiderivatives are the "simple" functions plus a finite number of logarithms of "simple" functions.

A proof of Liouville's theorem can be found in section 12.4 of Geddes, et al.[^o9] See Lützen's scientific bibliography for a sketch of Liouville's original proof [^o10] (Chapter IX. Integration in Finite Terms), its modern exposition and algebraic treatment (ibid. §61).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

As an example, the field $F := \mathbb{C}(x)$ of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in a single variable has a derivation given by the standard [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) with respect to that variable. The constants of this field are just the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C};$ that is, $\operatorname{Con}(\mathbb{C}(x)) = \mathbb{C},$

The function $f := \tfrac{1}{x},$ which exists in $\mathbb{C}(x),$ does not have an antiderivative in $\mathbb{C}(x).$ Its antiderivatives $\ln x + C$ do, however, exist in the logarithmic extension $\mathbb{C}(x, \ln x).$

Likewise, the function $\tfrac{1}{x^2+1}$ does not have an antiderivative in $\mathbb{C}(x).$ Its antiderivatives $\tan^{-1}(x) + C$ do not seem to satisfy the requirements of the theorem, since they are not (apparently) sums of rational functions and logarithms of rational functions. However, a calculation with [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613) $e^{i \theta} = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta$ shows that in fact the antiderivatives can be written in the required manner (as logarithms of rational functions).

$$\begin{aligned} e^{2i \theta} & = \frac{e^{i \theta}}{e^{-i \theta}} = \frac{\cos \theta + i \sin \theta}{\cos \theta - i \sin \theta} = \frac{1 + i \tan \theta}{1 - i \tan \theta} \\ \theta & = \frac{1}{2i} \ln \left(\frac{1 + i \tan \theta}{1 - i \tan \theta}\right) \\ \tan^{-1} x & = \frac{1}{2i} \ln \left(\frac{1+ix}{1-ix}\right) \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Relationship with differential Galois theory {#Relationship_with_differential_Galois_theory}

Liouville's theorem is sometimes presented as a theorem in [differential Galois theory](wikipedia://en/833506), but this is not strictly true. The theorem can be proved without any use of [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316). Furthermore, the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of a simple antiderivative is either trivial (if no field extension is required to express it), or is simply the additive group of the constants (corresponding to the [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939)). Thus, an antiderivative's [differential Galois group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_Galois_group) does not encode enough information to determine if it can be expressed using elementary functions, the major condition of Liouville's theorem.
