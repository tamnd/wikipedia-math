# Direction (geometry)

*Image: Like or Parallel vector .jpg.* Three line segments with the same direction

*Image: 2D Direction Vectors.svg.* Examples of two 2D direction vectors

*Image: QF A380 and NZ A320 SODPROPS Sydney Airport.jpg.* Two airplanes in nearly [parallel (and opposite) directions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_opposite_direction_parallel_runway_operations)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Direction"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **direction**, also known as **spatial direction**, **vector direction** or **relative direction**, is the common characteristic of all [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) which coincide when [translated](wikipedia://en/204682) to share a common endpoint; equivalently, it is the common characteristic of [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) (such as the [relative position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_position) between a pair of points) which can be made equal by [scaling](wikipedia://en/651752) (by some positive [scalar multiplier](wikipedia://en/244373)).

:::

Two vectors sharing the same direction are said to be **codirectional** or **equidirectional**.[^o2] All co[directional line segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directional_line_segment) sharing the same size (length) are said to be *[equipollent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equipollent_%28geometry%29)*. Two equipollent segments are not necessarily coincident; for example, a given direction can be evaluated at different starting [positions](wikipedia://en/1345771), defining different unit directed line segments (as a [bound vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bound_vector) instead of a [free vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_vector)). Two *[colinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colinear)* rays or oriented line segments (sharing the same supporting line) are not necessarily codirectional and vice versa.

A direction is often represented as a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053), the result of dividing a vector by its length. A direction can alternately be represented by a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) on a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) or [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), the [intersection](wikipedia://en/41604289) between the sphere and a ray in that direction emanating from the sphere's center; the tips of unit vectors emanating from a common [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) point lie on the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090).

A *two-dimensional direction* (*2D direction*), in [2D space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2D_space), can be represented by its [angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_%28geometry%29), measured from some reference direction, the angular component of [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) (ignoring or normalizing the polar radius). A *three-dimensional direction* (*3D direction*), in [3D space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_space), can be represented using two angles, the angular components of [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates): a polar angle relative to a fixed polar axis and an azimuthal angle about the polar axis.

An arbitrary direction can also be specified in a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), defined in terms of mutually orthogonal [coordinate axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_axes). Any arbitrary direction can be represented numerically by finding the [direction cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direction_cosines) (a list of [cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) of the angles), which are equivalent to the Cartesian coordinates of the associated unit vector.

A direction is used to represent linear objects such as [axes of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_rotation) and [normal vectors](wikipedia://en/173224). A direction may be used as part of the representation of a more complicated [object](wikipedia://en/455769)'s [attitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attitude_%28geometry%29) or orientation in [physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_space), which may be specified by means of three angles – two angles for specifying the 3D direction and a third one for a possible [axial rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_rotation) – or other equivalent set of three parameters (see [Rotation formulations in three dimensions](wikipedia://en/7290730)).

Two directions are said to be **opposite** if the unit vectors representing them are [additive inverses](wikipedia://en/228312), or if the points on a sphere representing them are [antipodal](wikipedia://en/367577), at the two opposite ends of a common diameter. The combination of a given direction and its corresponding opposite direction forms an undirected ["line"](wikipedia://en/946975), sometimes called the **direction line**[^o3] or an **orientation**, e.g., the east-west orientation supporting both east direction and west direction.[^o4]

Two directions are **parallel** (as in [parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines)) if they can be brought to lie on the same straight line without rotations; parallel directions are either codirectional or opposite.[^o2] Two directions are **obtuse** or **acute** if they form, respectively, an [obtuse angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obtuse_angle) (greater than a right angle) or [acute angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_angle) (smaller than a right angle); equivalently, obtuse directions and acute directions have, respectively, negative and positive [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product) (or [scalar projection](wikipedia://en/1508518)).

Non-oriented straight lines or [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) can also be considered to have a "direction", the common characteristic of all [parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines), which can be made to coincide by translation to pass through a common point. The direction of a non-oriented line in a two-dimensional plane can be represented numerically by its [slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_%28geometry%29) with respect to a reference axis. However, the "direction" of a non-oriented line corresponds to two opposite directions of coincident oriented lines.
