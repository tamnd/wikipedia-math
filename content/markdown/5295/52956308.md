# Fluxion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fluxion"}

*Image: The method of fluxions and infinite series p.20.gif.* Newton's introduction of the notions *"fluent"* and *"fluxion"* in his 1736 book

A **fluxion** is the [instantaneous rate of change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instantaneous_rate_of_change), or [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461), of a [fluent](wikipedia://en/53398219) (a time-varying quantity, or [function](wikipedia://en/185427)) at a given point.[^o2] Fluxions were introduced by [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) to describe his form of a [time derivative](wikipedia://en/1783069) (a [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) with respect to time). Newton introduced the concept in 1665 and detailed them in his [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) treatise, *[Method of Fluxions](wikipedia://en/252953)*. Fluxions and fluents made up Newton's early [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176).[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

If the fluent $y$ is defined as $y=t^2$ (where $t$ is time) the fluxion (derivative) at $t=2$ is:

$$\dot y = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} = \frac{(2+o)^2-2^2}{(2+o)-2} = \frac{4+4o+o^2-4}{2+o-2} = \frac{4o+o^2}{o}$$

Here $o$ is an [infinitely small](wikipedia://en/160990) amount of time.[^o7] So, the term $o^2$ is second order infinite small term and according to Newton, we can now ignore $o^2$ because of its second order infinite smallness comparing to first order infinite smallness of $o$.[^o8] So, the final equation gets the form:

$$\dot y = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}=\frac{4o}{o}=4$$

He justified the use of $o$ as a non-zero quantity by stating that fluxions were a consequence of movement by an object.

:::

## Criticism {#Criticism}

Bishop [George Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berkeley), a prominent [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) of the time, denounced Newton's fluxions in his essay *[The Analyst](wikipedia://en/341394)*, published in 1734.[^o10] Berkeley refused to believe that they were accurate because of the use of the [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) $o$. He did not believe it could be ignored and pointed out that if it was zero, the consequence would be [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663). Berkeley referred to them as "ghosts of departed quantities", a statement which unnerved mathematicians of the time and led to the eventual disuse of infinitesimals in calculus.

Towards the end of his life Newton revised his interpretation of $o$ as [infinitely small](wikipedia://en/160990), preferring to define it as approaching [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero), using a similar definition to the concept of [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602).[^o11] He believed this put fluxions back on safe ground. By this time, Leibniz's derivative (and his [notation](wikipedia://en/410712)) had largely replaced Newton's fluxions and fluents, and remains in use today.
