# Averaging argument

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Averaging argument"}

In [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543) and [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), **averaging argument** is a standard argument for proving theorems. It usually allows us to convert [probabilistic](wikipedia://en/495383) [polynomial-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial-time) algorithms into [non-uniform polynomial-size circuits](wikipedia://en/7641969).

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

::: {#o4 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example:** If every person likes at least 1/3 of the books in a library, then the library has a book, which at least 1/3 of people like.

:::

::: {#o5 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** Suppose there are $N$ people and $B$ books. Each person likes at least $B/3$ of the books. Let people leave a mark on the book they like. Then, there will be at least $M=(NB)/3$ marks. The averaging argument claims that there exists a book with at least $N/3$ marks on it. Assume, to the contradiction, that no such book exists. Then, every book has fewer than $N/3$ marks. However, since there are $B$ books, the total number of marks will be fewer than $(NB)/3$, contradicting the fact that there are at least $M$ marks. $\scriptstyle\blacksquare$

:::

:::

## Formalized definition of averaging argument {#Formalized_definition_of_averaging_argument}

Let *X* and *Y* be sets, let *p* be a [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29) on *X* × *Y* and let *f* be a real number in the interval \[0, 1\]. If for each *x* in *X* and at least *f |Y|* of the elements *y* in *Y* satisfy *p*(*x*, *y*), then there exists a *y* in *Y* such that there exist at least *f |X|* elements *x* in *X* that satisfy *p*(*x*, *y*).

There is another definition, defined using the terminology of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542).[^o7]

Let $f$ be some function. The averaging argument is the following claim: if we have a circuit $C$ such that $C(x, y) = f(x)$ with probability at least $\rho$, where $x$ is chosen at random and $y$ is chosen independently from some [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) $Y$ over $\{0, 1\}^m$ (which might not even be [efficiently sampleable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficiently_sampleable)) then there exists a **single** string $y_0 \in \{0, 1\}^m$ such that $\Pr_x[C(x, y_0) = f(x)] \ge \rho$.

Indeed, for every $y$ define $p_y$ to be $\Pr_x[C(x, y) = f(x)]$ then

$$\Pr_{x,y}[C(x, y) = f(x)] = E_y[p_y] \,$$

and then this reduces to the claim that for every random variable $Z$, if $E[Z] \ge \rho$ then $\Pr[Z \ge \rho] > 0$ (this holds since $E[Z]$ is the weighted average of $Z$ and clearly if the average of some values is at least $\rho$ then one of the values must be at least $\rho$).

::: {#o9 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

## Application {#Application}

This argument has wide use in [complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543) (e.g. proving $\mathsf{BPP}\subsetneq\mathsf{P/poly}$) and [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) (e.g. proving that [indistinguishable encryption](wikipedia://en/2187252) results in [semantic security](wikipedia://en/960585)). A plethora of such applications can be found in [Goldreich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oded_Goldreich)'s books.[^o10][^o11][^o12]

:::
