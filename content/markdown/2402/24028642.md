# Symbol (formal)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symbol"}

*Image: Formal languages.svg.* This diagram shows the [syntactic entities](wikipedia://en/546039) that may be constructed from [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939). The symbols and [strings of symbols](wikipedia://en/27701) may be broadly divided into [nonsense](wikipedia://en/387403) and well-formed formulas. A formal language can be thought of as identical to the set of its well-formed formulas. The set of well-formed formulas may be broadly divided into [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977) and non-theorems.

A **formal symbol** is a fundamental [concept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept) in [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [tokens](wikipedia://en/14934822) of which may be marks or a configuration of marks which form a particular pattern. Although the term *symbol* in common use sometimes refers to the idea being symbolized, and at other times to the marks on a piece of paper or chalkboard which are being used to express that idea; in the [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939) studied in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), the term *symbol* refers to the idea, and the marks are considered to be a [token](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-token_distinction) instance of the symbol, according to the [semiotic theory of Charles Sanders Peirce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotic_theory_of_Charles_Sanders_Peirce).[^o2] In logic, symbols build literal utility to illustrate ideas.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Symbols of a formal language need not be symbols *of* anything. For instance there are [logical constants](wikipedia://en/4089175) which do not refer to any idea, but rather serve as a form of punctuation in the language (e.g. parentheses). Symbols of a formal language must be capable of being specified without any reference to any [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671) of them.

A symbol or [string](wikipedia://en/27701) of symbols may comprise a [well-formed formula](wikipedia://en/404582) if it is consistent with the [formation rules](wikipedia://en/15115411) of the language.

In a [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102) a symbol may be used as a token in formal operations. The set of formal symbols in a [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939) is referred to as an alphabet (hence each symbol may be referred to as a "letter")[^o4]

A formal symbol as used in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) may be a variable (member from a [universe of discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe_of_discourse)), a constant, a function (mapping to another member of universe) or a [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29) (mapping to T/F).

Formal symbols are usually thought of as purely [syntactic](wikipedia://en/546039) structures, composed into larger structures using a [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716), though sometimes they may be associated with an interpretation or model (a [formal semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_semantics_%28logic%29)).

## Words modeled as formal symbols {#Words_modeled_as_formal_symbols}

The move to view units in natural language (e.g. English) as formal symbols was initiated by [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky) (it was this work that resulted in the [Chomsky hierarchy](wikipedia://en/6011) in formal languages). The [generative grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar) model looked upon syntax as autonomous from semantics. Building on these models, the logician [Richard Montague](wikipedia://en/610182) proposed that semantics could also be constructed on top of the formal structure:

There is in my opinion no important theoretical difference between natural languages and the artificial languages of logicians; indeed, I consider it possible to comprehend the syntax and semantics of both kinds of language within a single natural and mathematically precise theory. On this point I differ from a number of philosophers, but agree, I believe, with Chomsky and his associates."[^o6]

This is the philosophical premise underlying [Montague grammar](wikipedia://en/709308).

However, this attempt to equate linguistic symbols with formal symbols has been challenged widely, particularly in the tradition of [cognitive linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_linguistics), by philosophers like [Stevan Harnad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stevan_Harnad), and linguists like [George Lakoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Lakoff) and [Ronald Langacker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Langacker).
