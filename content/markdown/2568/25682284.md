# Mills ratio

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mills ratio"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **Mills ratio** (or **Mills's ratio**[^o19]) of a [continuous random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_random_variable#Continuous_probability_distribution) $X$ is the function

:::

$$m(x) := \frac{\bar{F}(x)}{f(x)} ,$$

where $f(x)$ is the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487), and

$$\bar{F}(x) := \Pr[X>x] = \int_x^{+\infty} f(u)\, du$$

is the [complementary cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793#Derived_functions) (also called [survival function](wikipedia://en/30876757)). The concept is named after [John P. Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_P._Mills).[^o2] The Mills ratio is related to the [hazard rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_rate) *h*(*x*) which is defined as[^o21]

$$h(x):=\lim_{\delta\to 0} \frac{1}{\delta}\Pr[x < X \leq x + \delta | X > x]$$

by

$$m(x) = \frac{1}{h(x)}.$$

## Upper and lower bounds {#Upper_and_lower_bounds}

When $X$ has a standard normal distribution then the following bounds hold for $x>0$:

$\frac{x}{x^2 + 1} < m(x) < \frac{1}{x}$[^o4][^o5]

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

If $X$ has [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution) then

$$m(x) \sim 1/x , \,$$

where the sign $\sim$ means that the quotient of the two functions converges to 1 as $x\to+\infty$, see [Q-function](wikipedia://en/14225958) for details. More precise asymptotics can be given.[^o20]

:::

## Inverse Mills ratio {#Inverse_Mills_ratio}

The **inverse Mills ratio** is the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) to the [complementary cumulative distribution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_cumulative_distribution_function) of a distribution. Its use is often motivated by the following property of the [truncated](wikipedia://en/18374571) [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). If *X* is a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) having a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) with mean *μ* and variance *σ*<sup>2</sup>, then

$$\begin{aligned} & \operatorname{E}[\,X\,|\ X > \alpha \,] = \mu + \sigma \frac {\phi\big(\tfrac{\alpha-\mu}{\sigma}\big)}{1-\Phi\big(\tfrac{\alpha-\mu}{\sigma}\big)}, \\ & \operatorname{E}[\,X\,|\ X < \alpha \,] = \mu - \sigma \frac {\phi\big(\tfrac{\alpha-\mu}{\sigma}\big)}{\Phi\big(\tfrac{\alpha-\mu}{\sigma}\big)}, \end{aligned}$$

where $\alpha$ is a constant, $\phi$ denotes the standard normal density function, and $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The two fractions are the inverse Mills ratios.[^o10]

### Use in regression {#Use_in_regression}

A common application of the inverse Mills ratio (sometimes also called “non-selection hazard”) arises in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) to take account of a possible [selection bias](wikipedia://en/394392). If a dependent variable is [censored](wikipedia://en/11548952) (i.e., not for all observations a positive outcome is observed) it causes a concentration of observations at zero values. This problem was first acknowledged by Tobin (1958), who showed that if this is not taken into consideration in the estimation procedure, an [ordinary least squares](wikipedia://en/1651906) estimation will produce [biased](wikipedia://en/184099) parameter estimates.[^o12] With censored dependent variables there is a violation of the [Gauss–Markov](wikipedia://en/170353) assumption of zero [correlation](wikipedia://en/157057) between independent variables and the [error term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics).[^o13]

[James Heckman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Heckman) proposed a [two-stage estimation procedure](wikipedia://en/16728089) using the inverse Mills ratio to correct for the selection bias.[^o15][^o14] In a first step, a regression for observing a positive outcome of the dependent variable is modeled with a [probit](wikipedia://en/1689101) model. The inverse Mills ratio must be generated from the estimation of a [probit model](wikipedia://en/1611247), a [logit](wikipedia://en/172068) cannot be used. The [probit model](wikipedia://en/1611247) assumes that the error term follows a [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution).[^o15] The estimated parameters are used to calculate the inverse Mills ratio, which is then included as an additional explanatory variable in the OLS estimation.[^o16]
