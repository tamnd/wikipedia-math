# Third derivative

Differential

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Third derivative"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **third derivative** or **third-order derivative** is the rate at which the [second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219), or the rate of change of the rate of change, is changing. The third derivative of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $y = f(x)$ can be denoted by

:::

$$\frac{d^3y}{dx^3},\quad f'''(x),\quad\text{or }\frac{d^3}{dx^3}[f(x)].$$

Other [notations for differentiation](wikipedia://en/10265555) can be used, but the above are the most common.

## Mathematical definitions {#Mathematical_definitions}

Let $f(x) = x^4$. Then $f'(x) = 4x^3$ and $f''(x) = 12x^2$. Therefore, the third derivative of *f* is, in this case,

$$f'''(x) = 24x$$

or, using [Leibniz notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_notation),

$$\frac{d^3}{dx^3}[x^4] = 24x.$$

Now for a more general definition. Let *f* be any function of *x* such that *f* ′′ is [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable). Then the third derivative of *f* is given by

$$\frac{d^3}{dx^3}[f(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}[f''(x)].$$

The third derivative is the rate at which the [second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219) (*f*′′(*x*)) is changing.

## Applications in geometry {#Applications_in_geometry}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the [torsion of a curve](wikipedia://en/3039281) — a fundamental property of curves in three dimensions — is computed using third derivatives of coordinate functions (or the position vector) describing the curve.[^o4]

## Applications in physics {#Applications_in_physics}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), particularly [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914), **jerk** is defined as the third derivative of the [position function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_function) of an object. It is, essentially, the rate at which [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) changes. In mathematical terms:

$$\mathbf{j}(t)=\frac{d^3\mathbf{r}}{dt^3}$$

where **j**(*t*) is the jerk function with respect to time, and **r**(*t*) is the position function of the object with respect to time.

## Examples in Economics {#Examples_in_Economics}

When campaigning for a second term in office, U.S. President [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixon) announced that the rate of increase of inflation was decreasing, which has been noted as "the first time a sitting president used the third derivative to advance his case for reelection."[^o7] Since [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation) is itself a derivative—the rate at which the purchasing power of money decreases—then the rate of increase of inflation is the derivative of inflation, opposite in sign to the second time derivative of the purchasing power of money. Stating that a function is [decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decreasing_function) is equivalent to stating that its derivative is negative, so Nixon's statement is that the second derivative of inflation is negative, and so the third derivative of purchasing power is positive.

Since Nixon's statement allowed for the rate of inflation to increase, his statement did not necessarily indicate immediate price stability but proposed a trend of more stability in the future.
