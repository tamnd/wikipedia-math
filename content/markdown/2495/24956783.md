# Matching polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matching polynomial"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) fields of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), a **matching polynomial** (sometimes called an **acyclic polynomial**) is a [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) of the numbers of [matchings](wikipedia://en/581797) of various sizes in a graph. It is one of several [graph polynomials](wikipedia://en/56012962) studied in [algebraic graph theory](wikipedia://en/1620216).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Several different types of matching polynomials have been defined. Let *G* be a graph with *n* vertices and let *m<sub>k</sub>* be the number of *k*-edge matchings.

One matching polynomial of *G* is

$$m_G(x) := \sum_{k\geq0} m_k x^k.$$

Another definition gives the matching polynomial as

$$M_G(x) := \sum_{k\geq0} (-1)^k m_k x^{n-2k}.$$

A third definition is the polynomial

$$\mu_G(x,y) := \sum_{k\geq0} m_k x^k y^{n-2k}.$$

Each type has its uses, and all are equivalent by simple transformations. For instance,

$$M_G(x) = x^n m_G(-x^{-2})$$

and

$$\mu_G(x,y) = y^n m_G(x/y^2).$$

:::

## Connections to other polynomials {#Connections_to_other_polynomials}

The first type of matching polynomial is a direct generalization of the [rook polynomial](wikipedia://en/14031635).

The second type of matching polynomial has remarkable connections with [orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718). For instance, if *G* = *K*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub>, the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320), then the second type of matching polynomial is related to the generalized [Laguerre polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laguerre_polynomial) *L*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>*α*</sup>(*x*) by the identity:

$$M_{K_{m,n}}(x) = n! L_n^{(m-n)}(x^2).$$

If *G* is the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) *K*<sub>*n*</sub>, then *M*<sub>*G*</sub>(*x*) is an Hermite polynomial:

$$M_{K_n}(x) = H_n(x),$$

where *H*<sub>*n*</sub>(*x*) is the "probabilist's Hermite polynomial" (1) in the definition of [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial). These facts were observed by (Godsil 1981).

If *G* is a [forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_%28graph_theory%29), then its matching polynomial is equal to the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of its [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463).

If *G* is a [path](wikipedia://en/638889) or a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609), then *M*<sub>*G*</sub>(*x*) is a [Chebyshev polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev_polynomial). In this case μ<sub>*G*</sub>(1,*x*) is a [Fibonacci polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_polynomial) or [Lucas polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucas_polynomial) respectively.

## Complementation {#Complementation}

The matching polynomial of a graph *G* with *n* vertices is related to that of its complement by a pair of (equivalent) formulas. One of them is a simple combinatorial identity due to (Zaslavsky 1981). The other is an integral identity due to (Godsil 1981).

There is a similar relation for a subgraph *G* of *K*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub> and its complement in *K*<sub>*m*,*n*</sub>. This relation, due to Riordan (1958), was known in the context of non-attacking rook placements and rook polynomials.

## Applications in chemical informatics {#Applications_in_chemical_informatics}

The [Hosoya index](wikipedia://en/14221581) of a graph *G*, its number of matchings, is used in [chemoinformatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemoinformatics) as a structural descriptor of a molecular graph. It may be evaluated as *m*<sub>*G*</sub>(1) (Gutman 1991).

The third type of matching polynomial was introduced by (Farrell 1980) as a version of the "acyclic polynomial" used in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry).

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

On arbitrary graphs, or even [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), computing the matching polynomial is [\#P-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-P-complete) (Jerrum 1987). However, it can be computed more efficiently when additional structure about the graph is known. In particular, computing the matching polynomial on *n*-vertex graphs of [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086) *k* is [fixed-parameter tractable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-parameter_tractability): there exists an algorithm whose running time, for any fixed constant *k*, is a [polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) in *n* with an exponent that does not depend on *k* (Courcelle Makowsky Rotics 2001). The matching polynomial of a graph with *n* vertices and [clique-width](wikipedia://en/16795502) *k* may be computed in time *n*<sup>O(*k*)</sup> (Makowsky Rotics Averbouch Godlin 2006).
