# Convergence proof techniques

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Convergence proof techniques"}

**Convergence proof techniques** are canonical patterns of mathematical proofs that [sequences](wikipedia://en/285773) or [functions](wikipedia://en/285759) converge to a finite [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) when the argument tends to infinity.

:::

There are many types of sequences and [modes of convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modes_of_convergence_%28annotated_index%29), and different proof techniques may be more appropriate than others for proving each type of convergence of each type of sequence. Below are some of the more common and typical examples. This article is intended as an introduction aimed to help practitioners explore appropriate techniques. The links below give details of necessary conditions and generalizations to more abstract settings. Proof techniques for the convergence of [series](wikipedia://en/15287), a particular type of sequences corresponding to sums of many terms, are covered in the article on [convergence tests](wikipedia://en/4576951).

## Convergence in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> {#Convergence_in_Rn}

It is common to want to prove convergence of a sequence $f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ or function $f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$, where $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ refer to the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) and the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), respectively, and convergence is with respect to the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm), $||\cdot||_2$.

Useful approaches for this are as follows.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="First principles"}

### First principles {#First_principles}

The [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_%28mathematics%29) [definition](wikipedia://en/285759#Limits_at_infinity) of convergence of $f$ to a limit $f_{\infty}$ is that[^o5] for all $\epsilon$ there exists a $k_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such for all $k > k_0$, $\|f(k) - f_{\infty}\| < \epsilon$. The most direct proof technique from this definition is to find such a $k_0$ and prove the required inequality. If the value of $f_{\infty}$ is not known in advance, the techniques below may be useful.

:::

### Contraction mappings {#Contraction_mappings}

In many cases, the function whose convergence is of interest has the form $f(k+1) = T(f(k))$ for some transformation $T$. For example, $T$ could map $f(k)$ to $f(k+1)=A f(k)$ for some [conformable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformable) [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$, so that $f(k)=A^k f(0)$, a matrix generalization of the [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670). Alternatively, $T$ may be an elementwise operation, such as replacing each element of $f(k)$ by the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of its magnitude.

In such cases, if the problem satisfies the conditions of [Banach fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/46676) (the domain is a [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832)) then it is sufficient to prove convergence to prove that $T$ is a [contraction mapping](wikipedia://en/6239) to prove that it has a fixed point. This requires that$\|T(x) - T(y)\| < \| \lambda (x - y)\|$ for some constant $|\lambda| < 1$ which is fixed for all $x$ and $y$. The composition of two contraction mappings is a contraction mapping, so if $T = T_1 \circ T_2$, then it is sufficient to show that $T_1$ and $T_2$ are both contraction mappings.

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

#### Example {#Example}

Famous examples of applications of this approach include

- If $T$ has the form $T(x) = Ax + B$ for some matrices $A$ and $B$, then $T^k(x)$ converges to $(I-A)^{-1}B$ if the magnitudes of all eigenvalues of $A$ are less than 1.

:::

#### Non-expansion mappings {#Non-expansion_mappings}

If both above inequalities in the definition of a contraction mapping are weakened from "strictly less than" to "less than or equal to", the mapping is a non-expansion mapping. It is not sufficient to prove convergence to prove that $T$ is a non-expansion mapping. For example, $T(x) = -x$ is a non-expansion mapping, but the sequence $T^n(x)$ does not converge for any $x \neq 0$. However, the composition of a contraction mapping and a non-expansion mapping (or vice versa) is a contraction mapping.

#### Contraction mappings on limited domains {#Contraction_mappings_on_limited_domains}

If $T$ is not a contraction mapping on its entire domain, but it is on its [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) (the image of the domain), that is also sufficient for convergence. This also applies for decompositions. For example, consider $T(x) = \cos(\sin(x))$. The function $\cos$ is not a contraction mapping, but it is on the restricted domain $[-1, 1]$, which is the codomain of $\sin$ for real arguments. Since $\sin$ is a non-expansion mapping, this implies $T$ is a contraction mapping.

### Convergent subsequences {#Convergent_subsequences}

Every bounded sequence in $\mathbb R^n$ has a convergent subsequence, by the [Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/31211). If these subsequences all have the same limit, then the original sequence also converges to that limit. If it can be shown that all of the subsequences of $f$ must have the same limit, such as by showing that there is a unique fixed point of the transformation $T$ and that there are no [invariant sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_set) of $T$ that contain no fixed points of $T$, then the initial sequence must also converge to that limit.

### Monotonicity (Lyapunov functions) {#Monotonicity_(Lyapunov_functions)}

Every [bounded monotonic sequence in $\mathbb R^n$ converges to a limit](wikipedia://en/236098#Theorems).

This fact can be used directly and can also be used to prove the convergence of sequences that are not monotonic using techniques and theorems named for [Aleksandr Lyapunov](wikipedia://en/65425). In these cases, one defines a function $V:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ such that $V(f(k))$ is monotonic in $k$ and thus $V(f(k))$ converges. If $V$ satisfies the conditions to be a [Lyapunov function](wikipedia://en/352267) then [Lyapunov's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyapunov's_theorem) implies that $f$ is also convergent. Lyapunov's theorem is normally stated for [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations), but it can also be applied to sequences of iterates by replacing derivatives with discrete differences.

The basic requirements on $V$ to be a Lyapunov function are that

1. $V(x) > 0$ for all $x\ne 0$ and $V(0) = 0$
1. $V(f(k+1)) - V(f(k)) < 0$ for $f(k) \ne 0$ (discrete case) or $\dot{V}(x) < 0$ for $x \ne 0$ (continuous case)
1. $V$ is "radially unbounded", i.e., that $\lim_{k\rightarrow \infty} V(f(k)) = \infty$ for any sequence with $\lim_{k\rightarrow \infty} ||f(k)|| = \infty$.

In many cases a quadratic Lyapunov function of the form $V(x) = x^T A x$ can be found, although more complex forms are also common, for instance [entropies](wikipedia://en/9891) in the study of convergence of [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543).

For [delay differential equations](wikipedia://en/3941333), a similar approach applies with Lyapunov functions replaced by Lyapunov functionals also called [Lyapunov-Krasovskii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyapunov-Krasovskii) functionals.

If the inequality in the condition 2 is weak, [LaSalle's invariance principle](wikipedia://en/4021739) may be used.

## Convergence of sequences of functions {#Convergence_of_sequences_of_functions}

To consider the convergence of sequences of functions,[^o14] it is necessary to define a distance between functions to replace the Euclidean norm. These often include

- [Convergence in the norm (strong convergence)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_in_the_norm_%28strong_convergence%29) -- a function norm, such as $\|g\|_f = \int_{x \in A} \|g(x)\| dx$ is defined, and convergence occurs if $||f(n)-f_\infty||_f \rightarrow 0$. For this case, all of the above techniques can be applied with this function norm.
- [Pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198) -- convergence occurs if for each $x$, $f_n(x) \rightarrow f_\infty(x)$. For this case, the above techniques can be applied for each point $x$ with the norm appropriate for $f(x)$.
- [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652) -- In pointwise convergence, some (open) regions can converge arbitrarily slowly. With uniform convergence, there is a fixed convergence rate such that all points converge at least that fast. Formally, $\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\sup\{\,\left|f_n(x)-f_\infty(x)\right| : x \in A \,\}=0,$ where $A$ is the domain of each $f_n$.

See also

- [Convergence of Fourier series](wikipedia://en/927236)

## Convergence of random variables {#Convergence_of_random_variables}

Random variables[^o16] are more complicated than simple elements of $\mathbb{R}^n$. (Formally, a random variable is a mapping $x:\Omega\rightarrow V$ from an event space $\Omega$ to a value space $V$. The value space may be $\mathbb{R}^n$, such as the roll of a dice, and such a random variable is often spoken of informally as being in $\mathbb{R}^n$, but convergence of sequence of random variables corresponds to convergence of the sequence of *functions*, or the *distributions*, rather than the sequence of *values*.)

There are multiple [types of convergence](wikipedia://en/50723), depending on how the *distance* between functions is measured.

- [Convergence in distribution](wikipedia://en/50723#convergence_in_distribution) -- pointwise convergence of the [distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) of the random variables to the limit
- [Convergence in probability](wikipedia://en/50723#Convergence_in_probability)
- [Almost sure convergence](wikipedia://en/50723#Almost_sure_convergence) -- pointwise convergence of the mappings $x_n:\Omega\rightarrow V$ to the limit, except at a set in $\Omega$ with measure 0 in the limit.
- [Convergence in the mean](wikipedia://en/50723#Convergence_in_the_mean)

Each has its own proof techniques, which are beyond the current scope of this article.

See also

- [Dominated convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominated_convergence)
- [Carleson's theorem](wikipedia://en/24105681) establishing the pointwise (Lebesgue) almost everywhere convergence of Fourier series of L2 functions
- [Doob's martingale convergence theorems](wikipedia://en/12261058) a random variable analogue of the [monotone convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/146263)

## Topological convergence {#Topological_convergence}

For all of the above techniques, some form the basic analytic definition of convergence above applies. However, [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) has its own definitions of convergence. For example, in a non-[Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) space, it is possible for a sequence to converge to multiple different limits.
