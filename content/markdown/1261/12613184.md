# Compass equivalence theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Compass equivalence theorem"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **compass equivalence theorem** is an important statement in [compass and straightedge constructions](wikipedia://en/61229). The tool advocated by [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) in these constructions is a *divider* or *collapsing compass*, that is, a [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_%28drafting%29) that "collapses" whenever it is lifted from a page, so that it may not be directly used to transfer distances. The *modern compass* with its fixable aperture can be used to transfer distances directly and so appears to be a more powerful instrument. However, the compass equivalence theorem states that any construction via a "modern compass" may be attained with a collapsing compass. This can be shown by establishing that with a collapsing compass, given a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) in the plane, it is possible to construct another circle of equal [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815), centered at any given point on the plane. This theorem is Proposition II of Book I of [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107). The [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of this [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) has had a chequered history.[^o2]

:::

## Construction {#Construction}

*Image: Euclid2.svg.* Diagram for proof of Euclid I.2

The following construction and proof of correctness are given by Euclid in his *Elements*.[^o5] Although there appear to be several cases in Euclid's treatment, depending upon choices made when interpreting ambiguous instructions, they all lead to the same conclusion,[^o2] and so, specific choices are given below.

Given points $A$, $B$, and $C$, construct a circle centered at $A$ with radius the length of $\overline{BC}$ (that is, equivalent to the solid green circle, but centered at $A$).

- Draw a circle centered at $A$ and passing through $B$ and vice versa (the red circles). They will intersect at point $D$ and form the [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) $\triangle ABD$.
- Extend $\overline{DB}$ past $B$ and find the intersection of $\overline{DB}$ and the circle $\text{◯}BC$, labeled $E$.
- Create a circle centered at $D$ and passing through $E$ (the blue circle).
- Extend $\overline{DA}$ past $A$ and find the intersection of $\overline{DA}$ and the circle $\text{◯}DE$, labeled $F$.
- Construct a circle centered at $A$ and passing through $F$ (the dotted green circle)
- Because $\triangle ADB$ is an equilateral triangle, $\overline{DA} = \overline{DB}$.
- Because $E$ and $F$ are on a circle around $D$, $\overline{DE} = \overline{DF}$.
- Therefore, $\overline{AF} = \overline{BE}$.
- Because $E$ is on the circle $\text{◯}BC$, $\overline{BE} = \overline{BC}$.
- Therefore, $\overline{AF} = \overline{BC}$.

:::

## Alternative construction without straightedge {#Alternative_construction_without_straightedge}

It is possible to prove compass equivalence without the use of the straightedge. This justifies the use of "fixed compass" moves (constructing a circle of a given radius at a different location) in proofs of the [Mohr–Mascheroni theorem](wikipedia://en/1196909), which states that any construction possible with straightedge and compass can be accomplished with compass alone.

*Image: Compass-equivalence-no-straightedge.png.* Construction without using straightedge

Given points $A$, $B$, and $C$, construct a circle centered at $A$ with the radius $\overline{BC}$, using only a collapsing compass and no straightedge.

- Draw a circle centered at $A$ and passing through $B$ and vice versa (the blue circles). They will intersect at points $D$ and $D'$.
- Draw circles through $C$ with centers at $D$ and $D'$ (the red circles). Label their other intersection $E$.
- Draw a circle (the green circle) with center $A$ passing through $E$. This is the required circle.[^o7][^o8]

There are several proofs of the correctness of this construction and it is often left as an exercise for the reader.[^o7][^o8] Here is a modern one using [transformations](wikipedia://en/43194879).

- The line $DD'$ is the [perpendicular bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of $\overline{AB}$. Thus $A$ is the [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28geometry%29) of $B$ through line $DD'$.
- By construction, $E$ is the reflection of $C$ through line $DD'$.
- Since reflection is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777), it follows that $\overline{AE} = \overline{BC}$ as desired.
