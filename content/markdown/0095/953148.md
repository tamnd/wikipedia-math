# Lagrange reversion theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lagrange reversion theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Lagrange reversion theorem** gives [series](wikipedia://en/15287) or [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) expansions of certain [implicitly defined functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicitly_defined_function); indeed, of compositions with such functions.

:::

Let $v$ be a function of $x$ and $y$ in terms of another function $f$ such that

$$v=x+yf(v)$$

Then for any function $g$, for small enough $y$:

$$g(v)=g(x)+\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{y^k}{k!}\left(\frac\partial{\partial x}\right)^{k-1}\left(f(x)^kg'(x)\right).$$

In particular, if $g$ is the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069) $g(x)=x$, this reduces to

$$v=x+\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{y^k}{k!}\left(\frac\partial{\partial x}\right)^{k-1}\left(f(x)^k\right),$$

in which case the equation can be derived using [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/102140).

In 1770, [Joseph Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) (1736–1813) published his [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) solution of the implicit equation for $v$ mentioned above. However, his solution used cumbersome series expansions of [logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithms).[^o2][^o3] In 1780, [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783) (1749–1827) published a simpler proof of the theorem, which was based on relations between [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) with respect to the variable $x$ and the parameter $y$.[^o4][^o5][^o6] [Charles Hermite](wikipedia://en/22870422) (1822–1901) presented the most straightforward proof of the theorem by using [contour integration](wikipedia://en/704359).[^o7][^o8][^o9]

Lagrange's reversion theorem is used to obtain numerical solutions to [Kepler's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler's_equation).

## Simple proof {#Simple_proof}

We start by writing

$$g(v) = \int \delta(y f(z) - z + x) g(z) (1-y f'(z)) \, dz.$$

Writing the [delta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta-function) as an integral, we have:

$$\begin{aligned} g(v) & = \iint \exp(\mathrm{i}k[y f(z) - z + x]) g(z) (1-y f'(z)) \, \frac{dk}{2\pi} \, dz \\[10pt] & =\sum_{n=0}^\infty \iint \frac{(\mathrm{i}k y f(z))^n}{n!} g(z) (1-y f'(z)) \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(x-z)}\, \frac{dk}{2\pi} \, dz \\[10pt] & =\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^n\iint \frac{(y f(z))^n}{n!} g(z) (1-y f'(z)) \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(x-z)} \, \frac{dk}{2\pi} \, dz \end{aligned}$$

The integral over $k$ then gives $\delta(x-z)$ and we have:

$$\begin{aligned} g(v) & = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^n \left[ \frac{(y f(x))^n}{n!} g(x) (1-y f'(x))\right] \\[10pt] & =\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)^n \left[ \frac{y^n f(x)^n g(x)}{n!} - \frac{y^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\left\{ (g(x) f(x)^{n+1})' - g'(x) f(x)^{n+1}\right\} \right] \end{aligned}$$

Rearranging the sum and cancelling then gives the result:

$$g(v)=g(x)+\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{y^k}{k!}\left(\frac\partial{\partial x}\right)^{k-1}\left(f(x)^kg'(x)\right)$$
