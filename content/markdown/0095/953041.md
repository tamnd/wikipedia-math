# Martin Davis (mathematician)

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Martin Davis"}

**Martin David Davis** (March 8, 1928 – January 1, 2023) was an American mathematician and [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist) who contributed to the fields of [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414) and [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636). His work on [Hilbert's tenth problem](wikipedia://en/101851) led to the [MRDP theorem](wikipedia://en/101700#Matiyasevich's_theorem). He also advanced the [Post–Turing model](wikipedia://en/3688147) and co-developed the [Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland_algorithm), which is foundational for [Boolean satisfiability solvers](wikipedia://en/4715).

:::

Davis won the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550), the [Chauvenet Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvenet_Prize) (with [Reuben Hersh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuben_Hersh)), and the [Lester R. Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford) Award. He was a fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) and a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Davis's parents were Jewish immigrants to the United States from [Łódź](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Łódź), Poland, and married after they met again in New York City. Davis was born in New York City on March 8, 1928. He grew up in the [Bronx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronx), where his parents encouraged him to obtain a full education.[^o4] He graduated from the prestigious Bronx High School of Science in 1944 and went on to receive his bachelor's degree in mathematics from [City College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York) in 1948 and his PhD from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in 1950.[^o6] His doctoral dissertation, entitled *On the Theory of Recursive Unsolvability*, was supervised by American mathematician and computer scientist [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731).[^o4][^o5]

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

During a research instructorship at the [University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_Urbana-Champaign) in the early 1950s, he joined the *Control Systems Lab* and became one of the early programmers of the [ORDVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ORDVAC).[^o4] He later worked at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) and the [RAND Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND_Corporation) before joining [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University).[^o4] During his time at the NYU, he helped set up the university's computer science department. He retired from NYU in 1996.[^o6][^o4] He was later a member of visiting faculty at [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley).[^o9]

### Hilbert's tenth problem {#Hilbert's_tenth_problem}

Davis first worked on Hilbert's tenth problem during his PhD dissertation, working with Alonzo Church. The theorem, as posed by the German mathematician [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), asks a question: given a [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109), is there an algorithm that can decide if the equation is solvable?[^o4] Davis's dissertation put forward a conjecture that the problem was unsolvable. In the 1950s and 1960s, Davis, along with American mathematicians [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233) and [Julia Robinson](wikipedia://en/952987), made progress toward solving this conjecture. The proof of the conjecture was finally completed in 1970 with the work of Russian mathematician [Yuri Matiyasevich](wikipedia://en/2627712). This resulted in the [MRDP or the DPRM theorem](wikipedia://en/101700#Matiyasevich's_theorem), named for Davis, Putnam, Robinson, and Matiyasevich.[^o4] Describing the problem, Davis had earlier mentioned that he found the problem "irresistibly seductive" when he was an undergraduate and later had progressively become his "lifelong obsession".[^o11]

### Other contributions {#Other_contributions}

Davis collaborated with Putnam, [George Logemann](wikipedia://en/48760367), and [Donald W. Loveland](wikipedia://en/31959476) in 1961 to introduce the [Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland (DPLL) algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davis–Putnam–Logemann–Loveland_algorithm), which was a [complete](wikipedia://en/31271451), [backtracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backtracking)-based [search algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_algorithm) for [deciding the satisfiability](wikipedia://en/4715) of [propositional logic formulae](wikipedia://en/18154) in [conjunctive normal form](wikipedia://en/73342), i.e., for solving the [CNF-SAT](wikipedia://en/4715) problem.[^o13] The algorithm was a refinement of the earlier [Davis–Putnam algorithm](wikipedia://en/2732435), which was a [resolution](wikipedia://en/2724082)-based procedure developed by Davis and Putnam in 1960.[^o14][^o15] The algorithm is foundational in the architecture of fast [Boolean satisfiability solvers.](wikipedia://en/4715)[^o11]

In addition to his work on [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414), Davis also made significant contributions to the fields of [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/6511) and [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636).[^o4][^o11][^o16] Davis was also known for his model of [Post–Turing machines](wikipedia://en/3688147).[^o6]

In 1974, Davis won the [Lester R. Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford) Award for his expository writing related to his work on Hilbert's tenth problem,[^o5][^o17] and in 1975 he won the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) and the [Chauvenet Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvenet_Prize) (with [Reuben Hersh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuben_Hersh)).[^o18] He became a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1982,[^o5] and in 2013, he was selected as one of the inaugural fellows of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o19]

Davis's 1958 book *Computability and Unsolvability* is considered a classic in [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392), while his 2000 book *The Universal Computer* traces the evolution and history of computing, from [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) to [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208).[^o4] His book *The Undecidable*, the first edition of which was published in 1965, was a collection of [unsolvable problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsolvable_problem) and [computable functions](wikipedia://en/1139338).[^o11]

## Personal life and death {#Personal_life_and_death}

Davis was married to Virginia Whiteford Palmer, a textile artist. The couple met during their time in the [Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbana–Champaign) area and subsequently married in 1951.[^o21] They had two children.[^o6] The couple lived in [Berkeley, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley,_California), after his retirement.[^o4]

Davis died on January 1, 2023, at age 94.[^o22] His wife died the same day several hours later.[^o23]
