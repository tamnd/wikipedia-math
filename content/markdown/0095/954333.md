# Box topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Box topology"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) can be given several different topologies. One of the more [natural](wikipedia://en/11934923) choices is the **box topology**, where a [base](wikipedia://en/59220) is given by the Cartesian products of open sets in the component spaces.[^o2] Another possibility is the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910), where a base is also given by the Cartesian products of open sets in the component spaces, but only finitely many of which can be unequal to the entire component space.

:::

While the box topology has a somewhat more intuitive definition than the product topology, it satisfies fewer desirable properties. In particular, if all the component spaces are [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), the box topology on their Cartesian product will not necessarily be compact, although the product topology on their Cartesian product will always be compact. In general, the box topology is [finer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finer_topology) than the product topology, although the two agree in the case of finite direct products (or when all but finitely many of the factors are [trivial](wikipedia://en/382447)).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given $X$ such that

$$X := \prod_{i \in I} X_i,$$

or the (possibly infinite) Cartesian product of the topological spaces $X_i$, [indexed](wikipedia://en/393055) by $i \in I$, the **box topology** on $X$ is generated by the [base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29)

$$\mathcal{B} = \left\{ \prod_{i \in I} U_i \mid U_i \text{ open in } X_i \right\}.$$

The name *box* comes from the case of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, in which the basis sets look like boxes. The set $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$ endowed with the box topology is sometimes denoted by $\underset{i \in I}{\square} X_i.$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Box topology on **R**<sup>*ω*</sup>:[^o7]

- The box topology is [completely regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_regular)
- The box topology is neither [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) nor [connected](wikipedia://en/6233)
- The box topology is not [first countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_countable) (hence not [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable))
- The box topology is not [separable](wikipedia://en/27855)
- The box topology is [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact) (and hence normal) if the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) is true. It is unknown whether the continuum hypothesis is necessary to show this.

### Example — failure of continuity {#Example_—_failure_of_continuity}

The following example is based on the [Hilbert cube](wikipedia://en/216389). Let **R**<sup>*ω*</sup> denote the countable cartesian product of **R** with itself, i.e. the set of all [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) in **R**. Equip **R** with the [standard topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line#As_a_topological_space) and **R**<sup>*ω*</sup> with the box topology. Define:

$$\begin{cases} f : \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}^\omega \\ x \mapsto (x,x,x, \ldots) \end{cases}$$

So all the component functions are the identity and hence continuous, however we will show *f* is not continuous. To see this, consider the open set

$$U = \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \left ( -\tfrac{1}{n}, \tfrac{1}{n} \right ).$$

Suppose *f* were continuous. Then, since:

$$f(0) = (0,0,0, \ldots ) \in U,$$

there should exist $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \subset f^{-1}(U).$ But this would imply that

$$f\left (\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \right ) = \left ( \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}, \ldots \right ) \in U,$$

which is false since $\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} > \tfrac{1}{n}$ for $n > \tfrac{2}{\varepsilon}.$ Thus *f* is not continuous even though all its component functions are.

### Example — failure of compactness {#Example_—_failure_of_compactness}

Consider the countable product $X = \prod_{i \in \mathbb{N}} X_i$ where for each *i*, $X_i = \{0,1\}$ with the [discrete topology](wikipedia://en/56061). The box topology on $X$ will also be the discrete topology. Since discrete spaces are compact [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are finite, we immediately see that $X$ is not compact, even though its component spaces are.

$X$ is not sequentially compact either: consider the sequence $\{x_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ given by

$$(x_n)_m=\begin{cases} 0 & m < n \\ 1 & m \ge n \end{cases}$$

Since no two points in the sequence are the same, the sequence has no limit point, and therefore $X$ is not sequentially compact.

### Convergence in the box topology {#Convergence_in_the_box_topology}

Topologies are often best understood by describing how sequences converge. In general, a Cartesian product of a space $X$ with itself over an [indexing set](wikipedia://en/393055) $S$ is precisely the space of functions from $S$ to $X$*,* denoted $\prod_{s \in S} X = X^S$. The product topology yields the topology of [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198); sequences of functions converge if and only if they converge at every point of $S$.

Because the box topology is finer than the product topology, convergence of a sequence in the box topology is a more stringent condition. Assuming $X$ is Hausdorff, a sequence $(f_n)_n$ of functions in $X^S$ converges in the box topology to a function $f\in X^S$ if and only if it converges pointwise to $f$ and there is a finite subset $S_0\subset S$ and there is an $N$ such that for all $n>N$ the sequence $(f_n(s))_n$ in $X$ is constant for all $s\in S\setminus S_0$. In other words, the sequence $(f_n(s))_n$ is eventually constant for nearly all $s$ and in a uniform way.[^o11]

:::

## Comparison with product topology {#Comparison_with_product_topology}

The basis sets in the product topology have almost the same definition as the above, *except* with the qualification that *all but finitely many* *U<sub>i</sub>* are equal to the component space *X<sub>i</sub>*. The product topology satisfies a very desirable property for maps *f<sub>i</sub>* : *Y* → *X<sub>i</sub>* into the component spaces: the product map *f*: *Y* → *X* defined by the component functions *f<sub>i</sub>* is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) if and only if all the *f<sub>i</sub>* are continuous. As shown above, this does not always hold in the box topology. This actually makes the box topology very useful for providing [counterexamples](wikipedia://en/143151)—many qualities such as [compactness](wikipedia://en/6042), [connectedness](wikipedia://en/6233), metrizability, etc., if possessed by the factor spaces, are not in general preserved in the product with this topology.
