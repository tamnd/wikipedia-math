# Luke's variational principle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Luke's variational principle"}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), **Luke's variational principle** is a [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/40752010) [variational](wikipedia://en/171882) description of the motion of [surface waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface_wave) on a [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) with a [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface), under the action of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity). This principle is named after J.C. Luke, who published it in 1967.[^o2] This variational principle is for [incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477) and [inviscid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inviscid) [potential flows](wikipedia://en/58320), and is used to derive approximate wave models like the [mild-slope equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mild-slope_equation),[^o3] or using the [averaged Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averaged_Lagrangian) approach for wave propagation in inhomogeneous media.[^o4]

:::

Luke's Lagrangian formulation can also be recast into a [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) formulation in terms of the surface elevation and velocity potential at the free surface.[^o5][^o6][^o7] This is often used when modelling the [spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672) evolution of the free-surface in a [sea state](wikipedia://en/1212631), sometimes called [wave turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_turbulence).

Both the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations can be extended to include [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) effects, and by using [Clebsch potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clebsch_potential) to include [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010).[^o2]

## Luke's Lagrangian {#Luke's_Lagrangian}

Luke's [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) formulation is for [non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear) surface gravity waves on an—[incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477), [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational) and [inviscid](wikipedia://en/18963754)—[potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320).

The relevant ingredients, needed in order to describe this flow, are:

- $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},z,t)$ is the [velocity potential](wikipedia://en/2448955),
- $\rho$ is the fluid [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density),
- $g$ is the acceleration by the [Earth's gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity),
- $\boldsymbol{x}$ is the horizontal coordinate vector with components $x$ and $y$,
- $x$ and $y$ are the horizontal coordinates,
- $z$ is the vertical coordinate,
- $t$ is time, and
- $\nabla$ is the horizontal [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) operator, so $\nabla\Phi$ is the horizontal [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) consisting of $\partial\Phi/\partial x$ and $\partial\Phi/\partial y$,
- $V(t)$ is the time-dependent fluid domain with free surface.

The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}$, as given by Luke, is:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \left\{ \iiint_{V(t)} \rho \left[ \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \left| \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \right|^2 + \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} \right)^2 + g\, z \right]\, \mathrm{d}x\; \mathrm{d}y\; \mathrm{d}z \right\} \mathrm{d}t.$$

From [Bernoulli's principle](wikipedia://en/64219), this Lagrangian can be seen to be the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the fluid [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) over the whole time-dependent fluid domain $V(t)$. This is in agreement with the variational principles for inviscid flow without a free surface, found by [Harry Bateman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Bateman).[^o9]

[Variation](wikipedia://en/171882) with respect to the velocity potential $\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},z,t)$ and free-moving surfaces like $z = \eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ results in the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation) for the potential in the fluid interior and all required [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions): [kinematic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematic) boundary conditions on all fluid boundaries and [dynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29) boundary conditions on free surfaces.[^o10] This may also include moving wavemaker walls and ship motion.

For the case of a horizontally unbounded domain with the free fluid surface at $z = \eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ and a fixed bed at $z = -h(\boldsymbol{x})$, Luke's variational principle results in the Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\, \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left\{ \int_{-h(\boldsymbol{x})}^{\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)} \rho\, \left[ \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t} +\, \frac{1}{2} \left| \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \right|^2 +\, \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} \right)^2 \right]\; \mathrm{d}z\; +\, \frac{1}{2}\, \rho\, g\, \eta^2 \right\}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t.$$

The bed-level term proportional to $h^{2}$ in the potential energy has been neglected, since it is a constant and does not contribute in the variations. Below, Luke's variational principle is used to arrive at the flow equations for non-linear surface gravity waves on a potential flow.

### Derivation of the flow equations resulting from Luke's variational principle {#Derivation_of_the_flow_equations_resulting_from_Luke's_variational_principle}

The variation $\delta\mathcal{L} = 0$ in the Lagrangian with respect to variations in the velocity potential Φ(***x***,*z*,*t*), as well as with respect to the surface elevation $\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)$, have to be zero. We consider both variations subsequently.

#### Variation with respect to the velocity potential {#Variation_with_respect_to_the_velocity_potential}

Consider a small variation $\delta\Phi$ in the velocity potential $\Phi$.[^o10] Then the resulting variation in the Lagrangian is:

$$\begin{aligned} \delta_\Phi\mathcal{L}\, &=\, \mathcal{L}(\Phi+\delta\Phi,\eta)\, -\, \mathcal{L}(\Phi,\eta) \\ &=\, -\, \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left\{ \int_{-h(\boldsymbol{x})}^{\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)} \rho\, \left( \frac{\partial(\delta\Phi)}{\partial t} +\, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} (\delta\Phi) +\, \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z}\, \frac{\partial(\delta \Phi)}{\partial z}\, \right)\; \mathrm{d}z\, \right\}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t. \end{aligned}$$

Using [Leibniz integral rule](wikipedia://en/2558855), this becomes, in case of constant density $\rho$:[^o10]

$$\begin{aligned} \delta_\Phi\mathcal{L}\, =\, &-\, \rho\, \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left\{ \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \int_{-h(\boldsymbol{x})}^{\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)} \delta\Phi\; \mathrm{d}z\; +\, \boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \int_{-h(\boldsymbol{x})}^{\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)} \delta\Phi\, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi\; \mathrm{d}z\, \right\}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t \\ &+\, \rho\, \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left\{ \int_{-h(\boldsymbol{x})}^{\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)} \delta\Phi\; \left( \boldsymbol{\nabla} \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi\, +\, \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial z^2} \right)\; \mathrm{d}z\, \right\}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t \\ &+\, \rho\, \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left[ \left( \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}\, +\, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} \eta\, -\, \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} \right)\, \delta\Phi \right]_{z=\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t \\ &+\, \rho\, \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left[ \left( \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} h\, +\, \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} \right)\, \delta\Phi \right]_{z=-h(\boldsymbol{x})}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t \\ =\, &0. \end{aligned}$$

The first integral on the right-hand side integrates out to the boundaries, in $\boldsymbol{x}$ and $t$, of the integration domain and is zero since the variations $\delta\Phi$ are taken to be zero at these boundaries. For variations $\delta\Phi$ which are zero at the free surface and the bed, the second integral remains, which is only zero for arbitrary $\delta\Phi$ in the fluid interior if there the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation) holds:

$$\Delta \Phi\, =\, 0 \qquad \text{ for } -h(\boldsymbol{x})\, <\, z\, <\, \eta(\boldsymbol{x},t),$$

with $\Delta = \nabla \cdot \nabla + \partial^{2}/\partial z^{2}$ the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706).

If variations $\delta\Phi$ are considered which are only non-zero at the free surface, only the third integral remains, giving rise to the kinematic free-surface boundary condition:

$$\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}\, +\, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} \eta\, -\, \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z}\, =\, 0. \qquad \text{ at } z\, =\, \eta(\boldsymbol{x},t).$$

Similarly, variations $\delta\Phi$ only non-zero at the bottom $z = -h$ result in the kinematic bed condition:

$$\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla} h\, +\, \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z}\, =\, 0 \qquad \text{ at } z\, =\, -h(\boldsymbol{x}).$$

#### Variation with respect to the surface elevation {#Variation_with_respect_to_the_surface_elevation}

Considering the variation of the Lagrangian with respect to small changes $\delta\eta$ gives:

$$\delta_\eta\mathcal{L}\, =\, \mathcal{L}(\Phi,\eta+\delta\eta)\, -\, \mathcal{L}(\Phi,\eta) =\, -\, \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left[ \rho\, \delta\eta\, \left( \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t} +\, \frac12\, \left| \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \right|^2\, +\, \frac12\, \left( \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} \right)^2 +\, g\, \eta \right)\, \right]_{z=\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t\, =\, 0.$$

This has to be zero for arbitrary $\delta\eta$, giving rise to the dynamic boundary condition at the free surface:

$$\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial t} +\, \frac12\, \left| \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \right|^2\, +\, \frac12\, \left( \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} \right)^2 +\, g\, \eta\, =\, 0 \qquad \text{ at } z\, =\, \eta(\boldsymbol{x},t).$$

This is the [Bernoulli equation](wikipedia://en/64219) for unsteady potential flow, applied at the free surface, and with the pressure above the free surface being a constant — which constant pressure is taken equal to zero for simplicity.

:::

## Hamiltonian formulation {#Hamiltonian_formulation}

The [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) structure of surface gravity waves on a potential flow was discovered by [Vladimir E. Zakharov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_E._Zakharov) in 1968, and rediscovered independently by [Bert Broer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambertus_Johannes_Folkert_Broer) and [John Miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Miles):[^o5][^o6][^o7]

$$\begin{aligned} \rho\, \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}\, &=\, +\, \frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\varphi},\\ \rho\, \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\, &=\, -\, \frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\eta}, \end{aligned}$$

where the surface elevation $\eta$ and surface potential $\varphi$ — which is the potential $\Phi$ at the free surface $z = \eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ — are the [canonical variables](wikipedia://en/706311). The Hamiltonian $\mathcal{H}(\varphi,\eta)$ is the sum of the [kinetic](wikipedia://en/17327) and [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) of the fluid:

$$\mathcal{H}\, = \, \iint \left\{ \int_{-h(\boldsymbol{x})}^{\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)} \frac12\, \rho\, \left[ \left| \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \right|^2\, +\, \left( \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} \right)^2 \right]\, \mathrm{d}z\, +\, \frac12\, \rho\, g\, \eta^2 \right\}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.$$

The additional constraint is that the flow in the fluid domain has to satisfy [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) with appropriate boundary condition at the bottom $z = -h(\boldsymbol{x})$ and that the potential at the free surface $z = \eta$ is equal to $\varphi$: $\delta\mathcal{H} / \delta\Phi \, = \, 0.$

### Relation with Lagrangian formulation {#Relation_with_Lagrangian_formulation}

The Hamiltonian formulation can be derived from Luke's Lagrangian description by using [Leibniz integral rule](wikipedia://en/2558855) on the integral of $\partial\Phi/\partial t$:[^o7]

$$\mathcal{L}_H = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \iint \left\{ \varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)\, \frac{\partial\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)}{\partial t}\, -\, H(\varphi,\eta;\boldsymbol{x},t) \right\}\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\; \mathrm{d}t,$$

with $\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t),t)$ the value of the velocity potential at the free surface, and $H(\varphi,\eta;\boldsymbol{x},t)$ the Hamiltonian density — sum of the kinetic and potential energy density — and related to the Hamiltonian as:

$$\mathcal{H}(\varphi,\eta)\, =\, \iint H(\varphi,\eta;\boldsymbol{x},t)\; \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}.$$

The Hamiltonian density is written in terms of the surface potential using [Green's third identity](wikipedia://en/805700) on the kinetic energy:[^o17]

$$H\, =\, \frac 1 2 \, \rho\, \sqrt{ 1\, +\, \left| \boldsymbol{\nabla} \eta \right|^2}\;\; \varphi\, \bigl( D(\eta)\; \varphi \bigr)\, +\, \frac12\, \rho\, g\, \eta^2,$$

where $D(\eta) \varphi$ is equal to the [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal) derivative of $\partial\Phi/\partial n$ at the free surface. Because of the linearity of the Laplace equation — valid in the fluid interior and depending on the boundary condition at the bed $z = -h$ and free surface $z = \eta$ — the normal derivative $\partial\Phi/\partial n$ is a *linear* function of the surface potential $\varphi$, but depends non-linear on the surface elevation $\eta$. This is expressed by the [Dirichlet-to-Neumann](wikipedia://en/25561272) operator $D(\eta)$, acting linearly on $\varphi$.

The Hamiltonian density can also be written as:[^o7]

$$H\, =\, \frac 1 2 \, \rho\, \varphi\, \Bigl[ w\, \left( 1\, +\, \left| \boldsymbol{\nabla} \eta \right|^2 \right) -\, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta \cdot \boldsymbol{\nabla}\, \varphi \Bigr]\, +\, \frac 1 2 \, \rho\, g\, \eta^2,$$

with $w(\boldsymbol{x},t) = \partial\Phi/\partial z$ the vertical velocity at the free surface $z = \eta$. Also $w$ is a *linear* function of the surface potential $\varphi$ through the Laplace equation, but $w$ depends non-linear on the surface elevation $\eta$:[^o17]

$$w\, =\, W(\eta)\, \varphi,$$

with $W$ operating linear on $\varphi$, but being non-linear in $\eta$. As a result, the Hamiltonian is a quadratic [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) of the surface potential $\varphi$. Also the potential energy part of the Hamiltonian is quadratic. The source of non-linearity in surface gravity waves is through the kinetic energy depending non-linear on the free surface shape $\eta$.[^o17]

Further $\nabla\varphi$ is not to be mistaken for the horizontal velocity $\nabla\Phi$ at the free surface:

$$\boldsymbol{\nabla}\varphi\, =\, \boldsymbol{\nabla} \Phi\bigl(\boldsymbol{x},\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t),t\bigr)\, =\, \left[ \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi\, +\, \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z}\, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta \right]_{z=\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)}\, =\, \Bigl[ \boldsymbol{\nabla}\Phi \Bigr]_{z=\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)}\, +\, w\, \boldsymbol{\nabla}\eta.$$

Taking the variations of the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}_H$ with respect to the canonical variables $\varphi(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ and $\eta(\boldsymbol{x},t)$ gives:

$$\begin{aligned} \rho\, \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}\, &=\, +\, \frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\varphi},\\ \rho\, \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\, &=\, -\, \frac{\delta\mathcal{H}}{\delta\eta}, \end{aligned}$$

provided in the fluid interior $\Phi$ satisfies the Laplace equation, $\Delta\Phi = 0$, as well as the bottom boundary condition at $z = -h$ and $\Phi = \varphi$ at the free surface.
