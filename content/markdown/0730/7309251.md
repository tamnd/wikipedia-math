# Neighbourhood (graph theory)

*Image: 6n-graf.svg.* In this graph, the vertices adjacent to 5 are 1, 2, and 4. The neighbourhood of 5 is the graph consisting of the vertices 1, 2, 4, and the edge connecting 1 and 2.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Neighbourhood"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **neighbourhood** of a vertex $v$ in a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ is the subgraph of $G$ [induced](wikipedia://en/1655188) by all the vertices that are connected to $v$ by an [edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) (vertices that are **adjacent to** $v$), i.e., the graph composed of all the vertices adjacent to $v$ and all the edges connecting them.

:::

The neighbourhood is often denoted by $N_G (v)$ or (when the graph is unambiguous) $N(v)$. The same neighbourhood notation may also be used to refer to the set of adjacent vertices rather than the corresponding induced subgraph. The neighbourhood described above does not include $v$ itself, and is more specifically the **open neighbourhood** of $v$; it is also possible to define a neighbourhood in which $v$ itself is included, called the **closed neighbourhood** of $v$ and denoted by $N_G [v]$. When stated without any qualification, a neighbourhood is assumed to be open.

Neighbourhoods may be used to represent graphs in computer algorithms, via the [adjacency list](wikipedia://en/392431) and [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) representations. Neighbourhoods are also used in the [clustering coefficient](wikipedia://en/1457636) of a graph, which is a measure of the average [density](wikipedia://en/2777098) of its neighbourhoods. In addition, many important classes of graphs may be defined by properties of their neighbourhoods, or by symmetries that relate neighbourhoods to each other.

An [isolated vertex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_vertex) has no adjacent vertex. The [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) of a vertex $v$ is the number of vertices adjacent to $v$. A special case is a [loop](wikipedia://en/1449083), i.e., an edge that connects a vertex to itself; if such an edge exists, then the vertex belongs to its own neighbourhood.

## Local properties in graphs {#Local_properties_in_graphs}

*Image: Octahedral skeleton.svg.* In the [octahedron graph](wikipedia://en/80177635), the neighbourhood of any vertex is a 4-[cycle](wikipedia://en/878962).

If all vertices in *G* have neighbourhoods that are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to the same graph *H*, *G* is said to be *locally H*, and if all vertices in *G* have neighbourhoods that belong to some graph family *F*, *G* is said to be *locally F*.[^o4] For instance, in the [octahedron graph](wikipedia://en/80177635), shown in the figure, each vertex has a neighbourhood isomorphic to a [cycle](wikipedia://en/878962) of four vertices, so the octahedron is locally *C*<sub>4</sub>.

For example:

- Any [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) *K*<sub>*n*</sub> is locally *K*<sub>*n−1*</sub>. The only graphs that are locally complete are disjoint unions of complete graphs.
- A [Turán graph](wikipedia://en/360581) *T*(*rs*,*r*) is locally *T*((*r*−1)*s*,*r*−1). More generally, any Turán graph is locally Turán.
- Every [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is locally [outerplanar](wikipedia://en/352343). However, not every locally outerplanar graph is planar.
- A graph is [triangle-free](wikipedia://en/7667996) if and only if it is locally [independent](wikipedia://en/524501).
- Every *k*-[chromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) graph is locally (*k*−1)-chromatic. Every locally *k*-chromatic graph has chromatic number $O(\sqrt{kn})$.[^n1]
- If a graph family *F* is closed under the operation of taking induced subgraphs, then every graph in *F* is also locally *F*. For instance, every [chordal graph](wikipedia://en/744165) is locally chordal; every [perfect graph](wikipedia://en/670531) is locally perfect; every [comparability graph](wikipedia://en/6978672) is locally comparable; every [(k)-(ultra)-homogeneous graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_graph) is locally (k)-(ultra)-homogeneous.
- A graph is locally cyclic if every neighbourhood is a [cycle](wikipedia://en/878962). For instance, the [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) is the unique connected locally *C*<sub>4</sub> graph, the [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084) is the unique connected locally *C*<sub>5</sub> graph, and the [Paley graph](wikipedia://en/5719307) of order 13 is locally *C*<sub>6</sub>. Locally cyclic graphs other than *K*<sub>4</sub> are exactly the underlying graphs of [Whitney triangulations](wikipedia://en/1440976), embeddings of graphs on surfaces in such a way that the faces of the embedding are the cliques of the graph.[^o6] Locally cyclic graphs can have as many as $n^{2-o(1)}$ edges.[^n2]
- [Claw-free graphs](wikipedia://en/12415907) are the graphs that are locally co-[triangle-free](wikipedia://en/7667996); that is, for all vertices, the [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) of the neighbourhood of the vertex does not contain a triangle. A graph that is locally *H* is claw-free if and only if the [independence number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_number) of *H* is at most two; for instance, the graph of the regular icosahedron is claw-free because it is locally *C*<sub>5</sub> and *C*<sub>5</sub> has independence number two.
- The [locally linear graphs](wikipedia://en/59938063) are the graphs in which every neighbourhood is an [induced matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_matching).[^n3]
- The [Johnson graphs](wikipedia://en/2948666) are locally grid, meaning that each neighborhood is a [rook's graph](wikipedia://en/7324959).[^n4]

:::

## Neighbourhood of a set {#Neighbourhood_of_a_set}

For a set *A* of vertices, the neighbourhood of *A* is the union of the neighbourhoods of the vertices, and so it is the set of all vertices adjacent to at least one member of *A*.

A set *A* of vertices in a graph is said to be a module if every vertex in *A* has the same set of neighbours outside of *A*. Any graph has a uniquely recursive decomposition into modules, its [modular decomposition](wikipedia://en/28310124), which can be constructed from the graph in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time); modular decomposition algorithms have applications in other graph algorithms including the recognition of [comparability graphs](wikipedia://en/6978672).
