# Leyland number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Leyland number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **Leyland number** is a number of the form

:::

$$x^y + y^x$$

where *x* and *y* are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) greater than 1.[^o2] They are named after the mathematician [Paul Leyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Leyland). The first few Leyland numbers are

[8](wikipedia://en/208174), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [32](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/32_%28number%29), [54](wikipedia://en/399148), [57](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/57_%28number%29), [100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/100_%28number%29), [145](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/145_%28number%29), [177](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/177_%28number%29), [320](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/320_%28number%29), [368](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/368_%28number%29), [512](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/512_%28number%29), [593](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/593_%28number%29), [945](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/945_%28number%29), [1124](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1124_%28number%29) .

The requirement that *x* and *y* both be greater than 1 is important, since without it every positive integer would be a Leyland number of the form *x*<sup>1</sup> + 1<sup>*x*</sup>. Also, because of the [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) property of addition, the condition *x* ≥ *y* is usually added to avoid double-covering the set of Leyland numbers (so we have 1 \< *y* ≤ *x*).

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Leyland prime"}

## Leyland primes {#Leyland_primes}

A **Leyland prime** is a Leyland number that is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). The first such primes are:

[17](wikipedia://en/208288), [593](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/593_%28number%29), 32993, 2097593, 8589935681, 59604644783353249, 523347633027360537213687137, 43143988327398957279342419750374600193, ...

corresponding to

3<sup>2</sup>+2<sup>3</sup>, 9<sup>2</sup>+2<sup>9</sup>, 15<sup>2</sup>+2<sup>15</sup>, 21<sup>2</sup>+2<sup>21</sup>, 33<sup>2</sup>+2<sup>33</sup>, 24<sup>5</sup>+5<sup>24</sup>, 56<sup>3</sup>+3<sup>56</sup>, 32<sup>15</sup>+15<sup>32</sup>.[^o5]

One can also fix the value of *y* and consider the sequence of *x* values that gives Leyland primes, for example *x*<sup>2</sup> + 2<sup>*x*</sup> is prime for *x* = 3, 9, 15, 21, 33, 2007, 2127, 3759, ... ().

By November 2012, the largest Leyland number that had been proven to be prime was 5122<sup>6753</sup> + 6753<sup>5122</sup> with 25050 digits. From January 2011 to April 2011, it was the largest prime whose primality was proved by [elliptic curve primality proving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_primality_proving).[^o6] In December 2012, this was improved by proving the primality of the two numbers 3110<sup>63</sup> + 63<sup>3110</sup> (5596 digits) and 8656<sup>2929</sup> + 2929<sup>8656</sup> (30008 digits), the latter of which surpassed the previous record.[^o7] In February 2023, 104824<sup>5</sup> + 5<sup>104824</sup> (73269 digits) was proven to be prime,[^o8] and it was also the largest prime proven using ECPP, until three months later a larger (non-Leyland) prime was proven using ECPP.[^o9] There are many larger known [probable primes](wikipedia://en/160560) such as 314738<sup>9</sup> + 9<sup>314738</sup>,[^o10] but it is hard to prove primality of large Leyland numbers. [Paul Leyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Leyland) writes on his website: "More recently still, it was realized that numbers of this form are ideal test cases for general purpose primality proving programs. They have a simple algebraic description but no obvious [cyclotomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomic) properties which special purpose algorithms can exploit."

There is a project called XYYXF to [factor](wikipedia://en/15491) [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) Leyland numbers.[^o11]

## Leyland number of the second kind {#Leyland_number_of_the_second_kind}

A **Leyland number of the second kind** is a number of the form

$$x^y - y^x$$

where *x* and *y* are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) greater than 1. The first such numbers are:

0, 1, [7](wikipedia://en/208159), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [28](wikipedia://en/362213), [79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/79_%28number%29), [118](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/118_%28number%29), [192](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/192_%28number%29), [399](wikipedia://en/444763), [431](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/431_%28number%29), 513, 924, 1844, 1927, 2800, 3952, 6049, 7849, 8023, 13983, 16188, 18954, 32543, 58049, 61318, 61440, 65280, 130783, 162287, 175816, 255583, 261820, ...

A **Leyland prime of the second kind** is a Leyland number of the second kind that is also prime. The first few such primes are:

7, 17, 79, 431, 58049, 130783, 162287, 523927, 2486784401, 6102977801, 8375575711, 13055867207, 83695120256591, 375700268413577, 2251799813682647, ... .
