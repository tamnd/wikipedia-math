# Metric dimension (graph theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Metric dimension"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **metric dimension** of a graph *G* is the minimum cardinality of a subset *S* of vertices such that all other vertices are uniquely determined by their distances to the vertices in *S*. Finding the metric dimension of a graph is an [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) problem; the decision version, determining whether the metric dimension is less than a given value, is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).

:::

## Detailed definition {#Detailed_definition}

For an ordered subset $W = \{w_1, w_2,\dots, w_k\}$ of vertices and a vertex *v* in a connected graph *G*, the representation of *v* with respect to *W* is the ordered *k*-tuple $r(v|W) = (d(v,w_1), d(v,w_2),\dots,d(v,w_k))$, where *d*(*x*,*y*) represents the distance between the vertices *x* and *y*. The set *W* is a resolving set (or locating set) for *G* if every two vertices of *G* have distinct representations. The metric dimension of *G* is the minimum cardinality of a resolving set for *G*. A resolving set containing a minimum number of vertices is called a basis (or reference set) for *G*. Resolving sets for graphs were introduced independently by (Slater 1975) and (Harary Melter 1976), while the concept of a resolving set and that of metric dimension were defined much earlier in the more general context of metric spaces by [Blumenthal](wikipedia://en/2354487) in his monograph *Theory and Applications of Distance Geometry*. Graphs are special examples of metric spaces with their intrinsic path metric.

## Trees {#Trees}

If a tree is a path, its metric dimension is one. Otherwise, let *L* denote the set of leaves, degree-one vertices in the tree. Let *K* be the set of vertices that have degree greater than two, and that are connected by paths of degree-two vertices to one or more leaves. Then the metric dimension is |*L*| − |*K*|. A basis of this cardinality may be formed by removing from *L* one of the leaves associated with each vertex in *K*.[^o6] The same algorithm is valid for the line graph of the tree, and thus any tree and its line graph have the same metric dimension.[^n1]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

In (Chartrand Eroh Johnson Oellermann 2000), it is proved that:

- The metric dimension of a graph $G$ is 1 if and only if $G$ is a path.
- The metric dimension of an $n$-vertex graph is $n - 1$ if and only if it is a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816).
- The metric dimension of an $n$-vertex graph is $n - 2$ if and only if the graph is a [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) $K_{s, t}$, a [split graph](wikipedia://en/9928681) $K_s+\overline{K_t} (s\geq 1, t\geq 2)$, or $K_s+(K_1\cup K_t) (s,t\geq 1)$.

### Relations between the order, the metric dimension and the diameter {#Relations_between_the_order,_the_metric_dimension_and_the_diameter}

(Khuller Raghavachari Rosenfeld 1996) prove the inequality $n\leq D^{\beta}+\beta$ for any $n$-vertex graph with [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) $D$ and metric dimension $\beta$. This bounds follows from the fact that each vertex that is not in the resolving set is uniquely determined by a distance vector of length $\beta$ with each entry being an integer between 1 and $D$ (there are precisely $D^{\beta}$ such vectors). However, the bound is only achieved for $D\leq 3$ or $\beta=1$; the more precise bound

$$n\leq \left(\lfloor 2D/3\rfloor+1\right)^\beta+\beta\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil D/3\rceil}(2i-1)^{\beta-1}$$

is proved by (Hernando Mora Pelayo Seara 2010).

For specific graph classes, smaller bounds can hold. For example, (Beaudou Dankelmann Foucaud Henning 2018) proved that $n\leq(\beta D+4)(D+2)/8$ for trees (the bound being tight for even values of $D$), and a bound of the form $n= O(D^2\beta)$ for [outerplanar graphs](wikipedia://en/352343). The same authors proved that $n\leq (D\beta+1)^{t-1}$ for graphs with no [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) of order $t$ as a [minor](wikipedia://en/353042) and also gave bounds for [chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/744165) and graphs of bounded [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086). The authors (Foucaud Mertzios Naserasr Parreau 2017a) proved bounds of the form $n= O(D\beta^2)$ for [interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423) and [permutation graphs](wikipedia://en/17625375), and bounds of the form $n=O(D\beta)$ for [unit interval graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_interval_graph), bipartite permutation graphs and [cographs](wikipedia://en/1175666).

:::

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

### Decision complexity {#Decision_complexity}

Deciding whether the metric dimension of a graph is at most a given integer is NP-complete.[^n2] It remains NP-complete for bounded-degree [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314),[^n3] [split graphs](wikipedia://en/9928681), [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431) and their [complements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28graph_theory%29), [line graphs](wikipedia://en/675231) of bipartite graphs,[^n4] [unit disk graphs](wikipedia://en/3686677),[^n5] [interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423) of diameter 2 and [permutation graphs](wikipedia://en/17625375) of diameter 2,[^n6] and graphs of bounded [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086).[^n7]

For any fixed constant *k*, the graphs of metric dimension at most *k* can be recognized in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time), by testing all possible *k*-tuples of vertices, but this algorithm is not [fixed-parameter tractable](wikipedia://en/603026) (for the natural parameter *k*, the solution size). Answering a question posed by (Lokshtanov 2010), (Hartung Nichterlein 2013) show that the metric dimension decision problem is complete for the parameterized complexity class W\[2\], implying that a time bound of the form *n*<sup>O(*k*)</sup> as achieved by this naive algorithm is likely optimal and that a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm (for the parameterization by *k*) is unlikely to exist. Nevertheless, the problem becomes [fixed-parameter tractable](wikipedia://en/603026) when restricted to [interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423),[^n6] and more generally to graphs of bounded tree-length,[^n8] such as [chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/744165), [permutation graphs](wikipedia://en/17625375) or asteroidal-triple-free graphs.

Deciding whether the metric dimension of a tree is at most a given integer can be done in linear time[^o28] Other linear-time algorithms exist for [cographs](wikipedia://en/1175666),[^n4] [chain graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_graph),[^n9] and cactus block graphs[^n10] (a class including both [cactus graphs](wikipedia://en/9944209) and [block graphs](wikipedia://en/29106883)). The problem may be solved in polynomial time on [outerplanar graphs](wikipedia://en/352343).[^n3] It may also be solved in polynomial time for graphs of bounded [cyclomatic number](wikipedia://en/1483049),[^n4] but this algorithm is again not fixed-parameter tractable (for the parameter "cyclomatic number") because the exponent in the polynomial depends on the cyclomatic number. There exist fixed-parameter tractable algorithms to solve the metric dimension problem for the parameters "[vertex cover](wikipedia://en/562782)",[^n11] "max leaf number",[^n12] and "modular width".[^n8] Graphs with bounded cyclomatic number, vertex cover number or max leaf number all have bounded [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086), however it is an open problem to determine the complexity of the metric dimension problem even on graphs of treewidth 2, that is, [series–parallel graphs](wikipedia://en/9938559).[^n8]

### Approximation complexity {#Approximation_complexity}

The metric dimension of an arbitrary *n*-vertex graph may be approximated in polynomial time to within an [approximation ratio](wikipedia://en/563105) of $2\log n$ by expressing it as a [set cover problem](wikipedia://en/870399), a problem of covering all of a given collection of elements by as few sets as possible in a given [family of sets](wikipedia://en/723043). [^n13] In the set cover problem formed from a metric dimension problem, the elements to be covered are the $\tbinom{n}{2}$ pairs of vertices to be distinguished, and the sets that can cover them are the sets of pairs that can be distinguished by a single chosen vertex. The approximation bound then follows by applying standard approximation algorithms for set cover. An alternative [greedy algorithm](wikipedia://en/89247) that chooses vertices according to the difference in [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) between the equivalence classes of distance vectors before and after the choice achieves an even better approximation ratio, $\log n+\log\log_2 n+1$.[^n14] This approximation ratio is close to best possible, as under standard complexity-theoretic assumptions a ratio of $(1-\epsilon)\log n$ cannot be achieved in polynomial time for any $\epsilon>0$.[^n14] The latter hardness of approximation still holds for instances restricted to subcubic graphs,[^n11] and even to [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431) subcubic graphs.[^n15]
