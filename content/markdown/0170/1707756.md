# Mapping cone (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mapping cone"}

*Image: Mapping cone.svg.* An illustration of a mapping cone; that is, a cone is glued to a space along some [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f \colon X \to Y$.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), the **mapping cone** is a construction in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) analogous to a [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) and denoted $C_f$. Alternatively, it is also called the **homotopy cofiber** and also notated $Cf$. Its dual, a [fibration](wikipedia://en/316826), is called the [mapping fiber](wikipedia://en/19207725). The mapping cone can be understood to be a [mapping cylinder](wikipedia://en/1663142) $Mf$ with the initial end of the cylinder collapsed to a point. Mapping cones are frequently applied in the homotopy theory of [pointed spaces](wikipedia://en/1236666).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a [map](wikipedia://en/6122) $f\colon X \to Y$, the mapping cone $C_f$ is defined to be the quotient space of the [mapping cylinder](wikipedia://en/1663142) $(X \times I) \sqcup_f Y$ with respect to the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\forall x,x' \in X, (x, 0) \sim \left(x', 0\right)\,$, $(x, 1) \sim f(x)$. Here $I$ denotes the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) \[0, 1\] with its standard [topology](wikipedia://en/30450). Note that some authors (like [J. Peter May](wikipedia://en/14644098)) use the opposite convention, switching 0 and 1.

Visually, one takes the cone on *X* (the cylinder $X \times I$ with one end (the 0 end) collapsed to a point), and glues the other end onto *Y* via the map *f* (the 1 end).

The above is the definition for a map of unpointed spaces; for a map of pointed spaces $f\colon (X, x_0) \to (Y, y_0)$ (so $f\colon x_0 \mapsto y_0$), one also identifies all of $x_0 \times I$. Formally, $(x_0, t) \sim \left(x_0, t'\right)$. Thus one end and the "seam" are all identified with $y_0.$

### Example of circle {#Example_of_circle}

If $X$ is the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) $S^1$, the mapping cone $C_f$ can be considered as the quotient space of the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of *Y* with the [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) $D^2$ formed by identifying each point *x* on the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of $D^2$ to the point $f(x)$ in *Y*.

Consider, for example, the case where *Y* is the disk $D^2$, and $f\colon S^1 \to Y = D^2$ is the standard [inclusion](wikipedia://en/492516) of the circle $S^1$ as the boundary of $D^2$. Then the mapping cone $C_f$ is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to two disks joined on their boundary, which is topologically the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) $S^2$.

### Double mapping cylinder {#Double_mapping_cylinder}

The mapping cone is a special case of the double [mapping cylinder](wikipedia://en/1663142). This is basically a cylinder $X \times I$ joined on one end to a space $Y_1$ via a [map](wikipedia://en/6122)

$$f_1: X \to Y_1$$

and joined on the other end to a space $Y_2$ via a map

$$f_2: X \to Y_2$$

The mapping cone is the degenerate case of the double mapping cylinder (also known as the homotopy pushout), in which one of $Y_1, Y_2$ is a single point.

:::

## Dual construction: the mapping fibre {#Dual_construction:_the_mapping_fibre}

The dual to the mapping cone is the [mapping fibre](wikipedia://en/19207725) $F_f$. Given the pointed map $f\colon (X, x_0) \to (Y, y_0),$ one defines the mapping fiber as[^o7]

$$F_f = \left\{(x, \omega) \in X \times Y^I : \omega(0) = y_0 \mbox{ and } \omega(1) = f(x)\right\}.$$

Here, *I* is the unit interval and $\omega$ is a continuous path in the space (the [exponential object](wikipedia://en/2237858)) $Y^I$. The mapping fiber is sometimes denoted as $Mf$; however this conflicts with the same notation for the mapping cylinder.

It is dual to the mapping cone in the sense that the product above is essentially the [fibered product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibered_product) or [pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508) $X\times_f Y$ which is dual to the [pushout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pushout) $X\sqcup_f Y$ used to construct the mapping cone.[^o8] In this particular case, the duality is essentially that of [currying](wikipedia://en/6600), in that the mapping cone $(X\times I)\sqcup_f Y$ has the curried form $X \times_f (I\to Y)$ where $I\to Y$ is simply an alternate notation for the space $Y^I$ of all continuous maps from the unit interval to $Y$. The two variants are related by an [adjoint functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor). Observe that the currying preserves the reduced nature of the maps: in the one case, to the tip of the cone, and in the other case, paths to the basepoint.

## Applications {#Applications}

### CW-complexes {#CW-complexes}

Attaching a cell.

### Effect on fundamental group {#Effect_on_fundamental_group}

Given a [space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* and a loop $\alpha\colon S^1 \to X$ representing an element of the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of *X*, we can form the mapping cone $C_\alpha$. The effect of this is to make the loop $\alpha$ [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible) in $C_\alpha$, and therefore the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of $\alpha$ in the fundamental group of $C_\alpha$ will be simply the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962).

Given a [group presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation) by generators and relations, one gets a 2-complex with that fundamental group.

### Homology of a pair {#Homology_of_a_pair}

The mapping cone lets one interpret the homology of a pair as the reduced homology of the quotient. Namely, if *E* is a [homology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory), and $i\colon A \to X$ is a [cofibration](wikipedia://en/1697216), then

$$E_*(X,A) = E_*(X/A,*) = \tilde E_*(X/A),$$

which follows by applying [excision](wikipedia://en/1248603) to the mapping cone.[^o8]

:::

## Relation to homotopy (homology) equivalences {#Relation_to_homotopy_(homology)_equivalences}

A map $f\colon X\rightarrow Y$ between simply-connected CW complexes is a [homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalence) if and only if its mapping cone is contractible.

More generally, a map is called [*n*-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-connected) (as a map) if its mapping cone is *n*-connected (as a space), plus a little more.[^o15]

Let $\mathbb{}H_*$ be a fixed [homology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory). The map $f\colon X\rightarrow Y$ induces [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) on $H_*$, if and only if the map $\{pt\}\hookrightarrow C_f$ induces an isomorphism on $H_*$, i.e., $H_*(C_f,pt)=0$.

Mapping cones are famously used to construct the long coexact [Puppe sequences](wikipedia://en/4734210), from which long exact sequences of homotopy and relative homotopy groups can be obtained.[^o7]
