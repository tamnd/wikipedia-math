# Sequence space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sequence space"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **sequence space** is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) whose elements are infinite [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) of [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers). Equivalently, it is a [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) whose elements are functions from the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) to the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb K$ of real or complex numbers. The set of all such functions is naturally identified with the set of all possible [infinite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence) with elements in $\mathbb K$, and can be turned into a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) under the operations of [pointwise addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointwise_addition) of functions and pointwise scalar multiplication. All sequence spaces are [linear subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) of this space. Sequence spaces are typically equipped with a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), or at least the structure of a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752).

:::

The most important sequence spaces in analysis are the $\textstyle \ell^p$ spaces, consisting of the $p$-power summable sequences, with the $p$-norm. These are special cases of [$L^p$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) for the [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786) on the set of natural numbers. Other important classes of sequences like [convergent sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_sequence) or null sequences form sequence spaces, respectively denoted $c$ and $c_0$, with the [sup norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm). Any sequence space can also be equipped with the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198), under which it becomes a special kind of [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) called [FK-space](wikipedia://en/1725128).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $\textstyle x_{\bullet} = (x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in a set $X$ is an $X$-valued map $x_{\bullet} : \mathbb{N} \to X$ whose value at $n \in \mathbb{N}$ is denoted by $x_n$ instead of the usual parentheses notation $x(n)$.

### Space of all sequences {#Space_of_all_sequences}

Let $\mathbb K$ denote the field either of real or complex numbers. The set $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ of all [sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29) of elements of $\mathbb K$ is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) for [componentwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Componentwise_operation) addition

$$\left(x_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} + \left(y_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} = \left(x_n + y_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}},$$

and componentwise [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373)

$$\alpha\left(x_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} = \left(\alpha x_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}.$$

A **sequence space** is any [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$.

As a topological space, $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ is naturally endowed with the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910). Under this topology, $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ is [Fréchet](wikipedia://en/233801), meaning that it is a [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856), [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078), [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS). However, this topology is rather pathological: there are no [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) norms on $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ (and thus the product topology cannot [be defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) by any [norm](wikipedia://en/990534)).[^n1] Among Fréchet spaces, $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ is minimal in having no continuous norms:

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n1].**

Let $X$ be a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) over $\mathbb K$. Then the following are equivalent:

$X$ admits no continuous norm (that is, any continuous seminorm on $X$ has a nontrivial null space). $X$ contains a vector subspace TVS-isomorphic to $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$. $X$ contains a complemented vector subspace TVS-isomorphic to $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$.

:::

But the product topology is also unavoidable: $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ does not admit a [strictly coarser](wikipedia://en/393069) Hausdorff, locally convex topology.[^n1] For that reason, the study of sequences begins by finding a strict [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of interest, and endowing it with a topology *different* from the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926).

### $\ell^{p}$ spaces {#spaces}

For $0 < p < \infty$, $\textstyle \ell^p$ is the subspace of $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ consisting of all sequences $\textstyle x_{\bullet} = (x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ satisfying

$$\sum_n |x_n|^p < \infty.$$

If $p \geq 1$, then the real-valued function $\|\cdot\|_p$ on $\textstyle \ell^p$ defined by

$$\|x\|_p ~=~ \Bigl(\sum_n|x_n|^p\Bigr)^{1/p} \qquad \text{ for all } x \in \ell^p$$

defines a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on $\textstyle \ell^p$. In fact, $\textstyle \ell^p$ is a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832) with respect to this norm, and therefore is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989).

If $p = 2$ then $\textstyle \ell^2$ is also a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) when endowed with its canonical [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product), called the **Euclidean inner product**, defined for all $\textstyle x_\bullet, y_\bullet \in \ell^p$ by

$$\langle x_\bullet, y_\bullet \rangle ~=~ \sum_n \overline{x_n\!}\, y_n.$$

The canonical norm induced by this inner product is the usual $\textstyle \ell^2$-norm, meaning that $\textstyle \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x} \rangle}$ for all $\textstyle \mathbf{x} \in \ell^p$.

If $p = \infty$, then $\textstyle \ell^\infty$ is defined to be the space of all [bounded sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_sequence) endowed with the norm

$$\|x\|_\infty ~=~ \sup_n |x_n|,$$

$\textstyle \ell^\infty$ is also a Banach space.

If $0 < p < 1$, then $\textstyle \ell^p$ does not carry a norm, but rather a [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) defined by

$$d(x,y) ~=~ \sum_n \left|x_n - y_n\right|^p.$$

### $c$, $c_{0}$ and $c_{00}$ {#,_and}

A [convergent sequence](wikipedia://en/285773) is any sequence $\textstyle x_{\bullet} \in \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ such that $\textstyle \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n$ exists. The set c of all convergent sequences is a vector subspace of $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$ called the [space of convergent sequences](wikipedia://en/17543372). Since every convergent sequence is bounded, $c$ is a linear subspace of $\ell^\infty$. Moreover, this sequence space is a closed subspace of $\textstyle \ell^\infty$ with respect to the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm), and so it is a Banach space with respect to this norm.

A sequence that converges to $0$ is called a [null sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_sequence) and is said to vanish. The set of all sequences that converge to $0$ is a closed vector subspace of $c$ that when endowed with the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm) becomes a Banach space that is denoted by c0 and is called the space of null sequences or the space of vanishing sequences.

The space of eventually zero sequences, c00, is the subspace of $c_0$ consisting of all sequences which have only finitely many nonzero elements. This is not a closed subspace and therefore is not a Banach space with respect to the infinity norm. For example, the sequence $\textstyle (x_{nk})_{k \in \mathbb{N}}$ where $x_{nk} = 1/k$ for the first $n$ entries (for $k = 1, \ldots, n$) and is zero everywhere else (that is, $\textstyle (x_{nk})_{k \in \mathbb{N}} = {}\!$$\bigl(1, \tfrac12, \ldots,{}$$\tfrac{1}{n-1}, \tfrac{1}{n}, {}$$0, 0, \ldots\bigr)$) is a [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) but it does not converge to a sequence in $c_{00}.$

### Space of all finite sequences {#Space_of_all_finite_sequences}

Let

$$\mathbb{K}^\infty=\left\{\left(x_1, x_2,\ldots\right)\in\mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N} : \text{all but finitely many }x_i\text{ equal }0\right\}$$

denote the **space of finite sequences over** $\mathbb K$. As a vector space, $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\infty$ is equal to $c_{00}$, but $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\infty$ has a different topology.

For every [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n \in \mathbb{N}$, let $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^n$ denote the usual [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) endowed with the [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) and let $\textstyle \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n} : \mathbb{K}^n \to \mathbb{K}^\infty$ denote the canonical inclusion

$$\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n}\left(x_1, \ldots, x_n\right) = \left(x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, \ldots \right).$$

The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of each inclusion is

$$\operatorname{Im} \left( \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n} \right) = \left\{ \left(x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, \ldots \right) : x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{K} \right\} = \mathbb{K}^n \times \left\{ (0, 0, \ldots) \right\}$$

and consequently,

$$\mathbb{K}^\infty = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \operatorname{Im} \left( \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n} \right).$$

This family of inclusions gives $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\infty$ a [final topology](wikipedia://en/1819736) $\textstyle \tau^\infty$, defined to be the [finest topology](wikipedia://en/393069) on $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\infty$ such that all the inclusions are continuous (an example of a [coherent topology](wikipedia://en/14602178)). With this topology, $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\infty$ becomes a [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856), [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844), [sequential](wikipedia://en/4668395), [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) that is not [Fréchet–Urysohn](wikipedia://en/28480560). The topology $\textstyle \tau^\infty$ is also [strictly finer](wikipedia://en/393069) than the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) induced on $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\infty$ by $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\mathbb{N}$.

Convergence in $\textstyle \tau^\infty$ has a natural description: if $\textstyle v \in \mathbb{K}^\infty$ and $v_\bullet$ is a sequence in $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^\infty$ then $v_\bullet \to v$ in $\textstyle \tau^\infty$ if and only $v_\bullet$ is eventually contained in a single image $\textstyle \operatorname{Im} \left( \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n} \right)$ and $v_\bullet \to v$ under the natural topology of that image.

Often, each image $\textstyle \operatorname{Im} \left( \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n} \right)$ is identified with the corresponding $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^n$; explicitly, the elements $\textstyle \left( x_1, \ldots, x_n \right) \in \mathbb{K}^n$ and $\left( x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, 0, \ldots \right)$ are identified. This is facilitated by the fact that the subspace topology on $\textstyle \operatorname{Im} \left( \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n} \right)$, the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) from the map $\textstyle \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n}$, and the Euclidean topology on $\textstyle \mathbb{K}^n$ all coincide. With this identification, $\textstyle \left( \left(\mathbb{K}^\infty, \tau^\infty\right), \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^n}\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\right)$ is the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of the directed system $\textstyle \left( \left(\mathbb{K}^n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}, \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^m\to\mathbb{K}^n}\right)_{m \leq n\in\mathbb{N}},\mathbb{N} \right),$ where every inclusion adds trailing zeros:

$$\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{K}^m\to\mathbb{K}^n}\left(x_1, \ldots, x_m\right) = \left(x_1, \ldots, x_m, 0, \ldots, 0 \right).$$

This shows $\textstyle \left(\mathbb{K}^\infty, \tau^\infty \right)$ is an [LB-space](wikipedia://en/63848931).

### Other sequence spaces {#Other_sequence_spaces}

The space of bounded [series](wikipedia://en/15287), denote by [bs](wikipedia://en/17555165), is the space of sequences $x$ for which

$$\sup_n \biggl\vert \sum_{i=0}^n x_i \biggr\vert < \infty.$$

This space, when equipped with the norm

$$\|x\|_{bs} = \sup_n \biggl\vert \sum_{i=0}^n x_i \biggr\vert,$$

is a Banach space isometrically isomorphic to $\textstyle \ell^\infty,$ via the [linear mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_mapping)

$$(x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \mapsto \biggl(\sum_{i=0}^n x_i\biggr)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}.$$

The subspace $cs$ consisting of all convergent series is a subspace that goes over to the space $c$ under this isomorphism.

The space $\Phi$ or $c_{00}$ is defined to be the space of all infinite sequences with only a finite number of non-zero terms (sequences with [finite support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_support)). This set is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in many sequence spaces.

:::

## Properties of $\ell^{p}$ spaces and the space $c_{0}$ {#Properties_of_spaces_and_the_space}

The space $\textstyle \ell^2$ is the only $\textstyle \ell^p$ space that is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), since any norm that is induced by an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) should satisfy the [parallelogram law](wikipedia://en/362953)

$$\|x+y\|_p^2 + \|x-y\|_p^2= 2\|x\|_p^2 + 2\|y\|_p^2.$$

Substituting two distinct unit vectors for $x$ and $y$ directly shows that the identity is not true unless $p = 2$.

Each $\textstyle \ell^p$ is distinct, in that $\textstyle \ell^p$ is a strict [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\textstyle \ell^s$ whenever $p < s$; furthermore, $\textstyle \ell^p$ is not linearly [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $\textstyle \ell^s$ when $p \neq s$. In fact, by Pitt's theorem (Pitt 1936), every bounded linear operator from $\textstyle \ell^s$ to $\textstyle \ell^p$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/577441) when $p < s$. No such operator can be an isomorphism; and further, it cannot be an isomorphism on any infinite-dimensional subspace of $\ell^s$, and is thus said to be [strictly singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_singular).

If $1 < p < \infty$, then the [(continuous) dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of $\textstyle \ell^p$ is isometrically isomorphic to $\textstyle \ell^q$, where $q$ is the [Hölder conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_conjugate) of $p$: $1/p + 1/q = 1$. The specific isomorphism associates to an element $x$ of $\textstyle \ell^q$ the functional

$$L_x(y) = \sum_n x_n y_n$$

for $y$ in $\textstyle \ell^p$. [Hölder's inequality](wikipedia://en/191538) implies that $L_x$ is a bounded linear functional on $\textstyle \ell^p$, and in fact

$$|L_x(y)| \le \|x\|_q\, \|y\|_p$$

so that the operator norm satisfies

$$\|L_x\|_{(\ell^p)^*} \mathrel{\stackrel{\rm{def}}{=}} \sup_{y\in\ell^p, y\not=0} \frac{|L_x(y)|}{\|y\|_p} \le \|x\|_q.$$

In fact, taking $y$ to be the element of $\textstyle \ell^p$ with

$$y_n = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if}\ x_n=0 \\ x_n^{-1}|x_n|^q & \text{if}~ x_n \neq 0 \end{cases}$$

gives $L_x(y) = \|x\|_q$, so that in fact

$$\|L_x\|_{(\ell^p)^*} = \|x\|_q.$$

Conversely, given a bounded linear functional $L$ on $\textstyle \ell^p$, the sequence defined by $x_n = L(e_n)$ lies in $\textstyle \ell^q$. Thus the mapping $x\mapsto L_x$ gives an isometry

$$\kappa_q : \ell^q \to (\ell^p)^*.$$

The map

$$\ell^q\xrightarrow{\kappa_q}(\ell^p)^*\xrightarrow{(\kappa_q^*)^{-1}}(\ell^q)^{**}$$

obtained by composing $\kappa_p$ with the inverse of its [transpose](wikipedia://en/7988#Transpose_of_a_continuous_linear_map) coincides with the [canonical injection](wikipedia://en/210091#Definitions) of $\textstyle \ell^q$ into its [double dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_dual). As a consequence $\textstyle \ell^q$ is a [reflexive space](wikipedia://en/210091). By [abuse of notation](wikipedia://en/1698066), it is typical to identify $\textstyle \ell^q$ with the dual of $\textstyle \ell^p$: $\textstyle (\ell^p)^* = \ell^q$. Then reflexivity is understood by the sequence of identifications $\textstyle (\ell^p)^{**} = (\ell^q)^* = \ell^p$.

The space $c_0$ is defined as the space of all sequences converging to zero, with norm identical to $\|x\|_\infty$. It is a closed subspace of $\textstyle \ell^\infty$, hence a Banach space. The [dual](wikipedia://en/7988) of $c_0$ is $\textstyle \ell^1$; the dual of $\textstyle \ell^1$ is $\textstyle \ell^\infty$. For the case of natural numbers index set, the $\textstyle \ell^p$ and $c_0$ are [separable](wikipedia://en/27855), with the sole exception of $\textstyle \ell^\infty$. The dual of $\textstyle \ell^\infty$ is the [ba space](wikipedia://en/629005).

The spaces $c_0$ and $\textstyle \ell^p$ (for $1 \leq p < \infty$) have a canonical unconditional [Schauder basis](wikipedia://en/3460662) $\{e_i : i = 1, 2, \ldots \}$, where $e_i$ is the sequence which is zero but for a $1$ in the $i$th entry.

The space ℓ<sup>1</sup> has the [Schur property](wikipedia://en/20899639): In ℓ<sup>1</sup>, any sequence that is [weakly convergent](wikipedia://en/2119266) is also [strongly convergent](wikipedia://en/2119266) (Schur 1921). However, since the [weak topology](wikipedia://en/33662) on infinite-dimensional spaces is strictly weaker than the [strong topology](wikipedia://en/589726), there are [nets](wikipedia://en/22170) in ℓ<sup>1</sup> that are weak convergent but not strong convergent.

The $\textstyle \ell^p$ spaces can be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) into many [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). The question of whether every infinite-dimensional Banach space contains an isomorph of some $\textstyle \ell^p$ or of $c_0$, was answered negatively by [B. S. Tsirelson](wikipedia://en/1661441)'s construction of [Tsirelson space](wikipedia://en/628352) in 1974. The dual statement, that every separable Banach space is linearly isometric to a [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) of $\textstyle \ell^1$, was answered in the affirmative by (Banach Mazur 1933). That is, for every separable Banach space $X$, there exists a quotient map $\textstyle Q: \ell^1 \to X$, so that $X$ is isomorphic to $\textstyle \ell^1 / \ker Q$. In general, $\operatorname{ker} Q$ is not complemented in $\textstyle \ell^1$, that is, there does not exist a subspace $Y$ of $\textstyle \ell^1$ such that $\textstyle \ell^1 = Y \oplus \ker Q$. In fact, $\textstyle \ell^1$ has uncountably many uncomplemented subspaces that are not isomorphic to one another (for example, take $\textstyle X=\ell^p$; since there are uncountably many such $X$'s, and since no $\textstyle \ell^p$ is isomorphic to any other, there are thus uncountably many ker *Q*'s).

Except for the trivial finite-dimensional case, an unusual feature of $\textstyle \ell^q$ is that it is not [polynomially reflexive](wikipedia://en/629068).

### $\ell^{p}$ spaces are increasing in $p$ {#spaces_are_increasing_in}

For $p \in [1,\infty]$, the spaces $\textstyle \ell^p$ are increasing in $p$, with the inclusion operator being continuous: for $1 \le p < q \le \infty$, one has $\|x\|_q\le\|x\|_p$. Indeed, the inequality is homogeneous in the $x_i$, so it is sufficient to prove it under the assumption that $\|x\|_p = 1$. In this case, we need only show that $\textstyle\sum |x_i|^q \le 1$ for $q > p$. But if $\|x\|_p = 1$, then $|x_i|\le 1$ for all $i$, and then $\textstyle \sum |x_i|^q \le {}\!$$\textstyle\sum |x_i|^p = 1$.

### $\ell^{2}$ is isomorphic to all separable, infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces {#is_isomorphic_to_all_separable,_infinite_dimensional_Hilbert_spaces}

Let $H$ be a [separable Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932#Separable_spaces). Every orthogonal set in $H$ is at most [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) (i.e. has finite [dimension](wikipedia://en/20598932#Hilbert_dimension) or $\aleph_0$).[^o19] The following two items are related:

- If $H$ is infinite dimensional, then it is isomorphic to $\textstyle \ell^2$,
- If $\operatorname{dim}(H) = N$, then $H$ is isomorphic to $\textstyle \mathbb{C}^N$.

## Properties of $\ell^{1}$ spaces {#Properties_of_spaces}

A sequence of elements in $\textstyle \ell^1$ converges in the space of complex sequences $\textstyle \ell^1$ if and only if it converges weakly in this space.[^n2] If $K$ is a subset of this space, then the following are equivalent:[^n2]

1. $K$ is compact;
1. $K$ is weakly compact;
1. $K$ is bounded, closed, and equismall at infinity.

Here $K$ being **equismall at infinity** means that for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a natural number $n_{\varepsilon} \geq 0$ such that $\textstyle \sum_{n = n_{\epsilon}}^\infty | s_n | < \varepsilon$ for all $\textstyle s = \left( s_n \right)_{n=1}^\infty \in K$.
