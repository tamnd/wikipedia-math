# Sturmian word

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sturmian word"}

*Image: Fibonacci word cutting sequence.png.* The [Fibonacci word](wikipedia://en/1702139) is an example of a Sturmian word. The start of the [cutting sequence](wikipedia://en/33509133) shown here illustrates the start of the word 0100101001.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in [combinatorics on words](wikipedia://en/20913490), a **Sturmian word** (**Sturmian sequence** or **billiard sequence**[^o2]) is a certain kind of infinitely long [sequence of characters](wikipedia://en/27701). The concept is named after [Jacques Charles François Sturm](wikipedia://en/1181554).

:::

Such a sequence can be generated by considering a game of [English billiards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_billiards) on a square table. The struck ball will successively hit the vertical and horizontal edges labelled 0 and 1 generating a sequence of letters.[^o3] This sequence is a Sturmian word.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Sturmian sequences can be defined strictly in terms of their combinatoric properties or geometrically as [cutting sequences](wikipedia://en/33509133) for lines of irrational slope or codings for [irrational rotations](wikipedia://en/9445837). They are traditionally taken to be infinite sequences on the alphabet of the two symbols 0 and 1.

### Combinatorial definitions {#Combinatorial_definitions}

#### Sequences of low complexity {#Sequences_of_low_complexity}

For an infinite sequence of symbols *w*, let *σ*(*n*) be the [complexity function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexity_function) of *w*; i.e., *σ*(*n*) = the number of distinct [contiguous subwords (factors)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subword) in *w* of length *n*. Then *w* is Sturmian if *σ*(*n*) = *n* + 1 for all *n*.

#### Balanced sequences {#Balanced_sequences}

A set *X* of binary strings is called *balanced* if the [Hamming weight](wikipedia://en/1127884) of elements of *X* takes at most two distinct values. That is, for any $s\in X$ |*s*|<sub>1</sub> = *k* or |*s*|<sub>1</sub> = *k**where |*****s*\|<sub>1</sub> is the number of 1s in*s*.***

Let *w* be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s and let $\mathcal L_n(w)$ denote the set of all length-*n* subwords of *w*. The sequence *w* is Sturmian if $\mathcal L_n(w)$ is balanced for all *n* and *w* is not eventually periodic.

### Geometric definitions {#Geometric_definitions}

#### Cutting sequence of irrational {#Cutting_sequence_of_irrational}

Let *w* be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s. The sequence *w* is Sturmian if for some $x\in[0,1)$ and some irrational $\theta\in(0,\infty)$, *w* is realized as the [cutting sequence](wikipedia://en/33509133) of the line $f(t)=\theta t+x$.

#### Difference of Beatty sequences {#Difference_of_Beatty_sequences}

Let *w* = (*w*<sub>*n*</sub>) be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s. The sequence *w* is Sturmian if it is the difference of non-homogeneous [Beatty sequences](wikipedia://en/9487872), that is, for some $x\in[0,1)$ and some irrational $\theta\in(0,1)$

$$w_n = \lfloor n\theta + x\rfloor - \lfloor (n-1)\theta + x \rfloor$$

for all $n$ or

$$w_n = \lceil n\theta + x\rceil - \lceil (n-1)\theta + x \rceil$$

for all $n$.

#### Coding of irrational rotation {#Coding_of_irrational_rotation}

*Image: Sturmian-sequence-from-irrational-rotation.gif.* Animation showing the Sturmian sequence generated by an irrational rotation with *θ* ≈ 0.2882 and *x* ≈ 0.0789

For $\theta\in [0,1)$, define $T_\theta:[0,1)\to[0,1)$ by $t\mapsto t+\theta\bmod 1$. For $x\in[0,1)$ define the *θ*-coding of *x* to be the sequence (*x*<sub>*n*</sub>) where

$$x_n= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } T_\theta^n(x)\in [0,\theta), \\ 0 & \text{else}. \end{cases}$$

Let *w* be an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s. The sequence *w* is Sturmian if for some $x\in[0,1)$ and some irrational $\theta\in(0,\infty)$, *w* is the *θ*-coding of *x*.

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

::: {#o15 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

A famous example of a (standard) Sturmian word is the [Fibonacci word](wikipedia://en/1702139);[^o16] its slope is $1/\phi$, where $\phi$ is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).

:::

### Balanced aperiodic sequences {#Balanced_aperiodic_sequences}

A set *S* of finite binary words is *balanced* if for each *n* the subset *S*<sub>*n*</sub> of words of length *n* has the property that the [Hamming weight](wikipedia://en/1127884) of the words in *S*<sub>*n*</sub> takes at most two distinct values. A **balanced sequence** is one for which the set of factors is balanced. A balanced sequence has at most *n*+1 distinct factors of length *n*.[^o18] An **aperiodic sequence** is one which does not consist of a finite sequence followed by a finite cycle. An aperiodic sequence has at least *n* + 1 distinct factors of length *n*.[^o18] A sequence is Sturmian if and only if it is balanced and aperiodic.[^o18]

::: {#o20 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Characteristic word"}

### Slope and intercept {#Slope_and_intercept}

A [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ over {0,1} is a Sturmian word if and only if there exist two [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), the *slope* $\alpha$ and the *intercept* $\rho$, with $\alpha$ [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689), such that

$$a_n=\lfloor\alpha(n+1)+\rho\rfloor -\lfloor\alpha n+\rho\rfloor-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor$$

for all $n$.[^o21][^o22] Thus a Sturmian word provides a [discretization](wikipedia://en/330017) of the straight line with slope $\alpha$ and intercept *ρ*. Without loss of generality, we can always assume $0<\alpha<1$, because for any integer *k* we have

$$\lfloor(\alpha + k)(n + 1) + \rho\rfloor - \lfloor(\alpha+k)n + \rho\rfloor - \lfloor\alpha + k\rfloor = a_n.$$

All the Sturmian words corresponding to the same slope $\alpha$ have the same set of factors; the word $c_\alpha$ corresponding to the intercept $\rho=0$ is the **standard word** or **characteristic word** of slope $\alpha$.[^o21] Hence, if $0<\alpha<1$, the characteristic word $c_\alpha$ is the [first difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_difference) of the [Beatty sequence](wikipedia://en/9487872) corresponding to the irrational number $\alpha$.

The standard word $c_\alpha$ is also the limit of a sequence of words $(s_n)_{n \ge 0}$ defined recursively as follows:

Let $[0; d_1+1, d_2, \ldots, d_n, \ldots]$ be the [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) expansion of $\alpha$, and define

- $$s_0=1$$
- $$s_1=0$$
- $$s_{n+1}=s_n^{d_n}s_{n-1}\text{ for }n>0$$

where the product between words is just their [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474). Every word in the sequence $(s_n)_{n>0}$ is a [prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_%28computer_science%29) of the next ones, so that the sequence itself [converges](wikipedia://en/285773) to an infinite word, which is $c_\alpha$.

The infinite sequence of words $(s_n)_{n \ge 0}$ defined by the above recursion is called the **standard sequence** for the standard word $c_\alpha$, and the infinite sequence *d* = (*d*<sub>1</sub>, *d*<sub>2</sub>, *d*<sub>3</sub>, ...) of nonnegative integers, with *d*<sub>1</sub> ≥ 0 and *d*<sub>*n*</sub> > 0 (*n* ≥ 2), is called its **directive sequence**.

A Sturmian word *w* over {0,1} is characteristic if and only if both 0*w* and 1*w* are Sturmian.[^o32]

:::

### Frequencies {#Frequencies}

If *s* is an infinite sequence word and *w* is a finite word, let μ<sub>*N*</sub>(*w*) denote the number of occurrences of *w* as a factor in the prefix of *s* of length *N* + |*w*| − 1. If *μ*<sub>*N*</sub>(*w*) has a limit as *N*→∞, we call this the **frequency** of *w*, denoted by *μ*(*w*).[^o18]

For a Sturmian word *s*, every finite factor has a frequency. The [three-gap theorem](wikipedia://en/57091271) implies that the factors of fixed length *n* have at most three distinct frequencies, and if there are three values then one is the sum of the other two.[^o18]

## Non-binary words {#Non-binary_words}

For words over an alphabet of size *k* greater than 2, we define a Sturmian word to be one with complexity function *n* + *k* − 1.[^o22] They can be described in terms of cutting sequences for *k*-dimensional space.[^o22] An alternative definition is as words of minimal complexity subject to not being ultimately periodic.[^o22]

## Associated real numbers {#Associated_real_numbers}

A real number for which the digits with respect to some fixed base form a Sturmian word is a [transcendental number](wikipedia://en/30325).[^o22]

::: {#o27 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Sturmian morphism"}

## Sturmian endomorphisms {#Sturmian_endomorphisms}

An endomorphism of the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) *B*<sup>∗</sup> on a 2-letter alphabet *B* is **Sturmian** if it maps every Sturmian word to a Sturmian word[^o28][^o29] and **locally Sturmian** if it maps some Sturmian word to a Sturmian word.[^o30] The Sturmian endomorphisms form a submonoid of the monoid of endomorphisms of *B*<sup>∗</sup>.[^o28]

Define endomorphisms φ and ψ of *B*<sup>∗</sup>, where *B* = {0,1}, by φ(0) = 01, φ(1) = 0 and ψ(0) = 10, ψ(1) = 0. Then *I*, φ and ψ are Sturmian,[^o31] and the Sturmian endomorphisms of *B*<sup>∗</sup> are precisely those endomorphisms in the submonoid of the endomorphism monoid generated by {*I*,φ,ψ}.[^o29][^o30][^o32]

A morphism is Sturmian if and only if the image of the word 10010010100101 is a [balanced sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balanced_sequence); that is, for each *n*, the [Hamming weights](wikipedia://en/1127884) of the subwords of length *n* take at most two distinct values.[^o29][^o33]

:::

## History {#History}

Although the study of Sturmian words dates back to [Johann III Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_III_Bernoulli) (1772),[^o35][^o21] it was [Gustav A. Hedlund](wikipedia://en/18794778) and [Marston Morse](wikipedia://en/856523) in 1940 who coined the term *Sturmian* to refer to such sequences,[^o21][^o36] in honor of the mathematician [Jacques Charles François Sturm](wikipedia://en/1181554) due to the relation with the [Sturm comparison theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm_comparison_theorem).[^o22]
