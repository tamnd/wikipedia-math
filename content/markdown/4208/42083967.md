# K-theory of a category

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="K-theory of a category"}

In [algebraic *K*-theory](wikipedia://en/598500), the ***K*-theory of a [category](wikipedia://en/53759)** *C* (usually equipped with some kind of additional data) is a sequence of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) *K*<sub>i</sub>(*C*) associated to it. If *C* is an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063), there is no need for extra data, but in general it only makes sense to speak of K-theory after specifying on *C* a structure of an [exact category](wikipedia://en/10987985), or of a [Waldhausen category](wikipedia://en/12727767), or of a [dg-category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dg-category), or possibly some other variants. Thus, there are several constructions of those groups, corresponding to various kinds of structures put on *C*. Traditionally, the *K*-theory of *C* is *defined* to be the result of a suitable construction, but in some contexts there are more conceptual definitions. For instance, the *K*-theory is a 'universal additive invariant' of dg-categories[^o2] and [small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category) [stable ∞-categories](wikipedia://en/42081371).[^o3]

:::

The motivation for this notion comes from [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) of [rings](wikipedia://en/48404). For a ring *R* [Daniel Quillen](wikipedia://en/435519) in (Quillen 1973) introduced two equivalent ways to find the higher K-theory. The [plus construction](wikipedia://en/598500#The_+-construction) expresses *K*<sub>i</sub>(*R*) in terms of *R* directly, but it's hard to prove properties of the result, including basic ones like functoriality. The other way is to consider the exact category of [projective](wikipedia://en/364488) [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over *R* and to set *K*<sub>i</sub>(*R*) to be the K-theory of that category, defined using the [Q-construction](wikipedia://en/41403415). This approach proved to be more useful, and could be applied to other exact categories as well. Later [Friedhelm Waldhausen](wikipedia://en/20163961) in (Waldhausen 1985) extended the notion of K-theory even further, to very different kinds of categories, including the category of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450).

## K-theory of Waldhausen categories {#K-theory_of_Waldhausen_categories}

In algebra, the **S-construction** is a construction in [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) that produces a model that can be used to define higher K-groups. It is due to [Friedhelm Waldhausen](wikipedia://en/20163961) and concerns a category with cofibrations and weak equivalences; such a category is called a [Waldhausen category](wikipedia://en/12727767) and generalizes Quillen's [exact category](wikipedia://en/10987985). A cofibration can be thought of as analogous to a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538), and a category with cofibrations is one in which, roughly speaking, monomorphisms are stable under [pushouts](wikipedia://en/582024).[^o7] According to Waldhausen, the "S" was chosen to stand for [Graeme B. Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeme_B._Segal).[^o15]

Unlike the [Q-construction](wikipedia://en/41403415), which produces a topological space, the S-construction produces a [simplicial set](wikipedia://en/1027784).

### Details {#Details}

The [arrow category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_category) $Ar(C)$ of a category *C* is a category whose objects are morphisms in *C* and whose morphisms are squares in *C*. Let a finite ordered set $[n] = \{ 0 < 1 < 2 < \cdots < n \}$ be viewed as a category in the usual way.

Let *C* be a category with cofibrations and let $S_n C$ be a category whose objects are functors $f: Ar[n] \to C$ such that, for $i \le j \le k$, $f(i = i) = *$, $f(i \le j) \to f(i \le k)$ is a cofibration, and $f(j \le k)$ is the pushout of $f(i \le j) \to f(i \le k)$ and $f(i \le j) \to f(j = j) = *$. The category $S_n C$ defined in this manner is itself a category with cofibrations. One can therefore iterate the construction, forming the sequence$S^{(m)}C = S \cdots SC$. This sequence is a [spectrum](wikipedia://en/1033697) called the **K-theory spectrum** of *C*.

## The additivity theorem {#The_additivity_theorem}

Most basic properties of algebraic K-theory of categories are consequences of the following important theorem.[^o11] There are versions of it in all available settings. Here's a statement for Waldhausen categories. Notably, it's used to show that the sequence of spaces obtained by the iterated S-construction is an [Ω-spectrum](wikipedia://en/1033697).

Let *C* be a [Waldhausen category](wikipedia://en/12727767). The category of extensions $\mathcal{E}(C)$ has as objects the sequences $A \rightarrowtail B \twoheadrightarrow A'$ in *C*, where the first map is a cofibration, and $B \twoheadrightarrow A'$ is a quotient map, i.e. a [pushout](wikipedia://en/582024) of the first one along the zero map *A* → *0*. This category has a natural Waldhausen structure, and the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) $[ A \rightarrowtail B \twoheadrightarrow A' ] \mapsto (A, A')$ from $\mathcal{E}(C)$ to *C* × *C* respects it. The **additivity theorem** says that the induced map on K-theory spaces $K(\mathcal{E}(C)) \to K(C) \times K(C)$ is a homotopy equivalence.[^o12]

For [dg-categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dg-category) the statement is similar. Let *C* be a small pretriangulated dg-category with a [semiorthogonal decomposition](wikipedia://en/63044039) $C \cong \langle C_1, C_2 \rangle$. Then the map of K-theory spectra K(*C*) → K(*C*<sub>1</sub>) ⊕ K(*C*<sub>2</sub>) is a homotopy equivalence.[^o13] In fact, K-theory is a universal functor satisfying this additivity property and [Morita invariance](wikipedia://en/3296134).[^o2]

:::

## Category of finite sets {#Category_of_finite_sets}

Consider the category of [pointed](wikipedia://en/2390225#P) finite sets. This category has an object $k_+ = \{0, 1, \ldots, k\}$ for every [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *k*, and the morphisms in this category are the functions $f : m_+ \to n_+$ which preserve the zero element. A theorem of [Barratt, Priddy and Quillen](wikipedia://en/34737474) says that the algebraic K-theory of this category is a [sphere spectrum](wikipedia://en/21118593).[^o15]

## Miscellaneous {#Miscellaneous}

More generally in abstract category theory, the K-theory of a category is a type of [decategorification](wikipedia://en/1596969) in which a set is created from an equivalence class of objects in a stable (∞,1)-category, where the elements of the set inherit an [Abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) structure from the [exact sequences](wikipedia://en/138215) in the category.[^o17]

### Group completion method {#Group_completion_method}

The [Grothendieck group](wikipedia://en/1634790) construction is a functor from the category of rings to the category of abelian groups. The higher *K*-theory should then be a functor from the category of rings but to the category of higher objects such as [simplicial abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_abelian_group).

### Topological Hochschild homology {#Topological_Hochschild_homology}

Waldhausen introduced the idea of a trace map from the algebraic *K*-theory of a ring to its [Hochschild homology](wikipedia://en/10499629); by way of this map, information can be obtained about the *K*-theory from the Hochschild homology. Bökstedt factorized this trace map, leading to the idea of a functor known as the topological Hochschild homology of the ring's [Eilenberg–MacLane spectrum](wikipedia://en/1073230).[^o20]

### K-theory of a simplicial ring {#K-theory_of_a_simplicial_ring}

If *R* is a constant simplicial ring, then this is the same thing as *K*-theory of a ring.
