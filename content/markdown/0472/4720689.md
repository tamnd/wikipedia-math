# Adams spectral sequence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Adams spectral sequence"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Adams spectral sequence** is a [spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/803894) introduced by (txt) which computes the [stable homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/2946655) of [topological spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_spaces). Like all spectral sequences, it is a computational tool; it relates [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) theory to what is now called [stable homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/2946655). It is a reformulation using [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), and an extension, of a technique called 'killing homotopy groups' applied by the French school of [Henri Cartan](wikipedia://en/362536) and [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483).

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

For everything below, once and for all, we fix a prime *p*. All spaces are assumed to be [CW complexes](wikipedia://en/353022). The [ordinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology) [cohomology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group) $H^*(X)$ are understood to mean $H^*(X; \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})$.

The primary goal of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) is to try to understand the collection of all maps, up to homotopy, between arbitrary spaces *X* and *Y*. This is extraordinarily ambitious: in particular, when *X* is $S^n$, these maps form the *n*th [homotopy group](wikipedia://en/297506) of *Y*. A more reasonable (but still very difficult!) goal is to understand the set $[X, Y]$ of maps (up to homotopy) that remain after we apply the [suspension functor](wikipedia://en/1251702) a large number of times. We call this the collection of stable maps from *X* to *Y*. (This is the starting point of [stable homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/2946655); more modern treatments of this topic begin with the concept of a [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_%28homotopy_theory%29). Adams' original work did not use spectra, and we avoid further mention of them in this section to keep the content here as elementary as possible.)

The set $[X, Y]$ turns out to be an abelian group, and if *X* and *Y* are reasonable spaces this group is finitely generated. To figure out what this group is, we first isolate a prime *p*. In an attempt to compute the *p*-torsion of $[X, Y]$, we look at cohomology: send $[X, Y]$ to Hom(*H*<sup>\*</sup>(*Y*), *H*<sup>\*</sup>(*X*)). This is a good idea because cohomology groups are usually tractable to compute.

The key idea is that $H^*(X)$ is more than just a graded [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), and more still than a graded [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) (via the [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684)). The representability of the cohomology functor makes *H*<sup>\*</sup>(*X*) a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over the algebra of its stable [cohomology operations](wikipedia://en/1226719), the [Steenrod algebra](wikipedia://en/2146220) *A*. Thinking about *H*<sup>\*</sup>(*X*) as an *A*-module forgets some cup product structure, but the gain is enormous: Hom(*H*<sup>\*</sup>(*Y*), *H*<sup>\*</sup>(*X*)) can now be taken to be *A*-linear! A priori, the *A*-module sees no more of \[*X*, *Y*\] than it did when we considered it to be a map of vector spaces over F<sub>*p*</sub>. But we can now consider the derived functors of Hom in the category of *A*-modules, [Ext](wikipedia://en/840758)<sub>*A*</sub><sup>*r*</sup>(*H*<sup>\*</sup>(*Y*), *H*<sup>\*</sup>(*X*)). These acquire a second grading from the grading on *H*<sup>\*</sup>(*Y*), and so we obtain a two-dimensional "page" of algebraic data. The Ext groups are designed to measure the failure of Hom's preservation of algebraic structure, so this is a reasonable step.

The point of all this is that *A* is so large that the above sheet of cohomological data contains all the information we need to recover the *p*-primary part of \[*X*, *Y*\], which is homotopy data. This is a major accomplishment because cohomology was designed to be computable, while homotopy was designed to be powerful. This is the content of the Adams spectral sequence.

## Classical formulation {#Classical_formulation}

### Formulation for computing homotopy groups of spectra {#Formulation_for_computing_homotopy_groups_of_spectra}

The classical Adams spectral sequence can be stated for any [connective spectrum](wikipedia://en/41125081) $X$ of [finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_finite_type), meaning $\pi_i(X)=0$ for $i < 0$ and $\pi_i(X)$ is a finitely generated Abelian group in each degree. Then, there is a spectral sequence $E_*^{*,*}(X)$[^o6] such that

1. $E_2^{s,t} = \text{Ext}_{A_p}^{s,t}(H^*(X), \mathbb{Z}/p)$ for $A_p$ the mod $p$ Steenrod algebra
1. For $X$ of finite type, $E_\infty^{*,*}$ is a bigraded group associated with a filtration of $\pi_*(X)\otimes \mathbb{Z}_p$ (the [p-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integers))

Note that this implies for $X = \mathbb{S}$, this computes the $p$-torsion of the homotopy groups of the [sphere spectrum](wikipedia://en/21118593), i.e. the stable homotopy groups of the spheres. Also, because for any CW-complex $Y$ we can consider the suspension spectrum $\Sigma^\infty Y$, this gives the statement of the previous formulation as well.

This statement generalizes a little bit further by replacing the $\mathcal{A}_p$-module $\mathbb{Z}/p$ with the cohomology groups $H^*(Y)$ for some connective spectrum $Y$ (or topological space $Y$). This is because the construction of the spectral sequence uses a "free" resolution of $H^*(X)$ as an $\mathcal{A}_p$-module, hence we can compute the Ext groups with $H^*(Y)$ as the second entry. We therefore get a spectral sequence with $E_2$-page given by

> $$E_2^{t,s} = \text{Ext}_{\mathcal{A}_p}^{s,t}(H^*(X),H^*(Y))$$

which has the convergence property of being isomorphic to the graded pieces of a filtration of the $p$-torsion of the stable homotopy group of homotopy classes of maps between $X$ and $Y$, that is

> $$E_2^{s,t} \Rightarrow \pi_{t-s}^{\mathbb{S}}([X,Y])\otimes \mathbb{Z}_{p}$$

### Spectral sequence for the stable homotopy groups of spheres {#Spectral_sequence_for_the_stable_homotopy_groups_of_spheres}

For example, if we let both spectra be the sphere spectrum, so $X = Y = \mathbb{S}$, then the Adams spectral sequence has the convergence property

> $$E_2^{t,s} = \text{Ext}_{\mathcal{A}_p}^{s,t}(H^*(\mathbb{S}),H^*(\mathbb{S})) \Rightarrow \pi_{t-s}(\mathbb{S})\otimes \mathbb{Z}_p$$

giving a technical tool for approaching a computation of the stable homotopy groups of spheres. It turns out that many of the first terms can be computed explicitly from purely algebraic information[^o8]<sup>pp 23–25</sup>. Also note that we can rewrite $H^*(\mathbb{S}) = \mathbb{Z}/p$, so the $E_2$-page is

> $$E_2^{t,s} = \operatorname{Ext}_{\mathcal{A}_p}^{s,t}(\mathbb{Z}/p,\mathbb{Z}/p) \Rightarrow \pi_{t-s}(\mathbb{S})\otimes \mathbb{Z}_p$$

We include this calculation information below for $p=2$.

### Ext terms from the resolution {#Ext_terms_from_the_resolution}

Given the [Adams resolution](wikipedia://en/66326711)

> $$\cdots \to H^*(F_2) \to H^*(F_1) \to H^*(F_0) \to H^*(X)$$

we have the $E_1$-terms as

> $$E_1^{s,t} = \operatorname{Hom}^t_{\mathcal{A}_p}(H^*(F_s),H^*(Y))$$

for the graded Hom-groups. Then the $E_1$-page can be written as

> $$E_1 = \begin{array}{c|ccc} 3 & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\ 2 & \text{Hom}^2(H^*(F_0), H^*(Y)) & \text{Hom}^2(H^*(F_1), H^*(Y)) & \text{Hom}^2(H^*(F_2), H^*(Y)) & \cdots \\ 1 & \text{Hom}^1(H^*(F_0), H^*(Y)) & \text{Hom}^1(H^*(F_1), H^*(Y)) & \text{Hom}^1(H^*(F_2), H^*(Y)) & \cdots \\ 0 & \text{Hom}^0(H^*(F_0), H^*(Y)) & \text{Hom}^0(H^*(F_1), H^*(Y)) & \text{Hom}^0(H^*(F_2), H^*(Y)) & \cdots \\ \hline & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{array}$$

so the degree of $s$ can be thought of how "deep" in the Adams resolution we go before we can find the generators.

## Calculations {#Calculations}

The sequence itself is not an algorithmic device, but lends itself to problem solving in particular cases.

### Grading of the Differential {#Grading_of_the_Differential}

The $r$th Adams differential always goes to the left 1, and up $r$. That is,

> $$d_r \colon E_r^{s, t} \to E_r^{s-1,t+r}.$$

### Examples with Eilenberg–Maclane spectra {#Examples_with_Eilenberg–Maclane_spectra}

Some of the simplest calculations are with [Eilenberg–Maclane spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilenberg–Maclane_spectrum) such as $X = H\mathbb{Z}$ and $X = H\mathbb{Z}/(p^k)$.[^o6] For the first case, we have the $E_1$ page

> $$E_1^{s,t} = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}/p & \text{ if } t = s \\ 0 & \text{ otherwise } \end{cases}$$

giving a collapsed spectral sequence, hence $E_1 = E_\infty$. This can be rewritten as

> $$\text{Ext}^{s,t}_{\mathcal{A}_p}(H^*(H\mathbb{Z}), \mathbb{Z}/p) = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}/p & \text{ if } t = s \\ 0 & \text{ if } t \neq s \end{cases}$$

giving the $E_2$-page. For the other case, note there is a cofiber sequence

> $$H\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\cdot p^k} H\mathbb{Z} \to H\mathbb{Z}/p^k \to \Sigma H\mathbb{Z}$$

which ends up giving a splitting in cohomology, so $H^*(H\mathbb{Z}/p^k) = H^*(H\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^*(\Sigma H\mathbb{Z})$ as $\mathcal{A}_p$-modules. Then, the $E_2$-page of $H^*(H\mathbb{Z}/p)$ can be read as

> $$E_2^{s,t} = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}/p & \text{if } t-s = 0,1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise } \end{cases}$$

The expected $E_\infty$-page is

> $$E_\infty^{s,t} = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z}/p^k & \text{ if } t = s \\ 0 & \text{ otherwise } \end{cases}.$$

The only way for this spectral sequence to converge to this page is if is there are non-trivial differentials supported on every element with Adams grading $(s, s+1)$.

### Other applications {#Other_applications}

Adams' original use for his spectral sequence was the first proof of the [Hopf invariant](wikipedia://en/4417198) 1 problem: $\mathbb{R}^n$ admits a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) structure only for *n* = 1, 2, 4, or 8. He subsequently found a much shorter proof using cohomology operations in [K-theory](wikipedia://en/246748).

The [Thom isomorphism theorem](wikipedia://en/1243451) relates [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) to stable homotopy theory, and this is where the Adams spectral sequence found its first major use: in 1960, [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) and [Sergei Novikov](wikipedia://en/628515) used the Adams spectral sequence to compute the coefficient ring of [complex cobordism](wikipedia://en/1033666). Further, Milnor and [C. T. C. Wall](wikipedia://en/2613478) used the spectral sequence to prove Thom's conjecture on the structure of the oriented [cobordism](wikipedia://en/391832) ring: two oriented manifolds are cobordant [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) their [Pontryagin](wikipedia://en/385944#Pontryagin_numbers) and [Stiefel–Whitney numbers](wikipedia://en/877761#Stiefel–Whitney_numbers) agree.

## Stable homotopy groups of spheres {#Stable_homotopy_groups_of_spheres}

*Image: E 2 page of Adams spectral sequence of spheres for p = 2.png.* Visual diagram demonstrating the $E_2$ page of the Adams spectral sequence computing the stable homotopy groups of spheres. The dots represent elements left over from the $E_1$ page and the diagonal lines moving up and to the left represent various differentials in the spectral sequence. The differential $d_r$ moves one unit to the left and $r$ units upward. The vertical lines are used as a book-keeping tool for determining the structure of the torsion groups. Moreover, they represent multiplication by $2$. The lines moving up and right by one unit represent multiplication by $h_1$.

Using the spectral sequence above for $X = Y = \mathbb{S}$ we can compute several terms explicitly, giving some of the first stable homotopy groups of spheres.[^o8] For $p=2$ this amounts to looking at the $E_2$-page with

> $$E_2^{s,t} = \text{Ext}_{\mathcal{A}_2}^{s,t}(\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z}/2)$$

This can be done by first looking at the Adams resolution of $\mathbb{Z}/2$. Since $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is in degree $0$, we have a surjection

> $$\mathcal{A}_2\cdot \iota \to \mathbb{Z}/2$$

where $\mathcal{A}_2$ has a generator in degree $0$ denoted $\iota$. The kernel $K_0$ consists of all elements $Sq^I\iota$ for admissible monomials $Sq^I$ generating $\mathcal{A}_2$, hence we have a map

> $$\bigoplus_{I \text{ admissible}} \mathcal{A}_2\cdot Sq^I\iota \to K_0$$

and we denote each of the generators mapping to $Sq^i\iota$ in the direct sum as $\alpha_i$, and the rest of the generators as $Sq^I\alpha_j$ for some $j$. For example,

> $$\begin{aligned} \alpha_1 \mapsto Sq^1\iota & &Sq^2\alpha_1 \mapsto Sq^{2,1}\iota \\ \alpha_2 \mapsto Sq^2\iota & & Sq^1\alpha_2 \mapsto Sq^3\iota \\ \alpha_4 \mapsto Sq^4\iota & & Sq^3\alpha_1 \mapsto Sq^{3,1}\iota \\ \alpha_8 \mapsto Sq^8 & & Sq^2\alpha_2 \mapsto Sq^{3,1}\iota \end{aligned}$$

Notice that the last two elements of $\alpha_i$ map to the same element, which follows from the Adem relations. Also, there are elements in the kernel, such as $Sq^1\alpha_1$ since

> $$Sq^1\alpha_1 \mapsto Sq^1Sq^1\iota = 0$$

because of the Adem relation. We call the generator of this element in $F_2$, $\beta_2$. We can apply the same process and get a kernel $K_1$, resolve it, and so on. When we do, we get an $E_1$-page which looks like

> $$E_1^{s,t} = \begin{array}{c|ccc} \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\ 4 & Sq^4\iota, Sq^{3,1}\iota & Sq^{2,1}\alpha_1, Sq^{3}\alpha_1, Sq^2\alpha_2, \alpha_4 & Sq^2\beta_2 & \cdots \\ 3 & Sq^3\iota, Sq^{2,1}\iota &Sq^2\alpha_1, Sq^1\alpha_2 & Sq^1\beta_2& \cdots \\ 2 & Sq^2\iota & \alpha_2, Sq^1\alpha_1 & \beta_2 & \cdots \\ 1 & Sq^1\iota & \alpha_1 & 0 & \cdots \\ 0 & \iota & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ \hline & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{array}$$

which can be expanded by computer up to degree $100$ with relative ease. Using the found generators and relations, we can calculate the $E_2$-page with relative ease. Sometimes homotopy theorists like to rearrange these elements by having the horizontal index denote $s$ and the vertical index denote $t - s$ giving a different type of diagram for the $E_2$-page[^o8]<sup>pg 21</sup>. See the diagram above for more information.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The Adams–Novikov spectral sequence is a generalization of the Adams spectral sequence introduced by (Novikov 1967) where ordinary cohomology is replaced by a [generalized cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_cohomology_theory), often [complex bordism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_bordism) or [Brown–Peterson cohomology](wikipedia://en/13698492). This requires knowledge of the algebra of stable cohomology operations for the cohomology theory in question, but enables calculations which are completely intractable with the classical Adams spectral sequence.
