# Limit of a function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Limit of a function"}

Delta-epsilon

{| class="wikitable" style="width:100%; margin:0px;" !$x$!!$\frac{\sin x}{x}$ |- |1||0.841471... |- |0.1||0.998334... |- |0.01||0.999983... |}

Although the function $\tfrac{\sin x}{x}$ is not defined at zero, as $x$ becomes closer and closer to zero, $\tfrac{\sin x}{x}$ becomes arbitrarily close to 1. In other words, the limit of $\tfrac{\sin x}{x},$ as $x$ approaches zero, equals 1.

:::

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **limit of a function** is a fundamental concept in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) concerning the behavior of that [function](wikipedia://en/185427) near a particular [input](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable) which may or may not be in the [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643) of the function.

Formal definitions, first devised in the early 19th century, are given below. Informally, a function $f$ assigns an [output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable) $f(x)$ to every input $x$. We say that the function has a limit $L$ at an input $p$, if $f(x)$ gets closer and closer to $L$ as $x$ moves closer and closer to $p$. More specifically, the output value can be made *arbitrarily* close to $L$ if the input to $f$ is taken *sufficiently* close to $p$. On the other hand, if some inputs very close to $p$ are taken to outputs that stay a fixed distance apart, then we say the limit *does not exist*.

The notion of a limit has many applications in [modern calculus](wikipedia://en/5176#Modern). In particular, the many definitions of [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) employ the concept of limit: roughly, a function is continuous if all of its limits agree with the values of the function. The concept of limit also appears in the definition of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921): in the calculus of one variable, this is the limiting value of the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of [secant lines](wikipedia://en/241886) to the [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352).

## History {#History}

Although implicit in the [development of calculus](wikipedia://en/746117) of the 17th and 18th centuries, the modern idea of the limit of a function goes back to [Bernard Bolzano](wikipedia://en/302185) who, in 1817, introduced the basics of the epsilon-delta technique (see (ε, δ)-definition of limit below) to define continuous functions. However, his work was not known during his lifetime.[^o3] Bruce Pourciau argues that [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), in his 1687 *[Principia](wikipedia://en/48781)*, demonstrates a more sophisticated understanding of limits than he is generally given credit for, including being the first to present an epsilon argument.[^o4][^o5]

In his 1821 book [Cours d'analyse](wikipedia://en/41007672), [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) discussed variable quantities, [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990) and limits, and defined continuity of $y=f(x)$ by saying that an infinitesimal change in $x$ necessarily produces an infinitesimal change in $y$, while Grabiner claims that he used a rigorous epsilon-delta definition in proofs.[^o6] In 1861, [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459) first introduced the epsilon-delta definition of limit in the form it is usually written today.[^o7] He also introduced the notations $\lim$ and $\textstyle \lim\limits_{x \to x_0}. \displaystyle$[^o8]

The modern notation of placing the arrow below the limit symbol is due to [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599), which is introduced in his book *[A Course of Pure Mathematics](wikipedia://en/641270)* in 1908.[^o9]

## Functions of a single variable {#Functions_of_a_single_variable}

Informally, a function $f(x)$ has limit $L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ if $f(x)$ approximates $L$ for $x$ near $a$. More precisely, the value of $f(x)$ is within a given tolerance of $L$, provided $x$ is within a corresponding tolerance of $a$. These two tolerances are often denoted, respectively, by $\varepsilon$ (the tolerance in the value of $f(x)$) and $\delta$ (the corresponding tolerance in $x$).[^o11] The value of the function *at* $x=a$ is usually omitted from the approximation; for example, in many cases where limits are useful, the function has *no* value at $x=a$ (it is undefined there).

### $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-definition of limit {#-definition_of_limit}

*Image: Epsilon-delta limit.svg.* For the depicted $f$, $a$, and $b$, we can ensure that the value $f(x)$ is within an arbitrarily small interval $(b - \varepsilon, b + \varepsilon)$ by restricting $x$ to a sufficiently small interval $(a - \delta, a + \delta).$ Hence $f(x) \to b$ as $x \to a$.

Suppose $f: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is a function defined on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), and there are two real numbers $p$ and $L$. One would say: "The limit of $f$ of $x$, as $x$ approaches $p$, exists, and it equals $L$". and write,[^o13]

$$\lim_{x \to p} f(x) = L,$$

or alternatively, say "$f(x)$ tends to $L$ as $x$ tends to $p$", and write,

$$f(x) \to L \text{ as } x \to p,$$

if the following property holds: for every real $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a real $\delta > 0$ such that for all real $x$, $0 < |x - p| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.[^o13] Symbolically,

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 ) \, (\exists \delta > 0) \, (\forall x \in \mathbb{R}) \, (0 < |x - p| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

For example, one may say

$$\lim_{x \to 2} (4x + 1) = 9$$

because for every real $\varepsilon > 0$, we can take $\delta = \varepsilon/4$, so that for all real $x$, if $0 < \lvert x - 2\rvert < \delta$, then $\lvert 4x + 1 - 9\rvert < \varepsilon$.

A more general definition applies for functions defined on [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of the real line. Let $S$ be a subset of $\mathbb{R}.$ Let $f: S \to \mathbb{R}$ be a [real-valued function](wikipedia://en/22284647). Let $p$ be a point such that there exists some open interval $(a, b)$ containing $p$ with $(a,p)\cup (p,b) \subset S.$ It is then said that the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $L$, if:

For every real $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a real $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x \in (a, b)$, $0 < |x - p| < \delta$ implies that $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$. Symbolically,

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 ) \, (\exists \delta > 0) \, (\forall x \in (a, b)) \, (0 < |x - p| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

For example, one may say

$$\lim_{x \to 1} \sqrt{x+3} = 2$$

because for every real $\varepsilon > 0$, we can take $\delta = \varepsilon$, so that for all real $x \ge -3$, if $0 < \lvert x - 1\rvert < \delta$, then $\lvert f(x) - 2\rvert < \varepsilon$. In this example, $S = [-3, \infty)$ contains open intervals around the point 1 (for example, the interval (0, 2)).

Here, note that the value of the limit does not depend on $f$ being defined at $p$, nor on the value $f(p)$—if it is defined. For example, let $f: [0,1)\cup (1,2] \to \mathbb{R}, f(x) = \tfrac{2x^2 - x - 1}{x-1}.$

$$\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = 3$$

because for every $\varepsilon > 0$, we can take $\delta = \varepsilon/2$, so that for all real $x \ne 1$, if $0 < \lvert x - 1\rvert < \delta$, then $\lvert f(x) - 3\rvert < \varepsilon$. Note that here $f(1)$ is undefined.

In fact, a limit can exist in $\{p\in \mathbb{R}\, |\, \exists (a,b) \subset \mathbb{R} : \, p \in (a,b) \text{ and } (a,p)\cup (p, b) \subset S\},$ which equals $\operatorname{int} S \cup \operatorname{iso} S^c,$ where $\operatorname{int} S$ is the [interior](wikipedia://en/55610) of $S$, and $\operatorname{iso} S^{c}$ are the [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557) of the complement of $S$. In our previous example where $S = [0,1) \cup (1,2],$ $\operatorname{int} S = (0,1) \cup (1,2),$ $\operatorname{iso} S^c = \{1\}.$ We see, specifically, this definition of limit allows a limit to exist at 1, but not at 0 or 2.

The letters $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ can be understood as "error" and "distance". In fact, Cauchy used $\varepsilon$ as an abbreviation for "error" in some of his work,[^o6] though in his definition of continuity, he used an infinitesimal $\alpha$ rather than either $\varepsilon$ or $\delta$ (see *[Cours d'Analyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cours_d'Analyse)*). In these terms, the error (*ε*) in the measurement of the value at the limit can be made as small as desired by reducing the distance (*δ*) to the limit point. As discussed below, this definition also works for functions in a more general context. The idea that $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$ represent distances helps suggest these generalizations.

### Existence and one-sided limits {#Existence_and_one-sided_limits}

*Image: Upper semi.svg.* The limit as $x \to x_0^+$ differs from that as $x \to x_0^-.$ Therefore, the limit as $x \to x_{0}$ does not exist.

Alternatively, $x$ may approach $p$ from above (right) or below (left), in which case the limits may be written as

$$\lim_{x \to p^+}f(x) = L$$

or

$$\lim_{x \to p^-}f(x) = L$$

*Image: Undefined limit examples.png.* The first three functions have points for which the limit does not exist, while the function$f(x) = \frac{\sin(x)}{x}$is not defined at $x = 0$, but its limit does exist.

respectively. If these limits exist at p and are equal there, then this can be referred to as *the* limit of $f(x)$ at $p$.[^n1] If the one-sided limits exist at $p$, but are unequal, then there is no limit at $p$ (i.e., the limit at $p$ does not exist). If either one-sided limit does not exist at $p$, then the limit at $p$ also does not exist.

A formal definition is as follows. The **limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ from above is $L$** if:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $0 < x - p < \delta$, we have $\lvert f(x) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 ) \, (\exists \delta > 0) \, (\forall x \in (a,b))\, (0 < x - p < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

The **limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ from below is $L$** if:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $0 < p - x < \delta$, we have $\lvert f(x) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists \delta > 0) \, (\forall x \in (a,b)) \, (0 < p - x < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

If the limit does not exist, then the [oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation_of_a_function_at_a_point) of $f$ at $p$ is non-zero.

### More general definition using limit points and subsets {#More_general_definition_using_limit_points_and_subsets}

Limits can also be defined by approaching from subsets of the domain.

In general:[^o17] Let $f : S \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function defined on some $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}.$ Let $p$ be a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of some $T \subset S$—that is, $p$ is the limit of some sequence of elements of $T$ distinct from $p$. Then we say **the limit of $f$, as $x$ approaches $p$ from values in $T$, is $L$**, written

$$\lim_{ {x \to p} \atop {x \in T} } f(x) = L$$

if the following holds: For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x \in T$, $0 < \lvert x - p\rvert < \delta$ implies that $\lvert f(x) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists \delta > 0) \,(\forall x \in T)\, (0 < |x - p| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

Note, $T$ can be any subset of $S$, the domain of $f$. And the limit might depend on the selection of $T$. This generalization includes as special cases limits on an interval, as well as left-handed limits of real-valued functions (e.g., by taking $T$ to be an open interval of the form $(-\infty, a)$), and right-handed limits (e.g., by taking $T$ to be an open interval of the form $(a, \infty)$). It also extends the notion of one-sided limits to the included endpoints of (half-)closed intervals, so the [square root function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root_function) $f(x) = \sqrt x$ can have limit 0 as $x$ approaches 0 from above:

$$\lim_{ {x\to 0} \atop {x\in [0, \infty)} } \sqrt{x} = 0$$

since for every $\varepsilon > 0$, we may take $\delta = \varepsilon^{2}$ such that for all $x \ge 0$, if $0 < \lvert x - 0\rvert < \delta$, then $\lvert f(x) - 0\rvert < \varepsilon$.

This definition allows a limit to be defined at limit points of the domain $S$, if a suitable subset $T$ which has the same limit point is chosen.

Notably, the previous two-sided definition works on $\operatorname{int} S \cup \operatorname{iso} S^c,$ which is a subset of the limit points of $S$.

For example, let $S = [0,1)\cup (1, 2].$ The previous two-sided definition would work at $1 \in \operatorname{iso} S^c = \{1\},$ but it wouldn't work at 0 or 2, which are limit points of $S$.

### Deleted versus non-deleted limits {#Deleted_versus_non-deleted_limits}

The definition of limit given here does not depend on how (or whether) $f$ is defined at $p$. Bartle[^o19] refers to this as a *deleted limit*, because it excludes the value of $f$ at $p$. The corresponding **non-deleted limit** does depend on the value of $f$ at $p$, if $p$ is in the domain of $f$. Let $f : S \to \mathbb{R}$ be a real-valued function. **The non-deleted limit of $f$, as $x$ approaches $p$, is $L$** if

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x \in S$, $|x - p| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists \delta > 0) \, (\forall x \in S)\, (|x - p| < \delta \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

The definition is the same, except that the neighborhood $|x - p| < \delta$ now includes the point $p$, in contrast to the [deleted neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deleted_neighborhood) $0 < |x - p| < \delta$. This makes the definition of a non-deleted limit less general. One of the advantages of working with non-deleted limits is that they allow to state the theorem about limits of compositions without any constraints on the functions (other than the existence of their non-deleted limits).[^o20]

Bartle[^o19] notes that although by "limit" some authors do mean this non-deleted limit, deleted limits are the most popular.[^o21]

### Examples {#Examples}

#### Non-existence of one-sided limit(s) {#Non-existence_of_one-sided_limit(s)}

*Image: Discontinuity essential.svg.* Function without a limit at an [essential discontinuity](wikipedia://en/2661487)

The function

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} \sin\frac{5}{x-1} & \text{ for } x<1 \\ 0 & \text{ for } x=1 \\[2pt] \frac{1}{10x-10}& \text{ for } x>1 \end{cases}$$

has no limit at $x_{0} = 1$ (the left-hand limit does not exist due to the oscillatory nature of the sine function, and the right-hand limit does not exist due to the asymptotic behaviour of the reciprocal function, see picture), but has a limit at every other $x$-coordinate.

The function

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} 1 & x \text{ rational } \\ 0 & x \text{ irrational } \end{cases}$$

(a.k.a., the [Dirichlet function](wikipedia://en/630005)) has no limit at any $x$-coordinate.

#### Non-equality of one-sided limits {#Non-equality_of_one-sided_limits}

The function

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{ for } x < 0 \\ 2 & \text{ for } x \ge 0 \end{cases}$$

has a limit at every non-zero $x$-coordinate (the limit equals 1 for negative $x$ and equals 2 for positive $x$). The limit at $x = 0$ does not exist (the left-hand limit equals 1, whereas the right-hand limit equals 2).

#### Limits at only one point {#Limits_at_only_one_point}

The functions

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} x & x \text{ rational } \\ 0 & x \text{ irrational } \end{cases}$$

and

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} |x| & x \text{ rational } \\ 0 & x \text{ irrational } \end{cases}$$

both have a limit at $x = 0$ and it equals 0.

#### Limits at countably many points {#Limits_at_countably_many_points}

The function

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} \sin x & x \text{ irrational } \\ 1 & x \text{ rational } \end{cases}$$

has a limit at any $x$-coordinate of the form $\tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2n\pi,$ where $n$ is any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563).

### Limits at infinity {#Limits_at_infinity}

[Limit at infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_at_infinity) redirects here

*Image: Limit Infinity SVG.svg.* The limit of this function at infinity exists

Let $f:S \to \mathbb{R}$ be a function defined on $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}.$ **The limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches infinity is $L$**, denoted

$$\lim_{x \to \infty}f(x) = L,$$

means that: For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $c > 0$ such that whenever $+x > c$, we have $\lvert f(x) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists c > 0) \,(\forall x \in S) \,(x > c \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

Similarly, **the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches minus infinity is $L$**, denoted

$$\lim_{x \to -\infty}f(x) = L,$$

means that: For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $c > 0$ such that whenever $x < -c$, we have $\lvert f(x) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0)\, (\exists c > 0) \,(\forall x \in S)\, (x < -c \implies |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon).$$

For example,

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \left(-\frac{3\sin x}{x} + 4\right) = 4$$

because for every $\varepsilon > 0$, we can take $c = 3/\varepsilon$ such that for all real $x$, if $x > c$, then $\lvert f(x) - 4\rvert < \varepsilon$.

Another example is that

$$\lim_{x \to -\infty}e^{x} = 0$$

because for every $\varepsilon > 0$, we can take $c = \max\{1, -\ln(\varepsilon)\}$ such that for all real $x$, if $x < -c$, then $\lvert f(x) - 0\rvert < \varepsilon$.

:::

### Infinite limits {#Infinite_limits}

For a function whose values grow without bound, the function diverges and the usual limit does not exist. However, in this case one may introduce limits with infinite values.

Let $f:S \to\mathbb{R}$ be a function defined on $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}.$ The statement **the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is infinity**, denoted

$$\lim_{x \to p} f(x) = \infty,$$

means that: For every $N > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $0 < \lvert x - p\rvert < \delta$, we have $f(x) > N$.

$$(\forall N > 0)\, (\exists \delta > 0)\, (\forall x \in S)\, (0 < | x-p | < \delta \implies f(x) > N) .$$

The statement **the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is minus infinity**, denoted

$$\lim_{x \to p} f(x) = -\infty,$$

means that: For every $N > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $0 < \lvert x - p\rvert < \delta$, we have $f(x) < -N$.

$$(\forall N > 0) \, (\exists \delta > 0) \, (\forall x \in S)\, (0 < | x-p | < \delta \implies f(x) < -N) .$$

For example,

$$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{1}{(x-1)^2} = \infty$$

because for every $N > 0$, we can take $\delta = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}\delta} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt N}$ such that for all real $x > 0$, if $0 < x - 1 < \delta$, then $f(x) > N$.

These ideas can be used together to produce definitions for different combinations, such as

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = \infty,$$

or $\lim_{x \to p^+}f(x) = -\infty.$

For example,

$$\lim_{x \to 0^+} \ln x = -\infty$$

because for every $N > 0$, we can take $\delta = e^{-N}$ such that for all real $x > 0$, if $0 < x - 0 < \delta$, then $f(x) < -N$.

Limits involving infinity are connected with the concept of [asymptotes](wikipedia://en/3107).

These notions of a limit attempt to provide a metric space interpretation to limits at infinity. In fact, they are consistent with the topological space definition of limit if

- a neighborhood of −∞ is defined to contain an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[-\infty, c)$ for some $c \in \mathbb{R},$
- a neighborhood of ∞ is defined to contain an interval $(c, \infty]$ where $c \in \mathbb{R},$ and
- a neighborhood of $a \in \mathbb{R}$ is defined in the normal way metric space $\mathbb{R}.$

In this case, $\overline \mathbb{R}$ is a topological space and any function of the form $f : X \to Y$ with $X, Y \subseteq \overline \mathbb{R}$ is subject to the topological definition of a limit. Note that with this topological definition, it is easy to define infinite limits at finite points, which have not been defined above in the metric sense.

### Alternative notation {#Alternative_notation}

Many authors[^o33] allow for the [projectively extended real line](wikipedia://en/1014534) to be used as a way to include infinite values as well as [extended real line](wikipedia://en/51698). With this notation, the extended real line is given as $\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}$ and the projectively extended real line is $\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}$ where a neighborhood of ∞ is a set of the form $\{x: |x| > c\}.$ The advantage is that one only needs three definitions for limits (left, right, and central) to cover all the cases. As presented above, for a completely rigorous account, we would need to consider 15 separate cases for each combination of infinities (five directions: −∞, left, central, right, and +∞; three bounds: −∞, finite, or +∞). There are also noteworthy pitfalls. For example, when working with the extended real line, $x^{-1}$ does not possess a central limit (which is normal):

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}}{1\over x} = +\infty, \quad \lim_{x \to 0^{-}}{1\over x} = -\infty.$$

In contrast, when working with the projective real line, infinities (much like 0) are unsigned, so, the central limit *does* exist in that context:

$$\lim_{x \to 0^{+}}{1\over x} = \lim_{x \to 0^{-}}{1\over x} = \lim_{x \to 0}{1\over x} = \infty.$$

In fact there are a plethora of conflicting formal systems in use. In certain applications of [numerical differentiation and integration](wikipedia://en/21506), it is, for example, convenient to have [signed zeroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_zero). A simple reason has to do with the converse of $\lim_{x \to 0^{-}}{x^{-1}} = -\infty,$ namely, it is convenient for $\lim_{x \to -\infty}{x^{-1}} = -0$ to be considered true. Such zeroes can be seen as an approximation to [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990).

### Limits at infinity for rational functions {#Limits_at_infinity_for_rational_functions}

*Image: Tamasol SVG.svg.* Horizontal asymptote about $y = 4$

There are three basic rules for evaluating limits at infinity for a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) $f(x) = \tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ (where $p$ and $q$ are polynomials):

- If the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of $p$ is greater than the degree of $q$, then the limit is positive or negative infinity depending on the signs of the leading coefficients;
- If the degree of $p$ and $q$ are equal, the limit is the leading coefficient of $p$ divided by the leading coefficient of $q$;
- If the degree of $p$ is less than the degree of $q$, the limit is 0.

If the limit at infinity exists, it represents a horizontal asymptote at $y = L$. Polynomials do not have horizontal asymptotes; such asymptotes may however occur with rational functions.

## Functions of more than one variable {#Functions_of_more_than_one_variable}

### Ordinary limits {#Ordinary_limits}

By noting that $\lvert x - p\rvert$ represents a [distance](wikipedia://en/39378), the definition of a limit can be extended to functions of more than one variable. In the case of a function $f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on $S \times T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2,$ we defined the limit as follows: **the limit of $f$ as $(x, y)$ approaches $(p, q)$ is $L$**, written

$$\lim_{(x,y) \to (p, q)} f(x, y) = L$$

if the following condition holds:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$ and $y$ in $T$, whenever $0 < \sqrt{(x-p)^2 + (y-q)^2} < \delta,$ we have $\lvert f(x, y) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$,[^o38]

or formally:

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0)\, (\exists \delta > 0)\, (\forall x \in S) \, (\forall y \in T)\, (0 < \sqrt{(x-p)^2 + (y-q)^2} < \delta \implies |f(x, y) - L| < \varepsilon)).$$

Here $\sqrt{(x-p)^2 + (y-q)^2}$ is the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between $(x, y)$ and $(p, q)$. (This can in fact be replaced by any [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\lvert \lvert (x, y) - (p, q)\rvert\rvert$, and be extended to any number of variables.)

For example, we may say

$$\lim_{(x,y) \to (0, 0)} \frac{x^4}{x^2+y^2} = 0$$

because for every $\varepsilon > 0$, we can take $\delta = \sqrt \varepsilon$ such that for all real $x \ne 0$ and real $y \ne 0$, if $0 < \sqrt{(x-0)^2 + (y-0)^2} < \delta,$ then $\lvert f(x, y) - 0\rvert < \varepsilon$.

Similar to the case in single variable, the value of $f$ at $(p, q)$ does not matter in this definition of limit.

For such a multivariable limit to exist, this definition requires the value of $f$ approaches $L$ along every possible path approaching $(p, q)$.[^n2] In the above example, the function

$$f(x, y) = \frac{x^4}{x^2+y^2}$$

satisfies this condition. This can be seen by considering the [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates)

$$(x,y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) \to (0, 0),$$

which gives

$$\lim_{r \to 0} f(r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta) = \lim_{r \to 0} \frac{r^4 \cos^4 \theta}{r^2} = \lim_{r \to 0} r^2 \cos^4 \theta.$$

Here $\theta = \theta(r)$ is a function of *r* which controls the shape of the path along which $f$ is approaching $(p, q)$. Since $\cos \theta$ is bounded between \[−1, 1\], by the [sandwich theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandwich_theorem), this limit tends to 0.

In contrast, the function

$$f(x, y) = \frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$$

does not have a limit at $(0, 0)$. Taking the path $(x, y) = (t, 0) \to (0, 0)$, we obtain

$$\lim_{t \to 0} f(t, 0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{0}{t^2} = 0,$$

while taking the path $(x, y) = (t, t) \to (0, 0)$, we obtain

$$\lim_{t \to 0} f(t, t) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{t^2}{t^2 + t^2} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

Since the two values do not agree, $f$ does not tend to a single value as $(x, y)$ approaches $(0, 0)$.

### Multiple limits {#Multiple_limits}

Although less commonly used, there is another type of limit for a multivariable function, known as the **multiple limit**. For a two-variable function, this is the **double limit**.[^o41] Let $f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined on $S \times T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2,$ we say **the double limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ and $y$ approaches $q$ is $L$**, written

$$\lim_{ {x \to p} \atop {y \to q} } f(x, y) = L$$

if the following condition holds: For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$ and $y$ in $T$, whenever $0 < \lvert x - p\rvert < \delta$ and $0 < \lvert y - q\rvert < \delta$, we have $\lvert f(x, y) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.[^o41]

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0)\, (\exists \delta > 0)\, (\forall x \in S) \, (\forall y \in T)\, ( (0 < |x-p| < \delta) \land (0 < |y-q| < \delta) \implies |f(x, y) - L| < \varepsilon) .$$

For such a double limit to exist, this definition requires the value of $f$ approaches $L$ along every possible path approaching $(p, q)$, excluding the two lines $x = p$ and $y = q$. As a result, the multiple limit is a weaker notion than the ordinary limit: if the ordinary limit exists and equals $L$, then the multiple limit exists and also equals $L$. The converse is not true: the existence of the multiple limits does not imply the existence of the ordinary limit. Consider the example

$$f(x,y) = \begin{cases} 1 \quad \text{for} \quad xy \ne 0 \\ 0 \quad \text{for} \quad xy = 0 \end{cases}$$

where

$$\lim_{ {x \to 0} \atop {y \to 0} } f(x, y) = 1$$

but

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (0, 0)} f(x, y)$$

does not exist.

If the domain of $f$ is restricted to $(S\setminus\{p\}) \times (T\setminus\{q\}),$ then the two definitions of limits coincide.[^o41]

### Multiple limits at infinity {#Multiple_limits_at_infinity}

The concept of multiple limit can extend to the limit at infinity, in a way similar to that of a single variable function. For $f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R},$ we say **the double limit of $f$ as $x$ and $y$ approaches infinity is $L$**, written

$$\lim_{ {x \to \infty} \atop {y \to \infty} } f(x, y) = L$$

if the following condition holds: For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $c > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$ and $y$ in $T$, whenever $x > c$ and $y > c$, we have $\lvert f(x, y) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0)\, (\exists c> 0)\, (\forall x \in S) \, (\forall y \in T)\, ( (x > c) \land (y > c) \implies |f(x, y) - L| < \varepsilon) .$$

We say **the double limit of $f$ as $x$ and $y$ approaches minus infinity is $L$**, written

$$\lim_{ {x \to -\infty} \atop {y \to -\infty} } f(x, y) = L$$

if the following condition holds: For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $c > 0$ such that $x$ in $S$ and $y$ in $T$, whenever $x < -c$ and $y < -c$, we have $\lvert f(x, y) - L\rvert < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0)\, (\exists c> 0)\, (\forall x \in S) \, (\forall y \in T)\, ( (x < -c) \land (y < -c) \implies |f(x, y) - L| < \varepsilon) .$$

### Pointwise limits and uniform limits {#Pointwise_limits_and_uniform_limits}

Let $f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}.$ Instead of taking limit as $(x, y) \to (p, q)$, we may consider taking the limit of just one variable, say, $x \to p$, to obtain a single-variable function of $y$, namely $g : T \to \mathbb{R}.$ In fact, this limiting process can be done in two distinct ways. The first one is called **pointwise limit**. We say **the pointwise limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $g$**, denoted

$$\lim_{x\to p}f(x, y) = g(y),$$

or

$$\lim_{x \to p}f(x, y) = g(y) \;\; \text{pointwise}.$$

Alternatively, we may say **$f$ tends to $g$ pointwise as $x$ approaches $p$**, denoted

$$f(x, y) \to g(y) \;\; \text{as} \;\; x \to p,$$

or

$$f(x, y) \to g(y) \;\; \text{pointwise} \;\; \text{as} \;\; x \to p.$$

This limit exists if the following holds: For every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every fixed $y$ in $T$, there exists a $\delta(\varepsilon, y) > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$, whenever $0 < \lvert x - p\rvert < \delta$, we have $\lvert f(x, y) - g(y)\rvert < \varepsilon$.[^n3]

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0)\, (\forall y \in T) \, (\exists \delta> 0)\, (\forall x \in S)\, ( 0 < |x-p| < \delta \implies |f(x, y) - g(y)| < \varepsilon) .$$

Here, $\delta = \delta(\varepsilon, y)$ is a function of both $\varepsilon$ and $y$. Each $\delta$ is chosen for a *specific point* of $y$. Hence we say the limit is pointwise in $y$. For example,

$$f(x, y) = \frac{x}{\cos y}$$

has a pointwise limit of constant zero function

$$\lim_{x \to 0}f(x, y) = 0(y) \;\; \text{pointwise}$$

because for every fixed $y$, the limit is clearly 0. This argument fails if $y$ is not fixed: if $y$ is very close to $\pi/2$, the value of the fraction may deviate from 0.

This leads to another definition of limit, namely the **uniform limit**. We say **the uniform limit of $f$ on $T$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $g$**, denoted

$$\underset{ {x \to p} \atop {y \in T} }{\mathrm{unif} \lim \;} f(x, y) = g(y),$$

or

$$\lim_{x \to p}f(x, y) = g(y) \;\; \text{uniformly on} \; T.$$

Alternatively, we may say **$f$ tends to $g$ uniformly on $T$ as $x$ approaches $p$**, denoted

$$f(x, y) \rightrightarrows g(y) \; \text{on} \; T \;\; \text{as} \;\; x \to p,$$

or

$$f(x, y) \to g(y) \;\; \text{uniformly on}\; T \;\; \text{as} \;\; x \to p.$$

This limit exists if the following holds: For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta(\varepsilon) > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$ and $y$ in $T$, whenever $0 < \lvert x - p\rvert < \delta$, we have $\lvert f(x, y) - g(y)\rvert < \varepsilon$.[^n3]

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0) \, (\exists \delta > 0)\, (\forall x \in S)\, (\forall y \in T)\, ( 0 < |x-p| < \delta \implies |f(x, y) - g(y)| < \varepsilon) .$$

Here, $\delta = \delta(\varepsilon)$ is a function of only $\varepsilon$ but not $y$. In other words, *δ* is *uniformly applicable* to all $y$ in $T$. Hence we say the limit is uniform in $y$. For example,

$$f(x, y) = x \cos y$$

has a uniform limit of constant zero function

$$\lim_{x \to 0}f(x, y) = 0(y) \;\; \text{ uniformly on}\; \mathbb{R}$$

because for all real $y$, $\cos y$ is bounded between $[-1, 1]$. Hence no matter how $y$ behaves, we may use the [sandwich theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandwich_theorem) to show that the limit is 0.

### Iterated limits {#Iterated_limits}

Let $f : S \times T \to \mathbb{R}.$ We may consider taking the limit of just one variable, say, $x \to p$, to obtain a single-variable function of $y$, namely $g : T \to \mathbb{R},$ and then take limit in the other variable, namely $y \to q$, to get a number $L$. Symbolically,

$$\lim_{y \to q} \lim_{x \to p} f(x, y) = \lim_{y \to q} g(y) = L.$$

This limit is known as **iterated limit** of the multivariable function.[^n4] The order of taking limits may affect the result, i.e.,

$$\lim_{y \to q} \lim_{x \to p} f(x,y) \ne \lim_{x \to p} \lim_{y \to q} f(x, y)$$

in general.

A sufficient condition of equality is given by the [Moore–Osgood theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore–Osgood_theorem), which requires the limit $\lim_{x \to p}f(x, y) = g(y)$ to be uniform on $T$.[^o48]

## Functions on metric spaces {#Functions_on_metric_spaces}

Suppose $M$ and $N$ are subsets of [metric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_spaces) $A$ and $B$, respectively, and $f : M \to N$ is defined between $M$ and $N$, with $x \in M$, $p$ a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of $M$ and $L \in N$. It is said that **the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $L$** and write

$$\lim_{x \to p}f(x) = L$$

if the following property holds:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all points $x \in M$, $0 < d_{A}(x, p) < \delta$ implies $d_{B}(f(x), L) < \varepsilon$.[^o50]

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists \delta > 0) \,(\forall x \in M) \,(0 < d_A(x, p) < \delta \implies d_B(f(x), L) < \varepsilon).$$

Again, note that $p$ need not be in the domain of $f$, nor does $L$ need to be in the range of $f$, and even if $f(p)$ is defined it need not be equal to $L$.

### Euclidean metric {#Euclidean_metric}

The limit in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is a direct generalization of limits to [vector-valued functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector-valued_functions). For example, we may consider a function $f:S \times T \to \mathbb{R}^3$ such that

$$f(x, y) = (f_1(x, y), f_2(x, y), f_3(x, y) ).$$

Then, under the usual [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric),

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (p, q)} f(x, y) = (L_1, L_2, L_3)$$

if the following holds:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$ and $y$ in $T$, $0 < \sqrt{(x-p)^2 + (y-q)^2} < \delta$ implies $\sqrt{(f_1-L_1)^2 + (f_2-L_2)^2 + (f_3-L_3)^2} < \varepsilon.$[^o52]

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists \delta > 0) \, (\forall x \in S) \, (\forall y \in T)\, \left(0 < \sqrt{(x-p)^2+(y-q)^2} < \delta \implies \sqrt{(f_1-L_1)^2 + (f_2-L_2)^2 + (f_3-L_3)^2} < \varepsilon \right).$$

In this example, the function concerned are finite-[dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) vector-valued function. In this case, the **limit theorem for vector-valued function** states that if the limit of each component exists, then the limit of a vector-valued function equals the vector with each component taken the limit:[^o52]

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (p, q)} \Bigl(f_1(x, y), f_2(x, y), f_3(x, y)\Bigr) = \left(\lim_{(x, y) \to (p, q)}f_1(x, y), \lim_{(x, y) \to (p, q)}f_2(x, y), \lim_{(x, y) \to (p, q)}f_3(x, y)\right).$$

### Manhattan metric {#Manhattan_metric}

One might also want to consider spaces other than Euclidean space. An example would be the Manhattan space. Consider $f:S \to \mathbb{R}^2$ such that

$$f(x) = (f_1(x), f_2(x)).$$

Then, under the [Manhattan metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_metric),

$$\lim_{x \to p} f(x) = (L_1, L_2)$$

if the following holds:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$, $0 < |x - p| < \delta$ implies $|f_{1} - L_{1}| + |f_{2} - L_{2}| < \varepsilon$.

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists \delta > 0) \,(\forall x \in S) \,(0 < |x - p| < \delta \implies |f_1 - L_1| + |f_2 - L_2| < \varepsilon).$$

Since this is also a finite-dimension vector-valued function, the limit theorem stated above also applies.[^n5]

### Uniform metric {#Uniform_metric}

Finally, we will discuss the limit in [function space](wikipedia://en/340630), which has infinite dimensions. Consider a function $f(x, y)$ in the function space $S \times T \to \mathbb{R}.$ We want to find out as $x$ approaches $p$, how $f(x, y)$ will tend to another function $g(y)$, which is in the function space $T \to \mathbb{R}.$ The "closeness" in this function space may be measured under the [uniform metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_metric).[^o56] Then, we will say **the uniform limit of $f$ on $T$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $g$** and write

$$\underset{ {x \to p} \atop {y \in T} }{\mathrm{unif} \lim \;} f(x, y) = g(y),$$

or

$$\lim_{x \to p}f(x, y) = g(y) \;\; \text{uniformly on} \; T,$$

if the following holds:

For every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that for all $x$ in $S$, $0 < |x - p| < \delta$ implies $\sup_{y \in T}|f(x,y) - g(y)| < \varepsilon.$

$$(\forall \varepsilon > 0 )\, (\exists \delta > 0) \,(\forall x \in S) \,(0 < |x-p| < \delta \implies \sup_{y \in T} | f(x, y) - g(y) | < \varepsilon).$$

In fact, one can see that this definition is equivalent to that of the uniform limit of a multivariable function introduced in the previous section.

## Functions on topological spaces {#Functions_on_topological_spaces}

Suppose $X$ and $Y$ are [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) with $Y$ a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637). Let $p$ be a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of $\Omega\subseteq X$, and $L\in Y$. For a function $f: \Omega \to Y$, it is said that the **limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $L$**, written

$$\lim_{x \to p}f(x) = L ,$$

if the following property holds:

for every open [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $V$ of $L$, there exists an open neighborhood $U$ of $p$ such that $f(U\cap \Omega-\{p\})\subseteq V$.

This last part of the definition can also be phrased as "there exists an open [punctured neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485#Deleted_neighbourhood) $U$ of $p$ such that $f(U\cap\Omega)\subseteq V$.

The domain of $f$ does not need to contain $p$. If it does, then the value of $f$ at $p$ is irrelevant to the definition of the limit. In particular, if the domain of $f$ is $X\setminus\{p\}$ (or all of $X$), then the limit of $f$ as $x\to p$ exists and is equal to $L$ if, for all subsets $\Omega$ of $X$ with limit point $p$, the limit of the restriction of $f$ to $\Omega$ exists and is equal to $L$. Sometimes this criterion is used to establish the *non-existence* of the two-sided limit of a function on $\mathbb{R}$ by showing that the [one-sided limits](wikipedia://en/379840) either fail to exist or do not agree. Such a view is fundamental in the field of [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649), where limits and continuity at a point are defined in terms of special families of subsets, called [filters](wikipedia://en/12646549), or generalized sequences known as [nets](wikipedia://en/22170).

Alternatively, the requirement that $Y$ be a Hausdorff space can be relaxed to the assumption that $Y$ be a general topological space, but then the limit of a function may not be unique. In particular, one can no longer talk about *the limit* of a function at a point, but rather *a limit* or *the set of limits* at a point.

A function is continuous at a limit point $p$ of and in its domain [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $f(p)$ is *the* (or, in the general case, *a*) limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ tends to $p$.

There is another type of limit of a function, namely the **sequential limit**. Let $f:X\to Y$ be a mapping from a topological space $X$ into a Hausdorff space $Y$, $p\in X$ a limit point of $X$ and $L \in Y$. The sequential limit of $f$ as $x$ tends to $p$ is $L$ if

For every [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29) $(x_n)$ in $X\setminus\{p\}$ that [converges](wikipedia://en/285773) to $p$, the sequence $f(x_n)$ [converges](wikipedia://en/285773) to $L$.

If $L$ is the limit (in the sense above) of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$, then it is a sequential limit as well; however, the converse need not hold in general. If in addition $X$ is [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable), then $L$ is the sequential limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$ if and only if it is the limit (in the sense above) of $f$ as $x$ approaches $p$.

## Other characterizations {#Other_characterizations}

### In terms of sequences {#In_terms_of_sequences}

For functions on the real line, one way to define the limit of a function is in terms of the limit of sequences. (This definition is usually attributed to [Eduard Heine](wikipedia://en/608289).) In this setting:

$$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$$

if, and only if, for all sequences $x_{n}$ (with, for all $n$, $x_{n}$ not equal to $a$) converging to $a$ the sequence $f(x_{n})$ converges to $L$. It was shown by [Sierpiński](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpiński) in 1916 that proving the equivalence of this definition and the definition above, requires and is equivalent to a weak form of the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840). Note that defining what it means for a sequence $x_{n}$ to converge to $a$ requires the [epsilon, delta method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsilon,_delta_method).

Similarly as it was the case of Weierstrass's definition, a more general Heine definition applies to functions defined on [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of the real line. Let $f$ be a real-valued function with the domain $\operatorname{Dm}(f )$. Let $a$ be the limit of a sequence of elements of $\operatorname{Dm}(f ) \backslash \{a\}.$ Then the limit (in this sense) of $f$ is $L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ if for every sequence $x_{n} \in \operatorname{Dm}(f ) \backslash \{a\}$ (so that for all $n$, $x_{n}$ is not equal to $a$) that converges to $a$, the sequence $f(x_{n})$ converges to $L$. This is the same as the definition of a sequential limit in the preceding section obtained by regarding the subset $\operatorname{Dm}(f )$ of $\mathbb{R}$ as a metric space with the induced metric.

### In non-standard calculus {#In_non-standard_calculus}

In non-standard calculus the limit of a function is defined by:

$$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$$

if and only if for all $x\in \mathbb{R}^*,$ $f^*(x)-L$ is infinitesimal whenever $x - a$ is infinitesimal. Here $\mathbb{R}^*$ are the [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429) and $f*$ is the natural extension of $f$ to the non-standard real numbers. [Keisler](wikipedia://en/10962250) proved that such a hyperreal [definition of limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_calculus#Limit) reduces the quantifier complexity by two quantifiers.[^o61] On the other hand, Hrbacek writes that for the definitions to be valid for all hyperreal numbers they must implicitly be grounded in the ε-δ method, and claims that, from the pedagogical point of view, the hope that non-standard calculus could be done without ε-δ methods cannot be realized in full.[^o62] Bŀaszczyk et al. detail the usefulness of [microcontinuity](wikipedia://en/33952467) in developing a transparent definition of [uniform continuity](wikipedia://en/32337), and characterize Hrbacek's criticism as a "dubious lament".[^o63]

### In terms of nearness {#In_terms_of_nearness}

At the 1908 international congress of mathematics [F. Riesz](wikipedia://en/1073126) introduced an alternate way defining limits and continuity in concept called "nearness".[^o65] A point $x$ is defined to be near a set $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}$ if for every $r > 0$ there is a point $a \in A$ so that $\lvert x - a\rvert < r$. In this setting the

$$\lim_{x\to a} f(x)=L$$

if and only if for all $A\subseteq \mathbb{R},$ $L$ is near $f(A)$ whenever $a$ is near $A$. Here $f(A)$ is the set $\{f(x) \mid x \in A\}.$ This definition can also be extended to metric and topological spaces.

## Relationship to continuity {#Relationship_to_continuity}

The notion of the limit of a function is very closely related to the concept of continuity. A function $f$ is said to be [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) at $c$ if it is both defined at $c$ and its value at $c$ equals the limit of $f$ as $x$ approaches $c$:

$$\lim_{x\to c} f(x) = f(c).$$

We have here assumed that $c$ is a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of the domain of $f$.

## Properties {#Properties}

If a function $f$ is real-valued, then the limit of $f$ at $p$ is $L$ if and only if both the right-handed limit and left-handed limit of $f$ at $p$ exist and are equal to $L$.[^n6]

The function $f$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) at $p$ if and only if the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $p$ exists and is equal to $f(p)$. If $f : M \to N$ is a function between metric spaces $M$ and $N$, then it is equivalent that $f$ transforms every sequence in $M$ which converges towards $p$ into a sequence in $N$ which converges towards $f(p)$.

If $N$ is a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538), then the limit operation is linear in the following sense: if the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $L$ and the limit of $g(x)$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $P$, then the limit of $f(x) + g(x)$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $L + P$. If $a$ is a scalar from the base [field](wikipedia://en/10603), then the limit of $af(x)$ as $x$ approaches $p$ is $aL$.

If $f$ and $g$ are real-valued (or complex-valued) functions, then taking the limit of an operation on $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ (e.g., $f + g$, $f - g$, $f \times g$, $f / g$, $f^{g}$) under certain conditions is compatible with the operation of limits of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$. This fact is often called the **algebraic limit theorem**. The main condition needed to apply the following rules is that the limits on the right-hand sides of the equations exist (in other words, these limits are finite values including 0). Additionally, the identity for division requires that the denominator on the right-hand side is non-zero (division by 0 is not defined), and the identity for exponentiation requires that the base is positive, or zero while the exponent is positive (finite).

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} (f(x) + g(x)) & = & \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} f(x) + \lim_{x \to p} g(x) \\ \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} (f(x) - g(x)) & = & \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} f(x) - \lim_{x \to p} g(x) \\ \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} (f(x)\cdot g(x)) & = & \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} f(x) \cdot \lim_{x \to p} g(x) \\ \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} (f(x)/g(x)) & = & \displaystyle {\lim_{x \to p} f(x) / \lim_{x \to p} g(x)} \\ \displaystyle \lim_{x \to p} f(x)^{g(x)} & = & \displaystyle {\lim_{x \to p} f(x) ^ {\lim_{x \to p} g(x)}} \end{array}$$

These rules are also valid for one-sided limits, including when $p$ is ∞ or −∞. In each rule above, when one of the limits on the right is ∞ or −∞, the limit on the left may sometimes still be determined by the following rules.

$$\begin{array}{rcl} q + \infty & = & \infty \text{ if } q \neq -\infty \\[8pt] q \times \infty & = & \begin{cases} \infty & \text{if } q > 0 \\ -\infty & \text{if } q < 0 \end{cases} \\[6pt] \displaystyle \frac q \infty & = & 0 \text{ if } q \neq \infty \text{ and } q \neq -\infty \\[6pt] \infty^q & = & \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } q < 0 \\ \infty & \text{if } q > 0 \end{cases} \\[4pt] q^\infty & = & \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } 0 < q < 1 \\ \infty & \text{if } q > 1 \end{cases} \\[4pt] q^{-\infty} & = & \begin{cases} \infty & \text{if } 0 < q < 1 \\ 0 & \text{if } q > 1 \end{cases} \end{array}$$

(see also [Extended real number line](wikipedia://en/51698)).

In other cases the limit on the left may still exist, although the right-hand side, called an *[indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723)*, does not allow one to determine the result. This depends on the functions $f$ and $g$. These indeterminate forms are:

$$\begin{array}{cc} \displaystyle \frac{0}{0} & \displaystyle \frac{\pm \infty}{\pm \infty} \\[6pt] 0 \times \pm \infty & \infty + -\infty \\[8pt] \qquad 0^0 \qquad & \qquad \infty^0 \qquad \\[8pt] 1^{\pm \infty} \end{array}$$

See further L'Hôpital's rule below and [Indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723).

### Limits of compositions of functions {#Limits_of_compositions_of_functions}

In general, from knowing that $\lim_{y \to b} f(y) = c$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = b,$ it does *not* follow that $\lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = c.$ However, this "[chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113)" does hold if one of the following *additional* conditions holds:

- $f(b) = c$ (that is, $f$ is continuous at $b$), or
- $g$ does not take the value $b$ near $a$ (that is, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that if $0 < \lvert x - a\rvert < \delta$ then $\lvert g(x) - b\rvert > 0$).

As an example of this phenomenon, consider the following function that violates both additional restrictions:

$$f(x) = g(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x\neq 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } x=0 \end{cases}$$

Since the value at $f(0)$ is a [removable discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removable_discontinuity),

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0$$

for all $a$. Thus, the naïve chain rule would suggest that the limit of $f(f(x))$ is 0. However, it is the case that

$$f(f(x))=\begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x\neq 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 0 \end{cases}$$

and so

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(f(x)) = 1$$

for all $a$.

### Limits of special interest {#Limits_of_special_interest}

#### Rational functions {#Rational_functions}

For $n$ a nonnegative integer and constants $a_1, a_2, a_3,\ldots, a_n$ and $b_1, b_2, b_3,\ldots, b_n,$

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{a_1 x^n + a_2 x^{n-1} + a_3 x^{n-2} + \dots + a_n}{b_1 x^n + b_2 x^{n-1} + b_3 x^{n-2} + \dots + b_n} = \frac{a_1}{b_1}$$

This can be proven by dividing both the numerator and denominator by $x^{n}$. If the numerator is a polynomial of higher degree, the limit does not exist. If the denominator is of higher degree, the limit is 0.

#### Trigonometric functions {#Trigonometric_functions}

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} & = & 1 \\[4pt] \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} & = & 0 \end{array}$$

#### Exponential functions {#Exponential_functions}

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} (1+x)^{\frac{1}{x}} & = & \displaystyle \lim_{r \to \infty} \left(1+\frac{1}{r}\right)^r = e \\[4pt] \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{x}-1}{x} & = & 1 \\[4pt] \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{ax}-1}{bx} & = & \displaystyle \frac{a}{b} \\[4pt] \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{c^{ax}-1}{bx} & = & \displaystyle \frac{a}{b}\ln c \\[4pt] \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^+} x^x & = & 1 \end{array}$$

#### Logarithmic functions {#Logarithmic_functions}

$$\begin{array}{lcl} \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1+x)}{x} & = & 1 \\[4pt] \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln(1+ax)}{bx} & = & \displaystyle \frac{a}{b} \\[4pt] \displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\log_c(1+ax)}{bx} & = & \displaystyle \frac{a}{b\ln c} \end{array}$$

### L'Hôpital's rule {#L'Hôpital's_rule}

This rule uses [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) to find limits of [indeterminate forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_forms) $0/0$ or $\pm\infty/\infty$, and only applies to such cases. Other indeterminate forms may be manipulated into this form. Given two functions $f(x)$ and $g(x)$, defined over an [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) $I$ containing the desired limit point $c$, then if:

1. $\lim_{x \to c}f(x)=\lim_{x \to c}g(x)=0,$ or $\lim_{x \to c}f(x)=\pm\lim_{x \to c}g(x) = \pm\infty,$ and
1. $f$ and $g$ are differentiable over $I \setminus \{c\},$ and
1. $g'(x)\neq 0$ for all $x \in I \setminus \{c\},$ and
1. $\lim_{x\to c}\tfrac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ exists,

then:

$$\lim_{x \to c} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}.$$

Normally, the first condition is the most important one.

For example: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin (2x)}{\sin (3x)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2 \cos (2x)}{3 \cos (3x)} = \frac{2 \cdot 1}{3 \cdot 1} = \frac{2}{3}.$

### Summations and integrals {#Summations_and_integrals}

Specifying an infinite bound on a summation or integral is a common shorthand for specifying a limit.

A short way to write the limit $\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=s}^n f(i)$ is $\sum_{i=s}^\infty f(i).$ An important example of limits of sums such as these are [series](wikipedia://en/15287).

A short way to write the limit $\lim_{x \to \infty} \int_a^x f(t) \; dt$ is $\int_a^\infty f(t) \; dt.$

A short way to write the limit $\lim_{x \to -\infty} \int_x^b f(t) \; dt$ is $\int_{-\infty}^b f(t) \; dt.$
