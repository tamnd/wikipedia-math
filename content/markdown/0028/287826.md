# Upper half-plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Upper half-plane"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **upper half-plane**, $\mathcal H,$ is the set of points $(x,y)$ in the [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane) with $y > 0.$ The **lower half-plane** is the set of points $(x,y)$ with $y < 0$ instead. Arbitrarily oriented half-planes can be obtained via a [planar rotation](wikipedia://en/596267). Half-planes are an example of two-dimensional [half-space](wikipedia://en/647297). A half-plane can be split in two [quadrants](wikipedia://en/18315778).

:::

## Affine geometry {#Affine_geometry}

The [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) of the upper half-plane include

1. shifts $(x,y)\mapsto (x+c,y)$, $c\in\mathbb{R}$, and
1. dilations $(x,y)\mapsto (\lambda x,\lambda y)$, $\lambda > 0.$

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** Let $A$ and $B$ be [semicircles](wikipedia://en/617831) in the upper half-plane with centers on the boundary. Then there is an affine mapping that takes $A$ to $B$.

Proof: First shift the center of $A$ to $(0,0).$ Then take $\lambda=(\text{diameter of}\ B)/(\text{diameter of}\ A)$

and dilate. Then shift $(0,0)$ to the center of $B.$

:::

## Inversive geometry {#Inversive_geometry}

::: {#o5 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition:** $\mathcal{Z} := \left\{\left( \cos^2\theta,\tfrac12 \sin 2\theta \right) \mid 0 < \theta < \pi \right\}$.

$\mathcal Z$ can be recognized as the circle of radius $\tfrac12$ centered at $\bigl(\tfrac12,0\bigr),$ and as the [polar plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_plot) of $\rho(\theta) = \cos \theta.$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** $(0,0),$ $\rho(\theta)$ in $\mathcal{Z},$ and $(1,\tan \theta)$ are [collinear points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear_points).

In fact, $\mathcal{Z}$ is the [inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_circle) of the line $\bigl\{(1, y) \mid y > 0 \bigr\}$ in the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). Indeed, the diagonal from $(0,0)$ to $(1, \tan \theta)$ has squared length $1 + \tan^2 \theta = \sec^2 \theta$, so that $\rho(\theta) = \cos \theta$ is the reciprocal of that length.

:::

## Metric geometry {#Metric_geometry}

The distance between any two points $p$ and $q$ in the upper half-plane can be consistently defined as follows: The [perpendicular bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of the segment from $p$ to $q$ either intersects the boundary or is parallel to it. In the latter case $p$ and $q$ lie on a ray perpendicular to the boundary and [logarithmic measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_measure) can be used to define a distance that is invariant under dilation. In the former case $p$ and $q$ lie on a circle centered at the intersection of their perpendicular bisector and the boundary. By the above proposition this circle can be moved by affine motion to $\mathcal Z.$ Distances on $\mathcal Z$ can be defined using the correspondence with points on $\bigl\{(1, y) \mid y > 0 \bigr\}$ and logarithmic measure on this ray. In consequence, the upper half-plane becomes a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). The generic name of this metric space is the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane). In terms of the models of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), this model is frequently designated the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942).

## Complex plane {#Complex_plane}

Mathematicians sometimes identify the Cartesian plane with the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), and then the upper half-plane corresponds to the set of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) with positive [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part):

$$\mathcal{H} := \{x + iy \mid y > 0;\ x, y \in \mathbb{R} \} .$$

The term arises from a common visualization of the complex number $x+iy$ as the point $(x,y)$ in [the plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) endowed with [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates). When the [$y$ axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-axis) is oriented vertically, the "upper [half-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-plane)" corresponds to the region above the $x$ axis and thus complex numbers for which $y > 0$.

It is the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of many functions of interest in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), especially [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000). The lower half-plane, defined by $y < 0$ is equally good, but less used by convention. The [open unit disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_unit_disk) $\mathcal D$ (the set of all complex numbers of [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) less than one) is equivalent by a [conformal mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_mapping) to $\mathcal H$ (see "[Poincaré metric](wikipedia://en/1487868)"), meaning that it is usually possible to pass between $\mathcal H$ and $\mathcal D.$

It also plays an important role in [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), where the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) provides a way of examining [hyperbolic motions](wikipedia://en/1229416). The Poincaré metric provides a hyperbolic [metric](wikipedia://en/195795) on the space.

The [uniformization theorem](wikipedia://en/288270) for [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) states that the **upper half-plane** is the [universal covering space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_map#Universal_covers) of surfaces with constant negative [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623).

The **closed upper half-plane** is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the upper half-plane and the real axis. It is the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of the upper half-plane.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

One natural generalization in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) is [hyperbolic $n$-space](wikipedia://en/318742) $\mathcal H^n,$ the maximally symmetric, [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected), $n$-dimensional [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) with constant [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) $-1$. In this terminology, the upper half-plane is $\mathcal H^2$ since it has [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_%28numbers%29) [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) $2.$

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the theory of [Hilbert modular forms](wikipedia://en/2333880) is concerned with the study of certain functions on the direct product $\mathcal H^n$ of $n$ copies of the upper half-plane. Yet another space interesting to number theorists is the [Siegel upper half-space](wikipedia://en/1141106) $\mathcal H_n,$ which is the domain of [Siegel modular forms](wikipedia://en/7721927).
