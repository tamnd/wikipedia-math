# Algebraic independence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic independence"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $L$ is **algebraically independent** over a [subfield](wikipedia://en/10603#Subfields_and_prime_fields) $K$ if the elements of $S$ do not satisfy any non-[trivial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_%28mathematics%29) [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) equation with coefficients in $K$.

:::

In particular, a one element set $\{\alpha\}$ is algebraically independent over $K$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\alpha$ is [transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_element) over $K$. In general, any element of an algebraically independent set $S$ over $K$ is by necessity transcendental over $K$, and over all of the [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634) of $K$ generated by the remaining elements of $S$.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\sqrt{\pi}$ and $2\pi+1$ are [transcendental numbers](wikipedia://en/30325): they are not the roots of any nontrivial polynomial whose coefficients are [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). Thus, the sets $\{\sqrt{\pi}\}$ and $\{2\pi+1\}$ are both algebraically independent over the rational numbers.

However, the set $\{ \sqrt{\pi}, 2\pi+1 \}$ is *not* algebraically independent over the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$, because the nontrivial polynomial

$$P(x,y)=2x^2-y+1$$

is zero when $x=\sqrt{\pi}$ and $y=2\pi+1$.

:::

## Algebraic independence of known constants {#Algebraic_independence_of_known_constants}

Although [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) and [$e$](wikipedia://en/9633) are transcendental, it is not known whether $\{ \pi , e \}$ is algebraically independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.[^o5] In fact, it is not even known whether $\pi+e$ is irrational.[^o6] [Nesterenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Valentinovich_Nesterenko) proved in 1996 that:

- the numbers $\pi$, $e^\pi$, and $\Gamma(1/4)$, where $\Gamma$ is the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316), are algebraically independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.[^o7]
- the numbers $\pi, e^{\pi\sqrt{3}}$, and $\Gamma(1/3)$ are algebraically independent over $\mathbb{Q}.$
- for all positive integers *n*, the numbers $\pi$ and $e^{\pi\sqrt{n}}$ are algebraically independent over $\mathbb{Q}.$[^o8]

## Results and open problems {#Results_and_open_problems}

The [Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/348976) can often be used to prove that some sets are algebraically independent over $\mathbb{Q}$. It states that whenever $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$ are [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158) that are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) over $\mathbb{Q}$, then $e^{\alpha_1},\ldots,e^{\alpha_n}$ are also algebraically independent over $\mathbb{Q}$.

The [Schanuel conjecture](wikipedia://en/2031353) would establish the algebraic independence of many numbers, including *$\pi$* and $e$*,* but remains unproven:

Let $\{z_1,...,z_n\}$ be any set of $n$ [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) that are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) over $\mathbb Q$. The [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $\mathbb Q (z_1,...,z_n,e^{z_1},...,e^{z_n})$ has [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) at least $n$ over $\mathbb Q$.

## Algebraic matroids {#Algebraic_matroids}

Given a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $L/K$ that is not algebraic, [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442) can be used to show that there always exists a maximal algebraically independent subset of $L$ over $K$. Further, all the maximal algebraically independent subsets have the same [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174), known as the [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) of the extension.

For every [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) $S$ of elements of $L$, the algebraically independent subsets of $S$ satisfy the axioms that define the independent sets of a [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321). In this matroid, the rank of a set of elements is its transcendence degree, and the flat generated by a set $T$ of elements is the intersection of $L$ with the field $K[T]$. A matroid that can be generated in this way is called an **algebraic matroid**. No good characterization of algebraic matroids is known, but certain matroids are known to be non-algebraic; the smallest is the [Vámos matroid](wikipedia://en/36886801).[^o11]

Many finite matroids may be [represented](wikipedia://en/37165960) by a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) over a field $K$, in which the matroid elements correspond to matrix columns, and a set of elements is independent if the corresponding set of columns is [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863). Every matroid with a linear representation of this type may also be represented as an algebraic matroid, by choosing an [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29) for each row of the matrix, and by using the matrix coefficients within each column to assign each matroid element a linear combination of these transcendentals. The converse is false: not every algebraic matroid has a linear representation.[^o12]
