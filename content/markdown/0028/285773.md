# Limit of a sequence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Limit of a sequence"}

*Image: Archimedes pi.svg.* The sequence given by the perimeters of regular *n*-sided [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) that [circumscribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribe) the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) has a limit equal to the perimeter of the circle, i.e. $2\pi$. The corresponding sequence for inscribed polygons has the same limit.

{| class="wikitable" style="width:100%;" |- !$n$!!$n\times \sin\left(\tfrac1{n}\right)$ |- |1||0.841471 |- |2||0.958851 |- |colspan="2"|... |- |10||0.998334 |- |colspan="2"|... |- |100||0.999983 |}

As the positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$ becomes larger and larger, the value $n\times \sin\left(\tfrac1{n}\right)$ becomes arbitrarily close to $1$. We say that "the limit of the sequence $n \times \sin\left(\tfrac1{n}\right)$ equals $1$."

:::

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **limit of a sequence** is the value that the terms of a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) "tend to", and is often denoted using the $\lim$ symbol (e.g., $\lim_{n \to \infty}a_n$).[^o2] If such a limit exists and is finite, the sequence is called **convergent**.[^o3] A sequence that does not converge is said to be **divergent**.[^o4] The limit of a sequence is said to be the fundamental notion on which the whole of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) ultimately rests.[^o2]

Limits can be defined in any [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) or [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), but are usually first encountered in the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).

## History {#History}

The Greek philosopher [Zeno of Elea](wikipedia://en/143331) is famous for formulating [paradoxes that involve limiting processes](wikipedia://en/34535).

[Leucippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leucippus), [Democritus](wikipedia://en/8211), [Antiphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiphon_%28person%29), [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388), and [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) developed the [method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284), which uses an infinite sequence of approximations to determine an area or a volume. Archimedes succeeded in summing what is now called a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) in his *[Quadrature of the Parabola](wikipedia://en/12745577)*, computing the area enclosed by a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) and a straight line.[^o6]

[Grégoire de Saint-Vincent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grégoire_de_Saint-Vincent) gave the first definition of limit (terminus) of a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) in his work *Opus Geometricum* (1647): "The *terminus* of a progression is the end of the series, which none progression can reach, even not if she is continued in infinity, but which she can approach nearer than a given segment."[^o7]

[Pietro Mengoli](wikipedia://en/11335853) anticipated the modern idea of limit of a sequence with his study of quasi-proportions in *Geometriae speciosae elementa* (1659). He used the term *quasi-infinite* for [unbounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbounded_function) and *quasi-null* for [vanishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_function).

[Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) dealt with series in his works on *Analysis with infinite series* (written in 1669, circulated in manuscript, published in 1711), *Method of fluxions and infinite series* (written in 1671, published in English translation in 1736, Latin original published much later) and *Tractatus de Quadratura Curvarum* (written in 1693, published in 1704 as an Appendix to his *Optiks*). In the latter work, Newton considers the binomial expansion of $(x+o)^n$, which he then linearizes by *taking the limit* as $o$ tends to $0$.

In the 18th century, [mathematicians](wikipedia://en/18902) such as [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) succeeded in summing some *divergent* series by stopping at the right moment; they did not much care whether a limit existed, as long as it could be calculated. At the end of the century, [Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793) in his *Théorie des fonctions analytiques* (1797) opined that the lack of rigour precluded further development in calculus. [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) in his study of [hypergeometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_series) (1813) for the first time rigorously investigated the conditions under which a series converged to a limit.

The modern definition of a limit (for any $\varepsilon$ there exists an index $N$ so that ...) was given by [Bernard Bolzano](wikipedia://en/302185) (*Der binomische Lehrsatz*, Prague 1816, which was little noticed at the time), and by [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459) in the 1870s.

## Real numbers {#Real_numbers}

*Image: Converging Sequence example.svg.* The plot of a convergent sequence {*a<sub>n</sub>*} is shown in blue. Here, one can see that the sequence is converging to the limit 0 as *n* increases.

In the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), a number $L$ is the limit of the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(x_n)$, if the numbers in the sequence become closer and closer to $L$, and not to any other number.

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Examples of limit of a sequence in real numbers are the following:

- If $x_n = c$ for constant $c$, then $x_n \to c$.[^o11][^o12]
- If $x_n = \frac{1}{n}$, then $x_n \to 0$.[^o13][^o12]
- If $x_n = \frac{1}{n}$ when $n$ is even, and $x_n = \frac{1}{n^2}$ when $n$ is odd, then $x_n \to 0$. (The fact that $x_{n+1} > x_n$ whenever $n$ is odd is irrelevant.)
- Given any real number, one may easily construct a sequence that converges to that number by taking decimal approximations. For example, the sequence $0.3, 0.33, 0.333, 0.3333, \dots$ converges to $\frac{1}{3}$. The [decimal representation](wikipedia://en/3293594) $0.3333\dots$ is the *limit* of the previous sequence, defined by $$0.3333... : = \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{3}{10^k}$$
- Finding the limit of a sequence is not always obvious. Two examples are $\lim_{n\to\infty} \left(1 + \tfrac{1}{n}\right)^n$ (the limit of which is the [number *e*](wikipedia://en/9633)) and the [arithmetic–geometric mean](wikipedia://en/3090). The [squeeze theorem](wikipedia://en/330994) is often useful in the establishment of such limits.

:::

::: {#o15 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

We call $x$ the **limit** of the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(x_n)$, which is written

$x_n \to x$, or

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n = x,$$

if the following condition holds:

For each [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $N$ such that, for every natural number $n \geq N$, we have $|x_n - x| < \varepsilon$.[^o16]

In other words, for every measure of closeness $\varepsilon$, the sequence's terms are eventually that close to the limit. The sequence $(x_n)$ is said to **converge to** or **tend to** the limit $x$.

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0 \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n \in \mathbb{N} \left(n \geq N \implies |x_n - x| < \varepsilon \right)\right)\right).$$

If a sequence $(x_n)$ converges to some limit $x$, then it is **convergent** and $x$ is the only limit; otherwise $(x_n)$ is **divergent**. A sequence that has zero as its limit is sometimes called a **null sequence**.

:::

### Properties {#Properties}

Some other important properties of limits of real sequences include the following:

- When it exists, the limit of a sequence is unique.[^o12]
- Limits of sequences behave well with respect to the usual [arithmetic operations](wikipedia://en/3118#Arithmetic_operations). If $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n$ and $\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n$ exists, then

$\lim_{n\to\infty} (a_n \pm b_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty} a_n \pm \lim_{n\to\infty} b_n$[^o12]

$\lim_{n\to\infty} c a_n = c \cdot \lim_{n\to\infty} a_n$[^o12]

$\lim_{n\to\infty} (a_n \cdot b_n) = \left(\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n \right)\cdot \left( \lim_{n\to\infty} b_n \right)$[^o12]

$\lim_{n\to\infty} \left(\frac{a_n}{b_n}\right) = \frac{\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} a_n}{\lim\limits_{n\to\infty} b_n}$ provided $\lim_{n\to\infty} b_n \ne 0$[^o12]

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n^p = \left( \lim_{n\to\infty} a_n \right)^p$$

- For any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) $f$, if $\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n$ exists, then $\lim_{n\to\infty} f \left(x_n \right)$ exists too. In fact, any real-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *$f$* is continuous if and only if it preserves the limits of sequences (though this is not necessarily true when using more general notions of continuity).
- If $a_n \leq b_n$ for all $n$ greater than some $N$, then $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n \leq \lim_{n\to\infty} b_n$.
- ([Squeeze theorem](wikipedia://en/330994)) If $a_n \leq c_n \leq b_n$ for all $n$ greater than some $N$, and $\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} b_n = L$, then $\lim_{n\to\infty} c_n = L$.
- ([Monotone convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/146263)) If $a_n$ is [bounded](wikipedia://en/27838#Bounded) and [monotonic](wikipedia://en/27838#Increasing_and_decreasing) for all $n$ greater than some $N$, then it is convergent.
- A sequence is convergent if and only if every subsequence is convergent.
- If every subsequence of a sequence has its own subsequence which converges to the same point, then the original sequence converges to that point.

These properties are extensively used to prove limits, without the need to directly use the cumbersome formal definition. For example, once it is proven that $1/n \to 0$, it becomes easy to show—using the properties above—that $\frac{a}{b+\frac{c}{n}} \to \frac{a}{b}$ (assuming that $b \ne 0$).

:::

### Infinite limits {#Infinite_limits}

A sequence $(x_n)$ is said to **tend to infinity**, written

$x_n \to \infty$, or

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n = \infty,$$

if the following holds:

For every real number $K$, there is a natural number $N$ such that for every natural number $n \geq N$, we have $x_n > K$; that is, the sequence terms are eventually larger than any fixed $K$.

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall K \in \mathbb{R} \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n \in \mathbb{N} \left(n \geq N \implies x_n > K \right)\right)\right).$$

Similarly, we say a sequence **tends to minus infinity**, written

$x_n \to -\infty$, or

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n = -\infty,$$

if the following holds:

For every real number $K$, there is a natural number $N$ such that for every natural number $n \geq N$, we have $x_n < K$; that is, the sequence terms are eventually smaller than any fixed $K$.

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall K \in \mathbb{R} \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n \in \mathbb{N} \left(n \geq N \implies x_n < K \right)\right)\right).$$

If a sequence tends to infinity or minus infinity, then it is divergent. However, a divergent sequence need not tend to plus or minus infinity, and the sequence $x_n=(-1)^n$ provides one such example.

## Metric spaces {#Metric_spaces}

::: {#o23 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A point $x$ of the [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) $(X, d)$ is the **limit** of the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(x_n)$ if:

For each [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\varepsilon > 0$, there is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $N$ such that, for every natural number $n \geq N$, we have $d(x_n, x) < \varepsilon$.

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0 \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n \in \mathbb{N} \left(n \geq N \implies d(x_n, x) < \varepsilon \right)\right)\right).$$

This coincides with the definition given for real numbers when $X = \mathbb{R}$ and $d(x, y) = |x-y|$.

:::

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

- When it exists, the limit of a sequence is unique, as distinct points are separated by some positive distance, so for $\varepsilon$ less than half this distance, sequence terms cannot be within a distance $\varepsilon$ of both points.
- For any [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) *f*, if $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n$ exists, then $\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = f\left(\lim_{n \to \infty}x_n \right)$. In fact, a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f* is continuous if and only if it preserves the limits of sequences.

:::

### Cauchy sequences {#Cauchy_sequences}

*Image: Cauchy sequence illustration.svg.* The plot of a Cauchy sequence (*x<sub>n</sub>*), shown in blue, as $x_n$ versus *n*. Visually, we see that the sequence appears to be converging to a limit point as the terms in the sequence become closer together as *n* increases. In the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) every Cauchy sequence converges to some limit.

A [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) is a sequence whose terms ultimately become arbitrarily close together, after sufficiently many initial terms have been discarded. The notion of a Cauchy sequence is important in the study of sequences in [metric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_spaces), and, in particular, in [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478). One particularly important result in real analysis is the *Cauchy criterion for convergence of sequences*: a sequence of real numbers is convergent if and only if it is a Cauchy sequence. This remains true in other [complete metric spaces](wikipedia://en/7832).

## Topological spaces {#Topological_spaces}

::: {#o29 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A point $x \in X$ of the topological space $(X, \tau)$ is a **Limit of a sequence in a topological space** or **Limit point of a sequence**[^n1][^n2] of the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $\left(x_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ if:

For every [neighbourhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_neighbourhood) $U$ of $x$, there exists some $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for every $n \geq N$, we have $x_n \in U$.[^o32]

This coincides with the definition given for metric spaces, if $(X, d)$ is a metric space and $\tau$ is the topology generated by $d$.

A limit of a sequence of points $\left(x_n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ in a topological space $T$ is a special case of a [limit of a function](wikipedia://en/285759#Functions_on_topological_spaces): the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is $\mathbb{N}$ in the space $\mathbb{N} \cup \lbrace + \infty \rbrace$, with the [induced topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_topology) of the [affinely extended real number system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affinely_extended_real_number_system), the [range](wikipedia://en/275991) is $T$, and the function argument $n$ tends to $+\infty$, which in this space is a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point_of_a_set) of $\mathbb{N}$.

:::

::: {#o34 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

In a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637), limits of sequences are unique whenever they exist. This need not be the case in non-Hausdorff spaces; in particular, if two points $x$ and $y$ are [topologically indistinguishable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_indistinguishable), then any sequence that converges to $x$ must converge to $y$ and vice versa.

:::

## Hyperreal numbers {#Hyperreal_numbers}

The definition of the limit using the [hyperreal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperreal_numbers) formalizes the intuition that for a "very large" value of the index, the corresponding term is "very close" to the limit. More precisely, a real sequence $(x_n)$ tends to *L* if for every infinite [hypernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypernatural) $H$, the term $x_H$ is infinitely close to $L$ (i.e., the difference $x_H - L$ is [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990)). Equivalently, *L* is the [standard part](wikipedia://en/19145800) of $x_H$:

$$L = {\rm st}(x_H).$$

Thus, the limit can be defined by the formula

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n= {\rm st}(x_H).$$

where the limit exists if and only if the righthand side is independent of the choice of an infinite *$H$*.

## Sequence of more than one index {#Sequence_of_more_than_one_index}

Sometimes one may also consider a sequence with more than one index, for example, a double sequence $(x_{n, m})$. This sequence has a limit $L$ if it becomes closer and closer to $L$ when both *n* and *m* becomes very large.

::: {#o38 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

- If $x_{n, m} = c$ for constant $c$, then $x_{n,m} \to c$.
- If $x_{n, m} = \frac{1}{n + m}$, then $x_{n, m} \to 0$.
- If $x_{n, m} = \frac{n}{n + m}$, then the limit does not exist. Depending on the relative "growing speed" of $n$ and $m$, this sequence can get closer to any value between $0$ and $1$.

:::

::: {#o40 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

We call $x$ the **double limit** of the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(x_{n, m})$, written

$x_{n, m} \to x$, or

$$\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix} n \to \infty \\ m \to \infty \end{smallmatrix}} x_{n, m} = x,$$

if the following condition holds:

For each [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $N$ such that, for every pair of natural numbers $n, m \geq N$, we have $|x_{n, m} - x| < \varepsilon$.[^o41]

In other words, for every measure of closeness $\varepsilon$, the sequence's terms are eventually that close to the limit. The sequence $(x_{n, m})$ is said to **converge to** or **tend to** the limit $x$.

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0 \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n, m \in \mathbb{N} \left(n, m \geq N \implies |x_{n, m} - x| < \varepsilon \right)\right)\right).$$

The double limit is different from taking limit in *n* first, and then in *m*. The latter is known as [iterated limit](wikipedia://en/39305952). Given that both the double limit and the iterated limit exists, they have the same value. However, it is possible that one of them exist but the other does not.

:::

### Infinite limits {#Infinite_limits}

A sequence $(x_{n,m})$ is said to **tend to infinity**, written

$x_{n,m} \to \infty$, or

$$\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix} n \to \infty \\ m \to \infty \end{smallmatrix}}x_{n,m} = \infty,$$

if the following holds:

For every real number $K$, there is a natural number $N$ such that for every pair of natural numbers $n,m \geq N$, we have $x_{n,m} > K$; that is, the sequence terms are eventually larger than any fixed $K$.

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall K \in \mathbb{R} \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n, m \in \mathbb{N} \left(n, m \geq N \implies x_{n, m} > K \right)\right)\right).$$

Similarly, a sequence $(x_{n,m})$ **tends to minus infinity**, written

$x_{n,m} \to -\infty$, or

$$\lim_{\begin{smallmatrix} n \to \infty \\ m \to \infty \end{smallmatrix}}x_{n,m} = -\infty,$$

if the following holds:

For every real number $K$, there is a natural number $N$ such that for every pair of natural numbers $n,m \geq N$, we have $x_{n,m} < K$; that is, the sequence terms are eventually smaller than any fixed $K$.

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall K \in \mathbb{R} \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n, m \in \mathbb{N} \left(n, m \geq N \implies x_{n, m} < K \right)\right)\right).$$

If a sequence tends to infinity or minus infinity, then it is divergent. However, a divergent sequence need not tend to plus or minus infinity, and the sequence $x_{n,m}=(-1)^{n+m}$ provides one such example.

### Pointwise limits and uniform limits {#Pointwise_limits_and_uniform_limits}

For a double sequence $(x_{n,m})$, we may take limit in one of the indices, say, $n \to \infty$, to obtain a single sequence $(y_m)$. In fact, there are two possible meanings when taking this limit. The first one is called **pointwise limit**, denoted

$x_{n, m} \to y_m\quad \text{pointwise}$, or

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} x_{n, m} = y_m\quad \text{pointwise},$$

which means:

For each [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\varepsilon > 0$ and each fixed [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $m$, there exists a natural number $N(\varepsilon, m) > 0$ such that, for every natural number $n \geq N$, we have $|x_{n, m} - y_m| < \varepsilon$.[^o44]

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0 \left( \forall m \in \mathbb{N} \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n \in \mathbb{N} \left(n \geq N \implies |x_{n, m} - y_m| < \varepsilon \right)\right)\right)\right).$$

When such a limit exists, we say the sequence $(x_{n, m})$ [converges pointwise](wikipedia://en/340198) to $(y_m)$.

The second one is called **uniform limit**, denoted

$$x_{n, m} \to y_m \quad \text{uniformly},$$

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} x_{n, m} = y_m \quad \text{uniformly},$$

$x_{n, m} \rightrightarrows y_m$, or

$$\underset{n\to\infty}{\mathrm{unif} \lim} \; x_{n, m} = y_m,$$

which means:

For each [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a natural number $N(\varepsilon) > 0$ such that, for every [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $m$ and for every natural number $n \geq N$, we have $|x_{n, m} - y_m| < \varepsilon$.[^o44]

Symbolically, this is:

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0 \left(\exists N \in \mathbb{N} \left( \forall m \in \mathbb{N} \left(\forall n \in \mathbb{N} \left(n \geq N \implies |x_{n, m} - y_m| < \varepsilon \right)\right)\right)\right).$$

In this definition, the choice of $N$ is independent of $m$. In other words, the choice of $N$ is *uniformly applicable* to all natural numbers $m$. Hence, one can easily see that uniform convergence is a stronger property than pointwise convergence: the existence of uniform limit implies the existence and equality of pointwise limit:

If $x_{n, m} \to y_m$ uniformly, then $x_{n, m} \to y_m$ pointwise.

When such a limit exists, we say the sequence $(x_{n, m})$ [converges uniformly](wikipedia://en/50652) to $(y_m)$.

### Iterated limit {#Iterated_limit}

For a double sequence $(x_{n,m})$, we may take limit in one of the indices, say, $n \to \infty$, to obtain a single sequence $(y_m)$, and then take limit in the other index, namely $m \to \infty$, to get a number $y$. Symbolically,

$$\lim_{m \to \infty} \lim_{n \to \infty} x_{n, m} = \lim_{m \to \infty} y_m = y.$$

This limit is known as **[iterated limit](wikipedia://en/39305952)** of the double sequence. The order of taking limits may affect the result, i.e.,

$\lim_{m \to \infty} \lim_{n \to \infty} x_{n, m} \ne \lim_{n \to \infty} \lim_{m \to \infty} x_{n, m}$ in general.

A sufficient condition of equality is given by the [Moore-Osgood theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore-Osgood_theorem), which requires the limit $\lim_{n \to \infty}x_{n, m} = y_m$ to be uniform in $m$.[^o41]
