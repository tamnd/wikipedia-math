# Global field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Global field"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **global field** is one of two types of [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) (the other one is [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375)) that are characterized using [valuations](wikipedia://en/347049), or [absolute values](wikipedia://en/5042667). There are two kinds of global [fields](wikipedia://en/10603):[^n1]

:::

- [Algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822): A [finite extension](wikipedia://en/4382697) of $\mathbb{Q}$
- **Global function field**: The [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) of an [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_space) [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), equivalently, a finite extension of $\mathbb{F}_q(T)$, the field of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in one variable over the finite field with $q=p^n$ elements.

An axiomatic characterization of these fields via [valuation theory](wikipedia://en/347049) was given by [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) and George Whaples in the 1940s.[^n2][^n3]

## Axiomatic definition {#Axiomatic_definition}

We say that field $K$ is **global field** when there exists a set $\mathfrak{M}$ of places (equivalence classes of absolute values on $K$) such that:

- For every nonzero element $\alpha\in K$, $\vert \alpha \vert_{\mathfrak{p}} = 1$ for all but finitely many $\mathfrak{p} \in \mathfrak{M}$, and $\prod_{\mathfrak{p} \in \mathfrak{M}}\vert \alpha \vert_{\mathfrak{p}}=1$
- At least one of places in $\mathfrak{M}$ is either discrete with finite residue field or archimedean.

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definitions"}

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

Only two kind of fields satisfy the axiomatic definition:

**An algebraic number field**

An algebraic number field *F* is a finite (and hence [algebraic](wikipedia://en/2125)) [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) of the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q**. Thus *F* is a field that contains **Q** and has finite [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_dimension) when considered as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over **Q**.

**The function field of an irreducible algebraic curve over a finite field**

A function field of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) is the set of all rational functions on that variety. On an irreducible algebraic curve (i.e. a one-dimensional variety *V*) over a finite field, we define a rational function on an open affine subset *U* as the ratio of two polynomials in the [affine coordinate ring](wikipedia://en/320468) of *U*, and a rational function on all of *V* consists of such local data that agree on the intersections of open affine subsets. This technically defines the rational functions on *V* to be the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the affine coordinate ring of any open affine subset, since all such subsets are dense.

:::

## Analogies between the two classes of global fields {#Analogies_between_the_two_classes_of_global_fields}

There are a number of formal similarities between the two kinds of global fields. A field of either type has the property that all of its [completions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) are [locally compact fields](wikipedia://en/35937890) (see [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375)). Every field of either type can be realized as the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) in which every non-zero [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) is of finite index. In each case, one has the *product formula* for non-zero elements *x*:

$$\prod_v |x|_v = 1,$$

where *v* varies over all [valuations](wikipedia://en/347049) of the field.

The analogy between the two kinds of fields has been a strong motivating force in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705). The idea of an analogy between number fields and [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181) goes back to [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) and [Heinrich M. Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_M._Weber) in the nineteenth century. The more strict analogy expressed by the 'global field' idea, in which a Riemann surface's aspect as algebraic curve is mapped to curves defined over a finite field, was built up during the 1930s, culminating in the [Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis_for_curves_over_finite_fields) settled by [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) in 1940. The terminology may be due to Weil, who wrote his *Basic Number Theory* (1967) in part to work out the parallelism.

It is usually easier to work in the function field case and then try to develop parallel techniques on the number field side. The development of [Arakelov theory](wikipedia://en/16364229) and its exploitation by [Gerd Faltings](wikipedia://en/862226) in his proof of the [Mordell conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell_conjecture) is a dramatic example. The analogy was also influential in the development of [Iwasawa theory](wikipedia://en/396150) and the [Main Conjecture](wikipedia://en/22091332). The proof of the [fundamental lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_lemma_of_Langlands_and_Shelstad) in the [Langlands program](wikipedia://en/171905) also made use of techniques that reduced the number field case to the function field case.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Hasse–Minkowski theorem"}

### Hasse–Minkowski theorem {#Hasse–Minkowski_theorem}

The [Hasse–Minkowski theorem](wikipedia://en/1109126) is a fundamental result in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) that states that two [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) over a global field are equivalent if and only if they are equivalent *locally at all places*, i.e. equivalent over every [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28ring_theory%29) of the field.

:::

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="Artin reciprocity law"}

### Artin reciprocity law {#Artin_reciprocity_law}

Artin's reciprocity law implies a description of the [abelianization](wikipedia://en/8847) of the absolute [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of a global field *K* that is based on the [Hasse local–global principle](wikipedia://en/489707). It can be described in terms of cohomology as follows:

Let *L*<sub>*v*</sub>/*K*<sub>*v*</sub> be a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) of [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375) with Galois group *G*. The **local reciprocity law** describes a canonical isomorphism

$$\theta_v: K_v^{\times}/N_{L_v/K_v}(L_v^{\times}) \to G^{\text{ab}},$$

called the **local Artin symbol**, the **local reciprocity map** or the **norm residue symbol**.[^n4][^n5]

Let *L*/*K* be a Galois extension of global fields and *C*<sub>*L*</sub> stand for the [idèle class group](wikipedia://en/1168608) of *L*. The maps *θ*<sub>*v*</sub> for different places *v* of *K* can be assembled into a single **global symbol map** by multiplying the local components of an idèle class. One of the statements of the **Artin reciprocity law** is that this results in a canonical isomorphism.[^n6][^n7]
