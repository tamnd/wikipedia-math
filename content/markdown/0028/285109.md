# Predicate (logic)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Predicate"}

In [logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a **predicate** is a [non-logical symbol](wikipedia://en/7791994) that represents a [property](wikipedia://en/508057) or a [relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_%28philosophy%29), though, formally, does not need to represent anything at all. For instance, in the [first-order formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_formula) $P(a)$, the symbol $P$ is a predicate that applies to the [individual constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individual_constant) $a$ which evaluates to either [true or false](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_or_false). Similarly, in the formula $R(a,b)$, the symbol $R$ is a predicate that applies to the individual constants $a$ and $b$. Predicates are considered a [primitive notion](wikipedia://en/23706) of first-order and higher-order logic, and are therefore not defined in terms of other more basic concepts.

:::

The term derives from the [grammatical term "predicate"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28grammar%29), meaning a word or phrase that represents a property or relation.

In the [semantics of logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_of_logic), predicates are interpreted as [relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_%28philosophy%29). For instance, in a standard semantics for first-order logic, the formula $R(a,b)$ would be true on an [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671) if the entities denoted by $a$ and $b$ stand in the relation denoted by $R$. Since predicates are [non-logical symbols](wikipedia://en/7791994), they can denote different relations depending on the interpretation given to them. While [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) only includes predicates that apply to individual objects, other logics may allow predicates that apply to collections of objects defined by other predicates.

Strictly speaking, a predicate does not need to be given any interpretation, so long as its syntactic properties are well-defined. For example, [equality](wikipedia://en/90446) may be understood solely through its reflexive and substitution properties (cf. *Equality (mathematics)*). Other properties can be derived from these, and they are sufficient for proving theorems in mathematics. Similarly, [set membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_membership) can be understood solely through the axioms of [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214).

## Predicates in different systems {#Predicates_in_different_systems}

A predicate is a statement or mathematical assertion that contains variables, sometimes referred to as predicate variables, and may be true or false depending on those variables’ value or values.

- In [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), [atomic formulas](wikipedia://en/4472066) are sometimes regarded as zero-place predicates.[^o3] In a sense, these are nullary (i.e. 0-[arity](wikipedia://en/42301)) predicates.
- In [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), a predicate is a [non-logical relation symbol](wikipedia://en/10983#Non-logical_symbols), which forms an atomic formula when applied to an appropriate number of [terms](wikipedia://en/24885593).
- In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) with the [law of excluded middle](wikipedia://en/17637), predicates are understood to be [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/240790) or set [indicator functions](wikipedia://en/240790) (i.e., [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) from a set element to a [truth value](wikipedia://en/161711)). [Set-builder notation](wikipedia://en/220089) makes use of predicates to define sets.
- In [autoepistemic logic](wikipedia://en/2738697), which rejects the law of excluded middle, predicates may be true, false, or simply *unknown*. In particular, a given collection of facts may be insufficient to determine the truth or falsehood of a predicate.
- In [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180), the strict true/false valuation of the predicate is replaced by a quantity interpreted as the degree of truth.
