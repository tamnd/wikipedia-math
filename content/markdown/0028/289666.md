# Unit (ring theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unit"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), a **unit** or **invertible element** of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is an [invertible element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_element) for the multiplication of the ring. That is, an element $u$ of a ring $R$ is a unit if there exists $v$ in $R$ such that

$$vu = uv = 1,$$

where $1$ is the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity); the element $v$ is unique for this property and is called the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of $u$.[^n1][^n2] The set of units of $R$ forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $R^{\times}$ under multiplication, called the **group of units** or **unit group** of $R$. Other notations for the unit group are $R^{\ast}$, $U(R)$, and $E(R)$ (from the German term Einheit).

:::

Less commonly, the term *unit* is sometimes used to refer to the element $1$ of the ring, in expressions like *ring with a unit* or *unit ring*, and also [unit matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_matrix). Because of this ambiguity, $1$ is more commonly called the "unity" or the "identity" of the ring, and the phrases "ring with unity" or a "ring with identity" may be used to emphasize that one is considering a ring instead of a [rng](wikipedia://en/3405004).

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The multiplicative identity $1$ and its additive inverse $-1$ are always units. More generally, any [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) in a ring $R$ is a unit: if $r^{n} = 1$, then $r^{n-1}$ is a multiplicative inverse of $r$. In a [nonzero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874), the [element 0](wikipedia://en/4178225) is not a unit, so $R^{\times}$ is not closed under addition. A nonzero ring $R$ in which every nonzero element is a unit (that is, $R^{\times} = R \setminus \{0\}$) is called a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) (or a skew-field). A commutative division ring is called a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). For example, the unit group of the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbf{R}$ is $\mathbf{R} \setminus \{0\}$.

### Integer ring {#Integer_ring}

In the ring of [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) $\mathbf{Z}$, the only units are $1$ and $-1$.

In the ring $\mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z}$ of [integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/20087#Integers_modulo_m), the units are the congruence classes $\pmod{n}$ represented by integers [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to $n$. They constitute the [multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/1195234).

### Ring of integers of a number field {#Ring_of_integers_of_a_number_field}

In the ring $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{3}]$ obtained by adjoining the [quadratic integer](wikipedia://en/21834785) $\sqrt{3}$ to $\mathbf{Z}$, one has $(2 + \sqrt{3})(2 - \sqrt{3}) = 1$, so $2 + \sqrt{3}$ is a unit, and so are its powers, so $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{3}]$ has infinitely many units.

More generally, for the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) $R$ in a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) $F$, [Dirichlet's unit theorem](wikipedia://en/460227) states that $R^{\times}$ is isomorphic to the group

$$\mathbf Z^n \times \mu_R$$

where $\mu_R$ is the (finite, cyclic) group of roots of unity in $R$ and $n$, the [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_of_a_module) of the unit group, is

$$n = r_1 + r_2 -1,$$

where $r_1, r_2$ are the number of real embeddings and the number of pairs of complex embeddings of $F$, respectively.

This recovers the $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{3}]$ example: The unit group of (the ring of integers of) a [real quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_quadratic_field) is infinite of rank 1, since $r_1=2, r_2=0$.

### Polynomials and power series {#Polynomials_and_power_series}

For a commutative ring $R$, the units of the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[x]$ are the polynomials

$$p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_n x^n$$

such that $a_{0}$ is a unit in $R$ and the remaining coefficients $a_1, \dots, a_n$ are [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235), i.e., satisfy $a_i^N = 0$ for some $N$.[^n3] In particular, if $R$ is a [domain](wikipedia://en/487627) (or more generally [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749)), then the units of $R[x]$ are the units of $R$. The units of the [power series ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series_ring) $R[[x]]$ are the power series

$$p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^\infty a_i x^i$$

such that $a_{0}$ is a unit in $R$.[^n4]

### Matrix rings {#Matrix_rings}

The unit group of the ring $M_{n}(R)$ of [$n\,\times\,n$ matrices](wikipedia://en/166022) over a ring $R$ is the group $\operatorname{GL}_{n}(R)$ of [invertible matrices](wikipedia://en/217122). For a commutative ring $R$, an element $A$ of $M_{n}(R)$ is invertible if and only if the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of $A$ is invertible in $R$. In that case, $A^{-1}$ can be given explicitly in terms of the [adjugate matrix](wikipedia://en/202840).

### In general {#In_general}

For elements $x$ and $y$ in a ring $R$, if $1 - xy$ is invertible, then $1 - yx$ is invertible with inverse $1 + y(1-xy)^{-1}x$;[^n5] this formula can be guessed, but not proved, by the following calculation in a ring of noncommutative power series:

$$(1-yx)^{-1} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (yx)^n = 1 + y \biggl(\sum_{n \ge 0} (xy)^n \biggr)x = 1 + y(1-xy)^{-1}x.$$

See [Hua's identity](wikipedia://en/38023412) for similar results.

## Group of units {#Group_of_units}

A [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) if $R \setminus R^{\times}$ is a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164).

As it turns out, if $R \setminus R^{\times}$ is an ideal, then it is necessarily a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) and $R$ is [local](wikipedia://en/153391) since a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) is disjoint from $R^{\times}$.

If $R$ is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), then $R^{\times}$ is a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order $\lvert R\rvert - 1$.

Every [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) $f : R \to S$ induces a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $R^{\times} \to S^{\times}$, since $f$ maps units to units. In fact, the formation of the unit group defines a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the [category of rings](wikipedia://en/15300181) to the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092). This functor has a [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) which is the integral [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) construction.[^n6]

The [group scheme](wikipedia://en/510523) $\operatorname{GL}_1$ is isomorphic to the [multiplicative group scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_group_scheme) $\mathbb{G}_m$ over any base, so for any commutative ring $R$, the groups $\operatorname{GL}_1(R)$ and $\mathbb{G}_m(R)$ are canonically isomorphic to $U(R)$. Note that the functor $\mathbb{G}_m$ (that is, $R \mapsto U(R)$) is [representable](wikipedia://en/844461) in the sense: $\mathbb{G}_m(R) \simeq \operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}[t, t^{-1}], R)$ for commutative rings $R$ (this for instance follows from the aforementioned adjoint relation with the group ring construction). Explicitly this means that there is a natural bijection between the set of the ring homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}[t, t^{-1}] \to R$ and the set of unit elements of $R$ (in contrast, $\mathbb{Z}[t]$ represents the additive group $\mathbb{G}_a$, the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) from the category of commutative rings to the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748)).

## Associatedness {#Associatedness}

Suppose that $R$ is commutative. Elements $r$ and $s$ of $R$ are called *associate* if there exists a unit $u$ in $R$ such that $r = us$; then write $r \sim s$. In any ring, pairs of [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) elements $x$ and $-x$ are [associate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_element), since any ring includes the unit $-1$. For example, 6 and −6 are associate in $\mathbf{Z}$. In general, $\sim$ is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on $R$.

Associatedness can also be described in terms of the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of $R^{\times}$ on $R$ via multiplication: Two elements of $R$ are associate if they are in the same $R^{\times}$-[orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29).

In an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), the set of associates of a given nonzero element has the same [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) as $R^{\times}$.

The equivalence relation $\sim$ can be viewed as any one of [Green's semigroup relations](wikipedia://en/1192012) specialized to the multiplicative [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) of a commutative ring $R$.
