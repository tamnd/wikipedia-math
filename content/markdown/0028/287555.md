# Pseudo-Riemannian manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudo-Riemannian manifold"}

In [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), a **pseudo-Riemannian manifold**,[^n1][^n2] also called a **semi-Riemannian manifold**, is a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) with a [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) that is everywhere [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate_bilinear_form). This is a generalization of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) in which the requirement of [positive-definiteness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_bilinear_form) is relaxed.

:::

Every [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) of a pseudo-Riemannian manifold is a [pseudo-Euclidean vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Euclidean_vector_space).

A special case used in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) is a four-dimensional **Lorentzian manifold** for modeling [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), where tangent vectors can be classified as [timelike, null, and spacelike](wikipedia://en/14246049).

## Introduction {#Introduction}

### Manifolds {#Manifolds}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) is a space that is locally similar to a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). In an *n*-dimensional Euclidean space any point can be specified by *n* real numbers. These are called the [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) of the point.

An *n*-dimensional differentiable manifold is a generalisation of *n*-dimensional Euclidean space. In a manifold it may only be possible to define coordinates *locally*. This is achieved by defining [coordinate patches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_patch): subsets of the manifold that can be mapped into *n*-dimensional Euclidean space.

See *[Manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470)*, *[Differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219)*, *[Coordinate patch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_patch)* for more details.

### Tangent spaces and metric tensors {#Tangent_spaces_and_metric_tensors}

Associated with each point $p$ in an $n$-dimensional differentiable manifold $M$ is a [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) (denoted $T_pM$). This is an $n$-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) whose elements can be thought of as [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of curves passing through the point $p$.

A [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) is a [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate), smooth, symmetric, [bilinear map](wikipedia://en/4365) that assigns a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) to pairs of tangent vectors at each tangent space of the manifold. Denoting the metric tensor by $g$ we can express this as

$$g : T_pM \times T_pM \to \mathbb{R}.$$

The map is symmetric and bilinear so if $X,Y,Z \in T_pM$ are tangent vectors at a point $p$ to the manifold $M$ then we have

- $$\,g(X,Y) = g(Y,X)$$
- $$\,g(aX + Y, Z) = a g(X,Z) + g(Y,Z)$$

for any real number $a\in\mathbb{R}$.

That $g$ is [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate) means there is no non-zero $X \in T_pM$ such that $g(X,Y) = 0$ for all $Y \in T_pM$.

### Metric signatures {#Metric_signatures}

Given a metric tensor *g* on an *n*-dimensional real manifold, the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) *q*(*x*) = *g*(*x*, *x*) associated with the metric tensor applied to each vector of any [orthogonal basis](wikipedia://en/755400) produces *n* real values. By [Sylvester's law of inertia](wikipedia://en/1127460#Law_of_inertia_for_quadratic_forms), the number of each positive, negative and zero values produced in this manner are invariants of the metric tensor, independent of the choice of orthogonal basis. The **[signature](wikipedia://en/463225)** (*p*, *q*, *r*) of the metric tensor gives these numbers, shown in the same order. A non-degenerate metric tensor has *r* = 0 and the signature may be denoted (*p*, *q*), where *p* + *q* = *n*.

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **pseudo-Riemannian manifold** (*M*, *g*) is a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) *M* that is equipped with an everywhere non-degenerate, smooth, symmetric [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) *g*.

Such a metric is called a **pseudo-Riemannian metric**. Applied to a vector field, the resulting scalar field value at any point of the manifold can be positive, negative or zero.

The signature of a pseudo-Riemannian metric is (*p*, *q*), where both *p* and *q* are non-negative. The non-degeneracy condition together with continuity implies that *p* and *q* remain unchanged throughout the manifold (assuming it is connected).

:::

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Lorentz manifold"}

## Lorentzian manifold {#Lorentzian_manifold}

A **Lorentzian manifold** is an important special case of a pseudo-Riemannian manifold in which the [signature of the metric](wikipedia://en/463225) is (1, *n*−1) (equivalently, (*n*−1, 1); see *[Sign convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_convention)*). Such metrics are called **Lorentzian metrics**. They are named after the Dutch physicist [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz).

### Applications in physics {#Applications_in_physics}

After Riemannian manifolds, Lorentzian manifolds form the most important subclass of pseudo-Riemannian manifolds. They are important in applications of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).

A principal premise of general relativity is that [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) can be modeled as a 4-dimensional Lorentzian manifold of signature (3, 1) or, equivalently, (1, 3). Unlike Riemannian manifolds with positive-definite metrics, an indefinite signature allows tangent vectors to be classified into *timelike*, *null* or *spacelike*. With a signature of (*p*, 1) or (1, *q*), the manifold is also locally (and possibly globally) time-orientable (see *[Causal structure](wikipedia://en/14246049)*).

:::

## Properties of pseudo-Riemannian manifolds {#Properties_of_pseudo-Riemannian_manifolds}

Just as [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be thought of as the local model of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) $\mathbb{R}^{n-1,1}$ with the flat [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric) is the local model of a Lorentzian manifold. Likewise, the model space for a pseudo-Riemannian manifold of signature (*p*, *q*) is [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546) $\mathbb{R}^{p,q}$, for which there exist coordinates *x*<sub>*i*</sub> such that

$$g = dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_p^2 - dx_{p+1}^2 - \cdots - dx_{p+q}^2$$

Some theorems of Riemannian geometry can be generalized to the pseudo-Riemannian case. In particular, the [fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/845060) is true of all pseudo-Riemannian manifolds. This allows one to speak of the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) on a pseudo-Riemannian manifold along with the associated [curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526). On the other hand, there are many theorems in Riemannian geometry that do not hold in the generalized case. For example, it is *not* true that every smooth manifold admits a pseudo-Riemannian metric of a given signature; there are certain [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) obstructions. Furthermore, a [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095) does not always inherit the structure of a pseudo-Riemannian manifold; for example, the metric tensor becomes zero on any [light-like](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space#Causal_structure) [curve](wikipedia://en/89246). The [Clifton–Pohl torus](wikipedia://en/34242725) provides an example of a pseudo-Riemannian manifold that is compact but not complete, a combination of properties that the [Hopf–Rinow theorem](wikipedia://en/681265) disallows for Riemannian manifolds.[^n3]
