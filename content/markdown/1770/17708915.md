# Zonal spherical function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zonal spherical function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **zonal spherical function** or often just **spherical function** is a function on a [locally compact group](wikipedia://en/1651204) *G* with compact subgroup *K* (often a [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726)) that arises as the [matrix coefficient](wikipedia://en/11017946) of a *K*-invariant vector in an [irreducible representation](wikipedia://en/385982) of *G*. The key examples are the matrix coefficients of the *[spherical principal series](wikipedia://en/10620358)*, the irreducible representations appearing in the decomposition of the [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788) of *G* on *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*). In this case the [commutant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutant) of *G* is generated by the algebra of biinvariant functions on *G* with respect to *K* acting by right [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519). It is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) if in addition *G*/*K* is a [symmetric space](wikipedia://en/1642877), for example when *G* is a connected semisimple Lie group with finite centre and *K* is a maximal compact subgroup. The matrix coefficients of the spherical principal series describe precisely the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum) of the corresponding [C\* algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C*_algebra) generated by the biinvariant functions of [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support), often called a [Hecke algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hecke_algebra_of_a_locally_compact_group). The spectrum of the commutative Banach \*-algebra of biinvariant *L*<sup>1</sup> functions is larger; when *G* is a semisimple Lie group with maximal compact subgroup *K*, additional characters come from matrix coefficients of the [complementary series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_series), obtained by analytic continuation of the spherical principal series.

:::

Zonal spherical functions have been explicitly determined for real semisimple groups by [Harish-Chandra](wikipedia://en/373190). For [special linear groups](wikipedia://en/144823), they were independently discovered by [Israel Gelfand](wikipedia://en/364643) and [Mark Naimark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Naimark). For complex groups, the theory simplifies significantly, because *G* is the [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) of *K*, and the formulas are related to analytic continuations of the [Weyl character formula](wikipedia://en/5011916) on *K*. The abstract [functional analytic](wikipedia://en/11180) theory of zonal spherical functions was first developed by [Roger Godement](wikipedia://en/971810). Apart from their group theoretic interpretation, the zonal spherical functions for a semisimple Lie group *G* also provide a set of simultaneous [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) for the natural action of the centre of the [universal enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551) of *G* on *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*), as [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) on the symmetric space *G*/*K*. For semisimple [p-adic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic) Lie groups, the theory of zonal spherical functions and Hecke algebras was first developed by Satake and [Ian G. Macdonald](wikipedia://en/3100378). The analogues of the [Plancherel theorem](wikipedia://en/454315) and [Fourier inversion formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_inversion_formula) in this setting generalise the eigenfunction expansions of Mehler, Weyl and Fock for [singular ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/16990545): they were obtained in full generality in the 1960s in terms of [Harish-Chandra's c-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harish-Chandra's_c-function).

The name "zonal spherical function" comes from the case when *G* is SO(3,**R**) acting on a 2-sphere and *K* is the subgroup fixing a point: in this case the zonal spherical functions can be regarded as certain functions on the sphere invariant under rotation about a fixed axis.

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let *G* be a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [unimodular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodular_group) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and *K* a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) and let *H*<sub>1</sub> = *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*). Thus, *H*<sub>1</sub> admits a [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788) π of *G* by left translation. This is a subrepresentation of the regular representation, since if *H*= *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*) with left and right [regular representations](wikipedia://en/360011) λ and ρ of *G* and *P* is the [orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection)

$$P =\int_K \rho(k) \, dk$$

from *H* to *H*<sub>1</sub> then *H*<sub>1</sub> can naturally be identified with *PH* with the action of *G* given by the restriction of λ.

On the other hand, by [von Neumann's commutation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutation_theorem)[^o4]

$$\lambda(G)^\prime= \rho(G)^{\prime\prime},$$

where *S**denotes the [commutant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutant) of a set of operators*****S*, so that***

$$\pi(G)^\prime = P \rho(G)^{\prime\prime}P.$$

Thus the commutant of π is generated as a [von Neumann algebra](wikipedia://en/294185) by operators

$$P\rho(f)P = \int_G f(g) (P \rho(g)P) \, dg$$

where *f* is a continuous function of compact support on *G*.

However *P*ρ(*f*) *P* is just the restriction of ρ(*F*) to *H*<sub>1</sub>, where

$$F(g) =\int_K \int_K f(kgk^\prime) \, dk\, dk^\prime$$

is the *K*-biinvariant continuous function of compact support obtained by averaging *f* by *K* on both sides.

Thus the commutant of π is generated by the restriction of the operators ρ(*F*) with *F* in *C*<sub>c</sub>(*K*\\*G*/*K*), the *K*-biinvariant continuous functions of compact support on *G*.

These functions form a [\* algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/*_algebra) under [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) with involution

$$F^*(g) =\overline{F(g^{-1})},$$

often called the [Hecke algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hecke_algebra_of_a_locally_compact_group) for the pair (*G*, *K*).

Let *A*(*K*\\*G*/*K*) denote the [C\* algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C*_algebra) generated by the operators ρ(*F*) on *H*<sub>1</sub>.

The pair (*G*, *K*) is said to be a [Gelfand pair](wikipedia://en/3018619)[^n1] if one, and hence all, of the following algebras are [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative):

- $$\pi(G)^\prime$$
- $$C_c(K\backslash G /K)$$
- $$A(K\backslash G /K).$$

Since *A*(*K*\\*G*/*K*) is a commutative [C\* algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C*_algebra), by the [Gelfand–Naimark theorem](wikipedia://en/315414) it has the form *C*<sub>0</sub>(*X*), where *X* is the locally compact space of norm continuous \* [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) of *A*(*K*\\*G*/*K*) into **C**.

A concrete realization of the \* homomorphisms in *X* as *K*-biinvariant [uniformly bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_bounded) functions on *G* is obtained as follows.[^n1][^n2][^n3][^n4][^n5]

Because of the estimate

$$\|\pi(F)\|\le \int_G |F(g)| \, dg \equiv \|F\|_1,$$

the representation π of *C*<sub>c</sub>(*K*\\*G*/*K*) in *A*(*K*\\*G*/*K*) extends by continuity to L<sup>1</sup>(*K*\\*G*/*K*), the [\* algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/*_algebra) of *K*-biinvariant integrable functions. The image forms a dense \* subalgebra of *A*(*K*\\*G*/*K*). The restriction of a \* homomorphism χ continuous for the operator norm is also continuous for the norm ||·||<sub>1</sub>. Since the [Banach space dual](wikipedia://en/3989) of L<sup>1</sup> is L<sup>∞</sup>, it follows that

$$\chi(\pi(F)) =\int_G F(g) h(g) \, dg,$$

for some unique uniformly bounded *K*-biinvariant function *h* on *G*. These functions *h* are exactly the **zonal spherical functions** for the pair (*G*, *K*).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

A zonal spherical function *h* has the following properties:[^n1]

1. *h* is uniformly continuous on *G*
1. $$h(x) h(y) = \int_K h(xky) \,dk \,\,(x,y\in G).$$
1. *h*(1) =1 (normalisation)
1. *h* is a [positive definite function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_function_on_a_group) on *G*
1. *f* \* *h* is proportional to *h* for all *f* in *C*<sub>c</sub>(*K*\\*G*/*K*).

These are easy consequences of the fact that the bounded linear functional χ defined by *h* is a homomorphism. Properties 2, 3 and 4 or properties 3, 4 and 5 characterize zonal spherical functions. A more general class of zonal spherical functions can be obtained by dropping positive definiteness from the conditions, but for these functions there is no longer any connection with [unitary representations](wikipedia://en/292788). For semisimple Lie groups, there is a further characterization as eigenfunctions of [invariant differential operators](wikipedia://en/6935363) on *G*/*K* (see below).

In fact, as a special case of the [Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction](wikipedia://en/297446), there is one-one correspondence between irreducible representations σ of *G* having a unit vector *v* fixed by *K* and zonal spherical functions *h* given by

$$h(g) = (\sigma(g) v,v).$$

Such irreducible representations are often described as having **class one**. They are precisely the irreducible representations required to decompose the [induced representation](wikipedia://en/357452) π on *H*<sub>1</sub>. Each representation σ extends uniquely by continuity to *A*(*K*\\*G*/*K*), so that each zonal spherical function satisfies

$$\left|\int_G f(g) h(g)\, dg\right| \le \|\pi(f)\|$$

for *f* in *A*(*K*\\*G*/*K*). Moreover, since the commutant π(*G*)' is commutative, there is a unique probability measure μ on the space of \* homomorphisms *X* such that

$$\int_G |f(g)|^2 \, dg = \int_X |\chi(\pi(f))|^2 \, d\mu(\chi).$$

μ is called the **[Plancherel measure](wikipedia://en/31244986)**. Since π(*G*)' is the [centre](wikipedia://en/294185) of the von Neumann algebra generated by *G*, it also gives the measure associated with the [direct integral](wikipedia://en/1256105) decomposition of *H*<sub>1</sub> in terms of the irreducible representations σ<sub>χ</sub>.

## Gelfand pairs {#Gelfand_pairs}

If *G* is a [connected](wikipedia://en/87141) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), then, thanks to the work of [Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274), [Malcev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Maltsev), [Iwasawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenkichi_Iwasawa) and [Chevalley](wikipedia://en/366040), *G* has a [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726), unique up to conjugation.[^n6][^n7] In this case *K* is connected and the quotient *G*/*K* is diffeomorphic to a Euclidean space. When *G* is in addition [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group), this can be seen directly using the [Cartan decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139145) associated to the [symmetric space](wikipedia://en/1642877) *G*/*K*, a generalisation of the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) of invertible matrices. Indeed, if τ is the associated period two automorphism of *G* with fixed point subgroup *K*, then

$$G=P\cdot K,$$

where

$$P= \{g\in G| \tau(g)=g^{-1}\}.$$

Under the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290), *P* is diffeomorphic to the -1 eigenspace of τ in the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of *G*. Since τ preserves *K*, it induces an automorphism of the Hecke algebra *C*<sub>c</sub>(*K*\\*G*/*K*). On the other hand, if *F* lies in *C*<sub>c</sub>(*K*\\*G*/*K*), then

*F*(τ*g*) = *F*(*g*<sup>−1</sup>),

so that τ induces an anti-automorphism, because inversion does. Hence, when *G* is semisimple,

- the Hecke algebra is commutative
- (*G*,*K*) is a Gelfand pair.

More generally the same argument gives the following criterion of Gelfand for (*G*,*K*) to be a Gelfand pair:[^n8]

- *G* is a unimodular locally compact group;
- *K* is a compact subgroup arising as the fixed points of a period two automorphism τ of *G*;
- *G* =*K*·*P* (not necessarily a direct product), where *P* is defined as above.

The two most important examples covered by this are when:

- *G* is a compact connected semisimple Lie group with τ a period two automorphism;[^n9][^n10]
- *G* is a semidirect product $A\rtimes K$, with *A* a locally compact Abelian group without 2-torsion and τ(*a*· *k*)= *k*·*a*<sup>−1</sup> for *a* in *A* and *k* in *K*.

The three cases cover the three types of [symmetric spaces](wikipedia://en/1642877) *G*/*K*:[^n4]

1. *Non-compact type*, when *K* is a maximal compact subgroup of a non-compact real semisimple Lie group *G*;
1. *Compact type*, when *K* is the fixed point subgroup of a period two automorphism of a compact semisimple Lie group *G*;
1. *Euclidean type*, when *A* is a finite-dimensional Euclidean space with an orthogonal action of *K*.

## Cartan–Helgason theorem {#Cartan–Helgason_theorem}

Let *G* be a compact semisimple connected and simply connected Lie group and τ a period two automorphism of a *G* with fixed point subgroup *K* = *G*<sup>τ</sup>. In this case *K* is a connected compact Lie group.[^n4] In addition let *T* be a [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136) of *G* invariant under τ, such that *T* $\cap$ *P* is a maximal torus in *P*, and set[^n11]

$$S= K\cap T = T^\tau.$$

*S* is the direct product of a torus and an [elementary abelian 2-group](wikipedia://en/4843202).

In 1929 [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) found a rule to determine the decomposition of L<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*) into the direct sum of finite-dimensional [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of *G*, which was proved rigorously only in 1970 by [Sigurdur Helgason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigurdur_Helgason_%28mathematician%29). Because the commutant of *G* on L<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*) is commutative, each irreducible representation appears with multiplicity one. By [Frobenius reciprocity](wikipedia://en/55690071) for compact groups, the irreducible representations *V* that occur are precisely those admitting a non-zero vector fixed by *K*.

From the [representation theory of compact semisimple groups](wikipedia://en/5011916), irreducible representations of *G* are classified by their [highest weight](wikipedia://en/277087). This is specified by a homomorphism of the maximal torus *T* into **T**.

The **Cartan–Helgason theorem**[^n12][^n13] states that

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5" |

\|the irreducible representations of *G* admitting a non-zero vector fixed by *K* are precisely those with highest weights corresponding to homomorphisms trivial on *S*. |}

The corresponding irreducible representations are called *spherical representations*.

The theorem can be proved[^n4] using the [Iwasawa decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139148):

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{k} \oplus \mathfrak{a} \oplus \mathfrak{n},$$

where $\mathfrak{g}$, $\mathfrak{k}$, $\mathfrak{a}$ are the complexifications of the [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) of *G*, *K*, *A* = *T* $\cap$ *P* and

$$\mathfrak{n}=\bigoplus \mathfrak{g}_\alpha,$$

summed over all eigenspaces for *T* in $\mathfrak{g}$ corresponding to [positive roots](wikipedia://en/277087) α not fixed by τ.

Let *V* be a spherical representation with highest weight vector *v*<sub>0</sub> and *K*-fixed vector *v*<sub>*K*</sub>. Since *v*<sub>0</sub> is an eigenvector of the solvable Lie algebra $\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}$, the [Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem](wikipedia://en/762696) implies that the *K*-module generated by *v*<sub>0</sub> is the whole of *V*. If *Q* is the orthogonal projection onto the fixed points of *K* in *V* obtained by averaging over *G* with respect to [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645), it follows that

$$\displaystyle{ v_K = c Qv_0}$$

for some non-zero constant *c*. Because *v*<sub>*K*</sub> is fixed by *S* and *v*<sub>0</sub> is an eigenvector for *S*, the subgroup *S* must actually fix *v*<sub>0</sub>, an equivalent form of the triviality condition on *S*.

Conversely if *v*<sub>0</sub> is fixed by *S*, then it can be shown[^n14] that the matrix coefficient

$$\displaystyle{f(g) =(gv_0,v_0)}$$

is non-negative on *K*. Since *f*(1) > 0, it follows that (*Qv*<sub>0</sub>, *v*<sub>0</sub>) > 0 and hence that *Qv*<sub>0</sub> is a non-zero vector fixed by *K*.

## Harish-Chandra's formula {#Harish-Chandra's_formula}

If *G* is a non-compact semisimple Lie group, its maximal compact subgroup *K* acts by conjugation on the component *P* in the [Cartan decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139145). If *A* is a maximal Abelian subgroup of *G* contained in *P*, then *A* is diffeomorphic to its Lie algebra under the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) and, as a [further generalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group_decompositions) of the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) of matrices, every element of *P* is conjugate under *K* to an element of *A*, so that[^o31]

*G* =*KAK*.

There is also an associated [Iwasawa decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139148)

*G* =*KAN*,

where *N* is a closed nilpotent subgroup, diffeomorphic to its Lie algebra under the exponential map and normalised by *A*. Thus *S*=*AN* is a closed [solvable subgroup](wikipedia://en/105012) of *G*, the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) of *N* by *A*, and *G* = *KS*.

If α in Hom(*A*,**T**) is a [character](wikipedia://en/293418) of *A*, then α extends to a character of *S*, by defining it to be trivial on *N*. There is a corresponding [unitary](wikipedia://en/292788) [induced representation](wikipedia://en/357452) σ of *G* on L<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*S*) = L<sup>2</sup>(*K*),[^n15] a so-called [(spherical) principal series representation](wikipedia://en/10620358).

This representation can be described explicitly as follows. Unlike *G* and *K*, the solvable Lie group *S* is not unimodular. Let *dx* denote left invariant Haar measure on *S* and Δ<sub>*S*</sub> the [modular function](wikipedia://en/40645#The_modular_function) of *S*. Then[^n4]

$$\int_G f(g) \,dg = \int_S\int_K f(x\cdot k) \, dx\, dk = \int_S\int_K f(k\cdot x) \Delta_S(x)\,dx\, dk.$$

The principal series representation σ is realised on L<sup>2</sup>(*K*) as[^n16]

$$(\sigma(g) \xi)(k) = \alpha^\prime(g^{-1}k)^{-1} \, \xi(U(g^{-1}k)),$$

where

$$g = U(g)\cdot X(g)$$

is the Iwasawa decomposition of *g* with *U*(*g*) in *K* and *X*(*g*) in *S* and

$$\alpha^\prime(kx) = \Delta_S(x)^{1/2} \alpha(x)$$

for *k* in *K* and *x* in *S*.

The representation σ is irreducible, so that if *v* denotes the constant function 1 on *K*, fixed by *K*,

$$\varphi_\alpha(g)=(\sigma(g)v,v)$$

defines a zonal spherical function of *G*.

Computing the inner product above leads to **Harish-Chandra's formula** for the zonal spherical function

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\varphi_\alpha(g) = \int_K \alpha^\prime(gk)^{-1}\, dk$ |}

as an integral over *K*.

Harish-Chandra proved that these zonal spherical functions exhaust the characters of the [C\* algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C*_algebra) generated by the *C*<sub>*c*</sub>(*K* \\ *G* / *K*) acting by right convolution on *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G* / *K*). He also showed that two different characters α and β give the same zonal spherical function if and only if α = β·*s*, where *s* is in the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) of *A*

$$W(A)=N_K(A)/C_K(A),$$

the quotient of the [normaliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normaliser) of *A* in *K* by its [centraliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centraliser), a [finite reflection group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_reflection_group).

It can also be verified directly[^n1] that this formula defines a zonal spherical function, without using representation theory. The proof for general semisimple Lie groups that every zonal spherical formula arises in this way requires the detailed study of *G*-[invariant differential operators](wikipedia://en/6935363) on *G*/*K* and their simultaneous [eigenfunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenfunctions) (see below).[^n3][^n4] In the case of complex semisimple groups, Harish-Chandra and [Felix Berezin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Berezin) realised independently that the formula simplified considerably and could be proved more directly.[^n4][^n17][^n18][^n19][^n20]

The remaining positive-definite zonal spherical functions are given by Harish-Chandra's formula with α in Hom(*A*,**C**\*) instead of Hom(*A*,**T**). Only certain α are permitted and the corresponding irreducible representations arise as analytic continuations of the spherical principal series. This so-called "[complementary series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_series)" was first studied by (Bargmann 1947) for *G* = SL(2,**R**) and by (Harish-Chandra 1947) and (Gelfand Naimark 1947) for *G* = SL(2,**C**). Subsequently in the 1960s, the construction of a [complementary series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_series) by analytic continuation of the spherical principal series was systematically developed for general semisimple Lie groups by Ray Kunze, [Elias Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Stein) and [Bertram Kostant](wikipedia://en/3520590).[^n21][^n22][^n23] Since these irreducible representations are not [tempered](wikipedia://en/10995628), they are not usually required for harmonic analysis on *G* (or *G* / *K*).

:::

## Eigenfunctions {#Eigenfunctions}

Harish-Chandra proved[^n3][^n4] that zonal spherical functions can be characterised as those normalised positive definite *K*-invariant functions on *G*/*K* that are eigenfunctions of *D*(*G*/*K*), the algebra of invariant differential operators on *G*. This algebra acts on *G*/*K* and commutes with the natural action of *G* by left translation. It can be identified with the subalgebra of the [universal enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551) of *G* fixed under the [adjoint action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_group) of *K*. As for the commutant of *G* on L<sup>2</sup>(*G*/*K*) and the corresponding Hecke algebra, this algebra of operators is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative); indeed it is a subalgebra of the [algebra of measurable operators](wikipedia://en/5123403) affiliated with the commutant π(*G*)', an Abelian von Neumann algebra. As Harish-Chandra proved, it is isomorphic to the algebra of *W*(*A*)-invariant polynomials on the Lie algebra of *A*, which itself is a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) by the [Chevalley–Shephard–Todd theorem](wikipedia://en/16426096) on polynomial invariants of [finite reflection groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_reflection_group). The simplest invariant differential operator on *G*/*K* is the [Laplacian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian_operator); up to a sign this operator is just the image under π of the [Casimir operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimir_operator) in the centre of the universal enveloping algebra of *G*.

Thus a normalised positive definite *K*-biinvariant function *f* on *G* is a zonal spherical function if and only if for each *D* in *D*(*G*/*K*) there is a constant λ<sub>*D*</sub> such that

$$\displaystyle\pi(D)f =\lambda_D f,$$

i.e. *f* is a simultaneous [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) of the operators π(*D*).

If ψ is a zonal spherical function, then, regarded as a function on *G*/*K*, it is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian there, an [elliptic differential operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_differential_operator) with [real analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_analytic) coefficients. By [analytic elliptic regularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI_transform#Holmgren's_uniqueness_theorem), ψ is a real analytic function on *G*/*K*, and hence *G*.

Harish-Chandra used these facts about the structure of the invariant operators to prove that his formula gave all zonal spherical functions for real semisimple Lie groups.[^n24][^o53][^n25] Indeed, the commutativity of the commutant implies that the simultaneous eigenspaces of the algebra of invariant differential operators all have dimension one; and the polynomial structure of this algebra forces the simultaneous eigenvalues to be precisely those already associated with Harish-Chandra's formula.

## Example: SL(2,C) {#Example:_SL(2,C)}

The group *G* = SL(2,**C**) is the [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) of the [compact Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Lie_group) *K* = SU(2) and the [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_covering_group) of the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489). The infinite-dimensional representations of the Lorentz group were first studied by [Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742) in 1945, who considered the [discrete series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_series) representations, which he termed *expansors*. A systematic study was taken up shortly afterwards by Harish-Chandra, Gelfand–Naimark and Bargmann. The irreducible representations of class one, corresponding to the zonal spherical functions, can be determined easily using the radial component of the [Laplacian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian_operator).[^n4]

Indeed, any unimodular complex 2×2 matrix *g* admits a unique [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) *g* = *pv* with *v* unitary and *p* positive. In turn *p* = *uau*\*, with *u* unitary and *a* a diagonal matrix with positive entries. Thus *g* = *uaw* with *w* = *u*\* *v*, so that any *K*-biinvariant function on *G* corresponds to a function of the diagonal matrix

$$a = \begin{pmatrix} e^{r/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-r/2} \end{pmatrix},$$

invariant under the Weyl group. Identifying *G*/*K* with hyperbolic 3-space, the zonal hyperbolic functions ψ correspond to radial functions that are eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. But in terms of the radial coordinate *r*, the Laplacian is given by[^n26]

$$L= -\partial_r^2 - 2 \coth r \partial_r.$$

Setting *f*(*r*) = sinh (*r*)·ψ(*r*), it follows that *f* is an [odd function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_function) of *r* and an eigenfunction of $\partial_r^2$.

Hence

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\varphi(r) = {\sin (\ell r)\over \ell \sinh r}$ |}

where $\ell$ is real.

There is a similar elementary treatment for the [generalized Lorentz groups](wikipedia://en/230489#Generalization_to_higher_dimensions) SO(*N*,1) in (Takahashi 1963) and (Faraut Korányi 1994) (recall that SO<sup>0</sup>(3,1) = SL(2,**C**) / ±I).

## Complex case {#Complex_case}

If *G* is a complex semisimple Lie group, it is the [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) of its maximal compact subgroup *K*. If ${\mathfrak g}$ and $\mathfrak{k}$ are their Lie algebras, then

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{k} \oplus i\mathfrak{k}.$$

Let *T* be a [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136) in *K* with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{t}$. Then

$$A= \exp i \mathfrak{t}, \,\, P= \exp i \mathfrak{k}.$$

Let

$$W= N_K(T)/T$$

be the [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) of *T* in *K*. Recall characters in Hom(*T*,**T**) are called [weights](wikipedia://en/277179) and can be identified with elements of the [weight lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight_lattice) Λ in Hom($\mathfrak{t}$, **R**) = $\mathfrak{t}^*$. There is a natural ordering on weights and every finite-dimensional irreducible representation (π, *V*) of *K* has a unique highest weight λ. The weights of the [adjoint representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_group) of *K* on $\mathfrak{k}\ominus \mathfrak{t}$ are called roots and ρ is used to denote half the sum of the [positive roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_root) α, [Weyl's character formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl's_character_formula) asserts that for *z* = exp *X* in *T*

$$\displaystyle\chi_\lambda(e^X)\equiv {\rm Tr} \, \pi(z) = A_{\lambda+\rho}(e^X)/A_{\rho}(e^X),$$

where, for μ in $\mathfrak{t}^*$, *A*<sub>μ</sub> denotes the antisymmetrisation

$$\displaystyle A_\mu(e^X) =\sum_{s\in W} \varepsilon(s) e^{i\mu(sX)},$$

and ε denotes the *sign character* of the [finite reflection group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_reflection_group) *W*.

[Weyl's denominator formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl's_denominator_formula) expresses the denominator *A*<sub>ρ</sub> as a product:

$$\displaystyle A_\rho(e^X) = e^{i\rho(X)} \prod_{\alpha>0}(1 - e^{-i\alpha(X)}),$$

where the product is over the positive roots.

[Weyl's dimension formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl's_dimension_formula) asserts that

$$\displaystyle\chi_\lambda(1) \equiv {\rm dim}\, V = {\prod_{\alpha>0} (\lambda + \rho,\alpha)\over \prod_{\alpha>0} (\rho,\alpha)}.$$

where the [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) on $\mathfrak{t}^*$ is that associated with the [Killing form](wikipedia://en/982386) on $\mathfrak{k}$.

Now

- every irreducible representation of *K* extends holomorphically to the complexification *G*
- every irreducible character χ<sub>λ</sub>(*k*) of *K* extends holomorphically to the complexification of *K* and $\mathfrak{t}^*$.
- for every λ in Hom(*A*,**T**) = $i\mathfrak{t}^*$, there is a zonal spherical function φ<sub>λ</sub>.

The **Berezin–Harish–Chandra formula**[^n4] asserts that for *X* in $i\mathfrak{t}$

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\varphi_\lambda(e^X) = {\chi_\lambda(e^X)\over\chi_\lambda(1)}.$ |}

In other words:

- ***the zonal spherical functions on a complex semisimple Lie group are given by analytic continuation of the formula for the normalised characters.***

One of the simplest proofs[^n27] of this formula involves the *radial component* on *A* of the Laplacian on *G*, a proof formally parallel to Helgason's reworking of [Freudenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freudenthal)'s classical proof of the [Weyl character formula](wikipedia://en/5011916), using the radial component on *T* of the Laplacian on *K*.[^n28]

In the latter case the [class functions](wikipedia://en/1001329) on *K* can be identified with *W*-invariant functions on *T*. The radial component of Δ<sub>*K*</sub> on *T* is just the expression for the restriction of Δ<sub>*K*</sub> to *W*-invariant functions on *T*, where it is given by the formula

$$\displaystyle \Delta_K= h^{-1}\circ \Delta_T \circ h + \|\rho\|^2,$$

where

$$\displaystyle h(e^X) = A_\rho(e^X)$$

for *X* in $\mathfrak{t}$. If χ is a character with highest weight λ, it follows that φ = *h*·χ satisfies

$$\Delta_T \varphi= (\|\lambda + \rho\|^2 -\|\rho\|^2) \varphi.$$

Thus for every weight μ with non-zero [Fourier coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_coefficient) in φ,

$$\displaystyle \|\lambda +\rho\|^2 = \|\mu+\rho\|^2.$$

The classical argument of Freudenthal shows that μ + ρ must have the form *s*(λ + ρ) for some *s* in *W*, so the character formula follows from the antisymmetry of φ.

Similarly *K*-biinvariant functions on *G* can be identified with *W*(*A*)-invariant functions on *A*. The radial component of Δ<sub>*G*</sub> on *A* is just the expression for the restriction of Δ<sub>*G*</sub> to *W*(*A*)-invariant functions on *A*. It is given by the formula

$$\displaystyle \Delta_G= H^{-1}\circ \Delta_A\circ H - \|\rho\|^2,$$

where

$$\displaystyle H(e^X) = A_\rho(e^X)$$

for *X* in $i\mathfrak{t}$.

The Berezin–Harish–Chandra formula for a zonal spherical function φ can be established by introducing the antisymmetric function

$$\displaystyle f= H\cdot\varphi,$$

which is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian Δ<sub>*A*</sub>. Since *K* is generated by copies of subgroups that are homomorphic images of SU(2) corresponding to [simple roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial), its complexification *G* is generated by the corresponding homomorphic images of SL(2,**C**). The formula for zonal spherical functions of SL(2,**C**) implies that *f* is a [periodic function](wikipedia://en/76848) on $i\mathfrak{t}$ with respect to some [sublattice](wikipedia://en/15782871). Antisymmetry under the Weyl group and the argument of Freudenthal again imply that ψ must have the stated form up to a multiplicative constant, which can be determined using the Weyl dimension formula.

## Example: SL(2,R) {#Example:_SL(2,R)}

The theory of zonal spherical functions for [SL(2,**R**)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL%282,R%29) originated in the work of [Mehler](wikipedia://en/31395561) in 1881 on hyperbolic geometry. He discovered the analogue of the Plancherel theorem, which was rediscovered by Fock in 1943. The corresponding eigenfunction expansion is termed the [Mehler–Fock transform](wikipedia://en/32798661). It was already put on a firm footing in 1910 by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544)'s important work on the [spectral theory of ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/16990545). The radial part of the Laplacian in this case leads to a [hypergeometric differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_differential_equation), the theory of which was treated in detail by Weyl. Weyl's approach was subsequently generalised by Harish-Chandra to study zonal spherical functions and the corresponding Plancherel theorem for more general semisimple Lie groups. Following the work of Dirac on the discrete series representations of SL(2,**R**), the general theory of unitary irreducible representations of SL(2,**R**) was developed independently by Bargmann, Harish-Chandra and Gelfand–Naimark. The irreducible representations of class one, or equivalently the theory of zonal spherical functions, form an important special case of this theory.

The group *G* = [SL(2,**R**)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL%282,R%29) is a [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_covering_group) of the 3-dimensional [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) SO(2,1), the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/318742) with its [Poincaré metric](wikipedia://en/1487868). It acts by [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493). The upper half-plane can be identified with the unit disc by the [Cayley transform](wikipedia://en/882010). Under this identification *G* becomes identified with the group [SU(1,1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%281,1%29), also acting by Möbius transformations. Because the action is [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_action), both spaces can be identified with *G*/*K*, where *K* = [SO(2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%282%29). The metric is invariant under *G* and the associated Laplacian is *G*-invariant, coinciding with the image of the [Casimir operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimir_operator). In the upper half-plane model the Laplacian is given by the formula[^n4][^n5]

$$\displaystyle\Delta=-4y^{2}(\partial_x^2 +\partial_y^2).$$

If *s* is a complex number and *z* = *x + i y* with *y* > 0, the function

$$\displaystyle f_s(z) =y^{s}= \exp({s}\cdot\log y),$$

is an eigenfunction of Δ:

$$\displaystyle \Delta f_s = 4s(1-s) f_s.$$

Since Δ commutes with *G*, any left translate of *f*<sub>*s*</sub> is also an eigenfunction with the same eigenvalue. In particular, averaging over *K*, the function

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\varphi_s(z) =\int_K f_s(k\cdot z)\, dk$ |}

is a *K*-invariant eigenfunction of Δ on *G*/*K*. When

$$\displaystyle s={1\over 2} + i\tau,$$

with τ real, these functions give all the zonal spherical functions on *G*. As with Harish-Chandra's more general formula for semisimple Lie groups, φ<sub>*s*</sub> is a zonal spherical function because it is the matrix coefficient corresponding to a vector fixed by *K* in the [principal series](wikipedia://en/10620358). Various arguments are available to prove that there are no others. One of the simplest classical [Lie algebraic](wikipedia://en/17944) arguments[^n4][^n5][^n29][^n30][^n31] is to note that, since Δ is an elliptic operator with analytic coefficients, by analytic elliptic regularity any eigenfunction is necessarily real analytic. Hence, if the zonal spherical function corresponds to the matrix coefficient for a vector *v* and representation σ, the vector *v* is an [analytic vector](wikipedia://en/292788) for *G* and

$$\displaystyle(\sigma(e^{X})v,v)= \sum_{n=0}^\infty (\sigma(X)^n v,v)/n!$$

for *X* in $i\mathfrak{t}$. The infinitesimal form of the irreducible unitary representations with a vector fixed by *K* were worked out classically by Bargmann.[^n29][^n30] They correspond precisely to the principal series of SL(2,**R**). It follows that the zonal spherical function corresponds to a principal series representation.

Another classical argument[^n32] proceeds by showing that on radial functions the Laplacian has the form

$$\displaystyle\Delta=-\partial_r^2 - \coth(r)\cdot \partial_r,$$

so that, as a function of *r*, the zonal spherical function φ(*r*) must satisfy the [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753)

$$\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime\prime} + \coth r\, \varphi^\prime = \alpha \, \varphi$$

for some constant α. The change of variables *t* = sinh *r* transforms this equation into the [hypergeometric differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_differential_equation). The general solution in terms of [Legendre functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_functions) of complex index is given by[^n1][^n33]

{| border="1" cellspacing="0" cellpadding="5"

\|$\varphi(r)=P_\rho(\cosh r) = {1\over 2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} (\cosh r + \sinh r \, \cos \theta)^\rho \, d\theta,$ |}

where α = ρ(ρ+1). Further restrictions on ρ are imposed by boundedness and positive-definiteness of the zonal spherical function on *G*.

There is yet another approach, due to Mogens Flensted-Jensen, which derives the properties of the zonal spherical functions on SL(2,**R**), including the Plancherel formula, from the corresponding results for SL(2,**C**), which are simple consequences of the Plancherel formula and Fourier inversion formula for **R**. This "method of descent" works more generally, allowing results for a real semisimple Lie group to be derived by descent from the corresponding results for its complexification.[^n34][^n35]

## Further directions {#Further_directions}

- *The theory of zonal functions that are not necessarily positive-definite.* These are given by the same formulas as above, but without restrictions on the complex parameter *s* or ρ. They correspond to non-unitary representations.[^n4]
- *[Harish-Chandra's eigenfunction expansion and inversion formula for spherical functions](wikipedia://en/18298785).*[^n36] This is an important special case of his [Plancherel theorem](wikipedia://en/454315) for real semisimple Lie groups.
- *The structure of the Hecke algebra*. Harish-Chandra and Godement proved that, as convolution algebras, there are natural isomorphisms between C<sub>c</sub><sup>∞</sup>(*K* \\ *G* / *K* ) and C<sub>c</sub><sup>∞</sup>(*A*)<sup>*W*</sup>, the subalgebra invariant under the Weyl group.[^n2] This is straightforward to establish for SL(2,**R**).[^n5]
- *Spherical functions for [Euclidean motion groups](wikipedia://en/652164) and [compact Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Lie_group)*.[^n4]
- *Spherical functions for [p-adic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic) Lie groups*. These were studied in depth by Satake and [Macdonald](wikipedia://en/3100378).[^n37][^n38] Their study, and that of the associated Hecke algebras, was one of the first steps in the extensive representation theory of semisimple p-adic Lie groups, a key element in the [Langlands program](wikipedia://en/171905).
