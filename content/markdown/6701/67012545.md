# Common ground (linguistics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Common ground"}

In [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_%28linguistics%29), [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics), and [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), the **common ground** of a conversation is the set of [propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositions) that the [interlocutors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlocutor_%28linguistics%29) have agreed to treat as true. For a proposition to be in the common ground, it must be [common knowledge](wikipedia://en/1409006) in the conversational context. The set of [possible worlds](wikipedia://en/780566) compatible with the common ground is often called the *context set*.[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5]

:::

The concept is fundamental to many theories of discourse. In such theories, the [speech act](wikipedia://en/44836) of [assertion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_assertion) is often analyzed as a proposal to add an additional proposition to the common ground. Similarly, [presuppositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presuppositions) are taken to be licensed when they are already established in the common ground. While such approaches are typically construed as [pragmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics), the framework of [dynamic semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_semantics) treats the semantic [denotations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denotation) of sentences as functions which update the common ground.[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5] In many theories, the common ground is one of several elements of the [conversational scoreboard](wikipedia://en/68477468).[^o6]
