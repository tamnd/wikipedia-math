# Welch's t-test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Welch's t-test"}

**Welch's *t*-test**, or **unequal variances *t*-test** in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) is a two-sample [location test](wikipedia://en/22747655) which is used to test the (null) hypothesis that two [populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_%28statistics%29) have equal means. It is named for its creator, [Bernard Lewis Welch](wikipedia://en/57043307), and is an adaptation of [Student's *t*-test](wikipedia://en/536080),[^o2] and is more reliable when the two samples have unequal variances and possibly unequal sample sizes.[^o3][^o4] These tests are often referred to as "unpaired" or "independent samples" *t*-tests, as they are typically applied when the statistical units underlying the two samples being compared are non-overlapping. Given that Welch's *t*-test has been less popular than Student's *t*-test[^o3] and may be less familiar to readers, a more informative name is "Welch's unequal variances *t*-test" — or "unequal variances *t*-test" for brevity.[^o4] Sometimes, it is referred as **Satterthwaite** or **Welch–Satterthwaite test**.

:::

## Assumptions {#Assumptions}

Student's *t*-test assumes that the sample means being compared for two populations are normally distributed, and that the populations have equal variances. Welch's *t*-test is designed for unequal population variances, but the assumption of normality is maintained.[^o2] Welch's *t*-test is an approximate solution to the [Behrens–Fisher problem](wikipedia://en/2908018).

## Calculations {#Calculations}

Welch's *t*-test defines the statistic *t* by the following formula:

$$t = \frac{\Delta\overline{X}}{s_{\Delta\bar{X}}} = \frac{\overline{X}_1 - \overline{X}_2}{\sqrt{ {s_{\bar{X}_1}^2} + {s_{\bar{X}_2}^2} }},$$

$$s_{\bar{X}_i} = \frac{s_i}{\sqrt{N_i}},$$

where $\overline{X}_i$ and $s_{\bar{X}_i}$ are the $i$-th [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) and its [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520), with $s_i$ denoting the [corrected sample standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590#Corrected_sample_standard_deviation), and [sample size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size) $N_i$. Unlike in [Student's *t*-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student's_t_test), the denominator is *not* based on a [pooled variance](wikipedia://en/10910481) estimate.

The [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606) $\nu$ associated with this variance estimate is approximated using the [Welch–Satterthwaite equation](wikipedia://en/5947843):[^o7]

$$\nu \approx \frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{N_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{N_2}\right)^2}{\frac{s_1^4}{N_1^2 \nu_1} + \frac{s_2^4}{N_2^2 \nu_2}} = \frac{s_{\Delta\bar{X}}^4}{\frac{s_{\bar{X}_1}^4}{\nu_1} + \frac{s_{\bar{X}_2}^4}{\nu_2}},$$

where $\nu_i = N_i - 1$ is the degrees of freedom associated with the *i*-th variance estimate.

This expression can be simplified when $N_1 = N_2$:

$$\nu \approx \nu' \frac{s_{\Delta\bar{X}}^4}{s_{\bar{X}_1}^4 + s_{\bar{X}_2}^4},$$

where $\nu' = \nu_1 = \nu_2 = N_1 - 1 = N_2 - 1$ is the degrees of freedom associated with each variance estimate.

The statistic is approximately from the [*t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375), since we have an approximation of the [chi-square distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-square_distribution). This approximation is better done when both $N_1$ and $N_2$ are larger than 5.[^o8][^o9]

## Statistical test {#Statistical_test}

Once *t* and *$\nu$* have been computed, these statistics can be used with the [*t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) to test one of two possible [null hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis):

- A [two-tailed test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-tailed_test), in which the two population means are equal; or
- A [one-tailed test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-tailed_test), in which one of the population means is greater than or equal to the other.

The approximate degrees of freedom are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\left(\nu\in\mathbb{R}^+\right)$ and used as such in statistics-oriented software, whereas they are rounded down to the nearest integer in spreadsheets.

## Confidence intervals {#Confidence_intervals}

Based on Welch's t-test, it's possible to also construct a two sided [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) for the difference in means (while not having to assume equal variances). This will be by taking:

$$CI(\mu_1-\mu_2): \overline{X}_1 - \overline{X}_2 \pm \sqrt{ {s_{\bar{X}_1}^2} + {s_{\bar{X}_2}^2} } \times t_{\nu, 1-\alpha/2}$$

Based on the above definitions of $s_{\bar{X}_i}$ and $\nu$.

## Advantages and limitations {#Advantages_and_limitations}

Welch's *t*-test is more robust than Student's *t*-test and maintains [type I error rates](wikipedia://en/5657877) close to nominal for unequal variances and for unequal sample sizes under normality. Furthermore, the [power](wikipedia://en/238695) of Welch's *t*-test comes close to that of Student's *t*-test, even when the population variances are equal and sample sizes are balanced.[^o3] Welch's *t*-test can be generalized to more than 2-samples,[^o13] which is more robust than [one-way analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/14344439) (ANOVA).

It is *not recommended* to pre-test for equal variances and then choose between Student's *t*-test or Welch's *t*-test.[^o14] Rather, Welch's *t*-test can be applied directly and without any substantial disadvantages to Student's *t*-test as noted above. Welch's *t*-test remains robust for skewed distributions and large sample sizes.[^o15] Reliability decreases for skewed distributions and smaller samples, where one could possibly perform Welch's *t*-test.[^o16]

## Resampling-based versions {#Resampling-based_versions}

A permutation and bootstrapped version of the Welch t-test has also been developed to address distributional requirements without relying on large sample sizes. The Welch t statistic additionally satisfies the requirement for exchangeable samples under permutation testing in the presence of unequal variances - an instance of the Behrens–Fisher problem. This approach has been extensively discussed in the statistical literature.[^o18][^o19][^o20][^o21][^o22][^o23][^o24]
