# Sylvester's sequence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sylvester's sequence"}

*Image: Sylvester-square.svg.* Graphical demonstration of the convergence of the sum 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/7 + 1/43 + ... to 1. Each row of *k* squares of side length 1/*k* has total [area](wikipedia://en/1209) 1/*k*, and all the squares together exactly cover a larger square with area 1. Squares with side lengths 1/1807 or smaller are too small to see in the figure and are not shown.

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Sylvester's sequence** is an [integer sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) in which each term is the product of the previous terms, plus one. Its first few terms are

:::

2, 3, 7, 43, 1807, 3263443, 10650056950807, 113423713055421844361000443 .

Sylvester's sequence is named after [James Joseph Sylvester](wikipedia://en/234861), who first investigated it in 1880.[^n1] Its values grow [doubly exponentially](wikipedia://en/8541166), and the sum of its [reciprocals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29) forms a [series](wikipedia://en/15287) of [unit fractions](wikipedia://en/491316) that [converges](wikipedia://en/1743431) to 1 more rapidly than any other series of unit fractions.[^o20] The [recurrence](wikipedia://en/146806) by which it is defined allows the numbers in the sequence to be [factored](wikipedia://en/15491) more easily than other numbers of the same magnitude,[^n2] but, due to the rapid growth of the sequence, complete [prime factorizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) are known only for a few of its terms.[^o40] Values derived from this sequence have also been used to construct finite [Egyptian fraction](wikipedia://en/336349) representations of 1, [Sasakian](wikipedia://en/4733638) [Einstein manifolds](wikipedia://en/644814),[^n3] and hard instances for [online algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_algorithm).

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definitions"}

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

Formally, Sylvester's sequence can be defined by the formula

$$s_n = 1 + \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} s_i.$$

The [product of the empty set](wikipedia://en/181289) is 1,[^n4] so this formula gives *s*<sub>0</sub> = 2, without need of a separate [base case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_case_%28recursion%29).

Alternatively, one may define the sequence by the [recurrence](wikipedia://en/146806)[^n2]

$\displaystyle s_i = s_{i-1}(s_{i-1}-1)+1,$ with the base case *s*<sub>0</sub> = 2.

It is straightforward to show by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) that this is equivalent to the other definition.[^o10]

:::

::: {#o12 .statement cue=named-heading name="Closed form formula and asymptotics"}

## Closed form formula and asymptotics {#Closed_form_formula_and_asymptotics}

The Sylvester numbers grow [doubly exponentially](wikipedia://en/8541166) as a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of *n*. Specifically, it can be shown that

$$s_n = \left\lfloor E^{2^{n+1}}+\frac{1}{2} \right\rfloor,\!$$

for a number *E* that is approximately 1.26408473530530...[^o13] . This formula has the effect of the following [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775):

*s*<sub>0</sub> is the nearest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) to *E*<sup>2</sup>; *s*<sub>1</sub> is the nearest integer to *E*<sup>4</sup>; *s*<sub>2</sub> is the nearest integer to *E*<sup>8</sup>; for *s<sub>n</sub>*, take *E*<sup>2</sup>, [square](wikipedia://en/659942) it *n* more times, and take the nearest integer.

This would only be a practical algorithm if we had a better way of calculating *E* to the requisite number of places than calculating *s*<sub>*n*</sub> and taking its repeated [square root](wikipedia://en/29208).[^n5]

The double-exponential growth of the Sylvester sequence is unsurprising if one compares it to the sequence of [Fermat numbers](wikipedia://en/91127) *F*<sub>*n*</sub>; the Fermat numbers are usually defined by a doubly exponential formula, $2^{2^n} \!+ 1$, but they can also be defined by a product formula very similar to that defining Sylvester's sequence:

$$F_n = 2 + \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} F_i.$$

:::

## Connection with Egyptian fractions {#Connection_with_Egyptian_fractions}

The [unit fractions](wikipedia://en/491316) formed by the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of the values in Sylvester's sequence generate an [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series):[^o17]

$$\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{s_i} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{43} + \frac{1}{1807} + \cdots.$$

The [partial sums](wikipedia://en/15287#Basic_properties) of this series have a simple form,

$$\sum_{i=0}^{j-1} \frac1{s_i} = 1 - \frac{1}{s_j-1} = \frac{s_j-2}{s_j-1},$$

which is already in lowest terms.[^n6] This may be [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by induction, or more directly by noting that the recursion implies that

$$\frac{1}{s_i-1}-\frac{1}{s_{i+1}-1} = \frac{1}{s_i},$$

so the sum [telescopes](wikipedia://en/508070)[^n6]

$$\sum_{i=0}^{j-1} \frac{1}{s_i} = \sum_{i=0}^{j-1} \left( \frac{1}{s_i-1}-\frac{1}{s_{i+1}-1} \right) = \frac{1}{s_0-1} - \frac{1}{s_j-1} = 1 - \frac{1}{s_j-1}.$$

Since this sequence of partial sums (*s*<sub>*j*</sub> − 2)/(*s*<sub>*j*</sub> − 1) converges to one, the overall series forms an infinite [Egyptian fraction](wikipedia://en/336349) representation of the number one:

$$1 = \frac12 + \frac13 + \frac17 + \frac1{43} + \frac1{1807} + \cdots.$$

One can find finite Egyptian fraction representations of one, of any length, by truncating this series and subtracting one from the last denominator:

$$1 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac16, \quad 1 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac17 + \tfrac1{42}, \quad 1 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac17 + \tfrac1{43} + \tfrac1{1806},\quad \dots.$$

The sum of the first *k* terms of the infinite series provides the closest possible underestimate of 1 by any *k*-term Egyptian fraction.[^o20] For example, the first four terms add to 1805/1806, and therefore any Egyptian fraction for a number in the [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) (1805/1806, 1) requires at least five terms.

It is possible to interpret the Sylvester sequence as the result of a [greedy algorithm for Egyptian fractions](wikipedia://en/7277012), that at each step chooses the smallest possible denominator that makes the partial sum of the series be less than one.[^n7]

## Uniqueness of quickly growing series with rational sums {#Uniqueness_of_quickly_growing_series_with_rational_sums}

As Sylvester himself observed, Sylvester's sequence seems to be unique in having such quickly growing values, while simultaneously having a series of reciprocals that converges to a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024). This sequence provides an example showing that double-exponential growth is not enough to cause an integer sequence to be an [irrationality sequence](wikipedia://en/37187938).[^n8]

To make this more precise, it follows from results of (Badea 1993) that, if a sequence of integers $a_n$ grows quickly enough that

$$a_n\ge a_{n-1}^2-a_{n-1}+1,$$

and if the series

$$A=\sum\frac1{a_i}$$

converges to a rational number *A*, then, for all *n* after some point, this sequence must be defined by the same recurrence

$$a_n= a_{n-1}^2-a_{n-1}+1$$

that can be used to define Sylvester's sequence.[^n9]

(Erdős Graham 1980) [conjectured](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdős_conjecture) that, in results of this type, the [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) bounding the growth of the sequence could be replaced by a weaker condition,[^n10]

$$\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{a_n}{a_{n-1}^2}=1.$$

(Badea 1995) surveys progress related to this [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138); see also (Brown 1979).

## Divisibility and factorizations {#Divisibility_and_factorizations}

If *i* \< *j*, it follows from the definition that *s*<sub>*j*</sub> ≡ 1 (mod *s*<sub>*i*</sub>). Therefore, every two numbers in Sylvester's sequence are [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime). The sequence can be used to prove that there are infinitely many [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), as any prime can divide at most one number in the sequence. More strongly, no prime factor of a number in the sequence can be [congruent](wikipedia://en/20087) to 5 modulo 6, and the sequence can be used to prove that there are infinitely many primes congruent to 7 modulo 12.[^n11] No term can be a [perfect power](wikipedia://en/4023591).[^n12]

::: {#o33 .problem cue=unsolved}

Are all the terms in Sylvester's sequence squarefree?

:::

Much remains unknown about the factorization of the numbers in Sylvester's sequence. For instance, it is not known if all numbers in the sequence are [squarefree](wikipedia://en/29525), although all the known terms are.[^o34]

As (Vardi 1991) describes, it is easy to determine which Sylvester number (if any) a given prime *p* divides: simply compute the recurrence defining the numbers modulo *p* until finding either a number that is congruent to zero (mod *p*) or finding a repeated modulus.[^n2] Using this technique he found that 1166 out of the first three million primes are [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of Sylvester numbers,[^o37] and that none of these primes has a square that divides a Sylvester number. The set of primes that can occur as factors of Sylvester numbers is of density zero in the set of all primes:[^n13] indeed, the number of such primes less than *x* is $O(\pi(x) / \log\log\log x)$.[^n14]

## Applications {#Applications}

(Boyer Galicki Kollár 2005) use the properties of Sylvester's sequence to define large numbers of [Sasakian](wikipedia://en/4733638) [Einstein manifolds](wikipedia://en/644814) having the [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562) of [odd](wikipedia://en/143135)-dimensional [spheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) or [exotic spheres](wikipedia://en/1114931). They show that the number of distinct Sasakian Einstein [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) on a [topological sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_sphere) of dimension 2*n* − 1 is at least proportional to *s*<sub>*n*</sub> and hence has double exponential growth with *n*.[^n3]

As (Galambos Woeginger 1995) describe, (Brown 1979) and (Liang 1980) used values derived from Sylvester's sequence to construct [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) examples for [online](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_algorithm) [bin packing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bin_packing) algorithms. (Seiden Woeginger 2005) similarly use the sequence to lower bound the performance of a two-dimensional cutting stock algorithm.[^o50]

[Znám's problem](wikipedia://en/1452979) concerns [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of numbers such that each number in the set divides but is not equal to the product of all the other numbers, plus one. Without the inequality requirement, the values in Sylvester's sequence would solve the problem; with that requirement, it has other solutions derived from recurrences similar to the one defining Sylvester's sequence. Solutions to Znám's problem have applications to the classification of surface singularities (Brenton and Hill 1988) and to the theory of [nondeterministic finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondeterministic_finite_automata).[^n15]

(Curtiss 1922) describes an application of the closest approximations to one by *k*-term sums of unit fractions, in lower-bounding the number of divisors of any [perfect number](wikipedia://en/23670), and (Miller 1919) uses the same property to [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) the size of certain [groups](wikipedia://en/19447).
