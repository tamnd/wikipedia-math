# Demographics of Africa

2026

*Image: Africa population density, 2019.jpg.* Population density in Africa in 2019 (persons per km<sup>2</sup>).

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Demographics of Africa"}

The **population of Africa** has [grown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth) rapidly over the past century[^o3] and consequently shows a large [youth bulge](wikipedia://en/989011#Youth_bulge), further reinforced by a low [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669) of below 50 years in some African countries.[^o4] Total population as of 2024 is estimated at more than 1.5 billion, with a growth rate of more than 2.5% p.a. The most populous African country is Nigeria with 233 million inhabitants as of 2024 and a [growth rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Nigeria) of 2.6% p.a.[^o5]

:::

## Population growth {#Population_growth}

*Image: Population growth rate world 2013.svg.* Most African countries have annual population growth rates above 2%.

, the total population of Africa is estimated at 1.59 billion as of 2026, representing 22% of the world's population.[^o5] According to UN estimates, the population of Africa may reach nearly 2.5 billion by 2050 (about 26% of the world's total) and 3.8 billion by 2100 (about 37% of the world's total).[^o5]

The population of Africa first surpassed one billion in 2009, with a [doubling time](wikipedia://en/6713437) of 27 years (growth rate 2.6% p.a.).[^o7]

Population growth has continued at almost the same pace, and total population is expected to surpass 2 billion by 2038 (doubling time 29 years, 2.4% p.a.).[^o8]

The reason for the population growth since the mid 20th century is the decrease of [infant mortality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality) and general increase of [life expectancy](wikipedia://en/18669) without a corresponding reduction in [fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_rate), due to a very limited use of [contraceptives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraceptives). Uncontrolled population growth threatens to overwhelm infrastructure development and crippling economic development.[^o9] Kenya and [Zambia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zambia) are pursuing programs to promote [family planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_planning) in an attempt to curb growth rates.[^o10]

The extreme population growth in Africa is driven by [East Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Africa), [Middle Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Africa) and [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa), which regions are projected to more than quintuple their populations over the 21st century. The most extreme of these is Middle Africa, with an estimated population increase by 698%, from less than 100 million in 2000 to almost 800 million in 2100 (more than half of this figure is driven by the [Democratic Republic of the Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo), projected to increase from 51 million in 2000 to 431 million in 2100). Projected population growth is less extreme in [Southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa) and [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa), which are expected, respectively, to increase by 104% and 156% over the same period.[^o5]

### National healthcare systems {#National_healthcare_systems}

National health systems vary between countries. In [Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana), most health care is provided by the government and largely administered by the [Ministry of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Health,_Ghana) and [Ghana Health Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghana_Health_Services). The healthcare system has five levels of providers: health posts which are first level primary care for rural areas, health centers and clinics, district hospitals, regional hospitals and tertiary hospitals. These programs are funded by the government of Ghana, financial credits, Internally Generated Fund, and Donors-pooled Health Fund.[^o27]

A shortage of health professionals compounded by migration of health workers from sub-Saharan Africa to other parts of the world (namely English-speaking nations such as the United States and the United Kingdom) has negatively impacted productivity and efficacy of the region's health systems.[^o28]

More than 85% of individuals in Africa use traditional medicine as an alternative to often expensive allopathic medical health care and costly pharmaceutical products. The Organization of African Unity (OAU) Heads of State and Government declared the 2000s decade as the African Decade on African Traditional Medicine in an effort to promote The WHO African Region's adopted resolution for institutionalizing traditional medicine in health care systems across the continent.[^o29] Public policy makers in the region are challenged with consideration of the importance of traditional/indigenous health systems and whether their coexistence with the modern medical and health sub-sector would improve the equitability and accessibility of health care distribution, the health status of populations, and the social-economic development of nations within sub-Saharan Africa.[^o30]

:::

### History of health care development in sub-Saharan Africa {#History_of_health_care_development_in_sub-Saharan_Africa}

In September 1987, [UNICEF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNICEF) and the [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization) (WHO) Regional Committee announced the launching of the [Bamako Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamako_Initiative)— chartered in response to financial issues occurring in the region during the 1980s, and with the aim of increasing access to vital medications through community involvement in revolving drug funds.[^o32][^o33] The 1987 [Bamako Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamako_Initiative) conference, organized by the [WHO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization) was held in [Bamako](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bamako), the capital of [Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali), and helped reshape the health policy of sub-Saharan Africa.[^o34] The meeting was attended by African [Ministers of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_minister) who advocated for improvement of healthcare access through the revitalization of primary healthcare.[^o32][^o33] The new strategy substantially increased accessibility through community-based [healthcare reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_reform), resulting in more efficient and equitable provision of services. The public health community within the region raised issues in response to the initiative, of which included: equity, access, affordability, integration issues, relative importance given to medications, management, dependency, logistics, and sustainability.[^o32] As a result of these critiques, the Initiative later transformed to address the increase of accessibility of health services, the enhancement of quality of health services, and the overall improvement of health system management.[^o32] A comprehensive approach strategy was extended to all areas of health care, with subsequent improvement in the health care indicators and improvement in health care efficiency and cost.[^o35][^o36]

### Major health challenges {#Major_health_challenges}

The sub-Saharan African region experiences disproportionate rates of infectious and chronic diseases in comparison to other global regions.[^o63]

#### Diabetes {#Diabetes}

[Type 2 diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_2_diabetes) persists as an epidemic in the region posing a public health and socioeconomic crisis for sub-Saharan Africa. Scarcity of data for pathogenesis and subtypes for diabetes in sub-Saharan African communities has led to gaps in documenting epidemiology for the disease. High rates of undiagnosed diabetes in many countries leaves individuals at a high risk of chronic health complications, thus, posing a high risk of diabetes-related morbidity and mortality in the region.[^o42]

#### HIV/AIDS {#HIV/AIDS}

In 2011, sub-Saharan Africa was home to 69% of all people living with [HIV/AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV/AIDS) worldwide.[^o44] In response, a number of initiatives have been launched to educate the public on HIV/AIDS. Among these are combination prevention programmes, considered to be the most effective initiative, the [abstinence, be faithful, use a condom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstinence,_be_faithful,_use_a_condom) campaign, and the [Desmond Tutu HIV Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desmond_Tutu_HIV_Foundation)'s outreach programs.[^o45] According to a 2013 special report issued by the [Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_United_Nations_Programme_on_HIV/AIDS) (UNAIDS), the number of HIV positive people in Africa receiving anti-retroviral treatment in 2012 was over seven times the number receiving treatment in 2005, with an almost 1 million added in the last year alone.[^o46][^o47] The number of AIDS-related deaths in sub-Saharan Africa in 2011 was 33 percent less than the number in 2005.[^o48] The number of new HIV infections in sub-Saharan Africa in 2011 was 25 percent less than the number in 2001.[^o48]

#### Malaria {#Malaria}

[Malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria) is an endemic illness in sub-Saharan Africa, where the majority of malaria cases and deaths worldwide occur.[^o52]

#### Maternal and infant mortality {#Maternal_and_infant_mortality}

Studies show that more than half of the world's [maternal deaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maternal_death) occur in sub-Saharan Africa.[^o54][^o55] However, progress has been made in this area, as maternal mortality rates have decreased for multiple countries in the region by about half since 1990.[^o55] Additionally, the [African Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Union) in July 2003 ratified the [Maputo Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maputo_Protocol), which pledges to prohibit [female genital mutilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Female_genital_mutilation)(FGM).[^o56]

The sub-Saharan African region alone accounts for about 45% of global [infant and child mortalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant_mortality). Studies have shown a relationship between infant survival and the education of mothers, as years of education positively correlate with infant survival rates. Geographic location is also a factor, as child mortality rates are higher in rural areas in comparison to urban regions.[^o57]

#### Measles {#Measles}

Routine immunization has been introduced to countries within sub-Saharan Africa in order to prevent [measles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measles) outbreaks within the region.[^o59]

#### Neglected tropical diseases {#Neglected_tropical_diseases}

[Neglected tropical diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neglected_tropical_diseases) such as [hookworm infection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hookworm_infection) encompass some of the most common health conditions which affect an estimated 500 million individuals in the sub-Saharan African region.[^o61]

#### Non-communicable diseases {#Non-communicable_diseases}

Results of [Global Burden of Disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Burden_of_Disease) studies reveal that the age-standardized death rates of [non-communicable diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-communicable_disease) in at least four sub-Saharan countries including South Africa, [Democratic Republic of Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo), Nigeria, and Ethiopia supersede that of identified high-income countries.[^o63] Improvement in statistics systems and increase in epidemiological studies with in-depth analysis of disease risk factors could improve the understanding of [non-communicable diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-communicable_disease) (i.e.: [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes_mellitus), [hypertension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertension), [cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer), [cardiovascular disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_disease), [obesity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obesity), etc.) in sub-Saharan Africa as well as better inform decisions surrounding healthcare policy in the region.[^o63]

#### Onchocerciasis {#Onchocerciasis}

[Onchocerciasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onchocerciasis) ("river blindness"), a common cause of [blindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blindness), is also endemic to parts of the region. More than 99% of people affected by the illness worldwide live in 31 countries therein.[^o65] In response, the African Programme for Onchocerciasis Control (APOC) was launched in 1995 with the aim of controlling the disease.[^o65]

#### Tuberculosis {#Tuberculosis}

[Tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality on a global scale, especially in high HIV-prevalent populations in the sub-Saharan African region, with a high [case fatality rate](wikipedia://en/3560385).[^o67]

## Socioeconomics {#Socioeconomics}

*Image: African countries by HDI (2020).svg.* }

The [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index) (HDI) is a composite measure of life expectancy, education, and income. As of 2020, HDI levels vary significantly across Africa, with the highest levels found in North Africa and parts of Southern Africa, and the lowest in Central Africa and the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel) region.[^o69]

## Ethnicities {#Ethnicities}

*Image: San tribesman.jpg.* [San](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_people) man from [Botswana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botswana).

*Image: Kwarastatedrummers.jpg.* [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_music) drummers in [Kwara State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwara_State), Nigeria (2004).

*Image: Mongo family in Equateur Province.jpg.* [Mongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongo_people) family in the [Province of Équateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Équateur), [DRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_Republic_of_the_Congo)

Speakers of [Bantu languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages) (part of the [Niger–Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger–Congo_languages) family) predominate in southern, central and southeast Africa. The [Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_peoples) farmers from West Africa's inland savanna progressively expanded over most of Africa.[^o72] But there are also several [Nilotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilotic) groups in [South Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sudan) and East Africa, the mixed [Swahili people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_people) on the [Swahili Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_Coast), and a few remaining [indigenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Africa) Khoisan ([San](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_people) and [Khoikhoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoikhoi)) and [Pygmy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pygmy) peoples in southern and central Africa, respectively. Bantu-speaking Africans also predominate in Gabon and Equatorial Guinea, and are found in parts of southern Cameroon. In the [Kalahari Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalahari_Desert) of [Southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa), the distinct people known as the "San" have long been present. Together with the [Khoikhoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoikhoi), they form the [Khoisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoisan). The San are the pre-Bantu indigenous people of southern Africa, while Pygmies are the pre-Bantu indigenous African peoples of Central Africa.[^o73] The peoples of [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa) primarily speak [Niger–Congo languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger–Congo_languages) belonging mostly, though not exclusively, to its non-[Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages) branches, though some [Nilo-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan_languages) and [Afroasiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages)-speaking groups are also found. The Niger–Congo-speaking [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_people), [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_people), [Fulani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulani_people), [Akan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akan_people) and [Wolof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolof_people) ethnic groups are the largest and most influential. In the central Sahara, [Mandinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandinka_people) or [Mande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandé_peoples) groups are most significant. [Chadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chadic_languages)-speaking groups, including the [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_people), are found in the more northerly parts of the region nearest to the Sahara and [Nilo-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan) communities such as the [Kanuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanuri_people),[^o74][^o75] [Zarma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarma_people)[^o75] and [Songhai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhai_people)[^o75][^o76] are present in eastern parts of West Africa bordering [Central Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Africa).

The peoples of North Africa comprise three main groups: [Berbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berbers) in the northwest, [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians) and [Libyans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libyans) in northeast, and [Nilo-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan)-speaking peoples in the east. The Muslim settlers who arrived in the 7th century introduced the Arabic language and Islam to the region, initiating a process of linguistic [Arabization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabization) of the region's inhabitants. The Semitic [Phoenicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia) (who founded [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage)) and [Hyksos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyksos), the Indo-Iranian [Alans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alans), the Indo-European [Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece), [Romans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) and [Vandals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandals) settled in North Africa as well. [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages)-speaking populations still make significant communities within Morocco and Algeria and are still also present in smaller numbers in Tunisia and Libya.[^o77] The Berber-speaking [Tuareg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuareg_people) and other often-[nomadic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nomad) peoples are the principal inhabitants of the Saharan interior of North Africa. In Mauritania, there is a small Berber community and Niger–Congo-speaking peoples in the South, though in both regions Arabic and Arab culture predominates. In Sudan, although Arabic and Arab culture predominates, it is also inhabited by originally Nilo-Saharan-speaking groups such as the [Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubians), [Fur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fur), [Masalit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masalit_people) and [Zaghawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaghawa_people)[^o78] who over the centuries have variously intermixed with migrants from the Arabian peninsula. Small communities of Afro-Asiatic-speaking [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_people) nomads can also be found in Egypt and Sudan.

*Image: Bedscha.jpg.* [Beja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beja_people) nomads from [Northeast Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_Africa)

In the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa), [Afro-Asiatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Asiatic_languages)-speaking groups predominate. Ethiopian and [Eritrean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrea) groups like the [Amhara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amhara_people) and [Tigrayans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigray-Tigrinya_people) (collectively known as [Habesha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habesha_people)) speak languages from the [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages) branch of Afro-Asiatic language family, while the [Oromo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_people) and [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_people) speak languages from the [Cushitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cushitic) branch of Afro-Asiatic. In southern Ethiopia and Eritrea, Nilotic peoples related to those in South Sudan are also found, while Bantu and Khoisan ethnic minorities inhabit parts of southern Somalia.

*Image: Boerfamily1886.jpg.* An [Afrikaner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrikaner) family from South Africa, 1886.

Prior to the [decolonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decolonization) movements of the post-[World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) era, [Europeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_of_Europe) were represented in every part of Africa.[^o79] Decolonisation during the 1960s and 1970s often resulted in the mass emigration of European-descended settlers out of Africa – especially from Algeria and Morocco (1.6 million *[pieds-noirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pied-noir)* in North Africa),[^o80] Kenya, Congo,[^o81] Rhodesia, Mozambique and Angola.[^o82] By the end of 1977, more than one million Portuguese were thought to have returned from Africa.[^o83] Nevertheless, [White Africans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Africans_of_European_ancestry) remain a minority in many African states, particularly South Africa, Zimbabwe, [Namibia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namibia) and [Réunion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Réunion).[^o84] The African country with the largest native European African population is South Africa.[^o85] The [Afrikaners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrikaner), the [British diaspora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_diaspora_in_Africa) and the [Coloureds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coloured) are the largest native European-descended groups in Africa today.

Native European colonization also brought sizable groups of Asians, particularly people from the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent), to British colonies. Large [Indian communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-resident_Indian_and_person_of_Indian_origin) are found in South Africa, and smaller ones are present in Kenya, Tanzania, and some other southern and East African countries. The large [Indian community in Uganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indians_in_Uganda) was [expelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expulsion_of_Asians_in_Uganda_in_1972) by the dictator [Idi Amin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idi_Amin) in 1972, though many have since returned. The islands in the Indian Ocean are also populated primarily by people of Asian origin, often mixed with Africans and Europeans. The [Malagasy people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malagasy_people) of [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madagascar) are [Austronesian people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_people) and native African people, but those along the coast are generally mixed with Bantu, Arab, Indian and European origins. Malay and Indian ancestries are also important components in the group of people known in South Africa as [Cape Coloureds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Coloured) (people with origins in two or more races and continents), In the 21st century, a few tens of thousands of Latin Americans, mainly Mexicans, Colombians, Chileans, Peruvians, Brazilians, and Central Americans, have migrated to Africa. Most of them are diplomats, missionaries, workers contracted by multinational companies, or people married to African locals, with the majority spread across South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria, Angola, Uganda, Egypt, Morocco, and Ghana. During the 20th century, small but economically important communities of [Lebanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics_of_Lebanon#The_Lebanese_Diaspora) and [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overseas_Chinese)[^o86] have also developed in the larger coastal cities of [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa) and East Africa, respectively.[^o87]

## Languages {#Languages}

There are three major linguistic phyla native to Africa: [Niger–Congo languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger–Congo_languages) (including [Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages)) in [West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa), [Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Africa), [Southeast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Africa) and [Southern Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Africa); [Nilo-Saharan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan_languages) (unity debated) spoken from Tanzania to Sudan and from [Chad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chad) to [Mali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mali); [Khoisan languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoisan_languages) (probably no phylogenetic unit, see [Khoe languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoe_languages)), concentrated in the [Kalahari Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalahari_Desert) of [Namibia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namibia) and [Botswana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botswana); There are several other small families and [language isolates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_isolate), as well as languages that have [yet to be classified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unclassified_language).

In addition, the [Afroasiatic languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_languages) are spread throughout [Western Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Asia), [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa), the [Horn of Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_of_Africa) and parts of the [Sahel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahel). The [Afroasiatic homeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afroasiatic_Urheimat) may be either in Western Asia or in Africa.

More recently introduced to Africa are [Austronesian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austronesian_languages) spoken in [Madagascar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madagascar), as well as [Indo-European languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-European_languages) spoken in South Africa and [Namibia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namibia) (Afrikaans, English, German), which were used as [lingua francas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_francas) in former [European colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_empire).

The total number of languages natively spoken in Africa is variously estimated (depending on the delineation of [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) vs. [dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialect)) at between 1,250 and 2,100,[^o89] and by some counts at "over 3,000",[^o90] Nigeria alone has over 500 languages (according to the count of [SIL Ethnologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIL_Ethnologue)),[^o91]

Around a hundred languages are widely used for inter-ethnic communication. [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Arabic), [Somali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somali_language), [Berber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berber_languages), [Amharic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amharic), [Oromo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oromo_language), [Igbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igbo_language), [Swahili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swahili_language), [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_language), [Manding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manding_languages), [Fulani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulani_language) and [Yoruba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoruba_language) are spoken by tens of millions of people. Twelve [dialect clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialect_cluster) (which may group up to a hundred linguistic varieties) are spoken by 75 percent, and fifteen by 85 percent, of Africans as a first or additional language.[^o92]

[Niger–Congo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niger–Congo_languages) is the largest phylum of African languages, with more than 500 million speakers (2017); it is dominated by the [Bantu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_languages) branch, spread throughout sub-Saharan Africa in the [Bantu expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bantu_expansion), Bantu speakers accounting for about half of Niger–Congo speakers. Arabic is the most widely spoken single language in Africa by far, with a population of [Arab Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Africa) of the order of 330 million (2017). Other Afroasiatic languages are spoken by of the order of 100 million speakers in Africa (2017). [Nilo-Saharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilo-Saharan_languages) are spoken by of the order of 100 million speakers (2017). [Khoisan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoisan) groups a number of mostly endangered [click languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Click_language), the largest being [Khoekhoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoekhoe_language) with of the order of 300,000 speakers (2016).
