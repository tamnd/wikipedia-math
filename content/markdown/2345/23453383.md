# KPSS test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="KPSS test"}

In [econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390), **Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin (KPSS) tests** are used for testing a [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) that an observable [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) is [stationary](wikipedia://en/329898) around a deterministic trend (i.e. [trend-stationary](wikipedia://en/1512988)) against the alternative of a [unit root](wikipedia://en/2398969).[^o2]

:::

Contrary to most [unit root tests](wikipedia://en/2473482), the presence of a unit root is not the null hypothesis but the alternative. Additionally, in the KPSS test, the absence of a unit root is not a proof of stationarity but, by design, of trend-stationarity. This is an important distinction since it is possible for a time series to be non-stationary, have no [unit root](wikipedia://en/2398969) yet be [trend-stationary](wikipedia://en/1512988). In both unit root and trend-stationary processes, the mean can be growing or decreasing over time; however, in the presence of a shock, trend-stationary processes are mean-reverting (i.e. transitory, the time series will converge again towards the growing mean, which was not affected by the shock) while [unit-root](wikipedia://en/2398969) processes have a permanent impact on the mean (i.e. no convergence over time).[^o3]

Later, [Denis Kwiatkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Kwiatkowski), [Peter C. B. Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_C._B._Phillips), Peter Schmidt and [Yongcheol Shin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongcheol_Shin) (1992) proposed a test of the null hypothesis that an observable series is [trend-stationary](wikipedia://en/1512988) (stationary around a deterministic trend). The series is expressed as the sum of deterministic trend, [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451), and stationary error, and the test is the [Lagrange multiplier test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_multiplier_test) of the hypothesis that the random walk has zero variance. KPSS-type tests are intended to complement [unit root tests](wikipedia://en/2473482), such as the [Dickey–Fuller tests](wikipedia://en/2473516). By testing both the unit root hypothesis and the stationarity hypothesis, one can distinguish series that appear to be stationary, series that appear to have a unit root, and series for which the data (or the tests) are not sufficiently informative to be sure whether they are stationary or integrated.
