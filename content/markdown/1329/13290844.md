# Bicentric polygon

*Image: Equilateral triangle bicentric 001.svg.* An [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285)

*Image: Bicentric kite 001.svg.* A [bicentric](wikipedia://en/23335118) [kite](wikipedia://en/84840)

*Image: Bicentric isosceles trapezoid 001.svg.* A bicentric [isosceles trapezoid](wikipedia://en/511691)

*Image: Pentagon 001.svg.* [A regular](wikipedia://en/333306) [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bicentric polygon"}

In geometry, a **bicentric polygon** is a [tangential](wikipedia://en/35520654) [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) (a polygon all of whose sides are tangent to an inner [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle)) which is also [cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon) — that is, [inscribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribe) in an [outer circle](wikipedia://en/1926432) that passes through each vertex of the polygon. All [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) and all [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) are bicentric. On the other hand, a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) with unequal sides is not bicentric, because no circle can be tangent to all four sides.

:::

## Triangles {#Triangles}

Every triangle is bicentric.[^o3] In a triangle, the radii *r* and *R* of the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle) and [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) respectively are related by the [equation](wikipedia://en/9284)

$$\frac{1}{R-x}+\frac{1}{R+x}=\frac{1}{r}$$

where *x* is the distance between the centers of the circles.[^o4] This is one version of [Euler's triangle formula](wikipedia://en/3338987).

## Bicentric quadrilaterals {#Bicentric_quadrilaterals}

Not all [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278) are bicentric (having both an incircle and a circumcircle). Given two circles (one within the other) with radii *R* and *r* where $R>r$, there exists a convex quadrilateral inscribed in one of them and tangent to the other [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) their radii satisfy

$$\frac{1}{(R-x)^2}+\frac{1}{(R+x)^2}=\frac{1}{r^2}$$

where *x* is the distance between their centers.[^o4][^o6] This condition (and analogous conditions for higher order polygons) is known as [Fuss' theorem](wikipedia://en/23335118#Fuss'_theorem).[^o7]

## Polygons with n > 4 {#Polygons_with_n_>_4}

A complicated general formula is known for any number *n* of sides for the relation among the circumradius *R*, the inradius *r*, and the distance *x* between the circumcenter and the incenter.[^o9] Some of these for specific *n* are:

$$n=5: \quad r(R-x)=(R+x)\sqrt{(R-r+x)(R-r-x)}+(R+x)\sqrt{2R(R-r-x)} ,$$

$$n=6: \quad 3(R^2-x^2)^4=4r^2(R^2+x^2)(R^2-x^2)^2+16r^4x^2R^2 ,$$

$$n=8: \quad 16p^4q^4(p^2-1)(q^2-1)=(p^2+q^2-p^2q^2)^4 ,$$

where $p=\tfrac{R+x}{r}$ and $q=\tfrac{R-x}{r}.$

## Regular polygons {#Regular_polygons}

Every [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) is bicentric.[^o4] In a regular polygon, the incircle and the circumcircle are [concentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric)—that is, they share a common center, which is also the center of the regular polygon, so the distance between the incenter and circumcenter is always zero. The radius of the inscribed circle is the [apothem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothem) (the shortest distance from the center to the boundary of the regular polygon).

For any regular polygon, the relations between the common [edge](wikipedia://en/12158034) length *a*, the radius *r* of the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle), and the radius *R* of the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) are:

$$R=\frac{a}{2\sin \frac{\pi}{n}}=\frac{r}{\cos \frac{\pi}{n}}.$$

For some regular polygons which can be [constructed with compass and ruler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions), we have the following [algebraic formulas](wikipedia://en/4283745) for these relations:

::: {#o11 .table cue=table}

| $n \!\,$ | $R \, \text{and} \, a \!\,$ | $r \, \text{and} \, a \!\,$ | $r\, \text{and} \, R \!\,$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| [3](wikipedia://en/173285) | $R\sqrt{3}=a \!\,$ | $2r = \frac{a}{3}\sqrt{3} \!\,$ | $2r= R \!\,$ |
| [4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) | $R\sqrt{2} = a\!\,$ | $r= \frac{a}{2} \!\,$ | $2r = R\sqrt{2} \!\,$ |
| [5](wikipedia://en/20741014) | $R\sqrt{\frac{5-\sqrt{5}}{2}}=a\!\,$ | $r\left(\sqrt{5}-1\right) = \frac{a}{10}\sqrt{50+10\sqrt{5}} \!\,$ | $r(\sqrt{5}-1) =R \!\,$ |
| [6](wikipedia://en/59733) | $R=a \!\,$ | $\frac{2r}{3}\sqrt{3} = a \!\,$ | $\frac{2r}{3}\sqrt{3} = R \!\,$ |
| [8](wikipedia://en/314575) | $R \sqrt{2+\sqrt{2}} = a\left(\sqrt{2}+1\right) \!\,$ | $r \sqrt{4-2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{a}{2}\sqrt{4+2\sqrt{2}} \!\,$ | $2r \left(\sqrt{2}-1\right) = R\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2}} \!\,$ |
| [10](wikipedia://en/327393) | $(\sqrt{5}-1)R=2a \!\,$ | $2r\sqrt{25-10\sqrt{5}}=5a \!\,$ | $\frac{2r}{5} \sqrt{25-10\sqrt{5}} = \frac{R}{2} \left(\sqrt{5}-1\right) \!\,$ |

:::

## Poncelet's porism {#Poncelet's_porism}

If two circles are the inscribed and circumscribed circles of a particular bicentric *n*-gon, then the same two circles are the inscribed and circumscribed circles of infinitely many bicentric *n*-gons. More precisely, every [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) to the inner of the two circles can be extended to a bicentric *n*-gon by placing vertices on the line at the points where it crosses the outer circle, continuing from each vertex along another tangent line, and continuing in the same way until the resulting [polygonal chain](wikipedia://en/12056032) closes up to an *n*-gon. The fact that it will always do so is implied by [Poncelet's closure theorem](wikipedia://en/2412120), which more generally applies for inscribed and circumscribed [conics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic).[^o20]

Moreover, given a circumcircle and incircle, each diagonal of the variable polygon is tangent to a fixed circle. [^o21]
