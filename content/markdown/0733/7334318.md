# Second-harmonic generation

*Image: Energy level scheme of SHG.png.* Energy level scheme of SHG process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Second-harmonic generation"}

**Second-harmonic generation** (**SHG**), also known as **frequency doubling**, is the lowest-order wave-wave nonlinear interaction that occurs in various systems, including optical, radio, atmospheric, and magnetohydrodynamic systems.[^o2] As a prototype behavior of waves, SHG is widely used, for example, in doubling laser frequencies. SHG was initially discovered as a [nonlinear optical](wikipedia://en/21723) process[^o3] in which two [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) with the same frequency interact with a nonlinear material, are "combined", and generate a new photon with twice the energy of the initial photons (equivalently, twice the [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) and half the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125)), that conserves the [coherence](wikipedia://en/240011) of the excitation. It is a special case of two-photon [sum-frequency generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum-frequency_generation), and more generally of [harmonic generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_generation).

:::

The second-order [nonlinear susceptibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_susceptibility) of a medium characterizes its tendency to cause SHG. Second-harmonic generation, like other even-order nonlinear optical phenomena, is not allowed in media with inversion symmetry (within the leading electric dipole contribution).[^o4] However, effects such as the [Bloch–Siegert shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch–Siegert_shift), which occurs when two-level systems are driven at Rabi frequencies comparable to their transition frequencies, can give rise to second-harmonic generation in centrosymmetric systems.[^o5][^o6] In addition, in [non-centrosymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-centrosymmetric) [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) belonging to [crystallographic point group](wikipedia://en/663786) 432, SHG is not possible,[^o7] and under [Kleinman's conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleinman_symmetry), SHG in the 422 and 622 point groups should vanish,[^o8] although some exceptions exist.[^o9]

In some cases, almost 100% of the light energy can be converted to the second-harmonic frequency. These cases typically involve intense pulsed laser beams passing through large crystals and careful alignment to obtain [phase matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_matching). In other cases, like [second-harmonic imaging microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-harmonic_imaging_microscopy), only a tiny fraction of the light energy is converted to the second harmonic, but this light can nevertheless be detected with the help of [optical filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_filter).

## History {#History}

*Image: Electron asymmetric motion animation.gif.* An electron (purple) is being pushed side-to-side by a [sinusoidally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinusoid) oscillating force, i.e. the light's electric field. But because the electron is in an [anharmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anharmonic) [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) environment (black curve), the electron motion is *not* sinusoidal. The three arrows show the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of the motion: The blue arrow corresponds to ordinary (linear) [susceptibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_susceptibility), the green arrow corresponds to second-harmonic generation, and the red arrow corresponds to [optical rectification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_rectification).

Second-harmonic generation was first demonstrated by [Peter Franken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Franken), A. E. Hill, C. W. Peters, and [G. Weinreich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Weinreich) at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan), Ann Arbor, in 1961.[^o11] The demonstration was made possible by the invention of the [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser), which created the required high-intensity coherent light. They focused a [ruby laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_laser) with a wavelength of 694 nm into a quartz sample. They sent the output light through a [spectrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrometer), recording the spectrum on [photographic paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photographic_paper), which indicated the production of light at 347 nm. Famously, when published in the journal *Physical Review Letters*,[^o11] the copy editor mistook the dim spot (at 347 nm) on the photographic paper as a speck of dirt and removed it from the publication.[^o12] The formulation of SHG was initially described by [N. Bloembergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaas_Bloembergen) and [P. S. Pershan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Pershan) at Harvard in 1962.[^o13] In their extensive evaluation of [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) at the planar interface between a linear and nonlinear medium, several rules for the interaction of light in non-linear media were elucidated.

## Types in crystals {#Types_in_crystals}

### Critical phase-matching {#Critical_phase-matching}

*Image: TypeX SHG.png.* Different types of second-harmonic generation phase-matching of a coherent light for strong conversion. The case of negative crystals ($n_o>n_e$) is considered, invert indices if positive crystal ($n_e>n_o$).

Second-harmonic generation occurs in three types for critical phase-matching,[^o16] denoted 0, I and II. In *Type 0 SHG,* two photons having [extraordinary polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraordinary_polarization) with respect to the crystal will combine to form a single photon with double the frequency/energy and extraordinary polarization. In *Type I SHG,* two photons having [ordinary polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_polarization) with respect to the crystal will combine to form one photon with double the frequency and extraordinary polarization. In *Type II SHG*, two photons having orthogonal polarizations will combine to form one photon with double the frequency and ordinary polarization. For a given crystal orientation, only one of these types of SHG occurs. In general, to utilize *Type 0* interactions, a [quasi-phase-matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-phase-matching) crystal type is required, such as [periodically poled lithium niobate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodically_poled_lithium_niobate) (PPLN).

### Non-critical phase-matching {#Non-critical_phase-matching}

Since phase-matching processes simply match the optical indices at ω and 2ω, they can also be done by controlling temperature in birefringent crystals where the optical index is temperature-dependent. For instance, [LBO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_triborate) presents a perfect phase-matching at 25 °C for a SHG excited at 1200 or 1400 nm,[^o18] but needs to be elevated at 200 °C for SHG with the usual laser line of 1064 nm. It is called "non-critical" because it does not depend on the crystal orientation, unlike usual phase-matching.

## Surface second-harmonic generation {#Surface_second-harmonic_generation}

*Image: Second Harmonic Generation.svg.* Diagram of the second-harmonic generation process

Since media with [inversion symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_symmetry) are forbidden from generating second-harmonic light via the leading-order electric dipole contribution (unlike [third harmonic generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_generation#Third-harmonic_generation_%28THG%29)), surfaces and interfaces make interesting subjects for study with SHG. In fact, second-harmonic generation and sum frequency generation discriminate against signals from the bulk, implicitly labeling them as surface specific techniques. In 1982, [T. F. Heinz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Heinz) and Y. R. Shen explicitly demonstrated for the first time that SHG could be used as a spectroscopic technique to probe molecular monolayers adsorbed to surfaces.[^o20] Heinz and Shen adsorbed monolayers of laser dye [rhodamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodamine) to a planar [fused silica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_silica) surface; the coated surface was then pumped by a nanosecond ultra-fast laser. SH light with characteristic spectra of the adsorbed molecule and its electronic transitions were measured as reflection from the surface and demonstrated a quadratic power dependence on the pump laser power.

In SHG surface spectroscopy, one focuses on measuring twice the incident frequency 2*ω* given an incoming electric field $E(\omega)$ in order to reveal information about a surface. Simply (for a more in-depth derivation see below), the induced second-harmonic dipole per unit volume, $P^{(2)}(2\omega)$, can be written as

$$E(2\omega) \propto P^{(2)}(2\omega) = \chi^{(2)} E(\omega) E(\omega)$$

where $\chi^{(2)}$ is known as the nonlinear susceptibility tensor and is a characteristic to the materials at the interface of study.[^o21] The generated $E(2\omega)$ and corresponding $\chi^{(2)}$ have been shown to reveal information about the orientation of molecules at a surface/interface, the interfacial analytical chemistry of surfaces, and chemical reactions at interfaces. SHG surface spectroscopy is also used extensively in the electrochemical characterization of materials, since the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) at the interface between the electrode and electrolyte introduces an additional term that affects the interfacial inversion symmetry.

### From planar surfaces {#From_planar_surfaces}

*Image: SHG phenol air-water.png.* A depiction of the second-harmonic generation setup for measuring the orientation of phenol at the air-water interface.

Early experiments in the field demonstrated second-harmonic generation from metal surfaces.[^o23] Eventually, SHG was used to probe the air-water interface, allowing for detailed information about molecular orientation and ordering at one of the most ubiquitous of surfaces.[^o24] It can be shown that the specific elements of $\chi^{(2)}$:

$$\begin{aligned} \chi^{(2)}_{zzz} &= N_s \left\langle \cos^3(\theta) \right\rangle \alpha^{(2)}_{zzz} \\ \chi^{(2)}_{xzx} &= \frac{1}{2} N_s \left\langle \cos(\theta) \sin^2(\theta) \right\rangle \alpha^{(2)}_{zzz} \end{aligned}$$

where *N*<sub>*s*</sub> is the adsorbate density, *θ* is the angle that the molecular axis *z* makes with the surface normal *Z*, and $\alpha^{(2)}_{zzz}$ is the dominating element of the nonlinear polarizability of a molecule at an interface, allow one to determine *θ*, given laboratory coordinates (*x*, *y*, *z*).[^o25] Using an interference SHG method to determine these elements of *χ*(2), the first molecular orientation measurement showed that the hydroxyl group of phenol pointed downwards into the water at the air-water interface (as expected due to the potential of hydroxyl groups to form hydrogen bonds). Additionally SHG at planar surfaces has revealed differences in *pK*<sup>*a*</sup> and rotational motions of molecules at interfaces.

### From non-planar surfaces {#From_non-planar_surfaces}

*Image: SHG from small sphere.png.* Cartoon depicting ordered molecules at a small spherical surface. An ultrafast pump laser pumps light with frequency ω which generates light at 2ω from the locally non-centrosymmetric media.

Second-harmonic light can also be generated from surfaces that are "locally" planar, but may have inversion symmetry (centrosymmetric) on a larger scale. Specifically, recent theory has demonstrated that SHG from small spherical particles (micro- and nanometer scale) is allowed by proper treatment of [Rayleigh scattering](wikipedia://en/26176) (scattering without a change in frequency from absorbed to emitted waves).[^o27] At the surface of a small sphere, inversion symmetry is broken, allowing for SHG and other even order harmonics to occur.

For a colloidal system of microparticles at relatively low concentrations, the total SH signal $I^\text{total}_{2\omega}$, is given by:

$$I^\text{total}_{2\omega} \propto \sum\limits_{j=1}^n \left(E^{2\omega}_j\right)^2 = n\left(E^{2\omega}\right)^2 = nI_{2\omega}$$

where $E^{2\omega}_j$ is the SH electric field generated by the *j*th particle, and *n* is the density of particles.[^o28] The SH light generated from each particle is [coherent](wikipedia://en/240011), but adds incoherently to the SH light generated by others (as long as density is low enough). Thus, SH light is only generated from the interfaces of the spheres and their environment and is independent of particle-particle interactions. It has also been shown that the second-harmonic electric field $E(2\omega)$ scales with the radius of the particle cubed, a<sup>3</sup>.

Besides spheres, other small particles like rods have been studied similarly by SHG.[^o29] Both immobilized and colloidal systems of small particles can be investigated. Recent experiments using second-harmonic generation of non-planar systems include transport kinetics across living cell membranes[^o30] and demonstrations of SHG in complex [nanomaterials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanomaterials).[^o31]

## Radiation pattern {#Radiation_pattern}

*Image: SHG radiation pattern from interfaces or not.png.* SHG radiation pattern excited with a Gaussian beam, in a homogeneous medium (A), or at an interface between opposite polarities that is parallel to the propagation (B). Only the forward SHG is represented.

The SHG [radiation pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_pattern) generated by an exciting Gaussian beam also has a (homogeneous) 2D Gaussian profile if the nonlinear medium being excited is homogeneous (A). However, if the exciting beam is positioned at an interface between opposite polarities (± boundary, *B*) that is parallel to the beam propagation (see figure), the SHG will be split into two lobes whose amplitudes have opposite sign, i.e. are $\pi$ phase-shifted. [^o33]

These boundaries can be found in the [sarcomeres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcomere) of [muscles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle) (protein = [myosin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myosin)), for instance. Note that we have considered here only the forward generation.

Additionally, the SHG [phase-matching](wikipedia://en/21723#Phase_matching) can result in $\vec k_{2\omega} = -2\vec k_\omega$: some SHG is also emitted in backward (epi direction). When the [phase-matching](wikipedia://en/21723#Phase_matching) is not fulfilled, as in [biological tissues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_tissue), the backward signal comes from a sufficiently high phase-mismatch which allow a small backward contribution to compensate for it.[^o34] Unlike fluorescence, the spatial coherence of the process constrain it to emit only in those two directions, where the coherence length in the backwards direction is always much smaller than in the forwards, meaning there is always more forward than backward SHG signal.[^o35]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Green lasers {#Green_lasers}

Second-harmonic generation is used by the laser industry to make green 532 nm lasers from a 1064 nm source. The 1064 nm light is fed through a bulk nonlinear crystal (typically made of [KDP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_dihydrogen_phosphate) or [KTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_titanyl_phosphate)). In high-quality diode lasers the crystal is coated on the output side with an infrared filter to prevent leakage of intense 1064 nm or 808 nm infrared light into the beam. Both of these wavelengths are invisible and do not trigger the defensive "blink-reflex" reaction in the eye and can therefore be a special hazard to human eyes. Furthermore, some [laser safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_safety) eyewear intended for argon or other green lasers may filter out the green component (giving a false sense of safety), but transmit the infrared. Nevertheless, some "green [laser pointer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_pointer)" products have become available on the market which omit the expensive infrared filter, often without warning.[^o39]

### Ultra-short pulse measurement {#Ultra-short_pulse_measurement}

Second-harmonic generation is also used for measuring ultra-short pulse widths with [autocorrelators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocorrelator). Characterizing an [ultrashort pulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrashort_pulse) (like measuring its temporal width) cannot be done directly with electronics only, as the time-scale is below 1ps ($10^{-12}$sec). One therefore needs to use the pulse itself, often through an autocorrelation function. Since SHG has the advantage of mixing two input fields to generate the harmonic field, it is a good candidate for performing such pulse measurements. [Optical autocorrelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_autocorrelation), in its [intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_autocorrelation#Intensity_autocorrelation) or fringe-resolved ([interferometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_autocorrelation#Interferometric_autocorrelation)) versions, uses SHG,[^o41] unlike linear [field autocorrelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_autocorrelation#Field_autocorrelation). Also, most versions of [FROG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-resolved_optical_gating) (called SHG-FROG) use SHG to mix the delayed fields.[^o42]

### Second-harmonic generation microscopy {#Second-harmonic_generation_microscopy}

In biological and medical science, the effect of second-harmonic generation is used for high-resolution optical microscopy. Because of the non-zero second-harmonic coefficient, only non-centrosymmetric structures are capable of emitting SHG light. One such structure is collagen, which is found in most load-bearing tissues. Using a short-pulse laser such as a [femtosecond laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femtosecond_laser) and a set of appropriate filters the excitation light can be easily separated from the emitted, frequency-doubled SHG signal. This allows for very high axial and lateral resolution comparable to that of [confocal microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confocal_microscopy) without having to use pinholes. SHG microscopy has been used for studies of the [cornea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornea)[^o44] and [lamina cribrosa sclerae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamina_cribrosa_sclerae),[^o45] both of which consist primarily of collagen. Second-harmonic generation can be produced by several non-centrosymmetric organic dyes; however, most of the organic dyes also generate collateral fluorescence along with second-harmonic generation signals.[^o46] Until now, only two classes of organic dyes have been shown which do not produce any collateral fluorescence and works purely on second-harmonic generation.[^o46][^o47] Recently, using two-photon excited fluorescence and second-harmonic generation-based microscopy, a group of Oxford University researchers showed that organic porphyrin-type molecules can have different transition dipole moments for two-photon fluorescence and second-harmonic generation,[^o48] which are otherwise thought to occur from the same transition dipole moment.[^o49]

Second-harmonic generation microscopy is also used in material science, for instance to characterize nanostructured materials.[^o50]

### Characterization of crystalline materials {#Characterization_of_crystalline_materials}

Second harmonic generation is also relevant to characterize organic or inorganic crystals[^o52] since is one of the most discriminant and rapid technique to detect [non-centrosymmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-centrosymmetric).[^o53] In addition, this technique can be used on [single crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_crystal) as well as on powdered samples. One should recall that SHG is only possible (from the bulk) in non-centrosymmetric (NC) [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal). The part of non-centroysmmetric crystals in nature is much lower than centrosymmetric crystals (circa 22% of the Cambridge structural database[^o54]), but the frequency of NC crystals increases by a lot in pharmaceutical, biological and electronic fields because of the particular properties of these crystals ([piezoelectricity](wikipedia://en/24975), [pyroelectricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyroelectricity), polar phases, [chirality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_%28chemistry%29), etc.).

In 1968[^o55] (7 years after the first experimental evidence of SHG on single crystal[^o11]), Kurtz and Perry started to develop a SHG analyzer to rapidly detect the presence or not of inversion center in powdered crystalline samples. The detection of a SHG signal has been shown to be reliable and sensitive test for the detection of crystalline non-centrosymmetry with the confidence level higher than 99%. It is a relevant tool to resolve space group ambiguities that can arise from Friedel's law in single-crystal X-ray diffraction.[^o56] Furthermore, the method is referenced in the International Tables for Crystallography and is described as a "powerful method of testing crystalline materials for the absence of a symmetry center."[^o57]

One possible application is also to rapidly discriminate chiral phases such as [conglomerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racemic_mixture) that are of particular interest for pharmaceutical industries.[^o58] It could also be used as a technique to probe the structural purity of material if one of the impurities is NC reaching a detection threshold as low as 1 ppm[^o59] using Kurtz–Perry apparatus up to one part in 10 billion by volume using a SHG microscope.[^o60]

Due to the high sensitivity of the technique, it can be a helpful tool in the accurate determination of [phase diagram](wikipedia://en/56398)[^o61] and can also be used to monitor phase transitions ([polymorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_%28materials_science%29) transition, dehydration, ...) when at least one of the phases is NC.[^o62][^o63][^o64]

## Theoretical derivation (plane wave) {#Theoretical_derivation_(plane_wave)}

### At low conversion {#At_low_conversion}

The simplest case for analysis of second-harmonic generation is a [plane wave](wikipedia://en/41559) of amplitude *E*(*ω*) traveling in a nonlinear medium in the direction of its [*k* vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_vector). A polarization is generated at the second-harmonic frequency:[^o67]

$$P(2\omega) = \varepsilon_0 \chi^{(2)} E^2(\omega) = 2\varepsilon_0 d_\text{eff}(2\omega; \omega, \omega) E^2(\omega),\,$$

where $d_\text{eff}$ is the effective nonlinear optical coefficient which is dependent on specific components of $\chi^{(2)}$ that are involved in this particular interaction. The wave equation at 2ω (assuming negligible loss and asserting the [slowly varying envelope approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slowly_varying_envelope_approximation)) is

$$\frac{\partial E(2\omega)}{\partial z} = -\frac{i\omega}{n_{2\omega}c} d_\text{eff} E^2(\omega)e^{i \, \Delta k \, z}$$

where $\Delta k = k(2\omega) - 2k(\omega)$.

At low conversion efficiency (*E*(2*ω*) ≪ *E*(*ω*)) the amplitude $E(\omega)$ remains essentially constant over the interaction length, $\ell$. Then, with the boundary condition $E(2\omega, z = 0) = 0$ we obtain

$$E(2\omega, z = \ell) = -\frac{i\omega d_\text{eff}}{n_{2\omega}c} E^2(\omega) \int_0^\ell e^{i\,\Delta k \, z} \, dz = -\frac{i\omega d_\text{eff}}{n_{2\omega}c} E^2(\omega) \ell\, \frac{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta k \, \ell\right)}{\frac{1}{2}\,\Delta k\, \ell} e^{\frac{i}{2} \, \Delta k\, \ell}$$

In terms of the optical intensity, $I = n/2\sqrt{\varepsilon_0/\mu_0}|E|^2$, this is,

$$I(2\omega,\ell) = \frac{2\omega^2 d^2_\text{eff} \ell^2}{n_{2\omega} n_\omega^2 c^3 \varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2} \,\Delta k \, \ell\right)}{\frac{1}{2} \,\Delta k\, \ell}\right)^2 I^2(\omega)$$

This intensity is maximized for the [phase-matched](wikipedia://en/21723#Phase_matching) condition Δ*k* = 0. If the process is not phase matched, the driving polarization at *ω* goes in and out of phase with generated wave *E*(2*ω*) and conversion oscillates as sin(Δ*kℓ*/2). The coherence length is defined as $\ell_c = \frac{\pi}{\Delta k}$. It does not pay to use a nonlinear crystal much longer than the coherence length. ([Periodic poling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_poling) and [quasi-phase-matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-phase-matching) provide another approach to this problem.)

### With depletion {#With_depletion}

*Image: Frequency doubling with perfect pase matching.gif.* Diagram of second-harmonic generation with perfect phase matching $\Delta k = 0$.

*Image: Frequency doubling with imperfect phase matching.gif.* Diagram of second-harmonic generation with an imperfect phase matching $\Delta k \neq 0$. In this case energy flows forth and back from the pump to the frequency doubled signal, and having a thick crystal can lead to a smaller amount of SHG produced.

When the conversion to 2nd harmonic becomes significant it becomes necessary to include depletion of the fundamental. The energy conversion states that all the involved fields verify the [Manley–Rowe relations](wikipedia://en/30246751). One then has the coupled equations:[^o69]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial E(2\omega)}{\partial z} &= -\frac{i\omega}{n_{2\omega}c} d_\text{eff} E^2(\omega) e^{i\,\Delta k \, z}, \\[5pt] \frac{\partial E(\omega)}{\partial z} &= -\frac{i\omega}{n_{\omega}c} d_\text{eff}^* E(2\omega) E^*(\omega)e^{-i\,\Delta k \, z}, \end{aligned}$$

where $*$ denotes the complex conjugate. For simplicity, assume phase matched generation ($\Delta k = 0$). Then, energy conservation requires that

$$n_{2\omega} \left[E^*(2\omega)\frac{\partial E(2\omega)}{\partial z} + \text{c.c.} \right] = -n_\omega \left[E(\omega)\frac{\partial E^*(\omega)}{\partial z} + \text{c.c.} \right]$$

where $\text{c.c.}$ is the complex conjugate of the other term, or

$$n_{2\omega} \left|E(2\omega)\right|^2 + n_\omega|E(\omega)|^2 = n_{2\omega} E_0^2.$$

*Image: Phase-matched SHG with source depletion.png.* Phase-matched SHG with source depletion (blue), and corresponding excitation (orange). *L* is the interaction length (*ℓ* in the text).

Now we solve the equations with the premise

$$\begin{aligned} E(\omega) &= \left|E(\omega)\right| e^{i\varphi(\omega)} \\ E(2\omega) &= \left|E(2\omega)\right| e^{i\varphi(2\omega)} \end{aligned}$$

and obtain

$$\frac{d\left|E(2\omega)\right|}{dz} = - \frac{i\omega d_\text{eff}}{n_\omega c}\left[E_0^2 - \left|E(2\omega)\right|^2\right] e^{2i\varphi(\omega) - i\varphi(2\omega)}$$

which leads to

$$\int_0^{\left|E(2\omega)\right|\ell} \frac{d\left|E(2\omega)\right|}{E_0^2 - \left|E(2\omega)\right|^2} = -\int_0^\ell \frac{i\omega d_\text{eff}}{n_\omega c} e^{2i\varphi(\omega) - i\varphi(2\omega)} \, dz.$$

Using

$$\int \frac{dx}{a^2 - x^2} = \frac{1}{a}\tanh^{-1} \frac{x}{a}$$

we get

$$\left|E(2\omega)\right|_{z=\ell} = E_0\tanh \left(\frac{-iE_0\ell\omega d_\text{eff}}{n_\omega c} e^{2i\varphi(\omega) - i\varphi(2\omega)} \right).$$

If we assume a real $d_\text{eff}$, the relative phases for real harmonic growth must be such that $e^{2i\varphi(\omega) - i\varphi(2\omega)} = i$. Then

$$I(2\omega, \ell) = I(\omega, 0) \tanh^2\left(\frac{E_0\omega d_\text{eff} \ell}{n_\omega c}\right)$$

or

$$I(2\omega, \ell) = I(\omega,0)\tanh^2 (\Gamma \ell),$$

where $\Gamma = \omega d_\text{eff} E_0/nc$. From $I(2\omega, \ell) + I(\omega,\ell) = I(\omega, 0)$, it also follows that

$$I(\omega, \ell) = I(\omega, 0) \operatorname{sech}^2(\Gamma \ell).$$

## Theoretical expression with Gaussian beams {#Theoretical_expression_with_Gaussian_beams}

The excitation wave is assumed to be a [Gaussian beam](wikipedia://en/41206), of amplitude: $A_1 = A_0 \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{z_R}{iq(z)} \exp \left( i k_1 \frac{x^2+y^2}{2q(z)} \right)$

with $q(z) = z-iz_R$, $z$ the direction of propagation, $z_R$ the Rayleigh range, ${k}_{1}$ the [wave vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_vector).

Each wave verifies the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691)

$$\left[ \frac{\partial} {\partial x^2} +\frac\partial {\partial y^2} +2i k_1 \frac\partial{\partial z} \right] A(x,y,z;k_1) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{for the fundamental}, \\ \frac{\omega_n^2 c^2}{\chi^{(n)}} A(x,y,z;k_1) e^{i\,\Delta k\, z} & \text{for } n\text{-th harmonic}. \end{cases}$$

where $\Delta k = k_n - k_1$.

### With phase-matching {#With_phase-matching}

It can be shown that: $A_n =-i\frac{\omega_n}{2n_{n\omega}c}{\left( A_0 \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \right)^n} z_R^2 \int_{-\infty }^z \frac{\chi^{(n)}(u)}{q(u)^2} \, du \exp \left( ik_n \frac{x^2+y^2}{2q(z)} \right)$

(a [Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian)), is a solution of the equation (*n* = 2 for SHG).

### No phase-matching {#No_phase-matching}

*Image: Intensity SHG, phase-matched or not.png.* Intensity SHG, phase-matched or not. The medium width is supposed to be much higher than *z*, the Rayleigh range at 20 μm, excitation wavelength of 0.8 μm, and optical index of 2.2.

A non-perfect [phase-matching](wikipedia://en/21723#Phase_matching) is a more realistic condition in practice, especially in biological samples. The paraxial approximation is however supposed still valid: $k_n = nk_1$, and in the harmonic expression, $\chi^{(n)}(z)$ is now $\chi^{(n)}(z)e^{i\,\Delta k \, z}$.

In the special case of SHG (*n* = 2), in a medium of length *L* and a focus position $z_0$, the intensity writes:[^o73]

$$I_{2\omega} = \frac{2\omega^2 d_{\text{eff}}^2}{\pi c^3 \varepsilon_0 w_0^2 n_{2\omega} n_\omega^2} I_\omega^2 \left( \int_{z_0}^{z_0+L} \frac{e^{i\,\Delta k \, z}}{1+iz/z_R} \right )^2 \, dz.$$

where $c$ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) in [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum), $\varepsilon_0$ the vacuum [permittivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permittivity), $n_{n\omega}$ the [optical index](wikipedia://en/25880) of the medium at $n\omega$ and $w_0$ the [waist](wikipedia://en/41206#Beam_waist) size of excitation.

Thus, the SHG intensity quickly decays in the bulk ($0 < z_0 < L$), due to the [Gouy phase-shift](wikipedia://en/41206#Gouy_phase) of the Gaussian beam.

In conformity with experiments, the SHG signal vanishes in the bulk (if the medium thickness is too large), and the SHG must be generated at the surface of the material: the conversion therefore does not strictly scales with the square of the number of scatterers, contrary to what the plane wave model indicates. Interestingly, the signal also vanishes in bulk for [higher orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_generation), like THG.

## Materials used {#Materials_used}

Materials capable of generating a second harmonic are crystals without inversion symmetry, except crystals with [point group](wikipedia://en/663786) 432. This eliminates water and glass.[^o67]

Notably, filamentous biological proteins with a cylindrical symmetric such as [collagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collagen), [tubulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tubulin) or [myosin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myosin), but also certain [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrates) (such as [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch) or [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose)) are also quite good converters of SHG (fundamental in the near infrared).[^o75]

Examples of [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) used with for SHG conversion:

- Fundamental excitation at 600–1500 nm:[^o76] BiBO (BiB<sub>3</sub>O<sub>6</sub>)
- Fundamental excitation at 570–4000 nm:[^o77] [lithium iodate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_iodate) LiIO<sub>3</sub>.
- Fundamental excitation at 800–1100 nm, often 860 or 980 nm:[^o78] [potassium niobate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_niobate) KNbO<sub>3</sub>.
- Fundamental excitation at 410–2000 nm: [BBO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_borate) (β-BaB<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>).[^o79]
- Fundamental excitation at 984–3400 nm: [KTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_titanyl_phosphate) (KTiOPO<sub>4</sub>) or KTA.[^o80]
- Fundamental excitation at ~1000–2000 nm: periodically poled crystals, like [PPLN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_niobate#Periodically_poled_lithium_niobate_%28PPLN%29).[^o81]

For common types of [diode-pumped solid state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diode-pumped_solid_state) lasers with input wavelengths:

- 1064 nm: [monopotassium phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopotassium_phosphate) KDP (KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>), [lithium triborate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_triborate) (LiB<sub>3</sub>O<sub>5</sub>), CsLiB<sub>6</sub>O<sub>10</sub> and [barium borate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_borate) BBO(β-BaB<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>).
- 1319 nm: KNbO<sub>3</sub>, [BBO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_borate) (β-BaB<sub>2</sub>O<sub>4</sub>), [monopotassium phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopotassium_phosphate) KDP (KH<sub>2</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>), LiIO<sub>3</sub>, LiNbO<sub>3</sub>, and [potassium titanyl phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_titanyl_phosphate) KTP (KTiOPO<sub>4</sub>).

Perovskite materials have also recently emerged as promising second-harmonic-generation media, particularly in non-centrosymmetric hybrid and metal-free perovskites, where strong second-order optical nonlinearity and direct SHG have been reported in crystals, low-dimensional ferroelectric perovskites, and optical-fiber core geometries.[^o82][^o83]
