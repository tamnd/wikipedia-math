# Weapons of Math Destruction

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Weapons of Math Destruction"}

***Weapons of Math Destruction*** is a 2016 American book about the societal impact of [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775), written by [Cathy O'Neil](wikipedia://en/40066499). It explores how some [big data](wikipedia://en/27051151) algorithms are increasingly used in ways that reinforce preexisting [inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_inequality). The book was widely reviewed.[^o3] It was longlisted for the 2016 [National Book Award for Nonfiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Book_Award_for_Nonfiction)[^o4][^o5][^o6] and won the [Euler Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Book_Prize).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

O'Neil, a mathematician, analyses how the use of [big data](wikipedia://en/27051151) and [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) in a variety of fields, including insurance, advertising, education, and policing, can lead to decisions that harm the poor, reinforce [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism), and amplify inequality. According to [National Book Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Book_Foundation):[^o4]

She posits that these problematic mathematical tools share three key features: they are opaque, unregulated, and difficult to contest. They are also scalable, thereby amplifying any inherent biases to affect increasingly larger populations. WMDs, or Weapons of Math Destruction, are mathematical algorithms that supposedly take human traits and quantify them, resulting in damaging effects and the perpetuation of bias against certain groups of people.

## Reception {#Reception}

The book received widespread praise for elucidating the consequences of reliance on big data models for structuring [socioeconomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socioeconomic) resources. [Clay Shirky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_Shirky) from [The New York Times Book Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times_Book_Review) said "O'Neil does a masterly job explaining the pervasiveness and risks of the algorithms that regulate our lives," while pointing out that "the section on solutions is weaker than the illustration of the problem".[^o9] [Kirkus Reviews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirkus_Reviews) praised the book for being "an unusually lucid and readable" discussion of a technical subject.[^o10]

In 2019, the book won the [Euler Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Book_Prize) of the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772).[^o11]
