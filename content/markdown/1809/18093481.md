# Strong law of small numbers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strong law of small numbers"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the "**strong law of small numbers**" is the [humorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humor) law that proclaims, in the words of [Richard K. Guy](wikipedia://en/816308) (1980, 1988):[^o2][^o3] There aren't enough small numbers to meet the many demands made of them. In other words, any given small number appears in far more contexts than may seem reasonable, leading to many apparently surprising coincidences in mathematics, simply because small numbers appear so often and yet are so few. Guy's 1988 paper gives numerous examples in support of this thesis and earned him the AMA [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award) in 1989.[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Second strong law of small numbers"}

## Second strong law of small numbers {#Second_strong_law_of_small_numbers}

*Image: circle\_division\_by\_chords.svg.* Guy gives [Moser's circle problem](wikipedia://en/399730) as an example. The number of points (*n*), chords (*c*) and regions (*r<sub>G</sub>*). The first five terms for the number of regions follow a simple sequence, broken by the sixth term.

Guy also formulated a **second strong law of small numbers**: When two numbers look equal, it ain't necessarily so![^o7]

Guy explains this latter law by the way of examples: he cites numerous sequences for which observing the first few members may lead to a wrong guess about the generating formula or law for the sequence. Many of the examples are the observations of other mathematicians.[^o7]

One example Guy gives is the conjecture that $2^{p} - 1$ is prime—in fact, a [Mersenne prime](wikipedia://en/18908)—when $p$ is prime; but this conjecture, while true for $p$ = 2, 3, 5 and 7, fails for $p$ = 11, 23, 29, ....

Another relates to the [prime number race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_race): primes congruent to 3 modulo 4 appear to be more numerous than those congruent to 1; however this is false, and first ceases being true at 26861.

A geometric example concerns [Moser's circle problem](wikipedia://en/399730) (pictured), which appears to have the solution of $2^{n-1}$ for $n$ points, but this pattern breaks at and above $n = 6$.
