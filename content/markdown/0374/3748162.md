# Multiple-conclusion logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multiple-conclusion logic"}

A **multiple-conclusion logic** is one in which [logical consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) is a [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558), $\vdash$, between two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of [sentences](wikipedia://en/2129527) (or [propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition_%28mathematics%29)). $\Gamma \vdash \Delta$ is typically interpreted as meaning that whenever each element of $\Gamma$ is true, some element of $\Delta$ is true; and whenever each element of $\Delta$ is false, some element of $\Gamma$ is false. Such a reading is related to [Gerhard Gentzen](wikipedia://en/46027)'s interpretation of the [multiple-succedent sequent calculus LK](wikipedia://en/252329#The_system_LK), though Gentzen interprets his sequents $\Gamma\vdash\Delta$ as formulae $(\bigwedge\Gamma)\supset(\bigvee\Delta)$.[^o2]

:::

This form of logic was developed in the 1970s by D. J. Shoesmith and [Timothy Smiley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Smiley)[^o3] but has not been widely adopted.

Some [logicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician) (for example, [Greg Restall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Restall)[^o4]) favor a multiple-conclusion consequence relation over the more traditional single-conclusion relation on the grounds that the latter is [asymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymmetry) (in the informal, non-mathematical sense) and favors truth over falsity (or assertion over denial).
