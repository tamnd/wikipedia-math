# Univalent function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Univalent function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the branch of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) on an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) is called **univalent** if it is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196).[^o2][^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The function $f \colon z \mapsto 2z + z^2$ is univalent in the open unit disc, as $f(z) = f(w)$ implies that $f(z) - f(w) = (z-w)(z+w+2) = 0$. As the second factor is non-zero in the open unit disc, $z = w$ so $f$ is injective.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

One can prove that if $G$ and $\Omega$ are two open [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) sets in the complex plane, and

$$f: G \to \Omega$$

is a univalent function such that $f(G) = \Omega$ (that is, $f$ is [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873)), then the derivative of $f$ is never zero, $f$ is [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible), and its inverse $f^{-1}$ is also holomorphic. More, one has by the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113)

$$(f^{-1})'(f(z)) = \frac{1}{f'(z)}$$

for all $z$ in $G.$

:::

## Comparison with real functions {#Comparison_with_real_functions}

For [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478), unlike for complex analytic (that is, holomorphic) functions, these statements fail to hold. For example, consider the function

$$f: (-1, 1) \to (-1, 1) \,$$

given by $f(x)=x^3$. This function is clearly injective, but its derivative is 0 at $x=0$, and its inverse is not analytic, or even differentiable, on the whole interval $(-1,1)$. Consequently, if we enlarge the domain to an open subset $G$ of the complex plane, it must fail to be injective; and this is the case, since (for example) $f(\varepsilon \omega) = f(\varepsilon)$ (where $\omega$ is a [primitive cube root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) and $\varepsilon$ is a positive real number smaller than the radius of $G$ as a neighbourhood of $0$).
