# Kleiber's law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kleiber's law"}

*Image: Kleiber1947.svg.* Kleiber's plot comparing body size to metabolic rate for a variety of species.[^o2]

**Kleiber's law**, named after [Max Kleiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Kleiber) for his biological research in the early 1930s, states that, in the vast majority of cases, the [basal metabolic rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basal_metabolic_rate) scales to the $\tfrac{3}{4}$ power of the animal's mass.[^o3]

:::

More precisely: using *w* to denote the mass of the animal in kilograms, then BMR ≈ 70*w*<sup>3/4</sup> kilocalories per day, or BMR ≈ 3.4*w*<sup>3/4</sup> watts.[^o4]

Thus, over the same time span, a cat having a mass 100 times that of a mouse will consume only about 32 times the energy the mouse uses.

It is presently unclear if the value of the exponent in Kleiber's law is correct, in part because the law currently lacks a single theoretical explanation that is entirely satisfactory.

More recently, Kleiber's law has also been shown to apply in [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plants),[^o5] suggesting that Kleiber's observation is much more general.

::: {#o7 .statement cue=named-heading name="Proposed explanations for the law"}

## Proposed explanations for the law {#Proposed_explanations_for_the_law}

Kleiber's law, like many other biological [allometric laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allometric_law), is a consequence of the [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and/or [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) of [circulatory systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_system) in biology.[^o8] Max Kleiber first discovered the law when analyzing a large number of independent studies on respiration within individual species.[^o3] Kleiber expected to find an exponent of $\tfrac{2}{3}$ (for reasons explained below), and was confounded by the discovery of a $\tfrac{3}{4}$ exponent.

::: {#o10 .statement cue=named-heading name="Historical context and the scaling surface law"}

### Historical context and the $\tfrac{2}{3}$ scaling surface law {#Historical_context_and_the_scaling_surface_law}

Before Kleiber's observation of the 3/4 power scaling, a 2/3 power scaling was largely anticipated based on the "surface law",[^o11] which states that the basal metabolism of animals differing in size is nearly proportional to their respective body surfaces. This surface law reasoning originated from simple geometrical considerations. As organisms increase in size, their volume (and thus mass) increases at a much faster rate than their surface area. Explanations for $\tfrac{2}{3}$-scaling tend to assume that metabolic rates scale to avoid [heat exhaustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_exhaustion). Because bodies lose heat passively via their surface but produce heat metabolically throughout their mass, the metabolic rate must scale in such a way as to counteract the [square–cube law](wikipedia://en/1730659). Because many physiological processes, like heat loss and nutrient uptake, were believed to be dependent on the surface area of an organism, it was hypothesized that metabolic rate would scale with the 2/3 power of body mass.[^o12] Rubner (1883) first demonstrated the law in accurate respiration trials on dogs.[^o13]

:::

### Kleiber's contribution {#Kleiber's_contribution}

Max Kleiber challenged this notion in the early 1930s. Through extensive research on various animals' metabolic rates, he found that a 3/4 power scaling provided a better fit to the empirical data than the 2/3 power.[^o3] His findings provided the groundwork for understanding allometric scaling laws in biology, leading to the formulation of the [Metabolic Scaling Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_Scaling_Theory) and the later work by West, Brown, and Enquist, among others.

Such an argument does not address the fact that different organisms exhibit different shapes (and hence have different [surface-area-to-volume ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface-area-to-volume_ratio), even when scaled to the same size). Reasonable estimates for organisms' surface area do appear to scale linearly with the metabolic rate.[^o15]

### Exponent $\tfrac{3}{4}$ {#Exponent}

[West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_West), [Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Brown_%28ecologist%29), and [Enquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Enquist) (hereafter WBE) proposed a general theory for the origin of many [allometric scaling laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allometry) in biology. According to the WBE theory, $\tfrac{3}{4}$-scaling arises because of efficiency in nutrient distribution and transport throughout an organism. In most organisms, metabolism is supported by a circulatory system featuring branching tubules (i.e., plant vascular systems, insect tracheae, or the human cardiovascular system). WBE claim that (1) metabolism should scale proportionally to nutrient flow (or, equivalently, total fluid flow) in this circulatory system and (2) in order to minimize the energy dissipated in transport, the volume of fluid used to transport nutrients (i.e., blood volume) is a fixed fraction of body mass.[^o17] The model assumes that the energy dissipated is minimized and that the terminal tubes do not vary with body size. It provides a complete analysis of numerous anatomical and physiological scaling relations for circulatory systems in biology that generally agree with empirical data.[^o18] More generally, the model predicts the structural and functional properties of vertebrate cardiovascular and respiratory systems, plant vascular systems, insect tracheal tubes, and other distribution networks.

They then analyze the consequences of these two claims at the level of the smallest circulatory tubules (capillaries, alveoli, etc.). Experimentally, the volume contained in those smallest tubules is constant across a wide range of masses. Because fluid flow through a tubule is determined by the volume thereof, the total fluid flow is proportional to the total number of smallest tubules. Thus, if $B$ denotes the basal metabolic rate, $Q$ the total fluid flow, and $N$ the number of minimal tubules,

$$B\propto Q\propto N\text{.}$$

Circulatory systems do not grow by simply scaling proportionally larger; they become [more deeply nested](wikipedia://en/10913). The depth of nesting depends on the [self-similarity exponents](wikipedia://en/285907) of the tubule dimensions, and the effects of that depth depend on how many "child" tubules each branching produces. Connecting these values to macroscopic quantities depends (very loosely) on a precise model of tubules. WBE show that if the tubules are well approximated by rigid cylinders, then, to prevent the fluid from ["getting clogged"](wikipedia://en/461477) in small cylinders, the total fluid volume $V$ satisfies[^o37]

$$N^4\propto V^3\text{.}$$

(Despite conceptual similarities, this condition is inconsistent with [Murray's law](wikipedia://en/21461479).)[^o19] Because blood volume is a fixed fraction of body mass,[^o18]

$$B\propto M^{\frac{3}{4}}\text{.}$$

::: {#o21 .statement cue=named-heading name="Non-power-law scaling"}

### Non-power-law scaling {#Non-power-law_scaling}

The WBE theory predicts that the scaling of metabolism is not a strict power law but rather should be slightly curvilinear. The 3/4 exponent holds exactly only in the limit of organisms of infinite size. As body size increases, WBE predict that the scaling of metabolism will converge to a ~3/4 scaling exponent.[^o22] Indeed, WBE predicts that the metabolic rates of the smallest animals tend to be greater than expected from the power-law scaling (see Fig. 2 in Savage et al. 2010 [^o23] ). Further, metabolic rates for smaller animals (birds under 10, or insects) typically fit to $\tfrac{2}{3}$ much better than $\tfrac{3}{4}$; for larger animals, the reverse holds.[^o24] As a result, log-log plots of metabolic rate versus body mass can "curve" slightly upward, and fit better to quadratic models.[^o25] In all cases, local fits exhibit exponents in the $[\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{3}{4}]$ range.[^o29]

#### Elaborated and Modified circulatory models {#Elaborated_and_Modified_circulatory_models}

Elaborations of the WBE model predict *larger* scaling exponents, worsening the discrepancy with observed data.[^o27] See also,[^o24][^o28]). However, one can retain a similar theory by relaxing WBE's assumption of a nutrient transport network that is both [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) and circulatory. Different networks are less efficient in that they exhibit a lower scaling exponent. Still, a metabolic rate determined by nutrient transport will always exhibit scaling between $\tfrac{2}{3}$ and $\tfrac{3}{4}$.[^o29] WBE argued that fractal-like circulatory networks are likely under strong [stabilizing selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizing_selection) to evolve to minimize energy used for transport. If selection for greater metabolic rates is favored, then smaller organisms will prefer to arrange their networks to scale as $\tfrac{2}{3}$. Still, selection for larger-mass organisms will tend to result in networks that scale as $\tfrac{3}{4}$, which produces the observed curvature.[^o30]

#### Modified thermodynamic models {#Modified_thermodynamic_models}

An alternative model notes that metabolic rate does not solely serve to generate heat. Metabolic rate contributing solely to useful work should scale with power 1 (linearly), whereas metabolic rate contributing to heat generation should be limited by surface area and scale with power $\tfrac{2}{3}$. Basal metabolic rate is then the [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of these two effects: if the proportion of useful work is $f$, then the basal metabolic rate should scale as

$$B=f\cdot kM+(1-f)\cdot k'M^{\frac{2}{3}}$$

where $k$ and $k$ are constants of proportionality. $k$ in particular describes the [surface area ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meeh_factor) of organisms and is approximately $0.1 \operatorname{kJ}\cdot h^{-1}\cdot g^{-2/3}$;[^o32] typical values for $f$ are 15-20%.[^o33] The theoretical maximum value of $f$ is 21%, because the efficiency of [glucose oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose_oxidation_reaction) is only 42%, and half of the [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate) so produced is wasted.[^o32]

## Criticism of explanations {#Criticism_of_explanations}

Kozłowski and Konarzewski have argued against attempts to explain Kleiber's law via any sort of limiting factor because metabolic rates vary by factors of 4–5 between rest and activity. Hence, any limits that affect the scaling of the *basal* metabolic rate would make elevated metabolism — and hence all animal activity — impossible.[^o35] WBE conversely argue that natural selection can indeed select for minimal transport energy dissipation during rest, without abandoning the ability for less efficient function at other times.[^o36]

Other researchers have also noted that Kozłowski and Konarzewski's criticism of the law tends to focus on precise structural details of the WBE circulatory networks but that the latter are not essential to the model.[^o37]

## Experimental support {#Experimental_support}

[Analyses of variance](wikipedia://en/634) for a variety of physical variables suggest that although most variation in basal metabolic rate is determined by mass, additional variables with significant effects include body temperature and taxonomic order.[^o39][^o40]

A 1932 work by Brody calculated that the scaling was approximately 0.73.[^o15][^o41]

A 2004 analysis of field metabolic rates for mammals conclude that they appear to scale with exponent 0.749.[^o30]

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Kleiber's law has been reported to interspecific comparisons and has been claimed not to apply at the intraspecific level.[^o44] The taxonomic level that body mass metabolic allometry should be studied has been debated [^o45][^o46] Nonetheless, several analyses suggest that while the exponents of the Kleiber's relationship between body size and metabolism can vary at the intraspecific level, statistically, intraspecific exponents in both plants and animals tend to cluster around 3/4.[^o47]

### In other kingdoms {#In_other_kingdoms}

A 1999 analysis concluded that biomass production in a given plant scaled with the $\tfrac{3}{4}$ power of the plant's mass during the plant's growth,[^o49] but a 2001 paper that included various types of unicellular photosynthetic organisms found scaling exponents intermediate between 0.75 and 1.00.[^o50] Similarly, a 2006 paper in *Nature* argued that the exponent of mass is close to 1 for plant seedlings, but that variation among species, phyla, and growth conditions overwhelm any "Kleiber's law"-like effects.[^o51] But, metabolic scaling theory can successfully resolve these apparent exceptions and deviations. For finite-size corrections in networks with both area-preserving and area-increasing branching, the WBE model predicts that fits to data for plants yield scaling exponents that are steeper than 3/4 in small plants but then converge to 3/4 in larger plants (see [^o52][^o27]).

### Intra-organismal results {#Intra-organismal_results}

Because cell protoplasm appears to have constant density across a range of organism masses, a consequence of Kleiber's law is that, in larger species, less energy is available to each cell volume. Cells appear to cope with this difficulty via choosing one of the following two strategies: smaller cells or a slower cellular metabolic rate. [Neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuron) and [adipocytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adipocytes) exhibit the former; every other type of cell, the latter.[^o54] As a result, different organs exhibit different allometric scalings (see table).[^o15]
