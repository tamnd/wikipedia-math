# Polynomial decomposition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polynomial decomposition"}

In mathematics, a **polynomial decomposition** expresses a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) *f* as the [functional composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_composition) $g \circ h$ of polynomials *g* and *h*, where *g* and *h* have [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) greater than 1; it is an algebraic [functional decomposition](wikipedia://en/11691). [Algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms) are known for decomposing [univariate polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univariate_polynomial) in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time).

:::

Polynomials which are decomposable in this way are **composite polynomials**; those which are not are **indecomposable polynomials** or sometimes **prime polynomials**[^o2] (not to be confused with [irreducible polynomials](wikipedia://en/188725), which cannot be [factored into *products* of polynomials](wikipedia://en/3776351)). The degree of a composite polynomial is always a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289), the product of the degrees of the composed polynomials.

The rest of this article discusses only univariate polynomials; algorithms also exist for multivariate polynomials of arbitrary degree.[^o3][^o4][^o5]

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In the simplest case, one of the polynomials is a [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416). For example,

$$f(x) = x^6 - 3 x^3 + 1$$

decomposes into $g \circ h$, where

$$g(x) = x^2 - 3 x + 1 \text{ and } h(x) = x^3$$

since

$$f(x) = (g \circ h)(x) = g(h(x)) = g(x^3) = (x^3)^2 - 3 (x^3) + 1,$$

using the [ring operator symbol $\boldsymbol{\circ}$](wikipedia://en/195947#Typography) to denote [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947). We write that as

$$x^6 - 3 x^3 + 1 = (x^2 - 3 x + 1) \circ (x^3)$$

treating the polynomials implicitly as functions of $x$.

Less trivially,

$$\begin{aligned} & x^6-6 x^5+21 x^4-44 x^3+68 x^2-64 x+41 \\ = {} & (x^3+9 x^2+32 x+41) \circ (x^2-2 x). \end{aligned}$$

:::

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ritt's polynomial decomposition theorem"}

## Uniqueness {#Uniqueness}

A polynomial may have distinct decompositions into indecomposable polynomials where $f = g_1 \circ g_2 \circ \cdots \circ g_m = h_1 \circ h_2 \circ \cdots\circ h_n$ where $g_i \neq h_i$ for some $i$. The restriction in the definition to polynomials of degree greater than one excludes the infinitely many decompositions possible with linear polynomials.

[Joseph Ritt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Ritt) proved that $m = n$, and the degrees of the components are the same, but possibly in different order; this is **Ritt's polynomial decomposition theorem**.[^o2][^o10] For example, $x^2 \circ x^3 = x^3 \circ x^2$. In fact, only three kinds of position swap are possible: between monomials; between [Chebyshev polynomials](wikipedia://en/184539) $T_i(x)$; and between $\left(x^{k}\,u\left(x\right)^{p} , x^{p}\right)\leftrightarrow \left(x^{p} , x^{k}\,u\left(x^{p}\right)\right)$— summarized as "Every polynomial can be written as a composition of indecomposables, uniquely up to permutations and units."[^o11] For example:

$$x^{14}\, \left(x^{98}+1\right)^2 = \left( x\,\left(x^7+1\right)^2 \right) \circ \left( x^7 \right) \circ\left( x^2 \right) = \left(x^2 \right) \circ \left(x\,\left(x^{14}+1\right)\right) \circ \left(x^7\right)$$

$$T_2(x) \circ T_3(x) = T_3(x) \circ T_2(x) = 32\,x^6-48\,x^4+18\,x^2-1$$

:::

::: {#o13 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

A polynomial decomposition may enable more efficient evaluation of a polynomial. For example,

$$\begin{aligned} & x^8 + 4 x^7 + 10 x^6 + 16 x^5 + 19 x^4 + 16 x^3 + 10 x^2 + 4 x - 1 \\ = {} & \left(x^2 - 2\right) \circ \left(x^2\right) \circ \left(x^2 + x + 1\right) \end{aligned}$$

can be calculated with 3 multiplications and 3 additions using the decomposition, while [Horner's method](wikipedia://en/14263) would require 7 multiplications and 8 additions.

A polynomial decomposition enables calculation of symbolic roots using [radicals](wikipedia://en/235029), even for some [irreducible polynomials](wikipedia://en/188725) of degree greater than 4. This technique is used in many [computer algebra systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_algebra_systems).[^o14] For example, using the decomposition

$$\begin{aligned} & x^6 - 6 x^5 + 15 x^4 - 20 x^3 + 15 x^2 - 6 x - 1 \\ = {} & \left(x^3 - 2\right) \circ \left(x^2 - 2 x + 1\right), \end{aligned}$$

the roots of this irreducible polynomial can be calculated as[^o15]

$$1 \pm 2^{1/6}, 1 \pm \frac{\sqrt{-1 \pm \sqrt{3}i}}{2^{1/3}}.$$

In the case of [quartic polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_polynomial), if there is a decomposition, it can give a simpler form than the general formula. For example, the decomposition

$$\begin{aligned} & x^4 - 8 x^3 + 18 x^2 - 8 x + 2 \\ = {} & (x^2 + 1) \circ (x^2 - 4 x + 1) \end{aligned}$$

gives the roots[^o15]

$$2 \pm \sqrt{3 \pm i}$$

but straightforward application of the [quartic formula](wikipedia://en/308152#General_formula_for_roots) gives a form that is difficult to [simplify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_computation#Simplification) and difficult to understand; one of the four roots is:

$$2-{ \frac{\sqrt{{{ 9 \left(\frac{8 \sqrt{10} i}{3^{3/2}} + 72\right)^{2/3} + 36 \left(\frac{8 \sqrt{10} i}{3^{3/2}} + 72\right)^{1/3} + 156} \over {\left({\frac{8 \sqrt{10} i}{3^{3/2}}} + 72\right)^{1/3}}}}} 6}-{{\sqrt{-\left(\frac{8 \sqrt{10} i}{3^{3/2}} + 72\right)^{1/3}-{{52}\over{3 \left(\frac{8 \sqrt{10} i}{3^{3/2}} +72\right)^{1/3}}} + 8}}\over 2} .$$

:::

::: {#o17 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithms"}

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

The first algorithm for polynomial decomposition was published in 1985,[^o18] though it had been discovered in 1976,[^o19] and implemented in the [Macsyma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macsyma)/[Maxima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_computer_algebra_system) [computer algebra system](wikipedia://en/54427).[^o20] That algorithm takes exponential time in worst case, but works independently of the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of the underlying [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29).

A 1989 algorithm runs in polynomial time but with restrictions on the characteristic.[^o21]

A 2014 algorithm calculates a decomposition in polynomial time and without restrictions on the characteristic.[^o22]

:::
