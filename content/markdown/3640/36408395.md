# Statistical manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Statistical manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **statistical manifold** is a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), each of whose points is a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). Statistical manifolds provide a setting for the field of [information geometry](wikipedia://en/487312). The [Fisher information metric](wikipedia://en/488687) provides a [metric](wikipedia://en/195795) on these manifolds. Following this definition, the [log-likelihood function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-likelihood_function) is a [differentiable map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_map) and the [score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Score_%28statistics%29) is an [inclusion](wikipedia://en/492516).[^o2]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

The family of all [normal distributions](wikipedia://en/21462) can be thought of as a 2-dimensional parametric space parametrized by the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) *μ* and the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) *σ*<sup>2</sup> ≥ 0. Equipped with the [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) given by the [Fisher information](wikipedia://en/598971) matrix, it is a statistical manifold with a geometry modeled on [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742). A way of picturing the manifold is done by inferring the parametric equations via the Fisher Information rather than starting from the likelihood-function.

A simple example of a statistical manifold, taken from physics, would be the [canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1137672): it is a one-dimensional manifold, with the [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) *T* serving as the coordinate on the manifold. For any fixed temperature *T*, one has a probability space: so, for a gas of atoms, it would be the probability distribution of the velocities of the atoms. As one varies the temperature *T*, the probability distribution varies.

Another simple example, taken from medicine, would be the probability distribution of patient outcomes, in response to the quantity of medicine administered. That is, for a fixed dose, some patients improve, and some do not: this is the base probability space. If the dosage is varied, then the probability of outcomes changes. Thus, the dosage is the coordinate on the manifold. To be a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold), one would have to measure outcomes in response to arbitrarily small changes in dosage; this is not a practically realizable example, unless one has a pre-existing mathematical model of dose-response where the dose can be arbitrarily varied.

:::

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *X* be an [orientable manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable_manifold), and let $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$ be a [measure](wikipedia://en/45270) on *X*. Equivalently, let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F},P)$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) on $\Omega=X$, with [sigma algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) $\mathcal{F}=\Sigma$ and probability $P=\mu$.

The statistical manifold *S*(*X*) of *X* is defined as the space of all measures $\mu$ on *X* (with the sigma-algebra $\Sigma$ held fixed). Note that this space is infinite-dimensional; it is commonly taken to be a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801). The points of *S*(*X*) are measures.

Rather than dealing with an infinite-dimensional space *S*(*X*), it is common to work with a finite-dimensional [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095), defined by considering a set of [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) parameterized by some smooth, continuously varying parameter $\theta$. That is, one considers only those measures that are selected by the parameter. If the parameter $\theta$ is *n*-dimensional, then, in general, the submanifold will be as well. All finite-dimensional statistical manifolds can be understood in this way.
