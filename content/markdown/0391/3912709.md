# Multiple (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multiple"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **multiple** is the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of any quantity and an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). In other words, for the quantities $a$ and $b$, it can be said that $b$ is a multiple of $a$ if $b=n\cdot a$ for some integer *n*, which is called the [multiplier](wikipedia://en/20845). If $a$ is not [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero), this is equivalent to saying that $b/a$ is an integer.

:::

When *a* and *b* are both integers, and *b* is a multiple of *a*, then *a* is called a *[divisor](wikipedia://en/49492)* of *b*. One says also that *a* divides *b*. If *a* and *b* are not integers, mathematicians prefer generally to use **integer multiple** instead of *multiple*, for clarification. In fact, *multiple* is used for other kinds of product; for example, a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) *p* is a multiple of another polynomial *q* if there exists third polynomial *r* such that *p* = *qr*.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

14, 49, −21 and 0 are multiples of 7, whereas 3 and −6 are not. This is because there are integers that 7 may be multiplied by to reach the values of 14, 49, 0 and −21, while there are no such *integers* for 3 and −6. Each of the products listed below, and in particular, the products for 3 and −6, is the *only* way that the relevant number can be written as a product of 7 and another real number:

- $$14 = 7 \times 2;$$
- $$49 = 7 \times 7;$$
- $$-21 = 7 \times (-3);$$
- $$0 = 7 \times 0;$$
- $3 = 7 \times (3/7), \quad 3/7$ is not an integer;
- $-6 = 7 \times (-6/7), \quad -6/7$ is not an integer.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- 0 is a multiple of every number ($0=0\cdot a$).
- The product of any integer $n$ and any integer is a multiple of $n$. In particular, $n$, which is equal to $n \times 1$, is a multiple of $n$ (every integer is a multiple of itself), since 1 is an integer.
- If $a$ and $b$ are multiples of $x,$ then $a + b$ and $a - b$ are also multiples of $x$.

:::

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Sub-multiple"}

## Submultiple {#Submultiple}

In some texts, "*a* is a **submultiple** of *b*" has the meaning of "*a* being a [unit fraction](wikipedia://en/491316) of *b*" (*a*=*b*/*n*) or, equivalently, "*b* being an [integer multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_multiple) *n* of *a*" (*b*=*n* *a*). This terminology is also used with [units of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement) (for example by the [BIPM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BIPM)[^o9] and [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST)[^o10]), where a *[unit submultiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_submultiple)* is obtained by [prefixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix) the main unit, defined as the quotient of the main unit by an integer, mostly a power of 10<sup>3</sup>. For example, a [millimetre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimetre) is the 1000-fold submultiple of a [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre).[^o9][^o10] As another example, one [inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inch) may be considered as a 12-fold submultiple of a [foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_%28unit%29), or a 36-fold submultiple of a [yard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yard_%28unit%29).

:::
