# Lyndon word

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lyndon word"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the areas of [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), a **Lyndon word** is a nonempty [string](wikipedia://en/27701) that is strictly smaller in [lexicographic order](wikipedia://en/567667) than all of its [rotations](wikipedia://en/1917276). Lyndon words are named after mathematician [Roger Lyndon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Lyndon), who investigated them in 1954, calling them **standard lexicographic sequences**.[^o2] [Anatoly Shirshov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Shirshov) introduced Lyndon words in 1953 calling them **regular words**.[^o3] Lyndon words are a special case of [Hall words](wikipedia://en/31782613); almost all properties of Lyndon words are shared by Hall words.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Several equivalent definitions exist.

A $k$-ary Lyndon word of length $n > 0$ is an $n$-character [string](wikipedia://en/27701#Formal_theory) over an [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_%28computer_science%29) of size $k$ which is the unique [lexicographically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicographical_order) minimal word in the [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) of all its rotations. In particular, a Lyndon word differs from all of its non-trivial rotations, so it is aperiodic by definition.[^o6]

Alternately, $w$ is a Lyndon word if and only if it is nonempty and lexicographically smaller than any of its proper suffixes: that is, for every factorization $w=uv$ with $u, v$ nonempty words, we have $w < v$ (or equivalently, $u < v$).

A recursive characterisation of Lyndon words is: $w$ is a single letter, or $w=uv$ for smaller Lyndon words $u, v$ with $u < v$.[^o26] Although there may be more than one choice of such $u, v$, there is a particular *standard factorization* in which $v$ is as long as possible.[^o7]

:::

## Enumeration {#Enumeration}

The Lyndon words over the two-symbol binary alphabet {0,1}, sorted by length and then lexicographically within each length class, form an infinite sequence that begins

0, 1, 01, 001, 011, 0001, 0011, 0111, 00001, 00011, 00101, 00111, 01011, 01111, ...

The first string that does not belong to this sequence, "00", is omitted because it is periodic (it consists of two repetitions of the substring "0"); the second omitted string, "10", is aperiodic but is not minimal in its permutation class as it can be cyclically permuted to the smaller string "01".

The empty string also meets the definition of a Lyndon word of length zero. The numbers of binary Lyndon words of each length, starting with length zero, form the [integer sequence](wikipedia://en/51421)

1, 2, 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18, 30, 56, 99, 186, 335, ...

Lyndon words correspond to aperiodic [necklace](wikipedia://en/6226587) class representatives and can thus be counted with [Moreau's necklace-counting polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moreau's_necklace-counting_function) $M_n(k)$.[^o6][^o10]

:::

## Generation {#Generation}

(Duval 1988) provides an efficient [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for listing the Lyndon words of length at most $n$ with a given alphabet size $s$ in [lexicographic order](wikipedia://en/567667). If $w$ is one of the words in the sequence, then the next word after $w$ can be found by the following steps:

1. Repeat $w$ and truncate it to a new word $x$ of length exactly $n$.
1. As long as the final symbol of $x$ is the last symbol in the sorted ordering of the alphabet, remove it, producing a shorter word.
1. Replace the final remaining symbol of $x$ by its successor in the sorted ordering of the alphabet.

For example, suppose we are generating the binary Lyndon words of length up to 7, and we have generated up to $w = 00111$, then the steps are:

1. Repeat and truncate to get $x = 00111\;00\cancel{111}$
1. Since the last symbol is 0, it is not the final symbol.
1. Increment the last symbol to obtain $x = 00111\;01$.

The worst-case time to generate the successor of a word $w$ by this procedure is $O(n)$. However, if the words being generated are stored in an [array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Array_data_structure) of length $n$, and the construction of $x$ from $w$ is performed by adding symbols onto the end of $w$ rather than by making a new copy of $w$, then the average time to generate the successor of $w$ (assuming each word is equally likely) is constant. Therefore, the sequence of all Lyndon words of length at most $n$ can be generated in time proportional to the length of the sequence.[^o13] A constant fraction of the words in this sequence have length exactly $n$, so the same procedure can be used to efficiently generate words of length exactly $n$ or words whose length divides $n$, by filtering out the generated words that do not fit these criteria.

## Standard factorization {#Standard_factorization}

According to the [Chen–Fox–Lyndon theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen–Fox–Lyndon_theorem), every string may be formed in a unique way by concatenating a sequence of Lyndon words, in such a way that the words in the sequence are nonincreasing lexicographically.[^o15] The final Lyndon word in this sequence is the lexicographically smallest suffix of the given string.[^o16] A factorization into a nonincreasing sequence of Lyndon words (the so-called Lyndon factorization) can be constructed in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time).[^o16] Lyndon factorizations may be used as part of a bijective variant of the [Burrows–Wheeler transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burrows–Wheeler_transform) for [data compression](wikipedia://en/8013),[^o17] and in algorithms for [digital geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_geometry).[^o18]

Such factorizations can be written (uniquely) as finite binary trees, with the leaves labelled by the alphabet, with each rightward branch given by the final Lyndon word in the sequence.[^n1] Such trees are sometimes called **standard bracketings** and can be taken as factorization of the elements of a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) or as the basis elements for a [free Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/3174646). These trees are a special case of [Hall trees](wikipedia://en/31782613) (as Lyndon words are a special case of Hall words), and so likewise, the Hall words provide a standard order, called the [commutator collecting process](wikipedia://en/46214749) for groups, and basis for Lie algebras.[^o20] Indeed, they provide an explicit construction for the commutators appearing in the [Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem](wikipedia://en/762696) needed for the construction of [universal enveloping algebras](wikipedia://en/363551).

Every Lyndon word can be understood as a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027), the **suffix standard permutation**.

### Duval algorithm {#Duval_algorithm}

(Duval 1983) developed an algorithm for finding the standard factorization that runs in linear time and constant space. It iterates over a string trying to find as long a Lyndon word as possible. When it finds one, it adds it to the result list and proceeds to search the remaining part of the string. The resulting list of strings is the standard factorization of the given string. More formal description of the algorithm follows.

Given a string *S* of length *N*, one should proceed with the following steps:

1. Let *m* be the index of the symbol-candidate to be appended to the already collected symbols. Initially, *m* = 1 (indices of symbols in a string start from zero).
1. Let *k* be the index of the symbol we would compare others to. Initially, *k* = 0.
1. While *k* and *m* are less than *N*, compare *S*\[*k*\] (the *k*-th symbol of the string *S*) to *S*\[*m*\]. There are three possible outcomes:
   1. *S*\[*k*\] is equal to *S*\[*m*\]: append *S*\[*m*\] to the current collected symbols. Increment *k* and *m*.
   1. *S*\[*k*\] is less than *S*\[*m*\]: if we append *S*\[*m*\] to the current collected symbols, we'll get a Lyndon word. But we can't add it to the result list yet because it may be just a part of a larger Lyndon word. Thus, just increment *m* and set *k* to 0 so the next symbol would be compared to the first one in the string.
   1. *S*\[*k*\] is greater than *S*\[*m*\]: if we append *S*\[*m*\] to the current collected symbols, it will be neither a Lyndon word nor a possible beginning of one. Thus, add the first *m*−*k* collected symbols to the result list, remove them from the string, set *m* to 1 and *k* to 0 so that they point to the second and the first symbol of the string respectively.
1. When *m* > *N*, it is essentially the same as encountering minus infinity, thus, add the first *m*−*k* collected symbols to the result list after removing them from the string, set *m* to 1 and *k* to 0, and return to the previous step.
1. Add *S* to the result list.

## Connection to de Bruijn sequences {#Connection_to_de_Bruijn_sequences}

If one concatenates together, in lexicographic order, all the Lyndon words that have length dividing a given number *n*, the result is a [de Bruijn sequence](wikipedia://en/1565267), a circular sequence of symbols such that each possible length-*n* sequence appears exactly once as one of its contiguous subsequences. For example, the concatenation of the binary Lyndon words whose length divides four is

0 0001 0011 01 0111 1

This construction, together with the efficient generation of Lyndon words, provides an efficient method for constructing a particular de Bruijn sequence in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) and [logarithmic space](wikipedia://en/1149999).[^o24]

## Additional properties and applications {#Additional_properties_and_applications}

Lyndon words have an application to the description of [free Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/3174646), in constructing a basis for the homogeneous part of a given degree; this was Lyndon's original motivation for introducing these words.[^o26] Lyndon words may be understood as a special case of [Hall sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_set).[^o26]

For prime *p*, the number of irreducible [monic polynomials](wikipedia://en/314730) of degree *d* over the field $\mathbb{F}_p$ is the same as the number of Lyndon words of length *d* in an alphabet of *p* symbols; they can be placed into explicit correspondence.[^n2]

A theorem of Radford states that a [shuffle algebra](wikipedia://en/32234404) over a field of characteristic 0 can be viewed as a polynomial algebra over the Lyndon words. More precisely, let *A* be an alphabet, let *k* be a field of characteristic 0 (or, more general, a commutative Q-algebra), and let *R* be the free noncommutative *k*-algebra $k \langle x_{a} | a \in A \rangle$. The words over *A* can then be identified with the "noncommutative monomials" (i.e., products of the *x<sub>a</sub>*) in *R*; namely, we identify a word (*a*<sub>1</sub>,*a*<sub>2</sub>,...,*a<sub>n</sub>*) with the monomial *x*<sub>*a*<sub>1</sub></sub>*x*<sub>*a*<sub>2</sub></sub>...*x<sub>a<sub>n</sub></sub>*. Thus, the words over *A* form a *k*-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) basis of *R*. Then, a *shuffle product* is defined on *R*; this is a *k*-bilinear, associative and commutative product, which is denoted by ш, and which on the words can be recursively defined by

1 ш *v* = *v* for any word *v*;

*u* ш 1 = *u* for any word *u*;

*ua* ш *vb* = (*u* ш *vb*)*a* + (*ua* ш *v*)*b* for any $a,b \in A$ and any words *u* and *v*.

The *shuffle algebra* on the alphabet *A* is defined to be the additive group *R* endowed with ш as multiplication. Radford's theorem[^o28] now states that the Lyndon words are algebraically independent elements of this shuffle algebra, and generate it; thus, the shuffle algebra is isomorphic to a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) over *k*, with the indeterminates corresponding to the Lyndon words.[^o28]
