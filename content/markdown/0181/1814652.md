# Frobenius algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frobenius algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in the fields of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) and [module theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_theory), a **Frobenius algebra** is a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_ring) [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) with a special kind of [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) which gives the algebras particularly nice duality theories. Frobenius algebras began to be studied in the 1930s by [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134) and [Cecil Nesbitt](wikipedia://en/14373679) and were named after [Georg Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048). [Tadashi Nakayama](wikipedia://en/17772141) discovered the beginnings of a rich duality theory (Nakayama 1939), (Nakayama 1941). [Jean Dieudonné](wikipedia://en/1070084) used this to characterize Frobenius algebras (Dieudonné 1958). Frobenius algebras were generalized to [quasi-Frobenius rings](wikipedia://en/31557888), those [Noetherian rings](wikipedia://en/56129) whose right [regular representation](wikipedia://en/360011) is [injective](wikipedia://en/473775). In recent times, interest has been renewed in Frobenius algebras due to connections to [topological quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/443757).

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A finite-dimensional, unital, [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) *A* defined over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k* is said to be a **Frobenius algebra** if *A* is equipped with a [nondegenerate bilinear form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_bilinear_form) *σ* : *A* × *A* → *k* that satisfies the following equation: *σ*(*a*·*b*, *c*) = *σ*(*a*, *b*·*c*). This bilinear form is called the **Frobenius form** of the algebra.

Equivalently, one may equip *A* with a [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) *λ* : *A* → *k* such that the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of *λ* contains no nonzero left [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of *A*.

A Frobenius algebra is called **symmetric** if *σ* is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1803375), or equivalently *λ* satisfies *λ*(*a*·*b*) = *λ*(*b*·*a*).

There is also a different, mostly unrelated notion of the [symmetric algebra](wikipedia://en/654098) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).

:::

## Nakayama automorphism {#Nakayama_automorphism}

For a Frobenius algebra *A* with *σ* as above, the automorphism *ν* of *A* such that *σ*(*a*, *b*) = *σ*(*ν*(*b*), *a*) is the **Nakayama automorphism** associated to *A* and *σ*.

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

1. Any [matrix algebra](wikipedia://en/487541) defined over a field *k* is a Frobenius algebra with Frobenius form *σ*(*a*,*b*)=tr(*a*·*b*) where tr denotes the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270).
1. Any finite-dimensional unital associative algebra *A* has a natural homomorphism to its own [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) End(*A*). A bilinear form can be defined on *A* in the sense of the previous example. If this bilinear form is nondegenerate, then it equips *A* with the structure of a Frobenius algebra.
1. Every [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) *k*\[*G*\] of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) *G* over a field *k* is a symmetric Frobenius algebra, with Frobenius form *σ*(*a*,*b*) given by the coefficient of the identity element in *a*·*b*.
1. For a field *k*, the four-dimensional *k*-algebra *k*\[*x*,*y*\]/ (*x*<sup>2</sup>, *y*<sup>2</sup>) is a Frobenius algebra. This follows from the characterization of commutative local Frobenius rings below, since this ring is a local ring with its maximal ideal generated by *x* and *y*, and unique minimal ideal generated by *xy*.
1. For a field *k*, the three-dimensional *k*-algebra *A*=*k*\[*x*,*y*\]/ (*x*, *y*)<sup>2</sup> is **not** a Frobenius algebra. The *A* homomorphism from *xA* into *A* induced by *x* ↦ *y* cannot be extended to an *A* homomorphism from *A* into *A*, showing that the ring is not self-injective, thus not Frobenius.
1. Any finite-dimensional [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714), by a 1969 theorem of Larson-Sweedler on Hopf modules and integrals. Note: the coalgebra structure for the Hopf algebra is different from the coalgebra structure for the Frobenius algebra.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- The [direct product](wikipedia://en/25063) and [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of Frobenius algebras are Frobenius algebras.
- A finite-dimensional [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) [local](wikipedia://en/153391) algebra over a field is Frobenius if and only if the right [regular module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_module) is injective, if and only if the algebra has a unique [minimal ideal](wikipedia://en/34233472).
- Commutative, local Frobenius algebras are precisely the [zero-dimensional](wikipedia://en/56079) local [Gorenstein rings](wikipedia://en/515613) containing their [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) and finite-dimensional over it.
- Frobenius algebras are [quasi-Frobenius rings](wikipedia://en/31557888), and in particular, they are left and right [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) and left and right [self-injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-injective_ring).
- For a field *k*, a finite-dimensional, unital, [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) is Frobenius if and only if the [injective](wikipedia://en/473779) right *A*-module Hom<sub>*k*</sub>(*A*,*k*) is isomorphic to the right [regular representation](wikipedia://en/360011) of *A*.
- For an infinite field *k*, a finite-dimensional, unital, associative *k*-algebra is a Frobenius algebra if it has only finitely many minimal [right ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_ideal).
- If *F* is a finite-dimensional [extension field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field) of *k*, then a finite-dimensional *F*-algebra is naturally a finite-dimensional *k*-algebra via [restriction of scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restriction_of_scalars), and is a Frobenius *F*-algebra if and only if it is a Frobenius *k*-algebra. In other words, the Frobenius property does not depend on the field, as long as the algebra remains a finite-dimensional algebra.
- Similarly, if *F* is a finite-dimensional extension field of *k*, then every *k*-algebra *A* gives rise naturally to an *F* algebra, *F* ⊗<sub>*k*</sub> *A*, and *A* is a Frobenius *k*-algebra if and only if *F* ⊗<sub>*k*</sub> *A* is a Frobenius *F*-algebra.
- Amongst those finite-dimensional, unital, associative algebras whose right regular representation is injective, the Frobenius algebras *A* are precisely those whose [simple modules](wikipedia://en/29430) *M* have the same dimension as their *A*-duals, Hom<sub>*A*</sub>(*M*,*A*). Amongst these algebras, the *A*-duals of simple modules are always simple.
- A finite-dimensional *bi-Frobenius algebra* or *strict double Frobenius algebra* is a *k*-vector-space *A* with two multiplication structures as unital Frobenius algebras (*A*, • , 1) and (*A*, $\star$ , $\iota$): there must be multiplicative homomorphisms $\phi$ and $\varepsilon$ of *A* into *k* with $\phi(a\cdot b)$ and $\varepsilon(a\star b)$ non-degenerate, and a *k*-isomorphism *S* of *A* onto itself which is an anti-automorphism for both structures, such that $\phi(a\cdot b) = \varepsilon(S(a)\star b).$ This is the case precisely when *A* is a finite-dimensional [Hopf algebra](wikipedia://en/325714) over *k* and *S* is its antipode. The group algebra of a finite group gives an example.[^o12][^o13][^o14][^o15]

:::

## Category-theoretical definition {#Category-theoretical_definition}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the notion of **Frobenius object** is an abstract definition of a Frobenius algebra in a category. A Frobenius object $(A,\mu,\eta,\delta,\varepsilon)$ in a [monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604) $(C,\otimes,I)$ consists of an object *A* of *C* together with four morphisms

$$\mu:A\otimes A\to A,\qquad \eta:I\to A,\qquad\delta:A\to A\otimes A\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad\varepsilon:A\to I$$

such that

- $(A,\mu,\eta)\,$ is a [monoid object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_object) in *C*,
- $(A,\delta,\varepsilon)$ is a [comonoid object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comonoid_object) in *C*,
- the diagrams

*Image: Frobenius obj coh 1.png.*

and

*Image: Frobenius obj coh 2.png.*

commute (for simplicity the diagrams are given here in the case where the monoidal category *C* is strict) and are known as **Frobenius conditions**.[^o17]

More compactly, a Frobenius algebra in **C** is a so-called Frobenius monoidal functor A:**1** → **C**, where **1** is the category consisting of one object and one arrow.

A Frobenius algebra is called **isometric** or **special** if $\mu\circ\delta = \mathrm{Id}_A$.

## Applications {#Applications}

Frobenius algebras originally were studied as part of an investigation into the [representation theory of finite groups](wikipedia://en/874400), and have contributed to the study of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170). They have been used to study [Hopf algebras](wikipedia://en/325714), [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869), and [cohomology rings](wikipedia://en/1069723) of [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [oriented](wikipedia://en/187446) [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470).

### Topological quantum field theories {#Topological_quantum_field_theories}

*Image: Pair of pants cobordism (pantslike).svg.* The product and coproduct on a Frobenius algebra can be interpreted as the functor of a (1+1)-dimensional [topological quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/443757), applied to a [pair of pants](wikipedia://en/2812977).

Recently, it has been seen that they play an important role in the algebraic treatment and axiomatic foundation of [topological quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/443757). A commutative Frobenius algebra determines uniquely (up to isomorphism) a (1+1)-dimensional TQFT. More precisely, the [category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_%28category_theory%29) of commutative Frobenius $K$-algebras is [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) to the category of [symmetric strong monoidal functors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_monoidal_functor) from $2$-$\textbf{Cob}$ (the category of 2-dimensional [cobordisms](wikipedia://en/391832) between 1-dimensional manifolds) to $\textbf{Vect}_K$ (the category of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over $K$).

The correspondence between TQFTs and Frobenius algebras is given as follows:

- 1-dimensional manifolds are disjoint unions of circles: a TQFT associates a vector space with a circle, and the tensor product of vector spaces with a disjoint union of circles,
- a TQFT associates (functorially) to each cobordism between manifolds a map between vector spaces,
- the map associated with a [pair of pants](wikipedia://en/2812977) (a cobordism between 1 circle and 2 circles) gives a product map $V \otimes V \to V$ or a coproduct map $V \to V \otimes V$, depending on how the boundary components are grouped – which is commutative or cocommutative, and
- the map associated with a disk gives a counit (trace) or unit (scalars), depending on grouping of boundary.

This relation between Frobenius algebras and (1+1)-dimensional TQFTs can be used to explain [Khovanov's categorification](wikipedia://en/2639906) of the [Jones polynomial](wikipedia://en/1107642).[^o21][^o22]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Frobenius extensions {#Frobenius_extensions}

Let *B* be a subring sharing the identity element of a unital associative ring *A*. This is also known as ring extension *A* | *B*. Such a ring extension is called **Frobenius** if

- There is a linear mapping *E*: *A* → *B* satisfying the bimodule condition *E*(*bac*) = *bE*(*a*)*c* for all *b,c* ∈ *B* and *a* ∈ *A*.
- There are elements in *A* denoted $\{x_i \}^n_{i=1}$ and $\{y_i \}^n_{i=1}$ such that for all *a* ∈ *A* we have:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n E(ax_i) y_i = a = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i E(y_i a)$$

The map *E* is sometimes referred to as a Frobenius homomorphism and the elements $x_i, y_i$ as dual bases. (As an exercise it is possible to give an equivalent definition of Frobenius extension as a Frobenius algebra-coalgebra object in the category of *B*-*B*-bimodules, where the equations just given become the counit equations for the counit *E*.)

For example, a Frobenius algebra *A* over a commutative ring *K*, with associative nondegenerate bilinear form (-,-) and projective K-bases $x_i, y_i$ is a Frobenius extension *A* | *K* with *E(a)* = (*a*,1). Other examples of Frobenius extensions are pairs of group algebras associated to a subgroup of finite index, Hopf subalgebras of a semisimple Hopf algebra, Galois extensions and certain [von Neumann algebra](wikipedia://en/294185) subfactors of finite index. Another source of examples of Frobenius extensions (and twisted versions) are certain subalgebra pairs of Frobenius algebras, where the subalgebra is stabilized by the symmetrizing automorphism of the overalgebra.

The details of the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) example are the following application of elementary notions in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). Let *G* be a group and *H* a subgroup of finite index *n* in *G*; let *g*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *g<sub>n</sub>*. be left coset representatives, so that *G* is a disjoint union of the cosets *g*<sub>1</sub>*H*, ..., *g<sub>n</sub>H*. Over any commutative base ring k define the group algebras *A* = *k*\[*G*\] and *B* = *k*\[*H*\], so *B* is a subalgebra of *A*. Define a Frobenius homomorphism *E*: *A* → *B* by letting *E*(*h*) = *h* for all *h* in *H*, and *E*(*g*) = 0 for *g* not in *H* : extend this linearly from the basis group elements to all of *A*, so one obtains the *B*-*B*-bimodule projection

$$E \left (\sum_{g \in G} n_g g \right ) = \sum_{h \in H} n_h h \ \ \ \text{ for } n_g \in k$$

(The orthonormality condition $E(g_i^{-1}g_j) = \delta_{ij} 1$ follows.) The dual base is given by $x_i = g_i, y_i = g_i^{-1}$, since

$$\sum_{i=1}^n g_i E\left(g_i^{-1} \sum_{g \in G} n_g g\right) = \sum_i \sum_{h \in H} n_{g_ih} g_ih = \sum_{g \in G} n_g g$$

The other dual base equation may be derived from the observation that G is also a disjoint union of the right cosets $Hg_1^{-1},\ldots,Hg_n^{-1}$.

Also Hopf-Galois extensions are Frobenius extensions by a theorem of Kreimer and Takeuchi from 1989. A simple example of this is a finite group *G* acting by automorphisms on an algebra *A* with subalgebra of invariants:

$$B = \{ x \in A \mid \forall g \in G, g(x) = x \}.$$

By DeMeyer's criterion *A* is *G*-Galois over *B* if there are elements $\{ a_i \}_{i=1}^n, \{ b_i \}_{i=1}^n$ in *A* satisfying:

$$\forall g \in G: \ \ \sum_{i=1}^n a_i g(b_i) = \delta_{g,1_G}1_A$$

whence also

$$\forall g \in G: \ \ \sum_{i=1}^n g(a_i) b_i = \delta_{g,1_G}1_A.$$

Then *A* is a Frobenius extension of *B* with *E*: *A* → *B* defined by

$$E(a) = \sum_{g \in G} g(a)$$

which satisfies

$$\forall x \in A: \ \ \sum_{i=1}^n E(xa_i)b_i = x = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i E(b_i x).$$

(Furthermore, an example of a [separable algebra](wikipedia://en/13205367) extension since $e = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \otimes_B b_i$ is a separability element satisfying *ea = ae* for all *a* in *A* as well as $\sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i = 1$. Also an example of a [depth two subring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_two_subring) (*B* in *A*) since

$$a \otimes_B 1 = \sum_{g \in G} t_g g(a)$$

where

$$t_g = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \otimes_B g(b_i)$$

for each *g* in *G* and *a* in *A*.)

Frobenius extensions have a well-developed theory of induced representations investigated in papers by Kasch and Pareigis, Nakayama and Tzuzuku in the 1950s and 1960s. For example, for each *B*-module *M*, the induced module *A* ⊗<sub>*B*</sub> *M* (if *M* is a left module) and co-induced module Hom<sub>*B*</sub>(*A, M*) are naturally isomorphic as *A*-modules (as an exercise one defines the isomorphism given *E* and dual bases). The endomorphism ring theorem of Kasch from 1960 states that if *A* | *B* is a Frobenius extension, then so is *A* → End(*A<sub>B</sub>*) where the mapping is given by *a* ↦ *λ<sub>a</sub>*(*x*) and *λ<sub>a</sub>*(*x*) = *ax* for each *a,x* ∈ *A*. Endomorphism ring theorems and converses were investigated later by Mueller, Morita, Onodera and others.

### Frobenius adjunctions {#Frobenius_adjunctions}

As already hinted at in the previous paragraph, Frobenius extensions have an equivalent categorical formulation. Namely, given a ring extension $S \subset R$, the induced induction functor $R \otimes_S - \colon \text{Mod}(S) \to \text{Mod}(R)$ from the category of, say, left *S*-modules to the category of left *R*-modules has both a left and a right adjoint, called co-restriction and restriction, respectively. The ring extension is then called *Frobenius* if and only if the left and the right adjoint are naturally isomorphic.[^o27]

This leads to the obvious abstraction to ordinary category theory: An adjunction $F \dashv G$ is called a *Frobenius adjunction* iff also $G \dashv F$. A functor *F* is a *Frobenius functor* if it is part of a Frobenius adjunction, i.e. if it has isomorphic left and right adjoints.
