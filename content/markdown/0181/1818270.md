# Hamiltonian vector field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hamiltonian vector field"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a **Hamiltonian vector field** on a [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356) is a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) defined for any **energy function** or **Hamiltonian**. Named after the physicist and mathematician [Sir William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817), a Hamiltonian vector field is a geometric manifestation of [Hamilton's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton's_equations) in [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586). The [integral curves](wikipedia://en/1322061) of a Hamiltonian vector field represent solutions to the [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) in the Hamiltonian form. The [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) of a symplectic manifold arising from the [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) of a Hamiltonian vector field are known as [canonical transformations](wikipedia://en/514534) in physics and (Hamiltonian) [symplectomorphisms](wikipedia://en/312304) in mathematics.[^n1]

:::

Hamiltonian vector fields can be defined more generally on an arbitrary [Poisson manifold](wikipedia://en/882902). The [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/10282799) of two Hamiltonian vector fields corresponding to functions $f$ and $g$ on the manifold is itself a Hamiltonian vector field, with the Hamiltonian given by the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) of $f$ and $g$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose that $(M,\omega)$ is a [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356). Since the [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/28356) $\omega$ is nondegenerate, it sets up a *fiberwise-linear* [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828)

$$\omega: TM \to T^*M,$$

between the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) $TM$ and the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) $T^*M$, with the inverse

$$\Omega: T^* M \to T M, \quad \Omega = \omega^{-1}.$$

Therefore, [one-forms](wikipedia://en/314692) on a symplectic manifold $M$ may be identified with [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) and every [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) $H:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ determines a unique [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $X_H$, called the *Hamiltonian vector field* with the *Hamiltonian* $H$, by defining for every vector field $Y$ on $M$,

$$\mathrm{d}H(Y) = \omega(X_H,Y).$$

Or more succinctly, $\iota_{X_H} \omega = dH$.

**Note**: Some authors define the Hamiltonian vector field with the opposite sign. One has to be mindful of varying conventions in physical and mathematical literature.

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Suppose that $M$ is a $2n$-dimensional symplectic manifold. Then locally, one may choose [canonical coordinates](wikipedia://en/706311) $(q^1,\cdots,q^n,p_1,\cdots,p_n)$ on $M$, in which the symplectic form is expressed as:[^n2] $\omega = \sum_i \mathrm{d}q^i \wedge \mathrm{d}p_i,$

where $\operatorname{d}$ denotes the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) and $\wedge$ denotes the [exterior product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product). Then the Hamiltonian vector field with Hamiltonian $H$ takes the form:[^n1] $\mathrm{X}_H = \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, - \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i} \right) = \Omega\,\mathrm{d}H,$

where $\Omega$ is a $2n\times 2n$ square matrix

$$\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix},$$

and

$$\mathrm{d}H = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i} \\ \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \end{bmatrix}.$$

The matrix $\Omega$ is frequently denoted with $\mathbf{J}$.

Suppose that $M=\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ is the $2n$-dimensional [symplectic vector space](wikipedia://en/292852) with (global) canonical coordinates.

- If $H = p_i$ then $X_H = \partial/\partial q^i;$
- if $H = q_i$ then $X_H = -\partial/\partial p^i;$
- if $H = \frac{1}{2} \sum (p_i)^2$ then $X_H = \sum p_i\partial/\partial q^i;$
- if $H = \frac{1}{2} \sum a_{ij} q^i q^j, a_{ij} = a_{ji}$ then $X_H = -\sum a_{ij} q_i\partial/\partial p^j.$

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The assignment $f\mapsto X_f$ is [linear](wikipedia://en/18102), so that the sum of two Hamiltonian functions transforms into the sum of the corresponding Hamiltonian vector fields.
- Suppose that $(q^1,\cdots,q^n,p_1,\cdots,p_n)$ are canonical coordinates on $M$ (see above). Then a curve $\gamma(t)=(q(t),p(t))$ is an [integral curve](wikipedia://en/1322061) of the Hamiltonian vector field $X_H$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is a solution of [Hamilton's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton's_equations):[^n1] $$\begin{aligned} \dot{q}^i & = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \\ \dot{p}_i & =-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}. \end{aligned}$$
- The Hamiltonian $H$ is constant along the integral curves, because $\langle dH, \dot{\gamma}\rangle = \omega(X_H(\gamma),X_H(\gamma)) = 0$. That is, $H(\gamma(t))$ is actually independent of $t$. This property corresponds to the [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) in [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319).
- More generally, if two functions $F$ and $H$ have a zero [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) (cf. below), then $F$ is constant along the integral curves of $H$, and similarly, $H$ is constant along the integral curves of $F$. This fact is the abstract mathematical principle behind [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159).[^o12]
- The [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/28356) $\omega$ is preserved by the Hamiltonian flow. Equivalently, the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437) $\mathcal{L}_{X_H} \omega=0$.

:::

## Poisson bracket {#Poisson_bracket}

The notion of a Hamiltonian vector field leads to a [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/449477#Symmetric,_skew-symmetric_and_alternating_forms) bilinear operation on the differentiable functions on a symplectic manifold $M$, the **[Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340)**, defined by the formula

$$\{f,g\} = \omega(X_g, X_f)= dg(X_f) = \mathcal{L}_{X_f} g$$

where $\mathcal{L}_X$ denotes the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437) along a vector field $X$. Moreover, one can check that the following identity holds:[^n1] $X_{\{f,g\}}= -[X_f,X_g]$,

where the right hand side represents the Lie bracket of the Hamiltonian vector fields with Hamiltonians $f$ and $g$. As a consequence (a proof at [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340)), the Poisson bracket satisfies the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370):[^n1] $\{\{f,g\},h\} + \{\{g,h\},f\} + \{\{h,f\},g\}=0$,

which means that the vector space of differentiable functions on $M$, endowed with the Poisson bracket, has the structure of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) over $\mathbb{R}$, and the assignment $f\mapsto X_f$ is a [Lie algebra homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra_homomorphism), whose [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915) consists of the locally constant functions (constant functions if $M$ is connected).
