# Final topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Final topology"}

In [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **final topology**[^o2] (or **coinduced**,[^o3] **strong**[^o4], **colimit**, or **inductive**[^o5] topology) on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $X,$ with respect to a family of functions from [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) into $X,$ is the [finest topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finest_topology) on $X$ that makes all those functions [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29).

:::

The [quotient topology on a quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) is a final topology, with respect to a single surjective function, namely the quotient map. The [disjoint union topology](wikipedia://en/1564380) is the final topology with respect to the [inclusion maps](wikipedia://en/492516). The final topology is also the topology that every [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) in the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) is endowed with, and it is in the context of direct limits that the final topology often appears. A topology is [coherent](wikipedia://en/14602178) with some collection of [subspaces](wikipedia://en/910926) if and only if it is the final topology induced by the natural inclusions.

The [dual](wikipedia://en/609737) notion is the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123), which for a given family of functions from a set $X$ into topological spaces is the [coarsest topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarsest_topology) on $X$ that makes those functions continuous.

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a set $X$ and an $I$-indexed family of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) $\left(Y_i, \upsilon_i\right)$ with associated functions

$$f_i : Y_i \to X,$$

the **final topology** on $X$ induced by the family of functions $\mathcal{F} := \left\{ f_i : i \in I \right\}$ is the [finest topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finest_topology) $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ on $X$ such that

$$f_i : \left(Y_i, \upsilon_i\right) \to \left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right)$$

is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29) for each $i\in I$. The final topology always exists, and is unique.

Explicitly, the final topology may be described as follows:

a subset $U$ of $X$ is open in the final topology $\left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right)$ (that is, $U \in \tau_{\mathcal{F}}$) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $f_i^{-1}(U)$ is open in $\left(Y_i, \upsilon_i\right)$ for each $i\in I$.

The closed subsets have an analogous characterization:

a subset $C$ of $X$ is closed in the final topology $\left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right)$ if and only if $f_i^{-1}(C)$ is closed in $\left(Y_i, \upsilon_i\right)$ for each $i\in I$.

The family $\mathcal{F}$ of functions that induces the final topology on $X$ is usually a *set* of functions. But the same construction can be performed if $\mathcal{F}$ is a [proper class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class) of functions, and the result is still well-defined in [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214). In that case there is always a subfamily $\mathcal{G}$ of $\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathcal{G}$ a set, such that the final topologies on $X$ induced by $\mathcal{F}$ and by $\mathcal{G}$ coincide. For more on this, see for example the discussion here.[^o8] As an example, a commonly used variant of the notion of [compactly generated space](wikipedia://en/899382) is defined as the final topology with respect to a proper class of functions.[^n1]

## Examples {#Examples}

The important special case where the family of maps $\mathcal{F}$ consists of a single surjective map can be completely characterized using the notion of [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29). A surjective function $f : (Y, \upsilon) \to \left(X, \tau\right)$ between topological spaces is a quotient map if and only if the topology $\tau$ on $X$ coincides with the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ induced by the family $\mathcal{F}=\{f\}$. In particular: the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) is the final topology on the quotient space induced by the [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29).

The final topology on a set $X$ induced by a family of $X$-valued maps can be viewed as a far reaching generalization of the quotient topology, where multiple maps may be used instead of just one and where these maps are not required to be surjections.

Given topological spaces $X_i$, the [disjoint union topology](wikipedia://en/1564380) on the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) $\coprod_i X_i$ is the final topology on the disjoint union induced by the natural injections.

Given a [family](wikipedia://en/723043) of topologies $\left(\tau_i\right)_{i \in I}$ on a fixed set $X,$ the final topology on $X$ with respect to the identity maps $\operatorname{id}_{\tau_i} : \left(X, \tau_i\right) \to X$ as $i$ ranges over $I,$ call it $\tau,$ is the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) (or meet) of these topologies $\left(\tau_i\right)_{i \in I}$ in the [lattice of topologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_of_topologies) on $X.$ That is, the final topology $\tau$ is equal to the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) $\tau = \bigcap_{i \in I} \tau_i.$

Given a topological space $(X,\tau)$ and a family $\mathcal C=\{ C_i : i\in I\}$ of subsets of $X$ each having the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926), the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal C}$ induced by all the inclusion maps of the $C_i$ into $X$ is [finer](wikipedia://en/393069) than (or equal to) the original topology $\tau$ on $X.$ The space $X$ is called [coherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_%28topology%29) with the family $\mathcal C$ of subspaces if the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal C}$ coincides with the original topology $\tau.$ In that case, a subset $U\subseteq X$ will be open in $X$ exactly when the intersection $U\cap C_i$ is open in $C_i$ for each $i\in I.$ (See the [coherent topology](wikipedia://en/14602178) article for more details on this notion and more examples.) As a particular case, one of the notions of [compactly generated space](wikipedia://en/899382) can be characterized as a certain coherent topology.

The [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of any direct system of spaces and continuous maps is the set-theoretic direct limit together with the final topology determined by the canonical morphisms. Explicitly, this means that if $\operatorname{Sys}_Y = \left(Y_i, f_{ji}, I\right)$ is a direct system in the [category **Top** of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) and if $\left(X, \left(f_i\right)_{i \in I}\right)$ is a direct limit of $\operatorname{Sys}_Y$ in the [category **Set** of all sets](wikipedia://en/26829), then by endowing $X$ with the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ induced by $\mathcal{F} := \left\{ f_i : i \in I \right\},$ $\left(\left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right), \left(f_i\right)_{i \in I}\right)$ becomes the direct limit of $\operatorname{Sys}_Y$ in the category **Top**.

The [étalé space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Étalé_space) of a sheaf is topologized by a final topology.

A [first-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-countable) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) $(X, \tau)$ is [locally path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_path-connected) if and only if $\tau$ is equal to the final topology on $X$ induced by the set $C\left([0, 1]; X\right)$ of all continuous maps $[0, 1] \to (X, \tau),$ where any such map is called a [path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_%28mathematics%29) in $(X, \tau).$

If a Hausdorff [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $(X, \tau)$ is a [Fréchet-Urysohn space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fréchet-Urysohn_space) then $\tau$ is equal to the final topology on $X$ induced by the set $\operatorname{Arc}\left([0, 1]; X\right)$ of all [arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28topology%29) in $(X, \tau),$ which by definition are continuous [paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_%28mathematics%29) $[0, 1] \to (X, \tau)$ that are also [topological embeddings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_embedding).

## Properties {#Properties}

### Characterization via continuous maps {#Characterization_via_continuous_maps}

Given functions $f_i : Y_i \to X,$ from topological spaces $Y_i$ to the set $X$, the final topology on $X$ with respect to these functions $f_i$ satisfies the following property:

a function $g$ from $X$ to some space $Z$ is continuous if and only if $g \circ f_i$ is continuous for each $i \in I.$

*Image: FinalTopology-01.png.* Characteristic property of the final topology

This property characterizes the final topology in the sense that if a topology on $X$ satisfies the property above for all spaces $Z$ and all functions $g:X\to Z$, then the topology on $X$ is the final topology with respect to the $f_i.$

### Behavior under composition {#Behavior_under_composition}

Suppose $\mathcal{F} := \left\{ f_i:Y_i \to X \mid i \in I \right\}$ is a family of maps, and for every $i \in I,$ the topology $\upsilon_i$ on $Y_i$ is the final topology induced by some family $\mathcal{G}_i$ of maps valued in $Y_i$. Then the final topology on $X$ induced by $\mathcal{F}$ is equal to the final topology on $X$ induced by the maps $\left\{ f_i \circ g ~:~ i \in I \text{ and } g \in \cal G_i \right\}.$

As a consequence: if $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ is the final topology on $X$ induced by the family $\mathcal{F} := \left\{ f_i : i \in I \right\}$ and if $\pi : X \to (S, \sigma)$ is any [surjective map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective_map) valued in some topological space $(S, \sigma),$ then $\pi : \left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right) \to (S, \sigma)$ is a [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29) if and only if $(S, \sigma)$ has the final topology induced by the maps $\left\{ \pi \circ f_i ~:~ i \in I \right\}.$

By the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) of the [disjoint union topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoint_union_topology) we know that given any family of continuous maps $f_i : Y_i \to X,$ there is a unique continuous map

$$f : \coprod_i Y_i \to X$$

that is compatible with the natural injections. If the family of maps $f_i$ covers $X$ (i.e. each $x \in X$ lies in the image of some $f_i$) then the map $f$ will be a [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29) if and only if $X$ has the final topology induced by the maps $f_i.$

### Effects of changing the family of maps {#Effects_of_changing_the_family_of_maps}

Throughout, let $\mathcal{F} := \left\{ f_i : i \in I \right\}$ be a family of $X$-valued maps with each map being of the form $f_i : \left(Y_i, \upsilon_i\right) \to X$ and let $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ denote the final topology on $X$ induced by $\mathcal{F}.$ The definition of the final topology guarantees that for every index $i,$ the map $f_i : \left(Y_i, \upsilon_i\right) \to \left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right)$ is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29).

For any subset $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{F},$ the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal{S}}$ on $X$ will be [finer than](wikipedia://en/393069) (and possibly equal to) the topology $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$; that is, $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ implies $\tau_{\mathcal{F}} \subseteq \tau_{\mathcal{S}},$ where set equality might hold even if $\mathcal{S}$ is a [proper subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\mathcal{F}.$

If $\tau$ is any topology on $X$ such that $\tau \neq \tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ and $f_i : \left(Y_i, \upsilon_i\right) \to (X, \tau)$ is continuous for every index $i \in I,$ then $\tau$ must be [strictly coarser](wikipedia://en/393069) than $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ (meaning that $\tau \subseteq \tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ and $\tau \neq \tau_{\mathcal{F}};$ this will be written $\tau \subsetneq \tau_{\mathcal{F}}$) and moreover, for any subset $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ the topology $\tau$ will also be strictly coarser than the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal{S}}$ that $\mathcal{S}$ induces on $X$ (because $\tau_{\mathcal{F}} \subseteq \tau_{\mathcal{S}}$); that is, $\tau \subsetneq \tau_{\mathcal{S}}.$

Suppose that in addition, $\mathcal{G} := \left\{g_a : a \in A\right\}$ is an $A$-indexed family of $X$-valued maps $g_a : Z_a \to X$ whose domains are topological spaces $\left(Z_a, \zeta_a\right).$ If every $g_a : \left(Z_a, \zeta_a\right) \to \left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right)$ is continuous then adding these maps to the family $\mathcal{F}$ will not change the final topology on $X;$ that is, $\tau_{\mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{G}} = \tau_{\mathcal{F}}.$ Explicitly, this means that the final topology on $X$ induced by the "extended family" $\mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{G}$ is equal to the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ induced by the original family $\mathcal{F} = \left\{ f_i : i \in I \right\}.$ However, had there instead existed even just one map $g_{a_0}$ such that $g_{a_0} : \left(Z_{a_0}, \zeta_{a_0}\right) \to \left(X, \tau_{\mathcal{F}}\right)$ was not continuous, then the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{G}}$ on $X$ induced by the "extended family" $\mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{G}$ would necessarily be [strictly coarser](wikipedia://en/393069) than the final topology $\tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ induced by $\mathcal{F};$ that is, $\tau_{\mathcal{F} \cup \mathcal{G}} \subsetneq \tau_{\mathcal{F}}$ (see this footnote[^o17] for an explanation).

:::

## Final topology on the direct limit of finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces {#Final_topology_on_the_direct_limit_of_finite-dimensional_Euclidean_spaces}

Let

$$\mathbb{R}^{\infty} ~:=~ \left\{ \left(x_1, x_2, \ldots\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}} ~:~ \text{ all but finitely many } x_i \text{ are equal to } 0 \right\},$$

denote the **[space of finite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_finite_sequences)**, where $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$ denotes the [space of all real sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_all_real_sequences). For every [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n \in \mathbb{N},$ let $\mathbb{R}^n$ denote the usual [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) endowed with the [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) and let $\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n} : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ denote the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) defined by $\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\left(x_1, \ldots, x_n\right) := \left(x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, \ldots\right)$ so that its [image](wikipedia://en/579311) is

$$\operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right) = \left\{ \left(x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, \ldots\right) ~:~ x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R} \right\} = \mathbb{R}^n \times \left\{ (0, 0, \ldots) \right\}$$

and consequently,

$$\mathbb{R}^{\infty} = \bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right).$$

Endow the set $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ with the final topology $\tau^{\infty}$ induced by the family $\mathcal{F} := \left\{ \; \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n} ~:~ n \in \mathbb{N} \; \right\}$ of all inclusion maps. With this topology, $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ becomes a [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [sequential](wikipedia://en/4668395) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) that is not a [Fréchet–Urysohn space](wikipedia://en/28480560). The topology $\tau^{\infty}$ is [strictly finer](wikipedia://en/393069) than the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) induced on $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ by $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}},$ where $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$ is endowed with its usual [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910). Endow the image $\operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right)$ with the final topology induced on it by the [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) $\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n} : \mathbb{R}^n \to \operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right);$ that is, it is endowed with the Euclidean topology transferred to it from $\mathbb{R}^n$ via $\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}.$ This topology on $\operatorname{Im} \left( \operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n} \right)$ is equal to the subspace topology induced on it by $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau^{\infty}\right).$ A subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ is open (respectively, closed) in $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau^{\infty}\right)$ if and only if for every $n \in \mathbb{N},$ the set $S \cap \operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right)$ is an open (respectively, closed) subset of $\operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right).$ The topology $\tau^{\infty}$ is coherent with the family of subspaces $\mathbb{S} := \left\{ \; \operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right) ~:~ n \in \mathbb{N} \; \right\}.$ This makes $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau^{\infty}\right)$ into an [LB-space](wikipedia://en/63848931). Consequently, if $v \in \mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ and $v_{\bullet}$ is a sequence in $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ then $v_{\bullet} \to v$ in $\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau^{\infty}\right)$ if and only if there exists some $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that both $v$ and $v_{\bullet}$ are contained in $\operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right)$ and $v_{\bullet} \to v$ in $\operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right).$

Often, for every $n \in \mathbb{N},$ the inclusion map $\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}$ is used to identify $\mathbb{R}^n$ with its image $\operatorname{Im} \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right)$ in $\mathbb{R}^{\infty};$ explicitly, the elements $\left( x_1, \ldots, x_n \right) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\left(x_1, \ldots, x_n, 0, 0, 0, \ldots\right)$ are identified together. Under this identification, $\left(\left(\mathbb{R}^{\infty}, \tau^{\infty}\right), \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^n}\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\right)$ becomes a [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of the direct system $\left(\left(\mathbb{R}^n\right)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}, \left(\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^m}^{\mathbb{R}^n}\right)_{m \leq n \text{ in } \mathbb{N}}, \mathbb{N}\right),$ where for every $m \leq n,$ the map $\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^m}^{\mathbb{R}^n} : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is the inclusion map defined by $\operatorname{In}_{\mathbb{R}^m}^{\mathbb{R}^n}\left(x_1, \ldots, x_m\right) := \left(x_1, \ldots, x_m, 0, \ldots, 0\right),$ where there are $n - m$ trailing zeros.

## Categorical description {#Categorical_description}

In the language of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the final topology construction can be described as follows. Let $Y$ be a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from a [discrete category](wikipedia://en/808519) $J$ to the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) **Top** that selects the spaces $Y_i$ for $i \in J.$ Let $\Delta$ be the [diagonal functor](wikipedia://en/4606683) from **Top** to the [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) **Top**<sup>*J*</sup> (this functor sends each space $X$ to the constant functor to $X$). The [comma category](wikipedia://en/840950) $(Y \,\downarrow\, \Delta)$ is then the [category of co-cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_co-cones) from $Y,$ i.e. objects in $(Y \,\downarrow\, \Delta)$ are pairs $(X, f)$ where $f = (f_i : Y_i \to X)_{i \in J}$ is a family of continuous maps to $X.$ If $U$ is the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) from **Top** to **Set** and Δ′ is the diagonal functor from **Set** to **Set**<sup>*J*</sup> then the comma category $\left(UY \,\downarrow\, \Delta^{\prime}\right)$ is the category of all co-cones from $UY.$ The final topology construction can then be described as a functor from $\left(UY \,\downarrow\, \Delta^{\prime}\right)$ to $(Y \,\downarrow\, \Delta).$ This functor is [left adjoint](wikipedia://en/53991) to the corresponding forgetful functor.
