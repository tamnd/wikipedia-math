# Grothendieck's relative point of view

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Grothendieck's relative point of view"}

**Grothendieck's relative point of view** is a [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) applied in certain abstract [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) situations, with a rough meaning of taking for consideration families of "objects" explicitly depending on [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) as the basic field of study, rather than a single such object. It is named after [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042), who made extensive use of it in treating foundational aspects of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). Outside that field, it has been influential particularly on [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) and [categorical logic](wikipedia://en/1063799).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulations"}

## Formulations {#Formulations}

In the usual formulation, the point of view treats not [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) *X* of a given category *C*, but [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891)

*f*: *X* → *S*

where *S* is a fixed object.

A statement in informal language is "the various aspects of an object can be made manifest once it is put in relation with other objects".[^o4] The concept of [representable functor](wikipedia://en/844461) can make that point more precise: an object is as good as its representable functor. Representable functors were defined explicitly in the "[Tôhoku paper](wikipedia://en/44572732)" of 1957.[^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem"}

## Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem {#Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch_theorem}

The [Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/1532606) from about 1956 is usually cited as the key moment for the introduction of this circle of ideas. The more classical types of [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261) are recovered in the case where *S* is a single point (i.e. the [final object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_object) in the working category *C*). Using other *S* is a way to have versions of theorems "with parameters", i.e. allowing for continuous variation, for which the "frozen" version reduces the parameters to [constants](wikipedia://en/51203).

In relation to the [Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/2892412) of 1954, [Michael Atiyah](wikipedia://en/20698) commented in 1984 that "Grothendieck made a very significant further advance."[^o8] He expanded his point:

> Roughly speaking Grothendieck considered not just one linear system, as Hirzebruch had done, but simultaneously all linear systems with the same parameter space.[^o8]

As a consequence, "Instead of ending up with a theorem he therefore obtained a whole theory, designated by the succinct but uninformative title of [K-theory](wikipedia://en/246748)."[^o8]

:::

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Reed wrote, in the setting of families of curves in algebraic geometry, that

> Grothendieck time and time again shows the power of the \[relative\] point of view by proving theorems in a "relative" context and showing how the usual "absolute" version becomes a simple consequence.[^o11]

In other applications, this way of thinking has been used in [topos theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topos_theory), to clarify the role of [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) in foundational matters. Assuming that we don't have a commitment to one 'set theory' (all topoi are in some sense equally set theories for some [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262)) it is possible to state everything relative to some given set theory that acts as a base topos.

In the respective contexts of [formal languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_languages) and [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173), the Grothendieck point of view has been cited as relevant to [type theory](wikipedia://en/40282)[^o12] and context-dependence.[^o13]

:::

## Slice categories, base change and descent {#Slice_categories,_base_change_and_descent}

This set of ideas is made more formal in the idea of the [slice category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slice_category) of objects of *C* "above" *S*.

A **base change** "along" a given morphism

*g*: *T* → *S*

is typically given by the [fiber product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_product), producing an object over *T* from one over *S*. The "fiber" terminology is significant: the underlying heuristic or intuition is that *X* over *S* is a family of fibers, one for each 'point' of *S*; the fiber product is then the family on *T*, which described by fibers is for each "point" of *T* the fiber at its image in *S*. This set-theoretic language is too naïve to fit the required context, certainly, in algebraic geometry. It fits well, though, by the use of the [Yoneda lemma](wikipedia://en/34359), to replace the "point" idea with that of treating an object such as *S*, as "as good as" the representable functor it sets up. Representable natural transformations are an example of Grothendieck's relative point of view.[^o15]

To move from one slice to another by "pullback" therefore requires base change. The related operation in the opposite direction is [descent](wikipedia://en/361449). These ideas persist in contemporary category theory, but the terminology used by Grothendieck, and at the same period by [Roger Godement](wikipedia://en/971810) writing about [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory), has undergone a number of changes.
