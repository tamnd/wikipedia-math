# Coherent duality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coherent duality"}

In mathematics, **coherent duality** is any of a number of generalisations of [Serre duality](wikipedia://en/359175), applying to [coherent sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_sheaves), in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) theory, as well as some aspects of [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) that are part of the 'local' theory.

:::

The historical roots of the theory lie in the idea of the [adjoint linear system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_linear_system) of a [linear system of divisors](wikipedia://en/1186886) in classical algebraic geometry. This was re-expressed, with the advent of [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory), in a way that made an analogy with [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732) more apparent. Then according to a general principle, [Grothendieck's relative point of view](wikipedia://en/1816636), the theory of [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) was extended to a [proper morphism](wikipedia://en/603782); Serre duality was recovered as the case of the morphism of a [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities) [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) (or [complete variety](wikipedia://en/1047627)) to a point. The resulting theory is now sometimes called **Serre–Grothendieck–Verdier duality**, and is a basic tool in algebraic geometry. A treatment of this theory, *Residues and Duality* (1966) by [Robin Hartshorne](wikipedia://en/5693379), became a reference. One concrete spin-off was the [Grothendieck residue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_residue).

To go beyond proper morphisms, as for the versions of Poincaré duality that are not for [closed manifolds](wikipedia://en/669475), requires some version of the *[compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support)* concept. This was addressed in [SGA2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SGA2) in terms of [local cohomology](wikipedia://en/3212091), and [Grothendieck local duality](wikipedia://en/38470542); and subsequently. The [Greenlees–May duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenlees–May_duality), first formulated in 1976 by [Ralf Strebel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralf_Strebel) and in 1978 by [Eben Matlis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eben_Matlis), is part of the continuing consideration of this area.

## Adjoint functor point of view {#Adjoint_functor_point_of_view}

While Serre duality uses a [line bundle](wikipedia://en/361366) or [invertible sheaf](wikipedia://en/581859) as a **[dualizing sheaf](wikipedia://en/47021614)**, the general theory (it turns out) cannot be quite so simple. (More precisely, it can, but at the cost of imposing the [Gorenstein ring](wikipedia://en/515613) condition.) In a characteristic turn, Grothendieck reformulated general coherent duality as the existence of a [right adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_adjoint) functor $f^{!}$, called *twisted* or *[exceptional inverse image functor](wikipedia://en/15606646)*, to a higher [direct image with compact support](wikipedia://en/13910217) functor $Rf_{!}$.

*Higher direct images* are a sheafified form of [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) in this case with proper (compact) support; they are bundled up into a single functor by means of the [derived category](wikipedia://en/985897) formulation of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410) (introduced with this case in mind). If $f$ is proper, then $Rf_{!} = Rf_{\ast}$ is a right adjoint to the *inverse image* functor $f^{\ast}$. The *existence theorem* for the twisted inverse image is the name given to the proof of the existence for what would be the [counit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counit) for the [comonad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comonad) of the sought-for adjunction, namely a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022)

$$Rf_{!}f^{!}\rightarrow id,$$

which is denoted by $Tr_f$ (Hartshorne) or $\int_f$ (Verdier). It is the aspect of the theory closest to the classical meaning, as the notation suggests, that duality is defined by integration.

To be more precise, $f^{!}$ exists as an [exact functor](wikipedia://en/453945) from a derived category of [quasi-coherent sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-coherent_sheaves) on $Y$, to the analogous category on $X$, whenever

$$f : X \rightarrow Y$$

is a proper or quasi-projective morphism of [noetherian schemes](wikipedia://en/2783891), of finite [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079).[^o3] From this the rest of the theory can be derived: dualizing complexes pull back via $f^{!}$, the [Grothendieck residue symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_residue_symbol), the dualizing sheaf in the [Cohen–Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen–Macaulay) case.

In order to get a statement in more classical language, but still wider than Serre duality, Hartshorne (*Algebraic Geometry*) uses the [Ext functor of sheaves](wikipedia://en/840758#Important_special_cases); this is a kind of stepping stone to the derived category.

The classical statement of Grothendieck duality for a projective or proper morphism $f : X \rightarrow Y$ of noetherian schemes of finite dimension, found in Hartshorne (*Residues and duality*) is the following quasi-isomorphism

$$Rf_{\ast}RHom_X(F^\bullet,f^! G^\bullet)\to RHom_Y(Rf_{\ast}F^\bullet ,G^\bullet)$$

for $F^\bullet$ a bounded above complex of $O_X$-modules with quasi-coherent cohomology and $G^\bullet$ a bounded below complex of $O_Y$-modules with coherent cohomology. Here the $Hom$'s are sheaves of homomorphisms.

## Construction of the *f*<sup>!</sup> pseudofunctor using rigid dualizing complexes {#Construction_of_the_f!_pseudofunctor_using_rigid_dualizing_complexes}

Over the years, several approaches for constructing the $f^{!}$ pseudofunctor emerged. One quite recent successful approach is based on the notion of a rigid dualizing complex. This notion was first defined by Van den Bergh in a noncommutative context.[^o5] The construction is based on a variant of derived [Hochschild cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochschild_cohomology) (Shukla cohomology): Let $k$ be a commutative ring, and let $A$ be a commutative $k-$algebra. There is a functor $RHom_{A\otimes^L_k A}(A,M\otimes^L_k M)$ which takes a cochain complex $M$ to an object $RHom_{A\otimes^L_k A}(A,M\otimes^L_k M)$ in the derived category over $A$.[^o6][^o7]

Assuming $A$ is noetherian, a rigid dualizing complex over $A$ relative to $k$ is by definition a pair $(R,\rho)$ where $R$ is a dualizing complex over $A$ which has finite flat dimension over $k$, and where $\rho: R\to RHom_{A\otimes^L_k A}(A,R\otimes^L_k R)$ is an isomorphism in the derived category $D(A)$. If such a rigid dualizing complex exists, then it is unique in a strong sense.[^o8]

Assuming $A$ is a [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of a finite type $k$-algebra, existence of a rigid dualizing complex over $A$ relative to $k$ was first proved by [Yekutieli](wikipedia://en/39207600) and Zhang[^o8] assuming $k$ is a regular noetherian ring of finite Krull dimension, and by [Avramov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luchezar_L._Avramov), Iyengar and [Lipman](wikipedia://en/41178158)[^o9] assuming $k$ is a [Gorenstein ring](wikipedia://en/515613) of finite Krull dimension and $A$ is of finite flat dimension over $k$.

If $X$ is a scheme of finite type over $k$, one can glue the rigid dualizing complexes that its affine pieces have,[^o10] and obtain a rigid dualizing complex $R_X$. Once one establishes a global existence of a rigid dualizing complex, given a map $f:X\to Y$ of schemes over $k$, one can define $f^{!} := D_X \circ Lf^* \circ D_Y$, where for a scheme $X$, we set $D_X:= RHom_{\mathcal{O}_X}(-,R_X)$.

## Dualizing complex examples {#Dualizing_complex_examples}

### Dualizing complex for a projective variety {#Dualizing_complex_for_a_projective_variety}

The dualizing complex for a projective variety $X \subset \mathbb{P}^n$ is given by the complex

$$\omega_X^\bullet = \mathrm{RHom}_{\mathbb{P}^n}(\mathcal{O}_X,\omega_{\mathbb{P}^n}[+n])$$

[^o13]

### Plane intersecting a line {#Plane_intersecting_a_line}

Consider the projective variety

$$X = \text{Proj}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,z,w]}{(x)(y,z)} \right) = \text{Proj}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,z,w]}{(xy,xz)} \right)$$

We can compute $\mathrm{RHom}_{\mathbb{P}^3}(\mathcal{O}_X,\omega_{\mathbb{P}^3}[+3])$ using a resolution $\mathcal{L}^\bullet \to \mathcal{O}_X$ by locally free sheaves. This is given by the complex

$$0 \to \mathcal{O}(-3) \xrightarrow{\begin{bmatrix}z \\ -y \end{bmatrix}} \mathcal{O}(-2) \oplus \mathcal{O}(-2) \xrightarrow{\begin{bmatrix}xy & xz \end{bmatrix}} \mathcal{O} \to \mathcal{O}_X \to 0$$

Since $\omega_{\mathbb{P}^3} \cong \mathcal{O}(-4)$ we have that

$$\omega_X^\bullet = \mathrm{RHom}_{\mathbb{P}^3}(\mathcal{L}^\bullet, \mathcal{O}(-4)[+3]) = \mathrm{RHom}_{\mathbb{P}^3}(\mathcal{L}^\bullet\otimes \mathcal{O}(4)[-3], \mathcal{O})$$

This is the complex

$$[\mathcal{O}(-4) \xrightarrow{\begin{bmatrix}xy \\ xz \end{bmatrix}} \mathcal{O}(-2) \oplus \mathcal{O}(-2) \xrightarrow{\begin{bmatrix}z & -y \end{bmatrix}} \mathcal{O}(-1)][-3]$$
