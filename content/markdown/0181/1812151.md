# Skeleton (category theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Skeleton"}

A **skeleton** of a mathematical [category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_%28category_theory%29) is a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) that, roughly speaking, does not contain any extraneous [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828). In a certain sense, the skeleton of a category is the "smallest" [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) category, which captures all "categorical properties" of the original. In fact, two categories are [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) [if and only if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) they have [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/489935) skeletons. A category is called **skeletal** if isomorphic objects are necessarily identical.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A skeleton of a category *C* is an [equivalent category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_%28category_theory%29) *D* in which isomorphic objects are equal. Typically, a skeleton is taken to be a [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) *D* of *C* such that:

- the inclusion of *D* into *C* is [full](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_subcategory) and [essentially surjective](wikipedia://en/4557668), and
- *D* is skeletal: any two isomorphic objects of *D* are equal.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Existence and uniqueness"}

## Existence and uniqueness {#Existence_and_uniqueness}

It is a basic fact that every small category has a skeleton; more generally, every [accessible category](wikipedia://en/15311782) has a skeleton. (This is equivalent to the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840).) Also, although a category may have many distinct skeletons, any two skeletons are [isomorphic as categories](wikipedia://en/489935), so [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism of categories, the skeleton of a category is [unique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_%28mathematics%29).

The importance of skeletons comes from the fact that they are (up to isomorphism of categories), canonical representatives of the equivalence classes of categories under the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) of [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051). This follows from the fact that any skeleton of a category *C* is equivalent to *C*, and that two categories are equivalent if and only if they have isomorphic skeletons.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The category **[Set](wikipedia://en/26829)** of all [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) has the subcategory of all [cardinal numbers](wikipedia://en/6173) as a skeleton.
- The category **[*K*-Vect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_spaces)** of all [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over a fixed [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ has the subcategory consisting of all powers $K^{(\alpha)}$, where *α* is any cardinal number, as a skeleton; for any finite *m* and *n*, the maps $K^m \to K^n$ are exactly the *n* × *m* [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with entries in *K*.
- **[FinSet](wikipedia://en/25044975)**, the category of all [finite sets](wikipedia://en/11742) has **[FinOrd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FinOrd)**, the category of all finite [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_numbers), as a skeleton.
- The category of all [well-ordered sets](wikipedia://en/33456) has the subcategory of all [ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_numbers) as a skeleton.
- A [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582), i.e. a small category such that for every pair of objects $A,B$, the set $\mbox{Hom}(A,B)$ either has one element or is empty, has a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) as a skeleton.
- There many examples of [skeletonization of fusion categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeletonization_of_fusion_categories) and related structures.

:::
