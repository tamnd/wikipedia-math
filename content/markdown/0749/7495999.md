# Adaptive grammar

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Adaptive grammar"}

In [formal language theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_language_theory) and [compiler design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler_design), an **adaptive grammar** is a type of [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) whose [production rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_rule_%28formal_languages%29) can be modified during the process of [parsing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsing) a sentence or generating language strings.

:::

Unlike conventional grammars that operate with a fixed set of rules, adaptive grammars dynamically add, remove, or alter their rules based on the input being processed or the current state of the grammar. This self-modifying capability allows adaptive grammars to recognize and generate [context-sensitive languages](wikipedia://en/6212) and handle complex linguistic phenomena such as [agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agreement_%28linguistics%29), [scope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scope_%28computer_science%29), and [cross-references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-reference) that would require cumbersome or impossible constructions in traditional [context-free grammars](wikipedia://en/6759). Adaptive grammars find applications in [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing), [extensible programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extensible_programming_language), and systems requiring runtime language modification.

## Overview {#Overview}

John N. Shutt defines adaptive grammar as a grammatical formalism that allows rule sets ( sets of production rules) to be explicitly manipulated within a grammar. Types of manipulation include rule addition, deletion, and modification.[^o3]

### Early history {#Early_history}

The first description of grammar adaptivity (though not under that name) in the literature is generally[^o5][^o6][^o7] taken to be in a paper by Alfonso Caracciolo di Forino published in 1963.[^o8] The next generally accepted reference to an adaptive formalism (*extensible context-free grammars*) came from Wegbreit in 1970[^o9] in the study of [extensible programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extensible_programming) languages, followed by the *dynamic syntax* of Hanford and Jones in 1973.[^o10]

### Collaborative efforts {#Collaborative_efforts}

Until fairly recently, much of the research into the [formal](wikipedia://en/396102) properties of adaptive grammars was uncoordinated between researchers, only first being summarized by Henning Christiansen in 1990[^o5] in response to a paper in *ACM [SIGPLAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIGPLAN) Notices* by Boris Burshteyn.[^o12] The Department of Engineering at the [University of São Paulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_São_Paulo) has its [Adaptive Languages and Techniques Laboratory](http://lta.poli.usp.br/), specifically focusing on research and practice in adaptive technologies and theory. The LTA also maintains a page naming researchers in the field.[^o13]

### Terminology and taxonomy {#Terminology_and_taxonomy}

While early efforts made reference to *dynamic syntax*[^o10] and *extensible*,[^o9] *modifiable*,[^o15] *dynamic*,[^o16] and *adaptable*[^o5][^o17] grammars, more recent usage has tended towards the use of the term *adaptive* (or some variant such as *adaptativa*,[^o18][^o19] depending on the publication language of the literature).[^o6] Iwai refers to her formalism as *adaptive grammars*,[^o18] but this specific use of simply *adaptive grammars* is not typically currently used in the literature without name qualification. Moreover, no standardization or categorization efforts have been undertaken between various researchers, although several have made efforts in this direction.[^o6][^o7]

#### The Shutt classification (and extensions) {#The_Shutt_classification_(and_extensions)}

Shutt categorizes adaptive grammar models into two main categories:[^o6][^o21]

- *[Imperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_programming) adaptive grammars* vary their rules based on a global [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_%28computer_science%29) changing over the *time* of the [generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar) of a [language](wikipedia://en/10939).
- *[Declarative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declarative_programming) adaptive grammars* vary their rules only over the *space* of the generation of a language (i.e., position in the syntax tree of the generated string).

Jackson refines Shutt's taxonomy, referring to changes over time as [global](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_variable) and changes over space as [local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_variable), and adding a hybrid *time-space* category:[^o7]

- *Time-space adaptive grammars* (*hybrids*) vary their rules over either the *time* or the *space* (or both) of the generation of a language (and local and global operations are explicitly differentiated by the notation for such changes).

## Adaptive formalisms in the literature {#Adaptive_formalisms_in_the_literature}

Adaptive formalisms may be divided into two main categories: full grammar formalisms (adaptive grammars), and adaptive machines, upon which some grammar formalisms have been based.

### Adaptive grammar formalisms {#Adaptive_grammar_formalisms}

The following is a list (by no means complete) of grammar formalisms that, by Shutt's definition above, are considered to be (or have been classified by their own inventors as being) adaptive grammars. They are listed in their historical order of first mention in the literature.

#### Extensible context-free grammars (Wegbreit) {#Extensible_context-free_grammars_(Wegbreit)}

Described in Wegbreit's doctoral dissertation in 1970,[^o9] an extensible context-free grammar consists of a [context-free grammar](wikipedia://en/6759) whose rule set is modified according to instructions output by a [finite state transducer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_state_transducer) when reading the terminal prefix during a leftmost derivation. Thus, the rule set varies over position in the generated string, but this variation ignores the hierarchical structure of the syntax tree. Extensible context-free grammars were classified by Shutt as *imperative*.[^o6]

#### Christiansen grammars (Christiansen) {#Christiansen_grammars_(Christiansen)}

First introduced in 1985 as *Generative Grammars*[^o26] and later more elaborated upon,[^o27] Christiansen grammars (apparently dubbed so by Shutt, possibly due to conflict with Chomsky generative grammars) are an adaptive extension of [attribute grammars](wikipedia://en/479975). Christiansen grammars were classified by Shutt as *declarative*.[^o6]

The redoubling language $L = \{ww | w \mbox{ is a letter}\}$ is demonstrated as follows:[^o27]

\<program↓*G*> → \<dcl↓*G*↑*w*> \<body↓{*w-rule*}>

where *w-rule* =

\<body↓*G*’> → *w*

\<dcl↓*G*↑*ch*•*w*> → \<char↓*G*↑*ch*> \<dcl↓*G*↑*w*> \<dcl↓G↑\<>> → \<ε> \<char↓G↑a> → a

#### Bottom-up modifiable grammars, top-down modifiable grammars, and USSA (Burshteyn) {#Bottom-up_modifiable_grammars,_top-down_modifiable_grammars,_and_USSA_(Burshteyn)}

First introduced in May 1990[^o12] and later expanded upon in December 1990,[^o15] *modifiable grammars* explicitly provide a mechanism for the addition and deletion of rules during a parse. In response to the *ACM SIGPLAN Notices* responses, Burshteyn later modified his formalism and introduced his adaptive *Universal Syntax and Semantics Analyzer* (USSA) in 1992.[^o29] These formalisms were classified by Shutt as *imperative*.[^o6]

::: {#o31 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="John N. Shutt"}

#### Recursive adaptive grammars (Shutt) {#Recursive_adaptive_grammars_(Shutt)}

Introduced in 1993, Recursive Adaptive Grammars (RAGs) were an attempt to introduce a [Turing powerful](wikipedia://en/30621) formalism that maintained much of the elegance of [context-free grammars](wikipedia://en/6759).[^o6] Shutt self-classifies RAGs as being a *declarative* formalism.

:::

#### Dynamic grammars (Boullier) {#Dynamic_grammars_(Boullier)}

Boullier's *dynamic grammars*, introduced in 1994,[^o16] appear to be the first adaptive grammar family of grammars to rigorously introduce the notion of a time continuum of a parse as part of the notation of the grammar formalism itself.[^o7] Dynamic grammars are a sequence of grammars, with each grammar *G<sub>i</sub>* differing in some way from other grammars in the sequence, over time. Boullier's main paper on dynamic grammars also defines a dynamic parser, the machine that effects a parse against these grammars, and shows examples of how his formalism can handle such things as [type checking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_checking), [extensible languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extensible_programming_language), [polymorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_%28computer_science%29), and other constructs typically considered to be in the [semantic domain](wikipedia://en/13689715) of programming language translation.

#### Adaptive grammars (Iwai) {#Adaptive_grammars_(Iwai)}

The work of Iwai in 2000[^o18] takes the adaptive automata of Neto[^o34] further by applying adaptive automata to [context-sensitive grammars](wikipedia://en/6211). Iwai's adaptive grammars (note the qualifier by name) allow for three operations during a parse: ? query (similar in some respects to a [syntactic predicate](wikipedia://en/7400895), but tied to inspection of rules from which modifications are chosen), + addition, and - deletion (which it shares with its predecessor adaptive automata).

::: {#o36 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="§-Calculus"}

#### §-calculus (Jackson) {#§-calculus_(Jackson)}

Introduced in 2000[^o37] and most fully discussed in 2006,[^o7] the §-Calculus (§ here pronounced *meta-ess*) allows for the explicit addition, deletion, and modification of productions within a grammar, as well as providing for [syntactic predicates](wikipedia://en/7400895). This formalism is self-classified by its creator as both *imperative* and *adaptive*, or, more specifically, as a *time-space* adaptive grammar formalism, and was further classified by others as being an [analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_grammar) formalism.[^o19][^o38]

The redoubling language $L = \{ww | w \in \{a,b\}^+\}$ is demonstrated as follows:

grammar ww { S ::= #phi(A.X\<-"") R; R ::= \$C('\[ab\]') #phi(A.X\<-A.X C) #phi(N\<=A.X) N | R; };

(Note on notation: In the above example, the #phi(...) statements identify the points in the production *R* that modify the grammar explicitly. #phi(A.X\<-A.X C) represents a *global* modification (over time) and the #phi(N\<=A.X) statement identifies a *local* modification (over space). The #phi(A.X\<-"") statement in the *S* production effectively declares a global production called *A.X* by placing the [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827) into that production before its reference by *R*.)

:::

#### Adaptive devices (Neto & Pistori) {#Adaptive_devices_(Neto_&_Pistori)}

First described by Neto in 2001,[^o40] adaptive devices were later enhanced and expanded upon by Pistori in 2003.[^o41]

#### Adapser (Carmi) {#Adapser_(Carmi)}

In 2002,[^o43] Adam Carmi introduced an [LALR(1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LALR_parser)-based adaptive grammar formalism known as *Adapser*. Specifics of the formalism do not appear to have been released.

#### Adaptive CFGs with appearance checking (Bravo) {#Adaptive_CFGs_with_appearance_checking_(Bravo)}

In 2004,[^o19] César Bravo introduced the notion of merging the concept of *appearance checking*[^o45] with *adaptive context-free grammars*, a restricted form of Iwai's adaptive grammars,[^o18] showing these new grammars, called *Adaptive CFGs with Appearance Checking* to be [Turing powerful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_complete).

### Adaptive machine formalisms {#Adaptive_machine_formalisms}

The formalisms listed below, while not grammar formalisms, either serve as the basis of full grammar formalisms, or are included here because they are adaptive in nature. They are listed in their historical order of first mention in the literature.

**Self-modifying finite state automata (Shutt & Rubinstein)**

Introduced in 1994 by Shutt and Rubinstein,[^o47] Self-Modifying Finite State Automata (SMFAs) are shown to be, in a restricted form, [Turing powerful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_complete).

**Adaptive automata (Neto)**

In 1994,[^o34] Neto introduced the machine he called a *structured pushdown automaton*, the core of adaptive [automata theory](wikipedia://en/103356) as pursued by Iwai,[^o18] Pistori,[^o41] Bravo[^o19] and others. This formalism allows for the operations of *inspection* (similar to [syntactic predicates](wikipedia://en/7400895), as noted above relating to Iwai's adaptive grammars), *addition*, and *deletion* of rules.
