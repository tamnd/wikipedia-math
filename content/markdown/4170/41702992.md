# Bundle theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bundle theorem"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), the **bundle theorem** is a statement about six circles and eight points in the Euclidean plane. In general [incidence geometry](wikipedia://en/1576323), it is a similar property that a [Möbius plane](wikipedia://en/38245519) may or may not satisfy. According to Kahn's Theorem, it is fulfilled by "ovoidal" Möbius planes only; thus, it is the analog for Möbius planes of [Desargues' Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desargues'_Theorem) for [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350).

:::

*Image: Bundletheorem-moeb.svg.* The property of the bundle theorem is that the dashed red cycle exists.

## Description {#Description}

If for eight different points $A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4, B_1,B_2,B_3,B_4$ five of the six quadruples $Q_{ij}:=\{A_i,B_i,A_j,B_j\}, \ i<j,$ are concyclic (contained in a cycle) on at least four cycles $c_{ij}$, then the sixth quadruple is also concyclic.[^o3]

The bundle theorem should not be confused with [Miquel's theorem](wikipedia://en/38245519#Miquelian_Möbius_planes) (though it is derivable from taking a [circle inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_inversion) on the diagram of the Miquel point of a complete quadrilateral).

An ovoidal Möbius plane in real Euclidean space may be considered as the geometry of the plane sections of an egglike surface, like a sphere, or an ellipsoid, or half a sphere glued to a suitable half of an ellipsoid, or the surface with equation $x^4+y^4+z^4=1$, etc. If the egglike surface is a sphere one gets the space model of the [classical real Möbius plane](wikipedia://en/38245519#The_classical_real_Möbius_plane), which is the "circle geometry" on the sphere.

The essential property of an ovoidal Möbius plane is the existence of a space model via an ovoid. An [ovoid](wikipedia://en/38264067#Definition_of_an_oval_and_an_ovoid) in a 3-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) is a set of points, which a) is intersected by lines in 0, 1, or 2 points and b) its tangents at an arbitrary point covers a plane (tangent plane). The geometry of an ovoid in projective 3-space is a Möbius plane, called an **ovoidal Möbius plane**. The point set of the geometry consists of the points of the ovoid and the curves ("cycles") are the plane sections of the ovoid. A suitable stereographical projection shows that for any ovoidal Möbius plane there exists a plane model.[^o4] In the classical case the plane model is the geometry of the circles and lines (where each line is completed by a point at infinity). The bundle theorem has a planar and a spatial interpretation. In the planar model there may be lines involved. The proof of the bundle theorem is performed within the spatial model.

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

### Theorem {#Theorem}

The bundle theorem holds in every ovoidal Möbius plane.

The proof is a consequence of the following considerations, which use essentially the fact that three planes in a 3-dimensional projective space intersect in a single point:

1. The planes containing the cycles $c_{23},c_{34},c_{24}$ intersect in a point $P$. Hence $P$ is the intersection point of the lines (in space !) $A_2B_2,\ A_4B_4$.
1. The planes containing the cycles $c_{12},c_{14},c_{24}$ intersect in a point $P'$. Hence $P'$ is the intersection point of the lines $A_2B_2,\ A_4B_4$, too.

This yields: a) $P=P'$ and b) $A_1B_1,\ A_3B_3$ intersect at point $P$, too. The last statement means: $A_1,B_1,A_3,B_3$ are concyclic. The planes involved have point $P$ in common, they are elements of a *bundle* of planes.

The importance of the bundle theorem was shown by [Jeff Kahn](wikipedia://en/26919476).

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Theorem of Kahn"}

## Theorem of Kahn {#Theorem_of_Kahn}

A Möbius plane is ovoidal if and only if it fulfills the bundle theorem.[^o9]

The bundle theorem is analogous for Möbius planes to the [Theorem of Desargues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem_of_Desargues) for [projective planes](wikipedia://en/24350). From the bundle theorem follows the existence of a) a [skewfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skewfield) (division ring) and b) an ovoid. If the more strict theorem of Miquel holds, the skewfield is even commutative (field) and the ovoid is a [quadric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadric_%28projective_geometry%29).

There are Möbius planes, which are not ovoidal.[^o10]

For ovoidal [Laguerre planes](wikipedia://en/38234611#Ovoidal_Laguerre_planes) there exists a bundle theorem with an analogous meaning.[^o11]
