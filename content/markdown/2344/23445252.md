# Line complex

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Line complex"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **line complex** is a set of lines that can be specified by a list of [homogeneous polynomial](wikipedia://en/1698977) equations. That is, a [projective variety](wikipedia://en/320469) of lines.

:::

A **linear line complex** is defined by a list of degree-1 polynomials. A **quadratic line complex** is defined by a list of degree-2 polynomials. Similarly for cubic, quartic, quintic, sextic, etc.

They were first studied by [Julius Plücker](wikipedia://en/52146) in *Neue Geometrie des Raumes* (1868). Other important figures include [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887), [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010), [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034), [William Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817), and [Alfred Clebsch](wikipedia://en/1369957).

## Setup {#Setup}

By the standard trick in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), a line in 3-dimensional space is lifted to a plane through the origin in 4-dimensional space. In other words, the space of lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is lifted to the space of planes through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^4$, which is the [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810) $G(2, 4)$. It is then embedded to the projective space $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$ via exterior product.

Note that during the projective embedding, we get lines that does not exist $\mathbb{R}^3$: the [lines at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142).

$\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$ is the projectivized space of [bivectors](wikipedia://en/884040) in $\mathbb{R}^4$, where $\wedge$ is the [exterior product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product). The space has [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) ([Plücker coordinates](wikipedia://en/1168486)) $[p_{12}, p_{13}, p_{14}, p_{23}, p_{24}, p_{34}]$. By convention, if $p_{ij}$ where $i > j$ is written, then $p_{ij} := -p_{ji}$.

$\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$ has 5 dimensions, and $G(2, 4)$ has 4 dimensions.

A plane in $G(2, 4)$ can be described by any two nonparallel vectors $v, w$, then it is mapped to $[v \wedge w]$ with coordinates $p_{ij} = v_iw_j - v_j w_i$. Thus we obtain an embedding $p : G(2, 4) \hookrightarrow \mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$. It is not the whole $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$, but only a subvariety defined by the [Klein quadric](wikipedia://en/2933091):

$$p_{12} p_{34}+p_{13} p_{42}+p_{14} p_{23} = 0$$

More succinctly, $p \wedge p = 0$. Note that this is not a trivial condition, as a generic bivector does not satisfy it, for example $(e_1 \wedge e_2 + e_3 \wedge e_4)$.

From now on we will simply write $G(2, 4)$ as if it is a subset of $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$, using the embedding.

### Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

The same construction shows that the space of lines in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is lifted to $G(2, n+1)$, then embedded as a subvariety of $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$. Since $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$ has $\tfrac 12 n(n-1)-1$ dimensions, and $G(2, n+1)$ has $2(n-2)$ dimensions, it should be possible to write it as the intersection of $\tfrac 12 (n-2)(n-3)$ hypersurfaces. However, $G(2, \mathbb{R}^n)$ is the intersection of the $\tfrac 14 n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)$ equations:

$$Q_{ijkl} := p_{i j} p_{k l}-p_{i k} p_{j l}+p_{i l} p_{j k}=0 \quad(1 \leq i<j<k<l \leq n)$$

Thus, these equations must be dependent on each other when $n \geq 5$. This is called a [sygyzy](wikipedia://en/11063114).

For example, when $n = 5$, the space $G(2, 5)$ is a codimension-3 subspace of $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^5)$ defined as the intersection of 5 equations, so there are 2 redundancies, which can be exhibited by the matrix equation:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & p_{12} & p_{13} & p_{14} & p_{15}\\ -p_{12} & 0 & p_{23} & p_{24} & p_{25}\\ -p_{13} & -p_{23} & 0 & p_{34} & p_{35}\\ -p_{14} & -p_{24} & -p_{34} & 0 & p_{45}\\ -p_{15} & -p_{25} & -p_{35} & -p_{45} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} Q_1\\ Q_2\\ Q_3\\ Q_4\\ Q_5 \end{pmatrix} =0, \quad \begin{aligned} Q_1 & =p_{23} p_{45}-p_{24} p_{35}+p_{25} p_{34} \\ Q_2 & =p_{13} p_{45}-p_{14} p_{35}+p_{15} p_{34} \\ Q_3 & =p_{12} p_{45}-p_{14} p_{25}+p_{15} p_{24} \\ Q_4 & =p_{12} p_{35}-p_{13} p_{25}+p_{15} p_{23} \\ Q_5 & =p_{12} p_{34}-p_{13} p_{24}+p_{14} p_{23} . \end{aligned}$$

In the subspace of $\mathbb A^9 \subset \mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^5)$ where $p_{12} = 1$, the two linear syzygies

$$Q_1=p_{23} Q_3+p_{24} Q_4+p_{25} Q_5, \quad Q_2=-p_{13} Q_3-p_{14} Q_4-p_{15} Q_5$$

show that if $Q_3, Q_4, Q_5 = 0$, then the other two conditions are automatically satisfied. This is the redundancy.

### Special families of lines {#Special_families_of_lines}

A [ruled surface](wikipedia://en/705158) in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a curve in the Klein quadric $G(2, 4) \subset \mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$. A ruled surface is a line complex when it is the intersection of 3 line complexes, each defined by a single homogeneous polynomial on $\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4$.

In general, a ruled surface is a 1-parameter family of lines. A congruence of lines is a 2-parameter family. A particular example is the set of lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ orthogonal to a given surface. The [Malus–Dupin theorem](wikipedia://en/72934739) shows that geometric optics preserves orthogonal congruences.

## Linear line complex {#Linear_line_complex}

A linear line complex is the intersection of $G(2, 4)$ with one or more hyperplanes in $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4)$, which would be defined by a homogeneous [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570). Equivalently, it is defined as the space of planes through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^4$ which make a [2-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-form) in $\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^4$ equal to zero.

*Image: Standard\_contact\_structure\_r\_3.png.* The standard contact structure is isomorphic to the cylindrically symmetric $dz - r^2 d\theta$.

For example, $p_{12} + p_{34} = 0$ defines the space of planes through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^4$ which make $\omega := dx_1 \wedge dx_2 + dx_3 \wedge dx_4$ equal to 0. There are $\infty^3$ such planes. Equivalently, it is a 3-dimensional space of lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (plus the lines at infinity), which satisfy $v_2 w_1 - v_1 w_2 + w_3 = 0$ given any $\{v + t w : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ parameterization of the line. Equivalently, these are lines that are tangent to the cylindrical [contact structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_structure) on $\mathbb{R}^3$:

$$dz -xdy + ydx = dz - r^2 d\theta$$

That is, they are linear [Legendrian submanifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendrian_submanifold).

More generally, $p_{12} + \dots + p_{2n-1, 2n} = 0$ defines the space of planes through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ which make $\omega := dx_1 \wedge dx_2 + \dots + dx_{2n-1} \wedge dx_{2n}$ equal to 0. There are $\infty^{2n -5}$ such planes. This form $\omega$ is a [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/28356), These planes are known as the [isotropic planes](wikipedia://en/28356#Submanifolds) of $\omega$.

### Symplectic transformation {#Symplectic_transformation}

Any linear transformation of $\mathbb{R}^n$ induces a transformation of $\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ that preserves $G(2, n+1)$. If the transformation also preserves $\omega$, then it is a **[symplectic transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_transformation)**. In the spirit of Erlangen program, [symplectic geometry](wikipedia://en/294298) studies invariants of symplectic transformations.

Symplectic transformations make up a group, called the [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977). The group acts on $\mathbb{R}^n$, splitting it into 2 orbits: one orbit for nonzero vectors, and another orbit for 0. It acts on $G(2, n+1)$ as well, splitting it into 2 orbits: one orbit for isotropic planes, and another orbit for symplectic planes. More generally, any projective transformation on $\mathbb P(\mathbb{R}^n)$ that preserves the linear line complex defined by $\omega$ is a symplectic transformation. This is why Lie always referred to the symplectic group as "the projective group of a linear line complex".[^o7]

### Degeneracy {#Degeneracy}

A 2-form $\sigma$ on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ can be [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate) or degenerate. The symplectic form is nondegenerate.

In the degenerate case, its kernel is a nontrivial even-dimensional linear subspace: $\ker \sigma:= \{v \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} | \sigma(v, \cdot) = 0\} \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, which projectives to an odd-dimensional flat in $\mathbb P(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})$.

A degenerate but nonzero 2-form on $\mathbb{R}^4$ has a 2-dimensional kernel, which projectivizes to a line in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Plücker called a linear line complex defined by a degenerate $\sigma$ **special**, and $\ker \sigma$ its **axis**.

### Involution {#Involution}

Define the [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) $B(p, q) := \sum_{i < j} p_{ij} q_{ij}$ on $\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^n$. Two linear line complexes defined by two linear subspaces $V, W \subset \wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^n$ are in **involution**, or in **Klein polarity**, if $V, W$ are [orthogonal complements](wikipedia://en/1058833) relative to $B$.

### Pencils {#Pencils}

Through a point in $\mathbb{R}^n$, a planar pencil is the set of all lines through this point within a 2-dimensional plane. It is embedded to a straight line in $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$.

More generally, the set of all lines through this point within a *k*-[flat](wikipedia://en/13005617) is embedded to a *(k-1)*-flat in $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$.

Given a 2-flat $F \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, the set of all lines in it is enumerated by the pencils of each point in a line. It is embedded to a 2-flat in $p(F) \subset \mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$. This can be regarded as a planar pencil of a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139). Dually, for each $p(l) \in p(F)$, there is a planar pencil of lines in $p(F)$. Each line in the pencil of $p(l)$ corresponds to a pencil in a point in $l \subset F$.

More generally, the set of all lines within a *k*-flat is embedded to a *(2k-2)*-flat in $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$.

### Constraints {#Constraints}

*Image: Ruled\_hyperboloid.jpg.* A string model of a portion of a regulus and its opposite to show the rules on a hyperboloid of one sheet

In general, the set of all lines that satisfy a number of linear constraints is a linear line complex.

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the linear constraints include: passing a point, intersecting a line, and parallel to a plane. The set of all lines that intersect 2 lines and parallel to a plane (in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736)) is a doubly ruled surface defined by intersecting 3 linear line complexes. In general, the set of all lines that satisfy 3 linear constraints is a [regulus](wikipedia://en/52079701) defined by intersecting 3 linear line complexes.

More exotic examples are available. For example, the aforementioned family of Legendrian lines defined by $p_{12} + p_{34} = 0$ is a linear line complex, which can be interpreted as a linear constraint of $dz -xdy + ydx$.

## Quadratic line complex {#Quadratic_line_complex}

A quadratic line complex is obtained by intersecting $G(2, n+1)$ with quadratic hypersurfaces in $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$. In homogeneous coordinates, they are of form $p^T M p$, where $M$ is a $\tfrac 12 n(n+1)\times\tfrac 12 n(n+1)$ [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474).

In the case of $n=3$, a general quadratic line complex is specified by a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) on $\mathbb P(\wedge^2 \mathbb{R}^3)$ modulo the Klein quadric, resulting in 19 parameters. In general, $\binom{\tfrac 12 n(n+1)}{2} - \binom{n+1}{4}- 1$ parameters are necessary.

*Image: Helixtgtdev.png.* The tangent developable of a helix

In general, the set of all lines that satisfy a number of linear constraints, and at least one quadratic constraint is a quadratic line complex. Examples in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

- Intersect a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673).
- Tangent to a [quadratic surface](wikipedia://en/145570). In particular, fixed distance from a point (i.e. tangent to a sphere) and fixed distance from a line (i.e. tangent to a cylinder).
- Parallel to exactly line in a nondegenerate quadratic cone (i.e. a cone whose cross-section is a conic section). In particular, all lines that make a fixed angle to a plane (i.e. parallel to a line in a circular cone). Equivalently, all lines that make a fixed skew-angle to a line.
- Given two quadratic surfaces and a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $c$, the lines that intersect the two surfaces at 4 points whose [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) is equal to $c$ forms a quadratic line complex. These are the **harmonic complexes**.

The intersection of 3 linear/quadratic constraints in general position produces a ruled surface. For example, the [tangent developable](wikipedia://en/9841185) of a [helix](wikipedia://en/179924) is obtained by intersecting the 3 constraints of: tangent to a cylinder, makes a fixed angle with a plane, and in the Legendrian line complex. Thus it is a quadratic line complex.

### Tetrahedral {#Tetrahedral}

In the special case where the two quadratic surfaces degenerate into 4 planes, the lines that intersect the 4 planes at 4 points whose [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767) is equal to a fixed number forms a quadratic line complex. This is a **tetrahedral complex**, which is a special case of the harmonic complex.

[von Staudt](wikipedia://en/2186050)'s theorem: given a line $l$ in general position with a tetrahedron with vertices $ABCD$ and their respective faces $abcd$, the cross-ratio of the 4 planes $[lA, lB, lC, lD]$ is equal to the cross-ratio of the 4 points $[l\cap a, l\cap b, l\cap c, l\cap d]$.

For any quadratic complex and linear complex in general position, their intersection is contained in a tetrahedral complex.

### Singular {#Singular}

*Image: Kummer surface.png.* Kummer's surface

In general, a quadratic line complex defined by a single [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) has 3 dimensions, so through each point in $\mathbb{R}^3$ there exists a whole 1-dimensional pencil of lines that sweeps out a quadratic cone. This can be constructed by intersecting the quadratic constraint with the linear constraint of "passing to a point".

However, there exists a 2-dimensional set of points where this breaks down. Specifically, at these points, the quadratic cone degenerates into two flat planes. These points are the **singular points** of the quadratic line complex. The set of these points is its **singular surface**. The singular surface is [Kummer's surface](wikipedia://en/647758). It is a [quartic surface](wikipedia://en/20133775). Each line meets the surface in 4 points, and through each line there are 4 tangent planes to the surface. It has 16 singular points ("nodes") and 16 singular planes ("tropes"), the maximum possible for a quartic surface. There are 6 nodes lying in each of the 16 tropes and 6 tropes passing through each of the 16 nodes.

Similarly, each plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$ intersects a quadratic line complex at a 1-dimensional quadratic line complex. In general, these lines sweep out a conic section as their [envelope](wikipedia://en/880235). However, there exists a 2-dimensional set of planes where this breaks down. These planes are the **singular planes**. The envelope of these planes is once again the same singular surface.

There is a 1-dimensional family of infinite number of quadratic complexes having the same singular surface. These are called **cosingular complexes**.

## Higher line complex {#Higher_line_complex}

*Image: Whitney\_umbrella.gif.* Whitney umbrella as a ruled surface, generated by a moving straight line

A ruled surface is a 1-parameter family of lines $\mathbb{R}^3$. They can be obtained by intersecting 3 constraints in general position. The only [structurally stable](wikipedia://en/2515807) singularity that can appear in a ruled surface is the cross-cap, which looks like a [Whitney unbrella](wikipedia://en/1964554).[^o16]

A congruence of lines is a 2-parameter family in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and are obtained by intersecting 2 constraints in general position. In general, through each point in $\mathbb{R}^3$ there passes exactly $n$ lines of the congruence, and through each plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$ there passes exactly $m$ lines of the congruence. Such a congruence is said to be of order $n$ and class $m$.

In particular, the intersection of a quadratic complex with a linear complex is a congruence with order 2 and class 2.

## History {#History}

At the death of Julius Plücker, his *Neue Geometrie des Raumes* was completed by his pupil Felix Klein in 1868. Klein used [invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762) that he learned from [Alfred Clebsch](wikipedia://en/1369957).

Klein and Lie studied line complexes, especially tetrahedral complexes. The group of all projective transformations that [stabilizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_%28group_theory%29) the 4 vertices of the tetrahedron is a 3-dimensional [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $G$. For any two points $x, y$ in general position with the tetrahedron, there exists a unique $g \in G$ such that $g(x) = y$. Each tetrahedral complex is obtained as the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of a single line under the action of $G$.

Lie asked Klein whether there are curves $\gamma$ such that, if $g\in G$ maps some $x \in \gamma$ to some $g(x) \in \gamma$, then $g$ also maps the tangent line $T_x \gamma$ to the tangent line $T_{g(x)} \gamma$. Klein discovered that there are curves which are stabilized by a 1-dimensional subgroup of $G$. He called these the [W-curves](wikipedia://en/27126377). Since the curve is essentially translated by the action of the group, it necessarily maps tangents to tangents. This was an application of [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) in the prehistory of Lie group theory. The investigation of such linear complexes also inspired Klein to propose the [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382).[^o7][^o18]

Their line of work in line geometry was taken up by [Corrado Segre](wikipedia://en/2422061), whose work, including the classification of quadratic line complexes, was foundation in the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424).[^o19]
