# Drucker stability

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Drucker stability"}

**Drucker stability** (also called the **Drucker stability postulates**) refers to a set of mathematical criteria that restrict the possible nonlinear [stress](wikipedia://en/3323565)-[strain](wikipedia://en/184011) relations that can be satisfied by a solid material.[^o2] The postulates are named after [Daniel C. Drucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_C._Drucker). A material that does not satisfy these criteria is often found to be [unstable](wikipedia://en/12683145) in the sense that application of a load to a material point can lead to arbitrary [deformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29) at that material point unless an additional length or time scale is specified in the [constitutive relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutive_relations).

:::

The Drucker stability postulates are often invoked in nonlinear [finite element analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_element_analysis). Materials that satisfy these criteria are generally well-suited for numerical analysis, while materials that fail to satisfy this criterion are likely to present difficulties (i.e. non-uniqueness or singularity) during the solution process.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Drucker's first stability criterion"}

## Drucker's first stability criterion {#Drucker's_first_stability_criterion}

Drucker's first stability criterion (first proposed by [Rodney Hill](wikipedia://en/20573612) and also called **Hill's stability criterion**[^o5]) is a strong condition on the incremental internal energy of a material which states that the incremental internal energy can only increase. The criterion may be written as follows:

$$\text{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\text{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \ge 0,$$

where d**σ** is the stress increment tensor associated with the strain increment tensor d**ε** through the constitutive relation.

:::

## Drucker's stability postulate {#Drucker's_stability_postulate}

Drucker's postulate is applicable to elastic-plastic materials and states that in a cycle of [plastic](wikipedia://en/240123) deformation the second degree plastic work is always positive. This postulate can be expressed in incremental form as

$$\text{d}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\text{d}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_p \ge 0,$$

where d**ε**<sub>p</sub> is the incremental plastic strain tensor.
