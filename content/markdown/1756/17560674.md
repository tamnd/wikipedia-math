# Space of continuous functions on a compact space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Space of continuous functions on a compact space"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), and especially [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a fundamental role is played by the space of [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_functions) on a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) $X$ with values in the [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) or [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers). This space, denoted by $\mathcal{C}(X),$ is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with respect to the pointwise addition of functions and scalar multiplication by constants. It is, moreover, a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) with norm defined by

$$\|f\| = \sup_{x\in X} |f(x)|,$$

the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755). The uniform norm defines the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652) of functions on $X.$ The space $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is a [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665) with respect to this norm.(Rudin 1991, §10.3(a))

:::

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- By [Urysohn's lemma](wikipedia://en/49253), $\mathcal{C}(X)$ [separates points](wikipedia://en/13972359) of $X$: If $x, y \in X$ are distinct points, then there is an $f \in \mathcal{C}(X)$ such that $f(x) \neq f(y).$
- The space $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is infinite-dimensional whenever $X$ is an infinite space (since it separates points). Hence, in particular, it is generally not [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact).
- The [Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem](wikipedia://en/38948504) gives a characterization of the [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) of $\mathcal{C}(X).$ Specifically, this dual space is the space of [Radon measures](wikipedia://en/521933) on $X$ (regular [Borel measures](wikipedia://en/4322)), denoted by $\operatorname{rca}(X).$ This space, with the norm given by the [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561) of a measure, is also a Banach space belonging to the class of [ba spaces](wikipedia://en/629005). (Dunford Schwartz 1958, §IV.6.3)
- [Positive linear functionals](wikipedia://en/454930) on $\mathcal{C}(X)$ correspond to (positive) [regular](wikipedia://en/3390453) [Borel measures](wikipedia://en/4322) on $X,$ by a different form of the Riesz representation theorem. (Rudin 1966, Chapter 2)
- If $X$ is infinite, then $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is not [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091), nor is it [weakly](wikipedia://en/33662) [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space).
- The [Arzelà–Ascoli theorem](wikipedia://en/596622) holds: A subset $K$ of $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is [relatively compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_compact) if and only if it is [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) in the norm of $\mathcal{C}(X),$ and [equicontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equicontinuous).
- The [Stone–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858) holds for $\mathcal{C}(X).$ In the case of real functions, if $A$ is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of $\mathcal{C}(X)$ that contains all constants and separates points, then the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of $A$ is $\mathcal{C}(X).$ In the case of complex functions, the statement holds with the additional hypothesis that $A$ is closed under [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation).
- If $X$ and $Y$ are two compact Hausdorff spaces, and $F : \mathcal{C}(X) \to \mathcal{C}(Y)$ is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) of algebras which commutes with complex conjugation, then $F$ is continuous. Furthermore, $F$ has the form $F(h)(y) = h(f(y))$ for some continuous function $f : Y \to X.$ In particular, if $C(X)$ and $C(Y)$ are isomorphic as algebras, then $X$ and $Y$ are [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) topological spaces.
- Let $\Delta$ be the space of [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) in $\mathcal{C}(X).$ Then there is a one-to-one correspondence between Δ and the points of $X.$ Furthermore, $\Delta$ can be identified with the collection of all complex homomorphisms $\mathcal{C}(X) \to \mathbb{C}.$ Equip $\Delta$with the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123) with respect to this pairing with $\mathcal{C}(X)$ (that is, the [Gelfand transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelfand_transform)). Then $X$ is homeomorphic to Δ equipped with this topology. (Rudin 1991, §11.13(a))
- A sequence in $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is [weakly](wikipedia://en/33662) [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/6085) if and only if it is (uniformly) bounded in $\mathcal{C}(X)$ and pointwise convergent. In particular, $\mathcal{C}(X)$ is only weakly complete for $X$ a finite set.
- The [vague topology](wikipedia://en/15393951) is the [weak\* topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak*_topology) on the dual of $\mathcal{C}(X).$
- The [Banach–Alaoglu theorem](wikipedia://en/577366) implies that any normed space is isometrically isomorphic to a subspace of $C(X)$ for some $X.$

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The space $C(X)$ of real or complex-valued continuous functions can be defined on any topological space $X.$ In the non-compact case, however, $C(X)$ is not in general a Banach space with respect to the uniform norm since it may contain unbounded functions. Hence it is more typical to consider the space, denoted here $C_B(X)$ of bounded continuous functions on $X.$ This is a Banach space (in fact a commutative Banach algebra with identity) with respect to the uniform norm. (Hewitt Stromberg 1965, Theorem 7.9)

It is sometimes desirable, particularly in [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), to further refine this general definition by considering the special case when $X$ is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) Hausdorff space. In this case, it is possible to identify a pair of distinguished subsets of $C_B(X)$: (Hewitt Stromberg 1965, §II.7)

- $C_{00}(X),$ the subset of $C(X)$ consisting of functions with [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support). This is called the space of functions **vanishing in a neighborhood of infinity**.
- $C_0(X),$ the subset of $C(X)$ consisting of functions such that for every $r > 0,$ there is a compact set $K \subseteq X$ such that $|f(x)| < r$ for all $x \in X \backslash K.$ This is called the space of functions **[vanishing at infinity](wikipedia://en/461986)**.

The closure of $C_{00}(X)$ is precisely $C_0(X).$ In particular, the latter is a Banach space.
