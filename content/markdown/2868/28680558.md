# Square-integrable function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Square-integrable function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **square-integrable function**, also called a **quadratically integrable function** or **$L^2$ function** or **square-summable function**,[^o2] is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)- or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) for which the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the square of the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) is finite. Thus, square-integrability on the real line $(-\infty, +\infty)$ is defined as follows.

:::

One may also speak of quadratic integrability over bounded intervals such as $[a,b]$ for $a \leq b$.[^o4]

An equivalent definition is to say that the square of the function itself (rather than of its absolute value) is [Lebesgue integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integrable). For this to be true, the integrals of the positive and negative portions of the real part must both be finite, as well as those for the imaginary part.

The [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of (equivalence classes of) square integrable functions (with respect to [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)) forms the [$L^p$ space](wikipedia://en/45194) with $p = 2.$ Among the $L^p$ spaces, the class of square integrable functions is unique in being compatible with an [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856), which allows notions like angle and orthogonality to be defined. Along with this inner product, the square integrable functions form a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), since all of the $L^p$ spaces are [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) under their respective [$p$-norms](wikipedia://en/45194#Lp_spaces_and_Lebesgue_integrals).

Often the term is used not to refer to a specific function, but to equivalence classes of functions that are equal [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688).

## Properties {#Properties}

The square integrable functions (in the sense mentioned in which a "function" actually means an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of functions that are equal almost everywhere) form an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) with [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) given by

$$\langle f, g \rangle = \int_A \overline{f(x)}\ g(x) \mathrm dx,$$

where

- $f$ and $g$ are square integrable functions,
- $\overline{f(x)}$ is the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $f(x),$
- $A$ is the set over which one integrates—in the first definition (given in the introduction above), $A$ is $(-\infty, +\infty)$, in the second, $A$ is $[a, b]$.

Since $|a|^2 = a \cdot \overline{a}$, square integrability is the same as saying

$$\langle f, f \rangle < \infty. \,$$

It can be shown that square integrable functions form a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832) under the metric induced by the inner product defined above. A complete metric space is also called a [Cauchy space](wikipedia://en/980328), because sequences in such metric spaces converge if and only if they are [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/6085). A space that is complete under the metric induced by a norm is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989). Therefore, the space of square integrable functions is a Banach space, under the metric induced by the norm, which in turn is induced by the inner product. As we have the additional property of the inner product, this is specifically a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), because the space is complete under the metric induced by the inner product.

This inner product space is conventionally denoted by $\left(L_2, \langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle_2\right)$ and many times abbreviated as $L_2.$ Note that $L_2$ denotes the set of square integrable functions, but no selection of metric, norm or inner product are specified by this notation. The set, together with the specific inner product $\langle\cdot, \cdot\rangle_2$ specifies the inner product space.

The space of square integrable functions is the [$L^p$ space](wikipedia://en/45194) in which $p = 2.$

## Examples {#Examples}

The function $\tfrac{1}{x^n},$ defined on $(0, 1),$ is in $L^2$ for $n < \tfrac{1}{2}$ but not for $n = \tfrac{1}{2}.$[^o2] The function $\tfrac{1}{x},$ defined on $[1, \infty),$ is square-integrable.[^o10]

Bounded functions, defined on $[0, 1],$ are square-integrable. These functions are also in $L^p,$ for any value of $p.$[^o10]

::: {#o12 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

The function $\tfrac{1}{x},$ defined on $[0, 1],$ where the value at $0$ is arbitrary. Furthermore, this function is not in $L^p$ for any value of $p$ in $[1, \infty).$[^o10]
