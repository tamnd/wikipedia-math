# Well-formed formula

Mathematical formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Well-formed formula"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), and [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic), a **well-formed formula**, abbreviated **WFF** or **wff**, often simply **formula**, is a finite [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [symbols](wikipedia://en/24028642) from a given [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_%28computer_science%29), constructed following the defined grammar of a [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939).[^o2]

:::

The abbreviation **wff** is pronounced "woof", or sometimes "wiff", "weff", or "whiff".

A formal language can be identified with the set of formulas in the language. A formula is a [syntactic](wikipedia://en/546039) object that can be given a semantic [meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_semantics_%28logic%29) by means of an [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671). Two key uses of formulas are in propositional logic and predicate logic.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

A key use of formulas is in [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) and [predicate logic](wikipedia://en/562883) such as [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983). In those contexts, a formula is a string of symbols φ for which it makes sense to ask "is φ true?", once any [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable) in φ have been instantiated. In formal logic, [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) can be represented by sequences of formulas with certain properties, and the final formula in the sequence is what is proven.

Although the term "formula" may be used for written marks (for instance, on a piece of paper or chalkboard), it is more precisely understood as the sequence of symbols being expressed, with the marks being a [token](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-token_distinction) instance of formula. This distinction between the vague notion of "property" and the inductively defined notion of well-formed formula has roots in [Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544)'s 1910 paper "Über die Definitionen der mathematischen Grundbegriffe".[^o4] Thus the same formula may be written more than once, and a formula might in principle be so long that it cannot be written at all within the physical universe.

Formulas themselves are syntactic objects. They are given meanings by interpretations. For example, in a propositional formula, each propositional variable may be interpreted as a concrete proposition, so that the overall formula expresses a relationship between these propositions. A formula need not be interpreted, however, to be considered solely as a formula.

## Propositional calculus {#Propositional_calculus}

The formulas of [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus), also called [propositional formulas](wikipedia://en/1557634),[^o6] are expressions such as $(A \land (B \lor C))$. Their definition begins with the arbitrary choice of a set *V* of [propositional variables](wikipedia://en/1557562). The alphabet consists of the letters in *V* along with the symbols for the [propositional connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) and parentheses "(" and ")", all of which are assumed to not be in *V*. The formulas will be certain expressions (that is, strings of symbols) over this alphabet.

The formulas are [inductively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_definition) defined as follows:

- Each propositional variable is, on its own, a formula.
- If φ is a formula, then ¬φ is a formula.
- If φ and ψ are formulas, and • is any binary connective, then ( φ • ψ) is a formula. Here • could be (but is not limited to) the usual operators ∨, ∧, →, or ↔.

This definition can also be written as a [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) in [Backus–Naur form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backus–Naur_form), provided the set of variables is finite:

Using this grammar, the sequence of symbols

(((*p* → *q*) ∧ (*r* → *s*)) ∨ (¬*q* ∧ ¬*s*))

is a formula, because it is grammatically correct. The sequence of symbols

((*p* → *q*)→(*qq*))*p*))

is not a formula, because it does not conform to the grammar.

A complex formula may be difficult to read, owing to, for example, the proliferation of parentheses. To alleviate this last phenomenon, precedence rules (akin to the [standard mathematical order of operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_mathematical_order_of_operations)) are assumed among the operators, making some operators more binding than others. For example, assuming the precedence (from most binding to least binding) 1. ¬ 2. → 3. ∧ 4. ∨. Then the formula

(((*p* → *q*) ∧ (*r* → *s*)) ∨ (¬*q* ∧ ¬*s*))

may be abbreviated as

*p* → *q* ∧ *r* → *s* ∨ ¬*q* ∧ ¬*s*

This is, however, only a convention used to simplify the written representation of a formula. If the precedence was assumed, for example, to be left-right associative, in following order: 1. ¬ 2. ∧ 3. ∨ 4. →, then the same formula above (without parentheses) would be rewritten as

(*p* → (*q* ∧ *r*)) → (*s* ∨ (¬*q* ∧ ¬*s*))

## Predicate logic {#Predicate_logic}

The definition of a formula in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) $\mathcal{QS}$ is relative to the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28mathematical_logic%29) of the theory at hand. This signature specifies the constant symbols, predicate symbols, and function symbols of the theory at hand, along with the [arities](wikipedia://en/42301) of the function and predicate symbols.

The definition of a formula comes in several parts. First, the set of **[terms](wikipedia://en/24885593)** is defined recursively. Terms, informally, are expressions that represent objects from the [domain of discourse](wikipedia://en/229180).

1. Any variable is a term.
1. Any constant symbol from the signature is a term
1. an expression of the form *f*(*t*<sub>1</sub>,...,*t*<sub>*n*</sub>), where *f* is an *n*-ary function symbol, and *t*<sub>1</sub>,...,*t*<sub>*n*</sub> are terms, is again a term.

The next step is to define the [atomic formulas](wikipedia://en/4472066).

1. If *t*<sub>1</sub> and *t*<sub>2</sub> are terms then *t*<sub>1</sub>=*t*<sub>2</sub> is an atomic formula
1. If *R* is an *n*-ary predicate symbol, and *t*<sub>1</sub>,...,*t*<sub>*n*</sub> are terms, then *R*(*t*<sub>1</sub>,...,*t*<sub>*n*</sub>) is an atomic formula

Finally, the set of formulas is defined to be the smallest set containing the set of atomic formulas such that the following holds:

1. $\neg\phi$ is a formula when $\phi$ is a formula
1. $(\phi \land \psi)$ and $(\phi \lor \psi)$ are formulas when $\phi$ and $\psi$ are formulas;
1. $\exists x\, \phi$ is a formula when $x$ is a variable and $\phi$ is a formula;
1. $\forall x\, \phi$ is a formula when $x$ is a variable and $\phi$ is a formula (alternatively, $\forall x\, \phi$ could be defined as an abbreviation for $\neg\exists x\, \neg\phi$).

If a formula has no occurrences of $\exists x$ or $\forall x$, for any variable $x$, then it is called **quantifier-free**. An *existential formula* is a formula starting with a sequence of [existential quantification](wikipedia://en/91420) followed by a quantifier-free formula.

## Atomic and open formulas {#Atomic_and_open_formulas}

An *atomic formula* is a formula that contains no [logical connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) nor [quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_%28logic%29), or equivalently a formula that has no strict subformulas. The precise form of atomic formulas depends on the formal system under consideration; for [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), for example, the atomic formulas are the [propositional variables](wikipedia://en/1557562). For [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic), the atoms are predicate symbols together with their arguments, each argument being a [term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_rules).

According to some terminology, an *open formula* is formed by combining atomic formulas using only logical connectives, to the exclusion of quantifiers.[^o10] This is not to be confused with a formula which is not closed.

:::

::: {#o12 .statement cue=named-heading name="Closed formulas"}

## Closed formulas {#Closed_formulas}

A *closed formula*, also *[ground](wikipedia://en/2011627) formula* or *sentence*, is a formula in which there are no [free occurrences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_bound_variables) of any [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109). If **A** is a formula of a first-order language in which the variables $v_{1}, \ldots, v_{n}$ have free occurrences, then **A** preceded by $\forall v_{1} \cdots \forall v_{n}$ is a *universal closure* of **A**.

:::

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="Properties applicable to formulas"}

## Properties applicable to formulas {#Properties_applicable_to_formulas}

- A formula **A** in a language $\mathcal{Q}$ is *[valid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiability_and_validity)* if it is true for every [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671) of $\mathcal{Q}$.
- A formula **A** in a language $\mathcal{Q}$ is *[satisfiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiability_and_validity)* if it is true for some [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671) of $\mathcal{Q}$.
- A formula **A** of the language of [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic) is *decidable* if it represents a [decidable set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decidable_set), i.e. if there is an [effective method](wikipedia://en/12979890) which, given a [substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_of_variables) of the free variables of **A**, says that either the resulting instance of **A** is provable or its negation is.

:::

## Usage of the terminology {#Usage_of_the_terminology}

In earlier works on mathematical logic (e.g. by [Church](wikipedia://en/49731)[^o16]), formulas referred to any strings of symbols and among these strings, well-formed formulas were the strings that followed the formation rules of (correct) formulas.

Several authors simply say formula.[^o17][^o18][^o19][^o20] Modern usages (especially in the context of computer science with mathematical software such as [model checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_model_checking_tools), [automated theorem provers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_theorem_prover), [interactive theorem provers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactive_theorem_proving)) tend to retain of the notion of formula only the algebraic concept and to leave the question of [well-formedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-formedness), i.e. of the concrete string representation of formulas (using this or that symbol for connectives and quantifiers, using this or that [parenthesizing convention](wikipedia://en/212980), using [Polish](wikipedia://en/25056) or [infix](wikipedia://en/199014) notation, etc.) as a mere notational problem.

The expression "well-formed formulas" (WFF) also crept into popular culture. *WFF* is part of an esoteric pun used in the name of the academic game "[WFF 'N PROOF](wikipedia://en/19120279): The Game of Modern Logic", by Layman Allen,[^o21] developed while he was at [Yale Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Law_School) (he was later a professor at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan)). The suite of games is designed to teach the principles of symbolic logic to children (in [Polish notation](wikipedia://en/25056)).[^o22] Its name is an echo of *[whiffenpoof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whiffenpoof)*, a [nonsense word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsense_word) used as a [cheer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheering) at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) made popular in *The Whiffenpoof Song* and [The Whiffenpoofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Whiffenpoofs).[^o23]
