# Witch of Agnesi

*Image: Witch of Agnesi curves.svg.* Selected witch of Agnesi curves (green), and the circles they are constructed from (blue), with radius parameters $a=1$, $a=2$, $a=4$, and $a=8$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Witch of Agnesi"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **witch of Agnesi** () is a [cubic plane curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_plane_curve) defined from two diametrically opposite points of a circle.

:::

The curve was studied as early as 1653 by [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966), in 1703 by [Guido Grandi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_Grandi), and by [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627). It gets its name from Italian mathematician [Maria Gaetana Agnesi](wikipedia://en/42842830) who published it in 1748. The Italian name *la versiera di Agnesi* is based on [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *versoria* ([sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_%28sailing%29) of sailing ships) and the [sinus versus](wikipedia://en/25804104#Half-angle_formulae). This was read by [John Colson](wikipedia://en/2285149) as *l'avversiera di Agnesi*, where *avversiera* is translated as "woman who is against God" and interpreted as "witch".[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5]

The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the derivative of the [arctangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent) function forms an example of the witch of Agnesi. As the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003), the witch of Agnesi has applications in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). It also gives rise to [Runge's phenomenon](wikipedia://en/202763) in the [approximation of functions](wikipedia://en/1107596) by [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), has been used to approximate the energy distribution of [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line), and models the shape of hills.

The witch is [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to its defining circle at one of the two defining points, and [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic) to the [tangent line to the circle](wikipedia://en/19491492) at the other point. It has a unique [vertex](wikipedia://en/6865890) (a point of extreme curvature) at the point of tangency with its defining circle, which is also its [osculating circle](wikipedia://en/1896705) at that point. It also has two finite [inflection points](wikipedia://en/379845) and one infinite inflection point. The area between the witch and its asymptotic line is four times the area of the defining circle, and the [volume of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400) of the curve around its defining line is twice the volume of the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) of revolution of its defining circle.

::: {#o7 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

*Image: Witch of Agnesi, construction.svg.* The witch of Agnesi (curve *MP*) with labeled points

*Image: Agnesi.gif.* An animation showing the construction of the witch of Agnesi

To construct this curve, start with any two points *O* and *M*, and draw a circle with *OM* as diameter. For any other point *A* on the circle, let *N* be the point of intersection of the [secant line](wikipedia://en/241886) *OA* and the tangent line at *M*. Let *P* be the point of intersection of a line perpendicular to *OM* through *A*, and a line parallel to *OM* through *N*. Then *P* lies on the witch of Agnesi. The witch consists of all the points *P* that can be constructed in this way from the same choice of *O* and *M*. It includes, as a limiting case, the point *M* itself.

:::

## Equations {#Equations}

Suppose that point *O* is at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) and point *M* lies on the positive $y$-axis, and that the circle with diameter *OM* has radius $a$. Then the witch constructed from *O* and *M* has the Cartesian equation

$$y = \frac{8a^3}{x^2+4a^2} =\frac{(2a)^3}{(x)^2+(2a)^2}.$$

This equation can be simplified, by choosing $a=\tfrac12$, to the form

$$y = \frac{1}{x^2+1}.$$

or equivalently, by [clearing denominators](wikipedia://en/56260115), as the cubic [algebraic equation](wikipedia://en/404001)

$$(x^2+1)y=1.$$

In its simplified form, this curve is the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [arctangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctangent) function.

The witch of Agnesi can also be described by [parametric equations](wikipedia://en/576108) whose parameter $\theta$ is the angle between *OM* and *OA*, measured clockwise:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= 2a \tan \theta, \\ y &= 2a \cos ^2 \theta. \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The main properties of this curve can be derived from [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus). The area between the witch and its asymptotic line is four times the area of the fixed circle, $4\pi a^2$. The [volume of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_of_revolution) of the witch of Agnesi about its asymptote is $4\pi^2a^3$. This is two times the volume of the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) formed by revolving the defining circle of the witch around the same line.

The curve has a unique [vertex](wikipedia://en/6865890) at the point of tangency with its defining circle. That is, this point is the only point where the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) reaches a local minimum or local maximum. The defining circle of the witch is also its [osculating circle](wikipedia://en/1896705) at the vertex, the unique circle that "kisses" the curve at that point by sharing the same orientation and curvature. Because this is an osculating circle at the vertex of the curve, it has [third-order contact](wikipedia://en/396068) with the curve.

The curve has two [inflection points](wikipedia://en/379845), at the points

$$\left( \pm\frac{2a}{\sqrt{3}}, \frac{3a}{2}\right)$$

corresponding to the angles $\theta=\pm\pi/6$. When considered as a curve in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) there is also a third infinite inflection point, at the point where the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142) is crossed by the asymptotic line. It has an isolated [singular point](wikipedia://en/54240), at the point where the line at infinity is crossed by its axis of symmetry. As the graph of a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) $1/(x^2+1)$, it is a rational [parametric curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_curve), but not one that can be given a polynomial parameterization.

The largest area of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) that can be inscribed between the witch and its asymptote is $4a^2$, for a rectangle whose height is the radius of the defining circle and whose width is twice the diameter of the circle.

:::

## History {#History}

### Early studies {#Early_studies}

*Image: Witch of Agnesi (Agnesi, 1748).jpg.* Agnesi's 1748 illustration of the curve and its construction

The curve was studied by [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) in his 1659 treatise on [quadrature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_%28geometry%29). In it, Fermat computes the area under the curve and (without details) claims that the same method extends as well to the [cissoid of Diocles](wikipedia://en/820041). Fermat writes that the curve was suggested to him "*ab erudito geometra*" \[by a learned geometer\]. (Paradís Pla Viader 2008) speculate that the geometer who suggested this curve to Fermat might have been [Antoine de Laloubère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_de_Laloubère).

The construction given above for this curve was found by (Grandi 1718); the same construction was also found earlier by [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), but only published posthumously later, in 1779. (Grandi 1718) also suggested the name *versiera* (in Italian) or *versoria* (in Latin) for the curve.[^o16] The Latin term is also used for a [sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheet_%28sailing%29), the rope which turns the sail, but Grandi may have instead intended merely to refer to the [versine](wikipedia://en/555782) function that appeared in his construction.

In 1748, [Maria Gaetana Agnesi](wikipedia://en/42842830) published *Instituzioni analitiche ad uso della gioventù italiana*, an early textbook on [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176). In it, after first considering two other curves, she includes a study of this curve. She defines the curve geometrically as the locus of points satisfying a certain proportion, determines its algebraic equation, and finds its vertex, asymptotic line, and inflection points.

### Etymology {#Etymology}

[Maria Gaetana Agnesi](wikipedia://en/42842830) named the curve according to Grandi, *versiera*. Coincidentally, at that time in Italy it was common to speak of the [Devil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil) through other words like *aversiero* or *versiero*, derived from Latin *adversarius*, the "adversary" of God. *Versiera*, in particular, was used to indicate the wife of the devil, or "witch". Because of this, Cambridge professor [John Colson](wikipedia://en/2285149) mistranslated the name of the curve as "witch". Different modern works about Agnesi and about the curve suggest slightly different guesses how exactly this mistranslation happened. [Struik](wikipedia://en/932453) mentions that: The word \[*versiera*\] is derived from Latin *vertere*, to turn, but is also an abbreviation of Italian *avversiera*, female devil. Some wit in England once translated it 'witch', and the silly pun is still lovingly preserved in most of our textbooks in English language. ... The curve had already appeared in the writings of [Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) (*Oeuvres*, I, 279–280; III, 233–234) and of others; the name *versiera* is from Guido Grandi (*Quadratura circuli et hyperbolae*, Pisa, 1703). The curve is type 63 in [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s classification. ... The first to use the term 'witch' in this sense may have been B. Williamson, *Integral calculus*, 7 (1875), 173; see *Oxford English Dictionary*. On the other hand, [Stephen Stigler](wikipedia://en/1276686) suggests that Grandi himself "may have been indulging in a play on words", a double pun connecting the devil to the versine and the sine function to the shape of the female breast (both of which can be written as "seno" in Italian).

::: {#o19 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

A scaled version of the curve is the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003). This is the probability distribution on the [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $x$ determined by the following [random experiment](wikipedia://en/19621951): for a fixed point $p$ above the $x$-axis, choose uniformly at random a line through $p$, and let $x$ be the coordinate of the point where this random line crosses the axis. The Cauchy distribution has a peaked distribution visually resembling the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), but its [heavy tails](wikipedia://en/8092200) prevent it from having an [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) by the usual definitions, despite its symmetry. In terms of the witch itself, this means that the $x$-coordinate of the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of the region between the curve and its asymptotic line is not well-defined, despite this region's symmetry and finite area.

In [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), when approximating functions using [polynomial interpolation](wikipedia://en/203565) with equally spaced interpolation points, it may be the case for some functions that using more points creates worse approximations, so that the interpolation diverges from the function it is trying to approximate rather than converging to it. This paradoxical behavior is called [Runge's phenomenon](wikipedia://en/202763). It was first discovered by [Carl David Tolmé Runge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_David_Tolmé_Runge) for Runge's function $y=1/(1+25x^2)$, another scaled version of the witch of Agnesi, when interpolating this function over the interval $[-1,1]$. The same phenomenon occurs for the witch $y=1/(1+x^2)$ itself over the wider interval $[-5,5]$.

The witch of Agnesi approximates the [spectral energy distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_energy_distribution) of [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_lines), particularly [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) lines.

The cross-section of a smooth [hill](wikipedia://en/1408983) has a similar shape to the witch. Curves with this shape have been used as the generic topographic obstacle in a flow in mathematical modeling. [Solitary waves](wikipedia://en/51654) in deep water can also take this shape.

A version of this curve was used by [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) to derive the [Leibniz formula for $\pi$](wikipedia://en/1301559). This formula, the [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series)

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 \,-\, \frac{1}{3} \,+\, \frac{1}{5} \,-\, \frac{1}{7} \,+\, \frac{1}{9} \,-\, \cdots,$$

can be derived by equating the area under the curve with the integral of the function $1/(1+x^2)$, using the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) expansion of this function as the infinite [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) $1-x^2+x^4-x^6+\cdots$, and integrating term-by-term.

:::

## In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

*The Witch of Agnesi* is the title of a novel by Robert Spiller. It includes a scene in which a teacher gives a version of the history of the term.

*Witch of Agnesi* is also the title of a music album by jazz quartet Radius. The cover of the album features an image of the construction of the witch.
