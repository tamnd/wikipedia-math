# Linear fractional transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear fractional transformation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **linear fractional transformation** (or fractional–linear transformation/map) is, roughly speaking, an [invertible](wikipedia://en/14907) transformation of the form

:::

$$z \mapsto \frac{az + b} {cz + d}.$$

The precise definition depends on the nature of $a, b, c, d$, and $z$. In other words, a linear fractional transformation is a *[transformation](wikipedia://en/1904373)* that is represented by a *fraction* whose numerator and denominator are *[linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polynomial)*.

In the most basic setting, $a, b, c, d$, and $z$ are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (in which case the transformation is also called a [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493)), or more generally elements of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). The invertibility condition is then $ad - bc \ne 0$. Over a field, a linear fractional transformation is the [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) to the field of a [projective transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation) or [homography](wikipedia://en/3051962) of the [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578).

When $a, b, c, d$ are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) (or, more generally, belong to an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462)), $z$ is supposed to be a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) (or to belong to the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the integral domain). In this case, the invertibility condition is that $ad - bc$ must be a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) of the domain (that is $1$ or $-1$ in the case of integers).[^o2]

In the most general setting, the $a, b, c, d$ and $z$ are elements of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), such as [square matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_matrices). An example of such linear fractional transformation is the [Cayley transform](wikipedia://en/882010), which was originally defined on the 3 × 3 real [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541).

Linear fractional transformations are widely used in various areas of mathematics and its applications to engineering, such as classical [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) (they are used, for example, in [Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/21950759)), [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="General definition"}

## General definition {#General_definition}

In general, a linear fractional transformation is a [homography](wikipedia://en/3051962#Over_a_ring) of $P(A)$, the [projective line over a ring](wikipedia://en/1271901) $A$. When $A$ is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), then a linear fractional transformation has the familiar form

$$z \mapsto \frac{az + b} {cz + d},$$

where $a, b, c, d$ are elements of $A$ such that $ad - bc$ is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) of $A$ (that is $ad - bc$ has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) in $A$).

In a non-commutative ring $A$, with $(z, t)$ in $A^{2}$, the units $u$ determine an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $(z,t) \sim (uz,ut) .$ An [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) in the projective line over *A* is written $U[z : t]$, where the brackets denote [projective coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_coordinates). Then linear fractional transformations act on the right of an element of $P(A)$:

$$U[z:t] \begin{pmatrix}a & c \\ b & d \end{pmatrix} = U[za + tb:\ zc + td] \sim U[(zc + td)^{-1}(za + tb):\ 1].$$

The ring is embedded in its projective line by $z \to U[z : 1]$, so $t = 1$ recovers the usual expression. This linear fractional transformation is well-defined since $U[za + tb: zc + td]$ does not depend on which element is selected from its equivalence class for the operation.

The linear fractional transformations over $A$ form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) denoted $\operatorname{PGL}_2(A).$

The group $\operatorname{PGL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ of the linear fractional transformations is called the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019). It has been widely studied because of its numerous applications to [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), which include, in particular, [Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/21950759).

:::

## Use in hyperbolic geometry {#Use_in_hyperbolic_geometry}

In the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) a [generalized circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_circle) is either a line or a circle. When completed with the point at infinity, the generalized circles in the plane correspond to circles on the surface of the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), an expression of the complex projective line. Linear fractional transformations permute these circles on the sphere, and the corresponding finite points of the generalized circles in the complex plane.

To construct models of the hyperbolic plane the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) and the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) are used to represent the points. These subsets of the complex plane are provided a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) with the [Cayley–Klein metric](wikipedia://en/30794512). Then the distance between two points is computed using the generalized circle through the points and perpendicular to the boundary of the subset used for the model. This generalized circle intersects the boundary at two other points. All four points are used in the [cross ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_ratio) which defines the Cayley–Klein metric. Linear fractional transformations leave cross ratio invariant, so any linear fractional transformation that leaves the unit disk or upper half-planes stable is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) of the hyperbolic plane [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). Since [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) explicated these models they have been named after him: the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) and the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942). Each model has a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of isometries that is a subgroup of the [Mobius group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobius_group): the isometry group for the disk model is $\operatorname{SU}(1, 1)$ where the linear fractional transformations are "special unitary", and for the upper half-plane the isometry group is $\operatorname{PSL}(2, \mathbf{R})$, a [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) of linear fractional transformations with real entries and [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) equal to one.[^o6]

## Use in higher mathematics {#Use_in_higher_mathematics}

Möbius transformations commonly appear in the theory of [continued fractions](wikipedia://en/739199), and in [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513) of [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) and [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000), as they describe automorphisms of the upper half-plane under the action of the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019). They also provide a canonical example of [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384), where the [geodesic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_flow) induced by the linear fractional transformation decomposes complex projective space into [stable and unstable manifolds](wikipedia://en/5466649), with the [horocycles](wikipedia://en/11084460) appearing perpendicular to the geodesics. See [Anosov flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anosov_flow) for a worked example of the fibration: in this example, the geodesics are given by the linear fractional transform

$$(i \exp(t),\ 1 )\begin{pmatrix} a & c \\ b & d \end{pmatrix}\ =\ (ai\exp(t)+b, \ ci\exp(t)+d) \thicksim \left(\frac{ai\exp(t)+b}{ci\exp(t)+d},\ 1\right)$$

with $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d$ [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), with $ad - bc = 1$. Roughly speaking, the [center manifold](wikipedia://en/3948656) is generated by the [parabolic transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_transformation), the unstable manifold by the hyperbolic transformations, and the stable manifold by the elliptic transformations.

## Use in control theory {#Use_in_control_theory}

Linear fractional transformations are widely used in [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) to solve plant-controller relationship problems in [mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_engineering) and [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering).[^o9][^o10] The general procedure of combining linear fractional transformations with the [Redheffer star product](wikipedia://en/67981364) allows them to be applied to the [scattering theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_theory) of general differential equations, including the [S-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) approach in quantum mechanics and quantum field theory, the scattering of acoustic waves in media (e.g. thermoclines and submarines in oceans, etc.) and the general analysis of scattering and bound states in differential equations. Here, the 3 × 3 matrix components refer to the incoming, bound and outgoing states. Perhaps the simplest example application of linear fractional transformations occurs in the analysis of the [damped harmonic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damped_harmonic_oscillator). Another elementary application is obtaining the [Frobenius normal form](wikipedia://en/1087818), i.e. the [companion matrix](wikipedia://en/532542) of a polynomial.

## Conformal property {#Conformal_property}

*Image: Planar\_rotations.png.*

The commutative rings of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) and [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169) join the ordinary [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) as rings that express angle and "rotation". In each case the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) applied to the imaginary axis produces an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) between [one-parameter groups](wikipedia://en/754851) in $(A, + )$ and in the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) $(U, \times )$:[^o12]

$$\exp(y j) = \cosh y + j \sinh y, \quad j^2 = +1 ,$$

$$\exp(y \epsilon) = 1 + y \epsilon, \quad \epsilon^2 = 0 ,$$

$$\exp(y i) = \cos y + i \sin y, \quad i^2 = -1 .$$

The "angle" $y$ is [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981), [slope](wikipedia://en/29368), or [circular angle](wikipedia://en/1196) according to the host ring.

Linear fractional transformations are shown to be [conformal maps](wikipedia://en/50627) by consideration of their [generators](wikipedia://en/3171371): [multiplicative inversion](wikipedia://en/229940) $z \to 1/z$ and [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) $z \to az + b$. Conformality can be confirmed by showing the generators are all conformal. The translation $z \to z + b$ is a change of origin and makes no difference to angle. To see that $z \to az$ is conformal, consider the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241#Alternative_planar_decompositions) of $a$ and $z$. In each case the angle of $a$ is added to that of $z$ resulting in a conformal map. Finally, inversion is conformal since $z \to 1/z$ sends $\exp(y b) \mapsto \exp(-y b), \quad b^2 = 1, 0, -1 .$
