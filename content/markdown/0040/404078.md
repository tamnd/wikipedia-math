# Brauer group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brauer group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Brauer group** of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) whose elements are [Morita equivalence](wikipedia://en/3296134) [classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of [central simple algebras](wikipedia://en/404082) over $K$, with addition given by the [tensor product of algebras](wikipedia://en/372395). It was defined by the algebraist [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134).

:::

The Brauer group arose out of attempts to classify [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) over a field. It can also be defined in terms of [Galois cohomology](wikipedia://en/1111315). More generally, the Brauer group of a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) is defined in terms of [Azumaya algebras](wikipedia://en/3125089), or equivalently using [projective bundles](wikipedia://en/35183882).

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

A [central simple algebra](wikipedia://en/404082) (CSA) over a field $K$ is a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [associative](wikipedia://en/2112) $K$-[algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) $A$ such that $A$ is a [simple ring](wikipedia://en/443387) and the [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) of $A$ is equal to $K$. Note that CSAs are in general *not* division algebras, though CSAs can be used to classify division algebras.

For example, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$ form a CSA over themselves, but not over $\mathbb{R}$ (the center is $\mathbb{C}$ itself, hence too large to be CSA over $\mathbb{R}$). The finite-dimensional division algebras with center $\mathbb{R}$ (that means the dimension over $\mathbb{R}$ is finite) are the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) by [a theorem of Frobenius](wikipedia://en/3966889), while any [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over the reals or quaternions – $M(n,\mathbb{R})$ or $M(n,\mathbb{H})$ – is a CSA over the reals, but not a division algebra (if $n>1$).

We obtain an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on CSAs over $K$ by the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem) ([Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494)'s part, in fact), to express any CSA as a [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) $M(n,D)$ for some division algebra $D$. If we look just at $D$, that is, if we impose an equivalence relation identifying $M(m,D)$ with $M(n,D)$ for all positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $m$ and $n$, we get the **Brauer equivalence** relation on CSAs over $K$. The elements of the Brauer group are the Brauer equivalence classes of CSAs over $K$.

Given central simple algebras $A$ and $B$, one can look at their [tensor product](wikipedia://en/372395) $A\otimes B$ as a $K$-algebra. It turns out that this is always central simple. A slick way to see this is to use a characterization: a central simple algebra $A$ over $K$ is a $K$-algebra that becomes a matrix ring when we extend the field of scalars to an [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of $K$. This result also shows that the dimension of a central simple algebra $A$ as a $K$-vector space is always a [square number](wikipedia://en/213919). The **degree** of $A$ is defined to be the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of its dimension.

As a result, the [isomorphism classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class) of CSAs over $K$ form a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) under tensor product, compatible with Brauer equivalence, and the Brauer classes are all [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible): the inverse of an algebra $A$ is given by its opposite algebra $A^{\mathrm{op}}$ (the [opposite ring](wikipedia://en/3129323) with the same action by $K$ since the image of $K\to A$ is in the center of $A$). Explicitly, for a CSA $A$ we have $A\otimes A^{\mathrm{op}}=M(n^2,K)$, where $n$ is the degree of $A$ over $K$.

The Brauer group of any field is a [torsion group](wikipedia://en/3568695). In more detail, define the **period** of a central simple algebra $A$ over $K$ to be its [order](wikipedia://en/493527) as an element of the Brauer group. Define the **index** of $A$ to be the degree of the division algebra that is Brauer equivalent to $A$. Then the period of $A$ divides the index of $A$ (and hence is finite).[^n1]

## Examples {#Examples}

In the following cases, every finite-dimensional central division algebra over a field $K$ is $K$ itself, so that the Brauer group $\operatorname{Br} K$ is [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059):

- $K$ is an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018).
- $K$ is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) ([Wedderburn's theorem](wikipedia://en/7132002)).[^n2] Equivalently, every finite [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346).
- $K$ is the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) of an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) over an algebraically closed field ([Tsen's theorem](wikipedia://en/10129446)).[^n3] More generally, the Brauer group vanishes for any [quasi-algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/2934213).
- $K$ is an [algebraic extension](wikipedia://en/2125) of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing all [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity).[^n2]

The Brauer group $\operatorname{Br}\mathbb{R}$ of the real numbers is the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) two. There are just two non-[isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) real division algebras with center $\mathbb{R}$: $\mathbb{R}$ itself and the quaternion algebra $\mathbb{H}$.[^n4] Since $\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{H}\cong M(4,\mathbb{R})$, the class of $\mathbb{H}$ has order two in the Brauer group.

Let $K$ be a [non-Archimedean](wikipedia://en/264158#Definition_for_normed_fields) [local field](wikipedia://en/145375), meaning that $K$ is complete under a [discrete valuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_valuation) with finite residue field. Then $\operatorname{Br}K$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$.[^n5]

## Severi–Brauer varieties {#Severi–Brauer_varieties}

Another important interpretation of the Brauer group of a field $K$ is that it classifies the [projective varieties](wikipedia://en/320469) over $K$ that become isomorphic to [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) over an algebraic closure of $K$. Such a variety is called a [Severi–Brauer variety](wikipedia://en/1669368), and there is a [one-to-one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_correspondence) between the isomorphism classes of Severi–Brauer varieties of dimension $n-1$ over $K$ and the central simple algebras of degree $n$ over $K$.[^n6]

For example, the Severi–Brauer varieties of dimension 1 are exactly the [smooth](wikipedia://en/35033819) [conics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic) in the projective plane over $K$. For a field $K$ of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) not 2, every conic over $K$ is isomorphic to one of the form $ax^2+by^2=z^2$ for some nonzero elements $a$ and $b$ of $K$. The corresponding central simple algebra is the [quaternion algebra](wikipedia://en/2776890)[^n7]

$$(a,b) = K\langle i,j\rangle/(i^2=a, j^2=b, ij=-ji).$$

The conic is isomorphic to the projective line $\mathbb{P}^1(K)$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the corresponding quaternion algebra is isomorphic to the matrix algebra $M(2,K)$.

## Cyclic algebras {#Cyclic_algebras}

For a positive integer $n$, let $K$ be a field in which $n$ is invertible such that $K$ contains a [primitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) $n$th root of unity $\zeta$. For nonzero elements $a$ and $b$ of $K$, the associated **cyclic algebra** is the central simple algebra of degree $n$ over $K$ defined by

$$(a,b)_{\zeta} = K\langle u,v\rangle/(u^n=a, v^n=b, uv=\zeta vu).$$

Cyclic algebras are the best-understood central simple algebras. (When $n$ is not invertible in $K$ or $K$ does not have a primitive $n$th root of unity, a similar construction gives the cyclic algebra $(\chi,a)$ associated to a cyclic $\mathbb{Z}/n$-extension $\chi$ of $K$ and a nonzero element $a$ of $K$.[^n8])

The [Merkurjev–Suslin theorem](wikipedia://en/27126857#History) in [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) has a strong consequence about the Brauer group. Namely, for a positive integer $n$, let $K$ be a field in which $n$ is invertible such that $K$ contains a primitive $n$th root of unity. Then the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the Brauer group of $K$ killed by $n$ is generated by cyclic algebras of degree $n$.[^n9] Equivalently, any division algebra of period dividing $n$ is Brauer equivalent to a tensor product of cyclic algebras of degree $n$. Even for a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$, there are examples showing that a division algebra of period $p$ need not be actually isomorphic to a tensor product of cyclic algebras of degree $p$.[^n10]

It is a major [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335) (raised by [Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Adrian_Albert)) whether every division algebra of prime degree over a field is cyclic. This is true if the degree is 2 or 3, but the problem is wide open for primes at least 5. The known results are only for special classes of fields. For example, if $K$ is a [global field](wikipedia://en/288358) or [local field](wikipedia://en/145375), then a division algebra of any degree over $K$ is cyclic, by Albert–[Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134)–[Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118)–[Noether](wikipedia://en/149896).[^n11] A "higher-dimensional" result in the same direction was [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by Saltman: if $K$ is a field of [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) 1 over the local field $\mathbb{Q}_p$, then every division algebra of prime degree $\ell\ne p$ over $K$ is cyclic.[^n12]

## The period-index problem {#The_period-index_problem}

For any central simple algebra $A$ over a field $K$, the period of $A$ divides the index of $A$, and the two numbers have the same prime factors.[^n13] The **period-index problem** is to bound the index in terms of the period, for fields $K$ of interest. For example, if $A$ is a central simple algebra over a local field or global field, then Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether showed that the index of $A$ is equal to the period of $A$.[^n11]

For a central simple algebra $A$ over a field $K$ of transcendence degree $n$ over an algebraically closed field, it is [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that $\operatorname{ind}(A)$ divides $\operatorname{per}(A)^{n-1}$. This is true for $n\le 2$, the case $n=2$ being an important advance by [de Jong](wikipedia://en/29023548), sharpened in positive characteristic by de Jong–Starr and Lieblich.[^n14]

## Class field theory {#Class_field_theory}

The Brauer group plays an important role in the modern formulation of [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695). If $K_v$ is a non-Archimedean local field, [local class field theory](wikipedia://en/669532) gives a canonical isomorphism $\operatorname{inv}_v:\operatorname{Br}K_v\to\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, the [Hasse invariant](wikipedia://en/36426069).[^n2]

The case of a global field $K$ (such as a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field)) is addressed by [global class field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_class_field_theory). If $D$ is a central simple algebra over $K$ and $v$ is a [place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Place_%28mathematics%29) of $K$, then $D\otimes K_v$ is a central simple algebra over $K_v$, the completion of $K$ at $v$. This defines a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from the Brauer group of $K$ into the Brauer group of $K_v$. A given central simple algebra $D$ splits for all but finitely many $v$, so that the image of $D$ under almost all such homomorphisms is 0. The Brauer group $\operatorname{Br}K$ fits into an [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) constructed by Hasse:[^n15][^n16]

$$0 \rightarrow \operatorname{Br}K \rightarrow \bigoplus_{v \in S} \operatorname{Br}K_v \rightarrow \mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} \rightarrow 0,$$

where $S$ is the set of all places of $K$ and the right arrow is the sum of the local invariants; the Brauer group of the real numbers is identified with $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. The [injectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) of the left arrow is the content of the [Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem](wikipedia://en/23502488).

The fact that the sum of all local invariants of a central simple algebra over $K$ is zero is a typical [reciprocity law](wikipedia://en/247622#Hilbert_reciprocity). For example, applying this to a quaternion algebra $(a,b)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ gives the [quadratic reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity_law).

## Galois cohomology {#Galois_cohomology}

For an arbitrary field $K$, the Brauer group can be expressed in terms of [Galois cohomology](wikipedia://en/1111315) as follows:[^n17]

$$\operatorname{Br}K \cong H^2(K, G_\text{m}),$$

where $G_m$ denotes the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742), viewed as an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) over $K$. More concretely, the cohomology group indicated means $H^2(\operatorname{Gal}(K_s/K),K_s^*)$, where $K_s$ denotes a [separable closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_closure) of *K*.

The isomorphism of the Brauer group with a Galois cohomology group can be described as follows. The [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of the algebra of $n\times n$ [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) is the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) $\operatorname{PGL}(n)$. Since all central simple algebras over $K$ become isomorphic to the matrix algebra over a separable closure of $K$, the set of isomorphism classes of central simple algebras of degree $n$ over $K$ can be identified with the Galois cohomology set $H^1(K,\operatorname{PGL}(K))$. The class of a central simple algebra in $H^2(K,G_m)$ is the image of its class in $H^1$ under the boundary homomorphism

$$H^1(K, \operatorname{PGL}(n)) \rightarrow H^2(K, G_\text{m})$$

associated to the [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) $1\to G_m\to\operatorname{GL}(n)\to\operatorname{PGL}(n)\to 1$.

## The Brauer group of a scheme {#The_Brauer_group_of_a_scheme}

The Brauer group was generalized from fields to [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) by [Auslander](wikipedia://en/30337642) and [Goldman](wikipedia://en/37060377). [Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) went further by defining the Brauer group of any [scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_%28algebraic_geometry%29).

There are two ways of defining the Brauer group of a scheme $X$, using either [Azumaya algebras](wikipedia://en/3125089) over $X$ or [projective bundles](wikipedia://en/35183882) over $X$. The second definition involves projective bundles that are locally trivial in the [étale topology](wikipedia://en/5707590), not necessarily in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744). In particular, a projective bundle is defined to be zero in the Brauer group if and only if it is the projectivization of some vector bundle.

The **cohomological Brauer group** of a [quasi-compact](wikipedia://en/12410777) scheme $X$ is defined to be the [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) of the [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) group $H^2(X,G_m)$. (The whole group $H^2(X,G_m)$ need not be torsion, although it is torsion for [regular, integral, quasi-compact schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular,_integral,_quasi-compact_scheme) $X$.[^n18]) The Brauer group is always a subgroup of the cohomological Brauer group. [Gabber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ofer_Gabber) showed that the Brauer group is equal to the cohomological Brauer group for any scheme with an [ample line bundle](wikipedia://en/1186896) (for example, any [quasi-projective](wikipedia://en/1321785) scheme over a commutative ring).[^n19]

The whole group $H^2(X,G_m)$ can be viewed as classifying the [gerbes](wikipedia://en/822043) over $X$ with structure group $G_m$.

For smooth projective varieties over a field, the Brauer group is an important [birational](wikipedia://en/382733) invariant. For example, when $X$ is also [rationally connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationally_connected_variety) over the complex numbers, the Brauer group of $X$ is isomorphic to the torsion subgroup of the [singular cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology) group $H^3(X,\mathbb{Z})$, which is therefore a birational invariant. [Artin](wikipedia://en/1519821) and [Mumford](wikipedia://en/394594) used this description of the Brauer group to give the first example of a [unirational variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unirational_variety) $X$ over $\mathbb{C}$ that is not stably rational (that is, no product of $X$ with a projective space is rational).[^n20]

## Relation to the Tate conjecture {#Relation_to_the_Tate_conjecture}

Artin conjectured that every [proper scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_scheme) over the integers has finite Brauer group.[^n21] This is far from known even in the special case of a smooth projective variety $X$ over a finite field. Indeed, the finiteness of the Brauer group for surfaces in that case is equivalent to the [Tate conjecture](wikipedia://en/3124804) for [divisors](wikipedia://en/1693055) on $X$, one of the main problems in the theory of [algebraic cycles](wikipedia://en/9520954).[^n22]

For a regular [integral](wikipedia://en/35457783#integral) scheme of dimension 2 which is [flat](wikipedia://en/542520) and proper over the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of a number field, and which has a [section](wikipedia://en/367621), the finiteness of the Brauer group is equivalent to the finiteness of the [Tate–Shafarevich group](wikipedia://en/25711736) $\text{Ш}$ for the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) of the general fiber (a curve over a number field).[^n23] The finiteness of $\text{Ш}$ is a central problem in the arithmetic of [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) and more generally [abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/247152).

:::

## The Brauer–Manin obstruction {#The_Brauer–Manin_obstruction}

Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety over a number field $K$. The [Hasse principle](wikipedia://en/489707) would predict that if $X$ has a [rational point](wikipedia://en/2960200) over all completions $K_v$ of $K$, then $X$ has a $K$-rational point. The Hasse principle holds for some special classes of varieties, but not in general. [Manin](wikipedia://en/1692086) used the Brauer group of $X$ to define the [Brauer–Manin obstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer–Manin_obstruction), which can be applied in many cases to show that $X$ has no $K$-points even when $X$ has points over all completions of $K$.
