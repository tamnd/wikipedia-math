# Dirichlet L-function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirichlet L-function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Dirichlet *L*-series** is a function of the form

:::

$$L(s,\chi) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s},$$

where $\chi$ is a [Dirichlet character](wikipedia://en/174515) and $s$ a [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) with [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) greater than $1$. It is a special case of a [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258). By [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842), it can be extended to a [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857) on the whole [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628); it is then called a **Dirichlet *L*-function**.

These functions are named after [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) who introduced them in 1837[^o2] to prove his [theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/101453). In his proof, Dirichlet showed that $L(s,\chi)$ is non-zero at $s = 1$. Moreover, if $\chi$ is principal, then the corresponding Dirichlet *L*-function has a [simple pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_pole) at $s = 1$. Otherwise, the *L*-function is [entire](wikipedia://en/10372).

## Euler product {#Euler_product}

Since a Dirichlet character $\chi$ is [completely multiplicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_multiplicative), its *L*-function can also be written as an [Euler product](wikipedia://en/398540) in the [half-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-plane) of [absolute convergence](wikipedia://en/61532):

$$L(s,\chi)=\prod_p\left(1-\chi(p)p^{-s}\right)^{-1}\text{ for }\text{Re}(s) > 1,$$

where the product is over all [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666).[^o4]

## Primitive characters {#Primitive_characters}

Results about *L*-functions are often stated more simply if the character is assumed to be primitive, although the results typically can be extended to imprimitive characters with minor complications.[^o6] This is because of the relationship between a imprimitive character $\chi$ and the primitive character $\chi^\star$ which induces it:[^o7]

$$\chi(n) = \begin{cases} \chi^\star(n) & \mathrm{if} \gcd(n,q) = 1, \\ \;\;\;0 & \mathrm{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

(Here, $q$ is the modulus of $\chi$.) An application of the Euler product gives a simple relationship between the corresponding *L*-functions:[^o8][^o9]

$$L(s,\chi) = L(s,\chi^\star) \prod_{p \,|\, q}\left(1 - \frac{\chi^\star(p)}{p^s} \right).$$

By analytic continuation, this formula holds for all complex $s$, even though the Euler product is only valid when $\operatorname{Re}(s)>1$. The formula shows that the *L*-function of $\chi$ is equal to the *L*-function of the primitive character which induces $\chi$, multiplied by only a finite number of factors.[^o10]

As a special case, the *L*-function of the principal character $\chi_0$ modulo $q$ can be expressed in terms of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809):[^o11][^o12]

$$L(s,\chi_0) = \zeta(s) \prod_{p \,|\, q}(1 - p^{-s}).$$

## Functional equation {#Functional_equation}

Dirichlet *L*-functions satisfy a [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377), which provides a way to analytically continue them throughout the complex plane. The functional equation relates the values of $L(s,\chi)$ to the values of $L(1-s, \overline{\chi})$.

Let *$\chi$* be a primitive character modulo $q$, where $q>1$. One way to express the functional equation is as[^o16]

$$L(s,\chi) = W(\chi) 2^s \pi^{s-1} q^{1/2-s} \sin \left( \frac{\pi}{2} (s + \delta) \right) \Gamma(1-s) L(1-s, \overline{\chi}),$$

where $\Gamma$ is the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316), $\delta\in\{0,1\}$ such that $\chi(-1)=(-1)^{\delta}$, and

$$W(\chi) = \frac{\tau(\chi)}{i^{\delta}\sqrt{q}},$$

where $\tau(\chi)$ is the [Gauss sum](wikipedia://en/6378204)

$$\tau(\chi) = \sum_{a=1}^q \chi(a)\exp(2\pi ia/q).$$

It is a property of Gauss sums that $|\tau(\chi)| = \sqrt{q}$, so $|W(\chi)| = 1$.[^o14][^o15] Another functional equation is

$$\Lambda(s,\chi) = q ^{s/2} \pi^{-(s+\delta)/2} \operatorname{\Gamma}\left(\frac{s+\delta}{2}\right) L(s,\chi),$$

which can be expressed as[^o16][^o15]

$$\Lambda(s,\chi) = W(\chi) \Lambda(1-s,\overline{\chi}).$$

This implies that $L(s,\chi)$ and $\Lambda(s,\chi)$ are [entire functions](wikipedia://en/10372) of $s$. Again, this assumes that $\chi$ is primitive character modulo $q$ with $q>1$. If $q=1$, then $L(s,\chi) = \zeta(s)$ has a pole at $s=1$.[^o16][^o15]

For generalizations, see the article on [functional equations of *L*-functions](wikipedia://en/595708).

## Zeros {#Zeros}

*Image: Mplwp dirichlet beta.svg.* The Dirichlet *L*-function *L*(*s*, *χ*) = 1 − 3<sup>−*s*</sup> + 5<sup>−*s*</sup> − 7<sup>−*s*</sup> + ⋅⋅⋅ (sometimes given the special name [Dirichlet beta function](wikipedia://en/2216061)), with trivial zeros at the negative odd integers

Let $\chi$ be a primitive character modulo $q$, with $q>1$.

There are no [zeros](wikipedia://en/264210) of $L(s,\chi)$ with $\operatorname{Re}(s)>1$. For $\operatorname{Re}(s) < 0$, there are zeros at certain negative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $s$:

- If $\chi(-1) = 1$, the only zeros of $L(s,\chi)$ with $\operatorname{Re}(s) < 0$ are simple zeros at $-2,-4,-6,\dots$ There is also a zero at $s = 0$ when $\chi$ is non-principal. These correspond to the poles of $\textstyle \Gamma(\frac{s}{2})$.[^o18]
- If $\chi(-1) = -1$, then the only zeros of $L(s,\chi)$ with $\operatorname{Re}(s) < 0$ are simple zeros at $-1,-3,-5,\dots$ These correspond to the poles of $\textstyle \Gamma(\frac{s+1}{2})$.[^o18]

These are called the trivial zeros.[^o16]

The remaining zeros lie in the critical strip $0 \leq \operatorname{Re}(s) \leq 1$, and are called the non-trivial zeros. The non-trivial zeros are symmetrical about the critical line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$. That is, if $L(\rho,\chi)=0$, then $L(1-\overline{\rho},\chi)=0$ too because of the functional equation. If $\chi$ is a real character, then the non-trivial zeros are also symmetrical about the real axis, but not if $\chi$ is a complex character. The [generalized Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/152567) is the conjecture that all the non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1/2$.[^o16]

Up to the possible existence of a [Siegel zero](wikipedia://en/1967197), zero-free regions including and beyond the line $\operatorname{Re}(s) = 1$ similar to that of the Riemann zeta function are known to exist for all Dirichlet *L*-functions: for example, for $\chi$ a non-real character of modulus $q$, we have

$$\beta < 1 - \frac{c}{\log\!\!\; \big(q(2+|\gamma|)\big)} \$$

for $\beta + i\gamma$ a non-real zero.[^o19]

## Relation to the Hurwitz zeta function {#Relation_to_the_Hurwitz_zeta_function}

Dirichlet *L*-functions may be written as linear combinations of the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) at rational values. Fixing an integer $k \geq 1$, Dirichlet *L*-functions for characters modulo $k$ are linear combinations with constant coefficients of the $\zeta(s,a)$ where $a = r/k$ and *$r = 1,2,\dots,k$*. This means that the Hurwitz zeta function for rational $a$ has analytic properties that are closely related to the Dirichlet *L*-functions. Specifically, if $\chi$ is a character modulo $k$, we can write its Dirichlet *L*-function as[^o21]

$$L(s,\chi) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} = \frac{1}{k^s} \sum_{r=1}^k \chi(r) \operatorname{\zeta}\left(s,\frac{r}{k}\right).$$
