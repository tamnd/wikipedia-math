# Divisor function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Divisor function"}

*Image: Divisor.svg.* Divisor function *σ*<sub>0</sub>(*n*) up to *n* = 250

*Image: Sigma function.svg.* Sigma function *σ*<sub>1</sub>(*n*) up to *n* = 250

*Image: Divisor square.svg.* Sum of the squares of divisors, *σ*<sub>2</sub>(*n*), up to *n* = 250

*Image: Divisor cube.svg.* Sum of cubes of divisors, *σ*<sub>3</sub>(*n*) up to *n* = 250

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and specifically in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **divisor function** is an [arithmetic function](wikipedia://en/3170) related to the [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). When referred to as *the* divisor function, it counts the *number of divisors of an integer* (including 1 and the number itself). It appears in a number of remarkable identities, including relationships on the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) and the [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458) of [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000). Divisor functions were studied by [Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan), who gave a number of important [congruences](wikipedia://en/20087) and [identities](wikipedia://en/298428); these are treated separately in the article [Ramanujan's sum](wikipedia://en/5884024).

:::

A related function is the [divisor summatory function](wikipedia://en/5943744), which, as the name implies, is a sum over the divisor function.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The **sum of positive divisors function** *σ*<sub>*z*</sub>(*n*), for a real or complex number *z*, is defined as the [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) of the *z*th [powers](wikipedia://en/99491) of the positive [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of *n*. It can be expressed in [sigma notation](wikipedia://en/246160#Capital-sigma_notation) as

$$\sigma_z(n)=\sum_{d\mid n} d^z\,\! ,$$

where ${d\mid n}$ is shorthand for "*d* [divides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divides) *n*". The notations *d*(*n*), *ν*(*n*) and *τ*(*n*) (for the German *Teiler* = divisors) are also used to denote *σ*<sub>0</sub>(*n*), or the **number-of-divisors function**[^o4][^o5] (). When *z* is 1, the function is called the **sigma function** or **sum-of-divisors function**,[^o4][^o6] and the subscript is often omitted, so *σ*(*n*) is the same as *σ*<sub>1</sub>(*n*) ().

The **[aliquot sum](wikipedia://en/12374274)** *s*(*n*) of *n* is the sum of the [proper divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_divisor) (that is, the divisors excluding *n* itself, ), and equals *σ*<sub>1</sub>(*n*) − *n*; the [aliquot sequence](wikipedia://en/486266) of *n* is formed by repeatedly applying the aliquot sum function.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

For example, *σ*<sub>0</sub>(12) is the number of the divisors of 12:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_0(12) & = 1^0 + 2^0 + 3^0 + 4^0 + 6^0 + 12^0 \\ & = 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 6, \end{aligned}$$

while *σ*<sub>1</sub>(12) is the sum of all the divisors:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_1(12) & = 1^1 + 2^1 + 3^1 + 4^1 + 6^1 + 12^1 \\ & = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 28, \end{aligned}$$

and the aliquot sum s(12) of proper divisors is:

$$\begin{aligned} s(12) & = 1^1 + 2^1 + 3^1 + 4^1 + 6^1 \\ & = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 = 16. \end{aligned}$$

*σ*<sub>−1</sub>(*n*) is sometimes called the [abundancy index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundancy_index) of *n*, and we have:

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_{-1}(12) & = 1^{-1} + 2^{-1} + 3^{-1} + 4^{-1} + 6^{-1} + 12^{-1} \\[6pt] & = \tfrac11 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac1{12} \\[6pt] & = \tfrac{12}{12} + \tfrac6{12} + \tfrac4{12} + \tfrac3{12} + \tfrac2{12} + \tfrac1{12} \\[6pt] & = \tfrac{12 + 6 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1}{12} = \tfrac{28}{12} = \tfrac73 = \tfrac{\sigma_1(12)}{12} \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas at prime powers"}

### Formulas at prime powers {#Formulas_at_prime_powers}

For a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*,

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_0(p) & = 2 \\ \sigma_0(p^n) & = n+1 \\ \sigma_1(p) & = p+1 \end{aligned}$$

because by definition, the factors of a prime number are 1 and itself. Clearly, $1 < \sigma_0(n) < n$ for all $n > 2$, and $\sigma_x(n) > n$ for all $n > 1$, $x > 0$.

In general, since the divisors of a prime power $p^a$, with $a$ a natural number, are $1=p^0,p=p^1,p^2,\ldots,p^a$, then

$$\sigma_x(p^a)=1^x+p^x+p^{2x}+\ldots+p^{ax}$$

:::

::: {#o16 .statement cue=named-heading name="General formula"}

### General formula {#General_formula}

If $n = \prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{a_i}$, where *r* = *ω*(*n*) is the [number of distinct prime factors](wikipedia://en/57197043) of *n*, *p<sub>i</sub>* is the *i*th prime factor, and *a<sub>i</sub>* is the maximum power of *p<sub>i</sub>* by which *n* is [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisible), then we have: [^n1]

$$\sigma_x(n) = \prod_{i=1}^r \sum_{j=0}^{a_i} p_i^{j x} = \prod_{i=1}^r \left (1 + p_i^x + p_i^{2x} + \cdots + p_i^{a_i x} \right ).$$

which, when *x* ≠ 0, is equivalent to: [^n1]

$$\sigma_x(n) = \prod_{i=1}^{r} \frac{p_{i}^{(a_{i}+1)x}-1}{p_{i}^x-1}.$$

When *x* = 0, $\sigma_0(n)$ is: [^n1]

$$\sigma_0(n)=\prod_{i=1}^r (a_i+1).$$

::: {#o20 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** The proof is [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/741875) in nature. Distributing (expanding) the product

$$\prod_{i=1}^r \left (1 + p_i^x + p_i^{2x} + \cdots + p_i^{a_i x} \right )$$

gives a sum in which each summand is a product. In the products there is one factor per parentheses, and each is one of the summands in the parentheses. Each such selection gives a different summand. Each such selection is equivalent to select, for each $i=1,2,\ldots,r$, a value $0\leq x_i\leq a_i$ which will represent selecting the summand $p_i^{x_ix}$ from the $i$-th parentheses. On the other hand, this is the same collection of choices needed to select a divisor of $n$, and write it as its prime factorization. This is because each divisor of $n$ can be written uniquely in the form $\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{x_i}$, for $0\leq x_i\leq a_i$.

For example, the divisors of $24=2^3\cdot 3^1$ are $2^0\cdot 3^0, 2^0\cdot 3^1, 2^1\cdot 3^0, 2^1\cdot 3^1, 2^2\cdot 3^0, 2^2\cdot 3^1, 2^3\cdot 3^0, 2^3\cdot 3^1$. These are the same summands obtained when expanding

$$(2^0+2^1+2^2+2^3)(3^0+3^1)$$

A direct consequence of the formula is that the function $n\mapsto\sigma_x(n)$ is [multiplicative](wikipedia://en/20059). In fact, if $a,b$ are relatively prime, then they have prime factorizations $\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{a_i}$ and $\prod_{i=r+1}^s p_i^{a_i}$ in which the set of primes $\{p_i\}_{i=1}^{r}$ and $\{p_i\}_{i=r+1}^s$ are disjoint. Then

$$\sigma_x(ab)=\prod_{i=1}^s \sum_{j=0}^{a_ix}p_i^{jx}=\prod_{i=1}^r \sum_{j=0}^{a_ix} p_i^{jx}\cdot\prod_{i=r+1}^s \sum_{j=0}^{a_ix} p_i^{jx}=\sigma_x(a)\sigma_x(b)$$

:::

:::

### Other properties and identities {#Other_properties_and_identities}

[Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler) proved the remarkable recurrence:[^o22][^o23][^o24]

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma_1(n) &= \sigma_1(n-1)+\sigma_1(n-2)-\sigma_1(n-5)-\sigma_1(n-7)+\sigma_1(n-12)+\sigma_1(n-15)+ \cdots \\[12mu] &= \sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}} (-1)^{i+1}\left( \sigma_1 \left( n-\frac{1}{2} \left( 3i^2-i \right) \right) + \sigma_1 \left( n-\frac{1}{2} \left( 3i^2+i \right) \right) \right), \end{aligned}$$

where $\sigma_1(0)=n$ if it occurs and $\sigma_1(x)=0$ for $x < 0$, and $\tfrac{1}{2} \left( 3i^2 \mp i \right)$ are consecutive pairs of generalized [pentagonal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentagonal_numbers) (, starting at offset 1). Indeed, Euler proved this by logarithmic differentiation of the identity in his [pentagonal number theorem](wikipedia://en/340294).

For a non-square integer, *n*, every divisor, *d*, of *n* is paired with divisor *n*/*d* of *n* and $\sigma_{0}(n)$ is even; for a square integer, one divisor (namely $\sqrt n$) is not paired with a distinct divisor and $\sigma_{0}(n)$ is odd. Similarly, the number $\sigma_{1}(n)$ is odd if and only if *n* is a square or twice a square.[^n2]

We also note *s*(*n*) = *σ*(*n*) − *n*. Here *s*(*n*) denotes the sum of the *proper* divisors of *n*, that is, the divisors of *n* excluding *n* itself. This function is used to recognize [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670), which are the *n* such that *s*(*n*) = *n*. If *s*(*n*) > *n*, then *n* is an [abundant number](wikipedia://en/321831), and if *s*(*n*) \< *n*, then *n* is a [deficient number](wikipedia://en/321843).

If $n$ is a power of 2, $n = 2^k$, then $\sigma(n) = 2 \cdot 2^k - 1 = 2n - 1$ and $s(n) = n - 1$, which makes *n* [almost-perfect](wikipedia://en/322008).

As an example, for two primes $p,q:p<q$, let

$$n = p\,q.$$

Then

$$\sigma(n) = (p+1)(q+1) = n + 1 + (p+q),$$

$$\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1) = n + 1 - (p+q),$$

and

$$n + 1 = (\sigma(n) + \varphi(n))/2,$$

$$p + q = (\sigma(n) - \varphi(n))/2,$$

where $\varphi(n)$ is [Euler's totient function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_phi).

Then, the roots of

$$(x-p)(x-q) = x^2 - (p+q)x + n = x^2 - [(\sigma(n) - \varphi(n))/2]x + [(\sigma(n) + \varphi(n))/2 - 1] = 0$$

express *p* and *q* in terms of *σ*(*n*) and *φ*(*n*) only, requiring no knowledge of *n* or $p+q$, as

$$p = (\sigma(n) - \varphi(n))/4 - \sqrt{[(\sigma(n) - \varphi(n))/4]^2 - [(\sigma(n) + \varphi(n))/2 - 1]},$$

$$q = (\sigma(n) - \varphi(n))/4 + \sqrt{[(\sigma(n) - \varphi(n))/4]^2 - [(\sigma(n) + \varphi(n))/2 - 1]}.$$

Also, knowing $n$ and either $\sigma(n)$ or $\varphi(n)$, or, alternatively, $p+q$ and either $\sigma(n)$ or $\varphi(n)$ allows an easy recovery of *p* and *q*.

In 1984, [Roger Heath-Brown](wikipedia://en/2984550) proved that the equality

$$\sigma_0(n) = \sigma_0(n + 1)$$

is true for infinitely many values of $n$, see .

## Series relations {#Series_relations}

Two [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258) involving the divisor function are: [^n3]

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\sigma_{a}(n)}{n^s} = \zeta(s) \zeta(s-a)\quad\text{for}\quad \Re(s)>1+\max\{\Re(a),0\},$$

where $\zeta$ is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). The series for *d*(*n*) = *σ*<sub>0</sub>(*n*) gives: [^n3]

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{d(n)}{n^s} = \zeta^2(s)\quad\text{for}\quad \Re(s)>1,$$

and a [Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan) identity[^n4]

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\sigma_a(n)\sigma_b(n)}{n^s} = \frac{\zeta(s) \zeta(s-a) \zeta(s-b) \zeta(s-a-b)}{\zeta(2s-a-b)},$$

which is a special case of the [Rankin–Selberg convolution](wikipedia://en/27989406).

A [Lambert series](wikipedia://en/1367040) involving the divisor function is: [^n5]

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty q^n \sigma_a(n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \sum_{j=1}^\infty n^a q^{j\,n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{n^a q^n}{1-q^n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \operatorname{Li}_{-a}(q^n)$$

for arbitrary [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) |*q*| ≤ 1 and *a* ($\operatorname{Li}$ is the [polylogarithm](wikipedia://en/482471)). This summation also appears as the [Fourier series of the Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458#Fourier_series) and the [invariants of the Weierstrass elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_elliptic_functions#Invariants).

For $k>0$, there is an explicit series representation with [Ramanujan sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan_sum) $c_m(n)$ as :[^o32]

$$\sigma_k(n) = \zeta(k+1)n^k\sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac {c_m(n)}{m^{k+1}}.$$

The computation of the first terms of $c_m(n)$ shows its oscillations around the "average value" $\zeta(k+1)n^k$:

$$\sigma_k(n) = \zeta(k+1)n^k \left[ 1 + \frac{(-1)^n}{2^{k+1}} + \frac{2\cos\frac {2\pi n}{3}}{3^{k+1}} + \frac{2\cos\frac {\pi n}{2}}{4^{k+1}} + \cdots\right]$$

## Growth rate {#Growth_rate}

In [little-o notation](wikipedia://en/44578#Little-o_notation), the divisor function satisfies the inequality:[^n6][^n7]

$$\mbox{for all }\varepsilon>0,\quad d(n)=o(n^\varepsilon).$$

More precisely, [Severin Wigert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severin_Wigert) showed that:[^n7]

$$\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log d(n)}{\log n/\log\log n}=\log2,$$

approached by $n$ taking the [primorials](wikipedia://en/422786) since

$$p_k\# = e^{(1 + o(1)) k \log k}.$$

On the other hand, since [there are infinitely many prime numbers](wikipedia://en/3143591),[^n7]

$$\liminf_{n\to\infty} d(n)=2.$$

In [Big-O notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big-O_notation), [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) showed that the [average order](wikipedia://en/18760872) of the divisor function satisfies the following inequality:[^n8][^n9]

$$\mbox{for all } x\geq1, \sum_{n\leq x}d(n)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(\sqrt{x}),$$

where $\gamma$ is [Euler's gamma constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant). Improving the bound $O(\sqrt{x})$ in this formula is known as [Dirichlet's divisor problem](wikipedia://en/5943744#Dirichlet's_divisor_problem).

The behaviour of the sigma function is irregular. The asymptotic growth rate of the sigma function can be expressed by: [^n10]

$$\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sigma(n)}{n\,\log \log n}=e^\gamma,$$

where lim sup is the [limit superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_superior). This result is **[Grönwall](wikipedia://en/3849173)'s theorem**, published in 1913 (Grönwall 1913). His proof uses [Mertens' third theorem](wikipedia://en/982970), which says that:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log n}\prod_{p\le n}\frac{p}{p-1}=e^\gamma,$$

where *p* denotes a prime. Grönwall also showed that

$$\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sigma_a(n)}{n^a}=\zeta(a),\quad a>1,$$

where $\zeta$ is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).

In 1915, Ramanujan proved that under the assumption of the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125), Robin's inequality

$\ \sigma(n) < e^\gamma n \log \log n$ (where γ is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant))

holds for all sufficiently large *n* (Ramanujan 1997). The largest known value that violates the inequality is *n*=[5040](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5040_%28number%29). In 1984, Guy Robin proved that the inequality is true for all *n* > 5040 [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the Riemann hypothesis is true (Robin 1984). This is **Robin's theorem** and the inequality became known after him. Robin furthermore showed that if the Riemann hypothesis is false then there are an infinite number of values of *n* that violate the inequality, and it is known that the smallest such *n* > 5040 must be [superabundant](wikipedia://en/1759187) (Akbary Friggstad 2009). It has been shown that the inequality holds for large odd and square-free integers, and that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the inequality just for *n* divisible by the fifth power of a prime (Choie Lichiardopol Moree Solé 2007).

Robin also proved, unconditionally, that the inequality:

$$\ \sigma(n) < e^\gamma n \log \log n + \frac{0.6483\ n}{\log \log n}$$

holds for all *n* ≥ 3.

A related bound was given by [Jeffrey Lagarias](wikipedia://en/4327781) in 2002, who proved that the Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that:

$$\sigma(n) < H_n + e^{H_n}\log(H_n)$$

for every [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *n* > 1, where $H_n$ is the *n*th [harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729), (Lagarias 2002).
