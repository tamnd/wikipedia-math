# Étienne Bazeries

*Image: Les Chiffres Secrets Devoiles, by Etienne Bazeries, 1901, with illustration of Bazeries's Cylindrical Cryptograph - National Cryptologic Museum - DSC07723.JPG.* *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés* (or *The Secret Numbers Unveiled* )by Étienne Bazeries

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Étienne Bazeries"}

**Étienne Bazeries** (21 August 1846, in Port Vendres – 7 November 1931, in Noyon) was a French military [cryptanalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalyst) active between 1890 and the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I). He is best known for developing the "[Bazeries Cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bazeries_Cylinder)", an improved version of [Thomas Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson)'s [cipher](wikipedia://en/5244) cylinder. It was later refined into the US Army [M-94](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-94_%28cipher_machine%29) cipher device. Historian [David Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Kahn_%28writer%29) describes him as "*the great pragmatist of cryptology. His theoretical contributions are negligible, but he was one of the greatest natural cryptanalysts the science has seen.*" (Kahn 1996, p244)

:::

Bazeries was born in [Port-Vendres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port-Vendres), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France), the son of a mounted [policeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policeman). In 1863 he enlisted in the army, and fought in the [Franco-Prussian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War), where he was taken [prisoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner_of_war), although he later managed to escape disguised as a [bricklayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bricklayer). In 1874 he was promoted to [lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant), and sent to [Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algeria) in 1875. He returned to France the following year and married Marie-Louise-Elodie Berthon, with whom he would father three daughters: Césarine, Fernande and Paule.

He apparently became interested in cryptography through solving [cryptograms](wikipedia://en/69794) in newspapers' personal columns, and soon applied his cryptanalytic skills in a military context when, in 1890, he solved messages enciphered with the official French military [transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposition_%28cipher%29) system, causing the War Ministry to change to a new scheme. In an effort to prompt reform within the government and enhance national security, Bazeries further exposed weaknesses in French cipher systems.[^o3] In 1891, news of his talent had spread, and he began work for the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' [Bureau du Chiffre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_du_Chiffre). Bazeries continued his cryptanalytic work there even after he retired from the Army in 1899, assisting in solving German military ciphers during [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I). However, many of Bazeries recommendations to the government for improvements in official cipher systems met severe bureaucracy and rebuffs, which became a constant source of frustration for him in an otherwise illustrious career.[^o4] He retired in 1924, aged 78.

In the 1890s he broke a famous nomenclator system called the "[Great Cipher](wikipedia://en/606309)", created by the [Rossignols](wikipedia://en/1627110) in the 17th century. One of the messages referred to the famous [Man in the Iron Mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_in_the_Iron_Mask) and provided a possible solution to the mystery. His influential 1901 text *Les Chiffres secrets dévoilés* ("Secret ciphers unveiled") is considered a landmark in [cryptographic](wikipedia://en/18934432) literature.
