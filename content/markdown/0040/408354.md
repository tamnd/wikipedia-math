# Taxicab geometry

*Image: Manhattan distance.svg.* In taxicab geometry, the lengths of the red, blue, green, and yellow paths all equal $12$, the taxicab distance between the opposite corners, and all four paths are shortest paths. Instead, in Euclidean geometry, the red, blue, and yellow paths still have length $12$ but the green path is the unique shortest path, with length equal to the Euclidean distance between the opposite corners, $6\surd2 \approx 8.49$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Taxicab geometry"}

**Taxicab geometry** or **Manhattan geometry** is [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) where the familiar [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) is ignored, and the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) between two [points](wikipedia://en/593693) is instead defined to be the sum of the [absolute differences](wikipedia://en/28021681) of their respective [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate), a distance function (or [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29)) called the *taxicab distance*, *Manhattan distance*, or *city block distance*. The name refers to the island of [Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan), or generically any planned city with a [rectangular grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_grid) of streets, in which a [taxicab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxi) can only travel along grid directions. In taxicab geometry, the distance between any two points equals the length of their shortest grid path. This different definition of distance also leads to a different definition of the length of a curve, for which a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) between any two points has the same length as a grid path between those points rather than its Euclidean length.

:::

The taxicab distance is also sometimes known as *rectilinear distance* or $L^{1}$ distance (see [*L<sup>p</sup>* space](wikipedia://en/45194)).[^o2] This geometry has been used in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) since the 18th century, and is often referred to as [LASSO](wikipedia://en/22349350). Its geometric interpretation dates to [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) of the 19th century and is due to [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537).

In the two-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) $\mathbb{R}^2$, the taxicab distance between two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is $\left|x_1 - x_2\right| + \left|y_1 - y_2\right|$. That is, it is the sum of the [absolute values](wikipedia://en/991) of the differences in both coordinates.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

The taxicab distance, $d_\text{T}$, between two points $\mathbf{p} = (p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n)$ and $\mathbf{q} = (q_1, q_2, \dots, q_n)$ in an *n*-dimensional [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) with fixed [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), is the sum of the lengths of the projections of the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) between the points onto the [coordinate axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_axes). More formally,

$$d_\text{T}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q}) = \left\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{q}\right\|_\text{T} = \sum_{i=1}^n \left|p_i - q_i\right|$$

For example, in $\mathbb{R}^2$, the taxicab distance between $\mathbf{p} = (p_1,p_2)$ and $\mathbf{q} = (q_1,q_2)$ is $\left| p_1 - q_1 \right| + \left| p_2 - q_2 \right|.$

:::

## History {#History}

The *L*<sup>1</sup> metric was used in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997), as a measure of [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821), in 1757 by [Roger Joseph Boscovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Joseph_Boscovich).[^o6] The interpretation of it as a distance between points in a geometric space dates to the late 19th century and the development of [non-Euclidean geometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometries). Notably it appeared in 1910 in the works of both [Frigyes Riesz](wikipedia://en/1073126) and [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537). The formalization of [*L*<sup>*p*</sup> spaces](wikipedia://en/45194), which include taxicab geometry as a special case, is credited to Riesz.[^o7] In developing the [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153), [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) established his [Minkowski inequality](wikipedia://en/192022), stating that these spaces define [normed vector spaces](wikipedia://en/21538).[^o8]

The name *taxicab geometry* was introduced by [Karl Menger](wikipedia://en/149431) in a 1952 booklet *You Will Like Geometry*, accompanying a geometry exhibit intended for the general public at the [Museum of Science and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Science_and_Industry_%28Chicago%29) in Chicago.[^o9]

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Thought of as an additional structure layered on [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), taxicab distance depends on the [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) of the coordinate system and is changed by Euclidean [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789) of the space, but is unaffected by [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) or axis-aligned [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829). Taxicab geometry satisfies all of [Hilbert's axioms](wikipedia://en/838151) (a formalization of [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417)) except that the congruence of angles cannot be defined to precisely match the Euclidean concept, and under plausible definitions of congruent taxicab angles, the [side-angle-side axiom](wikipedia://en/39330#Determining_congruence) is not satisfied as in general triangles with two taxicab-congruent sides and a taxicab-congruent angle between them are not [congruent triangles](wikipedia://en/39330#Congruence_of_triangles).

### Spheres {#Spheres}

*Image: Centered\_octahedral\_number\_lattice.svg.* 3D balls of radii 1 (red) and 2 (blue) are [regular octahedrons](wikipedia://en/22458): the number of integer lattice points enclosed form the [centered octahedral numbers](wikipedia://en/39529359)

*Image: TaxicabGeometryCircle.svg.* Progressively finer grid points on a circle in taxicab geometry

In any [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859#Metric_spaces) is a set of points at a fixed distance, the *[radius](wikipedia://en/1780815)*, from a specific *[center](wikipedia://en/4850775)* point. Whereas a Euclidean sphere is round and rotationally symmetric, under the taxicab distance, the shape of a sphere is a [cross-polytope](wikipedia://en/716401), the *n*-dimensional generalization of a [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458), whose points $\mathbf{p}$ satisfy the equation:

$$d_\text{T}(\mathbf p, \mathbf c) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left|p_i - c_i\right| = r,$$

where $\mathbf{c}$ is the center and *r* is the radius. Points $\mathbf{p}$ on the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090), a sphere of radius 1 centered at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432), satisfy the equation $d_\text{T}(\mathbf p, \mathbf 0) = \sum_{i=1}^n |p_i| = 1.$

In two dimensional taxicab geometry, the sphere (called a *[circle](wikipedia://en/6220)*) is a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) oriented diagonally to the coordinate axes. The image to the right shows in red the set of all points on a square grid with a fixed distance from the blue center. As the grid is made finer, the red points become more numerous, and in the limit tend to a continuous tilted square. Each side has taxicab length 2*r*, so the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) is 8*r*. Thus, in taxicab geometry, the value of the analog of the circle constant [π](wikipedia://en/23601), the ratio of circumference to [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007), is equal to 4.

A closed *[ball](wikipedia://en/160556#In_general_metric_spaces)* (or closed *[disk](wikipedia://en/329542)* in the 2-dimensional case) is a filled-in sphere, the set of points at distance less than or equal to the radius from a specific center. For [cellular automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_automata) on a square grid, a taxicab [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) is the [von Neumann neighborhood](wikipedia://en/8485219) of range *r* of its center.

A circle of radius *r* for the [Chebyshev distance](wikipedia://en/1503963) ([L<sub>∞</sub> metric](wikipedia://en/45194)) on a plane is also a square with side length 2*r* parallel to the coordinate axes, so planar Chebyshev distance can be viewed as equivalent by rotation and scaling to planar taxicab distance. However, this equivalence between L<sub>1</sub> and L<sub>∞</sub> metrics does not generalize to higher dimensions.

Whenever each pair in a collection of these circles has a nonempty intersection, there exists an intersection point for the whole collection; therefore, the Manhattan distance forms an [injective metric space](wikipedia://en/7840768).

### Arc length {#Arc_length}

Let $y = f(x)$ be a [continuously differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) function. Let $s$ be the taxicab [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) of the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of $f$ on some interval $[a,b]$. Take a [partition](wikipedia://en/471332) of the interval into equal infinitesimal subintervals, and let $\Delta s_i$ be the taxicab length of the $i$-th subarc. Then[^o14]

$$\Delta s_i = \Delta x_i + \Delta y_i = \Delta x_i+ |f(x_i) - f(x_{i-1})|.$$

By the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662), there exists some point $x^*_i$ between $x_i$ and $x_{i-1}$ such that $f(x_i) - f(x_{i-1}) = f'(x^*_i) \Delta x_i$.[^o15] Then the previous equation can be written

$$\Delta s_i = \Delta x_i + |f'(x^*_i)| \, \Delta x_i = \Delta x_i(1+|f'(x^*_i)|).$$

*Image: Three monotonically decreasing curves with same endpoints.png.* Curves defined by monotonically increasing or decreasing functions have the same taxicab arc length as long as they share the same endpoints.

Then $s$ is given as the sum of every partition of $s$ on $[a,b]$ as they get [arbitrarily small](wikipedia://en/1118832):

$$\begin{aligned} s &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^n \Delta x_i(1+|f'(x^*_i)|) \\ & = \int_a^b 1+|f'(x)| \,dx. \end{aligned}$$

To test this, take the taxicab circle of [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) $r$ centered at the origin. Its curve in the first [quadrant](wikipedia://en/18315778) is given by $f(x)=-x+r$ whose length is

$$s = \int_0^r 1+\left|-1\right| \, dx = 2r .$$

Multiplying this value by $4$ to account for the remaining quadrants gives $8r$, which agrees with the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of a taxicab circle.[^o16] Now take the [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417) circle of radius $r$ centered at the origin, which is given by $f(x) = \sqrt{r^2-x^2}$. Its arc length in the first quadrant is given by

$$\begin{aligned} s &= \int_0^r 1 + \left|\frac{-x}{\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}}\right| \, dx\\[6pt] &= \left. x + \sqrt{r^2-x^2} \vphantom{\frac11} \right|_0^r \\[6pt] &= r-(-r)\\[6pt] &= 2r \end{aligned}$$

Accounting for the remaining quadrants gives $4 \times 2r = 8r$ again. Therefore, the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of the taxicab circle and the [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417) circle in the taxicab [metric](wikipedia://en/20018) are equal.[^o17] In fact, for any function $f$ that is monotonic and [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) with a continuous [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) over an interval $[a,b]$, the arc length of $f$ over $[a, b]$ is $(b-a) + \left| f(b)-f(a) \right|$.[^o18]

### Triangle congruence {#Triangle_congruence}

*Image: Congruencetriangletaxicab.png.* Two taxicab right isoceles triangles. Three angles and two legs are congruent, but the triangles are not congruent. Therefore, ASASA is not a congruence theorem in taxicab geometry.

Two triangles are congruent if and only if three corresponding sides are equal in distance and three corresponding angles are equal in measure. There are several theorems that guarantee [triangle congruence](wikipedia://en/39330) in Euclidean geometry, namely Angle-Angle-Side (AAS), Angle-Side-Angle (ASA), Side-Angle-Side (SAS), and Side-Side-Side (SSS). In taxicab geometry, however, only SASAS guarantees triangle congruence.[^o20]

Take, for example, two right isosceles taxicab triangles whose angles measure 45-90-45. The two legs of both triangles have a taxicab length 2, but the [hypotenuses](wikipedia://en/251366) are not congruent. This counterexample eliminates AAS, ASA, and SAS. It also eliminates AASS, AAAS, and even ASASA. Having three congruent angles and two sides does not guarantee triangle congruence in taxicab geometry. Therefore, the only triangle congruence theorem in taxicab geometry is SASAS, where all three corresponding sides must be congruent and at least two corresponding angles must be congruent.[^o21] This result is mainly due to the fact that the length of a line segment depends on its orientation in taxicab geometry.

## Applications {#Applications}

### Compressed sensing {#Compressed_sensing}

In solving an [underdetermined system](wikipedia://en/15890311) of linear equations, the [regularization](wikipedia://en/2009061) term for the parameter vector is expressed in terms of the $\ell_1$ norm (taxicab geometry) of the vector.[^o25] This approach appears in the signal recovery framework called [compressed sensing](wikipedia://en/11403316).

### Differences of frequency distributions {#Differences_of_frequency_distributions}

Taxicab geometry can be used to assess the differences in discrete frequency distributions. For example, in [RNA splicing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA_splicing) positional distributions of [hexamers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexamers), which plot the probability of each hexamer appearing at each given [nucleotide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleotide) near a splice site, can be compared with L1-distance. Each position distribution can be represented as a vector where each entry represents the likelihood of the hexamer starting at a certain nucleotide. A large L1-distance between the two vectors indicates a significant difference in the nature of the distributions while a small distance denotes similarly shaped distributions. This is equivalent to measuring the area between the two distribution curves because the area of each segment is the absolute difference between the two curves' likelihoods at that point. When summed together for all segments, it provides the same measure as L1-distance.[^o27]
