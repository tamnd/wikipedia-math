# Debye function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Debye function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the family of **Debye functions** is defined by

$$D_n(x) = \frac{n}{x^n} \int_0^x \frac{t^n}{e^t - 1}\,dt.$$

:::

The functions are named in honor of [Peter Debye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Debye), who came across this function (with *n* = 3) in 1912 when he analytically computed the [heat capacity](wikipedia://en/194227) of what is now called the [Debye model](wikipedia://en/371968).

## Mathematical properties {#Mathematical_properties}

### Relation to other functions {#Relation_to_other_functions}

The Debye functions are closely related to the [polylogarithm](wikipedia://en/482471).

### Series expansion {#Series_expansion}

They have the series expansion[^o5]

$$D_n(x) = 1 - \frac{n}{2(n+1)} x + n \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{B_{2k}}{(2k+n)(2k)!} x^{2k}, \quad |x| < 2\pi,\ n \ge 1,$$

where $B_n$ is the $n$-th [Bernoulli number](wikipedia://en/4964).

### Limiting values {#Limiting_values}

$$\lim_{x \to 0} D_n(x) = 1.$$

If $\Gamma$ is the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) and $\zeta$ is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809), then, for $x \gg 0$,[^o7]

$$D_n(x) = \frac{n}{x^n} \int_0^x \frac{t^n\,dt}{e^t-1} \sim \frac{n}{x^n}\Gamma(n + 1) \zeta(n + 1), \qquad \operatorname{Re} n > 0,$$

### Derivative {#Derivative}

The derivative obeys the relation

$$x D^{\prime}_n(x) = n \left(B(x) - D_n(x)\right),$$

where $B(x) = x/(e^x-1)$ is the Bernoulli function.

## Applications in solid-state physics {#Applications_in_solid-state_physics}

### The Debye model {#The_Debye_model}

The [Debye model](wikipedia://en/371968) has a [density of vibrational states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_of_states)

$$g_\text{D}(\omega) = \frac{9\omega^2}{\omega_\text{D}^3} \,, \qquad 0\le\omega\le\omega_\text{D}$$

with the Debye frequency $\omega_{D}$.

### Internal energy and heat capacity {#Internal_energy_and_heat_capacity}

Inserting $g$ into the internal energy

$$U = \int_0^\infty d\omega\,g(\omega)\,\hbar\omega\,n(\omega)$$

with the [Bose–Einstein distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_distribution)

$$n(\omega) = \frac{1}{\exp(\hbar\omega / k_\text{B} T)-1}.$$

one obtains

$$U = 3 k_\text{B}T \, D_3(\hbar\omega_\text{D} / k_\text{B}T).$$

The heat capacity is the derivative thereof.

### Mean squared displacement {#Mean_squared_displacement}

The intensity of [X-ray diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_diffraction) or [neutron diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_diffraction) at wavenumber *q* is given by the [Debye-Waller factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debye-Waller_factor) or the [Lamb-Mössbauer factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamb-Mössbauer_factor). For isotropic systems it takes the form

$$\exp(-2W(q)) = \exp\left(-q^2\langle u_x^2\rangle\right).$$

In this expression, the [mean squared displacement](wikipedia://en/36713796) refers to just once Cartesian component $u_{x}$ of the vector $\mathbf{u}$ that describes the displacement of atoms from their equilibrium positions. Assuming harmonicity and developing into normal modes,[^o13] one obtains

$$2W(q) = \frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{6M k_\text{B}T} \int_0^\infty d\omega \frac{k_\text{B}T}{\hbar\omega}g(\omega) \coth\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_\text{B}T}=\frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{6M k_\text{B}T} \int_0^\infty d\omega \frac{k_\text{B}T}{\hbar\omega} g(\omega) \left[\frac{2}{\exp(\hbar\omega/k_\text{B}T)-1}+1\right].$$

Inserting the density of states from the Debye model, one obtains

$$2W(q) = \frac{3}{2} \frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{M\hbar\omega_\text{D}} \left[2\left(\frac{k_\text{B}T}{\hbar\omega_\text{D}}\right) D_1{\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_\text{D}}{k_\text{B}T}\right)} + \frac{1}{2}\right].$$

From the above [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) expansion of $D_1$ follows that the mean square displacement at high temperatures is linear in temperature

$$2W(q) = \frac{3 k_\text{B}T q^2}{M\omega_\text{D}^2}.$$

The absence of $\hbar$ indicates that this is a [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Physics) result. Because $D_1(x)$ goes to zero for $x \to \infty$ it follows that for $T = 0$

$$2W(q)=\frac{3}{4}\frac{\hbar^2 q^2}{M\hbar\omega_\text{D}}$$

([zero-point motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-point_energy)).
