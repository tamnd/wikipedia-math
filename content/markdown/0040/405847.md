# AKS primality test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="AKS primality test"}

The **AKS primality test** (also known as the **Agrawal–Kayal–Saxena primality test** and the **cyclotomic AKS test**) is a [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_algorithm) [primality-proving](wikipedia://en/183751) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) created and published by [Manindra Agrawal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manindra_Agrawal), [Neeraj Kayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neeraj_Kayal), and [Nitin Saxena](wikipedia://en/2297722), computer scientists at the [Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Institute_of_Technology_Kanpur), on August 6, 2002, in an article titled "PRIMES is in P".[^o2] The algorithm was the first one which is able to determine in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time), whether a given number is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) or [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) without relying on [mathematical conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_conjecture) such as the [generalized Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/152567). The proof is also notable for not relying on the field of [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_%28mathematics%29).[^o3] In 2006 the authors received both the [Gödel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel_Prize) and [Fulkerson Prize](wikipedia://en/1425916) for their work.

:::

## Importance {#Importance}

AKS is the first primality-proving algorithm to be simultaneously *general*, *polynomial-time*, *deterministic*, and *unconditionally correct*. Previous algorithms had been developed for centuries and achieved three of these properties at most, but not all four.

- The AKS algorithm can be used to verify the primality of any **general** number given. Many fast primality tests are known that work only for numbers with certain properties. For example, the [Lucas–Lehmer test](wikipedia://en/325739) works only for [Mersenne numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_number), while [Pépin's test](wikipedia://en/3266846) can be applied to [Fermat numbers](wikipedia://en/91127) only.
- The maximum running time of the algorithm can be bounded by a **[polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time#Polynomial_time)** over the number of digits in the target number. [ECPP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_primality_proving) and [APR](wikipedia://en/8198761) conclusively prove or disprove that a given number is prime, but are not known to have polynomial time bounds for all inputs.
- The algorithm is guaranteed to distinguish **[deterministically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_algorithm)** whether the target number is prime or composite. Randomized tests, such as [Miller–Rabin](wikipedia://en/188999) and [Baillie–PSW](wikipedia://en/3609890), can test any given number for primality in polynomial time, but are known to produce only a probabilistic result.
- The correctness of AKS is **not conditional** on any subsidiary unproved [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis). In contrast, Miller's version of the [Miller–Rabin test](wikipedia://en/188999#Deterministic_variants) is fully deterministic and runs in polynomial time over all inputs, but its correctness depends on the truth of the yet-unproved [generalized Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/152567).

While the algorithm is of immense theoretical importance, it is not used in practice, rendering it a [galactic algorithm](wikipedia://en/60819045). For 64-bit inputs, the [Baillie–PSW test](wikipedia://en/3609890) is deterministic and runs many orders of magnitude faster. For larger inputs, the performance of the (also unconditionally correct) ECPP and APR tests is *far* superior to AKS. Additionally, ECPP can output a [primality certificate](wikipedia://en/3691235) that allows independent and rapid verification of the results, which is not possible with the AKS algorithm.

## Concepts {#Concepts}

The AKS primality test is based upon the following theorem: Given an integer $n\ge 2$ and integer $a$ [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to $n$, $n$ is prime if and only if the [polynomial](wikipedia://en/373065) [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167#Modular_arithmetic)

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$(X + a)^{n} \equiv X^{n} + a \pmod{n} \tag{1}$$

:::

holds within the polynomial ring $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)[X]$.[^o2] Note that $X$ denotes the [indeterminate](wikipedia://en/373065#Definition_%28univariate_case%29) which generates this polynomial ring.

This theorem is a generalization to polynomials of [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453). In one direction it can easily be proved using the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) together with the following property of the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668):

${n \choose k} \equiv 0 \pmod{n}$ for all $0<k<n$ if $n$ is prime.

While the relation ((1)) constitutes a primality test in itself, verifying it takes [exponential time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_time): the [brute force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_force_method) approach would require the expansion of the $(X + a)^n$ polynomial and a reduction $\operatorname{mod} n$ of the resulting $n + 1$ coefficients.

The congruence is an equality in the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)[X]$. Evaluating in a quotient ring of $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)[X]$ creates an upper bound for the degree of the polynomials involved. The AKS evaluates the equality in $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)[X]/(X^r -1)$, making the [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/7543) dependent on the size of $r$. For clarity,[^o2] this is expressed as the congruence

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$(X + a)^{n} \equiv (X^{n} + a) \pmod{X^{r}-1, n} \tag{2}$$

:::

which is the same as:

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$(X + a)^n - (X^n + a) = (X^r - 1)g + nf \tag{3}$$

:::

for some polynomials $f$ and $g$.

Note that all primes satisfy this relation (choosing $g=0$ in ((3)) gives ((1)), which holds for $n$ prime). This congruence can be checked in polynomial time when $r$ is polynomial to the digits of $n$. The AKS algorithm evaluates this congruence for a large set of $a$ values, whose size is polynomial to the digits of $n$. The proof of validity of the AKS algorithm shows that one can find an $r$ and a set of $a$ values with the above properties such that if the congruences hold then $n$ is a power of a prime.[^o2]

## History and running time {#History_and_running_time}

In the first version of the above-cited paper, the authors proved the asymptotic time complexity of the algorithm to be $\tilde{O}(\log(n)^{12})$ (using [Õ](wikipedia://en/44578#Extensions_to_the_Bachmann–Landau_notations) from [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578))—the twelfth power of the number of digits in *n* times a factor that is polylogarithmic in the number of digits. However, this upper bound was rather loose; a widely-held conjecture about the distribution of the [Sophie Germain primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Germain_prime) would, if true, immediately cut the worst case down to $\tilde{O}(\log(n)^6)$.

In the months following the discovery, new variants appeared (Lenstra 2002, Pomerance 2002, Berrizbeitia 2002, Cheng 2003, Bernstein 2003a/b, Lenstra and Pomerance 2003), which improved the speed of computation greatly. Owing to the existence of the many variants, Crandall and Papadopoulos refer to the "AKS-class" of algorithms in their scientific paper "On the implementation of AKS-class primality tests", published in March 2003.

In response to some of these variants, and to other feedback, the paper "PRIMES is in P" was updated with a new formulation of the AKS algorithm and of its proof of correctness. (This version was eventually published in *[Annals of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/2785)*.) While the basic idea remained the same, *r* was chosen in a new manner, and the proof of correctness was more coherently organized. The new proof relied almost exclusively on the behavior of [cyclotomic polynomials](wikipedia://en/171992) over [finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_fields). The new upper bound on time complexity was $\tilde{O}(\log(n)^{10.5})$, later reduced using additional results from [sieve theory](wikipedia://en/1035915) to $\tilde{O}(\log(n)^{7.5})$.

In 2005, [Pomerance](wikipedia://en/2134159) and [Lenstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lenstra) demonstrated a variant of AKS that runs in $\tilde{O}(\log(n)^{6})$ operations,[^o10] leading to another updated version of the paper.[^o11] Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena proposed a variant which would run in $\tilde{O}(\log(n)^{3})$ if [Agrawal's conjecture](wikipedia://en/37303781) were true; however, a heuristic argument by Pomerance and Lenstra suggested that it is probably false.

## The algorithm {#The_algorithm}

The algorithm is as follows:[^o2]

Input: integer $n > 1$.

1. Check if $n$ is a [perfect power](wikipedia://en/4023591): if $n = a^b$ for integers $a > 1$ and $b > 1$, then output *composite*.
1. Find the smallest $r$ such that $\operatorname{ord}_r(n) > (\log_2 n)^2$. If $r$ and $n$ are not coprime, then output *composite*.
1. For all $2 \le a \le \min(r,n-1)$, check that $a$ does not divide $n$: If $a \mid n$ for some $2 \le a \le \min(r,n-1)$, then output *composite*.
1. If $n \le r$, then output *prime*.
1. For $a = 1$ to $\left\lfloor \sqrt{\varphi(r)}\log_2(n) \right\rfloor$ do

if $(X + a)^n \not\equiv X^n + a \pmod{X^r-1, n}$, then output *composite*;

1. Output *prime*.

Here $\operatorname{ord}_r(n)$ is the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) of $n$ [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $r$, $\log_2$ is the [binary logarithm](wikipedia://en/249992), and $\varphi(r)$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) of $r$.

Step 3 is shown in the paper as checking $1 < \gcd(a,n) < n$ for all $a \le r$. It can be seen this is equivalent to trial division up to $r$ which can be done very efficiently without using [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354). Similarly the comparison in step 4 can be replaced by having the trial division return *prime* once it has checked all values up to and including $\left\lfloor \sqrt{n} \right\rfloor.$

Once beyond very small inputs, step 5 dominates the time taken. The essential reduction in complexity (from exponential to polynomial) is achieved by performing all calculations in the finite ring

$$R = (\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)[X]/(X^r -1)$$

consisting of $n^r$ elements. This ring contains only the $r$ [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) $\{X^0,X^1,\ldots,X^{r-1}\}$, and the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) are in $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ which has $n$ elements, all of them codable within $\log_2(n)$ bits.

Most later improvements made to the algorithm have concentrated on reducing the size of $r$, which makes the core operation in step 5 faster, and in reducing the size of $s$ the number of loops performed in step 5.[^o14] Typically these changes do not change the computational complexity, but can lead to many orders of magnitude less time taken; for example, Bernstein's final version has a theoretical speedup by a factor of over 2 million.

### Proof of validity outline {#Proof_of_validity_outline}

For the algorithm to be correct, all steps that identify $n$ must be correct. Steps 1, 3, and 4 are trivially correct, since they are based on direct tests of the divisibility of $n$. Step 5 is also correct: since (2) is true for any choice of $a$ coprime to $n$ and $r$ if $n$ is prime, an inequality means that $n$ must be composite.

The difficult part of the proof is showing that step 6 is true. Its proof of correctness is based on the upper and lower bounds of a [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) in $\mathbb{Z}_{n}[x]$ constructed from the $(X + a)$ binomials that are tested in step 5. Step 4 guarantees that these binomials are $\left\lfloor \sqrt{\varphi(r)}\log_2(n) \right\rfloor$ distinct elements of $\mathbb{Z}_n[x]$. For the particular choice of $r$, the bounds produce a [contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction) unless $n$ is prime or a power of a prime. Together with the test of step 1, this implies that $n$ is always prime at step 6.[^o2]

:::
