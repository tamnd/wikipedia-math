# Central simple algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Central simple algebra"}

In [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) a **central simple algebra** (**CSA**) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* is a finite-dimensional [associative *K*-algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) *A* that is [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_algebra), and for which the [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) is exactly *K*.

:::

For example, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) **C** form a CSA over themselves, but not over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) **R** (the center of **C** is all of **C**, not just **R**). The [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) **H** form a 4-dimensional CSA over **R**, and in fact represent the only non-trivial element of the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) of the reals (see below). Finite-dimensionality is essential to the definition: for instance, for a field *F* of characteristic 0, the [Weyl algebra](wikipedia://en/491095) $F[X,\partial_X]$ is a simple algebra with center *F*, but is *not* a central simple algebra over *F* as it has infinite dimension as a *F*-module.

By the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem), a finite-dimensional simple algebra *A* is isomorphic to the matrix algebra [*M*(*n*,*S*)](wikipedia://en/487541) for some [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) *S*. Given two central simple algebras *A* ~ *M*(*n*,*S*) and *B* ~ *M*(*m*,*T*) over the same field *F*, *A* and *B* are called *similar* (or *[Brauer equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer_equivalent)*) if their division rings *S* and *T* are isomorphic. The set of all [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of central simple algebras over a given field *F*, under this equivalence relation, can be equipped with a [group operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_operation) given by the [tensor product of algebras](wikipedia://en/372395). The resulting group is called the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) Br(*F*) of the field *F*.[^o2] It is always a [torsion group](wikipedia://en/3568695).[^o3]

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- According to the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem) a finite-dimensional simple algebra *A* is isomorphic to the matrix algebra [*M*(*n*,*S*)](wikipedia://en/487541) for some [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) *S*. Hence, there is a unique division algebra in each Brauer equivalence class.[^o6]
- Every [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of a central simple algebra is an [inner automorphism](wikipedia://en/61839) (this follows from the [Skolem–Noether theorem](wikipedia://en/693453)).
- The [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of a central simple algebra as a vector space over its centre is always a square: the **degree** is the square root of this dimension.[^o7] The **Schur index** of a central simple algebra is the degree of the equivalent division algebra:[^o8] it depends only on the [Brauer class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer_class) of the algebra.[^o9]
- The **period** or **exponent** of a central simple algebra is the order of its Brauer class as an element of the Brauer group. It is a divisor of the index,[^o10] and the two numbers are composed of the same prime factors.[^o11][^o12][^o13]
- If *S* is a simple [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) of a central simple algebra *A* then dim<sub>*F*</sub> *S* divides dim<sub>*F*</sub> *A*.
- Every 4-dimensional central simple algebra over a field *F* is isomorphic to a [quaternion algebra](wikipedia://en/2776890); in fact, it is either a two-by-two [matrix algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_algebra), or a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625).
- If *D* is a central division algebra over *K* for which the index has prime factorisation

$$\mathrm{ind}(D) = \prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{m_i} \$$

then *D* has a tensor product decomposition

$$D = \bigotimes_{i=1}^r D_i \$$

where each component *D*<sub>*i*</sub> is a central division algebra of index $p_i^{m_i}$, and the components are uniquely determined up to isomorphism.[^o14]

:::

## Splitting field {#Splitting_field}

We call a field *E* a *splitting field* for *A* over *K* if *A*⊗*E* is isomorphic to a matrix ring over *E*. Every finite dimensional CSA has a splitting field: indeed, in the case when *A* is a division algebra, then a [maximal subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_subfield) of *A* is a splitting field. In general by theorems of [Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494) and Koethe there is a splitting field which is a [separable extension](wikipedia://en/363340) of *K* of degree equal to the index of *A*, and this splitting field is isomorphic to a subfield of *A*.[^o16][^o17] As an example, the field **C** splits the quaternion algebra **H** over **R** with

$$t + x \mathbf{i} + y \mathbf{j} + z \mathbf{k} \leftrightarrow \left({\begin{array}{*{20}c} t + x i & y + z i \\ -y + z i & t - x i \end{array}}\right) .$$

We can use the existence of the splitting field to define **reduced norm** and **reduced trace** for a CSA *A*.[^o18] Map *A* to a matrix ring over a splitting field and define the reduced norm and trace to be the composite of this map with determinant and trace respectively. For example, in the quaternion algebra **H**, the splitting above shows that the element *t* + *x* **i** + *y* **j** + *z* **k** has reduced norm *t*<sup>2</sup> + *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> + *z*<sup>2</sup> and reduced trace 2*t*.

The reduced norm is multiplicative and the reduced trace is additive. An element *a* of *A* is invertible if and only if its reduced norm is non-zero: hence a CSA is a division algebra if and only if the reduced norm is non-zero on the non-zero elements.[^o19]

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

CSAs over a field *K* are a non-commutative analog to [extension fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field) over *K* – in both cases, they have no non-trivial 2-sided ideals, and have a distinguished field in their center, though a CSA can be non-commutative and need not have inverses (need not be a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625)). This is of particular interest in [noncommutative number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_number_theory) as generalizations of [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) (extensions of the rationals **Q**); see [noncommutative number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_number_field).

:::
