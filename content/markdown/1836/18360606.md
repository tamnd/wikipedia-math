# K-topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="K-topology"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in the field of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the **K-topology**,[^n1] also called **Smirnov's deleted sequence topology**,[^n2] is a [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_%28structure%29) on the set **R** of real numbers which has some interesting properties. Relative to the [standard topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_topology) on **R**, the set $K = \{1/n:n=1,2,\dots\}$ is not [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_%28topology%29) since it doesn't contain its limit point 0. Relative to the K-topology however, the set *K* is declared to be closed by adding more [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) to the standard topology on **R**. Thus the K-topology on **R** is strictly finer than the standard topology on **R**. It is mostly useful for counterexamples in basic topology. In particular, it provides an example of a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) that is not [regular](wikipedia://en/53058).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let **R** be the set of real numbers and let $K = \{1/n:n=1,2,\dots\}.$ The **K-topology** on **R** is the topology obtained by taking as a [base](wikipedia://en/59220) the collection of all open intervals $(a,b)$ together with all sets of the form $(a,b)\setminus K.$[^n1] The [neighborhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29) of a point $x\ne 0$ are the same as in the usual Euclidean topology. The neighborhoods of $0$ are of the form $V\setminus K$, where $V$ is a neighborhood of $0$ in the usual topology.[^n3] The open sets in the K-topology are precisely the sets of the form $U\setminus B$ with $U$ open in the usual Euclidean topology and $B\subseteq K.$[^n2]

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Throughout this section, *T* will denote the K-topology and (**R**, *T*) will denote the set of all real numbers with the K-topology as a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450).

\1. The K-topology is strictly finer than the standard topology on **R**. Hence it is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), but not [compact](wikipedia://en/6042).

\2. The K-topology is not [regular](wikipedia://en/53058), because *K* is a closed set not containing $0$, but the set $K$ and the point $0$ have no disjoint neighborhoods. And as a further consequence, the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of the K-topology obtained by collapsing *K* to a point is not Hausdorff. This illustrates that a quotient of a Hausdorff space need not be Hausdorff.

\3. The K-topology is [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_%28topology%29). However, it is not [path connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_connected); it has precisely two [path components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_component): $(-\infty, 0]$ and $(0, +\infty).$

\4. The K-topology is not [locally path connected](wikipedia://en/6959754) at $0$ and not [locally connected](wikipedia://en/6959754) at $0$. But it is locally path connected and locally connected everywhere else.

\5. The closed interval \[0,1\] is not compact as a subspace of (**R**, *T*) since it is not even [limit point compact](wikipedia://en/9861462) (*K* is an infinite closed discrete subspace of (**R**, *T*), hence has no limit point in \[0,1\]). More generally, no subspace *A* of (**R**, *T*) containing *K* is compact.

:::
