# Steven Tainer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Steven Tainer"}

**Steven Arthur Tainer** (born 26 July 1947) is an instructor of Asian [contemplative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemplative) traditions.[^o2][^n1]

:::

## Spiritual education {#Spiritual_education}

Tainer began his study of Tibetan Buddhism in 1970. His primary teachers included [Tarthang Tulku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarthang_Tulku) [Rinpoche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rinpoche) and [Chogyal Namkhai Norbu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chogyal_Namkhai_Norbu) Rinpoche.

Upon the publication of *Time, Space, and Knowledge*[^n2] in 1977, which he ghostwrote for his first instructor, Tarthang Tulku Rinpoche, he earned an advanced degree in Tibetan Buddhist studies. He was eventually named a [Dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma) heir of Tarthang Tulku, however, he did not take up the position. After collaborating with Ming Liu (born Charles Belyea) in the 1980s, Tainer was declared a successor in a family lineage of yogic [Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism). In 1991, he co-authored a book with Ming Liu (Charles Belyea), titled *Dragon's Play* and together founded Da Yuen Circle of Yogic Taoism.[^n3][^n4]

Starting in the mid-1980s, he studied Confucian views of contemplation emphasizing exemplary conduct in ordinary life.

## Career {#Career}

He first taught under the direction of his masters in the early 1970s. Tainer began teaching his groups in 1990.

Since 1995, Tainer has been a faculty member of the Institute for World Religions[^o9] and the [Berkeley Buddhist Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Buddhist_Monastery).[^o10]

Tainer is one of the founders of the [Kira Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kira_Institute).[^o11] Between 1998 and 2002, [Piet Hut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piet_Hut) and Tainer organized a series of annual summer schools.

In 2024, Yuko Ishihara and Tainer published *Intercultural Phenomenology: Playing with Reality*,[^n5] which explores using play within "suspension of judgement", with roots in Western phenomenological and Eastern Buddhist, Taoist, and Confucian disciplines, for first-person direct examination of experience.
