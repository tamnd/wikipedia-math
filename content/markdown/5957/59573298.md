# Stanisław Knapowski

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stanisław Knapowski"}

**Stanisław Knapowski** (May 19, 1931 – September 28, 1967) was a Polish [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who worked on [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). Knapowski published 53 papers despite dying at the age of only 36.[^o4]

:::

## Life and education {#Life_and_education}

Stanisław Knapowski was the son of Zofia Krysiewicz and Roch Knapowski. His father, Roch Knapowski was a lawyer in [Poznań](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poznań) but later taught at [Poznań University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz_University_in_Poznań). The family moved to the [Kielce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kielce) province in south-eastern Poland after the German invasion of 1939 but returned to Poznań after the war.[^o4]

Stanisław completed his high school education in 1949 excelling at math and continued on at Poznań University to study mathematics. Later in 1952 he continued his studies at [University of Wrocław](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wrocław) and earned his master's degree in 1954.

Knapowski was appointment an assistant at [Adam Mickiewicz University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz_University) in Poznań under [Władysław Orlicz](wikipedia://en/362718) and worked towards his doctorate. He studied under the direction of [Pál Turán](wikipedia://en/614998) starting in [Lublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lublin) in 1956. He published many of his papers with Turán and Turán wrote a short biography of his life and work in 1971 after his death.[^o5] Knapowski began to work in this area and finished his doctorate in 1957 “Zastosowanie metod Turaná w analitycznej teorii liczb” ("Certain applications of Turan's methods in the analytical theory of numbers").

Knapowski spent a year in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge) where he worked with [Louis J. Mordell](wikipedia://en/991484) and listened to classes by [J.W.S. Cassels](wikipedia://en/1709206) and [Albert Ingham](wikipedia://en/5380138). He visited Belgium, France and The Netherlands.

Knapowski returned to Poznań to finish another thesis to complete a post-doctoral qualification needed to lecture at a German university.[^o4][^o5] "On new ["explicit formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_formulae_%28L-function%29)" in prime number theory" in 1960.[^o6] In 1962 the [Polish Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/2121791) awarded him their [Mazurkiewicz](wikipedia://en/629203) Prize and he moved to [Tulane University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulane_University) in [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans), United States. After a very short return to Poland, he left again and taught in [Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marburg) in Germany, [Gainesville, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gainesville,_Florida) and [Miami, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miami).[^o5][^o7]

## Personal life; and death {#Personal_life;_and_death}

Knapowski was a good classical pianist. He was an avid driver. He died in a traffic accident where he lost control of his car while leaving the Miami airport.[^o5]

## Work {#Work}

Knapowski expanded on the work of others in several fields of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692), [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) and [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610).

### Number of times the Δ(*n*) prime sign changes {#Number_of_times_the_Δ(n)_prime_sign_changes}

*Image: Prime-counting relative error.svg.* The [relative error](wikipedia://en/640422) of $\tfrac{n}{\log n}$ and the logarithmic integral $\operatorname{Li}(n)$ as approximations to the [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826). Both relative errors decrease to zero as $n$ grows, but the convergence to zero is much more rapid for the logarithmic integral.

Mathematicians work on [primality tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality_tests) to develop easier ways to find prime numbers when finding them by [trial division](wikipedia://en/557660) is not practical. This has many applications in cybersecurity. There is no formula to calculate prime numbers. However, the distribution of primes can be statistically modelled. The [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692), which was proven at the end of the 19th century, says that the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) of a randomly chosen number being prime is inversely [proportional](wikipedia://en/81863) to its number of digits ([logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860)). At the start of the 19th century, [Adrien-Marie Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) and [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) suggested that as $x$ becomes large, the number of primes up to $x$ [asymptotically](wikipedia://en/641995) approaches $x/\log x$, where $\log x$ is the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) of $x$.

$$\operatorname{li}(n) = \int_0^n \frac{dt}{\log t}$$

where the integral is evaluated at $t = n$, also fits the distribution.

The [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826) $\pi(n)$ is defined as the number of primes not greater than $n$.[^o11]

And $\Delta(n)=\pi(n)-\operatorname{li}(n)$

[Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) stated that $\Delta(n)$ was always negative but [J.E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.E._Littlewood) later disproved this. In 1914 [J.E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.E._Littlewood) proved that there are arbitrarily large values of *x* for which

$$\pi(x)>\operatorname{Li}(x) +\frac13\frac{\sqrt x}{\log x}\log\log\log x,$$

and that there are also arbitrarily large values of *x* for which

$$\pi(x)<\operatorname{Li}(x) -\frac13\frac{\sqrt x}{\log x}\log\log\log x.$$

Thus the difference $\pi$(*x*) − Li(*x*) changes sign infinitely many times.

[Stanley Skewes](wikipedia://en/11963095) then added an upper bound on the smallest [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $x$:

$$\pi(x) > \operatorname{li}(x),$$

Knapowski followed this up and published a paper on the number of times $\Delta(n)$ changes sign in the interval $\Delta(n)$.[^o12]

### Modular arithmetic {#Modular_arithmetic}

Knapowski worked in other areas of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). One area was on the distribution of prime numbers in different [residue classes modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $k$.

Modular arithmetic modifies usual arithmetic by only using the numbers $\{0,1,2,\dots,n-1\}$, for a natural number $n$ called the modulus. Any other natural number can be mapped into this system by replacing it by its remainder after division by $n$.[^o14]

The distribution of the primes looks random, without a pattern. Take a list of consecutive prime numbers and divide them by another prime (like 7) and keep only the remainder (this is called reducing them modulo 7). The result is a sequence of integers from 1 to 6. Knapowski worked to determine the parameters of this modular distribution[^o20]
