# Antonio Cagnoli

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Antonio Cagnoli"}

**Antonio Cagnoli** (29 September 1743, in [Zakynthos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakynthos) – 6 August 1816, in [Verona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verona)) was an Italian astronomer, mathematician and diplomat in the service of the [Republic of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Antonio Cagnoli was born in 1743, at [Zakynthos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakynthos), then belonging to the [Republic of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice). His father Ottavio was chancellor to the Venetian governor of the [Ionian Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionian_Islands).[^n1] In 1772 he was appointed Secretary to the Venetian Ambassador in Spain, Marco Zeno. In 1775 he moved to [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989), where he lived for several years. A pupil of the French astronomer [Jérôme Lalande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jérôme_Lalande), he collaborated in the *[Encyclopédie Méthodique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopédie_Méthodique)*. At Paris, he built an observatory furnished with the best astronomical instruments available. Back in Italy, in 1786 he built an observatory in Verona.[^o6]

After the death of [Antonio Maria Lorgna](wikipedia://en/46666197), in 1796, Cagnoli was appointed president of the [Accademia nazionale delle scienze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_nazionale_delle_scienze). He guided the Academy for the remaining 20 years of his life. The [Cisalpine Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cisalpine_Republic), formed in northern Italy by [Napoleon](wikipedia://en/69880), transferred the Academy in Milan and the instruments of Cagnoli were moved to the [Brera Astronomical Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brera_Astronomical_Observatory). Cagnoli ended his scientific career as professor of [Pure Mathematics](wikipedia://en/457210) at the [Military Academy of Modena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Academy_of_Modena), where he taught until 1807. In his later years he moved back to his native Verona. He died in Verona on 6 August 1816. The asteroid [11112 Cagnoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11112_Cagnoli) discovered in 1995 bears his name.

## Works {#Works}

In 1786, Cagnoli published his famous treatise *Trigonometria Piana e Sferica* (Plane and Spherical Trigonometry), translated into [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) by Nicolas Maurice Chompré and published in Paris with the title *Trigonométrie rectiligne et sphérique* in 1808. This work is considered a foundational text on [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), and the French translation helped disseminate Cagnoli's work and the trigonometric formula named after him throughout Europe. In 1803, he published an authoritative [star catalogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_catalogue) in the Proceedings of the Accademia nazionale delle scienze. The work was published separately in 1807, with tables for computing the [aberration](wikipedia://en/2703) and [nutation](wikipedia://en/22094). Cagnoli's paper on the figure of the earth published in the 6th volume of the Proceedings of the Accademia nazionale delle scienze (1792) was translated into [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) by [Francis Baily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Baily) and published in London in 1819.

## Cagnoli's Equation {#Cagnoli's_Equation}

Cagnoli is best known for Cagnoli's equation, a formula relating the sides and angles of a spherical triangle. It's essentially a variation of the cosine rule for spherical triangles, particularly useful for calculations involving multiple angles and sides. Multiplying the first cosine rule by $\cos A$ gives

$$\cos a \cos A = \cos b \,\cos c \,\cos A + \sin b \,\sin c - \sin b \,\sin c \,\sin^2 A.$$

Similarly multiplying the first supplementary cosine rule by $\cos a$ yields

$$\cos a \cos A = -\cos B \,\cos C \,\cos a + \sin B \,\sin C - \sin B \,\sin C \,\sin^2 a.$$

Subtracting the two and noting that it follows from the sine rules that $\sin b \,\sin c \,\sin^2 A = \sin B \,\sin C \,\sin^2 a$ produces Cagnoli's equation

$$\sin b \,\sin c + \cos b \,\cos c \,\cos A = \sin B \,\sin C - \cos B \,\cos C \,\cos a$$

which is a relation between the six parts of the spherical triangle.[^o9]
