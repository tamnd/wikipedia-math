# Marquois scales

*Image: Marquois\_Scales.jpg.* Marquois scales in a case of drawing instruments (top) and on their own (bottom)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Marquois scales"}

**Marquois scales** (also known as **Marquois parallel scales** or **Marquois scale and triangle** or **military scales**) are a mathematical instrument that found widespread use in Britain, particularly in military [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), from the late 18th century to [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II).[^o2]

:::

## Description {#Description}

Invented around 1778 by [Thomas Marquois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Marquois), the Marquois scales consist of a right-angle triangle (with sides at a 3:1 ratio) and two rulers (each with multiple [scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_ruler)).[^o4] The system could be used to aid precision when marking distances off scales, and to rapidly draw parallel lines a precise distance apart.[^o5] Quick construction of precise parallel lines was useful in cartography and engineering (especially before the availability of [graph paper](wikipedia://en/293606)) and Marquois scales were convenient in some challenging environments where larger equipment like a [drawing board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawing_board) and [T-square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-square) was impractical, such as field survey work and classrooms.[^o7] Marquois scales fell out of favour among draftsmen in the early 20th century,[^o6] although familiarity with their use was an entry requirement for the [Royal Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Military_Academy,_Woolwich) at Woolwich around the same time.[^o7]

## Material {#Material}

Marquois scales were normally made of [boxwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxwood), though sets were sometimes made in [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory)[^o9] or metal.

::: {#o11 .application cue=heading name="Use"}

## Use {#Use}

The triangle would be used for many regular [set square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_square) operations, the rulers likewise would function as rulers, but the unique function was the 3:1 reduction ratio between measured distance and drawn line.[^o4][^o5]

A line is drawn along the beveled edge (the side of middle-length) of the triangle. By placing a ruler against the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) of the triangle and sliding the triangle along the ruler for 3 units of the ruler's scale, drawing another line along the beveled edge results in a parallel line with a distance of only 1 unit from the original line.[^o4] Using larger distances on a ruler to draw lines smaller distances apart means that margin of error reading off the scale is reduced. Additionally, the end-state is the instruments already in place to slide the triangle again to quickly draw additional lines as desired. A similar method also allows the distance between points on plans or maps to be measured with increased precision.[^o4][^o5]

Regular set square triangles differ (from a Marquois scales triangle) by being made and used according to the angles of their triangle (e.g. 45-45-90 or 30-60-90 degrees) rather than according to the ratio between the lengths of their sides. Likewise the scales on other rulers are usually intended to be used directly and the selection differs accordingly.[^o4][^o5]
