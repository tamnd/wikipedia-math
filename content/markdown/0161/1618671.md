# Quadrature (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quadrature"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **quadrature** is a historic term for the computation of [areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_%28mathematics%29) and is thus used for computation of [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532).

:::

The word is derived from the Latin *quadratus* meaning "square". The reason is that, for [Ancient Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_mathematicians), the computation of an area consisted of constructing a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) of the same area. In this sense, the modern term is **squaring**. For example, the [quadrature of the circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_of_the_circle), (or squaring the circle) is a famous old problem that has been shown, in the 19th century, to be impossible with the methods available to the Ancient Greeks.

[Integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus), introduced in the 17th century, is a general method for computation of areas. *Quadrature* came to refer to the computation of any integral; such a computation is presently called more often "integral" or "integration". However, the computation of solutions of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) and [differential systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_system) is also called *integration*, and *quadrature* remains useful for distinguishing integrals from solutions of differential equations, in contexts where both problems are considered. This is the case in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506); see [numerical quadrature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_quadrature). Also, **reduction to quadratures** and **solving by quadratures** means expressing solutions of differential equations in terms of integrals.

The remainder of this article is devoted to the original meaning of quadrature, namely, computation of areas.

## History {#History}

### Antiquity {#Antiquity}

*Image: Lune.svg.* The [lune of Hippocrates](wikipedia://en/21854422) was the first curved figure to have its exact area calculated mathematically.

[Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) understood the determination of an [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of a figure as the process of geometrically constructing a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) having the same area (*squaring*), thus the name *quadrature* for this process. The Greek geometers were not always successful (see [squaring the circle](wikipedia://en/201359)), but they did carry out quadratures of some figures whose sides were not simply line segments, such as the [lune of Hippocrates](wikipedia://en/21854422) and the [parabola](wikipedia://en/12745577). By a certain Greek tradition, these constructions had to be performed using only a [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions), though not all Greek mathematicians adhered to this dictum.

*Image: Geometric mean.svg.* Antique method to find the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046)

For a quadrature of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) with the sides *a* and *b* it is necessary to construct a square with the side $x =\sqrt {ab}$ (the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of *a* and *b*). For this purpose it is possible to use the following: if one draws the circle with diameter made from joining line segments of lengths *a* and *b*, then the height (*BH* in the diagram) of the line segment drawn perpendicular to the diameter, from the point of their connection to the point where it crosses the circle, equals the geometric mean of *a* and *b*. A similar geometrical construction solves the problems of quadrature of a parallelogram and of a triangle.

*Image: Quadrature parabole2.png.* [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) proved that the area of a parabolic segment is 4/3 the area of an inscribed triangle.

Problems of quadrature for [curvilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvilinear) figures are much more difficult. The [quadrature of the circle with compass and straightedge](wikipedia://en/201359) was proved in the 19th century to be impossible.[^o4][^o5] Nevertheless, for some figures a quadrature can be performed. The quadratures of the surface of a sphere and a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) segment discovered by [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) became the highest achievement of analysis in antiquity.

- The area of the surface of a sphere is equal to four times the area of the circle formed by a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) of this sphere.
- The area of a segment of a parabola determined by a straight line cutting it is 4/3 the area of a triangle inscribed in this segment (specifically, of a triangle whose vertices are the parabola's two intersection points with the secant line and its intersection with a tangent line of the same slope).

For the proofs of these results, Archimedes used the [method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284) attributed to [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388).[^o6]

### Medieval mathematics {#Medieval_mathematics}

In medieval Europe, quadrature meant the calculation of area by any method. Most often the [method of indivisibles](wikipedia://en/21697672) was used; it was less rigorous than the geometric constructions of the Greeks, but it was simpler and more powerful. With its help, [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374) and [Gilles de Roberval](wikipedia://en/196880) found the area of a [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638) arch, [Grégoire de Saint-Vincent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grégoire_de_Saint-Vincent) investigated the area under a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) (*Opus Geometricum*, 1647),[^o6] and [Alphonse Antonio de Sarasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphonse_Antonio_de_Sarasa), de Saint-Vincent's pupil and commentator, noted the relation of this area to [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860).[^o6][^o8]

### Integral calculus {#Integral_calculus}

[John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290) algebrised this method; he wrote in his *Arithmetica Infinitorum* (1656) some series which are equivalent to what is now called the [definite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integral), and he calculated their values. [Isaac Barrow](wikipedia://en/97805) and [James Gregory](wikipedia://en/220425) made further progress: quadratures for some [algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_curves) and [spirals](wikipedia://en/61563). [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127) successfully performed a quadrature of the surface area of some [solids of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400).

The [quadrature of the hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregoire_de_Saint-Vincent#Quadrature_of_the_hyperbola) by [Gregoire de Saint-Vincent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregoire_de_Saint-Vincent) and [A. A. de Sarasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._A._de_Sarasa) provided a new [function](wikipedia://en/185427), the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), of critical importance. With the invention of [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus) came a universal method for area calculation. In response, the term *quadrature* has become traditional, and instead the modern phrase *finding the area* is more commonly used for what is technically the *computation of a univariate definite integral*.
