# Connectivity (graph theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Connectivity"}

*Image: Network Community Structure.svg.* This graph becomes disconnected when the right-most node in the gray area on the left is removed

*Image: Sample-graph.jpg.* This graph becomes disconnected when the dashed edge is removed.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), **connectivity** is one of the basic concepts of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401): it asks for the [minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum) number of elements (nodes or edges) that need to be removed to separate the remaining nodes into two or more [isolated subgraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29).[^o24] It is closely related to the theory of [network flow](wikipedia://en/645676) problems. The connectivity of a graph is an important measure of its resilience as a network.

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Connected graph"}

## Connected vertices and graphs {#Connected_vertices_and_graphs}

*Image: UndirectedDegrees.svg.* With vertex 0, this graph is disconnected. The rest of the graph is connected.

In an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) $G$, two [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) $u$ and $v$ are called **connected** if $G$ contains a [path](wikipedia://en/638889) from $u$ to $v$. Otherwise, they are called **disconnected**. If the two vertices are additionally connected by a path of length $1$ (that is, they are the endpoints of a single edge), the vertices are called **adjacent**.

A [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is said to be **connected** if every pair of vertices in the graph is connected. This means that there is a [path](wikipedia://en/638889) between every pair of vertices. An undirected graph that is not connected is called **disconnected**. An undirected graph $G$ is therefore disconnected if there exist two vertices in $G$ such that no path in $G$ has these vertices as endpoints. A graph with just one vertex is connected. An [edgeless graph](wikipedia://en/1520758) with two or more vertices is disconnected.

A [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) is called **weakly connected** if replacing all of its directed edges with undirected edges produces a connected (undirected) graph. It is **unilaterally connected** or unilateral (also called **semiconnected**) if it contains a directed path from $u$ to $v$ or a directed path from $v$ to $u$ for every pair of vertices $u, v$.[^o4] It is **strongly connected**, or simply strong, if it contains a directed path from $u$ to $v$ and a directed path from $v$ to $u$ for every pair of vertices $u, v$.

:::

## Components and cuts {#Components_and_cuts}

A [**connected component**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) is a maximal connected subgraph of an undirected graph. Each vertex belongs to exactly one connected component, as does each edge. A graph is connected [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it has exactly one connected component.

The [**strong components**](wikipedia://en/684680) are the maximal strongly connected subgraphs of a directed graph.

A **[vertex cut](wikipedia://en/2180494)** or **separating set** of a connected graph $G$ is a set of vertices whose removal renders $G$ disconnected. The [**vertex connectivity**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) $\kappa(G)$ (where $G$ is not a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816)) is the size of a smallest vertex cut. A graph is called ***$k$*-vertex-connected** or ***$k$*-connected** if its vertex connectivity is $k$ or greater.

More precisely, any graph $G$ (complete or not) is said to be *$k$-vertex-connected* if it contains at least $k + 1$ vertices, but does not contain a set of $k - 1$ vertices whose removal disconnects the graph; and $\kappa(G)$ is defined as the largest $k$ such that $G$ is $k$-connected. In particular, a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with $n$ vertices, denoted $K_{n}$, has no vertex cuts at all, but $\kappa(K_{n}) = n - 1$.

A **vertex cut** for two vertices $u$ and $v$ is a set of vertices whose removal from the graph disconnects $u$ and $v$. The **local connectivity** $\kappa(u, v)$ is the size of a smallest vertex cut separating $u$ and $v$. Local connectivity is symmetric for undirected graphs; that is, $\kappa(u, v) = \kappa(v, u)$. Moreover, except for complete graphs, $\kappa(G)$ equals the minimum of $\kappa(u, v)$ over all nonadjacent pairs of vertices $u, v$.

$2$-connectivity is also called *[biconnectivity](wikipedia://en/4989397)* and $3$-connectivity is also called *triconnectivity*. A graph $G$ which is connected but not $2$-connected is sometimes called *separable*.

Analogous concepts can be defined for edges. In the simple case in which cutting a single, specific edge would disconnect the graph, that edge is called a *[bridge](wikipedia://en/1305071)*. More generally, an edge cut of $G$ is a set of edges whose removal renders the graph disconnected. The *[edge-connectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge-connectivity)* $\lambda(G)$ is the size of a smallest edge cut, and the local edge-connectivity $\lambda(u, v)$ of two vertices $u, v$ is the size of a smallest edge cut disconnecting $u$ from $v$. Again, local edge-connectivity is symmetric. A graph is called *$k$-edge-connected* if its edge connectivity is $k$ or greater.

A graph is said to be *maximally connected* if its connectivity equals its minimum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448). A graph is said to be *maximally edge-connected* if its edge-connectivity equals its minimum degree.[^o6]

### Super- and hyper-connectivity {#Super-_and_hyper-connectivity}

A graph is said to be *super-connected* or *super-κ* if every minimum vertex cut isolates a vertex. A graph is said to be *hyper-connected* or *hyper-κ* if the deletion of each minimum vertex cut creates exactly two components, one of which is an isolated vertex. A graph is *semi-hyper-connected* or *semi-hyper-κ* if any minimum vertex cut separates the graph into exactly two components.[^o9]

More precisely: a $G$ connected graph is said to be *super-connected* or *super-κ* if all minimum vertex-cuts consist of the vertices adjacent with one (minimum-degree) vertex. A $G$ connected graph is said to be *super-edge-connected* or *super-λ* if all minimum edge-cuts consist of the edges incident on some (minimum-degree) vertex.[^o10]

A cutset $X$ of $G$ is called a non-trivial cutset if $X$ does not contain the neighborhood $N(u)$ of any vertex $u \notin X$. Then the *superconnectivity* $\kappa_1$ of $G$ is

$$\kappa_1(G) = \min\{ |X| : X \text{ is a non-trivial cutset} \}.$$

A non-trivial edge-cut and the *edge-superconnectivity* $\lambda_1(G)$ are defined analogously.[^o11]

:::

## Menger's theorem {#Menger's_theorem}

One of the most important facts about connectivity in graphs is [Menger's theorem](wikipedia://en/1155738), which characterizes the connectivity and edge-connectivity of a graph in terms of the number of independent paths between vertices.

If $u$ and $v$ are vertices of a graph $G$, then a collection of paths between $u$ and $v$ is called independent if no two of them share a vertex (other than $u$ and $v$ themselves). Similarly, the collection is edge-independent if no two paths in it share an edge. The number of mutually independent paths between $u$ and $v$ is written as $\kappa'(u, v)$, and the number of mutually edge-independent paths between $u$ and $v$ is written as $\lambda'(u, v)$.

Menger's theorem asserts that for distinct vertices *u*,*v*, $\lambda(u, v)$ equals $\lambda'(u, v)$, and if *u* is also not adjacent to *v* then $\kappa(u, v)$ equals $\kappa'(u, v)$.[^o14][^o15] This fact is actually a special case of the [max-flow min-cut theorem](wikipedia://en/78130).

:::

## Computational aspects {#Computational_aspects}

The problem of determining whether two vertices in a graph are connected can be solved efficiently using a [search algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_algorithm), such as [breadth-first search](wikipedia://en/97026). More generally, it is easy to determine computationally whether a graph is connected (for example, by using a [disjoint-set data structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoint-set_data_structure#Applications)), or to count the number of connected components. A simple algorithm might be written in [pseudo-code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-code) as follows:

1. Begin at any arbitrary node of the graph $G$.
1. Proceed from that node using either depth-first or breadth-first search, counting all nodes reached.
1. Once the graph has been entirely traversed, if the number of nodes counted is equal to the number of nodes of $G$, the graph is connected; otherwise it is disconnected.

By [Menger's theorem](wikipedia://en/1155738), for any two vertices $u$ and $v$ in a connected graph $G$, the numbers $\kappa(u, v)$ and $\lambda(u, v)$ can be determined efficiently using the [max-flow min-cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_flow_min_cut) algorithm. The connectivity and edge-connectivity of $G$ can then be computed as the minimum values of $\kappa(u, v)$ and $\lambda(u, v)$, respectively.

In [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543), [SL](wikipedia://en/1174919) is the class of problems [log-space reducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-space_reducible) to the problem of determining whether two vertices in a graph are connected, which was proved to be equal to [L](wikipedia://en/1149999) by [Omer Reingold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omer_Reingold) in 2004.[^o17] Hence, undirected graph connectivity may be solved in $O(\log n)$ space.

The problem of computing the probability that a [Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/199189) random graph is connected is called network reliability and the problem of computing whether two given vertices are connected the ST-reliability problem. Both of these are [\#P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-P)-hard.[^o18]

## Examples {#Examples}

- The vertex- and edge-connectivities of a disconnected graph are both $0$.
- $1$-connectedness is equivalent to connectedness for graphs of at least two vertices.
- The [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on $n$ vertices has edge-connectivity equal to $n - 1$. Every other [simple graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) on $n$ vertices has strictly smaller edge-connectivity.
- In a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560), the local edge-connectivity between any two distinct vertices is $1$.

:::

## Bounds on connectivity {#Bounds_on_connectivity}

- The vertex-connectivity of a graph is less than or equal to its edge-connectivity. That is, $\kappa(G) \le \lambda(G)$.
- The edge-connectivity for a graph with at least 2 vertices is less than or equal to the minimum [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) of the graph because removing all the edges that are incident to a vertex of minimum degree will disconnect that vertex from the rest of the graph.[^o24]
- For a [vertex-transitive graph](wikipedia://en/581175) of degree $d$, we have: $2(d + 1)/3 \le \kappa(G) \le \lambda(G) = d$.[^o25]
- For a vertex-transitive graph of degree $d \le 4$, or for any (undirected) minimal [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of degree $d$, or for any [symmetric graph](wikipedia://en/1577896) of degree $d$, both kinds of connectivity are equal: $\kappa(G) = \lambda(G) = d$.[^o26]

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

- Connectedness is preserved by [graph homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/676328).
- If $G$ is connected then its [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) $L(G)$ is also connected.
- A graph $G$ is $2$-edge-connected if and only if it has an orientation that is strongly connected.
- [Balinski's theorem](wikipedia://en/24732291) states that the [polytopal graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopal_graph) ($1$-[skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton_%28topology%29)) of a $k$-dimensional [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) is a $k$-vertex-connected graph.[^o29] [Steinitz](wikipedia://en/3966685)'s previous theorem that any 3-vertex-connected [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is a polytopal graph ([Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817)) gives a partial [converse](wikipedia://en/155941).
- According to a theorem of [G. A. Dirac](wikipedia://en/1643806), if a graph is $k$-connected for $k \ge 2$, then for every set of $k$ vertices in the graph there is a [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) that passes through all the vertices in the set.[^o30][^o31] The converse is true when $k = 2$.

:::
