# Q-analog

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Q-analog"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a ***q*-analog** of a theorem, identity or expression is a generalization involving a new parameter *q* that returns the original theorem, identity or expression in the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) as $q \to 1$. Typically, mathematicians are interested in *q*-analogs that arise naturally, rather than in arbitrarily contriving *q*-analogs of known results. The earliest *q*-analog studied in detail is the [basic hypergeometric series](wikipedia://en/2233526), which was introduced in the 19th century.[^o2]

:::

*q*-analogs are most frequently studied in the mathematical fields of [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and [special functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_function). In these settings, the limit $q \to 1$ is often formal, as $q$ is often discrete-valued (for example, it may represent a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483)). *q*-analogs find applications in a number of areas, including the study of [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913) and [multi-fractal measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-fractal_measure), and expressions for the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) of chaotic [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087). The relationship to fractals and dynamical systems results from the fact that many fractal patterns have the symmetries of [Fuchsian groups](wikipedia://en/471365) in general (see, for example [Indra's pearls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra's_Pearls_%28book%29) and the [Apollonian gasket](wikipedia://en/1049256)) and the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) in particular. The connection passes through [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) and [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986), where the [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960) and [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000) play a prominent role; the [*q*-series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-series) themselves are closely related to elliptic integrals.

*q*-analogs also appear in the study of [quantum groups](wikipedia://en/340678) and in *q*-deformed [superalgebras](wikipedia://en/469950). The connection here is similar, in that much of [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305) is set in the language of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), resulting in connections to [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225), which in turn relate to *q*-series.

## "Classical" *q*-theory {#"Classical"_q-theory}

Classical *q*-theory begins with the *q*-analogs of the nonnegative integers.[^o4] The equality

$$\lim_{q\rightarrow 1}\frac{1-q^n}{1-q}=n$$

suggests that we define the *q*-analog of *n*, also known as the ***q*-bracket** or ***q*-number** of *n*, to be

$$[n]_q=\frac{1-q^n}{1-q} = 1 + q + q^2 + \ldots + q^{n - 1}.$$

By itself, the choice of this particular *q*-analog among the many possible options is unmotivated. However, it appears naturally in several contexts. For example, having decided to use \[*n*\]<sub>*q*</sub> as the *q*-analog of *n*, one may define the *q*-analog of the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606), known as the [*q*-factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-factorial), by

$$\begin{aligned} \, [n]_q! & =[1]_q \cdot [2]_q \cdots [n-1]_q \cdot [n]_q \\[6pt] & =\frac{1-q}{1-q} \cdot \frac{1-q^2}{1-q} \cdots \frac{1-q^{n-1}}{1-q} \cdot \frac{1-q^n}{1-q} \\[6pt] & =1\cdot (1+q)\cdots (1+q+\cdots + q^{n-2}) \cdot (1+q+\cdots + q^{n-1}). \end{aligned}$$

This *q*-analog appears naturally in several contexts. Notably, while *n*! counts the number of [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of length *n*, \[*n*\]<sub>*q*</sub>! counts permutations while keeping track of the number of [inversions](wikipedia://en/21473801). That is, if inv(*w*) denotes the number of inversions of the permutation *w* and *S*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes the set of permutations of length *n*, we have

$$\sum_{w \in S_n} q^{\text{inv}(w)} = [n]_q ! .$$

In particular, one recovers the usual factorial by taking the limit as $q\rightarrow 1$.

The *q*-factorial also has a concise definition in terms of the [*q*-Pochhammer symbol](wikipedia://en/11149717), a basic building-block of all *q*-theories:

$$[n]_q!=\frac{(q;q)_n}{(1-q)^n}.$$

From the *q*-factorials, one can move on to define the ***q*-binomial coefficients**, also known as Gaussian coefficients, Gaussian polynomials, or [Gaussian binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/1353729):

$$\binom{n}{k}_q = \frac{[n]_q!}{[n-k]_q! [k]_q!}.$$

The [*q*-exponential](wikipedia://en/4120642) is defined as:

$$e_q(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x^n}{[n]_q!}.$$

*q*-trigonometric functions, along with a *q*-Fourier transform, have been defined in this context.

### Combinatorial *q*-analogs {#Combinatorial_q-analogs}

The Gaussian coefficients count subspaces of a finite [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Let *q* be the number of elements in a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615). (The number *q* is then a power of a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), *q* = *p*<sup>*e*</sup>, so using the letter *q* is especially appropriate.) Then the number of *k*-dimensional subspaces of the *n*-dimensional vector space over the *q*-element field equals

$$\binom nk_q .$$

Letting *q* approach 1, we get the binomial coefficient

$$\binom nk,$$

or in other words, the number of *k*-element subsets of an *n*-element set.

Thus, one can regard a finite vector space as a *q*-generalization of a set, and the subspaces as the *q*-generalization of the subsets of the set. As another example, the number of [flags](wikipedia://en/390646) is $[n]_q !$ as the order in which we build the flag matters, and after taking the limit we get $n!$. This has been a fruitful point of view in finding interesting new theorems. For example, there are *q*-analogs of [Sperner's theorem](wikipedia://en/748844)[^o6] and [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175).

### Cyclic sieving {#Cyclic_sieving}

Let *q* = (*e*<sup>2$\pi$*i*/*n*</sup>)<sup>*d*</sup> be the *d*-th power of a primitive *n*-th root of unity. Let *C* be a cyclic group of order *n* generated by an element *c*. Let *X* be the set of *k*-element subsets of the *n*-element set {1, 2, ..., *n*}. The group *C* has a canonical action on *X* given by sending *c* to the [cyclic permutation](wikipedia://en/449166) (1, 2, ..., *n*). Then the number of fixed points of *c*<sup>*d*</sup> on *X* is equal to

$$\binom nk_q .$$

## *q* → 1 {#q_→_1}

Conversely, by letting *q* vary and seeing *q*-analogs as deformations, one can consider the combinatorial case of *q* = 1 as a limit of *q*-analogs as *q* → 1 (often one cannot simply let *q* = 1 in the formulae, hence the need to take a limit).

This can be formalized in the [field with one element](wikipedia://en/13798040), which recovers combinatorics as linear algebra over the field with one element: for example, [Weyl groups](wikipedia://en/296332) are simple [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924) over the field with one element.

## Applications in the physical sciences {#Applications_in_the_physical_sciences}

*q*-analogs are often found in exact solutions of many-body problems. In such cases, the $q \to 1$ limit usually corresponds to relatively simple dynamics, e.g., without nonlinear interactions, while $q < 1$ gives insight into the complex nonlinear regime with feedbacks.

An example from atomic physics is the model of molecular condensate creation from an ultra cold fermionic atomic gas during a sweep of an external magnetic field through the [Feshbach resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feshbach_resonance).[^o10] This process is described by a model with a *q*-deformed version of the SU(2) algebra of operators, and its solution is described by *q*-deformed exponential and binomial distributions.
