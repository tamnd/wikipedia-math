# Dilemma

*Image: A Dilemma (BM 1945,0109.26).jpg.* Cartoon showing [William Ewart Gladstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ewart_Gladstone) in a dilemma: If he climbs to escape the guard dog he will face the man's wrath, but if he drops to avoid the man, the dog will attack him.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dilemma"}

A **dilemma** (grc) is a [problem](wikipedia://en/1467948) offering two possibilities, neither of which is unambiguously acceptable or preferable. The possibilities are termed the *horns* of the dilemma, a [clichéd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliché) usage, but distinguishing the dilemma from other kinds of predicament as a matter of usage.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

The term *dilemma* is attributed by [Gabriel Nuchelmans](wikipedia://en/25865208) to [Lorenzo Valla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenzo_Valla) in the 15th century, in later versions of his logic text traditionally called *Dialectica*. Valla claimed that it was the appropriate Latin equivalent of the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks) *dilemmaton*. Nuchelmans argued that his probable source was a logic text of c. 1433 of [George of Trebizond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_of_Trebizond).[^o5] He also concluded that Valla had reintroduced to the Latin West a type of argument that had fallen into disuse.[^o6]

Valla's [neologism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neologism) did not immediately take hold, preference being given to the established Latin term *complexio*, used by [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero), with *conversio* applied to the upsetting of dilemmatic reasoning. With the support of [Juan Luis Vives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Luis_Vives), however, *dilemma* was widely applied by the end of the 16th century.[^o7]

A dilemma is often phrased as "you must accept either A, or B", where A and B are propositions, each leading to some subsequent conclusion. In the case where this is true, it can be called a "dichotomy", but when it is not true, the dilemma constitutes a [false dichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_dichotomy), which is a logical [fallacy](wikipedia://en/53986). Traditional usage distinguished the dilemma as a "horned [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046)" from the [sophism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophism) that attracted the Latin name *cornutus*.[^o8] The original use of the word *horns* in English has been attributed to [Nicholas Udall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Udall) in his 1548 book *Paraphrases*, translating from the Latin term *cornuta interrogatio*.[^o9]

:::

## Dilemmatic arguments {#Dilemmatic_arguments}

The dilemma is sometimes used as a [rhetorical device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetorical_device). Its isolation as textbook material has been attributed to [Hermogenes of Tarsus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermogenes_of_Tarsus) in his work *On Invention*.[^o11] [C. S. Peirce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._S._Peirce) gave a definition of *dilemmatic argument* as any argument relying on [excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excluded_middle).[^o12]

### In logic {#In_logic}

In [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), *dilemma* is applied to a group of [rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference), which are in themselves valid rather than fallacious. They each have three premises, and include the [constructive dilemma](wikipedia://en/1316648) and [destructive dilemma](wikipedia://en/1316878).[^o14] Such arguments can be refuted by showing that the disjunctive premise—the "horns of the dilemma"—does not, in fact, hold, because it presents a [false dichotomy](wikipedia://en/17687). One is asked to accept "A or B", but counters by showing that that is not all. Successfully undermining that premise is called "escaping through the horns of the dilemma".[^o15]

### In philosophy {#In_philosophy}

Dilemmatic reasoning has been attributed to [Melissus of Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melissus_of_Samos), a [Presocratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presocratic) philosopher whose works survive in fragmentary form, making the origins of the technique in philosophy imponderable.[^o17] It was established with [Diodorus Cronus](wikipedia://en/2437676) (died c. 284 BCE).[^o18] The paradoxes of [Zeno of Elea](wikipedia://en/143331) were reported by [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) in dilemma form, but that may have been to conform with what [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) said about Zeno's style.[^o19]

### Moral dilemmas along with ethical dilemmas {#Moral_dilemmas_along_with_ethical_dilemmas}

In cases where two [moral principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_principle) appear to be inconsistent, an actor confronts a dilemma in terms of which principle to follow. This kind of moral case study is attributed to [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero), in book III of his *[De Officiis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Officiis)*.[^o21] In the Christian tradition of [casuistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casuistry), an approach to abstract ranking of principles introduced by [Bartolomé de Medina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartolomé_de_Medina_%28theologian%29) in the 16th century became tainted with the accusation of [laxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laxism), as did casuistry itself.[^o22] Another approach, with legal roots, is to lay emphasis on particular features present in a given case: in other words, the exact framing of the dilemma.[^o23]

::: {#o25 .statement cue=named-heading name="In law"}

### In law {#In_law}

In law, [Valentin Jeutner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentin_Jeutner) has argued that the term "legal dilemma" could be used as a term-of-art, to describe a situation where a legal subject is confronted with two or more legal norms that the legal subject cannot simultaneously comply with.[^o26]

Examples include contradictory contracts where one clause directly negates another clause, or conflicts between fundamental (e.g. [constitutional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_law)) legal norms. [Leibniz's](wikipedia://en/12281) 1666 doctoral dissertation *De casibus perplexis* (Perplexing Cases) is an early study of contradictory legal conditions.[^o27] In domestic law, it has been argued that the [German Constitutional Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Constitutional_Court) confronted a legal dilemma when determining, in connection with proceedings relating to the German [Aviation Security Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luftsicherheitsgesetz), whether a government official could intentionally kill innocent civilians by shooting down a hijacked airplane that would otherwise have crashed into a football stadium, killing tens of thousands.[^o28]

In international law, it has been suggested that the [International Court of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice) confronted a legal dilemma in its 1996 [Nuclear Weapons Advisory Opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Court_of_Justice_advisory_opinion_on_the_Legality_of_the_Threat_or_Use_of_Nuclear_Weapons). It was faced with the question whether, in an extreme circumstance of self-defence, it is a [state's right to self-defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-defence_in_international_law) or international law's general prohibition of [nuclear weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon) that should take priority.[^o29]
