# Theorem of the highest weight

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Theorem of the highest weight"}

In [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), a branch of mathematics, the **theorem of the highest weight** classifies the [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/318712#Classifying_finite-dimensional_representations_of_Lie_algebras) of a complex [semisimple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1337587) $\mathfrak g$.[^o2][^o3] There is a closely related theorem classifying the [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/750326#Representation_theory_of_a_connected_compact_Lie_group) of a connected compact [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $K$.[^o4] The theorem states that there is a bijection

:::

$$\lambda \mapsto [V^\lambda]$$

from the set of "dominant integral elements" to the set of equivalence classes of irreducible representations of $\mathfrak g$ or $K$. The difference between the two results is in the precise notion of "integral" in the definition of a dominant integral element. If $K$ is simply connected, this distinction disappears.

The theorem was originally proved by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) in his 1913 paper.[^o5] The version of the theorem for a compact Lie group is due to [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544). The theorem is one of the key pieces of [representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/62881966).

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

### Lie algebra case {#Lie_algebra_case}

Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a finite-dimensional semisimple [complex Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/62595897) with [Cartan subalgebra](wikipedia://en/1336000) $\mathfrak{h}$. Let $R$ be the associated [root system](wikipedia://en/277087). We then say that an element $\lambda\in\mathfrak h^*$ is **integral**[^o9] if

$$2\frac{\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}$$

is an integer for each root $\alpha$. Next, we choose a set $R^+$ of positive roots and we say that an element $\lambda\in\mathfrak h^*$ is **dominant** if $\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle\geq 0$ for all $\alpha\in R^+$. An element $\lambda\in\mathfrak h^*$ is **dominant integral** if it is both dominant and integral. Finally, if $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are in $\mathfrak h^*$, we say that $\lambda$ is **higher**[^o10] than $\mu$ if $\lambda-\mu$ is expressible as a linear combination of positive roots with non-negative real coefficients.

A [weight](wikipedia://en/277179#Weights_in_the_representation_theory_of_semisimple_Lie_algebras) $\lambda$ of a representation $V$ of $\mathfrak g$ is then called a **highest weight** if $\lambda$ is higher than every other weight $\mu$ of $V$.

The theorem of the highest weight then states:[^o3]

- If $V$ is a finite-dimensional irreducible representation of $\mathfrak{g}$, then $V$ has a unique highest weight, and this highest weight is dominant integral.
- If two finite-dimensional irreducible representations have the same highest weight, they are isomorphic.
- For each dominant integral element $\lambda$, there exists a finite-dimensional irreducible representation with highest weight $\lambda$.

The most difficult part is the last one; the construction of a finite-dimensional irreducible representation with a prescribed highest weight.

### The compact group case {#The_compact_group_case}

Let $K$ be a connected [compact Lie group](wikipedia://en/750326) with Lie algebra $\mathfrak k$ and let $\mathfrak g:=\mathfrak k+i\mathfrak k$ be the complexification of $\mathfrak k$. Let $T$ be a [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136) in $K$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak t$. Then $\mathfrak h:=\mathfrak t+i\mathfrak t$ is a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak g$, and we may form the associated root system $R$. The theory then proceeds in much the same way as in the Lie algebra case, with one crucial difference: the notion of integrality is different. Specifically, we say that an element $\lambda\in\mathfrak h$ is **analytically integral**[^o12] if

$$\langle\lambda,H\rangle$$

is an integer whenever

$$e^{2\pi H}=I$$

where $I$ is the identity element of $K$. Every analytically integral element is integral in the Lie algebra sense,[^o13] but there may be integral elements in the Lie algebra sense that are not analytically integral. This distinction reflects the fact that if $K$ is not simply connected, there may be representations of $\mathfrak g$ that do not come from representations of $K$. On the other hand, if $K$ is simply connected, the notions of "integral" and "analytically integral" coincide.[^o4]

The theorem of the highest weight for representations of $K$[^o14] is then the same as in the Lie algebra case, except that "integral" is replaced by "analytically integral."

## Proofs {#Proofs}

There are at least four proofs:

- Hermann Weyl's original proof from the compact group point of view,[^o17] based on the [Weyl character formula](wikipedia://en/5011916) and the [Peter–Weyl theorem](wikipedia://en/294474).
- The theory of [Verma modules](wikipedia://en/2975234) contains the highest weight theorem. This is the approach taken in many standard textbooks (e.g., Humphreys and Part II of Hall).
- The [Borel–Weil–Bott theorem](wikipedia://en/725331) constructs an irreducible representation as the space of global sections of an ample line bundle; the highest weight theorem results as a consequence. (The approach uses a fair bit of algebraic geometry but yields a very quick proof.)
- The [invariant theoretic](wikipedia://en/363762) approach: one constructs irreducible representations as subrepresentations of a tensor power of the standard representations. This approach is essentially due to H. Weyl and works quite well for classical groups.
