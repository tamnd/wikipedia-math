# Equidissection

*Image: Square dissected into 6 equal area triangles no border.svg.* A 6-equidissection of a square

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equidissection"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **equidissection** is a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) into [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) of equal [area](wikipedia://en/1209). The study of equidissections began in the late 1960s with [Monsky's theorem](wikipedia://en/35098575), which states that a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) cannot be equidissected into an odd number of triangles.[^n1] In fact, [most](wikipedia://en/1309) polygons cannot be equidissected at all.[^n2]

:::

Much of the literature is aimed at generalizing Monsky's theorem to broader classes of polygons. The general question is: Which polygons can be equidissected into how many pieces? Particular attention has been given to [trapezoids](wikipedia://en/201826), [kites](wikipedia://en/84840), [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306), [centrally symmetric polygons](wikipedia://en/55312103), [polyominos](wikipedia://en/72634), and [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783).[^n3]

Equidissections do not have many direct applications.[^n4] They are considered interesting because the results are counterintuitive at first, and for a geometry problem with such a simple definition, the theory requires some surprisingly sophisticated algebraic tools. Many of the results rely upon extending [*p*-adic valuations](wikipedia://en/16047618) to the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and extending [Sperner's lemma](wikipedia://en/465067) to more general [colored graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colored_graph).[^n5]

## Overview {#Overview}

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

A *dissection* of a polygon *P* is a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) of triangles that do not overlap and whose union is all of *P*. A dissection into *n* triangles is called an *n*-dissection, and it is classified as an *even dissection* or an *odd dissection* according to whether *n* is [even or odd](wikipedia://en/143135).[^n5]

An *equidissection* is a dissection in which every triangle has the same area. For a polygon *P*, the set of all *n* for which an *n*-equidissection of *P* exists is called the *spectrum* of *P* and denoted *S*(*P*). A general theoretical goal is to compute the spectrum of a given polygon.[^n6]

A dissection is called *[simplicial](wikipedia://en/252686)* if the triangles meet only along common edges. Some authors restrict their attention to simplicial dissections, especially in the secondary literature, since they are easier to work with. For example, the usual statement of Sperner's lemma applies only to simplicial dissections. Often simplicial dissections are called *[triangulations](wikipedia://en/679384)*, although the vertices of the triangles are not restricted to the vertices or edges of the polygon. Simplicial equidissections are therefore also called *equal-area triangulations*.[^n7]

The terms can be extended to higher-dimensional [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471): an equidissection is set of [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) having the same *n*-volume.[^n8]

:::

### Preliminaries {#Preliminaries}

It is easy to find an *n*-equidissection of a triangle for all *n*. As a result, if a polygon has an *m*-equidissection, then it also has an *mn*-equidissection for all *n*. In fact, often a polygon's spectrum consists precisely of the multiples of some number *m*; in this case, both the spectrum and the polygon are called *principal* and the spectrum is denoted $\langle m \rangle$.[^n2] For example, the spectrum of a triangle is $\langle 1 \rangle$. A simple example of a non-principal polygon is the quadrilateral with vertices (0, 0), (1, 0), (0, 1), (3/2, 3/2); its spectrum includes 2 and 3 but not 1.[^n9]

[Affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) of the plane are useful for studying equidissections, including [translations](wikipedia://en/204682), uniform and non-uniform [scaling](wikipedia://en/651752), [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829), [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789), [shears](wikipedia://en/799405), and other [similarities](wikipedia://en/39377) and [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102). Since an affine transformation preserves straight lines and ratios of areas, it sends equidissections to equidissections. This means that one is free to apply any affine transformation to a polygon that might give it a more manageable form. For example, it is common to choose coordinates such that three of the vertices of a polygon are (0, 1), (0, 0), and (1, 0).[^n10]

The fact that affine transformations preserve equidissections also means that certain results can be easily generalized. All results stated for a regular polygon also hold for [affine-regular polygons](wikipedia://en/36803313); in particular, results concerning the [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301) also apply to other parallelograms, including [rectangles](wikipedia://en/70048) and [rhombuses](wikipedia://en/183884). All results stated for polygons with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) coordinates also apply to polygons with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) coordinates, or polygons whose vertices fall on any other [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246).[^n11]

### Best results {#Best_results}

[Monsky's theorem](wikipedia://en/35098575) states that a square has no odd equidissections, so its spectrum is $\langle 2 \rangle$.[^n1] More generally, it is known that [centrally symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrally_symmetric) polygons and [polyominos](wikipedia://en/72634) have no odd equidissections.[^o21] A conjecture by [Sherman K. Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherman_K._Stein) proposes that no *special polygon* has an odd equidissection, where a special polygon is one whose [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) edges each sum to the [zero vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_vector). Squares, [centrally symmetric polygons](wikipedia://en/55312103), [polyominos](wikipedia://en/72634), and [polyhexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhex_%28mathematics%29) are all special polygons.[^n12]

For *n* > 4, the spectrum of a regular *n*-gon is $\langle n \rangle$.[^n13] For *n* > 1, the spectrum of an *n*-dimensional cube is $\langle n! \rangle$, where *n*! is the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) of *n*.[^n14] and the spectrum of an *n*-dimensional [cross-polytope](wikipedia://en/716401) is $\langle 2^{n-1} \rangle$. The latter follows [mutatis mutandis](wikipedia://en/742665) from the proof for the octahedron in [^n2]

Let *T*(*a*) be a [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/201826) where *a* is the ratio of parallel side lengths. If *a* is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024), then *T*(*a*) is principal. In fact, if *r*/*s* is a fraction in lowest terms, then $S(T(r/s)) = \langle r + s \rangle$.[^n15] More generally, all [convex polygons](wikipedia://en/660019) with rational coordinates can be equidissected,[^n16] although not all of them are principal; see the above example of a kite with a vertex at (3/2, 3/2).

At the other extreme, if *a* is a [transcendental number](wikipedia://en/30325), then *T*(*a*) has no equidissection. More generally, no polygon whose vertex coordinates are [algebraically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_independent) has an equidissection.[^o28] This means that [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) polygons with more than three sides cannot be equidissected. Although most polygons cannot be cut into equal-area triangles, all polygons can be cut into equal-area quadrilaterals.[^n17]

If *a* is an [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158) [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689), then *T*(*a*) is a trickier case. If *a* is algebraic of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) 2 or 3 ([quadratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_irrational) or cubic), and its [conjugates](wikipedia://en/458467) all have positive [real parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part), then *S*(*T*(*a*)) contains all sufficiently large *n* such that *n*/(1 + *a*) is an [algebraic integer](wikipedia://en/98981).[^n18] It is conjectured that a similar condition involving [stable polynomials](wikipedia://en/2057699) may determine whether or not the spectrum is empty for algebraic numbers *a* of all degrees.[^o31]

## History {#History}

The idea of an equidissection seems like the kind of elementary geometric concept that should be quite old. (Aigner Ziegler 2010) remark of Monsky's theorem, "one could have guessed that surely the answer must have been known for a long time (if not to the Greeks)."[^n19] But the study of equidissections did not begin until 1965, when Fred Richman was preparing a [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree) exam at [New Mexico State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_State_University).

::: {#o36 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Monsky's theorem"}

### Monsky's theorem {#Monsky's_theorem}

Richman wanted to include a question on geometry in the exam, and he noticed that it was difficult to find (what is now called) an odd equidissection of a square. Richman proved to himself that it was impossible for 3 or 5, that the existence of an *n*-equidissection implies the existence of an (*n* + 2)-dissection, and that certain quadrilaterals arbitrarily close to being squares have odd equidissections.[^n20] However, he did not solve the general problem of odd equidissections of squares, and he left it off the exam. Richman's friend John Thomas became interested in the problem; in his recollection,

"Everyone to whom the problem was put (myself included) said something like 'that is not my area but the question surely must have been considered and the answer is probably well known.' Some thought they had seen it, but could not remember where. I was interested because it reminded me of [Sperner's Lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperner's_Lemma) in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), which has a clever odd-even proof."[^n21]

Thomas proved that an odd equidissection was impossible if the coordinates of the vertices are rational numbers with odd denominators. He submitted this proof to *[Mathematics Magazine](wikipedia://en/4230329)*, but it was put on hold:

"The referee's reaction was predictable. He thought the problem might be fairly easy (although he could not solve it) and was possibly well-known (although he could find no reference to it)."[^n22]

The question was instead given as an Advanced Problem in the *[American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly)* (Richman Thomas 1967). When nobody else submitted a solution, the proof was published in *Mathematics Magazine* (Thomas 1968), three years after it was written. (Monsky 1970) then built on Thomas' argument to prove that there are no odd equidissections of a square, without any rationality assumptions.[^n22]

Monsky's proof relies on two pillars: a [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) result that generalizes Sperner's lemma and an [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) result, the existence of a [2-adic valuation](wikipedia://en/16047618) on the real numbers. A clever [coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) of the plane then implies that in all dissections of the square, at least one triangle has an area with what amounts to an even denominator, and therefore all equidissections must be even. The essence of the argument is found already in (Thomas 1968), but (Monsky 1970) was the first to use a 2-adic valuation to cover dissections with arbitrary coordinates.[^o46]

:::

::: {#o48 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The first generalization of Monsky's theorem was (Mead 1979), who proved that the spectrum of an *n*-dimensional cube is $\langle n! \rangle$. The proof is revisited by (Bekker Netsvetaev 1998).

Generalization to regular polygons arrived in 1985, during a geometry seminar run by G. D. Chakerian at [UC Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Davis). [Elaine Kasimatis](wikipedia://en/67205175), a graduate student, "was looking for some algebraic topic she could slip into" the seminar.[^n6] Sherman Stein suggested dissections of the square and the cube: "a topic that Chakerian grudgingly admitted was geometric."[^n6] After her talk, Stein asked about regular pentagons. Kasimatis answered with (Kasimatis 1989), proving that for *n* > 5, the spectrum of a regular *n*-gon is $\langle n \rangle$. Her proof builds on Monsky's proof, extending the *p*-adic valuation to the complex numbers for each prime [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of *n* and applying some elementary results from the theory of [cyclotomic fields](wikipedia://en/30872462). It is also the first proof to explicitly use an affine transformation to set up a convenient coordinate system.[^n23] (Kasimatis Stein 1990) then framed the problem of finding the spectrum of a general polygon, introducing the terms *spectrum* and *principal*.[^n6] They proved that almost all polygons lack equidissections, and that not all polygons are principal.[^n2]

(Kasimatis Stein 1990) began the study of the spectra of two particular generalizations of squares: trapezoids and kites. Trapezoids have been further studied by (Jepsen 1996), (Monsky 1996), and (Jepsen Monsky 2008). Kites have been further studied by (Jepsen Sedberry Hoyer 2009). General quadrilaterals have been studied in (Su Ding 2003). Several papers have been authored at [Hebei Normal University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebei_Normal_University), chiefly by Professor Ding Ren and his students Du Yatao and Su Zhanjun.[^o64]

Attempting to generalize the results for regular *n*-gons for even *n*, (Stein 1989) conjectured that no centrally symmetric polygon has an odd equidissection, and he proved the *n* = 6 and *n* = 8 cases. The full conjecture was proved by (Monsky 1990). A decade later, Stein made what he describes as "a surprising breakthrough", conjecturing that no polyomino has an odd equidissection. He proved the result of a polyomino with an odd number of squares in (Stein 1999). The full conjecture was proved when (Praton 2002) treated the even case.

The topic of equidissections has recently been popularized by treatments in *[The Mathematical Intelligencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Intelligencer)* (Stein 2004), a volume of the [Carus Mathematical Monographs](wikipedia://en/2941952) (Stein Szabó 2008), and the fourth edition of *[Proofs from THE BOOK](wikipedia://en/4500115)* (Aigner Ziegler 2010).

:::

## Related problems {#Related_problems}

(Sakai Nara Urrutia 2005) consider a variation of the problem: Given a convex polygon *K*, how much of its area can be covered by *n* non-overlapping triangles of equal area inside *K*? The ratio of the area of the best possible coverage to the area of *K* is denoted *t*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*). If *K* has an *n*-equidissection, then *t*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) = 1; otherwise it is less than 1. The authors show that for a quadrilateral *K*, *t*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) ≥ 4*n*/(4*n* + 1), with *t*<sub>2</sub>(*K*) = 8/9 if and only if *K* is affinely congruent to the trapezoid *T*(2/3). For a pentagon, *t*<sub>2</sub>(*K*) ≥ 2/3, *t*<sub>3</sub>(*K*) ≥ 3/4, and *t*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) ≥ 2*n*/(2*n* + 1) for *n* ≥ 5.

[Günter M. Ziegler](wikipedia://en/14628647) asked the converse problem in 2003: Given a dissection of the whole of a polygon into *n* triangles, how close can the triangle areas be to equal? In particular, what is the smallest possible difference between the areas of the smallest and largest triangles? Let the smallest difference be *M*(*n*) for a square and *M*(*a*, *n*) for the trapezoid *T*(*a*). Then *M*(*n*) is 0 for even *n* and greater than 0 for odd *n*. (Mansow 2003) gave the asymptotic upper bound *M*(*n*) = O(1/*n*<sup>2</sup>) (see [Big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578)).[^n24] (Schulze 2011) improves the bound to *M*(*n*) = O(1/*n*<sup>3</sup>) with a better dissection, and he proves that there exist values of *a* for which *M*(*a*, *n*) decreases arbitrarily quickly. (Labbé Rote Ziegler 2018) obtain a superpolynomial upper bound, derived from an explicit construction that uses the [Thue–Morse sequence](wikipedia://en/491903).
