# Ernst Zermelo

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ernst Zermelo"}

**Ernst Friedrich Ferdinand Zermelo** (; ; 27 July 1871 – 21 May 1953) was a German [logician](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), whose work has major implications for the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358). He is known for his role in developing [Zermelo–Fraenkel axiomatic set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) and his proof of the [well-ordering theorem](wikipedia://en/33458). Furthermore, his 1929[^o3] work on ranking chess players is the first description of a model for [pairwise comparison](wikipedia://en/4395832) that continues to have a profound impact on various applied fields utilizing this method.

:::

## Life {#Life}

*Image: Ernst Zermelo.jpeg.* Ernst Zermelo in Freiburg (1953)

Ernst Zermelo graduated from Berlin's Luisenstädtisches Gymnasium (now Heinrich-Schliemann-Oberschule) in 1889. He then studied [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) at the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin), the [University of Halle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Halle), and the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg). He finished his doctorate in 1894 at the University of Berlin, awarded for a dissertation on the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) (*Untersuchungen zur Variationsrechnung*). Zermelo remained at the University of Berlin, where he was appointed assistant to [Planck](wikipedia://en/19848), under whose guidance he began to study [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics). In 1897, Zermelo went to the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen), at that time the leading centre for mathematical research in the world, where he completed his [habilitation thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation_thesis) in 1899.

In 1910, Zermelo left Göttingen upon being appointed to the chair of mathematics at the [University of Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zurich), which he resigned in 1916. He was appointed to an honorary chair at the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg) in 1926, which he resigned in 1935 because he disapproved of [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler)'s regime.[^o5] At the end of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) and at his request, Zermelo was reinstated to his honorary position in Freiburg.

## Research in set theory {#Research_in_set_theory}

In 1900, in the Paris conference of the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842), [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) challenged the mathematical community with his famous [Hilbert's problems](wikipedia://en/154584), a list of 23 unsolved fundamental questions which mathematicians should attack during the coming century. The first of these, a problem of [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), was the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) introduced by [Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) in 1878, and in the course of its statement Hilbert also mentioned the need to prove the [well-ordering theorem](wikipedia://en/33458).

Zermelo began to work on the problems of set theory under Hilbert's influence and in 1902 published his first work concerning the addition of [transfinite cardinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_numbers). By that time he had also discovered the so-called [Russell paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_paradox) as evidenced by Husserl’s *Nachlass*.[^o7] In 1904, he succeeded in taking the first step suggested by Hilbert towards the continuum hypothesis when he proved the [well-ordering theorem](wikipedia://en/33458) (*every set can be well ordered*). This result brought fame to Zermelo, who was appointed Professor in Göttingen, in 1905. His proof of the [well-ordering theorem](wikipedia://en/33458), based on the powerset axiom and the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), was not accepted by all mathematicians, mostly because the axiom of choice was a paradigm of non-constructive mathematics. In 1908, Zermelo succeeded in producing an improved proof making use of Dedekind's notion of the "chain" of a set, which became more widely accepted; this was mainly because that same year he also offered an [axiomatization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatization) of set theory.

Zermelo began to axiomatize set theory in 1905; in 1908[^o8], he published his results despite his failure to prove the consistency of his axiomatic system. See the article on [Zermelo set theory](wikipedia://en/322623) for an outline of this paper, together with the original axioms, and the original numbering.

In 1922, [Abraham Fraenkel](wikipedia://en/528451) and [Thoralf Skolem](wikipedia://en/453765) independently expanded Zermelo's axiom system, adding the axioms of [replacement](wikipedia://en/52387) and [regularity](wikipedia://en/2113). The resulting system, now called [Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms](wikipedia://en/152214) (ZF), is now the most commonly used system for [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory).

## Zermelo's navigation problem {#Zermelo's_navigation_problem}

Proposed in 1931, [Zermelo's navigation problem](wikipedia://en/51357884) is a classic [optimal control](wikipedia://en/362565) problem. The problem deals with a boat navigating on a body of water, originating from a point O to a destination point D. The boat is capable of a certain maximum speed, and we want to derive the best possible control to reach D in the least possible time.

Without considering external forces such as current and wind, the optimal control is for the boat to always head towards D. Its path then is a line segment from O to D, which is trivially optimal. With consideration of current and wind, if the combined force applied to the boat is non-zero, the control for no current and wind does not yield the optimal path.
