# Carsten Niebuhr

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carsten Niebuhr"}

**Carsten Niebuhr**, or **Karsten Niebuhr** (17 March 1733 [Lüdingworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuxhaven) – 26 April 1815 [Meldorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meldorf), [Dithmarschen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dithmarschen)), was a German [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), [cartographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography), and [explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_exploration) in the service of [Denmark-Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark-Norway). He is renowned for participating in the Danish Arabia Expedition (1761–1767) and for publishing the first [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_language) language vocabulary in Latin script, in collaboration with the Tripolitan diplomat Abd al-Rahman Aga.

:::

He was the father of the Danish-German statesman and historian [Barthold Georg Niebuhr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barthold_Georg_Niebuhr), who published an account of his father's life in 1817.

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

*Image: Niebuhr.jpg.* Carsten Niebuhr in the attire of a distinguished Arab in Yemen, gift from al-Mahdi Abbas, Imam of Yemen

Niebuhr was born in Lüdingworth (now a part of [Cuxhaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuxhaven), [Lower Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Saxony)) in what was then [Bremen-Verden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bremen-Verden). His father Barthold Niebuhr (1704-1749) was a successful [farmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farmer) and owned his own property. Carsten and his sister were educated at home by a local school teacher, then he attended the Latin School in [Otterndorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otterndorf), near [Cuxhaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuxhaven).

Originally Niebuhr had intended to become a [surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), but in 1757 he went to the *Georgia Augusta* [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen), at this time Germany's most progressive institution of higher education. Niebuhr was probably a bright student because in 1760 [Johann David Michaelis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_David_Michaelis) (1717-1791) recommended him as a participant in the [Danish Arabia expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Arabia_expedition_%281761–1767%29) (1761-1767), mounted by [Frederick V of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_V_of_Denmark) (1722–1766). For a year and a half before the expedition Niebuhr studied mathematics, cartography and navigational astronomy under [Tobias Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobias_Mayer) (1723–1762), one of the premier astronomers of the 18th century, and the author of the [Lunar Distance Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance_%28navigation%29) for determining longitude. Niebuhr's observations during the Arabia Expedition proved the accuracy and the practicality of this method for use by mariners at sea.

## Expeditions {#Expeditions}

*Image: 1843 Murphy Map of Island of Bombay and Colaba.png.* Map of Bombay prepared by Niebuhr

The expedition sailed in January 1761 via [Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marseille) and [Malta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malta) to [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul) and [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria). Then the members of the expedition visited [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo) and [Sinai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinai_Peninsula), before traversing the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea) via [Jiddah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiddah) to [Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen), which was their main destination[^o5].

In [Mokha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mokha), on 25 May 1763, the expedition's philologist, [Frederik Christian von Haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederik_Christian_von_Haven), died, and on 11 July 1763, on the way to [Sanaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanaa), the capital of Yemen, its naturalist [Peter Forsskål](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Forsskål) also died.

In Sanaa the remaining members of the expedition had an audience with the Imam of Yemen [al-Mahdi Abbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mahdi_Abbas) (1719–1775), but suffered from the climate and returned to Mocha. Niebuhr seems to have preserved his own life and restored his health by adopting native dress and eating native food.[^n1]

From Mocha the expedition continued to [Bombay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumbai), the expedition's artist Georg Wilhelm Baurenfeind died on the 29th of August and the expedition's servant Lars Berggren on the following day; both were buried at sea. The surgeon Christian C. Kramer (1732–1763) also died, soon after landing in Bombay. Niebuhr was the only surviving member. He stayed in Bombay for fourteen months and then returned home by way of [Muscat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscat), [Bushire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushire), [Shiraz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiraz), and [Persepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persepolis).[^n1] His copies of the cuneiform inscriptions at Persepolis proved to be a key turning-point in the decipherment of cuneiform, and the birth of [Assyriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyriology).[^o8][^o9]

His transcriptions were especially useful to [Georg Friedrich Grotefend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Friedrich_Grotefend), who made the first correct decipherments of [Old Persian cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Persian_cuneiform):[^o10]

He also visited the ruins of [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon) (making many important sketches), [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad), [Mosul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosul), and [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo). He seems also to have visited the [Behistun Inscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behistun_Inscription) in around 1764. After a visit to [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus), he made a tour through [Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_%28region%29), crossed the [Taurus Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taurus_Mountains) to [Bursa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bursa), reached [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople) in February 1767 and finally arrived in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen) in the following November.[^n1]

*Image: Map of Yemen, prepared by Niebuhr.tif.* Map of Yemen, prepared by Niebuhr

Niebuhr's production during the expedition is indeed impressive. It includes small-scale maps and charts of [Yemen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yemen), the [Red Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea), the [Persian Gulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_Gulf) and [Oman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oman), and other larger scale maps covering the [Nile Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile_Delta), the [Gulf of Suez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Suez) and the regions surrounding various port cities he visited, including [Mocha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mocha,_Yemen) and [Surat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surat). He completed 28 town plans of significant historical value because of their uniqueness for that period.

In summary, Niebuhr's maps, charts and plans constitute the greatest single addition to the cartography of the region that was produced through field research and published in the 18th century.[^o14]

## Scientific collaboration with Abd-al-Rahman Aga {#Scientific_collaboration_with_Abd-al-Rahman_Aga}

In the summer of 1772, an embassy from the [Regency of Tripoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regency_of_Tripoli) arrived in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen). Carsten Niebhur was asked to assist the official interpreter, Andreas Æreboe, who did not have a sufficient command of Arabic.[^o16] The [embassy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomatic_mission) was led by the Tripolitan diplomat and statesman Abd-al-Rahman Aga, who had been a frequent visitor to European courts since 1763. The two men met for the first time at the Tripolitan ambassador’s residence on July 5, and then on numerous occasions thereafter, until November 1772.[^o17] During these meetings, a very fruitful intellectual collaboration developed, involving the exchange of [manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript) and translations.

Niebhur then wrote a series of articles drawing on the knowledge of Abd-al-Rahman Aga, as well as that of two of his enslaved servants who had accompanied him to Copenhagen—one from [Timbuktu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timbuktu) and the other from [Hausaland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausaland). This series of articles consists of “observations by a figure from Tripoli on the interior of Africa, cross-referenced with the accounts of two African servants or slaves, then analyzed and narrated by a German explorer specializing in Arabia”.[^o17] The text also contains the first list of [Hausa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausa_language) language vocabulary ever transcribed into Latin script.[^o18] It is therefore an exceptional source for the history of the co-production of knowledge between African and European intellectuals.

## Family and later career {#Family_and_later_career}

In 1773, Niebuhr married Christiane Sophia Blumenberg, the daughter of the crown physician, and for some years he held a post in the Danish military service, which enabled him to remain in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen). In 1776 he was elected a foreign member of the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences). In 1778 he accepted a position in the civil service of Danish [Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holstein), and went to reside at [Meldorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meldorf) ([Ditmarschen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ditmarschen)).[^n1] In 1806 he was promoted to *Etatsrat*, and in 1809 was made a Knight of the [Order of the Dannebrog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Dannebrog), one of [Denmark–Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark–Norway)'s most valued honours for service.

## Writing and research {#Writing_and_research}

Niebuhr's first book, *Beschreibung von Arabien*, was published in Copenhagen in 1772, the Danish government providing subsidies for the engraving and printing of its numerous illustrations. This was followed in 1774 and 1778 by the first two volumes of Niebuhr's *Reisebeschreibung nach Arabien und andern umliegender Ländern*. These works (particularly the one published in 1778), and most specifically the accurate copies of the cuneiform inscriptions found at Persepolis, were to prove to be extremely important to the decipherment of cuneiform writing. Before Niebuhr's publication, cuneiform inscriptions were often thought to be merely decorations and embellishments, and no accurate decipherments or translations had been made up to that point. Niebuhr demonstrated that the three trilingual inscriptions found at Persepolis were in fact three distinct forms of cuneiform writing (which he termed Class I, Class II, and Class III) to be read from left to right. His accurate copies of the trilingual inscriptions gave Orientalists the key to finally crack the cuneiform code, leading to the discovery of Old Persian, Akkadian, and Sumerian.[^o22]

*Image: Niebuhr - Kriegsübungen der Araber in Yemen.jpg.* "War Exercises of the Arabs in Yemen", original print from Niebuhr's *Reisebeschreibung nach Arabien und andern umliegender Ländern*, 1774-1778

The third volume of the *Reisebeschreibung*, also based on materials from the expedition, was not published till 1837, long after Niebuhr's death, under the editorship of his daughter and his assistant, Johan Nicolaus Gloyer. Niebuhr also contributed papers on the interior of [Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa), the political and military condition of the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire), and other subjects to a German periodical, the *[Deutsches Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsches_Museum_%28magazine%29)*. In addition, he edited and published the work of his friend Peter Forsskål, the naturalist on the Arabian expedition, under the titles *Descriptiones animalium, Flora Aegyptiaco-Arabica* and *Icones rerum naturalium* (Copenhagen, 1775 and 1776).[^n1]

Between 1790 and 1791, Niebuhr finally published the results of his collaboration with Abd-al-Rahman Aga and his two slaves in the Deutsches Museum's journal, *Neues Deutsches Museum*, in the form of four articles: “Das Innere von Afrika” (1790), “Noch Etwas Über die Mohammedanischen Freistaaten in der Barbarei” (1791), “Noch Etwas Über die Mohammedanischen Freistaaten in der Barbarei” (1791), and “Noch Etwas Über das Innere von Afrika” (1791).

[French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) and [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) translations of Niebuhr's narratives were published during his lifetime, and a condensed English translation of his own three volumes, prepared by [Robert Heron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Heron_%28writer%29), was published in Edinburgh in 1792, under the title "Travels through Arabia". A facsimile edition of this translation, as by "M. Niebuhr", was published in two volumes by the Libraire du Liban, Beirut (undated).

The government funds covered only a fraction of the printing costs for Niebuhr's first book, and probably a similar or smaller proportion of the costs for the other two volumes. To ensure that the volumes were published, Niebuhr had to pay over 80% of the costs himself. In all, Niebuhr devoted ten years of his life, the years 1768–1778, to the publication of six volumes of findings from the expedition. He had virtually no help from the academics who had conceived and shaped the expedition in [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen) and Copenhagen. It was only Niebuhr's determination to publish the findings of the expedition that ensured that the Danish Arabia expedition would produce results that would benefit the world of scholarship.[^o14]

## Death and legacy {#Death_and_legacy}

Niebuhr died in [Meldorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meldorf) in 1815.

[Johann Wolfgang von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe) (1749-1832) highly prized Niebuhr's works. In 1811 he wrote to Niebuhr's son, [Barthold Georg Niebuhr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barthold_Georg_Niebuhr), that "You carry a name which I have learned to honour since my youth."

Carsten Niebuhrs Gade, a street in the port area of [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen), is named after him.

In 2011, Copenhagen's National Library and National Museum held exhibitions of Carsten Niebuhr's life and work, celebrating the 250th anniversary of the Danish Arabia Expedition's commencement. Commemorative Carsten Niebuhr postal stamps were issued. And in the same year the Danish Ministry of Foreign Affairs had planned a series of cultural events based on the Expedition and Niebuhr's work that would take place in Ankara, Cairo, Damascus, Beirut, Tehran, and Yemen. It has been suggested that these efforts were intended in part to repair the reputational damage in the Islamic world caused by the [Danish cartoon controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_cartoon_controversy). Ultimately, the planned events were prevented by the [Arab Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_Spring).[^o25]
