# Normal matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Normal matrix"}

In mathematics, a [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) $A$ is **normal** if it [commutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commute_%28mathematics%29) with its [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) $A^{*}$:

:::

$$A \text{ normal} \iff A^*A = AA^* .$$

The concept of normal matrices can be extended to [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167) on [infinite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [normed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) and to [normal elements](wikipedia://en/21454688) in [C\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184). As in the matrix case, normality means commutativity is preserved, to the extent possible, in the noncommutative setting. This makes normal operators, and normal elements of C\*-algebras, more amenable to analysis.

The [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) states that a matrix is normal if and only if it is [unitarily similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_matrix) to a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080); implying in particular that any matrix $A$ satisfying the equation $A^{*}A = AA^{*}$ is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325). (The converse does not hold because diagonalizable matrices may have non-orthogonal eigenspaces.) Thus $A = U D U^*$ and $A^* = U D^* U^*$where $D$ is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal values are in general complex and $U$ is a unitary matrix.

The left and right singular vectors in the [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207) of a normal matrix $A = U D V^*$ differ only in complex phase from each other and from the corresponding eigenvectors, since the phase must be factored out of the eigenvalues to form singular values.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

Among complex matrices, all [unitary](wikipedia://en/129222), [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682), and [skew-Hermitian](wikipedia://en/509788) matrices are normal, with all eigenvalues being unit modulus, real, and imaginary, respectively. Likewise, among real matrices, all [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/105620), [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474), and [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055) matrices are normal, with all eigenvalues being complex conjugate pairs on the unit circle, real, and imaginary, respectively. However, it is *not* the case that all normal matrices are either unitary or (skew-)Hermitian, as their eigenvalues can be any complex number, in general. For example,

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

is neither unitary, Hermitian, nor skew-Hermitian, because its eigenvalues are $2, (1\pm i\sqrt{3})/2$; yet it is normal because

$$AA^* = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = A^*A.$$

:::

## Consequences {#Consequences}

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=math-theorem name="Proposition"}

**Proposition.**

A normal [triangular matrix](wikipedia://en/374222) is [diagonal](wikipedia://en/174080).

:::

::: {#o6 .proof cue=math-proof}

Let $A$ be any normal upper triangular matrix. Since

$$(A^* A)_{ii} = (A A^*)_{ii},$$

using subscript notation, one can write the equivalent expression using instead the $i$th unit vector ($\hat \mathbf e_i$) to select the $i$th row and $i$th column:

$$\hat \mathbf e_i^\intercal \left(A^* A\right) \hat \mathbf e_i = \hat \mathbf e_i^\intercal \left(A A^*\right) \hat \mathbf e_i.$$

The expression

$$\left( A \hat \mathbf e_i \right)^* \left( A \hat \mathbf e_i\right) = \left( A^* \hat \mathbf e_i \right)^* \left( A^* \hat \mathbf e_i\right)$$

is equivalent, and so is

$$\left \|A \hat \mathbf e_i \right\|^2 = \left \|A^* \hat \mathbf e_i \right \|^2,$$

which shows that the $i$th row must have the same norm as the $i$th column. Consider $i = 1$. The first entry of row 1 and column 1 are the same, and the rest of column 1 is zero (because of triangularity). This implies the first row must be zero for entries 2 through $n$. Continuing this argument for row–column pairs 2 through $n$ shows $A$ is diagonal. [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)

:::

The concept of normality is important because normal matrices are precisely those to which the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) applies:

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=math-theorem name="Proposition"}

**Proposition.**

A matrix $A$ is normal if and only if there exist a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) $\Lambda$ and a [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222) $U$ such that $A = U\Lambda U^{*}$.

:::

The diagonal entries of $\Lambda$ are the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $A$, and the columns of $U$ are the [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of $A$. The matching eigenvalues in $\Lambda$ come in the same order as the eigenvectors are ordered as columns of $U$.

Another way of stating the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) is to say that normal matrices are precisely those matrices that can be represented by a diagonal matrix with respect to a properly chosen [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) of $\mathbf{C}^{n}$. Phrased differently: a matrix is normal if and only if its [eigenspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) span $\mathbf{C}^{n}$ and are pairwise [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) with respect to the standard inner product of $\mathbf{C}^{n}$.

The spectral theorem for normal matrices is a special case of the more general [Schur decomposition](wikipedia://en/372514) which holds for all square matrices. Let $A$ be a square matrix. Then by Schur decomposition it is unitary similar to an upper-triangular matrix, say, $B$. If $A$ is normal, so is $B$. But then $B$ must be diagonal, for, as noted above, a normal upper-triangular matrix is diagonal.

The spectral theorem permits the classification of normal matrices in terms of their spectra, for example:

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=math-theorem name="Proposition"}

**Proposition.**

A normal matrix is unitary if and only if all of its eigenvalues (its spectrum) lie on the unit circle of the complex plane.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=math-theorem name="Proposition"}

**Proposition.**

A normal matrix is [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) if and only if its spectrum is contained in [$\mathbb{R}$](wikipedia://en/20646438). In other words: A normal matrix is [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) if and only if all its eigenvalues are [real](wikipedia://en/20646438).

:::

In general, the sum or product of two normal matrices need not be normal. However, the following holds:

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=math-theorem name="Proposition"}

**Proposition.**

If $A$ and $B$ are normal with $AB = BA$, then both $AB$ and $A + B$ are also normal. Furthermore there exists a unitary matrix $U$ such that $UAU^{*}$ and $UBU^{*}$ are diagonal matrices. In other words $A$ and $B$ are [simultaneously diagonalizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneously_diagonalizable).

:::

In this special case, the columns of $U^{*}$ are eigenvectors of both $A$ and $B$ and form an orthonormal basis in $\mathbf{C}^{n}$. This follows by combining the theorems that, over an algebraically closed field, [commuting matrices](wikipedia://en/25264990) are [simultaneously triangularizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneously_triangularizable) and a normal matrix is diagonalizable – the added result is that these can both be done simultaneously.

::: {#o12 .definition cue=heading name="Equivalent definitions"}

## Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

It is possible to give a fairly long list of equivalent definitions of a normal matrix. Let $A$ be a $n \times n$ complex matrix. Then the following are equivalent:

1. $A$ is normal.
1. $A$ is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325) by a unitary matrix.
1. There exists a set of eigenvectors of $A$ which forms an orthonormal basis for $\mathbf{C}^{n}$.
1. $\left\| A \mathbf{x} \right\| = \left\| A^* \mathbf{x} \right\|$ for every $\mathbf{x}$.
1. The [Frobenius norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_norm) of $A$ can be computed by the eigenvalues of $A$: $\operatorname{tr} \left(A^* A\right) = \sum_j \left| \lambda_j \right|^2$.
1. The [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) part $\tfrac{1}{2}(A + A^{*})$ and [skew-Hermitian](wikipedia://en/509788) part $\tfrac{1}{2}(A - A^{*})$ of $A$ commute.
1. $A^{*}$ is a polynomial (of degree $\le n - 1$) in $A$.[^o13]
1. $A^{*} = AU$ for some unitary matrix $U$.[^o14]
1. $U$ and $P$ commute, where we have the [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241) $A = UP$ with a unitary matrix $U$ and some [positive semidefinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) $P$.
1. $A$ commutes with some normal matrix $N$ with distinct eigenvalues.
1. $\sigma_{i} = \lvert \lambda_{i}\rvert$ for all $1 \le i \le n$ where $A$ has [singular values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_values) $\sigma_{1} \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_{n}$ and has eigenvalues that are indexed with ordering $\lvert \lambda_{1}\rvert \ge \cdots \ge \lvert \lambda_{n}\rvert$.[^o15]

Some but not all of the above generalize to normal operators on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. For example, a bounded operator satisfying (9) is only [quasinormal](wikipedia://en/7771798).

:::

## Normal matrix analogy {#Normal_matrix_analogy}

analogies in the below list It is occasionally useful (but sometimes misleading) to think of the relationships of special kinds of normal matrices as analogous to the relationships of the corresponding type of complex numbers of which their eigenvalues are composed. This is because any function (that can be expressed as a power series) of a non-defective matrix acts directly on each of its eigenvalues, and the conjugate transpose of its spectral decomposition $VD V^*$ is $VD^*V^*$, where $D$ is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Likewise, if two normal matrices commute and are therefore simultaneously diagonalizable, any operation between these matrices also acts on each corresponding pair of eigenvalues.

- The [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) is analogous to the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918).
- [Unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222) are analogous to [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).
- [Hermitian matrices](wikipedia://en/189682) are analogous to [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).
- Hermitian [positive definite matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) are analogous to [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571).
- [Skew Hermitian matrices](wikipedia://en/509788) are analogous to purely [imaginary numbers](wikipedia://en/14554).
- [Invertible matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_matrix) are analogous to non-zero [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).
- The inverse of a matrix has each eigenvalue inverted.
- A uniform [scaling matrix](wikipedia://en/651752) is analogous to a constant number.
- In particular, the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888) is analogous to 0, and
- the [identity](wikipedia://en/59718) matrix is analogous to 1.
- An [idempotent matrix](wikipedia://en/2974863) is an orthogonal projection with each eigenvalue either 0 or 1.
- A normal [involution](wikipedia://en/7917643) has eigenvalues $\pm 1$.

As a special case, the complex numbers may be embedded in the normal 2×2 real matrices by the mapping

$$a + bi \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ -b & a \end{bmatrix} = a\, \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + b\, \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\,.$$

which preserves addition and multiplication. It is easy to check that this embedding respects all of the above analogies.
