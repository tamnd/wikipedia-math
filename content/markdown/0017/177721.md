# Pythagorean comma

*Image: Pythagorean comma (difference A1-m2).PNG.* Pythagorean comma (**PC**) defined in [Pythagorean tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_tuning) as difference between semitones (A1 – m2), or interval between [enharmonically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enharmonic) notes (from Db to C\#). The [diminished second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_second) has the same width but an opposite direction (from to C\# to Db).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pythagorean comma"}

In [musical tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_tuning), the **Pythagorean comma** (or **ditonic comma**), named after the ancient mathematician and philosopher [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275), is the small [interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) (or [comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma_%28music%29)) existing in [Pythagorean tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_tuning) between two [enharmonically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enharmonic) notes such as C and B#, or Db and C#.[^o2] It is equal to the [frequency ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_ratio) $\tfrac{(1.5)^{12}}{2^{7}}$ = $\tfrac{531441}{524288}$ [&asymp;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/&asymp;) 1.01364, or about 23.46 [cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_%28music%29), roughly a quarter of a [semitone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone) (in between 75:74 and 74:73[^o3]). The comma that [musical temperaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_temperament) often "temper" is the Pythagorean comma.[^o4]

:::

The Pythagorean comma can be also defined as the difference between a [Pythagorean apotome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_apotome) and a [Pythagorean limma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_limma)[^o5] (i.e., between a chromatic and a diatonic [semitone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone), as determined in Pythagorean tuning); the difference between 12 [just](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_intonation) [perfect fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) and seven [octaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave); or the difference between three Pythagorean [ditones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ditone) and one octave. (This is why the Pythagorean comma is also called a *ditonic comma*.)

The [diminished second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diminished_second), in Pythagorean tuning, is defined as the difference between limma and apotome. It coincides, therefore, with the opposite of a Pythagorean comma, and can be viewed as a *descending* Pythagorean comma (e.g. from C# to Db), equal to about −23.46 cents.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

As described in the introduction, the Pythagorean comma may be derived in multiple ways:

- Difference between two [enharmonically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enharmonic) notes in a Pythagorean scale, such as C and B#, or Db and C# (see [below](wikipedia://en/177721#Circle_of_fifths_and_enharmonic_change)).
- Difference between [Pythagorean apotome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_apotome) and [Pythagorean limma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_limma).
- Difference between 12 just [perfect fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) and seven [octaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_octave).
- Difference between three Pythagorean [ditones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ditone) ([major thirds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third)) and one octave.

A just perfect fifth has a [frequency ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_ratio) of 3:2. It is used in Pythagorean tuning, together with the octave, as a yardstick to define, with respect to a given initial note, the frequency of any other note.

Apotome and limma are the two kinds of [semitones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone) defined in Pythagorean tuning. Namely, the apotome (about 113.69 cents, e.g. from C to C#) is the chromatic semitone, or augmented unison (A1), while the limma (about 90.23 cents, e.g. from C to Db) is the diatonic semitone, or minor second (m2).

A ditone (or [major third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_third)) is an interval formed by two [major tones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_tone). In Pythagorean tuning, a major tone has a size of about 203.9 cents (frequency ratio 9:8), thus a Pythagorean ditone is about 407.8 cents.

## Size {#Size}

*Image: Pythagorean\_tuning\_geometric.svg.* The Pythagorean comma shown as the gap (on the right side) which causes a 12-pointed star to fail to close, which star represents the Pythagorean scale; each line representing a just perfect fifth. That gap has a central angle of 7.038 degrees, which is 23.46% of 30 degrees.

The size of a Pythagorean comma, measured in [cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_%28music%29), is

$$\hbox{apotome} - \hbox{limma} \approx 113.69 - 90.23 \approx 23.46 ~\hbox{cents} \!$$

or more exactly, in terms of [frequency ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_ratio):

$$\frac{\hbox{apotome}}{\hbox{limma}} =\frac{3^7/2^{11}}{2^8/3^5} = \frac{3^{12}}{2^{19}} = \frac{531441}{524288} = 1.0136432647705078125 \!$$

## Circle of fifths and enharmonic change {#Circle_of_fifths_and_enharmonic_change}

The Pythagorean comma can also be thought of as the discrepancy between 12 [justly tuned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_intonation) [perfect fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fifth) (ratio 3:2) and seven octaves (ratio 2:1):

$$\frac{\hbox{twelve fifths}}{\hbox{seven octaves}} =\left(\tfrac32\right)^{12} \!\!\Big/\, 2^{7} = \frac{3^{12}}{2^{19}} = \frac{531441}{524288} = 1.0136432647705078125 \!$$

In the following table of [musical scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_scale) in the [circle of fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_fifths), the Pythagorean comma is visible as the small interval between, e.g., Fsharp and Gflat. Going around the circle of fifths with just intervals results in a [comma pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma_pump) by the Pythagorean comma.

The 6flat and the 6sharp scales are not identical—even though they are on the [piano keyboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piano_keyboard)—but the flat scales are one Pythagorean comma lower. Disregarding this difference leads to [enharmonic change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enharmonic).

*Image: Circle of fifths unrolled, pythagorean comma.svg.*

This interval has serious implications for the various [tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_tuning) schemes of the [chromatic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_scale), because in Western music, [12 perfect fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_fifths) and seven octaves are treated as the same interval. [Equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_temperament), today the most common tuning system in the West, reconciled this by flattening each fifth by a twelfth of a Pythagorean comma (approximately 2 cents), thus producing perfect octaves.

Another way to express this is that the just fifth has a frequency ratio (compared to the tonic) of 3:2 or 1.5 to 1, whereas the seventh semitone (based on 12 equal logarithmic divisions of an octave) is the seventh power of the [twelfth root of two](wikipedia://en/1129156) or 1.4983... to 1, which is not quite the same (a difference of about 0.1%). Take the just fifth to the 12th power, then subtract seven octaves, and you get the Pythagorean comma (about a 1.4% difference).

## History {#History}

The first to mention the comma's proportion of 531441:524288 was [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331), who takes as a basis the whole tone of Pythagorean tuning with the ratio of 9:8, the octave with the ratio of 2:1, and a number A = 262144. He concludes that raising this number by six whole tones yields a value, G, that is larger than that yielded by raising it by an octave (two times A). He gives G to be 531441.[^o10] The necessary calculations read:

Calculation of G:

$$262144 \cdot \left(\textstyle{\frac 9 8}\right)^6 = 531441$$

Calculation of the double of A:

$$262144 \cdot \left(\textstyle{\frac 2 1}\right)^1 = 524288$$

Chinese mathematicians were aware of the Pythagorean comma as early as 122 BC (its calculation is detailed in the *[Huainanzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huainanzi)*), and ca. 50 BC, [Jing Fang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jing_Fang) discovered that if the cycle of perfect fifths were continued beyond 12 all the way to 53, the difference between this 53rd pitch and the starting pitch would be much smaller than the Pythagorean comma. This much smaller interval was later named [Mercator's comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercator's_comma) (*see: [history of 53 equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53_equal_temperament#History_and_use)*).

In George Russell's *[Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydian_Chromatic_Concept_of_Tonal_Organization)* (1953), the half step between the Lydian Tonic and b2 in his Altered Major and Minor Auxiliary Diminished Blues scales is theoretically based on the Pythagorean comma.[^o11]
