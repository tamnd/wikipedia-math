# Complex conjugate

*Image: Complex conjugate picture.svg.* Geometric representation ([Argand diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argand_diagram)) of $z$ and its conjugate $\overline{z}$ in the complex plane. The complex conjugate is found by [reflecting](wikipedia://en/1390873) $z$ across the real axis.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complex conjugate"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **complex conjugate** of a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) is the number with an equal [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) part and an [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) part equal in [magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnitude_of_a_complex_number) but with opposite [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270). That is, if $a$ and $b$ are real numbers, then the complex conjugate of $a + bi$ is $a - bi$. The complex conjugate of $z$ is often denoted as $\overline{z}$ or $z^*$.

:::

In [polar form](wikipedia://en/25120#Complex_numbers), if $r$ and $\varphi$ are real numbers then the conjugate of $r e^{i \varphi}$ is $r e^{{(}}-i \varphi{{)}}$. This can be shown using [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613).

The product of a complex number and its conjugate is a real number: $a^2 + b^2$ (or $r^2$ in [polar coordinates](wikipedia://en/25120)).

If a root of a [univariate](wikipedia://en/2398982) polynomial with real coefficients is complex, then its [complex conjugate is also a root](wikipedia://en/9178245).

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

The complex conjugate of a complex number $z$ is written as $\overline z$ or $z^*$. The first notation, a [vinculum](wikipedia://en/673457), avoids confusion with the notation for the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859), which can be thought of as a generalization of the complex conjugate. The second is preferred in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), where [dagger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagger_%28mark%29) ($\dagger$) is used for the conjugate transpose, as well as electrical engineering and [computer engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_engineering), where bar notation can be confused for the [logical negation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_negation) ("NOT") [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844) symbol, while the bar notation is more common in [pure mathematics](wikipedia://en/457210).

If a complex number is [represented as a $2 \times 2$ matrix](wikipedia://en/5826#Matrix_representation_of_complex_numbers), the notations are identical, and the complex conjugate corresponds to the [matrix transpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_transpose), which is a flip along the diagonal.[^o4]

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The following properties apply for all complex numbers $z$ and $w$, unless stated otherwise, and can be proved by writing $z$ and $w$ in the form $a + b i$.

For any two complex numbers, conjugation is [distributive](wikipedia://en/103118) over addition, subtraction, multiplication and division:[^o7]

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{z + w} &= \overline{z} + \overline{w}, \\ \overline{z - w} &= \overline{z} - \overline{w}, \\ \overline{zw} &= \overline{z} \; \overline{w}, \quad \text{and} \\ \overline{\left(\frac{z}{w}\right)} &= \frac{\overline{z}}{\overline{w}},\quad \text{if } w \neq 0. \end{aligned}$$

A complex number is equal to its complex conjugate if its imaginary part is zero, that is, if the number is real. In other words, real numbers are the only [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) of conjugation.

Conjugation does not change the modulus of a complex number: $\left| \overline{z} \right| = |z|.$

Conjugation is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450), that is, the conjugate of the conjugate of a complex number $z$ is $z$. In symbols, $\overline{ \overline{z} } = z$.[^o7]

The product of a complex number with its conjugate is equal to the square of the number's modulus:

$$z\overline{z} = {\left| z \right|}^2.$$

This allows easy computation of the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) of a complex number given in rectangular coordinates:

$$z^{-1} = \frac{\overline{z}}{{\left| z \right|}^2},\quad \text{ for all } z \neq 0.$$

Conjugation is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) under composition with exponentiation to integer powers, with the exponential function, and with the natural logarithm for nonzero arguments:

$$\overline{z^n} = \left(\overline{z}\right)^n,\quad \text{ for all } n \in \mathbb{Z}$$

[^o8]

$$\exp\left(\overline{z}\right) = \overline{\exp(z)}$$

$$\ln\left(\overline{z}\right) = \overline{\ln(z)} \text{ if } z \text{ is not zero or a negative real number }$$

If $p$ is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) coefficients and $p(z) = 0,$ then $p\left(\overline{z}\right) = 0$ as well. Thus, non-real roots of real polynomials occur in complex conjugate pairs (*see* [Complex conjugate root theorem](wikipedia://en/9178245)).

In general, if $\varphi$ is a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) whose restriction to the real numbers is real-valued, and $\varphi(z)$ and $\varphi(\overline{z})$ are defined, then

$$\varphi\left(\overline{z}\right) = \overline{\varphi(z)}.\,\!$$

The map $\sigma(z) = \overline{z}$ from $\mathbb{C}$ to $\mathbb{C}$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) (where the topology on $\mathbb{C}$ is taken to be the standard topology) and [antilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antilinear), if one considers $\mathbb{C}$ as a complex [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over itself. Even though it appears to be a [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved) function, it is not [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110); it reverses orientation whereas holomorphic functions locally preserve orientation. It is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) and compatible with the arithmetical operations, and hence is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160). As it keeps the real numbers fixed, it is an element of the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of the [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$. This Galois group has only two elements: $\sigma$ and the identity on $\mathbb{C}$. Thus the only two field automorphisms of $\mathbb{C}$ that leave the real numbers fixed are the identity map and complex conjugation.

:::

## Use as a variable {#Use_as_a_variable}

Once a complex number $z = x + yi$ or $z = re^{i\theta}$ is given, its conjugate is sufficient to reproduce the parts of the $z$-variable:

- Real part: $x = \operatorname{Re}(z) = \dfrac{z + \overline{z}}{2}$
- Imaginary part: $y = \operatorname{Im}(z) = \dfrac{z - \overline{z}}{2i}$
- [Modulus (or absolute value)](wikipedia://en/991): $r= \left| z \right| = \sqrt{z\overline{z}}$
- [Argument](wikipedia://en/14699765): $e^{i\theta} = e^{i\arg z} = \sqrt{\dfrac{z}{\overline z}},$ so $\theta = \arg z = \dfrac{1}{i} \ln\sqrt{\frac{z}{\overline{z}}} = \dfrac{\ln z - \ln \overline{z}}{2i}$

Furthermore, $\overline{z}$ can be used to specify lines in the plane: the set

$$\left\{z : z \overline{r} + \overline{z} r = 0 \right\}$$

is a line through the origin and perpendicular to ${r},$ since the real part of $z\cdot\overline{r}$ is zero only when the cosine of the angle between $z$ and ${r}$ is zero. Similarly, for a fixed complex unit $u = e^{i b},$ the equation

$$\frac{z - z_0}{\overline{z} - \overline{z_0}} = u^2$$

determines the line through $z_0$ parallel to the line through 0 and $u$.

These uses of the conjugate of $z$ as a variable are illustrated in [Frank Morley](wikipedia://en/589167)'s book *Inversive Geometry* (1933), written with his son Frank Vigor Morley.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The other planar real unital algebras, [dual numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_numbers), and [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) are also analyzed using complex conjugation.

For matrices of complex numbers, $\overline{\mathbf{AB}} = \left(\overline{\mathbf{A}}\right) \left(\overline{\mathbf{B}}\right),$ where $\overline{\mathbf{A}}$ represents the element-by-element conjugation of $\mathbf{A}.$[^o12] Contrast this to the property $\left(\mathbf{AB}\right)^*=\mathbf{B}^* \mathbf{A}^*,$ where $\mathbf{A}^*$ represents the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of $\mathbf{A}.$

Taking the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) (or adjoint) of complex [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) generalizes complex conjugation. Even more general is the concept of [adjoint operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_operator) for operators on (possibly infinite-dimensional) complex [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932). All this is subsumed by the \*-operations of [C\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184).

One may also define a conjugation for [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) and [split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265): the conjugate of $a + bi + cj + dk$ is $a - bi - cj - dk$.

All these generalizations are multiplicative only if the factors are reversed:

$${\left(zw\right)}^* = w^* z^*.$$

Since the multiplication of planar real algebras is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), this reversal is not needed there.

There is also an abstract notion of conjugation for [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_spaces) $V$ over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). In this context, any [antilinear map](wikipedia://en/293432) $\varphi: V \to V$ that satisfies

1. $\varphi^2 = \operatorname{id}_V\,,$ where $\varphi^2 = \varphi \circ \varphi$ and $\operatorname{id}_V$ is the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) on $V$,
1. $\varphi(zv) = \overline{z} \varphi(v)$ for all $v \in V, z \in \mathbb{C},$ and
1. $\varphi\left(v_1 + v_2\right) = \varphi\left(v_1\right) + \varphi\left(v_2\right)\,$ for all $v_1, v_2 \in V,$

is called a complex conjugation, or a [real structure](wikipedia://en/10775645). As the involution $\varphi$ is [antilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antilinear), it cannot be the identity map on $V$.

Of course, $\varphi$ is a $\mathbb{R}$-linear transformation of $V,$ if one notes that every complex space $V$ has a real form obtained by taking the same [vectors](wikipedia://en/23218956) as in the original space and restricting the scalars to be real. The above properties actually define a real structure on the complex vector space $V$.[^o13]

One example of this notion is the conjugate transpose operation of complex matrices defined above. However, on generic complex vector spaces, there is no [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) notion of complex conjugation.
