# Splitting field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Splitting field"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **splitting field** of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) with [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) in a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is the smallest [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) of that field over which the polynomial *splits*, i.e., decomposes into [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polynomial) factors. For example, the polynomial $x^2 - 2$ does not factor over the rationals; its splitting field is the smallest extension of the rationals containing $\sqrt{2}$ and $-\sqrt{2}$.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **splitting field** of a polynomial *p*(*X*) over a field *K* is a field extension *L* of *K* over which *p* factors into linear factors

$$p(X) = c \prod_{i=1}^{\deg p} (X - a_i)$$

where $c \in K$ and for each $i$ we have $X - a_i \in L[X]$ with *a<sub>i</sub>* not necessarily distinct and such that the [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) *a<sub>i</sub>* generate *L* over *K*. The extension *L* is then an extension of minimal [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) over *K* in which *p* splits. It can be shown that such splitting fields exist and are unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). The amount of freedom in that isomorphism is known as the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of *p* (if we assume it is [separable](wikipedia://en/362400)).

A splitting field of a set *P* of polynomials is the smallest field over which each of the polynomials in *P* splits.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

An extension *L* that is a [splitting field for a set of polynomials](wikipedia://en/3129#Existence_of_an_algebraic_closure_and_splitting_fields) *p*(*X*) over *K* is called a [normal extension](wikipedia://en/11634#Normal,_separable_and_Galois_extensions) of *K*.

Given an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) *A* containing *K*, there is a unique splitting field *L* of *p* between *K* and *A*, generated by the roots of *p*. If *K* is a [subfield](wikipedia://en/11634) of the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the existence is immediate. On the other hand, the existence of [algebraic closures](wikipedia://en/3129) in general is often [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by 'passing to the limit' from the splitting field result, which therefore requires an independent proof to avoid [circular reasoning](wikipedia://en/68038).

Given a [separable extension](wikipedia://en/363340) *K*′ of *K*, a **Galois closure** *L* of *K*′ is a type of splitting field, and also a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) of *K* containing *K*′ that is minimal, in an obvious sense. Such a Galois closure should contain a splitting field for all the polynomials *p* over *K* that are [minimal polynomials](wikipedia://en/9667106) over *K* of elements of *K*′.

:::

## Constructing splitting fields {#Constructing_splitting_fields}

::: {#o8 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="The construction"}

### The construction {#The_construction}

Let *F* be a field and *p*(*X*) be a polynomial in the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) *F*\[*X*\] of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) *n*. The general process for constructing *K*, the splitting field of *p*(*X*) over *F*, is to construct a [chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_%28ordered_set%29) of fields $F=K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_{r-1} \subseteq K_r=K$ such that *K<sub>i</sub>* is an extension of *K*<sub>*i*−1</sub> containing a new root of *p*(*X*). Since *p*(*X*) has at most *n* roots the construction will require at most *n* extensions. The steps for constructing *K<sub>i</sub>* are given as follows:

- [Factorize](wikipedia://en/3776351#Factoring_over_algebraic_extensions_%28Trager's_method%29) *p*(*X*) over *K<sub>i</sub>* into [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) factors $f_1(X)f_2(X) \cdots f_k(X)$.
- Choose any nonlinear irreducible factor *f*(*X*).
- Construct the [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> of *K<sub>i</sub>* as the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> = *K*<sub>*i*</sub>\[*X*\] / (*f*(*X*)) where (*f*(*X*)) denotes the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) in *K*<sub>*i*</sub>\[*X*\] generated by *f*(*X*).
- Repeat the process for *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> until *p*(*X*) completely factors.

The irreducible factor *f*(*X*) used in the quotient construction may be chosen arbitrarily. Although different choices of factors may lead to different subfield sequences, the resulting splitting fields will be isomorphic.

Since *f*(*X*) is irreducible, (*f*(*X*)) is a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of *K*<sub>*i*</sub>\[*X*\] and *K*<sub>*i*</sub>\[*X*\] / (*f*(*X*)) is, in fact, a field, the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) for that maximal ideal. Moreover, if we let $\pi : K_i[X] \to K_i[X]/(f(X))$ be the natural projection of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) onto its quotient then

$$f(\pi(X)) = \pi(f(X)) = f(X)\ \bmod\ f(X) = 0$$

so *π*(*X*) is a root of *f*(*X*) and of *p*(*X*).

The degree of a single extension $[K_{i+1} : K_i]$ is equal to the degree of the irreducible factor *f*(*X*). The degree of the extension \[*K* : *F*\] is given by $[K_r : K_{r-1}] \cdots [K_2 : K_1] [K_1 : F]$ and is at most *n*!.

:::

### The field *K*<sub>*i*</sub>\[*X*\]/(*f*(*X*)) {#The_field_Ki[X]/(f(X))}

As mentioned above, the quotient ring *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> = *K*<sub>*i*</sub>\[*X*\]/(*f*(*X*)) is a field when *f*(*X*) is irreducible. Its elements are of the form

$$c_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + c_{n-2}\alpha^{n-2} + \cdots + c_1\alpha + c_0$$

where the *c<sub>j</sub>* are in *K<sub>i</sub>* and *α* = *π*(*X*). (If one considers *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over *K<sub>i</sub>* then the powers *α*<sup>*j*</sup> for 0 ≤ *j* ≤ *n*−1 form a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420).)

The elements of *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> can be considered as polynomials in *α* of degree less than *n*. Addition in *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> is given by the rules for polynomial addition, and multiplication is given by polynomial multiplication modulo *f*(*X*). That is, for *g*(*α*) and *h*(*α*) in *K*<sub>*i*+1</sub> their product is *g*(*α*)*h*(*α*) = *r*(α) where *r*(*X*) is the remainder of *g*(*X*)*h*(*X*) when divided by *f*(*X*) in *K*<sub>*i*</sub>\[*X*\].

The remainder *r*(*X*) can be computed through [polynomial long division](wikipedia://en/314205); however there is also a straightforward reduction rule that can be used to compute *r*(*α*) = *g*(*α*)*h*(*α*) directly. First let

$$f(X) = X^n + b_{n-1} X^{n-1} + \cdots + b_1 X + b_0.$$

The polynomial is over a field so one can take *f*(*X*) to be [monic](wikipedia://en/314730) [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000). Now *α* is a root of *f*(*X*), so

$$\alpha^n = -(b_{n-1} \alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + b_1 \alpha + b_0).$$

If the product *g*(*α*)*h*(*α*) has a term *α*<sup>*m*</sup> with *m* ≥ *n* it can be reduced as follows:

$$\alpha^n\alpha^{m-n} = -(b_{n-1} \alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + b_1 \alpha + b_0) \alpha^{m-n} = -(b_{n-1} \alpha^{m-1} + \cdots + b_1 \alpha^{m-n+1} + b_0 \alpha^{m-n}).$$

As an example of the reduction rule, take *K<sub>i</sub>* = **Q**\[*X*\], the ring of polynomials with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) coefficients, and take *f*(*X*) = *X*<sup>7</sup> − 2. Let $g(\alpha) = \alpha^5 + \alpha^2$ and *h*(*α*) = *α*<sup>3</sup> +1 be two elements of **Q**\[*X*\]/(*X*<sup>7</sup> − 2). The reduction rule given by *f*(*X*) is *α*<sup>7</sup> = 2 so

$$g(\alpha)h(\alpha) = (\alpha^5 + \alpha^2)(\alpha^3 + 1) = \alpha^8 + 2 \alpha^5 + \alpha^2 = (\alpha^7)\alpha + 2\alpha^5 + \alpha^2 = 2 \alpha^5 + \alpha^2 + 2\alpha.$$

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### The complex numbers {#The_complex_numbers}

Consider the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) **R**\[*x*\], and the [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) *x*<sup>2</sup> + 1. The [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) **R**\[*x*\] / (*x*<sup>2</sup> + 1) is given by the [congruence](wikipedia://en/48167) *x*<sup>2</sup> ≡ −1. As a result, the elements (or [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260)) of **R**\[*x*\] / (*x*<sup>2</sup> + 1) are of the form *a* + *bx* where *a* and *b* belong to **R**. To see this, note that since *x*<sup>2</sup> ≡ −1 it follows that *x*<sup>3</sup> ≡ −*x*, *x*<sup>4</sup> ≡ 1, *x*<sup>5</sup> ≡ *x*, etc.; and so, for example *p* + *qx* + *rx*<sup>2</sup> + *sx*<sup>3</sup> ≡ *p* + *qx* + *r*(−1) + *s*(−*x*) = (*p* − *r*) + (*q* − *s*)*x*.

The addition and multiplication operations are given by firstly using ordinary polynomial addition and multiplication, but then reducing modulo *x*<sup>2</sup> + 1, i.e. using the fact that *x*<sup>2</sup> ≡ −1, *x*<sup>3</sup> ≡ −*x*, *x*<sup>4</sup> ≡ 1, *x*<sup>5</sup> ≡ *x*, etc. Thus:

$$(a_1 + b_1x) + (a_2 + b_2x) = (a_1 + a_2) + (b_1 + b_2)x,$$

$$(a_1 + b_1x)(a_2 + b_2x) = a_1a_2 + (a_1b_2 + b_1a_2)x + (b_1b_2)x^2 \equiv (a_1a_2 - b_1b_2) + (a_1b_2 + b_1a_2)x \, .$$

If we identify *a* + *bx* with (*a*, *b*) then we see that addition and multiplication are given by

$$(a_1,b_1) + (a_2,b_2) = (a_1 + a_2,b_1 + b_2),$$

$$(a_1,b_1)\cdot (a_2,b_2) = (a_1a_2 - b_1b_2,a_1b_2 + b_1a_2).$$

We claim that, as a field, the quotient ring **R**\[*x*\] / (*x*<sup>2</sup> + 1) is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), **C**. A general complex number is of the form *a* + *bi*, where *a* and *b* are real numbers and *i*<sup>2</sup> = −1. Addition and multiplication are given by

$$(a_1 + b_1 i) + (a_2 + b_2 i) = (a_1 + a_2) + i(b_1 + b_2),$$

$$(a_1 + b_1 i) \cdot (a_2 + b_2 i) = (a_1a_2 - b_1b_2) + i(a_1b_2 + a_2b_1).$$

If we identify *a* + *bi* with (*a*, *b*) then we see that addition and multiplication are given by

$$(a_1,b_1) + (a_2,b_2) = (a_1 + a_2,b_1 + b_2),$$

$$(a_1,b_1)\cdot (a_2,b_2) = (a_1a_2 - b_1b_2,a_1b_2 + b_1a_2).$$

The previous calculations show that addition and multiplication behave the same way in **R**\[*x*\] / (*x*<sup>2</sup> + 1) and **C**. In fact, we see that the map between **R**\[*x*\] / (*x*<sup>2</sup> + 1) and **C** given by *a* + *bx* → *a* + *bi* is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) with respect to addition *and* multiplication. It is also obvious that the map *a* + *bx* → *a* + *bi* is both [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) and [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective); meaning that *a* + *bx* → *a* + *bi* is a [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) homomorphism, i.e., an [isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism). It follows that, as claimed: **R**\[*x*\] / (*x*<sup>2</sup> + 1) ≅ **C**.

In 1847, [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) used this approach to *define* the complex numbers.[^o13]

### Cubic example {#Cubic_example}

Let $K$ be the [rational number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number_field) $\mathbf{Q}$ and $p(x) = x^{3} - 2$. Each root of $p$ equals $\sqrt[3]{2}$ times a [cube root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_root_of_unity). Therefore, if we denote the cube roots of unity by

$$\omega_1 = 1,\,$$

$$\omega_2 = -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} i,$$

$$\omega_3 = -\frac{1}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} i.$$

any field containing two distinct roots of $p$ will contain the quotient between two distinct cube roots of unity. Such a quotient is a [primitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) cube root of unity—either $\omega_2$ or $\omega_3=1/\omega_2$. It follows that a splitting field $L$ of $p$ will contain *ω*<sub>2</sub>, as well as the real [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506) of 2; [conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), any extension of $\mathbf{Q}$ containing these elements contains all the roots of $p$. Thus

$$L = \mathbf{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega_2) = \{ a + b\sqrt[3]{2} + c{\sqrt[3]{2}}^2 + d\omega_2 + e\sqrt[3]{2}\omega_2 + f{\sqrt[3]{2}}^2 \omega_2 \mid a,b,c,d,e,f \in \mathbf{Q} \}$$

Note that applying the construction process outlined in the previous section to this example, one begins with $K_0 = \mathbf{Q}$ and constructs the field $K_1 = \mathbf{Q}[X] / (X^3 - 2)$. This field is not the splitting field, but contains one (any) root. However, the polynomial $Y^3 - 2$ is not [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) over $K_1$ and in fact:

$$Y^3 -2 = (Y - X)(Y^2 + XY + X^2).$$

Note that $X$ is not an [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29), and is in fact an element of $K_1$. Now, continuing the process, we obtain $K_2 = K_1[Y] / (Y^2 + XY + X^2)$, which is indeed the splitting field and is spanned by the $\mathbf{Q}$-basis $\{1, X, X^2, Y, XY, X^2 Y\}$. Notice that if we compare this with $L$ from above we can identify $X = \sqrt[3]{2}$ and $Y = \omega_2 \sqrt[3]{2}$.

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

- The splitting field of *x<sup>q</sup>* − *x* over **F**<sub>*p*</sub> is the unique [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) **F**<sub>*q*</sub> for *q* = *p<sup>n</sup>*.[^o16] Sometimes this field is denoted by GF(*q*).
- The splitting field of *x*<sup>2</sup> + 1 over **F**<sub>7</sub> is **F**<sub>49</sub>; the polynomial has no roots in **F**<sub>7</sub>, i.e., −1 is not a [square](wikipedia://en/659942) there, because 7 is not [congruent](wikipedia://en/20087) to 1 modulo 4.[^o17]
- The splitting field of *x*<sup>2</sup> − 1 over **F**<sub>7</sub> is **F**<sub>7</sub> since *x*<sup>2</sup> − 1 = (*x* + 1)(*x* − 1) already splits into linear factors.
- We calculate the splitting field of *f*(*x*) = *x*<sup>3</sup> + *x* + 1 over **F**<sub>2</sub>. It is easy to verify that *f*(*x*) has no roots in **F**<sub>2</sub>; hence *f*(*x*) is irreducible in **F**<sub>2</sub>\[*x*\]. Put *r* = *x* + (*f*(*x*)) in **F**<sub>2</sub>\[*x*\]/(*f*(*x*)) so **F**<sub>2</sub>(*r*) is a field and *x*<sup>3</sup> + *x* + 1 = (*x* + *r*)(*x*<sup>2</sup> + *ax* + *b*) in **F**<sub>2</sub>(*r*)\[*x*\]. Note that we can write + for − since the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) is two. Comparing coefficients shows that *a* = *r* and *b* = 1 + *r*<sup>2</sup>. The elements of **F**<sub>2</sub>(*r*) can be listed as *c* + *dr* + *er*<sup>2</sup>, where *c*, *d*, *e* are in **F**<sub>2</sub>. There are eight elements: 0, 1, *r*, 1 + *r*, *r*<sup>2</sup>, 1 + *r*<sup>2</sup>, *r* + *r*<sup>2</sup> and 1 + *r* + *r*<sup>2</sup>. Substituting these in *x*<sup>2</sup> + *rx* + 1 + *r*<sup>2</sup> we reach (*r*<sup>2</sup>)<sup>2</sup> + *r*(*r*<sup>2</sup>) + 1 + *r*<sup>2</sup> = *r*<sup>4</sup> + *r*<sup>3</sup> + 1 + *r*<sup>2</sup> = 0, therefore *x*<sup>3</sup> + *x* + 1 = (*x* + *r*)(*x* + *r*<sup>2</sup>)(*x* + (*r* + *r*<sup>2</sup>)) for *r* in **F**<sub>2</sub>\[*x*\]/(*f*(*x*)); *E* = **F**<sub>2</sub>(*r*) is a splitting field of *x*<sup>3</sup> + *x* + 1 over **F**<sub>2</sub>.
