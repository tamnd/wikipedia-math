# Uncorrelatedness

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Uncorrelatedness"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), two real-valued [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), $X$, $Y$, are said to be **uncorrelated** if their [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059), $\operatorname{cov}[X,Y] = \operatorname{E}[XY] - \operatorname{E}[X] \operatorname{E}[Y]$, is zero. If two variables are uncorrelated, there is no linear relationship between them.

:::

Uncorrelated random variables have a [Pearson correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/221708), when it exists, of zero, except in the trivial case when either variable has zero [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) (is a constant). In this case the correlation is undefined.

In general, uncorrelatedness is not the same as [orthogonality](wikipedia://en/102221), except in the special case where at least one of the two random variables has an [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of 0. In this case, the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) is the expectation of the product, and $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\operatorname{E}[XY] = 0$.

If $X$ and $Y$ are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence), with finite [second moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_moment), then they are uncorrelated. However, not all uncorrelated variables are independent.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Definition for two real random variables {#Definition_for_two_real_random_variables}

Two random variables $X,Y$ are called uncorrelated if their covariance $\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]=\operatorname{E}[(X-\operatorname{E}[X]) (Y-\operatorname{E}[Y])]$ is zero.[^o2][^o6] Formally:

### Definition for two complex random variables {#Definition_for_two_complex_random_variables}

Two [complex random variables](wikipedia://en/49434972) $Z,W$ are called uncorrelated if their covariance $\operatorname{K}_{ZW}=\operatorname{E}[(Z-\operatorname{E}[Z])\overline{(W-\operatorname{E}[W])}]$ and their pseudo-covariance $\operatorname{J}_{ZW}=\operatorname{E}[(Z-\operatorname{E}[Z]) (W-\operatorname{E}[W])]$ is zero, i.e.

$$Z,W \text{ uncorrelated} \quad \iff \quad \operatorname{E}[Z\overline{W}] = \operatorname{E}[Z] \cdot \operatorname{E}[\overline{W}] \text{ and } \operatorname{E}[ZW] = \operatorname{E}[Z] \cdot \operatorname{E}[W]$$

### Definition for more than two random variables {#Definition_for_more_than_two_random_variables}

A set of two or more random variables $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ is called uncorrelated if each pair of them is uncorrelated. This is equivalent to the requirement that the non-diagonal elements of the [autocovariance matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocovariance_matrix) $\operatorname{K}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}}$ of the [random vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) $\mathbf{X} = [X_1 \ldots X_n]^\mathrm{T}$ are all zero. The autocovariance matrix is defined as:

$$\operatorname{K}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}} = \operatorname{cov}[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}] = \operatorname{E}[(\mathbf{X}-\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{X}])(\mathbf{X}-\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{X}]))^{\rm T}]= \operatorname{E}[\mathbf{X} \mathbf{X}^T] - \operatorname{E}[\mathbf{X}]\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{X}]^T$$

:::

## Examples of dependence without correlation {#Examples_of_dependence_without_correlation}

### Example 1 {#Example_1}

- Let $X$ be a random variable that takes the value 0 with probability 1/2, and takes the value 1 with probability 1/2.
- Let $Y$ be a random variable, *independent* of $X$, that takes the value −1 with probability 1/2, and takes the value 1 with probability 1/2.
- Let $U$ be a random variable constructed as $U=XY$.

The claim is that $U$ and $X$ have zero covariance (and thus are uncorrelated), but are not independent.

Proof:

Taking into account that

$$\operatorname{E}[U] = \operatorname{E}[XY] = \operatorname{E}[X] \operatorname{E}[Y] = \operatorname{E}[X] \cdot 0 = 0,$$

where the second equality holds because $X$ and $Y$ are independent, one gets

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{cov}[U,X] & = \operatorname{E}[(U-\operatorname E[U])(X-\operatorname E[X])] = \operatorname{E}[ U (X-\tfrac12)] \\ & = \operatorname{E}[X^2 Y - \tfrac12 XY] = \operatorname{E}[(X^2-\tfrac12 X)Y] = \operatorname{E}[(X^2-\tfrac12 X)] \operatorname E[Y] = 0 \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, $U$ and $X$ are uncorrelated.

Independence of $U$ and $X$ means that for all $a$ and $b$, $\Pr(U=a\mid X=b) = \Pr(U=a)$. This is not true, in particular, for $a=1$ and $b=0$.

- $$\Pr(U=1\mid X=0) = \Pr(XY=1\mid X=0) = 0$$
- $$\Pr(U=1) = \Pr(XY=1) = 1/4$$

Thus $\Pr(U=1\mid X=0)\ne \Pr(U=1)$ so $U$ and $X$ are not independent.

Q.E.D.

### Example 2 {#Example_2}

If $X$ is a continuous random variable [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) on $[-1,1]$ and $Y = X^2$, then $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated even though $X$ determines $Y$ and a particular value of $Y$ can be produced by only one or two values of $X$ :

$$f_X(t)= {1 \over 2} I_{[-1,1]} ; f_Y(t)= {1 \over {2 \sqrt{t}}} I_{]0,1]}$$

on the other hand, $f_{X,Y}$ is 0 on the triangle defined by $0<X<Y<1$ although $f_X \times f_Y$ is not null on this domain. Therefore $f_{X,Y} (X,Y) \neq f_X (X) \times f_Y (Y)$ and the variables are not independent.

$$E[X] = {{1-1} \over 4} = 0 ; E[Y]= {{1^3 - (-1)^3}\over {3 \times 2} } = {1 \over 3}$$

$$Cov[X,Y]=E \left [(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y]) \right ] = E \left [X^3- {X \over 3} \right ] = {{1^4-(-1)^4}\over{4 \times 2}}=0$$

Therefore the variables are uncorrelated.

## When uncorrelatedness implies independence {#When_uncorrelatedness_implies_independence}

There are cases in which uncorrelatedness does imply independence. One of these cases is the one in which both random variables are two-valued (so each can be linearly transformed to have a [Bernoulli distribution](wikipedia://en/199189)).[^o14] Further, two [jointly normally distributed](wikipedia://en/50347) random variables are independent if they are uncorrelated,[^o15] although this does not hold for variables whose marginal distributions are normal and uncorrelated but whose [joint distribution](wikipedia://en/879637) is not joint normal (see [Normally distributed and uncorrelated does not imply independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normally_distributed_and_uncorrelated_does_not_imply_independent)).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Uncorrelated random vectors {#Uncorrelated_random_vectors}

Two [random vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_vector) $\mathbf{X}=(X_1,\ldots,X_m)^T$ and $\mathbf{Y}=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)^T$ are called uncorrelated if

$$\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{X} \mathbf{Y}^T] = \operatorname{E}[\mathbf{X}]\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{Y}]^T.$$

They are uncorrelated if and only if their [cross-covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/21163447) $\operatorname{K}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}$ is zero.[^o19]

Two complex random vectors $\mathbf{Z}$ and $\mathbf{W}$ are called **uncorrelated** if their cross-covariance matrix and their pseudo-cross-covariance matrix is zero, i.e. if

$$\operatorname{K}_{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{W}}=\operatorname{J}_{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{W}}=0$$

where

$$\operatorname{K}_{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{W}} =\operatorname{E}[(\mathbf{Z}-\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{Z}]){(\mathbf{W}-\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{W}])}^{\mathrm H}]$$

and

$$\operatorname{J}_{\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{W}} =\operatorname{E}[(\mathbf{Z}-\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{Z}]){(\mathbf{W}-\operatorname{E}[\mathbf{W}])}^{\mathrm T}].$$

### Uncorrelated stochastic processes {#Uncorrelated_stochastic_processes}

Two [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895) $\left\{X_t\right\}$ and $\left\{Y_t\right\}$ are called **uncorrelated** if their cross-covariance $\operatorname{K}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}(t_1,t_2) = \operatorname{E} \left[ \left( X(t_1)- \mu_X(t_1) \right) \left( Y(t_2)- \mu_Y(t_2) \right) \right]$ is zero for all times.[^o6] Formally:

$$\left\{X_t\right\},\left\{Y_t\right\} \text{ uncorrelated} \quad :\iff \quad \forall t_1,t_2 \colon \operatorname{K}_{\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Y}}(t_1,t_2) = 0.$$

:::
