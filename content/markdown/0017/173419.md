# Time domain

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Time domain"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), the **time domain** is a representation of how a signal, function, or data set varies with time.[^o2] It is used for the analysis of [mathematical functions](wikipedia://en/185427), physical [signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_%28information_theory%29) or [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) of [economic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) or [environmental](wikipedia://en/8919339) data.

:::

In the time domain, the [independent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable) is time, and the [dependent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable) is the value of the signal. This contrasts with the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346), where the signal is represented by its constituent frequencies. For [continuous-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous-time) signals, the value of the signal is defined for all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) representing time. For [discrete-time](wikipedia://en/40240263) signals, the value is known at discrete, often equally-spaced, time intervals.[^o3] It is commonly visualized using a graph where the x-axis represents time and the y-axis represents the signal's value.[^o4] An [oscilloscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscilloscope) is a common tool used to visualize real-world signals in the time domain.

Though most precisely referring to [time in physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_in_physics), the term *time domain* may occasionally informally refer to [position](wikipedia://en/1345771) in [space](wikipedia://en/27667) when dealing with [spatial frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_frequency), as a substitute for the more precise term *spatial domain*.

*Image: Fourier\_transform\_time\_and\_frequency\_domains\_(small).gif.* The [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) relates the function in the time domain, shown in red, to the function in the frequency domain, shown in blue. The component frequencies, spread across the frequency spectrum, are represented as peaks in the frequency domain.

## Origin of term {#Origin_of_term}

The use of the contrasting terms *time domain* and *[frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346)* developed in U.S. [communication engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_engineering) in the late 1940s, with the terms appearing together without definition by 1950.[^o6] When an analysis uses the [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second) or one of its multiples as a [unit of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement), then it is in the time domain. When analysis concerns the reciprocal units such as [Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz), then it is in the frequency domain.
