# Dirichlet convolution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirichlet convolution"}

*Image: Dirichlet.jpg.* Johann Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Dirichlet convolution** (or **divisor convolution**) is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) defined for [arithmetic functions](wikipedia://en/3170); it is important in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). It was developed by [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If $f , g : \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}$ are two [arithmetic functions](wikipedia://en/3170), their Dirichlet convolution $f*g$ is a new arithmetic function defined by:

$$(f*g)(n) \ =\ \sum_{d\,\mid \,n} f(d)\,g\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) \ =\ \sum_{ab\,=\,n}\!f(a)\,g(b),$$

where the sum extends over all positive [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) $d$ of $n$, or equivalently over all distinct pairs $(a,b)$ of positive integers whose product is $n$.

This product occurs naturally in the study of [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258) such as the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). It describes the multiplication of two Dirichlet series in terms of their coefficients:

$$\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{f(n)}{n^s}\right) \left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{g(n)}{n^s}\right) \ = \ \left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(f*g)(n)}{n^s}\right).$$

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The set of arithmetic functions forms a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the **Dirichlet ring**, with addition given by [pointwise addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointwise_addition) and multiplication by Dirichlet convolution. The multiplicative identity is the [unit function](wikipedia://en/2352048) $\varepsilon$ defined by $\varepsilon(n)=1$ if $n=1$ and $0$ otherwise. The [units](wikipedia://en/289666) (invertible elements) of this ring are the arithmetic functions $f$ with $f(1) \neq 0$. Since the non-units are closed under addition, this ring is [local](wikipedia://en/153391).

Specifically, Dirichlet convolution is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity),[^o6]

$$(f * g) * h = f * (g * h),$$

[distributive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity) over addition

$$f * (g + h) = f * g + f * h,$$

[commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity),

$$f * g = g * f,$$

and has an identity element,

$f * \varepsilon$ = $\varepsilon * f = f$.

Furthermore, for each function $f$ having $f(1) \neq 0$, there exists another arithmetic function $f^{-1}$ satisfying $f * f^{-1} = \varepsilon$, called the **Dirichlet inverse** of $f$.

The Dirichlet convolution of two [multiplicative functions](wikipedia://en/20059) is again multiplicative, and every not constantly zero multiplicative function has a Dirichlet inverse which is also multiplicative. In other words, multiplicative functions form a subgroup of the group of invertible elements of the Dirichlet ring. Beware however that the sum of two multiplicative functions is not multiplicative (since $(f+g)(1)=f(1)+g(1)=2 \neq 1$), so the subset of multiplicative functions is not a subring of the Dirichlet ring. The article on multiplicative functions lists several convolution relations among important multiplicative functions.

Another operation on arithmetic functions is pointwise multiplication: $fg$ is defined by $(fg)(n)=f(n)g(n)$. Given a [completely multiplicative function](wikipedia://en/1055940) $h$, pointwise multiplication by $h$ distributes over Dirichlet convolution: $(f * g)h = (fh) * (gh)$.[^o7] The convolution of two completely multiplicative functions is multiplicative, but not necessarily completely multiplicative.

:::

## Properties and examples {#Properties_and_examples}

In these formulas, we use the following [arithmetical functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetical_function):

- $\varepsilon$ is the multiplicative identity: $\varepsilon(1) = 1$, otherwise 0 ($\varepsilon(n)=\lfloor \tfrac1n \rfloor$).
- $1$ is the constant function with value 1: $1(n) = 1$ for all $n$. Keep in mind that $1$ is not the identity. (Some authors [denote this](wikipedia://en/166980#Special_elements) as $\zeta$ because the associated Dirichlet series is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).)
- $1_C$ for $C \subset \mathbb{N}$ is a set [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790): $1_C(n) = 1$ iff $n \in C$, otherwise 0.
- $\text{Id}$ is the identity function with value *n*: $\text{Id}(n) = n$.
- $\text{Id}_k$ is the *k*th power function: $\text{Id}_k(n)=n^k$.

The following relations hold:

- $1 * \mu = \varepsilon$, the Dirichlet inverse of the constant function $1$ is the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961) (see [proof](wikipedia://en/20961#Proof_of_the_formula_for_the_sum_of_μ_over_divisors)). Hence:
- $g = f * 1$ if and only if $f = g * \mu$, the [Möbius inversion formula](wikipedia://en/20963).
- $\sigma_k = \text{Id}_k * 1$, the [kth-power-of-divisors sum function](wikipedia://en/407249) *σ*<sub>*k*</sub>.
- $\sigma = \text{Id} * 1$, the sum-of-divisors function *σ* = *σ*<sub>1</sub>.
- $\tau = 1 * 1$ , the number-of-divisors function *τ*(*n*) = *σ*<sub>0</sub>.
- $\text{Id}_k = \sigma_k * \mu$, by Möbius inversion of the formulas for *σ*<sub>*k*</sub>, *σ*, and *τ*.
- $$\text{Id} = \sigma * \mu$$
- $$1 = \tau * \mu$$
- $\phi * 1 = \text{Id}$ , proved under [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452#Divisor_sum).
- $\phi = \text{Id} * \mu$ , by Möbius inversion.
- $\sigma = \phi * \tau$ , from convolving 1 on both sides of $\phi * 1 = \text{Id}$.
- $\lambda * |\mu| = \varepsilon$ where *λ* is [Liouville's function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville's_function).
- $\text{Id} * \phi = P$, where $P$ is [Pillai's arithmetical function](wikipedia://en/41198662), also known as the gcd-sum function.
- $\lambda * 1 = 1_{\text{Sq}}$ where Sq = {1, 4, 9, ...} is the set of squares.
- $$\text{Id}_k * (\text{Id}_k \mu) = \varepsilon$$
- $$\tau^3 * 1 = (\tau * 1)^2$$
- $J_k * 1 = \text{Id}_k$, [Jordan's totient function](wikipedia://en/19486387).
- $$(\text{Id}_s J_r) * J_s = J_{s + r}$$
- $\Lambda * 1 = \log$, where $\Lambda$ is [von Mangoldt's function](wikipedia://en/1928862).
- $|\mu| \ast 1 = 2^{\omega},$ where $\omega(n)$ is the [prime omega function](wikipedia://en/57197043) counting *distinct* prime factors of *n*.
- $\Omega \ast \mu = 1_{\mathcal{P}}$, the characteristic function of the prime powers.
- $\omega \ast \mu = 1_{\mathbb{P}}$ where $1_{\mathbb{P}}(n) \mapsto \{0,1\}$ is the characteristic function of the primes.

This last identity shows that the [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826) is given by the summatory function

$$\pi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} (\omega \ast \mu)(n) = \sum_{d=1}^{x} \omega(d) M\left(\left\lfloor \frac{x}{d} \right\rfloor\right)$$

where $M(x)$ is the [Mertens function](wikipedia://en/435639) and $\omega$ is the distinct prime factor counting function from above. This expansion follows from the identity for the sums over Dirichlet convolutions given on the [divisor sum identities](wikipedia://en/57218361) page (a standard trick for these sums).[^o9]

## Dirichlet inverse {#Dirichlet_inverse}

::: {#o12 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Given an arithmetic function $f$ its Dirichlet inverse $g = f^{-1}$ may be calculated recursively: the value of $g(n)$ is in terms of $g(m)$ for $m<n$.

For $n=1$:

$(f * g) (1) = f(1) g(1) = \varepsilon(1) = 1$, so

$g(1) = 1/f(1)$. This implies that $f$ does not have a Dirichlet inverse if $f(1) = 0$.

For $n=2$:

$$(f * g) (2) = f(1) g(2) + f(2) g(1) = \varepsilon(2) = 0,$$

$$g(2) = -(f(2) g(1))/f(1),$$

For $n=3$:

$$(f * g) (3) = f(1) g(3) + f(3) g(1) = \varepsilon(3) = 0,$$

$$g(3) = -(f(3) g(1))/f(1),$$

For $n=4$:

$$(f * g) (4) = f(1) g(4) + f(2) g(2) + f(4) g(1) = \varepsilon(4) = 0,$$

$$g(4) = -(f(4) g(1) + f(2) g(2))/f(1),$$

and in general for $n>1$,

$$g(n) \ =\ \frac {-1}{f(1)} \mathop{\sum_{d\,\mid \,n}}_{d < n} f\left(\frac{n}{d}\right) g(d).$$

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The following properties of the Dirichlet inverse hold:[^o15]

- The function *f* has a Dirichlet inverse if and only if *f*(1) ≠ 0.
- The Dirichlet inverse of a [multiplicative function](wikipedia://en/20059) is again multiplicative.
- The Dirichlet inverse of a Dirichlet convolution is the convolution of the inverses of each function: $(f \ast g)^{-1} = f^{-1} \ast g^{-1}$.
- A multiplicative function *f* is [completely multiplicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_multiplicative) if and only if $f^{-1}(n) = \mu(n) f(n)$.
- If *f* is [completely multiplicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_multiplicative) then $(f \cdot g)^{-1} = f \cdot g^{-1}$ whenever $g(1) \neq 0$ and where $\cdot$ denotes pointwise multiplication of functions.

:::

::: {#o17 .statement cue=named-heading name="Other formulas"}

### Other formulas {#Other_formulas}

::: {#o18 .table cue=table}

| Arithmetic function | Dirichlet inverse:[^o19] |
|---|---|
| Constant function with value 1 | [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961) *μ* |
| $n^{\alpha}$ | $\mu(n) \,n^\alpha$ |
| [Liouville's function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville's_function) *λ* | Absolute value of Möbius function $\lvert \mu\rvert$ |
| [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) $\varphi$ | $\sum_{d\|n} d\, \mu(d)$ |
| The [generalized sum-of-divisors function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_of_divisors) $\sigma_{\alpha}$ | $\sum_{d\|n} d^{\alpha} \mu(d) \mu\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)$ |

:::

An exact, non-recursive formula for the Dirichlet inverse of any [arithmetic function](wikipedia://en/3170) *f* is given in [Divisor sum identities](wikipedia://en/57218361#The_Dirichlet_inverse_of_an_arithmetic_function). A more [partition theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_theory) expression for the Dirichlet inverse of *f* is given by

$$f^{-1}(n) = \sum_{k=1}^{\Omega(n)} \left\{ \sum_{{\lambda_1+2\lambda_2+\cdots+k\lambda_k=n} \atop {\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_k | n}} \frac{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\cdots+\lambda_k)!}{1! 2! \cdots k!} (-1)^k f(\lambda_1) f(\lambda_2)^2 \cdots f(\lambda_k)^k\right\}.$$

The following formula provides a compact way of expressing the Dirichlet inverse of an invertible arithmetic function *f* :

$$f^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(f(1)\varepsilon-f)^{*k}}{f(1)^{k+1}}$$

where the expression $(f(1)\varepsilon-f)^{*k}$ stands for the arithmetic function $f(1)\varepsilon-f$ convoluted with itself *k* times. Notice that, for a fixed positive integer $n$, if $k>\Omega(n)$ then $(f(1)\varepsilon-f)^{*k}(n)=0$ , this is because $f(1)\varepsilon(1) - f(1) = 0$ and every way of expressing *n* as a product of *k* positive integers must include a 1, so the series on the right hand side converges for every fixed positive integer *n.*

:::

## Dirichlet series {#Dirichlet_series}

If *f* is an arithmetic function, the [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258) [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) is defined by

$$DG(f;s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{f(n)}{n^s}$$

for those [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) arguments *s* for which the series converges (if there are any). The multiplication of Dirichlet series is compatible with Dirichlet convolution in the following sense:

$$DG(f;s) DG(g;s) = DG(f*g;s)\,$$

for all *s* for which both series of the left hand side converge, one of them at least converging absolutely (note that simple convergence of both series of the left hand side *does not* imply convergence of the right hand side!). This is akin to the [convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/53268) if one thinks of Dirichlet series as a [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247).

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

The restriction of the divisors in the convolution to [unitary](wikipedia://en/5488222), [bi-unitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-unitary_divisor) or infinitary divisors defines similar commutative operations which share many features with the Dirichlet convolution (existence of a Möbius inversion, persistence of multiplicativity, definitions of totients, Euler-type product formulas over associated primes, etc.).

Dirichlet convolution is a special case of the convolution multiplication for the [incidence algebra](wikipedia://en/166980) of a [poset](wikipedia://en/23572), in this case the poset of positive integers ordered by divisibility.

The [Dirichlet hyperbola method](wikipedia://en/57667287) computes the summation of a convolution in terms of its functions and their summation functions.
