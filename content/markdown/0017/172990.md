# Use–mention distinction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Use–mention distinction"}

In [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211),[^o2] a fundamental distinction is made between the ordinary use of a term (a word, name, phrase, etc.) versus the self-aware mention of it.[^o3][^n1] The distinction between use and mention can be illustrated with the English word *cheese*:[^o3][^n1]

:::

1. Cheese is derived from milk.
1. *Cheese* is derived from the [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) word ċēse.

The first sentence is a statement about the edible substance called *cheese*. It demonstrates a use or usage; the writer is using the normal English word *cheese*, in its typical way, to refer to the common dairy product. The second is a statement about the very word *cheese* itself as a linguistic entity. This second sentence provides a mention; the writer is mentioning the word but not using it to refer to anything other than itself.

The use–mention distinction can sometimes be pedantic, especially in simple cases where it is obvious.[^o3][^o16] However, scholars argue that many philosophical works have been misguided, or misinterpreted by others, based on a failure to understand or recognize this basic distinction.[^o3]

## Overview {#Overview}

In written language, mentioned words or phrases often appear between single or double [quotation marks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotation_marks) or in [italics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italics). In philosophy, single quotation marks are typically used, while in other fields (such as linguistics) italics are more common.[^o7] Some style authorities, such as [Strunk and White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strunk_and_White), recommend that mentioned words or phrases should be visually distinct. On the other hand, used words or phrases do not carry typographic markings.[^o8]

The phenomenon of a term having different [references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/References) in various contexts was referred to as *[suppositio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supposition_Theory)* (supposition) by medieval logicians.[^o9] *Supposition* describes how a term is substituted in a sentence based on its referent. For nouns, a term can be used in different ways:

- With a concrete and real referent: "That is my *pig*." (personal supposition)
- With a concrete but unreal referent: "Santa Claus's *pig* is very big." (personal supposition)
- With a generic referent: "Any *pig* breathes air." (simple supposition)
- Metaphorically: "Your grandfather is a *pig*." (improper supposition)
- As a pure term: "*Pig* has only three letters." (material supposition)

The use–mention distinction is particularly significant in [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211).[^o10] Confusing use with mention can lead to misleading or incorrect statements, such as [category errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_error).

[Self-referential](wikipedia://en/28545) statements also engage the use–mention distinction and are often central to logical paradoxes, such as [Quine's paradox](wikipedia://en/752836). In mathematics, this concept appears in [Gödel's incompleteness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_incompleteness_theorem), where the [diagonal lemma](wikipedia://en/348969) plays a crucial role.

## Commentary {#Commentary}

[Stanisław Leśniewski](wikipedia://en/422271) extensively employed this distinction, noting the fallacies that can result from confusing it in [Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) and [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395)'s *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*.[^o12]

[Donald Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Davidson_%28philosopher%29) argued that quotation cannot always be treated as mere mention, giving examples where quotations carry both use and mention functions.[^o13]

[Douglas Hofstadter](wikipedia://en/8758) explains the distinction between use and mention as follows:[^o14] When a word is used to refer to something, it is being used. When a word is quoted, the focus is on its surface aspects, such as typography or phonetics, and it is being mentioned. Issues arise when a mention itself is mentioned. Notating this with italics or repeated quotation marks can lead to ambiguity.[^o15]

In *[Limited Inc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limited_Inc)*, a 1977 response to analytic philosopher [John Searle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Searle), [Jacques Derrida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Derrida) mentioned the distinction as "rather laborious and problematical".[^o16]
