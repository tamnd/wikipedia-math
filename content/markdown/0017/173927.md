# Ladislaus Bortkiewicz

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Ladislaus Bortkiewicz"}

**Ladislaus Josephovich Bortkiewicz** ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language) Владислав Иосифович Борткевич, [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language) *Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz* or *Ladislaus von Bortkewitsch*) (7 August 1868 – 15 July 1931) was a Russian [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist) and [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661) of [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland) ancestry. He wrote a book showing how the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144), a discrete [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543), can be useful in applied statistics, and he made contributions to [mathematical economics](wikipedia://en/18967255). He lived most of his professional life in [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany), where he taught at Strassburg University (Privatdozent, 1895–1897) and Berlin University (1901–1931).

:::

## Life and work {#Life_and_work}

Ladislaus Bortkiewicz was born in [Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg), [Imperial Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Russia), to two ethnic Polish parents: Józef Bortkiewicz and Helena Bortkiewicz (née Rokicka). His father was a [Polish nobleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szlachta) who served in the [Russian Imperial Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Russian_Army).

Bortkiewicz graduated from the Law Faculty in 1890. In 1898 he published a book about the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144), titled *The Law of Small Numbers*.[^o4] In this book he first noted that events with low frequency in a large population follow a Poisson distribution even when the probabilities of the events varied. It was that book that made the Prussian horse-kicking data famous. The data gave the number of soldiers killed by being kicked by a horse each year in each of 14 cavalry corps over a 20-year period. Bortkiewicz showed that those numbers followed a Poisson distribution. The book also examined data on child-suicides. Some[^o5] have suggested that the Poisson distribution should have been named the "Bortkiewicz distribution."

*Image: Bortkiewicz - Opere, 1971 - 5795778.tif.* Bortkiewicz' 1971 book *The economic theory of Marx*

In political economy, Bortkiewicz is important for his analysis of [Karl Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx)'s reproduction schema in the last two volumes of *[Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Das_Kapital)*. Bortkiewicz identified a [transformation problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_problem) in Marx's work. Making use of [Dmitriev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Karpovich_Dmitriev)'s analysis of [Ricardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ricardo), Bortkiewicz proved that the data used by Marx was sufficient to calculate the general profit rate and relative prices. Though Marx's transformation procedure was not correct—because it did not calculate prices and profit rate simultaneously, but sequentially—Bortkiewicz has shown that it is possible to get the correct results using the Marxian framework, i.e. using the Marxian variables constant capital and variable capital it is possible to obtain the profit rate and the relative prices in a three-sector model. This "correction of the Marxian system" has been the great contribution of Bortkiewicz to classical and Marxian economics but it was completely unnoticed until [Paul Sweezy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Sweezy)'s 1942 book "Theory of Capitalist Development". [Piero Sraffa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_Sraffa) (1960) has provided the complete generalization of the simultaneous method for classical and Marxian analysis.

Bortkiewicz died in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic). His papers, including a voluminous correspondence file (some 1,000 letters 1876–1931), were deposited at [Uppsala University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uppsala_University) in Sweden,[^o6] except for his correspondence with [Léon Walras](wikipedia://en/46769) which went into the collection of the Walras scholar [William Jaffé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jaffé) in the USA.
