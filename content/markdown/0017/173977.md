# Symplectic group

Classical

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symplectic group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **symplectic group** is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) that preserve the geometric structure of [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), the space of position and momentum variables used in [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586). It is defined as the group of linear changes of coordinates on phase space that preserve the [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/292852).

:::

The symplectic groups are usually denoted $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$, where $n$ is a positive integer and $\mathbb{F}$ is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), often the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). They are among the four families of [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878) and play a central role in [symplectic geometry](wikipedia://en/294298), [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319), and [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200). A related but different family is the **compact symplectic group**, usually denoted $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ or $\mathrm{USp}(n)$.

## Terminology and notation {#Terminology_and_notation}

The name "symplectic" was introduced by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) as a replacement for older terminology such as **line complex group**. It was intended as a Greek-based analogue of the word "[complex](wikipedia://en/5826)".

The notation $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ usually denotes the symplectic group of a $2n$-dimensional symplectic vector space over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $\mathbb{F}$. A related but different family is the **compact symplectic group**, denoted $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ or $\mathrm{USp}(n)$, which is the compact real form of the complex symplectic group.

Many authors use slightly different notations, often differing by factors of $2$. In [Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274)'s classification, the Lie algebra of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})$ has type $C_n$.

## $\operatorname{Sp}(2n, \mathbf{F})$

The symplectic group is a [classical group](wikipedia://en/10172878) defined as the set of [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformations) of a $2n$-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the field $\mathbb{F}$ which preserve a [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form) [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055) [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477). Such a vector space is called a [symplectic vector space](wikipedia://en/292852), and the symplectic group of an abstract symplectic vector space $V$ is denoted $\operatorname{Sp}(V)$. Upon fixing a basis for $V$, the symplectic form is represented by a [nonsingular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsingular_matrix) skew-symmetric matrix $J$, and $\operatorname{Sp}(V)$ is identified with the group of $2n\times 2n$ matrices over $\mathbb{F}$ satisfying

$$\{M \in M_{2n\times 2n}(\mathbb{F}) : M^\mathrm{T} J M = J\}.$$

This matrix group is denoted $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ or $\operatorname{Sp}(n,\mathbb{F})$, although the notation depends on the convention being used. Here $M^T$ denotes the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of $M$.

In an arbitrary basis, the matrix $J$ need not have any particular form. However, one can choose a *symplectic basis*, in which the form is represented by the standard matrix

$$\Omega = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$

where $I_n$ is the identity matrix. In such a basis,

$$\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})=\{M\in M_{2n\times 2n}(\mathbb{F}):M^\mathrm{T}\Omega M=\Omega\}.$$

In this case, $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ can be expressed as those block matrices $(\begin{smallmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{smallmatrix})$, where $A, B, C, D \in M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{F})$, satisfying the three equations:

$$\begin{aligned} -C^\mathrm{T}A + A^\mathrm{T}C &= 0, \\ -C^\mathrm{T}B + A^\mathrm{T}D &= I_n, \\ -D^\mathrm{T}B + B^\mathrm{T}D &= 0. \end{aligned}$$

Since all symplectic matrices have [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $1$, the symplectic group is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\operatorname{SL}(2n,\mathbb{F})$. When $n=1$, the symplectic condition on a matrix is satisfied [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the determinant is one, so that $\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{F})=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F})$. For $n>1$, there are additional conditions, i.e. $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ is then a proper subgroup of $\operatorname{SL}(2n,\mathbb{F})$.

Typically, the field $\mathbb{F}$ is the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$ or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$. In these cases $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ is a real or complex [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of real or complex dimension $n(2n+1)$, respectively. These groups are [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) but [non-compact](wikipedia://en/750326).

The [center](wikipedia://en/7125) of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ consists of the matrices $I_{2n}$ and $-I_{2n}$ as long as the [characteristic of the field](wikipedia://en/1066621) is not $2$.[^o3] Since the center of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ is discrete and its quotient modulo the center is a [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600), $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ is considered a [simple Lie group](wikipedia://en/292831#Comments_on_the_definition).

The real rank of the corresponding Lie algebra, and hence of the Lie group $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$, is $n$.

The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ is the set

$$\mathfrak{sp}(2n,F) = \{X \in M_{2n \times 2n}(F) : \Omega X + X^\mathrm{T} \Omega = 0\},$$

equipped with the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193#Ring_theory) as its Lie bracket.[^o4] For the standard skew-symmetric bilinear form $\Omega = (\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & I \\ -I & 0 \end{smallmatrix})$, this Lie algebra is the set of all block matrices $(\begin{smallmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{smallmatrix})$ subject to the conditions

$$\begin{aligned} A &= -D^\mathrm{T}, \\ B &= B^\mathrm{T}, \\ C &= C^\mathrm{T}. \end{aligned}$$

### $\operatorname{Sp}(2n, \mathbf{C})$

The symplectic group over the field of complex numbers is a [non-compact](wikipedia://en/750326), [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected), [simple Lie group](wikipedia://en/292831). The definition of this group includes *no* conjugates (contrary to what one might naively expect) but instead it is exactly the same as the definition bar the field change.[^n1]

### $\operatorname{Sp}(2n, \mathbf{R})$

$\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ is a real, non-compact, connected simple Lie group of dimension $n(2n+1)$.[^o7] It has fundamental group isomorphic to the integers under addition.[^n2] As the real form of a simple complex Lie algebra, its Lie algebra is the split real form of $\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})$.[^o7]

The exponential map from $\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ to $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ is not surjective.[^o8]

For every $S$ in $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$, one has a Cartan/polar decomposition[^o7][^o9]

$$S = OZO'$$

with $O,O' \in \operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})\cap\operatorname{SO}(2n)\cong\operatorname{U}(n)$ and $Z=\begin{pmatrix}D&0\\0&D^{-1}\end{pmatrix}$. As a manifold, $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ is diffeomorphic to $\operatorname{U}(n)$ times a vector space of dimension $n(n+1)$.[^o7]

### Generators {#Generators}

#### Infinitesimal generators {#Infinitesimal_generators}

The members of the symplectic Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ are the [Hamiltonian matrices](wikipedia://en/7852809).

These are matrices, $Q$ such that

$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & -A^\mathrm{T} \end{pmatrix}$$

where $B$ and $C$ are [symmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/126474). See [classical group](wikipedia://en/10172878) for a derivation.

#### Generation by transvections {#Generation_by_transvections}

The symplectic group is generated by its symplectic [transvections](wikipedia://en/799405). More precisely, if $(V,J)$ is a finite-dimensional symplectic vector space over $\mathbb{F}$, then ${\mathrm{Sp}(V,\omega)}$ is generated by the transformations

$$T_{v,\lambda}(x)=x+\lambda\,(x^TJv)\,v,$$

where $v\in V$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$. This is a general part of the structure theory of the classical groups, and appears in early form in work of Dieudonné; see also later treatments devoted specifically to symplectic transvections and the subgroups they generate.[^o13][^o14]

#### Generation by involutions {#Generation_by_involutions}

The symplectic group $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$ can be generated by linear transformations $A:V\to V$ of the symplectic vector space $(V,J)$ such that $A^2 = I$ and $A^TJA = -J$.[^o13][^o16]

### Example of symplectic matrices {#Example_of_symplectic_matrices}

For $\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R})$, the group of $2\times 2$ matrices with determinant $1$, the three symplectic $(0,1)$-matrices are:[^o18]

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\quad \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\quad \text{and} \quad \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

#### Sp(2n, R) {#Sp(2n,_R)}

It turns out that $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ can have a fairly explicit description using generators. If we let $\operatorname{Sym}(n)$ denote the symmetric $n\times n$ matrices, then $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ is generated by $D(n)\cup N(n) \cup \{\Omega\},$ where

$$\begin{aligned} D(n) &= \left\{ \left. \begin{bmatrix} A & 0 \\ 0 & (A^T)^{-1} \end{bmatrix} \,\right| \, A \in \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \right\} \\[6pt] N(n) &= \left\{ \left. \begin{bmatrix} I_n & B \\ 0 & I_n \end{bmatrix} \, \right| \, B \in \operatorname{Sym}(n)\right\} \end{aligned}$$

are subgroups of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$.[^o20][^o21]

### Relationship with symplectic geometry {#Relationship_with_symplectic_geometry}

[Symplectic geometry](wikipedia://en/294298) is the study of [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356). The [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at any point on a symplectic manifold is a [symplectic vector space](wikipedia://en/292852).[^o23] As noted earlier, structure preserving transformations of a symplectic vector space form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) and this group is $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{F})$, depending on the dimension of the space and the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) over which it is defined.

A symplectic vector space is itself a symplectic manifold. A transformation under an [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of the symplectic group is thus, in a sense, a linearised version of a [symplectomorphism](wikipedia://en/312304) which is a more general structure preserving transformation on a symplectic manifold.

## $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$

The **compact symplectic group**[^o24] $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ is the intersection of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})$ with the $2n\times 2n$ unitary group:

$$\operatorname{Sp}(n):=\operatorname{Sp}(2n;\mathbb{C})\cap\operatorname{U}(2n)=\operatorname{Sp}(2n;\mathbb{C})\cap\operatorname {SU} (2n).$$

It is sometimes written as $\operatorname{USp}(2n)$. Alternatively, $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ can be described as the subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{H})$ (invertible [quaternionic](wikipedia://en/51440) matrices) that preserves the standard [hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) on $\mathbb{H}^n$:

$$\langle x, y\rangle = \bar x_1 y_1 + \cdots + \bar x_n y_n.$$

That is, $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ is just the [quaternionic unitary group](wikipedia://en/10172878#Sp%28p,_q%29_–_the_quaternionic_unitary_group), $\operatorname{U}(n,\mathbb{H})$.[^o25] Indeed, it is sometimes called the **hyperunitary group**. Also $\operatorname{Sp}(1)$ is the group of quaternions of norm $1$, equivalent to $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ and topologically a [$3$-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) $\mathcal{S}^3$.

Note that $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ is *not* a symplectic group in the sense of the previous section—it does not preserve a non-degenerate skew-symmetric $\mathbb{H}$-bilinear form on $\mathbb{H}^n$: there is no such form except the zero form. Rather, it is isomorphic to a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})$, and so does preserve a complex [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/28356) in a vector space of twice the dimension. As explained below, the Lie algebra of $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ is the compact [real form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_form) of the complex symplectic Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})$.

$\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ is a real Lie group with (real) dimension $n(2n+1)$. It is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected).[^o26]

The Lie algebra of $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ is given by the quaternionic [skew-Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-Hermitian) matrices, the set of $n\times n$ quaternionic matrices that satisfy

$$A+A^{\dagger} = 0$$

where $A^{\dagger}$ is the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of $A$ (here one takes the quaternionic conjugate). The Lie bracket is given by the commutator.

### Important subgroups {#Important_subgroups}

Some main subgroups are:

$$\operatorname{Sp}(n) \supset \operatorname{Sp}(n-1)$$

$$\operatorname{Sp}(n) \supset \operatorname{U}(n)$$

$$\operatorname{Sp}(2) \supset \operatorname{O}(4)$$

Conversely it is itself a subgroup of some other groups:

$$\operatorname{SU}(2n) \supset \operatorname{Sp}(n)$$

$$\operatorname{F}_4 \supset \operatorname{Sp}(4)$$

$$\operatorname{G}_2 \supset \operatorname{Sp}(1)$$

There are also the [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) of the [Lie algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebras) $\mathfrak{sp}(2)=\mathfrak{so}(5)$ and $\mathfrak{sp}(1)=\mathfrak{so}(3)=\mathfrak{su}(2)$.

The unitary symplectic group $\operatorname{USp}(n)$ can be represented in terms of a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) defined as a [tensor product](wikipedia://en/372395) of quaternion algebras called hyperquaternion numbers. One has, $\mathbb{H}^{\otimes 2}=\mathbb{H}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{H}=M_{4\times 4}(\mathbb{R})= Cl_{3,1}\mathbb{(R)}$. Hence, $\mathbb{H}^{\otimes 3}=M_{4\times 4}(\mathbb{H})$ entails the compact symplectic group $\operatorname{USp}(4)$.[^o28]

## $\operatorname{Sp}(2n, \mathbf{Z})$

The integral symplectic group $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})$ is the subgroup of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ consisting of matrices with integer entries. Equivalently, it is the group of $2n\times 2n$ integer matrices preserving the standard symplectic form:

$$\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})=\{M\in M_{2n}(\mathbb{Z}): M^\mathrm{T}JM=J\},$$

where

$$J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_n\\-I_n&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

For $n=1$, this group coincides with the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.

The group $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})$ acts on the [Siegel upper half-space](wikipedia://en/1141106), the space of symmetric complex $n\times n$ matrices with positive-definite imaginary part, by fractional linear transformations

$$\gamma\cdot\tau=(A\tau+B)(C\tau+D)^{-1}, \qquad \gamma=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix}\in \operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z}).$$

For this reason, $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})$ is also called the [Siegel modular group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegel_modular_group) of degree $n$.

Its [congruence subgroups](wikipedia://en/382694), such as the principal congruence subgroup

$$\Gamma_n(m)=\{\gamma\in\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z}): \gamma\equiv I_{2n}\pmod m\},$$

play a central role in the theory of [Siegel modular forms](wikipedia://en/7721927). The quotient of Siegel upper half-space by $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{Z})$ is the moduli space of [principally polarized abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principally_polarized_abelian_variety) of dimension $n$, and quotients by congruence subgroups correspond to adding level structure.

Other [arithmetic subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_subgroup) of the symplectic group, such as [paramodular groups](wikipedia://en/44488972) are also studied in arithmetic geometry.

## Relationship between the symplectic groups {#Relationship_between_the_symplectic_groups}

Every complex, [semisimple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1337587) has a [split real form](wikipedia://en/22385144#Split_real_form) and a [compact real form](wikipedia://en/22385144#Compact_real_form); the former is called a [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) of the latter two.

The Lie algebra of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})$ is [semisimple](wikipedia://en/1337587) and is denoted $\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{C})$. Its [split real form](wikipedia://en/22385144#Split_real_form) is $\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ and its [compact real form](wikipedia://en/22385144#Compact_real_form) is $\mathfrak{sp}(n)$. These correspond to the Lie groups $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ and $\operatorname{Sp}(n)$ respectively.

The algebras, $\mathfrak{sp}(p,n-p)$, which are the Lie algebras of $\operatorname{Sp}(p,n-p)$, are the [indefinite signature](wikipedia://en/463225) equivalent to the compact form.

### Accidental isomorphisms {#Accidental_isomorphisms}

A number of [accidental isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/82804771) also exist, with various [spin groups](wikipedia://en/411231):

- $\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{F})\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{F})$ (in characteristic different from two), and $\operatorname{Sp}(2,\mathbb{R})\cong\operatorname{Spin}_0(1,2)$. This is because the special linear group automatically preserves any symplectic form in two dimensions.
- $$\operatorname{Sp}(1)\cong\operatorname{SU}(2)\cong\operatorname{Spin}(3)$$
- $$\operatorname{Spin}(4)\cong\operatorname{Sp}(1)\times\operatorname{Sp}(1)$$
- $\operatorname{Sp}(2)\cong\operatorname{Spin}(5)$. This isomorphism is exhibited by identifying $\operatorname{Sp}(2)$ with the group of $2\times 2$ quaternionic matrices $g\in M_2(\mathbb H)$ such that $g^*g=I$ where $g^*=\bar g^T$ is the conjugate-transpose of $x$. The [reduced trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_trace) on $M_2(\mathbb H)$ is invariant under $\operatorname{Sp}(2)$-conjugation, and the space of (reduced-)trace-free $x\in M_2(\mathbb H)$ such that $x^*=x$ is a five-dimensional euclidean space, with quadratic form $\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{red}}(x^2)$.
- $\operatorname{Sp}(1,1)\cong\operatorname{Spin}_0(1,4)$. This isomorphism is exhibited by identifying $\operatorname{Sp}(1,1)$ with the group of $2\times 2$ quaternionic matrices $g\in M_2(\mathbb H)$ such that $g^*Kg=K$ where $K=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1 &0\end{bmatrix}$ and $g^*=\bar g^T$ is the conjugate-transpose of $x$. The reduced trace is invariant under $\operatorname{Sp}(1,1)$-conjugation, and the space of (reduced-)trace-free $x\in M_2(\mathbb H)$ such that $Kx^*K=x$ is a five-dimensional pseudo-euclidean space, with quadratic form $\operatorname{tr}_{\mathrm{red}}(x^2)$, of signature $(1,4)$.
- $\operatorname{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R})\cong\operatorname{Spin}_0(2,3)$. Identify $\operatorname{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R})$ as the subset of the ring $M_4(\mathbb{R})$ of $4\times 4$ real matrices $g$ such that $gJg^T = J$ where $J=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\-I&0\end{bmatrix}$ (with $2\times 2$ blocks, and $I$ the $2\times 2$ identity matrix). The subspace $V\subset M_4(\mathbb{R})$ consisting of the trace-free matrices $J$ such that $x = -Jx^TJ$ is five-dimensional, and the trace form defines a signature $(2,3)$ pseudo-euclidean metric on it, which is preserved by the action $x\mapsto gxg^{-1}$ of $\operatorname{Sp}(4,\mathbb{R})$.

## Physical significance {#Physical_significance}

### Classical mechanics {#Classical_mechanics}

The real symplectic group $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ arises in [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) as the group of linear [canonical transformations](wikipedia://en/514534) of phase space. In canonical coordinates

$$(q^1,\dots,q^n,p_1,\dots,p_n),$$

its elements are precisely the linear changes of variables that preserve the standard symplectic form, or equivalently the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340).

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ is naturally identified with quadratic Hamiltonians on phase space: if

$$H(z)=\tfrac12 z^\mathrm{T}Kz,$$

then the corresponding Hamiltonian flow is linear and defines a one-parameter subgroup of $\operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$. In this sense, the symplectic group is generated by quadratic Hamiltonians.

### Quantum mechanics and the metaplectic group {#Quantum_mechanics_and_the_metaplectic_group}

The symplectic group acts by linear changes of coordinates in the phase space of classical mechanics. When one tries to make the same transformations act on the [wavefunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), there is a phase ambiguity, and it is necessary to pass to a [double cover](wikipedia://en/1378699). The [metaplectic group](wikipedia://en/2981795) is a double cover of the symplectic group over $\mathbb{R}$. (It has analogues over other [local fields](wikipedia://en/145375), [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615), and [adele rings](wikipedia://en/247978).)
