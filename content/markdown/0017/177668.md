# Voronoi diagram

*Image: Euclidean Voronoi diagram.svg.* 20 points and their Voronoi cells (larger version below)

*Image: Animated Voronoi Diagram.gif.* Voronoi diagram rendered in [Desmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desmos)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Voronoi diagram"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Voronoi diagram** is a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) into regions close to each of a given set of objects. It can be classified also as a [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671). In the simplest case, these objects are just finitely many points in the plane (called seeds, sites, or generators). For each seed there is a corresponding [region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_%28mathematics%29), called a **Voronoi cell** comprising all points of the plane closer to that seed than to any other. The Voronoi diagram of a set of points is [dual](wikipedia://en/609737) to that set's [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864).

:::

The Voronoi diagram is named after mathematician [Georgy Voronoy](wikipedia://en/3033246), and is also called a **Voronoi tessellation**, a **Voronoi decomposition**, a **Voronoi partition**, or a **Dirichlet tessellation** (after [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002)). Voronoi cells are also known as **Thiessen polygons**[^o2], after [Alfred H. Thiessen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_H._Thiessen).[^o3][^o4][^o5] Voronoi diagrams have practical and theoretical applications in many fields, mainly in [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and [technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technology), but also in [visual art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_art).[^o6][^o7]

## Simplest case {#Simplest_case}

In the simplest case, shown in the first picture, we are given a finite set of points $\{p_1,\dots p_n\}$ in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410). In this case, each point $p_k$ has a corresponding cell $R_k$ consisting of the points in the Euclidean plane for which $p_k$ is the nearest site: the distance to $p_k$ is less than or equal to the minimum distance to any other site $p_j$. For one other site $p_j$, the points that are closer to $p_k$ than to $p_j$, or equally distant, form a [closed half-space](wikipedia://en/647297), whose boundary is the [perpendicular bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of line segment $p_jp_k$. Cell $R_k$ is the intersection of all of these $n-1$ half-spaces, and hence it is a [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019).[^o9] When two cells in the Voronoi diagram share a boundary, it is a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860), [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29), or line, consisting of all the points in the plane that are equidistant to their two nearest sites. The [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of the diagram, where three or more of these boundaries meet, are the points that have three or more equally distant nearest sites.

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let $X$ be a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) with distance function $d$. Let $K$ be a set of indices and let $(P_k)_{k \in K}$ be a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) (indexed collection) of nonempty [subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsets) (the sites) in the space $X$. The Voronoi cell, or Voronoi region, $R_k$, associated with the site $P_k$ is the set of all points in $X$ whose distance to $P_k$ is not greater than their distance to the other sites $P_j$, where $j$ is any index different from $k$. In other words, if $d(x,\, A) = \inf\{d(x,\, a) \mid a \in A\}$ denotes the distance between the point $x$ and the subset $A$, then

$$R_k = \{x \in X \mid d(x, P_k) \leq d(x, P_j)\; \text{for all}\; j \neq k\}$$

The Voronoi diagram is simply the [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) of cells $(R_k)_{k \in K}$. In principle, some of the sites can intersect and even coincide (an application is described below for sites representing shops), but usually they are assumed to be disjoint. In addition, infinitely many sites are allowed in the definition (this setting has applications in [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153) and [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography)), but again, in many cases only finitely many sites are considered.

In the particular case where the space is a [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-dimensional) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), each site is a point, there are finitely many points and all of them are different, then the Voronoi cells are [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) and they can be represented in a combinatorial way using their vertices, sides, two-dimensional faces, etc. Sometimes the induced combinatorial structure is referred to as the Voronoi diagram. In general however, the Voronoi cells may not be convex or even connected.

In the usual Euclidean space, we can rewrite the formal definition in usual terms. Each Voronoi polygon $R_k$ is associated with a generator point $P_k$. Let $X$ be the set of all points in the Euclidean space. Let $P_1$ be a point that generates its Voronoi region $R_1$, $P_2$ that generates $R_2$, and $P_3$ that generates $R_3$, and so on. Then, as expressed by Tran *et al*,[^o12] "all locations in the Voronoi polygon are closer to the generator point of that polygon than any other generator point in the Voronoi diagram in Euclidean plane".

:::

## Illustration {#Illustration}

As a simple illustration, consider a group of shops in a city. Suppose we want to estimate the number of customers of a given shop. With all else being equal (price, products, quality of service, etc.), it is reasonable to assume that customers choose their preferred shop simply by distance considerations: they will go to the shop located nearest to them. In this case the Voronoi cell $R_k$ of a given shop $P_k$ can be used for giving a rough estimate on the number of potential customers going to this shop (which is modeled by a point in our city).

For most cities, the distance between points can be measured using the familiar [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932):

$$\ell_2 = d\left[\left(a_1, a_2\right), \left(b_1, b_2\right)\right] = \sqrt{\left(a_1 - b_1\right)^2 + \left(a_2 - b_2\right)^2}$$

or the [Manhattan distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_distance):

$$d\left[\left(a_1, a_2\right), \left(b_1, b_2\right)\right] = \left|a_1 - b_1\right| + \left|a_2 - b_2\right|$$

.

The corresponding Voronoi diagrams look different for different distance metrics.

## Properties {#Properties}

- The [dual graph](wikipedia://en/2536864) for a Voronoi diagram (in the case of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) with point sites) corresponds to the [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) for the same set of points.
- The [closest pair among the seed points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closest_pair_among_the_seed_points) necessarily must be found in two adjacent cells in the Voronoi diagram.
- If the setting is the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) and a discrete set of points is given, then two points of the set are adjacent on the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) if and only if their Voronoi cells share an infinitely long side.
- If the space is a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) and the distance to each site is attained (e.g., when a site is a [compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) or a closed ball), then each Voronoi cell can be represented as a union of line segments emanating from the sites.[^o16] As shown there, this property does not necessarily hold when the distance is not attained.
- Under relatively general conditions (the space is a possibly infinite-dimensional [uniformly convex space](wikipedia://en/9133418), there can be infinitely many sites of a general form, etc.) Voronoi cells enjoy a certain stability property: a small change in the shapes of the sites, e.g., a change caused by some translation or distortion, yields a small change in the shape of the Voronoi cells. This is the geometric stability of Voronoi diagrams.[^o17] As shown there, this property does not hold in general, even if the space is two-dimensional (but non-uniformly convex, and, in particular, non-Euclidean) and the sites are points.

:::

## History and research {#History_and_research}

Informal use of Voronoi diagrams can be traced back to [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes) in 1644.[^o19] [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) used two-dimensional and three-dimensional Voronoi diagrams in his study of quadratic forms in 1850. British physician [John Snow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Snow_%28physician%29) used a Voronoi-like diagram in 1854 to illustrate how the majority of people who died in the [Broad Street cholera outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak) lived closer to the infected [Broad Street pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho#Broad_Street_pump) than to any other water pump.

Voronoi diagrams are named after [Georgy Feodosievych Voronoy](wikipedia://en/3033246) who defined and studied the general *n*-dimensional case in 1908.[^o20] Voronoi diagrams that are used in [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics) and [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904) to analyse spatially distributed data are called Thiessen polygons after American meteorologist [Alfred H. Thiessen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_H._Thiessen), who used them to estimate rainfall from scattered measurements in 1911. Other equivalent names for this concept (or particular important cases of it): Voronoi polyhedra, Voronoi polygons, domain(s) of influence, Voronoi decomposition, Voronoi tessellation(s), Dirichlet tessellation(s).

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Coloured Voronoi 3D slice.svg.* This is a slice of the Voronoi diagram of a random set of points in a 3D box. In general, a cross section of a 3D Voronoi tessellation is a [power diagram](wikipedia://en/35793286), a weighted form of a 2d Voronoi diagram, rather than being an unweighted Voronoi diagram.

Voronoi tessellations of regular [lattices](wikipedia://en/469246) of points in two or three dimensions give rise to many familiar tessellations. The corresponding cells are known as [Wigner–Seitz cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigner–Seitz_cell).

- A 2D lattice gives an irregular honeycomb tessellation, with equal hexagons with point symmetry; in the case of a regular triangular lattice it is regular; in the case of a rectangular lattice the hexagons reduce to rectangles in rows and columns; a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) lattice gives the regular tessellation of squares; note that the rectangles and the squares can also be generated by other lattices (for example the lattice defined by the vectors (1,0) and (1/2,1/2) gives squares).
- A [simple cubic lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_cubic_lattice) gives the [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318).
- A [hexagonal close-packed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_close-packed) lattice gives a tessellation of space with [trapezo-rhombic dodecahedra](wikipedia://en/3521213).
- A [face-centred cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-centred_cubic) lattice gives a tessellation of space with [rhombic dodecahedra](wikipedia://en/607631).
- A [body-centred cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body-centred_cubic) lattice gives a tessellation of space with [truncated octahedra](wikipedia://en/279654).
- Parallel planes with regular triangular lattices aligned with each other's centers give the [hexagonal prismatic honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_prismatic_honeycomb).
- Certain body-centered tetragonal lattices give a tessellation of space with [rhombo-hexagonal dodecahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombo-hexagonal_dodecahedron).

For the set of points (*x*, *y*) with *x* in a discrete set *X* and *y* in a discrete set *Y*, we get rectangular tiles with the points not necessarily at their centers.

## Higher-order Voronoi diagrams {#Higher-order_Voronoi_diagrams}

Although a normal Voronoi cell is defined as the set of points closest to a single point in *S*, an *n*th-order Voronoi cell is defined as the set of points having a particular set of *n* points in *S* as its *n* nearest neighbors. Higher-order Voronoi diagrams also subdivide space.

Higher-order Voronoi diagrams can be generated recursively. To generate the *n*<sup>th</sup>-order Voronoi diagram from set *S*, start with the (*n* − 1)<sup>th</sup>-order diagram and replace each cell generated by *X* = {*x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*−1</sub>} with a Voronoi diagram generated on the set *S* − *X*.

### Farthest-point Voronoi diagram {#Farthest-point_Voronoi_diagram}

For a set of *n* points, the (*n* − 1)<sup>th</sup>-order Voronoi diagram is called a farthest-point Voronoi diagram.

For a given set of points *P* = {*p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *p*<sub>*n*</sub>}, the farthest-point Voronoi diagram divides the plane into cells in which the same point of *P* is the farthest point. A point of *P* has a cell in the farthest-point Voronoi diagram if and only if it is a vertex of the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of *P*. Let *H* = {*h*<sub>1</sub>, *h*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *h*<sub>*k*</sub>} be the convex hull of *P*; then the farthest-point Voronoi diagram is a subdivision of the plane into *k* cells, one for each point in *H*, with the property that a point *q* lies in the cell corresponding to a site *h*<sub>*i*</sub> if and only if d(*q*, *h*<sub>*i*</sub>) > d(*q*, *p*<sub>*j*</sub>) for each *p*<sub>*j*</sub> ∈ *P* with *h*<sub>*i*</sub> ≠ *p*<sub>*j*</sub>, where d(*p*, *q*) is the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between two points *p* and *q*.[^o25][^o26]

The boundaries of the cells in the farthest-point Voronoi diagram have the structure of a [topological tree](wikipedia://en/371914), with infinite [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28mathematics%29) as its leaves. Every finite tree is isomorphic to the tree formed in this way from a farthest-point Voronoi diagram.[^o27]

## Generalizations and variations {#Generalizations_and_variations}

As implied by the definition, Voronoi cells can be defined for metrics other than Euclidean, such as the [Mahalanobis distance](wikipedia://en/799760) or [Manhattan distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_distance). However, in these cases the boundaries of the Voronoi cells may be more complicated than in the Euclidean case, since the equidistant locus for two points may fail to be subspace of codimension 1, even in the two-dimensional case.

*Image: Approximate Voronoi Diagram.svg.* Approximate Voronoi diagram of a set of points. Notice the blended colors in the fuzzy boundary of the Voronoi cells.

A [weighted Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/23400470) is the one in which the function of a pair of points to define a Voronoi cell is a distance function modified by multiplicative or additive weights assigned to generator points. In contrast to the case of Voronoi cells defined using a distance which is a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), in this case some of the Voronoi cells may be empty. A [power diagram](wikipedia://en/35793286) is a type of Voronoi diagram defined from a set of circles using the [power distance](wikipedia://en/2094044); it can also be thought of as a weighted Voronoi diagram in which a weight defined from the radius of each circle is added to the [squared Euclidean distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squared_Euclidean_distance) from the circle's center.[^o29]

The Voronoi diagram of $n$ points in $d$-dimensional space can have $O(n^{\lceil d/2 \rceil})$ vertices, requiring the same bound for the amount of memory needed to store an explicit description of it. Therefore, Voronoi diagrams are often not feasible for moderate or high dimensions. A more space-efficient alternative is to use approximate Voronoi diagrams.[^o30]

Voronoi diagrams are also related to other geometric structures such as the [medial axis](wikipedia://en/4537039) (which has found applications in image segmentation, [optical character recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_character_recognition), and other computational applications), [straight skeleton](wikipedia://en/6757195), and [zone diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zone_diagram).

## Applications {#Applications}

### Meteorology/Hydrology {#Meteorology/Hydrology}

It is used in meteorology and engineering hydrology to find the weights for precipitation data of stations over an area (watershed). The points generating the polygons are the various station that record precipitation data. Perpendicular bisectors are drawn to the line joining any two stations. This results in the formation of polygons around the stations. The area $(A_i)$ touching station point is known as influence area of the station. The average precipitation is calculated by the formula $\bar{P}=\frac{\sum A_i P_i}{\sum A_i}$

### Humanities and social sciences {#Humanities_and_social_sciences}

- In [classical archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_archaeology), specifically [art history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_history), the symmetry of [statue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue) heads is analyzed to determine the type of statue a severed head may have belonged to. An example of this that made use of Voronoi cells was the identification of the [Sabouroff head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabouroff_head), which made use of a high-resolution [polygon mesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh).[^o71][^o72]
- In [dialectometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectometry), Voronoi cells are used to indicate a supposed linguistic continuity between survey points.
- In [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science), Voronoi diagrams have been used to study multi-dimensional, multi-party competition.[^o35]

### Natural sciences {#Natural_sciences}

*Image: Voronoi growth euclidean.gif.* A Voronoi tessellation emerges by radial growth from seeds outward.

- In [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), Voronoi diagrams are used to model a number of different biological structures, including [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28biology%29)[^o37] and [bone microarchitecture.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancellous_bone)[^o38] Indeed, Voronoi tessellations work as a geometrical tool to understand the physical constraints that drive the organization of biological tissues.[^o39]
- In [hydrology](wikipedia://en/13435), Voronoi diagrams are used to calculate the rainfall of an area, based on a series of point measurements. In this usage, they are generally referred to as Thiessen polygons.
- In [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology), Voronoi diagrams are used to study the growth patterns of forests and forest canopies, and may also be helpful in developing predictive models for forest fires.
- In [ethology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethology), Voronoi diagrams are used to model domains of danger in the [selfish herd theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfish_herd_theory).
- In [computational chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_chemistry), ligand-binding sites are transformed into Voronoi diagrams for [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) applications (e.g., to classify binding pockets in proteins).[^o40] In other applications, Voronoi cells defined by the positions of the nuclei in a molecule are used to compute [atomic charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_charge). This is done using the [Voronoi deformation density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voronoi_deformation_density) method.
- In [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), Voronoi diagrams are used to generate adaptative smoothing zones on images, adding signal fluxes on each one. The main objective of these procedures is to maintain a relatively constant [signal-to-noise ratio](wikipedia://en/41706) on all the images.
- In [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924), the Voronoi tessellation of a set of points can be used to define the computational domains used in [finite volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_volume) methods, e.g. as in the moving-mesh cosmology code AREPO.[^o41]
- In [computational physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_physics), Voronoi diagrams are used to calculate profiles of an object with [Shadowgraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadowgraph) and proton radiography in [High energy density physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_energy_density_physics).[^o42]

### Health {#Health}

- In [medical diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_diagnosis), models of muscle tissue, based on Voronoi diagrams, can be used to detect neuromuscular diseases.[^o39]
- In [epidemiology](wikipedia://en/66997), Voronoi diagrams can be used to correlate sources of infections in epidemics. One of the early applications of Voronoi diagrams was implemented by [John Snow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Snow_%28physician%29) to study the [1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1854_Broad_Street_cholera_outbreak) in Soho, England. He showed the correlation between residential areas on the map of Central London whose residents had been using a specific water pump, and the areas with the most deaths due to the outbreak.[^o44]

### Engineering {#Engineering}

- In [polymer physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer_physics), Voronoi diagrams can be used to represent free volumes of polymers.
- In [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science), polycrystalline microstructures in metallic alloys are commonly represented using Voronoi tessellations.
- In [island growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_growth), the Voronoi diagram is used to estimate the growth rate of individual islands.[^o46][^o47][^o48][^o49][^o50]
- In [solid-state physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_physics), the [Wigner-Seitz cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigner-Seitz_cell) is the Voronoi tessellation of a solid, and the [Brillouin zone](wikipedia://en/562067) is the Voronoi tessellation of reciprocal ([wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber)) space of crystals which have the symmetry of a space group.
- In [aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation), Voronoi diagrams are superimposed on oceanic plotting charts to identify the nearest airfield for in-flight diversion (see [ETOPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETOPS)), as an aircraft progresses through its flight plan.
- In [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), Voronoi patterns were the basis for the winning entry for the redevelopment of [The Arts Centre Gold Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Arts_Centre_Gold_Coast).[^o51]
- In [urban planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_planning), Voronoi diagrams can be used to evaluate the Freight Loading Zone system.[^o52]
- In [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining), Voronoi polygons are used to estimate the reserves of valuable materials, minerals, or other resources. Exploratory drillholes are used as the set of points in the Voronoi polygons.
- In [surface metrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_metrology), Voronoi tessellation can be used for [surface roughness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_roughness) modeling.[^o53]
- In [robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotics), some of the control strategies and path planning algorithms[^o54] of [multi-robot systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-agent_system) are based on the Voronoi partitioning of the environment.[^o55][^o56]

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

- A [point location](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_location) data structure can be built on top of the Voronoi diagram in order to answer [nearest neighbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearest_neighbor_search) queries, where one wants to find the object that is closest to a given query point. Nearest neighbor queries have numerous applications. For example, one might want to find the nearest hospital or the most similar object in a [database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database). A large application is [vector quantization](wikipedia://en/47805), commonly used in [data compression](wikipedia://en/8013).
- In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), Voronoi diagrams can be used to find the [largest empty circle](wikipedia://en/23268223) amid a set of points, and in an enclosing polygon; e.g. to build a new supermarket as far as possible from all the existing ones, lying in a certain city.
- Voronoi diagrams together with farthest-point Voronoi diagrams are used for efficient algorithms to compute the [roundness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundness_%28object%29) of a set of points.[^o25] The Voronoi approach is also put to use in the evaluation of circularity/[roundness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roundness_%28object%29) while assessing the dataset from a [coordinate-measuring machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate-measuring_machine).
- Zeroes of iterated derivatives of a rational function on the complex plane accumulate on the edges of the Voronoi diagam of the set of the poles ([Pólya's shire theorem](wikipedia://en/76539956)[^o58]).

### Informatics {#Informatics}

- In [networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network), Voronoi diagrams can be used in derivations of the capacity of a [wireless network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_network).
- In [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics), Voronoi diagrams are used to calculate 3D shattering / fracturing geometry patterns. It is also used to [procedurally generate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procedural_generation) organic or lava-looking textures.
- In autonomous [robot navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_navigation), Voronoi diagrams are used to find clear routes. If the points are obstacles, then the edges of the graph will be the routes furthest from obstacles (and theoretically any collisions).
- In [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), Voronoi diagrams are used to do [1-NN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-nearest_neighbor_algorithm) classifications.[^o60]
- In global scene reconstruction, including with random sensor sites and unsteady wake flow, geophysical data, and 3D turbulence data, Voronoi tesselations are used with [deep learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning).[^o61]
- In [user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_interface) development, Voronoi patterns can be used to compute the best hover state for a given point.[^o62]

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

Several efficient algorithms are known for constructing Voronoi diagrams, either directly (as the diagram itself) or indirectly by starting with a [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) and then obtaining its dual. Direct algorithms include [Fortune's algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune's_algorithm), an [O](wikipedia://en/44578)(*n* log(*n*)) algorithm for generating a Voronoi diagram from a set of points in a plane. [Bowyer–Watson algorithm](wikipedia://en/11454768), an [O](wikipedia://en/44578)(*n* log(*n*)) to [O](wikipedia://en/44578)(*n*<sup>2</sup>) algorithm for generating a Delaunay triangulation in any number of dimensions, can be used in an indirect algorithm for the Voronoi diagram.

For discrete grid approximations (such as pixel rendering), the [Jump Flooding Algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_Flooding_Algorithm) (JFA) and its variants are widely used on commodity graphics hardware ([GPUs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPU)). Originally introduced by Rong Guodong in 2006, the standard JFA computes an approximate Voronoi diagram in $O(\log N)$ parallel passes over an $N \times N$ grid.[^o65][^o66] Subsequent optimizations, such as the 2019 randomized [JFA-star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_flooding_algorithm#Complexity_Comparison) variant introduced by Maciej A. Czyzewski, shift the pass complexity from scaling with grid resolution to the total number of seed points ($n$), achieving faster convergence in roughly $O(\log^* n)$ passes.[^o67]

[Lloyd's algorithm](wikipedia://en/2607912) and its generalization via the [Linde–Buzo–Gray algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linde–Buzo–Gray_algorithm) (aka [k-means clustering](wikipedia://en/1860407)) use the construction of Voronoi diagrams as a subroutine. These methods alternate between steps in which one constructs the Voronoi diagram for a set of seed points, and steps in which the seed points are moved to new locations that are more central within their cells. These methods can be used in spaces of arbitrary dimension to iteratively converge towards a specialized form of the Voronoi diagram, called a [Centroidal Voronoi tessellation](wikipedia://en/14087640), where the sites have been moved to points that are also the geometric centers of their cells.
