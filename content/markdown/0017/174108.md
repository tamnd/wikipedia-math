# Abc conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abc conjecture"}

::: {#o2 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="abc conjecture"}

**abc* conjecture.**

:::

*Image: Oesterle Joseph.jpg.* Mathematician [Joseph Oesterlé](wikipedia://en/1604631)

*Image: David Masser.jpg.* Mathematician [David Masser](wikipedia://en/2172777)

The ***abc* conjecture** (also known as the **Oesterlé–Masser conjecture**) is a [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) that arose out of a discussion of [Joseph Oesterlé](wikipedia://en/1604631) and [David Masser](wikipedia://en/2172777) in 1985.[^n1][^n2] It is stated in terms of three [positive integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) *$a, b$* and *$c$* (hence the name) that are [relatively prime](wikipedia://en/6556) and satisfy *$a+b=c$*. The conjecture essentially states that the product of the distinct [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) of *$abc$* cannot often be much smaller than *$c$*. A number of famous conjectures and theorems in number theory would follow immediately from the *abc* conjecture or its versions. Mathematician [Dorian Goldfeld](wikipedia://en/7536770) described the *abc* conjecture as "The most important unsolved problem in [Diophantine analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_analysis)".[^n3]

:::

The *abc* conjecture originated as the outcome of attempts by Oesterlé and Masser to understand the [Szpiro conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szpiro_conjecture) about [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225),[^o6] which involves more geometric structures in its statement than the *abc* conjecture. The *abc* conjecture was shown to be equivalent to the modified Szpiro conjecture.[^n1]

Various attempts to prove the *abc* conjecture have been made, but none have gained broad acceptance. [Shinichi Mochizuki](wikipedia://en/36940578) claimed to have a proof in 2012, but the conjecture is still regarded as unproven by the mainstream mathematical community.[^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11]

::: {#o13 .definition cue=heading name="Formulations"}

## Formulations {#Formulations}

Before stating the conjecture, the notion of the [radical of an integer](wikipedia://en/1634886) must be introduced: for a [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) *$n$*, the radical of *$n$*, denoted *$\text{rad}(n)$*, is the product of the distinct [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) of *$n$*. For example,

$$\begin{aligned} \text{rad}(16) &= \text{rad}(2^4) = 2, \\ \text{rad}(17) &= 17, \\ \text{rad}(18) &= \text{rad}(2\cdot 3^2) = 2\cdot3 = 6, \\ \text{rad}(1000000) &= \text{rad}(2^6 \cdot 5^6) = 2\cdot5 = 10. \end{aligned}$$

If *a*, *b*, and *c* are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime)[^o14] positive integers such that *a* + *b* = *c*, it turns out that "usually" *$c<\text{rad}(abc)$*. The *abc conjecture* deals with the exceptions. Specifically, it states that:

For every positive [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) *ε*, there exist only finitely many triples (*a*, *b*, *c*) of coprime positive integers, with *a* + *b* = *c*, such that[^n4] $c > \operatorname{rad}(abc)^{1+\varepsilon}.$

An equivalent formulation is:

For every positive real number *ε*, there exists a constant *K<sub>ε</sub>* such that for all triples (*a*, *b*, *c*) of coprime positive integers, with *a* + *b* = *c*:[^n4] $c < K_{\varepsilon} \cdot \operatorname{rad}(abc)^{1+\varepsilon}.$

Equivalently (using the [little o notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_o_notation)):

For all triples (*a*, *b*, *c*) of coprime positive integers with *a* + *b* = *c*, rad(*abc*) is at least *c*<sup>1-*o*(1)</sup>.

A fourth equivalent formulation of the conjecture involves the *quality* *q*(*a*, *b*, *c*) of the triple (*a*, *b*, *c*), which is defined as

$$q(a, b, c) = \frac{\log(c)}{\log\big(\textrm{rad}(abc)\big)}.$$

For example:

*q*(4, 127, 131) = log(131) / log(rad(4·127·131)) = log(131) / log(2·127·131) = 0.46820...

*q*(3, 125, 128) = log(128) / log(rad(3·125·128)) = log(128) / log(30) = 1.426565...

A typical triple (*a*, *b*, *c*) of coprime positive integers with *a* + *b* = *c* will have *c* \< rad(*abc*), i.e. *q*(*a*, *b*, *c*) \< 1. Triples with *q* > 1 such as in the second example are rather special; they consist of numbers divisible by high powers of small [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666). The fourth formulation is:

For every positive real number *ε*, there exist only finitely many triples (*a*, *b*, *c*) of coprime positive integers with *a* + *b* = *c* such that *q*(*a*, *b*, *c*) > 1 + *ε*.

Whereas it is known that there are infinitely many triples (*a*, *b*, *c*) of coprime positive integers with *a* + *b* = *c* such that *q*(*a*, *b*, *c*) > 1, the conjecture predicts that only finitely many of those have *q* > 1.01 or *q* > 1.001 or even *q* > 1.0001, etc. In particular, if the conjecture is true, then there must exist a triple (*a*, *b*, *c*) that achieves the maximal possible quality *q*(*a*, *b*, *c*).

:::

## Examples of triples with small radical {#Examples_of_triples_with_small_radical}

The condition that *ε* > 0 is necessary as there exist infinitely many triples *a*, *b*, *c* with *c* > rad(*abc*). For example, let

$$a = 1, \quad b = 2^{6n} - 1, \quad c = 2^{6n}, \qquad n > 1.$$

The integer *b* is divisible by 9:

$$b = 2^{6n} - 1 = 64^n - 1 = (64 - 1) (\cdots) = 9 \cdot 7 \cdot (\cdots).$$

Using this fact, the following calculation is made:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{rad}(abc) &= \operatorname{rad}(a) \operatorname{rad}(b) \operatorname{rad}(c) \\ &= \operatorname{rad}(1) \operatorname{rad} \left ( 2^{6n} -1 \right ) \operatorname{rad} \left (2^{6n} \right ) \\ &= 2 \operatorname{rad} \left ( 2^{6n} -1 \right ) \\ &= 2 \operatorname{rad} \left ( 9 \cdot \tfrac{b}{9} \right ) \\ &\leqslant 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \tfrac{b}{9} \\ &= \tfrac{2}{3} b \\ &< \tfrac{2}{3} c. \end{aligned}$$

By replacing the exponent 6*n* with other exponents forcing *b* to have larger square factors, the ratio between the radical and *c* can be made arbitrarily small. Specifically, let *p* > 2 be a prime and consider

$$a = 1, \quad b = 2^{p(p-1)n} - 1, \quad c = 2^{p(p-1)n}, \qquad n > 1.$$

Now it may be plausibly claimed that *b* is divisible by *p*<sup>2</sup>:

$$\begin{aligned} b &= 2^{p(p-1)n} - 1 \\ &= \left(2^{p(p-1)}\right)^n - 1 \\ &= \left(2^{p(p-1)} - 1\right) (\cdots) \\ &= p^2 \cdot r (\cdots). \end{aligned}$$

The last step uses the fact that *p*<sup>2</sup> divides 2<sup>*p*(*p*−1)</sup> − 1. This follows from [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453), which shows that, for *p* > 2, 2<sup>*p*−1</sup> = *pk* + 1 for some integer *k*. Raising both sides to the power of *p* then shows that 2<sup>*p*(*p*−1)</sup> = *p*<sup>2</sup>(...) + 1.

And now with a similar calculation as above, the following results:

$$\operatorname{rad}(abc) < \tfrac{2}{p} c.$$

A list of the highest-quality triples (triples with a particularly small radical relative to *c*) is given below; the highest quality, 1.6299, was found by Eric Reyssat (Lando Zvonkin 2004, 137) for *a* = 2, *b* = 3<sup>10</sup>·109 = 6,436,341, *c* = 23<sup>5</sup> = 6,436,343, rad(*abc*) = 15042.

## Consequences {#Consequences}

The *abc* conjecture has a large number of consequences. These include both known results (some of which have been proven separately only since the conjecture has been stated) and conjectures for which it gives a [conditional proof](wikipedia://en/6562). The consequences include:

- [Roth's theorem](wikipedia://en/787853) on [Diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558) of [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158).[^n5][^n4]
- The [Mordell conjecture](wikipedia://en/172199) (already proven in general by [Gerd Faltings](wikipedia://en/862226)).[^n6]
- As equivalent, [Vojta's conjecture](wikipedia://en/34682455) in dimension 1.[^n7]
- The [Erdős–Woods conjecture](wikipedia://en/2616413) allowing for a finite number of counterexamples.[^n8]
- The existence of infinitely many non-[Wieferich primes](wikipedia://en/323631) in every base *b* > 1.[^n9]
- The weak form of [Marshall Hall's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Hall's_conjecture) on the separation between squares and cubes of integers.[^n10]
- [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) has [a famously difficult proof by Andrew Wiles](wikipedia://en/21950759). However it follows easily, at least for $n \ge 6$, from an effective form of a weak version of the *abc* conjecture. The *abc* conjecture says the [lim sup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_superior_and_limit_inferior) of the set of all qualities (defined above) is 1, which implies the much weaker assertion that there is a finite upper bound for qualities. The conjecture that 2 is such an upper bound suffices for a very short proof of Fermat's Last Theorem for $n \ge 6$.[^o27]
- The [Fermat–Catalan conjecture](wikipedia://en/22736152), a generalization of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) concerning powers that are sums of powers.[^n11]
- The [*L*-function](wikipedia://en/400092) *L*(*s*, *χ<sub>d</sub>*) formed with the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567), has no [Siegel zero](wikipedia://en/1967197), given a uniform version of the *abc* conjecture in [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field), not just the *abc* conjecture as formulated above for rational integers.[^n12]
- A [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) *P*(*x*) has only finitely many [perfect powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_powers) for all [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) *x* if *P* has at least three [simple zeros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_zero).[^o30]
- A generalization of [Tijdeman's theorem](wikipedia://en/5001103) concerning the number of solutions of *y<sup>m</sup>* = *x<sup>n</sup>* + *k* (Tijdeman's theorem answers the case *k* = 1), and Pillai's conjecture (1931) concerning the number of solutions of *Ay<sup>m</sup>* = *Bx<sup>n</sup>* + *k*.
- As equivalent, the Granville–Langevin conjecture, that if *f* is a square-free binary form of degree *n* > 2, then for every real *β* > 2 there is a constant *C*(*f*, *β*) such that for all coprime integers *x*, *y*, the radical of *f*(*x*, *y*) exceeds *C* · max{|*x*|, |*y*|}<sup>*n*−*β*</sup>.[^o31]
- All of the polynomials (*x*<sup>*n*</sup>-1)/(*x*-1) have infinitely many square-free values.[^o32]
- As equivalent, the modified [Szpiro conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szpiro_conjecture), which would yield a bound of rad(*abc*)<sup>\1.2+*ε*</sup>.[^n1]
- (Dąbrowski 1996) has shown that the *abc* conjecture implies that [the Diophantine equation *n*! + *A* = *k*<sup>2</sup>](wikipedia://en/11301521) has only finitely many solutions for any given integer *A*.
- There are ~*c*<sub>*f*</sub>*N* positive integers *n* ≤ *N* for which *f*(*n*)/B' is square-free, with *c*<sub>*f*</sub> > 0 a positive constant defined as:[^n13]$c_f = \prod_{\text{prime }p} x_i \left ( 1 - \frac{\omega\,\!_f (p)}{p^{2+q_p}} \right ).$
- The [Beal conjecture](wikipedia://en/2397539), a generalization of Fermat's Last Theorem proposing that if *A*, *B*, *C*, *x*, *y*, and *z* are positive integers with *A<sup>x</sup>* + *B<sup>y</sup>* = *C<sup>z</sup>* and *x*, *y*, *z* > 2, then *A*, *B*, and *C* have a common prime factor. The *abc* conjecture would imply that there are only finitely many counterexamples.
- [Lang's conjecture](wikipedia://en/9236652#Lower_bounds_for_the_Néron–Tate_height), a lower bound for the [height](wikipedia://en/3141855) of a non-torsion rational point of an elliptic curve.
- A negative solution to the [Erdős–Ulam problem](wikipedia://en/52483865) on dense sets of Euclidean points with rational distances.[^o36]
- An effective version of [Siegel's theorem about integral points on algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/3989092).[^o37]

## Theoretical results {#Theoretical_results}

The *abc* conjecture implies that *c* can be [bounded above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) by a near-linear function of the radical of *abc*. Bounds are known that are [exponential](wikipedia://en/9678). Specifically, the following bounds have been proven:

$c < \exp{ \left(K_1 \operatorname{rad}(abc)^{15}\right) }$ (Stewart Tijdeman 1986), $c < \exp{ \left(K_2 \operatorname{rad}(abc)^{\frac{2}{3} + \varepsilon}\right) }$ (Stewart Yu 1991), and $c < \exp{ \left(K_3 \operatorname{rad}(abc)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\log(\operatorname{rad}(abc)\right)^3\right) }$ (Stewart Yu 2001).

In these bounds, *K*<sub>1</sub> and *K*<sub>3</sub> are [constants](wikipedia://en/24758132) that do not depend on *a*, *b*, or *c*, and *K*<sub>2</sub> is a constant that depends on *ε* (in an [effectively computable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effectively_computable) way) but not on *a*, *b*, or *c*. The bounds apply to any triple for which *c* > 2.

There are also theoretical results that provide a lower bound on the best possible form of the *abc* conjecture. In particular, (Stewart Tijdeman 1986) showed that there are infinitely many triples (*a*, *b*, *c*) of coprime integers with *a* + *b* = *c* and

$$c > \operatorname{rad}(abc) \exp{ \left(k \sqrt{\log c}/\log\log c \right) }$$

for all *k* \< 4. The constant *k* was improved to *k* = 6.068 by (van Frankenhuysen 2000).

## Computational results {#Computational_results}

In 2006, the Mathematics Department of [Leiden University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University) in the Netherlands, together with the Dutch Kennislink science institute, launched the [ABC@Home](wikipedia://en/17174687) project, a [grid computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_computing) system, which aims to discover additional triples *a*, *b*, *c* with rad(*abc*) \< *c*. Although no finite set of examples or counterexamples can resolve the *abc* conjecture, it is hoped that patterns in the triples discovered by this project will lead to insights about the conjecture and about number theory more generally.

## Refined forms, generalizations and related statements {#Refined_forms,_generalizations_and_related_statements}

The *abc* conjecture is an integer analogue of the [Mason–Stothers theorem](wikipedia://en/30962576) for polynomials.

A strengthening, proposed by (Baker 1998), states that in the *abc* conjecture one can replace rad(*abc*) by

*ε*<sup>−*ω*</sup> rad(*abc*),

where *ω* is the total number of distinct primes dividing *a*, *b* and *c*.[^n14]

[Andrew Granville](wikipedia://en/2910434) noticed that the minimum of the function $\big(\varepsilon^{-\omega}\operatorname{rad}(abc)\big)^{1+\varepsilon}$ over $\varepsilon > 0$ occurs when $\varepsilon = \frac{\omega}{\log\big(\operatorname{rad}(abc)\big)}.$

This inspired (Baker 2004) to propose a sharper form of the *abc* conjecture, namely: $c < \kappa \operatorname{rad}(abc) \frac{\Big(\log\big(\operatorname{rad}(abc)\big)\Big)^\omega}{\omega!}$ with *κ* an absolute constant. After some computational experiments he found that a value of $6/5$ was admissible for *κ*. This version is called the "explicit *abc* conjecture".

(Baker 1998) also describes related conjectures of [Andrew Granville](wikipedia://en/2910434) that would give upper bounds on *c* of the form

$$K^{\Omega(a b c)} \operatorname{rad}(a b c),$$

where Ω(*n*) is the total number of prime factors of *n*, and

$$O\big(\operatorname{rad}(a b c) \Theta(a b c)\big),$$

where Θ(*n*) is the number of integers up to *n* divisible only by primes dividing *n*.

(Robert Stewart Tenenbaum 2014) proposed a more precise inequality based on (Robert Tenenbaum 2013). Let *k* = rad(*abc*). They conjectured there is a constant *C*<sub>1</sub> such that

$$c < k \exp\left(4\sqrt{\frac{3\log k}{\log\log k}}\left(1+\frac{\log\log\log k}{2\log\log k}+\frac{C_{1}}{\log\log k}\right)\right)$$

holds whereas there is a constant *C*<sub>2</sub> such that

$$c > k \exp\left(4\sqrt{\frac{3\log k}{\log\log k}}\left(1+\frac{\log\log\log k}{2\log\log k}+\frac{C_{2}}{\log\log k}\right)\right)$$

holds infinitely often.

(Browkin Brzeziński 1994) formulated the [n conjecture](wikipedia://en/47297286)—a version of the *abc* conjecture involving *n* > 2 integers.

## Claimed proofs {#Claimed_proofs}

[Lucien Szpiro](wikipedia://en/26992384) proposed a solution in 2007, but it was found to be incorrect shortly afterwards.[^o59]

Since August 2012, [Shinichi Mochizuki](wikipedia://en/36940578) has claimed a proof of Szpiro's conjecture and therefore the *abc* conjecture.[^o8] He released a series of four preprints developing a new theory he called [inter-universal Teichmüller theory](wikipedia://en/36968172) (IUTT), which is then applied to prove the *abc* conjecture.[^o60] The papers have not been widely accepted by the mathematical community as providing a proof of *abc*.[^o61] This is not only because of their length and the difficulty of understanding them,[^o62] but also because at least one specific point in the argument has been identified as a gap by some other experts.[^o68] Although a few mathematicians have vouched for the correctness of the proof[^o63] and have attempted to communicate their understanding via workshops on IUTT, they have failed to convince the number theory community at large.[^o64][^o65]

In March 2018, [Peter Scholze](wikipedia://en/39409306) and [Jakob Stix](wikipedia://en/58556057) visited [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_University) for discussions with Mochizuki.[^o66][^o67] While they did not resolve the differences, they brought them into clearer focus. Scholze and Stix wrote a report asserting and explaining an error in the logic of the proof and claiming that the resulting gap was "so severe that ... small modifications will not rescue the proof strategy";[^o68] Mochizuki claimed that they misunderstood vital aspects of the theory and made invalid simplifications.[^o69][^o70][^o71]

On April 3, 2020, two mathematicians from [the Kyoto research institute](wikipedia://en/1978962) where Mochizuki works announced that his claimed proof would be published in *Publications of the Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences (RIMS)*, the institute's journal. Mochizuki is chief editor of the journal but recused himself from the review of the paper.[^o9] The announcement was received with skepticism by [Kiran Kedlaya](wikipedia://en/31688876) and [Edward Frenkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Frenkel), as well as being described by [*Nature*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_%28journal%29) as "unlikely to move many researchers over to Mochizuki's camp".[^o9] In March 2021, Mochizuki's proof was published in RIMS.[^o72]
