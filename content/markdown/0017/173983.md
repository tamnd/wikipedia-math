# Symplectic matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symplectic matrix"}

In mathematics, a **symplectic matrix** is a $2n\times 2n$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $M$ with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) entries that satisfies the condition

:::

::: {#o2 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$M^\text{T} \Omega M = \Omega, \tag{1}$$

:::

where $M^\text{T}$ denotes the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of $M$ and $\Omega$ is a fixed $2n\times 2n$ [nonsingular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsingular_matrix), [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055). This definition can be extended to $2n\times 2n$ matrices with entries in other [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), such as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615), [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423), and [function fields](wikipedia://en/2783843).

Typically $\Omega$ is chosen to be the [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464)

$$\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0 \\ \end{bmatrix},$$

where $I_n$ is the $n\times n$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). The matrix $\Omega$ has [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $+1$ and its inverse is $\Omega^{-1} = \Omega^\text{T} = -\Omega$.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

### Generators for symplectic matrices {#Generators_for_symplectic_matrices}

Every symplectic matrix has determinant $+1$, and the $2n\times 2n$ symplectic matrices with real entries form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\mathrm{GL}(2n;\mathbb{R})$ under [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) since being symplectic is a property stable under matrix multiplication. [Topologically](wikipedia://en/29954), this [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977) is a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [noncompact](wikipedia://en/6042) [real Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_Lie_group) of real dimension $n(2n+1)$, and is denoted $\mathrm{Sp}(2n;\mathbb{R})$. The symplectic group can be defined as the set of [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformations) that preserve the symplectic form of a real [symplectic vector space](wikipedia://en/292852).

This symplectic group has a distinguished [set of generators](wikipedia://en/99945), which can be used to find all possible symplectic matrices. This includes the following sets

$$\begin{aligned} D(n) =& \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} A & 0 \\ 0 & (A^T)^{-1} \end{pmatrix} : A \in \text{GL}(n;\mathbb{R}) \right\} \\ N(n) =& \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} I_n & B \\ 0 & I_n \end{pmatrix} : B \in \text{Sym}(n;\mathbb{R}) \right\} \end{aligned}$$

where $\text{Sym}(n;\mathbb{R})$ is the set of $n\times n$ [symmetric matrices](wikipedia://en/126474). Then, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n;\mathbb{R})$ is generated by the set[^o6]<sup>p. 2</sup>

$$\{\Omega \} \cup D(n) \cup N(n)$$

of matrices. In other words, any symplectic matrix can be constructed by multiplying matrices in $D(n)$ and $N(n)$ together, along with some power of $\Omega$.

### Inverse matrix {#Inverse_matrix}

Every symplectic matrix is invertible with the [inverse matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_matrix) given by

$$M^{-1} = \Omega^{-1} M^\text{T} \Omega.$$

Furthermore, the [product](wikipedia://en/125280) of two symplectic matrices is, again, a symplectic matrix. This gives the set of all symplectic matrices the structure of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). There exists a natural [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) structure on this group which makes it into a (real or complex) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) called the [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977).

### Determinantal properties {#Determinantal_properties}

It follows easily from the definition that the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of any symplectic matrix is ±1. Actually, it turns out that the determinant is always +1 for any field. One way to see this is through the use of the [Pfaffian](wikipedia://en/530060) and the identity

$$\mbox{Pf}(M^\text{T} \Omega M) = \det(M)\mbox{Pf}(\Omega).$$

Since $M^\text{T} \Omega M = \Omega$ and $\mbox{Pf}(\Omega) \neq 0$ we have that $\det(M) = 1$.

When the underlying field is real or complex, one can also show this by factoring the inequality $\det(M^\text{T} M + I) \ge 1$.[^o9]

### Block form of symplectic matrices {#Block_form_of_symplectic_matrices}

Suppose Ω is given in the standard form and let $M$ be a $2n\times 2n$ [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464) given by

$$M = \begin{pmatrix}A & B \\ C & D\end{pmatrix}$$

where $A,B,C,D$ are $n\times n$ matrices. The condition for $M$ to be symplectic is equivalent to the two following equivalent conditions[^o11]

> $A^\text{T}C,B^\text{T}D$ symmetric, and $A^\text{T} D - C^\text{T} B = I$

> $AB^\text{T},CD^\text{T}$ symmetric, and $AD^\text{T} - BC^\text{T} = I$

The second condition comes from the fact that if $M$ is symplectic, then $M^T$ is also symplectic. When $n=1$ these conditions reduce to the single condition $\det(M)=1$. Thus a $2\times 2$ matrix is symplectic if and only if it has unit determinant.

#### Inverse matrix of block matrix {#Inverse_matrix_of_block_matrix}

With $\Omega$ in standard form, the inverse of $M$ is given by

$$M^{-1} = \Omega^{-1} M^\text{T} \Omega=\begin{pmatrix}D^\text{T} & -B^\text{T} \\-C^\text{T} & A^\text{T}\end{pmatrix}.$$

The group has dimension $n(2n+1)$. This can be seen by noting that $( M^\text{T} \Omega M)^\text{T} = -M^\text{T} \Omega M$ is anti-symmetric. Since the space of anti-symmetric matrices has dimension $\binom{2n}{2},$ the identity $M^\text{T} \Omega M = \Omega$ imposes $2n \choose 2$ constraints on the $(2n)^2$ coefficients of $M$ and leaves $M$ with $n(2n+1)$ independent coefficients.

:::

## Symplectic transformations {#Symplectic_transformations}

In the abstract formulation of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), matrices are replaced with [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-dimensional) [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_spaces). The abstract analog of a symplectic matrix is a **symplectic transformation** of a [symplectic vector space](wikipedia://en/292852). Briefly, a symplectic vector space $(V,\omega)$ is a $2n$-dimensional vector space $V$ equipped with a [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form), [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055) [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) $\omega$ called the [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/292852).

A symplectic transformation is then a linear transformation $L:V\to V$ which preserves $\omega$, i.e.

$$\omega(Lu, Lv) = \omega(u, v).$$

Fixing a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for $V$, $\omega$ can be written as a matrix $\Omega$ and $L$ as a matrix $M$. The condition that $L$ be a symplectic transformation is precisely the condition that *M* be a symplectic matrix:

$$M^\text{T} \Omega M = \Omega.$$

Under a [change of basis](wikipedia://en/1179451), represented by a matrix *A*, we have

$$\Omega \mapsto A^\text{T} \Omega A$$

$$M \mapsto A^{-1} M A.$$

One can always bring $\Omega$ to either the standard form given in the introduction or the block diagonal form described below by a suitable choice of *A*.

:::

## The matrix Ω {#The_matrix_Ω}

Symplectic matrices are defined relative to a fixed [nonsingular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsingular_matrix), [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055) $\Omega$. As explained in the previous section, $\Omega$ can be thought of as the coordinate representation of a [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form) [skew-symmetric bilinear form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-symmetric_bilinear_form). It is a basic result in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) that any two such matrices differ from each other by a [change of basis](wikipedia://en/1179451).

The most common alternative to the standard $\Omega$ given above is the [block diagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_diagonal) form

$$\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} \begin{matrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{matrix} & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \begin{matrix}0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0\end{matrix} \end{bmatrix}.$$

This choice differs from the previous one by a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vectors).

Sometimes the notation $J$ is used instead of $\Omega$ for the skew-symmetric matrix. This is a particularly unfortunate choice as it leads to confusion with the notion of a [complex structure](wikipedia://en/1549805), which often has the same coordinate expression as $\Omega$ but represents a very different structure. A complex structure $J$ is the coordinate representation of a linear transformation that squares to $-I_n$, whereas $\Omega$ is the coordinate representation of a nondegenerate skew-symmetric bilinear form. One could easily choose bases in which $J$ is not skew-symmetric or $\Omega$ does not square to $-I_n$.

Given a [hermitian structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_structure) on a vector space, $J$ and $\Omega$ are related via

$$\Omega_{ab} = -g_{ac}{J^c}_b$$

where $g_{ac}$ is the [metric](wikipedia://en/195795). That $J$ and $\Omega$ usually have the same coordinate expression (up to an overall sign) is simply a consequence of the fact that the metric *g* is usually the identity matrix.

## Diagonalization and decomposition {#Diagonalization_and_decomposition}

For any [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) symmetric $2n\times 2n$ real symplectic matrix $S$, there is a symplectic unitary $U$,

$$U \in \mathrm{U}(2n,\mathbb{R}) \cap \operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{O}(2n) \cap \operatorname{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R}),$$

such that

$$S = U^\text{T} D U \quad \text{for} \quad D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n,\lambda_1^{-1},\ldots,\lambda_n^{-1}),$$

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

where the diagonal elements of $D$ are the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of $S$.[^o17][^o18]

:::

## Complex matrices {#Complex_matrices}

If instead *M* is a 2*n* × 2*n* [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) entries, the definition is not standard throughout the literature. Many authors [^o23] adjust the definition above to

::: {#o24 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$M^* \Omega M = \Omega\,. \tag{3}$$

:::

where *M<sup>\*</sup>* denotes the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of *M*. In this case, the determinant may not be 1, but will have [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) 1. In the 2×2 case (*n*=1), *M* will be the product of a real symplectic matrix and a complex number of absolute value 1.

Other authors [^o25] retain the definition ((1)) for complex matrices and call matrices satisfying ((3)) *conjugate symplectic*.

::: {#o27 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Transformations described by symplectic matrices play an important role in [quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Optics) and in [continuous-variable quantum information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous-variable_quantum_information). For instance, symplectic matrices can be used to describe [Gaussian (Bogoliubov) transformations](wikipedia://en/1146338) of a quantum state of light.[^o28] In turn, the Bloch-Messiah decomposition ((2)) means that such an arbitrary Gaussian transformation can be represented as a set of two passive [linear-optical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_optics) interferometers (corresponding to orthogonal matrices *O* and *O'*) intermitted by a layer of active non-linear [squeezing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squeezed_coherent_state#Operator_representation) transformations (given in terms of the matrix *D*).[^o29] In fact, one can circumvent the need for such *in-line* active squeezing transformations if [two-mode squeezed vacuum states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squeezed_coherent_state) are available as a prior resource only.[^o30]
