# Special unitary group

Classical

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Special unitary group"}

In mathematics, the **special unitary group** of degree $n$, denoted $\operatorname{SU}(n)$, is the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of $n \times n$ [unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222) with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $1$.

:::

The [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) of the more general [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) may have [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) determinants with absolute value 1, rather than real 1 in the special case.

The group operation is [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). The special unitary group is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $U(n)$, consisting of all $n \times n$ unitary matrices. As a [compact classical group](wikipedia://en/10172878), $U(n)$ is the group that preserves the [standard inner product](wikipedia://en/14856#Some_examples) on $\mathbb{C}^n$. It is itself a subgroup of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), $\operatorname{SU}(n) \subset \operatorname{U}(n) \subset \operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbb{C} ).$

The $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ groups find wide application in the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641) of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics), especially $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ in the [electroweak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_interaction) and $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ in [quantum chromodynamics](wikipedia://en/25264).[^o2]

The simplest case, $\operatorname{SU}(1)$, is the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059), having only a single element. The group $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the group of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) of [norm](wikipedia://en/51440#Conjugation,_the_norm,_and_reciprocal) 1, and is thus [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic) to the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792). Since [unit quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_quaternion) can be used to represent rotations in 3-dimensional space (uniquely up to sign), there is a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ to the [rotation group $\operatorname{SO}(3)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29) whose [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is $\{+I, -I\}$. Since the quaternions can be identified as the even subalgebra of the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) $\operatorname{Cl}(3)$, $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ is identical to one of the symmetry groups of [spinors](wikipedia://en/29276), [Spin](wikipedia://en/411231)(3), that enables a spinor presentation of rotations.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The special unitary group $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ is a strictly real [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) (vs. a more general [complex Lie group](wikipedia://en/2187847)). Its dimension as a [real manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) is $n^{2} - 1$. Topologically, it is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected).[^o5] Algebraically, it is a [simple Lie group](wikipedia://en/292831) (meaning its [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) is simple; see below).[^o6]

The [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) of $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ is isomorphic to the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and is composed of the diagonal matrices $\zeta I$ for $\zeta$ an $n$th root of unity and $I$ the $n \times n$ identity matrix.

Its [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) for $n \ge 3$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, while the outer automorphism group of $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ is the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059).

A [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136) of [rank](wikipedia://en/504697#Split_rank_of_a_semisimple_group) $n - 1$ is given by the set of diagonal matrices with determinant $1$. The [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332#The_Weyl_group_of_a_connected_compact_Lie_group) of $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$, which is represented by [signed permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/246320#Signed_permutation_group) (the signs being necessary to ensure that the determinant is $1$).

The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of $\operatorname{SU}(n)$, denoted by $\mathfrak{su}(n)$, can be identified with the set of [traceless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traceless) [anti‑Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AntiHermitian) $n \times n$ complex matrices, with the regular [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) as a Lie bracket. [Particle physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics) often use a different, equivalent representation: The set of traceless [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) $n \times n$ complex matrices with Lie bracket given by $-i$ times the commutator.

## Lie algebra {#Lie_algebra}

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ of $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ consists of $n \times n$ [skew-Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_Hermitian_matrix) matrices with trace zero.[^o9] This (real) Lie algebra has dimension $n^{2} - 1$.

The [complexification](wikipedia://en/755604) of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ is $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{C})$, the space of all $n \times n$ complex matrices with trace zero.[^o10] A [Cartan subalgebra](wikipedia://en/1336000) then consists of the diagonal matrices with trace zero,[^o11] which we identify with vectors in $\mathbb C^n$ whose entries sum to zero. The [roots](wikipedia://en/277087) then consist of all the $n(n - 1)$ permutations of $(1, -1, 0, ..., 0)$.

A choice of [simple roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_root_%28root_system%29) is

$$\begin{aligned} (&1, -1, 0, \dots, 0, 0), \\ (&0, 1, -1, \dots, 0, 0), \\ &\vdots \\ (&0, 0, 0, \dots, 1, -1). \end{aligned}$$

So, $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ is of [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) $n - 1$ and its [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109) is given by $A_{n-1}$, a chain of $n - 1$ nodes: node...3.[^o12] Its [Cartan matrix](wikipedia://en/1146433) is

$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Its [Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332) or [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004) is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{n}$, the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of the $(n - 1)$-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781).

### Fundamental representation {#Fundamental_representation}

In the physics literature, it is common to identify the Lie algebra with the space of trace-zero *Hermitian* (rather than the skew-Hermitian) matrices. That is to say, the physicists' Lie algebra differs by a factor of $i$ from the mathematicians'. With this convention, one can then choose generators $T_{a}$ that are [traceless](wikipedia://en/43270) [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) complex $n \times n$ matrices, where:

$$T_a \, T_b = \tfrac{1}{\, 2n \,}\,\delta_{ab}\,I_n + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sum_{c=1}^{n^2 -1}\left(if_{abc} + d_{abc} \right) \, T_c$$

where the $f$ are the [structure constants](wikipedia://en/20285142) and are antisymmetric in all indices, while the $d$-coefficients are symmetric in all indices.

As a consequence, the commutator is:

$$~ \left[T_a, \, T_b\right] ~ = ~ i \sum_{c=1}^{n^2 -1} \, f_{abc} \, T_c \;,$$

and the corresponding anticommutator is:

$$\left\{T_a, \, T_b\right\} ~ = ~ \tfrac{1}{n} \, \delta_{ab} \, I_n + \sum_{c=1}^{n^2 -1}{d_{abc} \, T_c} ~.$$

The factor of $i$ in the commutation relation arises from the physics convention and is not present when using the mathematicians' convention.

The conventional normalization condition is

$$\sum_{c,e=1}^{n^2 - 1} d_{ace}\,d_{bce} = \frac{\, n^2 - 4 \,}{n} \, \delta_{ab}~ .$$

The generators satisfy the Jacobi identity:[^o14]

$$[T_a,[T_b,T_c]]+[T_b,[T_c,T_a]]+[T_c,[T_a,T_b]]=0.$$

By convention, in the physics literature the generators $T_a$ are defined as the traceless Hermitian complex matrices with a $1/2$ factor: for $\operatorname{SU}(2)$, the generators are chosen as $\tfrac{1}{2} \sigma_1$, $\tfrac{1}{2} \sigma_2$, $\tfrac{1}{2} \sigma_3$ where $\sigma_a$ are the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868), while for the case of $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ one defines $T_a = \tfrac{1}{2} \lambda_a$ where $\lambda_a$ are the [Gell-Mann matrices](wikipedia://en/563694).[^o15] With these definitions, the generators satisfy the following normalization condition:

$$Tr(T_a T_b) = \frac{1}{2} \delta_{ab}.$$

### Adjoint representation {#Adjoint_representation}

In the $(n^{2} - 1)$-dimensional [adjoint representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_group), the generators are represented by $(n^{2} - 1) \times (n^{2} - 1)$ matrices, whose elements are defined by the structure constants themselves:

$$\left(T_a\right)_{jk} = -if_{ajk}.$$

## SU(2) {#SU(2)}

Using [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) for the binary operation, $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ forms a group,[^o18]

$$\operatorname{SU}(2) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & -\overline{\beta} \\ \beta & \overline{\alpha} \end{pmatrix}: \ \ \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{C}, |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1 \right\}~,$$

where the overline denotes [complex conjugation](wikipedia://en/173918).

### Diffeomorphism with the 3-sphere *S*<sup>3</sup> {#Diffeomorphism_with_the_3-sphere_S3}

If we consider $\alpha,\beta$ as a pair in $\mathbb{C}^2$ where $\alpha=a+bi$ and $\beta=c+di$ with real numbers $a,b,c,d$, then the equation $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$ becomes

$$a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 = 1.$$

This is the equation of the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) $S^{3}$. This can also be seen using an embedding: the map

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi \colon \mathbb{C}^2 \to{} &\operatorname{M}(2, \mathbb{C}) \\[5pt] \varphi(\alpha, \beta) ={} &\begin{pmatrix} \alpha & -\overline{\beta}\\ \beta & \overline{\alpha}\end{pmatrix}, \end{aligned}$$

where $\operatorname{M}(2,\mathbb{C})$ denotes the set of 2-by-2 complex matrices, is an injective real linear map (by considering $\mathbb{C}^2$ [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isomorphism) to $\mathbb{R}^4$ and $\operatorname{M}(2,\mathbb{C})$ isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^8$). Hence, the [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) of $\varphi$ to the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) $S^{3}$ is an embedding of the 3-sphere onto a compact submanifold of $\operatorname{M}(2,\mathbb{C})$, namely $\varphi(S^{3}) = \operatorname{SU}(2)$.

Therefore, as a manifold, $S^{3}$ is [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic) to $\operatorname{SU}(2)$, which shows that $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ is [simply connected](wikipedia://en/523879) and that $S^{3}$ can be endowed with the structure of a compact, connected [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945).

### Isomorphism with group of versors and relation to spatial rotations {#Isomorphism_with_group_of_versors_and_relation_to_spatial_rotations}

The ring of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) can be associated with a subring of 2-by-2 complex matrices by the [ring isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) mapping

$$a\,\hat{1} + b\,\hat{i} + c\,\hat{j} + d\,\hat{k}$$

to

$$\begin{pmatrix} a + bi & c + di \\ -c + di & a - bi \end{pmatrix} \quad (a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}).$$

Additionally, the determinant of the matrix is the squared norm of the corresponding quaternion. Clearly any matrix in $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ is of this form and, since it has determinant $1$, the corresponding quaternion has norm $1$. Thus, restricting the above map gives a [group isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) between $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ and the group of quaternions with norm $1$.[^o21]

[Quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) of norm $1$ are called [versors](wikipedia://en/2644621) since they can be associated with rotations in 3-dimensional space: Every versor is naturally associated to a spatial rotation in 3 dimensions, and the product of versors is associated to the composition of the associated rotations. Furthermore, every rotation arises from exactly two versors in this fashion. In short: there is a 2:1 surjective homomorphism from $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ to the [rotation group $\operatorname{SO}(3)$](wikipedia://en/173965); consequently $\operatorname{SO}(3)$ is isomorphic to the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $\operatorname{SU}(2)/\{\pm I\}$, the manifold underlying $\operatorname{SO}(3)$ is obtained by identifying antipodal points of the 3-sphere $S^{3}$, and $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ is the [universal cover](wikipedia://en/1378699#Universal_covering_group) of $\operatorname{SO}(3)$.

### Lie algebra {#Lie_algebra}

The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ consists of $2 \times 2$ [skew-Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_Hermitian_matrix) matrices with trace zero.[^o23] Explicitly, this means

$$\mathfrak{su}(2) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} i\ a & -\overline{z} \\ z & -i\ a \end{pmatrix}:\ a \in \mathbb{R}, z \in \mathbb{C} \right\}~.$$

The Lie algebra is then generated by the following matrices,

$$u_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad u_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad u_3 = \begin{pmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}~,$$

which have the form of the general element specified above.

This can also be written as $\mathfrak{s u}(2)=\operatorname{span}\left\{i \sigma_{1}, i \sigma_{2}, i \sigma_{3}\right\}$ using the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868#SU%282%29).

These satisfy the [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) relationships $u_2 u_3 = -u_3 u_2 = u_1$, $u_3 u_1 = -u_1 u_3 = u_2$, and $u_1 u_2 = -u_2 u_1 = u_3$. The [commutator bracket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutator_bracket) is therefore specified by

$$\left[u_3, u_1\right] = 2\ u_2, \quad \left[u_1, u_2\right] = 2\ u_3, \quad \left[u_2, u_3\right] = 2\ u_1~.$$

The above generators are related to the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) by $u_1 = i\ \sigma_1, u_2 = -i\ \sigma_2$ and $u_3 = i\ \sigma_3$. This representation is routinely used in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) to represent the [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) of [fundamental particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_particle) such as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron). They also serve as [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) for the description of our 3 spatial dimensions in [loop quantum gravity](wikipedia://en/152664). They also correspond to the [Pauli X, Y, and Z gates](wikipedia://en/888587#Pauli_gates_%28X,Y,Z%29), which are standard generators for the single qubit gates, corresponding to 3D rotations about the axes of the [Bloch sphere](wikipedia://en/729572).

The Lie algebra serves to work out the [representations of $\operatorname{SU}(2)$](wikipedia://en/1378619).

## SU(3) {#SU(3)}

The group $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ is an 8-dimensional [simple Lie group](wikipedia://en/292831) consisting of all $3 \times 3$ [unitary](wikipedia://en/129222) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) $1$.

### Topology {#Topology}

The group $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ is a simply-connected, compact Lie group.[^o27] Its topological structure can be understood by noting that $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ acts [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_action) on the unit sphere $S^5$ in $\mathbb{C}^3 \cong \mathbb{R}^6$. The [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Fixed_points_and_stabilizer_subgroups) of an arbitrary point in the sphere is isomorphic to $\operatorname{SU}(2)$, which topologically is a 3-sphere. It then follows that $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ is a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) over the base $S^{5}$ with fiber $S^{3}$. Since the fibers and the base are simply connected, the simple connectedness of $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ then follows by means of a standard topological result (the [long exact sequence of homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506#Long_exact_sequence_of_a_fibration) for fiber bundles).[^o28]

The $\operatorname{SU}(2)$-bundles over $S^{5}$ are classified by $\pi_4\mathord\left(S^3\right) = \mathbb{Z}_2$ since any such bundle can be constructed by looking at trivial bundles on the two hemispheres $S^5_\text{N}, S^5_\text{S}$ and looking at the transition function on their intersection, which is a copy of $S^{4}$, so

$$S^5_\text{N} \cap S^5_\text{S} \simeq S^4$$

Then, all such transition functions are classified by homotopy classes of maps

$$\left[S^4, \mathrm{SU}(2)\right] \cong \left[S^4, S^3\right] = \pi_4\mathord\left(S^3\right) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$$

and as $\pi_4(\mathrm{SU}(3)) = \{0\}$ rather than $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ cannot be the trivial bundle $\operatorname{SU}(2) \times S^{5} \cong S^{3} \times S^{5}$, and therefore must be the unique nontrivial (twisted) bundle. This can be shown by looking at the induced long exact sequence on homotopy groups.

### Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

The representation theory of $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ is well-understood.[^o30] Descriptions of these representations, from the point of view of its complexified Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}(3; \mathbb{C})$, may be found in the articles on [Lie algebra representations](wikipedia://en/318712#Classifying_finite-dimensional_representations_of_Lie_algebras) or [the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients for $\operatorname{SU}(3)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clebsch–Gordan_coefficients_for_SU%283%29#Representations_of_the_SU%283%29_group).

### Lie algebra {#Lie_algebra}

The generators, $T$, of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(3)$ of $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ in the defining (particle physics, Hermitian) representation, are

$$T_a = \frac{\lambda_a}{2}~,$$

where $\lambda_{a}$, the [Gell-Mann matrices](wikipedia://en/563694), are the $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ analog of the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) for $\operatorname{SU}(2)$:

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda_1 ={} &\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, & \lambda_2 ={} &\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i & 0 \\ i & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, & \lambda_3 ={} &\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\[6pt] \lambda_4 ={} &\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, & \lambda_5 ={} &\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -i \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ i & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \\[6pt] \lambda_6 ={} &\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, & \lambda_7 ={} &\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -i \\ 0 & i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, & \lambda_8 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} &\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

These $\lambda_{a}$ span all [traceless](wikipedia://en/43270) [Hermitian matrices](wikipedia://en/189682) $H$ of the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944), as required. Note that $\lambda_{2}$, $\lambda_{5}$, $\lambda_{7}$ are antisymmetric.

They obey the relations

$$\begin{aligned} \left[T_a, T_b\right] &= i \sum_{c=1}^8 f_{abc} T_c, \\ \left\{T_a, T_b\right\} &= \frac{1}{3} \delta_{ab} I_3 + \sum_{c=1}^8 d_{abc} T_c, \end{aligned}$$

or, equivalently,

$$\begin{aligned} \left[\lambda_a, \lambda_b\right] &= 2i \sum_{c=1}^8 f_{abc} \lambda_c, \\ \{\lambda_a, \lambda_b\} &= \frac{4}{3}\delta_{ab} I_3 + 2\sum_{c=1}^8{d_{abc} \lambda_c}. \end{aligned}$$

The $f$ are the [structure constants](wikipedia://en/20285142) of the Lie algebra, given by

$$\begin{aligned} f_{123} &= 1, \\ f_{147} = -f_{156} = f_{246} = f_{257} = f_{345} = -f_{367} &= \frac{1}{2}, \\ f_{458} = f_{678} &= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \end{aligned}$$

while all other $f_{abc}$ not related to these by permutation are zero. In general, they vanish unless they contain an odd number of indices from the set $\{2, 5, 7\}$.

The symmetric coefficients $d$ take the values

$$\begin{aligned} d_{118} = d_{228} = d_{338} = -d_{888} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\ d_{448} = d_{558} = d_{668} = d_{778} &= -\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}} \\ d_{344} = d_{355} = -d_{366} = -d_{377} = -d_{247} = d_{146} = d_{157} = d_{256} &= \frac{1}{2} ~. \end{aligned}$$

They vanish if the number of indices from the set $\{2, 5, 7\}$ is odd.

A generic $\operatorname{SU}(3)$ group element generated by a traceless $3 \times 3$ Hermitian matrix $H$, normalized as $\operatorname{tr}(H^{2}) = 2$, can be expressed as a *second order* matrix polynomial in $H$:[^o32]

$$\begin{aligned} \exp(i\theta H) ={} &\left[-\frac{1}{3} I\sin\left(\varphi + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) \sin\left(\varphi - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}~H\sin(\varphi) - \frac{1}{4}~H^2\right] \frac{\exp\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}~i\theta\sin(\varphi)\right)} {\cos\left(\varphi + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) \cos\left(\varphi - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)} \\[6pt] & {} + \left[-\frac{1}{3}~I\sin(\varphi) \sin\left(\varphi - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}~H\sin\left(\varphi + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) - \frac{1}{4}~H^{2}\right] \frac{\exp\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}~i\theta \sin\left(\varphi + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)\right)} {\cos(\varphi) \cos\left(\varphi - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)} \\[6pt] & {} + \left[-\frac{1}{3}~I\sin(\varphi) \sin\left(\varphi + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) - \frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}~H \sin\left(\varphi - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) - \frac{1}{4}~H^2\right] \frac{\exp\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}~i\theta \sin\left(\varphi - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)\right)} {\cos(\varphi)\cos\left(\varphi + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)} \end{aligned}$$

where

$$\varphi \equiv \frac{1}{3}\left[\arccos\left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\det H\right) - \frac{\pi}{2}\right].$$

:::

## Important subgroups {#Important_subgroups}

In physics the special unitary group is used to represent [fermionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermionic) symmetries. In theories of [symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_breaking) it is important to be able to find the subgroups of the special unitary group. Subgroups of $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ that are important in [GUT physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_unification_theory) are, for $p > 1, n - p > 1$,

$$\operatorname{SU}(n) \supset \operatorname{SU}(p) \times \operatorname{SU}(n - p) \times \operatorname{U}(1),$$

where $\times$ denotes the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) and $U(1)$, known as the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177), is the multiplicative group of all [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) with [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991#Complex_numbers) 1.

For completeness, there are also the [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/173954) and [symplectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_symplectic_group) subgroups,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{SU}(n) &\supset \operatorname{SO}(n), \\ \operatorname{SU}(2n) &\supset \operatorname{Sp}(n). \end{aligned}$$

Since the [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_of_a_Lie_group) of $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ is $n - 1$ and of $U(1)$ is 1, a useful check is that the sum of the ranks of the subgroups is less than or equal to the rank of the original group. $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ is a subgroup of various other Lie groups,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{SO}(2n) &\supset \operatorname{SU}(n) \\ \operatorname{Sp}(n) &\supset \operatorname{SU}(n) \\ \operatorname{Spin}(4) &= \operatorname{SU}(2) \times \operatorname{SU}(2) \\ \operatorname{E}_6 &\supset \operatorname{SU}(6) \\ \operatorname{E}_7 &\supset \operatorname{SU}(8) \\ \operatorname{G}_2 &\supset \operatorname{SU}(3) \end{aligned}$$

See *[Spin group](wikipedia://en/411231)* and *[Simple Lie group](wikipedia://en/292831)* for $E_{6}$, $E_{7}$, and $G_{2}$.

There are also the [accidental isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/411231#Exceptional_isomorphisms): $\operatorname{SU}(4) = \operatorname{Spin}(6)$, $\operatorname{SU}(2) = \operatorname{Spin}(3) = \operatorname{Sp}(1)$, and $U(1) = \operatorname{Spin}(2) = \operatorname{SO}(2)$.

One may finally mention that $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ is the [double covering group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_covering_group) of $\operatorname{SO}(3)$, a relation that plays an important role in the theory of rotations of 2-[spinors](wikipedia://en/29276) in non-relativistic [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202).

## Generalized special unitary group {#Generalized_special_unitary_group}

For a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$, the **generalized special unitary group over *F***, $\operatorname{SU}(p, q; F)$, is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) 1 of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of rank $n = p + q$ over $F$ which leave invariant a [nondegenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondegenerate_form), [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) of [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_of_a_quadratic_form) $(p, q)$. This group is often referred to as the **special unitary group of signature $p q$ over $F$**. The field $F$ can be replaced by a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), in which case the vector space is replaced by a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454).

Specifically, fix a [Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/189682) $A$ of signature $p q$ in $\operatorname{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, then all

$$M \in \operatorname{SU}(p, q, \mathbb{R})$$

satisfy

$$\begin{aligned} M^{*} A M &= A \\ \det M &= 1. \end{aligned}$$

Often one will see the notation $\operatorname{SU}(p, q)$ without reference to a ring or field; in this case, the ring or field being referred to is $\mathbb C$ and this gives one of the classical [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945). The standard choice for $A$ when $\operatorname{F} = \mathbb{C}$ is

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & i \\ 0 & I_{n-2} & 0 \\ -i & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

However, there may be better choices for $A$ for certain dimensions which exhibit more behaviour under restriction to subrings of $\mathbb C$.

::: {#o36 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

An important example of this type of group is the [Picard modular group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard_modular_group) $\operatorname{SU}(2, 1; \mathbb{Z}[i])$ which acts (projectively) on [complex hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/69557765) of dimension two, in the same way that $\operatorname{SL}(2,9;\mathbb{Z})$ acts (projectively) on real [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) of dimension two. In 2005 Gábor Francsics and [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365) computed an explicit fundamental domain for the action of this group on $\operatorname{HC}^{2}$.[^o37]

A further example is $\operatorname{SU}(1, 1; \mathbb{C})$, which is isomorphic to $\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$.

:::

::: {#o39 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="SU(1,1)"}

### SU(1, 1) {#SU(1,_1)}

$\mathrm{SU}(1,1) = \left \{ \begin{pmatrix}u & v \\ v^* & u^* \end{pmatrix} \in M(2,\mathbb{C}): u u^* - v v^* = 1 \right \},$ where $~u^*~$ denotes the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of the complex number $u$.

This group is isomorphic to $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ and $\operatorname{Spin}(2,1)$[^o40] where the numbers separated by a comma refer to the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28quadratic_form%29) of the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) preserved by the group. The expression $u u^* - v v^*$ in the definition of $\operatorname{SU}(1,1)$ is an [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) which becomes an [isotropic quadratic form](wikipedia://en/10917170) when $u$ and $v$ are expanded with their real components.

An early appearance of this group was as the "unit sphere" of [coquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquaternion) (split-quaternions), introduced by [James Cockle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cockle) in 1852. Let

$$j = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\,, \quad k = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \;~0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\,, \quad i = \begin{bmatrix} \;~0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}~.$$

Then $j\,k = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & \;~0 \end{bmatrix} = -i$, $i\,j\,k = I_2 \equiv \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, the $2 \times 2$ identity matrix, $k\,i = j$, and $i\,j = k$, and the elements $i$, $j$, and $k$ all [anticommute](wikipedia://en/294358), as in [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440). Also $i$ is still a square root of $-I_{2}$ (negative of the identity matrix), whereas $j^{2} = k^{2} = I_{2}$ are not, unlike in quaternions. For both quaternions and [coquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coquaternion), all scalar quantities are treated as implicit multiples of $I$${}_{2}$ and notated as $\mathbf{1}$.

The coquaternion $q = w + x\,i + y\,j + z\,k$ with scalar $w$, has conjugate $q = w - x\,i - y\,j - z\,k$ similar to Hamilton's quaternions. The quadratic form is $q\,q^* = w^2 + x^2 - y^2 - z^2$.

Note that the 2-sheet [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844) $\left\{ x i + y j + z k : x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = 1 \right\}$ corresponds to the [imaginary units](wikipedia://en/52358) in the algebra so that any point $p$ on this hyperboloid can be used as a **pole** of a sinusoidal wave according to [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613).

The hyperboloid is stable under $\operatorname{SU}(1, 1)$, illustrating the isomorphism with $\operatorname{Spin}(2, 1)$. The variability of the pole of a wave, as noted in studies of [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564), might view [elliptical polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_polarization) as an exhibit of the elliptical shape of a wave with pole $p \ne \pm i$. The [Poincaré sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_sphere_%28optics%29) model used since 1892 has been compared to a 2-sheet hyperboloid model,[^o41] and the practice of [$\operatorname{SU}(1, 1)$ interferometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%281,1%29_interferometry) has been introduced.

When an element of $\operatorname{SU}(1, 1)$ is interpreted as a [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493), it leaves the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) stable, so this group represents the [motions](wikipedia://en/36132944) of the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) of hyperbolic plane geometry. Indeed, for a point $[z, 1]$ in the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line), the action of $\operatorname{SU}(1,1)$ is given by

$$\bigl[\;z,\;1\;\bigr] \begin{pmatrix}u & v^* \\ v & u^* \end{pmatrix}\, = [\;u\,z + v, \, v^*\,z +u^*\;] \, = \, \left[\;\frac{uz + v}{v^*z +u^*}, \, 1 \;\right]$$

since in [projective coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_coordinates) $(\;u\,z + v, \; v^*\,z +u^*\;) \thicksim \left(\;\frac{\,u\,z + v\,}{v^*\,z +u^*}, \; 1 \;\right)$.

Writing $suv + \overline{suv} = 2\,\Re\mathord\bigl(\,suv\,\bigr)$, complex number arithmetic shows

$$\bigl|u\,z + v\bigr|^2 = S + z\,z^* \quad \text{ and } \quad \bigl|v^*\,z + u^*\bigr|^2 = S + 1~,$$

where $S = v\,v^* \left(z\,z^* + 1\right) + 2\,\Re\mathord\bigl(\,uvz\,\bigr)$.

Therefore, $z\,z^* < 1 \implies \left|uz + v\right| < \left|v^*\,z + u^*\right|$ so that their ratio lies in the open disk.[^o42]
