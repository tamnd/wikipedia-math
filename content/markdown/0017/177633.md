# Hypothetical syllogism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hypothetical syllogism"}

::: {#o2 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Hypothetical syllogism"}

**Hypothetical syllogism.**

Whenever instances of $P \to Q$, and $Q \to R$ appear on lines of a [proof](wikipedia://en/1197184), $P \to R$ can be placed on a subsequent line.

:::

In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), a **hypothetical syllogism** is a valid [argument](wikipedia://en/13018310) form, a [deductive](wikipedia://en/61093) [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046) with a [conditional statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence) for one or both of its [premises](wikipedia://en/7993337). Ancient references point to the works of [Theophrastus](wikipedia://en/98409) and [Eudemus](wikipedia://en/3454204) for the first investigation of this kind of syllogism.[^o3][^o4]

:::

## Types {#Types}

Hypothetical syllogisms come in two types: mixed and pure. A *mixed* hypothetical syllogism has two premises: one conditional statement and one statement that either affirms or denies the [antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755) or [consequent](wikipedia://en/804778) of that conditional statement. For example,

If P, then Q.

P.

∴ Q.

In this example, the first premise is a conditional statement in which "P" is the antecedent and "Q" is the consequent. The second premise "affirms" the antecedent. The conclusion, that the consequent must be true, is [deductively](wikipedia://en/61093) [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742).

A mixed hypothetical syllogism has four possible forms, two of which are valid, while the other two are invalid. A valid mixed hypothetical syllogism either affirms the antecedent ([modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900)) or denies the consequent ([modus tollens](wikipedia://en/18901)). An invalid hypothetical syllogism either [affirms the consequent](wikipedia://en/675) (fallacy of the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941)) or [denies the antecedent](wikipedia://en/144793) (fallacy of the [inverse](wikipedia://en/155942)).

A *pure* hypothetical syllogism is a syllogism in which both premises and the conclusion are all [conditional statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence). The antecedent of one premise must match the consequent of the other for the conditional to be valid. Consequently, one of the conditionals contains the remained term as antecedent and the other conditional contains the removed term as consequent.

If P, then Q.

If Q, then R.

∴ If P, then R.

An example in English:

If I do not wake up, then I cannot go to work.

If I cannot go to work, then I will not get paid.

Therefore, if I do not wake up, then I will not get paid.

## Propositional logic {#Propositional_logic}

In [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), **hypothetical syllogism** is the name of a valid [rule of inference](wikipedia://en/252311) (often abbreviated **HS** and sometimes also called the **chain argument**, **chain rule**, or the principle of **transitivity of implication**). The rule may be stated:

$$\frac{P \to Q, Q \to R}{\therefore P \to R}$$

In other words, whenever instances of "$P \to Q$", and "$Q \to R$" appear on lines of a [proof](wikipedia://en/1197184), "$P \to R$" can be placed on a subsequent line.

## Applicability {#Applicability}

The rule of hypothetical syllogism holds in [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), most systems of [relevance logic](wikipedia://en/185076), and many other systems of logic. However, it does not hold in all logics, including, for example, [non-monotonic logic](wikipedia://en/341086), [probabilistic logic](wikipedia://en/5017608) and [default logic](wikipedia://en/889639). The reason for this is that these logics describe [defeasible reasoning](wikipedia://en/2628057), and conditionals that appear in real-world contexts typically allow for exceptions, default assumptions, [ceteris paribus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceteris_paribus) conditions, or just simple uncertainty.

An example, derived from Ernest W. Adams,[^o8]

1. If Jones wins the election, Smith will retire after the election.
1. If Smith dies before the election, Jones will win the election.
1. If Smith dies before the election, Smith will retire after the election.

Clearly, (3) does not follow from (1) and (2). (1) is true by default, but fails to hold in the exceptional circumstances of Smith dying. In practice, real-world conditionals always tend to involve default assumptions or contexts, and it may be infeasible or even impossible to specify all the exceptional circumstances in which they might fail to be true. For similar reasons, the rule of hypothetical syllogism does not hold for [counterfactual conditionals](wikipedia://en/491578).

## Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

The *hypothetical syllogism* inference rule may be written in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation, which amounts to a specialization of the cut rule:

$$\frac{P \vdash Q\quad Q \vdash R}{P \vdash R}$$

where $\vdash$ is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) symbol and $A \vdash B$ meaning that $B$ is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) of $A$ in some [logical system](wikipedia://en/396102);

and expressed as a truth-functional [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) or [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus):

$$((P \to Q) \land (Q \to R)) \to (P \to R)$$

where $P$, $Q$, and $R$ are propositions expressed in some [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102).

## Alternative forms {#Alternative_forms}

An alternative form of hypothetical syllogism, more useful for [classical propositional calculus systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Hilbert_systems#Classical_propositional_calculus_systems) with implication and negation (i.e. without the conjunction symbol), is the following:

(HS1) $(Q \to R) \to ((P \to Q) \to (P \to R))$

Yet another form is:

(HS2) $(P \to Q) \to ((Q \to R) \to (P \to R))$

::: {#o15 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

An example of the proofs of these theorems in such systems is given below. We use two of the three axioms used in [one of the popular systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus#Łukasiewicz's_P2) described by [Jan Łukasiewicz](wikipedia://en/305064). The proofs relies on two out of the three axioms of this system:

(A1) $\phi \to \left( \psi \to \phi \right)$

(A2) $\left( \phi \to \left( \psi \rightarrow \xi \right) \right) \to \left( \left( \phi \to \psi \right) \to \left( \phi \to \xi \right) \right)$

The proof of (HS1) is as follows:

(1) $((p\to(q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))) \to ((q \to r) \to ((p\to(q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))))$ (instance of (A1))

(2) $(p\to(q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))$ (instance of (A2))

(3) $(q \to r) \to ((p\to(q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r)))$ (from (1) and (2) by [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900))

(4) $((q \to r) \to ((p\to(q \to r)) \to ((p \to q) \to (p \to r))))\to (((q \to r) \to (p\to(q \to r))) \to ((q \to r)\to((p \to q) \to(p \to r))))$ (instance of (A2))

(5) $((q \to r) \to (p\to(q \to r))) \to ((q \to r)\to((p \to q) \to(p \to r)))$ (from (3) and (4) by modus ponens)

(6) $(q \to r) \to (p\to(q \to r))$ (instance of (A1))

(7) $(q \to r)\to((p \to q) \to(p \to r))$ (from (5) and (6) by modus ponens)

The proof of (HS2) is given [here](wikipedia://en/8529655#Łukasiewicz's_P2).

:::

### As a metatheorem {#As_a_metatheorem}

Whenever we have two theorems of the form $T_1 = (Q \to R)$ and $T_2 = (P \to Q)$, we can prove $(P \to R)$ by the following steps:

(1) $(Q \to R) \to ((P \to Q) \to (P \to R)))$ (instance of the theorem proved above)

(2) $Q \to R$ (instance of (T1))

(3) $(P \to Q) \to (P \to R)$ (from (1) and (2) by modus ponens)

(4) $P \to Q$ (instance of (T2))

(5) $P \to R$ (from (3) and (4) by modus ponens)
