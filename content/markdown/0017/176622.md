# Degrees of freedom

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Degrees of freedom"}

In many scientific fields, the **degrees of freedom** of a system is the number of [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) of the system that may vary independently. For example, a point in the plane has two degrees of freedom for [translation](wikipedia://en/204682): its two [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates); a non-infinitesimal object on the plane might have additional degrees of freedoms related to its [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098). Another example is the [hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanoid_hand) having 27 independent degrees of freedom.

:::

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), this notion is formalized as the [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics_and_physics%29) of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) or an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808). When *degrees of freedom* is used instead of *dimension*, this usually means that the manifold or variety that models the system is only implicitly defined. See:

- [Degrees of freedom (mechanics)](wikipedia://en/2321587), number of independent motions that are allowed to the body or, in case of a [mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanism_%28engineering%29) made of several bodies, number of possible independent relative motions between the pieces of the mechanism
- [Degrees of freedom (physics and chemistry)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29), a term used in explaining dependence on parameters, or the dimensions of a phase space
- [Degrees of freedom (statistics)](wikipedia://en/2321606), the number of values in the final calculation of a statistic that are free to vary[^o2]
- [Degrees of freedom problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_problem), the problem of controlling motor movement given abundant degrees of freedom
