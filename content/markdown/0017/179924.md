# Helix

*Image: Springs 016.JPG.* (l-r) Tension, compression and torsion coil springs

*Image: Senkschraube.jpg.* A machine screw

*Image: Helix.svg.* The right-handed helix $(\cos t, \sin t, t)$ for $0 \le t \le 4\pi$ with arrowheads showing direction of increasing $t$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Helix"}

A **helix** (; **helices**) is a shape like a cylindrical [coil spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coil_spring) or the thread of a [machine screw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_screw). It is a type of [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoothness_%28mathematics%29) [skew curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_curve) with [tangent lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) at a constant [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) to a fixed axis. Helices are important in [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), as the [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) molecule is formed as [two intertwined helices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_helix), and many [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) have helical substructures, known as [alpha helices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_helix). The word *helix* comes from the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) word ἕλιξ, "twisted, curved".[^o2] A "filled-in" helix – for example, a "spiral" (helical) ramp – is a surface called a *[helicoid](wikipedia://en/684207)*.

:::

## Properties and types {#Properties_and_types}

The **pitch** of a helix is the height of one complete helix [turn](wikipedia://en/855329), measured parallel to the axis of the helix.

A **double helix** consists of two (typically [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330)) helices with the same axis, differing by a translation along the axis.[^o5]

A **circular helix** (i.e. one with constant radius) has constant band [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) and constant [torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_of_curves). The slope of a circular helix is commonly defined as the ratio of the circumference of the circular cylinder that it spirals around, and its pitch (the height of one complete helix turn).

A *[conic helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_helix)*, also known as a *conic spiral*, may be defined as a [spiral](wikipedia://en/61563) on a conic surface, with the distance to the apex an exponential function of the angle indicating direction from the axis.

A curve is called a **general helix** or **cylindrical helix**[^o6] if its tangent makes a constant angle with a fixed line in space. A curve is a general helix if and only if the ratio of [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) to [torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_of_curves) is constant.[^o7]

A curve is called a **slant helix** if its principal normal makes a constant angle with a fixed line in space.[^o8] It can be constructed by applying a transformation to the moving frame of a general helix.[^o9]

For more general helix-like space curves can be found, see [space spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_spiral); e.g., [spherical spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_spiral).

### Handedness {#Handedness}

Helices can be either right-handed or left-handed. With the line of sight along the helix's axis, if a clockwise screwing motion moves the helix away from the observer, then it is called a right-handed helix; if towards the observer, then it is a left-handed helix. Handedness (or [chirality](wikipedia://en/1170160)) is a property of the helix, not of the perspective: a right-handed helix cannot be turned to look like a left-handed one unless it is viewed in a mirror, and vice versa.

Each row compares the two helices from a different perspective. The chirality is a property of the object, not of the [perspective](wikipedia://en/23912960) (view-angle)

## Mathematical description {#Mathematical_description}

*Image: Rising circular.gif.* A helix composed of sinusoidal $x$ and $y$ components

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a helix is a [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry_of_curves) in 3-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) space. The following [parametrisation](wikipedia://en/576108) in [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) defines a particular helix; perhaps the simplest equations for one is

$$\begin{aligned} x(t) &= \cos(t),\\ y(t) &= \sin(t),\\ z(t) &= t. \end{aligned}$$

As the [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065) $t$ increases, the point $(x(t), y(t), z(t))$ traces a right-handed helix of pitch $2\pi$ (or slope 1) and radius 1 about the $z$-axis, in a right-handed coordinate system.

In [cylindrical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates) $(r, \theta, h)$, the same helix is parametrised by:

$$\begin{aligned} r(t) &= 1,\\ \theta(t) &= t,\\ h(t) &= t. \end{aligned}$$

A circular helix of radius $a$ and slope $\tfrac{a}{b}$ (or pitch $2\pi b$) is described by the following parametrisation:

$$\begin{aligned} x(t) &= a\cos(t),\\ y(t) &= a\sin(t),\\ z(t) &= bt. \end{aligned}$$

Another way of mathematically constructing a helix is to plot the complex-valued function $e^{xi}$ as a function of the real number $x$ (see [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613)). The value of $x$ and the real and imaginary parts of the function value give this plot three real dimensions.

Except for [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789), [translations](wikipedia://en/204682), and changes of scale, all right-handed helices are equivalent to the helix defined above. The equivalent left-handed helix can be constructed in a number of ways, the simplest being to negate any one of the $x$, $y$ or $z$ components.

### Arc length, curvature and torsion {#Arc_length,_curvature_and_torsion}

A circular helix of radius $a>0$ and slope $\tfrac{a}{b}$ (or pitch $2\pi b$) expressed in Cartesian coordinates as the [parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108)

$$t\mapsto (a\cos t, a\sin t, bt), t\in [0,T]$$

has an [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) of

$$A = T\cdot \sqrt{a^2+b^2},$$

a [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770#One_dimension_in_three_dimensions:_Curvature_of_space_curves) of

$$\frac{a}{a^2+b^2},$$

and a [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039281) of

$$\frac{b}{a^2+b^2}.$$

Arc length per revolution ($T=2 \pi$): $A = \sqrt{(2\pi a)^2+(2\pi b)^2}$ or $A = \sqrt{(2\pi a)^2+p^2}$ where $p =$pitch.

Twisted length per unit straight length (axial length): $A = \frac{ \sqrt{(2\pi a)^2+p^2}}{p}$

A helix has constant non-zero curvature and torsion.

A helix is the vector-valued function

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}&=a\cos t \mathbf{i}+a\sin t \mathbf{j}+ b t\mathbf{k}\\[6px] \mathbf{v}&=-a\sin t \mathbf{i}+a\cos t \mathbf{j}+ b \mathbf{k}\\[6px] \mathbf{a}&=-a\cos t \mathbf{i}-a\sin t \mathbf{j}+ 0\mathbf{k}\\[6px] |\mathbf{v}|&=\sqrt{(-a\sin t )^2 +(a\cos t)^2 + b^2}=\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}\\[6px] |\mathbf{a}| &= \sqrt{(-a\sin t )^2 +(a\cos t)^2 } = a\\[6px] s(t) &= \int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}d\tau = \sqrt{a^2 +b^2} t \end{aligned}$$

So a helix can be reparameterized as a function of $s$, which must be unit-speed:

$$\mathbf{r}(s) = a\cos \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} } \mathbf{i}+a\sin \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}} \mathbf{j}+ \frac{bs}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}} \mathbf{k}$$

The unit tangent vector is

$$\frac{d \mathbf{r}}{d s} = \mathbf{T} = \frac{-a}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} }\sin \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} } \mathbf{i}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} }\cos \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} }\mathbf{j}+ \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}} \mathbf{k}$$

The normal vector is

$$\frac{d \mathbf{T}}{d s} = \kappa \mathbf{N} = \frac{-a}{a^2 +b^2 }\cos \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} } \mathbf{i}+\frac{-a}{a^2 +b^2} \sin \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} }\mathbf{j}+ 0 \mathbf{k}$$

Its curvature is

$$\kappa = \left|\frac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds}\right|= \frac{a}{a^2 +b^2 }$$

.

The unit normal vector is

$$\mathbf{N}=-\cos \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} } \mathbf{i} - \sin \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2} } \mathbf{j} + 0 \mathbf{k}$$

The binormal vector is

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{B}=\mathbf{T}\times\mathbf{N} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2 }} \left( b\sin \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}}\mathbf{i} - b\cos \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}}\mathbf{j}+ a \mathbf{k}\right)\\[12px] \frac{d\mathbf{B}}{ds} &= \frac{1}{a^2 +b^2} \left( b\cos \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}} \mathbf{i} + b\sin \frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2 +b^2}}\mathbf{j}+ 0 \mathbf{k} \right) \end{aligned}$$

Its torsion is

$$\tau = \left| \frac{d\mathbf{B}}{ds} \right| = \frac{b}{a^2 +b^2}.$$

## Examples {#Examples}

An example of a double helix in molecular biology is the [nucleic acid double helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid_double_helix).

An example of a conic helix is the [Corkscrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corkscrew_%28Cedar_Point%29) roller coaster at [Cedar Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cedar_Point) amusement park.

Some curves found in nature consist of multiple helices of different handedness joined together by transitions known as [tendril perversions](wikipedia://en/19667315).

Most hardware [screw threads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw_thread) are right-handed helices. The alpha helix in biology as well as the [A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-DNA) and [B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-DNA) forms of DNA are also right-handed helices. The [Z form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-DNA) of DNA is left-handed.

In [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), [pitch space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_space) is often modeled with helices or double helices, most often extending out of a circle such as the [circle of fifths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_fifths), so as to represent [octave equivalency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave_equivalency).

In aviation, *geometric pitch* is the distance an element of an airplane propeller would advance in one revolution if it were moving along a helix having an angle equal to that between the chord of the element and a plane perpendicular to the propeller axis; see also: [pitch angle (aviation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_angle_%28aviation%29).
