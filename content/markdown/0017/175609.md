# Cayley–Dickson construction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cayley–Dickson construction"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Cayley–Dickson construction**, sometimes also known as the **Cayley–Dickson process** or the **Cayley–Dickson procedure** produces a sequence of [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788) over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), each with twice the [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_of_a_vector_space) of the previous one. It is named after [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) and [Leonard Eugene Dickson](wikipedia://en/1790247). The algebras produced by this process are known as **Cayley–Dickson algebras**, for example [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), and [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436). These examples are useful [composition algebras](wikipedia://en/3861635) frequently applied in [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416).

:::

The Cayley–Dickson construction defines a new algebra as a [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of an algebra with itself, with [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) defined in a specific way (different from the [componentwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Componentwise_operation) multiplication) and an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) known as *conjugation*. The product of an element and its [conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) (or sometimes the square root of this product) is called the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534).

The symmetries of the real field disappear as the Cayley–Dickson construction is repeatedly applied: first losing [order](wikipedia://en/22430), then [commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) of multiplication, [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) of multiplication, and finally [alternativity](wikipedia://en/186050).

More generally, the Cayley–Dickson construction takes any algebra with involution to another algebra with involution of twice the dimension.[^o2]

[Hurwitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/25167317) states that the reals, complex numbers, quaternions, and octonions are the only finite-dimensional [normed](wikipedia://en/1991870) [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) over the real numbers, while the [Frobenius theorem](wikipedia://en/3966889) states that the first three are the only finite-dimensional associative division algebras over the real numbers.

The **Cayley–Dickson construction** is due to [Leonard Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Dickson) in 1919 showing how the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) can be constructed as a two-dimensional algebra over [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440). In fact, starting with a field *F*, the construction yields a sequence of *F*-algebras of dimension 2<sup>*n*</sup>. For *n* = 2 it is an associative algebra called a [quaternion algebra](wikipedia://en/2776890), and for *n* = 3 it is an [alternative algebra](wikipedia://en/3160) called an [octonion algebra](wikipedia://en/10277277). These instances *n* = 1, 2 and 3 produce [composition algebras](wikipedia://en/3861635) as shown below.

The case *n* = 1 starts with elements (*a*, *b*) in *F* × *F* and defines the conjugate (*a*, *b*)\* to be (*a*\*, –*b*) where *a*\* = *a* in case *n* = 1, and subsequently determined by the formula. The essence of the *F*-algebra lies in the definition of the product of two elements (*a*, *b*) and (*c*, *d*):

$$(a,b) \times (c,d) = (ac - d^*b, da + bc^*).$$

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition 1"}

**Proposition 1:** For $z = (a,b)$ and $w = (c,d),$ the conjugate of the product is $w^*z^* = (zw)^*.$

proof: $(c^*,-d)(a^*,-b) = (c^*a^* + b^*(-d), -bc^*-da) = (zw)^*.$

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition 2"}

**Proposition 2:** If the *F*-algebra is associative and $N(z) = zz^*$,then $N(zw) = N(z)N(w).$

proof: $N(zw) = (ac-d^*b, da+bc^*)(c^*a^*-b^*d, -da -bc^*) = (aa^* + bb^*)(cc^* + dd^*)$ + terms that cancel by the associative property.

:::

## Stages in construction of real algebras {#Stages_in_construction_of_real_algebras}

Details of the construction of the classical real algebras are as follows:

### Complex numbers as ordered pairs {#Complex_numbers_as_ordered_pairs}

The [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) can be written as [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) $(a, b)$ of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $a$ and $b$, with the addition operator being component-wise and with multiplication defined by

$$(a, b) (c, d) = (a c - b d, a d + b c).\,$$

A complex number whose second component is zero is associated with a real number: the complex number $(a, 0)$ is associated with the real number $a$.

The [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) $(a, b)*$ of $(a, b)$ is given by

$$(a, b)^* = (a^*, -b) = (a, -b)$$

since $a$ is a real number and is its own conjugate.

The conjugate has the property that

$$(a, b)^* (a, b) = (a a + b b, a b - b a) = \left(a^2 + b^2, 0\right),\,$$

which is a non-negative real number. In this way, conjugation defines a *[norm](wikipedia://en/990534)*, making the complex numbers a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) over the real numbers: the norm of a complex number $z$ is

$$|z| = \left(z^* z\right)^\frac12.\,$$

Furthermore, for any non-zero complex number $z$, conjugation gives a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/45199),

$$z^{-1} = \frac{z^*}{|z|^2}.$$

As a complex number consists of two independent real numbers, they form a two-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the real numbers.

Besides being of higher dimension, the complex numbers can be said to lack one algebraic property of the real numbers: a real number is its own conjugate.

### Quaternions {#Quaternions}

*Image: Cayley\_Q8\_multiplication\_graph.svg.* Cayley Q8 graph of quaternion multiplication showing cycles of multiplication of *i* (red), *j* (green) and *k* (blue). In \[ the SVG file,\] hover over or click a path to highlight it.

The next step in the construction is to generalize the multiplication and conjugation operations.

Form ordered pairs $(a, b)$ of complex numbers $a$ and $b$, with multiplication defined by

$$(a, b) (c, d) = (a c - d^* b, d a + b c^*).\,$$

Slight variations on this formula are possible; the resulting constructions will yield structures identical up to the signs of bases.

The order of the factors seems odd now, but will be important in the next step.

Define the conjugate $(a, b)*$ of $(a, b)$ by

$$(a, b)^* = (a^*, -b).\,$$

These operators are direct extensions of their complex analogs: if $a$ and $b$ are taken from the real subset of complex numbers, the appearance of the conjugate in the formulas has no effect, so the operators are the same as those for the complex numbers.

The product of a nonzero element with its conjugate is a non-negative real number:

$$\begin{aligned} (a, b)^* (a, b) &= (a^*, -b) (a, b) \\ &= (a^* a + b^* b, b a^* - b a^*) \\ &= \left(|a|^2 + |b|^2, 0 \right).\, \end{aligned}$$

As before, the conjugate thus yields a norm and an inverse for any such ordered pair. So in the sense we explained above, these pairs constitute an algebra something like the real numbers. They are the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), named by [Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1843.

As a quaternion consists of two independent complex numbers, they form a four-dimensional vector space over the real numbers.

The multiplication of quaternions is not quite like the multiplication of real numbers, though; it is not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) – that is, if $p$ and $q$ are quaternions, it is not always true that $pq = qp$.

### Octonions {#Octonions}

All the steps to create further algebras are the same from octonions onwards.

This time, form ordered pairs $(p, q)$ of quaternions $p$ and $q$, with multiplication and conjugation defined exactly as for the quaternions:

$$(p, q) (r, s) = (p r - s^* q, s p + q r^*).\,$$

Note, however, that because the quaternions are not commutative, the order of the factors in the multiplication formula becomes important—if the last factor in the multiplication formula were $r*q$ rather than $qr*$, the formula for multiplication of an element by its conjugate would not yield a real number.

For exactly the same reasons as before, the conjugation operator yields a norm and a multiplicative inverse of any nonzero element.

This algebra was discovered by [John T. Graves](wikipedia://en/14633748) in 1843, and is called the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436) or the "[Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) numbers".[^o17]

As an octonion consists of two independent quaternions, they form an eight-dimensional vector space over the real numbers.

The multiplication of octonions is even stranger than that of quaternions; besides being non-commutative, it is not [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) – that is, if $p$, $q$, and $r$ are octonions, it is not always true that $(pq)r = p(qr)$.

For the reason of this non-associativity, octonions have [no matrix representation](wikipedia://en/51436#Properties).

### Sedenions {#Sedenions}

The algebra immediately following the octonions is called the [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434).[^o19] It retains the algebraic property of [power associativity](wikipedia://en/25054), meaning that if $s$ is a sedenion, $s^{n}s^{m} = s^{n + m}$, but loses the property of being an [alternative algebra](wikipedia://en/3160) and hence cannot be a [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635). It is also at this point that the algebras formed by the Cayley-Dickson construction begin to have nontrivial [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441), in that this and every further algebra created by the construction will have pairs of nonzero values (for example,⁠ $(e_3 + e_{10})$ and ⁠$(e_6 - e_{15})$) which when multiplied give $0$.

### Trigintaduonions {#Trigintaduonions}

The algebra immediately following the [sedenions](wikipedia://en/51434) is the [trigintaduonions](wikipedia://en/25474499),[^o21][^o22][^o23] which form a 32-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_of_a_vector_space) [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)[^o24] and can be represented by [blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276) $\mathbb T$.[^o25]

### Further algebras {#Further_algebras}

The Cayley–Dickson construction can be carried on *[ad infinitum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_infinitum)*, at each step producing a power-associative algebra whose dimension is double that of the algebra of the preceding step. These include the 64-dimensional sexagintaquatronions (or 64-nions), the 128-dimensional centumduodetrigintanions (or 128-nions), the 256-dimensional ducentiquinquagintasexions (or 256-nions), and *ad infinitum*.[^o27] All the algebras generated in this way over a field are *quadratic*: that is, each element satisfies a quadratic equation with coefficients from the field.[^o2]

In 1954, [R. D. Schafer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_D._Schafer) proved that the algebras generated by the Cayley–Dickson process over a field $F$ satisfy the [flexible identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flexible_identity). He also proved that any [derivation algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_algebra) of a Cayley–Dickson algebra is isomorphic to the derivation algebra of Cayley numbers, a 14-dimensional [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) over $F$.[^o28]

## Modified Cayley–Dickson construction {#Modified_Cayley–Dickson_construction}

The Cayley–Dickson construction, starting from the real numbers $\mathbb R$, generates the [composition algebras](wikipedia://en/3861635) $\mathbb C$ (the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)), $\mathbb H$ (the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440)), and $\mathbb O$ (the [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436)). There are also composition algebras whose norm is an [isotropic quadratic form](wikipedia://en/10917170), which are obtained through a slight modification, by replacing the minus sign in the definition of the product of ordered pairs with a plus sign, as follows:

$$(a, b) (c, d) = (a c + d^* b, d a + b c^*).$$

When this modified construction is applied to $\mathbb R$, one obtains the [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559), which are [ring-isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) to the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) $\mathbb R \times \mathbb R;$ following that, one obtains the [split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265), an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to that of the 2 × 2 real [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859); and the [split-octonions](wikipedia://en/3861649), which are isomorphic to $\operatorname{Zorn}(\mathbf{R})$. Applying the original Cayley–Dickson construction to the split-complexes also results in the split-quaternions and then the split-octonions.[^o30]

## General Cayley–Dickson construction {#General_Cayley–Dickson_construction}

(Albert 1942, 171) gave a slight generalization, defining the product and involution on $B = A \oplus A$ for $A$ an [algebra with involution](wikipedia://en/293426) (with $(xy)* = y*x*$) to be

$$\begin{aligned} (p, q) (r, s) &= (p r - \gamma s^* q, s p + q r^*)\, \\ (p, q)^* &= (p^*, -q)\, \end{aligned}$$

for $\gamma$ an additive map that commutes with $*$ and left and right multiplication by any element. (Over the reals all choices of $\gamma$ are equivalent to −1, 0 or 1.) In this construction, $A$ is an algebra with involution, meaning:

- $A$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) under $+$
- $A$ has a product that is left and right [distributive](wikipedia://en/103118) over $+$
- $A$ has an involution $*$, with $(x*)* = x$, $(x + y)* = x* + y*$, $(xy)* = y*x*$.

The algebra $B = A \oplus A$ produced by the Cayley–Dickson construction is also an algebra with involution.

$B$ inherits properties from $A$ unchanged as follows.

- If $A$ has an identity $1_{A}$, then $B$ has an identity $(1_{A}, 0)$.
- If $A$ has the property that $x + x*$, $xx*$ associate and commute with all elements, then so does $B$. This property implies that any element generates a commutative associative \*-algebra, so in particular the algebra is power associative.

Other properties of $A$ only induce weaker properties of $B$:

- If $A$ is commutative and has trivial involution, then $B$ is commutative.
- If $A$ is commutative and associative then $B$ is associative.
- If $A$ is associative and $x + x*$, $xx*$ associate and commute with everything, then $B$ is an [alternative algebra](wikipedia://en/3160).
