# Cyclotomic polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cyclotomic polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the $n$-th **cyclotomic polynomial**, for any [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer) $n$, is the unique [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) with integer [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) that is a [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of $x^n-1$ and is not a divisor of $x^k-1$ for any $k<n$. Its [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) are all $n$-th [primitive roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) $e^{2i\pi\frac{k}{n}}$, where $k$ runs over the positive integers up to $n$ and [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556) to $n$ (where $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358)). In other words, the $n$-th cyclotomic polynomial is equal to

:::

$$\Phi_n(x) = \prod_\stackrel{1\le k\le n}{\gcd(k,n)=1} \left(x-e^{2i\pi\frac{k}{n}}\right).$$

It may also be defined as the [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) with integer coefficients that is the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) of any [primitive *n*th-root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) ($e^{2i\pi/n}$ is an example of such a root).

An important relation linking cyclotomic polynomials and primitive roots of unity is

$$\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(x) = x^n - 1,$$

showing that $\alpha$ is a root of $x^n - 1$ if and only if it is a $d$-th primitive root of unity for some $d$ that divides $n$.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

If *n* is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), then

$$\Phi_n(x) = 1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^{n-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x^k.$$

$$\Phi_{2p}(x) = 1-x+x^2-\cdots+x^{p-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1} (-x)^k.$$

## Properties {#Properties}

### Fundamental tools {#Fundamental_tools}

The cyclotomic polynomials are monic polynomials with integer coefficients that are [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) over the field of the rational numbers. Except for *n* equal to 1 or 2, they are [palindromes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindromic_polynomial) of even degree.

The degree of $\Phi_n$, or in other words the number of *n*th primitive roots of unity, is $\varphi (n)$, where $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452).

The fact that $\Phi_n$ is an irreducible polynomial of degree $\varphi (n)$ in the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a nontrivial result due to [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125). Depending on the chosen definition, it is either the value of the degree or the irreducibility which is a nontrivial result. The case of prime *n* is easier to prove than the general case, thanks to [Eisenstein's criterion](wikipedia://en/385989#Cyclotomic_polynomials).

A fundamental relation involving cyclotomic polynomials is

$$\begin{aligned} x^n - 1 &=\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n} \left(x- e^{2i\pi\frac{k}{n}} \right) \\ &= \prod_{d \mid n} \prod_{1 \leqslant k \leqslant n \atop \gcd(k, n) = d} \left(x- e^{2i\pi\frac{k}{n}} \right) \\ &=\prod_{d \mid n} \Phi_{\frac{n}{d}}(x) = \prod_{d\mid n} \Phi_d(x).\end{aligned}$$

which means that each *n*-th root of unity is a primitive *d*-th root of unity for a unique *d* dividing *n*.

The [Möbius inversion formula](wikipedia://en/20963#Multiplicative_notation) allows $\Phi_n(x)$ to be expressed as an explicit rational fraction:

$$\Phi_n(x)=\prod_{d\mid n}(x^d-1)^{\mu \left (\frac{n}{d} \right )},$$

where $\mu$ is the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961).

This provides a [recursive formula](wikipedia://en/1047605) for the cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_{n}(x)$, which may be computed by [dividing](wikipedia://en/314205) $x^n-1$ by the cyclotomic polynomials $\Phi_d(x)$ for the proper divisors *d* dividing *n*, starting from $\Phi_{1}(x)=x-1$:

$$\Phi_n(x)=\frac{x^{n}-1}{\prod_{\stackrel{d|n}{{}_{d<n}}}\Phi_{d}(x)}.$$

This gives an algorithm for computing any $\Phi_n(x)$, provided [integer factorization](wikipedia://en/15491) and [division of polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_division_of_polynomials) are available. Many [computer algebra systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_algebra_systems), such as [SageMath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SageMath), [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29), [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica), and [PARI/GP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PARI/GP), have a built-in function to compute the cyclotomic polynomials.

### Easy cases for computation {#Easy_cases_for_computation}

As noted above, if $n = p$ is a prime number, then

$$\Phi_p(x) = 1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^{p-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}x^k\;.$$

If *n* is an odd integer greater than one, then

$$\Phi_{2n}(x) = \Phi_n(-x)\;.$$

In particular, if $n = 2p$ is twice an odd prime, then (as noted above)

$$\Phi_{2p}(x) = 1-x+x^2-\cdots+x^{p-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}(-x)^k\;.$$

If $n = p^{m}$ is a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) (where *p* is prime), then

$$\Phi_{p^m}(x) = \Phi_p(x^{p^{m-1}}) =\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}x^{kp^{m-1}}\;.$$

More generally, if $n = p^{m}r$ with $r$ [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) to $p$, then

$$\Phi_{p^mr}(x) = \Phi_{pr}(x^{p^{m-1}})\;.$$

These formulas may be applied repeatedly to get a simple expression for any cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_n(x)$ in terms of a cyclotomic polynomial of [square free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-free_number) index: If $q$ is the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of the prime divisors of $n$ (its [radical](wikipedia://en/1634886)), then[^o12]

$$\Phi_n(x) = \Phi_q(x^{n/q})\;.$$

This allows formulas to be given for the $n$th cyclotomic polynomial when $n$ has at most one odd prime factor: If $p$ is an odd prime number, and $\ell$ and $m$ are positive integers, then

$$\Phi_{2^m}(x) = x^{2^{m-1}}+1\;,$$

$$\Phi_{p^m}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{p-1}x^{jp^{m-1}}\;,$$

$$\Phi_{2^\ell p^m}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{p-1}(-1)^jx^{j2^{\ell-1}p^{m-1}}\;.$$

For other values of $n$, the computation of the $n$th cyclotomic polynomial is similarly reduced to that of $\Phi_q(x),$ where $q$ is the product of the distinct odd prime divisors of $n$. To deal with this case, one has that, for $p$ prime and not dividing $n$,

$$\Phi_{np}(x)=\Phi_{n}(x^p)/\Phi_n(x)\;.$$

### Integers appearing as coefficients {#Integers_appearing_as_coefficients}

The problem of bounding the magnitude of the coefficients of the cyclotomic polynomials has been the object of a number of research papers.[^o15]

If *n* has at most two distinct odd prime factors, then Migotti showed that the coefficients of $\Phi_n$ are all in the set {1, −1, 0}.[^o16]

The first cyclotomic polynomial for a product of three different odd prime factors is $\Phi_{105}(x);$ it has a coefficient −2 (see above). The converse is not true: $\Phi_{231}(x)=\Phi_{3\times 7\times 11}(x)$ only has coefficients in {1, −1, 0}.

If *n* is a product of more different odd prime factors, the coefficients may increase to very high values. E.g., $\Phi_{15015}(x) =\Phi_{3\times 5\times 7\times 11\times 13}(x)$ has coefficients running from −22 to 23; also $\Phi_{255255}(x)=\Phi_{3\times 5\times 7\times 11\times 13\times 17}(x)$, the smallest *n* with 6 different odd primes, has coefficients of magnitude up to 532.

Let *A*(*n*) denote the maximum [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the coefficients of $\Phi_{n}(x)$. It is known that for any positive *k*, the number of *n* up to *x* with *A*(*n*) > *n*<sup>*k*</sup> is at least *c*(*k*)⋅*x* for a positive *c*(*k*) depending on *k* and *x* sufficiently large. In the opposite direction, for any function ψ(*n*) tending to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824) with *n* we have *A*(*n*) bounded above by *n*<sup>ψ(*n*)</sup> for almost all *n*.[^o17]

A combination of theorems of Bateman and Vaughan states thatp. 10 on the one hand, for every $\varepsilon>0$, we have

$$A(n) < e^{\left(n^{(\log 2+\varepsilon)/(\log\log n)}\right)}$$

for all sufficiently large positive integers $n$, and on the other hand, we have

$$A(n) > e^{\left(n^{(\log 2)/(\log\log n)}\right)}$$

for infinitely many positive integers $n$. This implies in particular that [univariate polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univariate_polynomial) (concretely $x^n-1$ for infinitely many positive integers $n$) can have factors (like $\Phi_n$) whose coefficients are [superpolynomially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superpolynomial) larger than the original coefficients. This is not too far from the general [Landau-Mignotte bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau-Mignotte_bound).

### Gauss's formula {#Gauss's_formula}

Let *n* be odd, [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525), and greater than 3. Then:[^o20][^o21]

$$4\Phi_n(z) = A_n^2(z) - (-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}nz^2B_n^2(z)$$

for certain polynomials *A<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) and *B<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) with integer coefficients, *A<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) of degree *φ*(*n*)/2, and *B<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) of degree *φ*(*n*)/2 − 2. Furthermore, *A<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) is palindromic when its degree is even; if its degree is odd it is antipalindromic. Similarly, *B<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) is palindromic unless *n* is composite and *n* ≡ 3 (mod 4), in which case it is antipalindromic.

The first few cases are

$$\begin{aligned} 4\Phi_5(z) &=4(z^4+z^3+z^2+z+1)\\ &= (2z^2+z+2)^2 - 5z^2 \\[6pt] 4\Phi_7(z) &=4(z^6+z^5+z^4+z^3+z^2+z+1)\\ &= (2z^3+z^2-z-2)^2+7z^2(z+1)^2 \\ [6pt] 4\Phi_{11}(z) &=4(z^{10}+z^9+z^8+z^7+z^6+z^5+z^4+z^3+z^2+z+1)\\ &= (2z^5+z^4-2z^3+2z^2-z-2)^2+11z^2(z^3+1)^2 \end{aligned}$$

### Lucas's formula {#Lucas's_formula}

Let *n* be odd, square-free and greater than 3. Then

$$\Phi_n(z) = U_n^2(z) - (-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}nzV_n^2(z)$$

for certain polynomials *U<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) and *V<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) with integer coefficients, *U<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) of degree *φ*(*n*)/2, and *V<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) of degree *φ*(*n*)/2 − 1. This can also be written

$$\Phi_n \left ((-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}z \right ) = C_n^2(z) - nzD_n^2(z).$$

If *n* is even, square-free and greater than 2 (this forces *n*/2 to be odd),

$$\Phi_{\frac{n}{2}} (-z^2) = \Phi_{2n}(z)= C_n^2(z) - nzD_n^2(z)$$

for *C<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) and *D<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) with integer coefficients, *C<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) of degree *φ*(*n*), and *D<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) of degree *φ*(*n*) − 1. *C<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) and *D<sub>n</sub>*(*z*) are both palindromic.

The first few cases are:

$$\begin{aligned} \Phi_3(-z) &=\Phi_6(z) =z^2-z+1 \\ &= (z+1)^2 - 3z \\[6pt] \Phi_5(z) &=z^4+z^3+z^2+z+1 \\ &= (z^2+3z+1)^2 - 5z(z+1)^2 \\[6pt] \Phi_{6/2}(-z^2) &=\Phi_{12}(z)=z^4-z^2+1 \\ &= (z^2+3z+1)^2 - 6z(z+1)^2 \end{aligned}$$

### Sister Beiter conjecture {#Sister_Beiter_conjecture}

The [Sister Beiter conjecture](wikipedia://en/73599200) is concerned with the maximal size (in absolute value) $A(pqr)$ of coefficients of *ternary cyclotomic polynomials* $\Phi_{pqr}(x)$ where $p\leq q\leq r$ are three odd primes.[^o26]

## Cyclotomic polynomials over a finite field and over the $p$-adic integers {#Cyclotomic_polynomials_over_a_finite_field_and_over_the_-adic_integers}

Over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with a prime number $p$ of elements, for any integer $n$ that is not a multiple of $p$, the cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_n$ factorizes into $\frac{\varphi (n)}{d}$ irreducible polynomials of degree $d$, where $\varphi (n)$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) and $d$ is the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) of $p$ modulo $n$. In particular, $\Phi_n$ is irreducible [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $p$ is a [primitive root modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/186864), that is, $p$ does not divide $n$, and its multiplicative order modulo $n$ is $\varphi(n)$, the degree of $\Phi_n$.[^o28]

These results are also true over the [$p$-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer), since [Hensel's lemma](wikipedia://en/1633368) allows lifting a factorization over the field with $p$ elements to a factorization over the $p$-adic integers.

## Polynomial values {#Polynomial_values}

If $x$ takes any real value, then $\Phi_n(x)>0$ for every $n \ge 3$ (this follows from the fact that the roots of a cyclotomic polynomial are all non-real, for $n \ge 3$).

For studying the values that a cyclotomic polynomial may take when $x$ is given an integer value, it suffices to consider only the case $n \ge 3$, as the cases $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ are trivial (one has $\Phi_1(x)=x-1$ and $\Phi_2(x)=x+1$).

For $n \ge 2$, one has

$$\Phi_n(0) =1,$$

$\Phi_n(1) =1$ if $n$ is not a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483),

$\Phi_n(1) =p$ if $n=p^k$ is a prime power with $k \ge 1$.

The values that a cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_n(x)$ may take for other integer values of $x$ is strongly related with the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) modulo a prime number.

More precisely, given a prime number $p$ and an integer $b$ coprime with $p$, the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ such that $p$ is a divisor of $b^n-1.$ For $b > 1$, the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$ is also the [shortest period](wikipedia://en/76848) of the representation of $1/p$ in the [numeral base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_base) $b$ (see [Unique prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_prime); this explains the notation choice).

The definition of the multiplicative order implies that, if $n$ is the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$, then $p$ is a divisor of $\Phi_n(b).$ The converse is not true, but one has the following.

If $n > 0$ is a positive integer and $b > 1$ is an integer, then (see below for a proof)

$$\Phi_n(b)=2^kgh,$$

where

- $k$ is a non-negative integer, always equal to 0 when $b$ is even. (In fact, if $n$ is neither 1 nor 2, then $k$ is either 0 or 1. Besides, if $n$ is not a [power of 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_2), then $k$ is always equal to 0)
- $g$ is 1 or the largest odd prime factor of $n$.
- $h$ is odd, coprime with $n$, and its [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) are exactly the odd primes $p$ such that $n$ is the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$.

This implies that, if $p$ is an odd prime divisor of $\Phi_n(b),$ then either $n$ is a divisor of $p - 1$ or $p$ is a divisor of $n$. In the latter case, $p^2$ does not divide $\Phi_n(b).$

[Zsigmondy's theorem](wikipedia://en/5767229) implies that the only cases where $b > 1$ and $h = 1$ are

$$\begin{aligned} \Phi_1(2) &=1 \\ \Phi_2 \left (2^k-1 \right ) & =2^k && k >0 \\ \Phi_6(2) &=3 \end{aligned}$$

It follows from above factorization that the odd prime factors of

$$\frac{\Phi_n(b)}{\gcd(n,\Phi_n(b))}$$

are exactly the odd primes $p$ such that $n$ is the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$. This fraction may be even only when $b$ is odd. In this case, the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $2$ is always $1$.

There are many pairs $(n, b)$ with $b > 1$ such that $\Phi_n(b)$ is prime. In fact, [Bunyakovsky conjecture](wikipedia://en/12511846) implies that, for every $n$, there are infinitely many $b > 1$ such that $\Phi_n(b)$ is prime. See for the list of the smallest $b > 1$ such that $\Phi_n(b)$ is prime (the smallest $b > 1$ such that $\Phi_n(b)$ is prime is about $\gamma \cdot \varphi(n)$, where $\gamma$ is [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant), and $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452)). See also for the list of the smallest primes of the form $\Phi_n(b)$ with $n > 2$ and $b > 1$, and, more generally, , for the smallest positive integers of this form.

- *Values of* $\Phi_n(1).$ If $n=p^{k+1}$ is a prime power, then

$$\Phi_n(x)=1+x^{p^k}+x^{2p^{k}}+\cdots+x^{(p-1)p^k} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \Phi_n(1)=p.$$

If $n$ is not a prime power, let $P(x)=1+x+\cdots+x^{n-1},$ we have $P(1)=n,$ and $P$ is the product of the $\Phi_k(x)$ for $k$ dividing $n$ and different of $1$. If $p$ is a prime divisor of multiplicity $m$ in $n$, then $\Phi_p(x), \Phi_{p^2}(x), \cdots, \Phi_{p^m}(x)$ divide $P(x)$, and their values at $1$ are $m$ factors equal to $p$ of $n=P(1).$ As $m$ is the multiplicity of $p$ in $n$, $p$ cannot divide the value at $1$ of the other factors of $P(x).$ Thus there is no prime that divides $\Phi_n(1).$

- *If* $n$ *is the multiplicative order of* $b$ *modulo* $p$, *then* $p \mid \Phi_n(b).$ By definition, $p \mid b^n-1.$ If $p \nmid \Phi_n(b),$ then $p$ would divide another factor $\Phi_k(b)$ of $b^n-1,$ and would thus divide $b^k-1,$ showing that, if there would be the case, $n$ would not be the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$.
- *The other prime divisors of* $\Phi_n(b)$ *are divisors of* $n$. Let $p$ be a prime divisor of $\Phi_n(b)$ such that $n$ is not be the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$. If $k$ is the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$, then $p$ divides both $\Phi_n(b)$ and $\Phi_k(b).$ The [resultant](wikipedia://en/2195020) of $\Phi_n(x)$ and $\Phi_k(x)$ may be written $P\Phi_k+Q\Phi_n,$ where $P$ and $Q$ are polynomials. Thus $p$ divides this resultant. As $k$ divides $n$, and the resultant of two polynomials divides the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of any common multiple of these polynomials, $p$ divides also the discriminant $n^n$ of $x^n-1.$ Thus $p$ divides $n$.
- $g$ *and* $h$ *are coprime*. In other words, if $p$ is a prime common divisor of $n$ and $\Phi_n(b),$ then $n$ is not the multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$. By [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453), the multiplicative order of $b$ is a divisor of $p - 1$, and thus smaller than $n$.
- $g$ *is square-free*. In other words, if $p$ is a prime common divisor of $n$ and $\Phi_n(b),$ then $p^2$ does not divide $\Phi_n(b).$ Let $n = pm$. It suffices to prove that $p^2$ does not divide $S(b)$ for some polynomial $S(x)$, which is a multiple of $\Phi_n(x).$ We take

$$S(x)=\frac{x^n-1}{x^m-1} = 1 + x^m + x^{2m} + \cdots + x^{(p-1)m}.$$

The multiplicative order of $b$ modulo $p$ divides $\gcd(n, p - 1)$, which is a divisor of $m = n/p$. Thus $c = b^{m} - 1$ is a multiple of $p$. Now,

$$S(b) = \frac{(1+c)^p-1}{c} = p+ \binom{p}{2}c + \cdots + \binom{p}{p}c^{p-1}.$$

As $p$ is prime and greater than 2, all the terms but the first one are multiples of $p^2.$ This proves that $p^2 \nmid \Phi_n(b).$

::: {#o31 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Using $\Phi_n$, one can give an elementary proof for the infinitude of [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) [congruent](wikipedia://en/48167) to 1 modulo *n*,[^o32] which is a special case of [Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/101453).

Suppose $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k$ is a finite list of primes congruent to $1$ modulo $n.$ Let $N = np_1p_2\cdots p_k$ and consider $\Phi_n(N)$. Let $q$ be a prime factor of $\Phi_n(N)$ (to see that $\Phi_n(N) \neq \pm 1$ decompose it into linear factors and note that 1 is the closest root of unity to $N$). Since $\Phi_n(x) \equiv \pm 1 \pmod x,$ we know that $q$ is a new prime not in the list. We will show that $q \equiv 1 \pmod n.$

Let $m$ be the order of $N$ modulo $q.$ Since $\Phi_n(N) \mid N^n - 1$ we have $N^n -1 \equiv 0 \pmod{q}$. Thus $m \mid n$. We will show that $m = n$.

Assume for contradiction that $m < n$. Since

$$\prod_{d \mid m} \Phi_d(N) = N^m - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod q$$

we have

$$\Phi_d(N) \equiv 0 \pmod q,$$

for some $d < n$. Then $N$ is a double root of

$$\prod_{d \mid n} \Phi_d(x) \equiv x^n -1 \pmod q.$$

Thus $N$ must be a root of the derivative so

$$\left.\frac{d(x^n -1)}{dx}\right|_N \equiv nN^{n-1} \equiv 0 \pmod q.$$

But $q \nmid N$ and therefore $q \nmid n.$ This is a contradiction so $m = n$. The order of $N \pmod q,$ which is $n$, must divide $q-1$. Thus $q \equiv 1 \pmod n.$

### Periodic recursive sequences {#Periodic_recursive_sequences}

The constant-coefficient [linear recurrences](wikipedia://en/50700194) which are periodic are precisely the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) coefficients of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) whose denominators are products of cyclotomic polynomials.

In the theory of combinatorial [generating functions](wikipedia://en/160993), the denominator of a rational function determines a linear recurrence for its power series coefficients. For example, the [Fibonacci sequence](wikipedia://en/10918) has generating function

> $$F(x) = F_1x + F_2x^2 + F_3x^3 + \cdots = \frac{x}{1-x-x^2} ,$$

and [equating coefficients](wikipedia://en/6874521) on both sides of $F(x)(1-x-x^2) = x$ gives $F_n - F_{n-1} - F_{n-2} = 0$ for $n\geq 2$.

Any rational function whose denominator is a divisor of $x^n - 1$ has a recursive sequence of coefficients which is periodic with period at most *n*. For example,

> $$P(x) = -\frac{1+2x}{\Phi_6(x)} = \frac{1+2x}{1-x+x^2} = \sum_{n\geq 0} P_n x^n = 1 + 3 x + 2 x^2 - x^3 - 3 x^4 - 2 x^5 + x^6 + 3 x^7 + 2 x^8 + \cdots$$

has coefficients defined by the recurrence $P_n - P_{n-1} + P_{n-2} = 0$ for $n\geq 2$, starting from $P_0=1, P_1=3$. But $1-x^6 = \Phi_6(x)\Phi_3(x)\Phi_2(x)\Phi_1(x)$, so we may write

> $$P(x) = \frac{(1+2x)\Phi_3(x)\Phi_2(x)\Phi_1(x)}{1 - x^6} = \frac{1 + 3 x + 2 x^2 - x^3 - 3 x^4-2 x^5}{1 - x^6},$$

which means $P_n - P_{n-6} = 0$ for $n\geq 6$, and the sequence has period 6 with initial values given by the coefficients of the numerator.
