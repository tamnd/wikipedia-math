# Optic equation

*Image: optic\_equation\_integer\_solutions.svg.* Integer solutions to the optic equation $\tfrac{1}{a} + \tfrac{1}{b} = \tfrac{1}{c}$ for $1 \le a,b \le 99$. The number in the circle is $c$. In \[ the SVG file,\] hover over a circle to see its solution.

Octic equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Optic equation"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **optic equation** is an equation that requires the sum of the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of two positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $a$ and $b$ to equal the reciprocal of a third positive integer $c$:[^o2]

$$\frac{1}{a}+\frac{1}{b}=\frac{1}{c}.$$

:::

Multiplying both sides by $abc$ shows that the optic equation is equivalent to a [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109) (a [polynomial equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation) in multiple integer variables).

## Solution {#Solution}

All solutions in integers $a, b, c$ are given in terms of positive integer parameters $m, n, k$ by[^o2]

$$\begin{aligned} a &= km(m+n), \\ b &= kn(m+n), \\ c &= kmn, \end{aligned}$$

where $m, n$ are [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556).

## Appearances in geometry {#Appearances_in_geometry}

*Image: inverse\_pythagorean\_theorem.svg.* The optic equation with squares appears in the [inverse Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/62694924) (red). For triangle $ABC$ with right angle at $C$, then altitude $red$ (red) perpendicular to hypotenuse $blue$ (blue) is given by the inverse Pythagorean theorem $\tfrac{1}{\operatorname{red}^{2}} = \tfrac{1}{AC^{2}} + \tfrac{1}{BC^{2}}$. The Pythagorean relation also holds: $\operatorname{blue}^{2} = AC^{2} + BC^{2}$.

The optic equation, permitting but not requiring integer solutions, appears in several contexts in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446).

In a [bicentric quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/23335118), the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) $r$, the circumradius $R$, and the distance $x$ between the incenter and the circumcenter are related by [Fuss' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuss'_theorem) according to

$$\frac{1}{(R-x)^2}+\frac{1}{(R+x)^2}=\frac{1}{r^2},$$

and the distances of the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) $I$ from the vertices $A, B, C, D$ are related to the inradius according to

$$\frac{1}{IA^2}+\frac{1}{IC^2}=\frac{1}{IB^2}+\frac{1}{ID^2}=\frac{1}{r^2}.$$

*Image: CrossedLadders.svg.* Crossed ladders. $\tfrac{1}{h} = \tfrac{1}{A} + \tfrac{1}{B}$.

In the [crossed ladders problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossed_ladders_problem),[^o5] two ladders braced at the bottoms of vertical walls cross at the height $h$ and lean against the opposite walls at heights of $A$ and $B$. We have $\tfrac{1}{h}=\tfrac{1}{A}+\tfrac{1}{B}.$ Moreover, the formula continues to hold if the walls are slanted and all three measurements are made parallel to the walls.

Let $P$ be a point on the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) of an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) $\triangle ABC$, on the [minor arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) $AB$. Let $a$ be the distance from $P$ to $A$ and $b$ be the distance from $P$ to $B$. On a line passing through $P$ and the far vertex $C$, let $c$ be the distance from $P$ to the triangle side $AB$. Then[^o6] $\tfrac{1}{a}+\tfrac{1}{b}=\tfrac{1}{c}.$

In a [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/201826), draw a segment parallel to the two parallel sides, passing through the intersection of the diagonals and having endpoints on the non-parallel sides. Then if we denote the lengths of the parallel sides as $a$ and $b$ and half the length of the segment through the diagonal intersection as $c$, the sum of the reciprocals of $a$ and $b$ equals the reciprocal of $c$.[^o7]

The special case in which the integers whose reciprocals are taken must be [square numbers](wikipedia://en/213919) appears in two ways in the context of [right triangles](wikipedia://en/39420). First, the sum of the reciprocals of the squares of the altitudes from the legs (equivalently, of the squares of the legs themselves) equals the reciprocal of the square of the altitude from the hypotenuse. This holds whether or not the numbers are integers; there is a formula (see [here](wikipedia://en/27430123#Pythagorean_triangles_with_integer_altitude_from_the_hypotenuse)) that generates all integer cases.[^o8][^o9] Second, also in a right triangle the sum of the squared reciprocal of the side of one of the two inscribed squares and the squared reciprocal of the hypotenuse equals the squared reciprocal of the side of the other inscribed square.

The sides of a [heptagonal triangle](wikipedia://en/50790424), which shares its vertices with a regular [heptagon](wikipedia://en/365349), satisfy the optic equation.

## Other appearances {#Other_appearances}

### Thin lens equation {#Thin_lens_equation}

*Image: Lens3.svg.* Distances in the thin lens equation

For a lens of negligible thickness, and focal length $f$, the distances from the lens to an object, $S_{1}$, and from the lens to its image, $S_{2}$, are related by the [thin lens formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29#Imaging_properties):

$$\frac{1}{S_1}+\frac{1}{S_2}=\frac{1}{f} .$$

### Electrical engineering {#Electrical_engineering}

Components of an electrical circuit or electronic circuit can be connected in what is called a [series or parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_and_parallel_circuits) configuration. For example, the total [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance) value $R_{t}$ of two [resistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resistor) with resistances $R_{1}$ and $R_{2}$ connected in *parallel* follows the optic equation:

$$\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{1}{R_t}.$$

Similarly, the total [inductance](wikipedia://en/165146) $L_{t}$ of two [inductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor) with inductances $L_{1}, L_{2}$ connected in *parallel* is given by:

$$\frac{1}{L_1} + \frac{1}{L_2} = \frac{1}{L_t},$$

and the total [capacitance](wikipedia://en/140711) $C_{t}$ of two [capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor) with capacitances $C_{1}, C_{2}$ connected in *series* is as follows:

$$\frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} = \frac{1}{C_t}.$$

### Paper folding {#Paper_folding}

*Image: Folding\_paper\_into\_thirds.svg.* Folding a rectangular sheet of paper into thirds using the crossed ladders problem

The optic equation of the crossed ladders problem can be applied to folding rectangular paper into three equal parts. One side (the left one illustrated here) is partially folded in half and pinched to leave a mark. The intersection of a line from this mark to an opposite corner, with a diagonal is exactly one third from the bottom edge. The top edge can then be folded down to meet the intersection.[^o14]

### Harmonic mean {#Harmonic_mean}

The [harmonic mean](wikipedia://en/14463) of $a$ and $b$ is $\tfrac{2}{\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b}}$ or $2c$. In other words, $c$ is half the harmonic mean of $a$ and $b$.

## Relation to Fermat's Last Theorem {#Relation_to_Fermat's_Last_Theorem}

[Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) states that the sum of two integers each raised to the same integer power $n$ cannot equal another integer raised to the power $n$ if $n > 2$. This implies that no solutions to the optic equation have all three integers equal to [perfect powers](wikipedia://en/4023591) with the same power $n > 2$. For if $\tfrac{1}{x^n}+\tfrac{1}{y^n}=\tfrac{1}{z^n},$ then multiplying through by $(xyz)^n$ would give $(yz)^n+(xz)^n=(xy)^n,$ which is impossible by Fermat's Last Theorem.

:::
