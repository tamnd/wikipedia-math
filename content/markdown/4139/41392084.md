# Cyclic subspace

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cyclic subspace"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a **cyclic subspace** is a certain special [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) associated with a vector in the vector space and a [linear transformation](wikipedia://en/18102) of the vector space. The cyclic subspace associated with a vector *v* in a vector space *V* and a linear transformation *T* of *V* is called the ***T*-cyclic subspace generated by *v***. The concept of a cyclic subspace is a basic component in the formulation of the cyclic decomposition theorem in linear algebra.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $T:V\rightarrow V$ be a linear transformation of a vector space $V$ and let $v$ be a vector in $V$. The $T$-cyclic subspace of $V$ generated by $v$, denoted $Z(v;T)$, is the subspace of $V$ generated by the set of vectors $\{ v, T(v), T^2(v), \ldots, T^r(v), \ldots\}$. In the case when $V$ is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752), $v$ is called a **cyclic vector** for $T$ if $Z(v;T)$ is dense in $V$. For the particular case of [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) spaces, this is equivalent to saying that $Z(v;T)$ is the whole space $V$. [^o4]

There is another equivalent definition of cyclic spaces. Let $T:V\rightarrow V$ be a linear transformation of a topological vector space over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$ and $v$ be a vector in $V$. The set of all vectors of the form $g(T)v$, where $g(x)$ is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $F[x]$ of all polynomials in $x$ over $F$, is the $T$-cyclic subspace generated by $v$.[^o4]

The subspace $Z(v;T)$ is an [invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826) for $T$, in the sense that $T Z(v;T) \subset Z(v;T)$.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

1. For any vector space $V$ and any linear operator $T$ on $V$, the $T$-cyclic subspace generated by the zero vector is the zero-subspace of $V$.
1. If $I$ is the [identity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_operator) then every $I$-cyclic subspace is one-dimensional.
1. $Z(v;T)$ is one-dimensional if and only if $v$ is a [characteristic vector](wikipedia://en/2161429) (eigenvector) of $T$.
1. Let $V$ be the two-dimensional vector space and let $T$ be the linear operator on $V$ represented by the matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 0&1\\ 0&0\end{bmatrix}$ relative to the standard ordered basis of $V$. Let $v=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $Tv = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T^2v=0, \ldots, T^rv=0, \ldots$. Therefore $\{ v, T(v), T^2(v), \ldots, T^r(v), \ldots\} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$ and so $Z(v;T)=V$. Thus $v$ is a cyclic vector for $T$.

:::

## Companion matrix {#Companion_matrix}

Let $T:V\rightarrow V$ be a linear transformation of a $n$-dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $F$ and $v$ be a cyclic vector for $T$. Then the vectors

$$B=\{v_1=v, v_2=Tv, v_3=T^2v, \ldots v_n = T^{n-1}v\}$$

form an ordered basis for $V$. Let the characteristic polynomial for $T$ be

$$p(x)=c_0+c_1x+c_2x^2+\cdots + c_{n-1}x^{n-1}+x^n.$$

Then

$$\begin{aligned} Tv_1 & = v_2\\ Tv_2 & = v_3\\ Tv_3 & = v_4\\ \vdots & \\ Tv_{n-1} & = v_n\\ Tv_n &= -c_0v_1 -c_1v_2 - \cdots c_{n-1}v_n \end{aligned}$$

Therefore, relative to the ordered basis $B$, the operator $T$ is represented by the matrix

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -c_0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & 0 & -c_1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \ldots & 0 & -c_2 \\ \vdots & & & & & \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \ldots & 1 & -c_{n-1} \end{bmatrix}$$

This matrix is called the *companion matrix* of the polynomial $p(x)$.[^o4]
