# Semisimple representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Semisimple representation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), a **semisimple representation** (also called a **completely reducible representation**) is a [linear representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_representation) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) or an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) that is a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of [simple representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_representation) (also called [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982)).[^o2] It is an example of the general mathematical notion of [semisimplicity](wikipedia://en/45256442).[^o3][^o4]

:::

Many representations that appear in applications of representation theory are semisimple or can be approximated by semisimple representations. A [semisimple module](wikipedia://en/1001361) over an algebra over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is an example of a semisimple representation. [Conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), a semisimple representation of a group *G* over a field *k* is a semisimple module over the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) *k*\[*G*\].

## Equivalent characterizations {#Equivalent_characterizations}

Let *V* be a representation of a group *G*; or more generally, let *V* be a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with a set of linear [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) acting on it. In general, a vector space acted on by a set of linear endomorphisms is said to be [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_object) (or irreducible) if the only invariant subspaces for those operators are zero and the vector space itself; a semisimple representation then is a direct sum of simple representations in that sense.[^o2]

The following are equivalent:[^o6]

1. *V* is semisimple as a representation.
1. *V* is a sum of simple [subrepresentations](wikipedia://en/55943952).
1. Each subrepresentation *W* of *V* admits a [complementary representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_representation): a subrepresentation *W'* such that $V = W \oplus W'$.

The equivalence of the above conditions can be [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) based on the following [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634), which is of independent interest:

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=math-theorem name="Lemma"}

**Lemma[^o7].**

Let *p*:*V* → *W* be a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [equivariant map](wikipedia://en/19378200#Equivariant_maps_and_isomorphisms) between representations. If *V* is semisimple, then *p* [splits](wikipedia://en/138378); i.e., it admits a [section](wikipedia://en/10630303).

:::

*Proof of the lemma*: Write $V = \bigoplus_{i\in I} V_i$ where $V_i$ are simple representations. [Without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000), we can assume $V_i$ are subrepresentations; i.e., we can assume the direct sum is internal. Now, consider the family of all possible direct sums $V_J := \bigoplus_{i \in J} V_i \subset V$ with various subsets $J \subset I$. Put the [partial ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) on it by saying the direct sum over *K* is less than the direct sum over *J* if $K \subset J$. By [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442), we can find a maximal $J \subset I$ such that $\operatorname{ker}p \cap V_J = 0$. We claim that $V=\operatorname{ker}p\oplus V_J$. By definition, $\operatorname{ker}p\cap V_J=0$ so we only need to show that $V=\operatorname{ker}p+V_J$. If $\operatorname{ker}p+V_J$ is a proper subrepresentation of $V$ then there exists $k\in I - J$ such that $V_k\not\subset \operatorname{ker}p+V_J$. Since $V_k$ is simple (irreducible), $V_k\cap(\operatorname{ker}p+V_J)=0$. This contradicts the maximality of $J$, so $V=\operatorname{ker}p\oplus V_J$ as claimed. Hence, $W \simeq V/\operatorname{ker}p\simeq V_J \to V$ is a section of *p*. $\square$

Note that we cannot take $J$ to the set of $i$ such that $\ker(p) \cap V_i = 0$. The reason is that it can happen, and frequently does, that $X$ is a subspace of $Y\oplus Z$ and yet $X\cap Y = 0 = X\cap Z$. For example, take $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ to be three distinct lines through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$. For an explicit counterexample, let $A = \operatorname{Mat}_2 F$ be the algebra of 2-by-2 [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) and set $V=A$, the regular representation of $A$. Set $V_1=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\b&0\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}$ and $V_2=\Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\0&d\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}$ and set $W = \Bigl\{\begin{pmatrix}c&c\\d&d\end{pmatrix}\Bigr\}$. Then $V_1$, $V_2$ and $W$ are all [irreducible $A$-modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_module) and $V = V_1\oplus V_2$. Let $p: V\to V/W$ be the natural surjection. Then $\operatorname{ker}p = W \ne 0$ and $V_1 \cap \operatorname{ker}p = 0 = V_2 \cap \operatorname{ker}p$. In this case, $W\simeq V_1\simeq V_2$ but $V \ne \operatorname{ker}p \oplus V_1\oplus V_2$ because this sum is not direct.

*Proof of equivalences*[^o9] $1. \Rightarrow 3.$: Take *p* to be the natural surjection $V \to V/W$. Since *V* is semisimple, *p* splits and so, through a section, $V/W$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to a subrepresentation that is complementary to *W*.

$3. \Rightarrow 2.$: We shall first observe that every nonzero subrepresentation *W* has a simple subrepresentation. Shrinking *W* to a (nonzero) [cyclic subrepresentation](wikipedia://en/4128720) we can assume it is finitely generated. Then it has a [maximal subrepresentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_submodule) *U*. By the condition 3., $V = U \oplus U'$ for some $U'$. By modular law, it implies $W = U \oplus (W \cap U')$. Then $(W \cap U') \simeq W/U$ is a simple subrepresentation of *W* ("simple" because of maximality). This establishes the observation. Now, take $W$ to be the sum of all simple subrepresentations, which, by 3., admits a complementary representation $W'$. If $W' \ne 0$, then, by the early observation, $W'$ contains a simple subrepresentation and so $W \cap W' \ne 0$, a nonsense. Hence, $W' = 0$.

$2. \Rightarrow 1.$:[^o10] The implication is a direct generalization of a basic fact in linear algebra that a basis can be extracted from a spanning set of a vector space. That is we can prove the following slightly more precise statement:

- When $V = \sum_{i \in I} V_i$ is a sum of simple subrepresentations, a semisimple decomposition $V = \bigoplus_{i\in I'} V_i$, some subset $I' \subset I$, can be extracted from the sum.

As in the proof of the lemma, we can find a maximal direct sum $W$ that consists of some $V_i$'s. Now, for each *i* in *I*, by simplicity, either $V_i \subset W$ or $V_i \cap W = 0$. In the second case, the direct sum $W \oplus V_i$ is a contradiction to the maximality of *W*. Hence, $V_i \subset W$. $\square$

## Examples and non-examples {#Examples_and_non-examples}

### Unitary representations {#Unitary_representations}

A finite-dimensional [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788) (i.e., a representation factoring through a [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993)) is a basic example of a semisimple representation. Such a representation is semisimple since if *W* is a subrepresentation, then the orthogonal complement to *W* is a complementary representation[^o13] because if $v \in W^{\bot}$ and $g \in G$, then $\langle \pi(g) v, w \rangle = \langle v, \pi(g^{-1}) w \rangle = 0$ for any *w* in *W* since *W* is *G*-invariant, and so $\pi(g) v \in W^{\bot}$.

For example, given a continuous finite-dimensional [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) representation $\pi: G \to GL(V)$ of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) or a [compact group](wikipedia://en/750326) *G*, by the averaging argument, one can define an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) $\langle\, , \rangle$ on *V* that is *G*-invariant: i.e., $\langle \pi(g) v, \pi(g) w \rangle = \langle v, w \rangle$, which is to say $\pi(g)$ is a unitary operator and so $\pi$ is a unitary representation.[^o13] Hence, every finite-dimensional continuous complex representation of *G* is semisimple.[^o14] For a finite group, this is a special case of [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007), which says a finite-dimensional representation of a finite group *G* over a field *k* with [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) not dividing the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) of *G* is semisimple.[^n1][^n2]

### Representations of semisimple Lie algebras {#Representations_of_semisimple_Lie_algebras}

By [Weyl's theorem on complete reducibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl's_theorem_on_complete_reducibility), every finite-dimensional representation of a [semisimple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1337587) over a field of characteristic zero is semisimple.[^o18]

### Separable minimal polynomials {#Separable_minimal_polynomials}

Given a linear endomorphism *T* of a vector space *V*, *V* is semisimple as a representation of *T* (i.e., *T* is a [semisimple operator](wikipedia://en/7252030)) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the minimal polynomial of *T* is separable; i.e., a product of distinct irreducible polynomials.[^o20]

### Associated semisimple representation {#Associated_semisimple_representation}

Given a finite-dimensional representation *V*, the [Jordan–Hölder theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Hölder_theorem) says there is a filtration by subrepresentations: $V = V_0 \supset V_1 \supset \cdots \supset V_n = 0$ such that each successive quotient $V_i/V_{i+1}$ is a simple representation. Then the associated vector space $\operatorname{gr} V := \bigoplus_{i = 0}^{n - 1}V_i/V_{i+1}$ is a semisimple representation called an **associated semisimple representation**, which, [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an isomorphism, is uniquely determined by *V*.[^o22]

### Unipotent group non-example {#Unipotent_group_non-example}

A representation of a [unipotent group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unipotent_group) is generally not semisimple. Take $G$ to be the group consisting of [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) matrices $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & a \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$; it acts on $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ in a natural way and makes *V* a representation of *G*. If *W* is a subrepresentation of *V* that has dimension 1, then a simple calculation shows that it must be spanned by the vector $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. That is, there are exactly three *G*-subrepresentations of *V*; in particular, *V* is not semisimple (as a unique one-dimensional subrepresentation does not admit a complementary representation).[^o24]

## Semisimple decomposition and multiplicity {#Semisimple_decomposition_and_multiplicity}

The decomposition of a semisimple representation into simple ones, called a semisimple decomposition, need not be unique; for example, for a trivial representation, simple representations are one-dimensional vector spaces and thus a semisimple decomposition amounts to a choice of a basis of the representation vector space.[^o27] The isotypic decomposition, on the other hand, is an example of a unique decomposition.[^o33]

However, for a finite-dimensional semisimple representation *V* over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018), the numbers of simple representations up to isomorphism appearing in the decomposition of *V* (1) are unique and (2) completely determine the representation up to isomorphism;[^o28] this is a consequence of [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696) in the following way. Suppose a finite-dimensional semisimple representation *V* over an algebraically closed field is given: by definition, it is a direct sum of simple representations. By grouping together simple representations in the decomposition that are isomorphic to each other, up to an isomorphism, one finds a decomposition (not necessarily unique):[^o28]

$$V \simeq \bigoplus_i V_i^{\oplus m_i}$$

where $V_i$ are simple representations, mutually non-isomorphic to one another, and $m_i$ are positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). By Schur's lemma,

$$m_i = \dim \operatorname{Hom}_{\text{equiv}}(V_i, V) = \dim \operatorname{Hom}_{\text{equiv}}(V, V_i),$$

where $\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{equiv}}$ refers to the [equivariant linear maps](wikipedia://en/422711). Also, each $m_i$ is unchanged if $V_i$ is replaced by another simple representation isomorphic to $V_i$. Thus, the integers $m_i$ are independent of chosen decompositions; they are the *multiplicities* of simple representations $V_i$, up to isomorphism, in *V*.[^o29]

In general, given a finite-dimensional representation $\pi: G \to GL(V)$ of a group *G* over a field *k*, the composition $\chi_V : G \,\overset{\pi}{\to}\, GL(V) \,\overset{\operatorname{tr}}{\to}\, k$ is called the [character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_of_a_representation) of $(\pi, V)$.[^o30] When $(\pi, V)$ is semisimple with the decomposition $V \simeq \bigoplus_i V_i^{\oplus m_i}$ as above, the trace $\operatorname{tr}(\pi(g))$ is the sum of the traces of $\pi(g) : V_i \to V_i$ with multiplicities and thus, as functions on *G*,

$$\chi_V = \sum_i m_i \chi_{V_i}$$

where $\chi_{V_i}$ are the characters of $V_i$. When *G* is a finite group or more generally a compact group and $V$ is a unitary representation with the inner product given by the averaging argument, the [Schur orthogonality relations](wikipedia://en/7947411) say:[^o31] the irreducible characters (characters of simple representations) of *G* are an orthonormal subset of the space of complex-valued functions on *G* and thus $m_i = \langle \chi_V, \chi_{V_i} \rangle$.

:::

## Isotypic decomposition {#Isotypic_decomposition}

There is a decomposition of a semisimple representation that is unique, called *the* isotypic decomposition of the representation. By definition, given a simple representation *S*, the isotypic component of type *S* of a representation *V* is the sum of all subrepresentations of *V* that are isomorphic to *S*;[^o33] note the component is also isomorphic to the direct sum of some choice of subrepresentations isomorphic to *S* (so the component is unique, while the summands are not necessary so).

Then the isotypic decomposition of a semisimple representation *V* is the (unique) direct sum decomposition:[^o33][^o34]

$$V = \bigoplus_{\lambda \in \widehat{G}} V^{\lambda}$$

where $\widehat{G}$ is the set of isomorphism classes of simple representations of *G* and $V^{\lambda}$ is the isotypic component of *V* of type *S* for some $S \in \lambda$.

### Isotypic component {#Isotypic_component}

The **isotypic component** of [weight](wikipedia://en/277179) $\lambda$ of a [Lie algebra module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra_module) is the sum of all submodules which are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the highest weight module with weight $\lambda$.

::: {#o37 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

#### Definition {#Definition}

- A finite-dimensional [module](wikipedia://en/276410) $V$ of a [reductive Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/863720) $\mathfrak{g}$ (or of the corresponding [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945)) can be decomposed into [irreducible submodules](wikipedia://en/385982)

$$V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^N V_i.$$

- Each finite-dimensional irreducible representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is uniquely identified (up to isomorphism) by its [highest weight](wikipedia://en/277179#Highest_weight)

$\forall i \in \{1,\ldots,N\} \,\exists \lambda \in P(\mathfrak{g}) : V_i \simeq M_\lambda$, where $M_\lambda$ denotes the [highest weight module](wikipedia://en/277179#Highest_weight_module) with highest weight $\lambda$.

- In the decomposition of $V$, a certain [isomorphism class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class) might appear more than once, hence

$$V \simeq \bigoplus_{\lambda \in P(\mathfrak{g})} (\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d_\lambda} M_{\lambda}).$$

This defines the isotypic component of weight $\lambda$ of $V$: $\lambda(V) := \bigoplus_{i=1}^{d_\lambda} V_i \simeq \mathbb{C}^{d_\lambda} \otimes M_{\lambda}$ where $d_\lambda$ is maximal.

:::

::: {#o39 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Let $V$ be the space of homogeneous degree-three polynomials over the complex numbers in variables $x_1,x_2,x_3$. Then $S_3$ acts on $V$ by permutation of the three variables. This is a finite-dimensional complex representation of a finite group, and so is semisimple. Therefore, this 10-dimensional representation can be broken up into three isotypic components, each corresponding to one of the three irreducible representations of $S_3$. In particular, $V$ contains three copies of the trivial representation, one copy of the sign representation, and three copies of the two-dimensional irreducible representation $W$ of $S_3$. For example, the span of $x_1^2x_2-x_2^2x_1 + x_1^2x_3-x_2^2x_3$ and $x_2^2x_3-x_3^2x_2 + x_2^2x_1-x_3^2x_1$ is isomorphic to $W$. This can more easily be seen by writing this two-dimensional subspace as

$$W_1=\{a(x_1^2x_2+x_1^2x_3)+b(x_2^2x_1+x_2^2x_3)+c(x_3^2x_1+x_3^2x_2)\mid a+b+c = 0\}.$$

Another copy of $W$ can be written in a similar form:

$$W_2=\{a(x_2^2x_1+x_3^2x_1)+b(x_1^2x_2+x_3^2x_2)+c(x_1^2x_3+x_2^2x_3)\mid a+b+c = 0\}.$$

So can the third:

$$W_3=\{ax_1^3+bx_2^3+cx_3^3\mid a+b+c = 0\}.$$

Then $W_1 \oplus W_2 \oplus W_3$ is the isotypic component of type $W$ in $V$.

:::

## Completion {#Completion}

In [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659), one decomposes a (nice) function as the *limit* of the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of the function. In much the same way, a representation itself may not be semisimple but it may be the completion (in a suitable sense) of a semisimple representation. The most basic case of this is the [Peter–Weyl theorem](wikipedia://en/294474), which decomposes the left (or right) [regular representation](wikipedia://en/360011) of a compact group into the Hilbert-space completion of the direct sum of all simple unitary representations. As a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865),[^o41] there is a natural decomposition for $W = L^2(G)$ = the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) of (classes of) square-integrable functions on a compact group *G*:

$$W \simeq \widehat{\bigoplus_{[(\pi, V)]}} V^{\oplus \dim V}$$

where $\widehat{\bigoplus}$ means the completion of the direct sum and the direct sum runs over all isomorphism classes of simple finite-dimensional unitary representations $(\pi, V)$ of *G*.To be precise, the theorem concerns the regular representation of $G \times G$ and the above statement is a corollary. Note here that every simple unitary representation (up to an isomorphism) appears in the sum with the multiplicity the dimension of the representation.

When the group *G* is a finite group, the vector space $W = \mathbb{C}[G]$ is simply the group algebra of *G* and also the completion is vacuous. Thus, the theorem simply says that

$$\mathbb{C}[G] = \bigoplus_{[(\pi, V)]} V^{\oplus \dim V}.$$

That is, each simple representation of *G* appears in the regular representation with multiplicity the dimension of the representation.[^o42] This is one of standard facts in the representation theory of a finite group (and is much easier to prove).

When the group *G* is the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) $S^1$, the theorem exactly amounts to the classical Fourier analysis.[^o43]

## Applications to physics {#Applications_to_physics}

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics), the [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) of an object can be described by [complex representations of the rotation group SO(3)](wikipedia://en/292786#An_example:_The_rotation_group_SO%283%29), all of which are semisimple.[^o45] Due to [connection between SO(3) and SU(2)](wikipedia://en/173965#Connection_between_SO%283%29_and_SU%282%29), the non-relativistic [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) of an [elementary particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) is described by [complex representations of SU(2)](wikipedia://en/1378619) and the relativistic spin is described by [complex representations of SL<sub>2</sub>(**C**)](wikipedia://en/2249718), all of which are semisimple.[^o45] In [angular momentum coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_momentum_coupling), [Clebsch–Gordan coefficients](wikipedia://en/1074990) arise from the multiplicities of irreducible representations occurring in the semisimple decomposition of a tensor product of irreducible representations.[^o46]
