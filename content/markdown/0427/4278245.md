# Deformation quantization

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Deformation quantization"}

In mathematics and physics, **deformation quantization** roughly amounts to finding a ([quantum](wikipedia://en/28993982)) algebra whose classical limit is a given (classical) algebra such as a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) or a [Poisson algebra](wikipedia://en/345704).

:::

## In physics {#In_physics}

Intuitively, a [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) of a mathematical object is a family of the same kind of objects that depend on some parameter(s). Here, it provides rules for how to deform the "classical" commutative algebra of [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) to a quantum non-commutative algebra of observables.

The basic setup in deformation theory is to start with an algebraic structure (say a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944)) and ask: Does there exist a one or more parameter(s) family of *similar* structures, such that for an initial value of the parameter(s) one has the same structure (Lie algebra) one started with? (The oldest illustration of this may be the realization of [Eratosthenes](wikipedia://en/46117) in the ancient world that a flat Earth was deformable to a spherical Earth, with deformation parameter 1/*R*<sub>⊕</sub>.) E.g., one may define a [noncommutative torus](wikipedia://en/295917) as a deformation quantization through a ★-product to implicitly address all convergence subtleties (usually not addressed in formal deformation quantization). Insofar as the algebra of functions on a space determines the geometry of that space, the study of the star product leads to the study of a [non-commutative geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_geometry) deformation of that space.

In the context of the above flat phase-space example, the star product ([Moyal product](wikipedia://en/1946702), actually introduced by [Groenewold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbrand_J._Groenewold) in 1946), ★<sub>*ħ*</sub>, of a pair of functions in $f_{1}, f_{2} \in C^{\infty}(\Re^{2})$, is specified by

$$\Phi [f_1 \star f_2] = \Phi [f_1]\Phi [f_2].\,$$

where $\Phi$ is the [Wigner–Weyl transform](wikipedia://en/4277086).

The star product is not commutative in general, but goes over to the ordinary commutative product of functions in the limit of $\text{ħ} \to 0$. As such, it is said to define a [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) of the commutative algebra of $C^{\infty}(\Re^{2})$.

For the Weyl-map example above, the ★-product may be written in terms of the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) as

$$f_1 \star f_2 = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac {1}{n!} \left(\frac{i\hbar}{2} \right)^n \Pi^n(f_1, f_2).$$

Here, Π is the [Poisson bivector](wikipedia://en/882902#The_Poisson_Bivector), an operator defined such that its powers are

$$\Pi^0(f_1,f_2)=f_1f_2$$

and

$$\Pi^1(f_1,f_2)=\{f_1,f_2\}= \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial q} \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial p} \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial q} ~,$$

where {*f*<sub>1</sub>, *f*<sub>2</sub>} is the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340). More generally,

$$\Pi^n(f_1,f_2)= \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k {n \choose k} \left( \frac{\partial^k }{\partial p^k} \frac{\partial^{n-k}}{\partial q^{n-k}} f_1 \right) \times \left( \frac{\partial^{n-k} }{\partial p^{n-k}} \frac{\partial^k}{\partial q^k} f_2 \right)$$

where ${n \choose k}$ is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668).

Thus, e.g.,[^o3] Gaussians compose [hyperbolically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function#Comparison_with_circular_trigonometric_functions),

$$\exp \left (-{a } (q^2+p^2)\right ) ~ \star ~ \exp \left (-{b} (q^2+p^2)\right ) = {1\over 1+\hbar^2 ab} \exp \left (-{a+b\over 1+\hbar^2 ab} (q^2+p^2)\right ) ,$$

or

$$\delta (q) ~ \star ~ \delta(p) = {2\over h} \exp \left (2i{qp\over\hbar}\right ) ,$$

etc. These formulas are predicated on coordinates in which the [Poisson bivector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_bivector) is constant (plain flat Poisson brackets). For the general formula on arbitrary [Poisson manifolds](wikipedia://en/882902), cf. the [Kontsevich quantization formula](wikipedia://en/26049975).

Antisymmetrization of this ★-product yields the [Moyal bracket](wikipedia://en/8875186), the proper quantum deformation of the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340), and the phase-space isomorph (Wigner transform) of the quantum [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) in the more usual Hilbert-space formulation of quantum mechanics. As such, it provides the cornerstone of the dynamical equations of observables in this phase-space formulation.

There results a complete [phase space formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space_formulation) of quantum mechanics, ***completely equivalent to the Hilbert-space operator representation***, with star-multiplications paralleling operator multiplications isomorphically.[^o3]

Expectation values in phase-space quantization are obtained isomorphically to tracing operator observables $\Phi$ with the density matrix in Hilbert space: they are obtained by phase-space integrals of observables such as the above $f$ with the [Wigner quasi-probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigner_quasi-probability_distribution) effectively serving as a measure.

Thus, by expressing quantum mechanics in phase space (the same ambit as for classical mechanics), the above Weyl map facilitates recognition of quantum mechanics as a [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) (generalization, cf. [correspondence principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_principle)) of classical mechanics, with deformation parameter $\text{ħ}/S$. (Other familiar deformations in physics involve the deformation of classical Newtonian into relativistic mechanics, with deformation parameter *v*/*c*; or the deformation of Newtonian gravity into General Relativity, with deformation parameter Schwarzschild-radius/characteristic-dimension. Conversely, [group contraction](wikipedia://en/34976899) leads to the vanishing-parameter undeformed theories—[classical limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit).)

Classical expressions, observables, and operations (such as Poisson brackets) are modified by $\text{ħ}$-dependent quantum corrections, as the conventional commutative multiplication applying in classical mechanics is generalized to the *noncommutative star-multiplication* characterizing quantum mechanics and underlying its uncertainty principle.
