# Total ring of fractions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Total ring of fractions"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **total quotient ring**[^n1] or **total ring of fractions**[^n2] is a construction that generalizes the notion of the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) to [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* that may have [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441). The construction [embeds](wikipedia://en/52119) *R* in a larger [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), giving every non-zero-divisor of *R* an inverse in the larger ring. If the [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from *R* to the new ring is to be [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), no further elements can be given an inverse.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and let $S$ be the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of elements that are not zero divisors in $R$; then $S$ is a [multiplicatively closed set](wikipedia://en/21653550). Hence we may [localize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) the ring $R$ at the set $S$ to obtain the total quotient ring $S^{-1}R=Q(R)$.

If $R$ is a [domain](wikipedia://en/15462), then $S = R-\{0\}$ and the total quotient ring is the same as the field of fractions. This justifies the notation $Q(R)$, which is sometimes used for the field of fractions as well, since there is no ambiguity in the case of a domain.

Since $S$ in the construction contains no zero divisors, the natural map $R \to Q(R)$ is injective, so the total quotient ring is an extension of $R$.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- For a [product ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_ring) *A* × *B*, the total quotient ring *Q*(*A* × *B*) is the product of total quotient rings *Q*(*A*) × *Q*(*B*). In particular, if *A* and *B* are integral domains, it is the product of quotient fields.
- For the ring of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) on an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) *D* of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the total quotient ring is the ring of [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) on *D*, even if *D* is not [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_set).
- In an [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273), all elements are [units](wikipedia://en/289666) or zero divisors. Hence the set of non-zero-divisors is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of units of the ring, $R^{\times}$, and so $Q(R) = (R^{\times})^{-1}R$. But since all these elements already have inverses, $Q(R) = R$.
- In a commutative [von Neumann regular](wikipedia://en/907524) ring *R*, the same thing happens. Suppose *a* in *R* is not a zero divisor. Then in a von Neumann regular ring *a* = *axa* for some *x* in *R*, giving the equation *a*(*xa* − 1) = 0. Since *a* is not a zero divisor, *xa* = 1, showing *a* is a unit. Here again, $Q(R) = R$.
- In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) one considers a [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466) of total quotient rings on a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754), and this may be used to give the definition of a [Cartier divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartier_divisor).

:::

## The total ring of fractions of a reduced ring {#The_total_ring_of_fractions_of_a_reduced_ring}

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=math-theorem name="Proposition"}

**Proposition.**

Let *A* be a [reduced ring](wikipedia://en/539749) that has only finitely many [minimal prime ideals](wikipedia://en/16143832), $\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_r$ (e.g., a [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) reduced ring). Then

$Q(A) \simeq \prod_{i=1}^r Q(A/\mathfrak{p}_i).$ Geometrically, $\operatorname{Spec}(Q(A))$ is the [Artinian scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artinian_scheme) consisting (as a finite set) of the generic points of the irreducible components of $\operatorname{Spec} (A)$.

:::

Proof: Every element of *Q*(*A*) is either a unit or a zero divisor. Thus, any [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_ideal) [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *I* of *Q*(*A*) is contained in the set of zero divisors of *Q*(*A*); that set equals the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the minimal prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}_i Q(A)$ since *Q*(*A*) is [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749). By [prime avoidance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_avoidance), *I* must be contained in some $\mathfrak{p}_i Q(A)$. Hence, the ideals $\mathfrak{p}_i Q(A)$ are [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) of *Q*(*A*). Also, their [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) is [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal). Thus, by the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) applied to *Q*(*A*),

$$Q(A) \simeq \prod_i Q(A)/\mathfrak{p}_i Q(A).$$

Let *S* be the [multiplicatively closed set](wikipedia://en/21653550) of non-zero-divisors of *A*. By [exactness](wikipedia://en/453945) of localization,

$$Q(A)/\mathfrak{p}_i Q(A) = A[S^{-1}] / \mathfrak{p}_i A[S^{-1}] = (A / \mathfrak{p}_i)[S^{-1}],$$

which is already a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and so must be $Q(A/\mathfrak{p}_i)$. $\square$

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

If $R$ is a commutative ring and $S$ is any [multiplicatively closed set](wikipedia://en/21653550) in $R$, the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) $S^{-1}R$ can still be constructed, but the [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from $R$ to $S^{-1}R$ might fail to be injective. For example, if $0 \in S$, then $S^{-1}R$ is the [trivial ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_ring).

:::
