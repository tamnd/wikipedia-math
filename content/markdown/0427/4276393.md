# Group with operators

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Group with operators"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **group with operators** or Ω-**group** is an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) that can be viewed as a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) together with a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) Ω that operates on the elements of the group in a special way.

:::

Groups with operators were extensively studied by [Emmy Noether](wikipedia://en/149896) and her school in the 1920s. She employed the concept in her original formulation of the three [Noether isomorphism theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether_isomorphism_theorems).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **group with operators** $(G, \Omega)$ can be defined[^n1] as a group $G = (G, \cdot)$ together with an action of a set $\Omega$ on $G$:

$$\Omega \times G \rightarrow G : (\omega, g) \mapsto g^\omega$$

that is [distributive](wikipedia://en/103118) relative to the group law:

$$(g \cdot h)^\omega = g^\omega \cdot h^\omega.$$

For each $\omega \in \Omega$, the map $g \mapsto g^\omega$ is then an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396#Types) of *G*. From this, it results that a Ω-group can also be viewed as a group *G* with an [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) $\left(u_\omega\right)_{\omega \in \Omega}$ of endomorphisms of *G*.

$\Omega$ is called the **operator domain**. The associate endomorphisms[^n2] are called the **homotheties** of *G*.

Given two groups *G*, *H* with same operator domain $\Omega$, a **homomorphism** of groups with operators from $(G, \Omega)$ to $(H, \Omega)$ is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $\phi: G \to H$ satisfying

$\phi\left(g^\omega\right) = (\phi(g))^\omega$ for all $\omega \in \Omega$ and $g \in G.$

A [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *S* of *G* is called a **stable subgroup**, **$\Omega$-subgroup** or **$\Omega$-invariant subgroup** if it respects the homotheties, that is

$s^\omega \in S$ for all $s \in S$ and $\omega \in \Omega.$

:::

## Category-theoretic remarks {#Category-theoretic_remarks}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a **group with operators** can be defined[^n3] as an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) of a [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) **Grp**<sup>*M*</sup> where *M* is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) (i.e. a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) with one object) and **Grp** denotes the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092). This definition is equivalent to the previous one, provided $\Omega$ is a monoid (if not, we may expand it to include the [identity](wikipedia://en/15069) and all [compositions](wikipedia://en/195947)).

A [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) in this category is a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) between two [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) (i.e., two groups with operators sharing same operator domain *M*). Again we recover the definition above of a homomorphism of groups with operators (with *f* the [component](wikipedia://en/45022#Definition) of the natural transformation).

A group with operators is also a mapping

$$\Omega \rightarrow \operatorname{End}_\mathbf{Grp}(G),$$

where $\operatorname{End}_\mathbf{Grp}(G)$ is the set of group endomorphisms of *G*.

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Given any group *G*, (*G*, ∅) is trivially a group with operators
- Given a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) *M* over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R*, *R* acts by [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) on the underlying [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) of *M*, so (*M*, *R*) is a group with operators.
- As a special case of the above, every [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* is a group with operators (*V*, *K*).

:::

::: {#o11 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The [Jordan–Hölder theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Hölder_theorem) also holds in the context of groups with operators. The requirement that a group have a [composition series](wikipedia://en/297493) is analogous to that of [compactness](wikipedia://en/6042) in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), and can sometimes be too strong a requirement. It is natural to talk about "compactness relative to a set", i.e. talk about composition series where each ([normal](wikipedia://en/21918)) subgroup is an operator-subgroup relative to the operator set *X*, of the group in question.

:::
