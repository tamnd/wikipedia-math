# Distributional data analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Distributional data analysis"}

**Distributional data analysis** is a branch of [nonparametric statistics](wikipedia://en/223369) that is related to [functional data analysis](wikipedia://en/2141368). It is concerned with random objects that are probability distributions, i.e., the statistical analysis of samples of random distributions where each atom of a sample is a distribution. One of the main challenges in distributional data analysis is that although the space of probability distributions is a convex space, it is not a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

Let $\nu$ be a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) on $D$, where $D \subset \mathbb{R}^p$ with $p \ge 1$. The probability measure $\nu$ can be equivalently characterized as [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) $F$ or [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) $f$ if it exists. For univariate distributions with $p = 1$, [quantile function](wikipedia://en/10167616) $Q=F^{-1}$ can also be used.

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a space of distributions $\nu$ and let $d$ be a metric on $\mathcal{F}$ so that $(\mathcal{F}, d)$ forms a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). There are various metrics available for $d$.[^o4] For example, suppose $\nu_1, \; \nu_2 \in \mathcal{F}$, and let $f_1$ and $f_2$ be the density functions of $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$, respectively. The Fisher-Rao metric is defined as

$$d_{FR}(f_1, f_2) = \arccos \left( \int_D \sqrt{f_1(x) f_2(x)} dx \right).$$

For univariate distributions, let $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ be the quantile functions of $\nu_1$ and $\nu_2$. Denote the $L^p$-Wasserstein space as $\mathcal{W}_p$, which is the space of distributions with finite $p$-th moments. Then, for $\nu_1, \; \nu_2 \in \mathcal{W}_p$, the $L^p$-[Wasserstein metric](wikipedia://en/6704603) is defined as

$$d_{W_p}(\nu_1, \nu_2) = \left( \int_0^1 [Q_1(s) - Q_2(s)]^p ds \right)^{1/p}.$$

:::

## Mean and variance {#Mean_and_variance}

For a probability measure $\nu \in \mathcal{F}$, consider a [random process](wikipedia://en/47895) $\mathfrak{F}$ such that $\nu \sim \mathfrak{F}$. One way to define mean and variance of $\nu$ is to introduce the [Fréchet mean](wikipedia://en/25021971) and the Fréchet variance. With respect to the metric $d$ on $\mathcal{F}$, the *Fréchet mean* $\mu_\oplus$, also known as the [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter), and the *Fréchet variance* $V_\oplus$ are defined as[^o6]

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_\oplus &= \operatorname{argmin}_{\mu \in \mathcal{F}} \mathbb{E}[d^2(\nu, \mu)], \\ V_\oplus &= \mathbb{E}[d^2(\nu, \mu_\oplus)]. \end{aligned}$$

A widely used example is the Wasserstein-Fréchet mean, or simply the *Wasserstein mean*, which is the Fréchet mean with the $L^2$-Wasserstein metric $d_{W_2}$.[^o7] For $\nu, \; \mu \in \mathcal{W}_2$, let $Q_\nu, \; Q_\mu$ be the quantile functions of $\nu$ and $\mu$, respectively. The Wasserstein mean and Wasserstein variance is defined as

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_\oplus^* &= \operatorname{argmin}_{\mu \in \mathcal{W}_2} \mathbb{E} \left[ \int_0^1 (Q_\nu (s) - Q_\mu (s))^2 ds \right], \\ V_\oplus^* &= \mathbb{E} \left[ \int_0^1 (Q_\nu (s) - Q_{\mu_\oplus^*} (s))^2 ds \right]. \end{aligned}$$

## Modes of variation {#Modes_of_variation}

[Modes of variation](wikipedia://en/63169268) are useful concepts in depicting the variation of data around the mean function. Based on the [Karhunen-Loève representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosambi-Karhunen-Loève_theorem), modes of variation show the contribution of each [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) to the mean.

### Functional principal component analysis {#Functional_principal_component_analysis}

[Functional principal component analysis](wikipedia://en/41204236) (FPCA) can be directly applied to the probability density functions.[^o10] Consider a distribution process $\nu \sim \mathfrak{F}$ and let $f$ be the density function of $\nu$. Let the mean density function as $\mu(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[f(t)\right]$ and the [covariance function](wikipedia://en/9793263) as $G(s,t) = \operatorname{Cov}(f(s), f(t))$ with orthonormal eigenfunctions $\{\phi_j\}_{j=1}^\infty$ and eigenvalues $\{\lambda_j\}_{j=1}^\infty$.

By the Karhunen-Loève theorem, $f(t) = \mu(t) + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \xi_j \phi_j(t)$, where principal components $\xi_j = \int_D [f(t) - \mu(t)] \phi_j(t) dt$. The $j$th mode of variation is defined as $g_{j}(t, \alpha) = \mu(t) + \alpha \sqrt{\lambda_j} \phi_j(t), \quad t \in D, \; \alpha \in [-A, A]$ with some constant $A$, such as 2 or 3.

### Transformation FPCA {#Transformation_FPCA}

Assume the probability density functions $f$ exist, and let $\mathcal{F}_f$ be the space of density functions. Transformation approaches introduce a continuous and invertible transformation $\Psi: \mathcal{F}_f \to \mathbb{H}$, where $\mathbb{H}$ is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) of functions. For instance, the log quantile density transformation or the centered log ratio transformation are popular choices.[^o12][^o13]

For $f \in \mathcal{F}_f$, let $Y = \Psi(f)$, the transformed functional variable. The mean function $\mu_Y(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[Y(t)\right]$ and the covariance function $G_Y(s,t) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y(s), Y(t))$ are defined accordingly, and let $\{\lambda_j, \phi_j\}_{j=1}^\infty$ be the eigenpairs of $G_Y(s,t)$. The Karhunen-Loève decomposition gives $Y(t) = \mu_Y(t) + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \xi_j \phi_j(t)$, where $\xi_j = \int_D [Y(t) - \mu_Y(t)] \phi_j(t) dt$. Then, the $j$th transformation mode of variation is defined as[^o14] $g_{j}^{TF}(t, \alpha) = \Psi^{-1} \left( \mu_Y + \alpha \sqrt{\lambda_j}\phi_j \right)(t), \quad t \in D, \; \alpha \in [-A, A].$

### Log FPCA and Wasserstein Geodesic PCA {#Log_FPCA_and_Wasserstein_Geodesic_PCA}

Endowed with metrics such as the Wasserstein metric $d_{W_2}$ or the Fisher-Rao metric $d_{FR}$, we can employ the (pseudo) Riemannian structure of $\mathcal{F}$. Denote the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at the Fréchet mean $\mu_\oplus$ as $T_{\mu_\oplus}$, and define the logarithm and exponential maps $\log_{\mu_\oplus}:\mathcal{F} \to T_{\mu_\oplus}$ and $\exp_{\mu_\oplus}: T_{\mu_\oplus} \to \mathcal{F}$. Let $Y$ be the projected density onto the tangent space, $Y = \log_{\mu_\oplus}(f)$.

In Log FPCA, FPCA is performed to $Y$ and then projected back to $\mathcal{F}$ using the exponential map.[^o16] Therefore, with $Y(t) = \mu_Y(t) + \sum_{j=1}^\infty \xi_j \phi_j(t)$, the $j$th Log FPCA mode of variation is defined as $g_j^{Log}(t, \alpha) = \exp_{f_\oplus} \left( \mu_{f_\oplus} + \alpha \sqrt{\lambda_j} \phi_j \right)(t), \quad t \in D, \; \alpha \in [-A, A].$

As a special case, consider $L^2$-Wasserstein space $\mathcal{W}_2$, a random distribution $\nu \in \mathcal{W}_2$, and a subset $G \subset \mathcal{W}_2$. Let $d_{W_2}(\nu, G) = \inf_{\mu \in G} d_{W_2}(\nu, \mu)$ and $K_{W_2}(G) = \mathbb{E}\left[d_{W_2}^2(\nu, G) \right]$. Let $\text{CL}(\mathcal{W}_2)$ be the metric space of nonempty, closed subsets of $\mathcal{W}_2$, endowed with [Hausdorff distance](wikipedia://en/429296), and define $\operatorname{CG}_{\nu_0, k}(\mathcal{W}_2) = \{G \in \operatorname{CL}(\mathcal{W}_2) : \nu_0 \in G, G \text{ is a geodesic set s.t. }\operatorname{dim}(G) \le k \}, \; k \ge 1.$ Let the reference measure $\nu_0$ be the Wasserstein mean $\mu_\oplus$. Then, a *principal geodesic subspace (PGS)* of dimension $k$ with respect to $\mu_\oplus$ is a set $G_k = \operatorname{argmin}_{G \in \text{CG}_{\nu_\oplus, k}(\mathcal{W}_2)} K_{W_2}(G)$.[^o17][^o18]

Note that the tangent space $T_{\mu_\oplus}$ is a subspace of $L^2_{\mu_\oplus}$, the Hilbert space of ${\mu_\oplus}$-square-integrable functions. Obtaining the PGS is equivalent to performing PCA in $L^2_{\mu_\oplus}$ under constraints to lie in the convex and closed subset.[^o18] Therefore, a simple approximation of the Wasserstein Geodesic PCA is the Log FPCA by relaxing the geodesicity constraint, while alternative techniques are suggested.[^o17][^o18]

## Distributional regression {#Distributional_regression}

### Fréchet regression {#Fréchet_regression}

Fréchet regression is a generalization of regression with responses taking values in a metric space and Euclidean predictors.[^o21][^o22] Using the Wasserstein metric $d_{W_2}$, Fréchet regression models can be applied to distributional objects. The global Wasserstein-Fréchet regression model is defined as

::: {#o23 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{aligned} m_\oplus (x) &= \operatorname{argmin}_{\omega \in \mathcal{F}} \mathbb{E}\left[ s_G(X,x) d_{W_2}^2(\nu,\omega) \right], \\ s_G(X,x)& = 1 + (X - \mathbb{E}[X])^\top \text{Var}(X)^{-1} (x - \mathbb{E}[X]), \end{aligned} \tag{1}$$

:::

which generalizes the standard linear regression.

For the local Wasserstein-Fréchet regression, consider a scalar predictor $X\in \mathbb{R}$ and introduce a smoothing kernel $K_h(\cdot) = h^{-1}K(\cdot/h)$. The local Fréchet regression model, which generalizes the local linear regression model, is defined as

$$\begin{aligned} l_\oplus (x) &= \operatorname{argmin}_{\omega \in \mathcal{F}} \mathbb{E}\left[ s_L(X,x,h) d_{W_2}^2(\nu,\omega) \right],\\ s_L(X,x,h) &= \sigma_0^{-2} \{ K_h(X-x)[\mu_2 - \mu_1 (X-x)]\}, \end{aligned}$$

where $\mu_j = \mathbb{E} \left[K_h(X-x)(X-x)^j \right]$, $j = 0,1,2,$ and $\sigma_0^2 = \mu_0 \mu_2 - \mu_1^2$.

### Transformation based approaches {#Transformation_based_approaches}

Consider the response variable $\nu$ to be probability distributions. With the space of density functions $\mathcal{F}_f$ and a Hilbert space of functions $\mathbb{H}$, consider continuous and invertible transformations $\Psi: \mathcal{F}_f \to \mathbb{H}$. Examples of transformations include log hazard transformation, log quantile density transformation, or centered log-ratio transformation. Linear methods such as [functional linear models](wikipedia://en/53439194#Functional_linear_models_%28FLMs%29) are applied to the transformed variables. The fitted models are interpreted back in the original density space $\mathcal{F}$ using the inverse transformation.[^o22]

### Random object approaches {#Random_object_approaches}

In Wasserstein regression, both predictors $\omega$ and responses $\nu$ can be distributional objects. Let $\omega{\oplus}$ and $\nu_{\oplus}$ be the Wasserstein mean of $\omega$ and $\nu$, respectively. The Wasserstein regression model is defined as

$$\mathbb{E}(\log_{\nu_{\oplus}} \nu | \log_{\omega{\oplus}} \omega) = \Gamma(\log_{\omega{\oplus}} \omega),$$

with a linear regression operator

$$\Gamma g(t) = \langle \beta(\cdot, t),g \rangle_{\omega{\oplus}}, \; t \in D, \; g \in T_{\omega{\oplus}}, \; \beta:D^2 \to \mathbb{R}.$$

Estimation of the regression operator is based on empirical estimators obtained from samples.[^o26] Also, the Fisher-Rao metric $d_{FR}$ can be used in a similar fashion.[^o22][^o27]

## Hypothesis testing {#Hypothesis_testing}

### Wasserstein F-test {#Wasserstein_F-test}

Wasserstein $F$-test has been proposed to test for the effects of the predictors in the Fréchet regression framework with the Wasserstein metric.[^o31] Consider Euclidean predictors $X \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and distributional responses $\nu \in \mathcal{W}_2$. Denote the Wasserstein mean of $\nu$ as $\mu_\oplus^*$, and the sample Wasserstein mean as $\hat{\mu}_\oplus^*$. Consider the global Wasserstein-Fréchet regression model $m_\oplus (x)$ defined in ((1)), which is the conditional Wasserstein mean given $X=x$. The estimator of $m_\oplus (x)$, $\hat{m}_\oplus (x)$ is obtained by minimizing the empirical version of the criterion.

Let $F$, $Q$, $f$, $F_\oplus^*$, $Q_\oplus^*$, $f_\oplus^*$, $F_\oplus(x)$, $Q_\oplus(x)$, and $f_\oplus(x)$ denote the cumulative distribution, quantile, and density functions of $\nu$, $\mu_\oplus^*$, and $m_\oplus(x)$, respectively. For a pair $(X, \nu)$, define $T = Q \circ F_\oplus (X)$ be the optimal transport map from $m_\oplus(X)$ to $\nu$. Also, define $S = Q_\oplus (X) \circ F_\oplus^*$, the optimal transport map from $\mu_\oplus^*$ to $m_\oplus(x)$. Finally, define the covariance kernel $K(u, v) = \mathbb{E}[\text{Cov}((T\circ S)(u), (T\circ S)(v) )]$ and by the Mercer decomposition, $K(u, v) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j \phi_j(u) \phi_j(v)$.

If there are no regression effects, the conditional Wasserstein mean would equal the Wasserstein mean. That is, hypotheses for the test of no effects are

$$H_0: m_\oplus (x) \equiv \mu_\oplus^* \quad \text{v.s.} \quad H_1: \text{Not }H_0.$$

To test for these hypotheses, the proposed global Wasserstein $F$-statistic and its [asymptotic distribution](wikipedia://en/3001287) are

$$F_G = \sum_{i=1}^n d_{W_2}^2( \hat{m}_\oplus (x), \hat{\mu}_\oplus^*), \quad F_G|X_1, \cdots, X_n \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} \sum_{j=1}^\infty \lambda_j V_j \; a.s.,$$

where $V_j \overset{iid}{\sim} \chi_p^2$.[^o31] An extension to hypothesis testing for partial regression effects, and alternative testing approximations using the [Satterthwaite's approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch-Satterthwaite_equation) or a [bootstrap](wikipedia://en/6885770) approach are proposed.[^o31]

### Tests for the intrinsic mean {#Tests_for_the_intrinsic_mean}

The Hilbert sphere $\mathcal{S}^\infty$ is defined as $\mathcal{S}^\infty = \left\{f \in \mathbb{H} : \| f \|_{\mathbb{H}}=1 \right\}$, where $\mathbb{H}$ is a separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space with inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\mathbb{H}}$ and norm $\| \cdot \|_{\mathbb{H}}$. Consider the space of square root densities $\mathcal{X} = \left\{ x:D \to \mathbb{R}: x = \sqrt{f}, \int_D f(t)dt = 1 \right\}$. Then with the Fisher-Rao metric $d_{FR}$ on $f$, $\mathcal{X}$ is the positive orthant of the Hilbert sphere $\mathcal{S}^\infty$ with $\mathbb{H} = L^2(D)$.

Let a chart $\tau: U \subset \mathcal{S}^\infty \to \mathbb{G}$ as a smooth [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) that maps $U$ onto an open subset $\tau(U)$ of a separable Hilbert space $\mathbb{G}$ for coordinates. For example, $\tau$ can be the logarithm map.[^o27]

Consider a random element $x = \sqrt{f} \in \mathcal{X}$ equipped with the Fisher-Rao metric, and write its Fréchet mean as $\mu$. Let the empirical estimator of $\mu$ using $n$ samples as $\hat{\mu}$. Then [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406) for $\hat{\mu}_\tau = \tau(\hat{\mu})$ and $\mu_\tau = \tau(\mu)$ holds: $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mu}_\tau - \mu_\tau ) \overset{L}{\longrightarrow} Z, \; n \to \infty$, where $Z$ is a Gaussian random element in $\mathbb{G}$ with mean 0 and covariance operator $\mathcal{T}$. Let the eigenvalue-eigenfunction pairs of $\mathcal{T}$ and the estimated covariance operator $\hat{\mathcal{T}}$ as $(\lambda_k, \phi_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ and $(\hat\lambda_k, \hat\phi_k)_{k=1}^\infty$, respectively.

Consider one-sample hypothesis testing

$$H_0: \mu = \mu_0 \quad \text{v.s.} \quad H_1: \mu \neq \mu_0,$$

with $\mu_0 \in \mathcal{S}^\infty$. Denote $\| \cdot \|_{\mathbb{G}}$ and $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\mathbb{G}}$ as the norm and inner product in $\mathbb{G}$. The test statistics and their limiting distributions are

$$\begin{aligned} T_1 &= n \| \tau(\hat{\mu}) - \tau(\mu_0)\|_\mathbb{G}^2 \overset{L}{\longrightarrow} \lambda_k W_k, \\ S_1 &= n \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{\langle \tau(\hat{\mu}) - \tau(\mu_0), \hat{\phi}_k \rangle_\mathbb{G}^2}{\hat{\lambda}_k} \overset{L}{\longrightarrow} \chi_K^2, \end{aligned}$$

where $W_k \overset{iid}{\sim} \chi_1^2$. The actual testing procedure can be done by employing the limiting distributions with Monte Carlo simulations, or bootstrap tests are possible. An extension to the two-sample test and paired test are also proposed.[^o27]

:::

## Distributional time series {#Distributional_time_series}

[Autoregressive (AR) models](wikipedia://en/1434444) for distributional time series are constructed by defining [stationarity](wikipedia://en/329898) and utilizing the notion of difference between distributions using $d_{W_2}$ and $d_{FR}$.

In Wasserstein autoregressive model (WAR), consider a stationary density time series $f_t$ with Wasserstein mean $f_\oplus$.[^o34] Denote the difference between $f_t$ and $f_\oplus$ using the logarithm map, $f_t \ominus f_{\oplus} = \log_{f_\oplus} f_t = T_t - \text{id}$, where $T_t = Q_t \circ F_\oplus$ is the optimal transport from $f_\oplus$ to $f_t$ in which $F_t$ and $F_{\oplus}$ are the cdf of $f_t$ and $f_{\oplus}$. An $AR(1)$ model on the tangent space $T_{f_\oplus}$ is defined as $V_t = \beta V_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \; t \in \mathbb{Z},$ for $V_t \in T_{f_\oplus}$ with the autoregressive parameter $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ and mean zero random i.i.d. innovations $\epsilon_t$. Under proper conditions, $\mu_t = \exp_{f_\oplus}(V_t)$ with densities $f_t$ and $V_t = \log_{f_\oplus}(\mu_t)$. Accordingly, $WAR(1)$, with a natural extension to order $p$, is defined as

$$T_t - \text{id} = \beta (T_{t-1} - \text{id} ) + \epsilon_t.$$

On the other hand, the spherical autoregressive model (SAR) considers the Fisher-Rao metric.[^o35] Following the settings of ##Tests for the intrinsic mean, let $x_t \in \mathcal{X}$ with Fréchet mean $\mu_x$. Let $\theta = \arccos(\langle x_t, \mu_x \rangle )$, which is the geodesic distance between $x_t$ and $\mu_x$. Define a rotation operator $Q_{x_t, \mu_x}$ that rotates $x_t$ to $\mu_x$. The spherical difference between $x_t$ and $\mu_x$ is represented as $R_t = x_t \ominus \mu_x = \theta Q_{x_t, \mu_x}$. Assume that $R_t$ is a stationary sequence with the Fréchet mean $\mu_R$, then $SAR(1)$ is defined as

$$R_t - \mu_R = \beta (R_{t-1} - \mu_R) + \epsilon_t,$$

where $\mu_R = \mathbb{E}R_t$ and mean zero random i.i.d innovations $\epsilon_t$. An alternative model, the differenced based spherical autoregressive (DSAR) model is defined with $R_t = x_{t+1} \ominus x_t$, with natural extensions to order $p$. A similar extension to the Wasserstein space was introduced.[^o36]
