# Snub disphenoid

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Snub disphenoid"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **snub disphenoid** is a [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) with 12 [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285) as its [faces](wikipedia://en/149926). It is an example of [deltahedron](wikipedia://en/493995) and [Johnson solid](wikipedia://en/16034). It can be constructed in different approaches. This shape is also called **Siamese dodecahedron**, **triangular dodecahedron**, **trigonal dodecahedron**, or **dodecadeltahedron**.

:::

The snub disphenoid can be visualized as an [atom cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom_cluster) surrounding a central atom, that is, the [dodecahedral molecular geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodecahedral_molecular_geometry). Its [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) may be [placed on a sphere](wikipedia://en/64419430) and can also be used as a minimum possible [Lennard-Jones potential](wikipedia://en/227686) among all eight-sphere clusters.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

The snub disphenoid can be constructed in different ways. As suggested by the name, the snub disphenoid is constructed from a [tetragonal disphenoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragonal_disphenoid) by cutting all the edges from its faces, and adding [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285) (the light blue colors in the following image) that are twisted in a certain angle between them. This process construction is known as [snubification](wikipedia://en/9428917).

The snub disphenoid may also be constructed from a triangular bipyramid, by cutting its two edges along the apices. These apices can be pushed toward each other, resulting in the new two vertices pushed away. Alternatively, the snub disphenoid can be constructed from [pentagonal bipyramid](wikipedia://en/728038) by cutting the two edges along that connecting the base of the bipyramid and then inserting two equilateral triangles between them. Another way to construct the snub disphenoid starts from the [square antiprism](wikipedia://en/2860045), by replacing the two square faces with pairs of equilateral triangles. Another construction of the *snub disphenoid* is as a digonal [gyrobianticupola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyrobianticupola). It has the same topology and symmetry, but without equilateral triangles. It has 4 vertices in a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) on a center plane as two [anticupolae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticupola) attached with rotational symmetry.

A physical model of the snub disphenoid can be formed by folding a [net](wikipedia://en/1148129) formed by 12 equilateral triangles (a [12-iamond](wikipedia://en/185271)), as shown. An alternative net suggested by [John Montroll](wikipedia://en/1572530) has fewer concave vertices on its boundary, making it more convenient for [origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origami) construction. It can also be constructed like the [snub square antiprism](wikipedia://en/1133918), but with a [digon](wikipedia://en/5442728) instead,

The eight vertices of the snub disphenoid may then be given [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate):

$$\begin{aligned} (\pm t, r, 0), &\qquad (0, -r, \pm t), \\ (\pm 1, -s, 0), &\qquad (0, s, \pm 1). \end{aligned}$$

Here, $q \approx 0.16902$ is the positive real [solution of the cubic polynomial](wikipedia://en/11153189) $2x^3 +11x^2+4x-1$. The three variables $r$, $s$, and $t$ is the expression of:

$$r = \sqrt{q} \approx 0.41112, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1-q}{2q}} \approx 1.56786, \qquad t = 2rs = \sqrt{2-2q} \approx 1.28917.$$

Because this construction involves the solution to a cubic equation, the snub disphenoid cannot be [constructed with a compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass-and-straightedge_construction), unlike the other seven deltahedra.

## Properties {#Properties}

As a consequence of such constructions, the snub disphenoid has 12 equilateral triangles. A [deltahedron](wikipedia://en/493995) is a polyhedron in which all faces are equilateral triangles. There are eight convex deltahedra, one of which is the snub disphenoid. More generally, the [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) polyhedra in which all faces are [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) are the [Johnson solids](wikipedia://en/16034), and every convex deltahedron is a Johnson solid. The snub disphenoid is among them, enumerated as the 84th Johnson solid $J_{84}$. It is an [elementary polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_polyhedron), meaning that it cannot produce convex regular-faced polyhedra again by slicing on its edges with a plane. Its dual polyhedron is the [elongated gyrobifastigium](wikipedia://en/53773496).

### Measurement {#Measurement}

A snub disphenoid with edge length $a$ has a surface area, obtained by the area of 12 equilateral triangles in total:

$$A = 3 \sqrt{3}a^2 \approx 5.19615a^2,$$

Its volume can be calculated as the formula:

$$V \approx 0.85949 a^3.$$

The snub disphenoid has 18 edges, all of which are triangle-to-triangle, forming three different [dihedral angles](wikipedia://en/338046), an angle formed by two polygonal faces. In approximation, four edges form an angle of 164.2°, eight form 121.74°, and six form 96.2°.

### Symmetry and geodesic {#Symmetry_and_geodesic}

*Image: J84 snub disphenoid.stl.* 3D model of a snub disphenoid

The snub disphenoid has the same symmetries as a [tetragonal disphenoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetragonal_disphenoid), the [antiprismatic symmetry](wikipedia://en/2922089) $D_{2 \mathrm{d}}$ of order 8: it has an axis of 180° rotational symmetry through the midpoints of its two opposite edges, two perpendicular planes of [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873) through this axis, and four additional symmetry operations given by a reflection perpendicular to the axis followed by a quarter-turn and possibly another reflection parallel to the axis..

Up to symmetries and parallel translation, the snub disphenoid has five types of simple (non-self-crossing) [closed geodesics](wikipedia://en/19903176). These are paths on the surface of the polyhedron that avoid the vertices and locally look like the shortest path: they follow straight line segments across each face of the polyhedron that they intersect, and when they cross an edge of the polyhedron, they make complementary angles on the two incident faces to the edge. Intuitively, one could stretch a rubber band around the polyhedron along this path, and it would stay in place: there is no way to locally change the path and make it shorter. For example, one type of geodesic crosses the two opposite edges of the snub disphenoid at their midpoints (where the symmetry axis exits the polytope) at an angle of $\pi/3$. A second type of geodesic passes near the intersection of the snub disphenoid with the plane that perpendicularly bisects the symmetry axis (the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) of the polyhedron), crossing the edges of eight triangles at angles that alternate between $\pi/2$ and $\pi/6$. Shifting a geodesic on the surface of the polyhedron by a small amount (small enough that the shift does not cause it to cross any vertices) preserves the property of being a geodesic and preserves its length, so both of these examples have shifted versions of the same type that are less symmetrically placed. The lengths of the five simple closed geodesics on a snub disphenoid with unit-length edges are

$2\sqrt{3}\approx 3.464$ (for the equatorial geodesic), $\sqrt{13}\approx 3.606$, $4$ (for the geodesic through the midpoints of opposite edges), $2\sqrt{7}\approx 5.292$, and $\sqrt{19}\approx 4.359$.

Except for the tetrahedron, which has infinitely many types of simple closed geodesics, the snub disphenoid has the most types of geodesics of any deltahedron.

### Representation by the graph {#Representation_by_the_graph}

The snub disphenoid is [4-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph), meaning that it takes the removal of four vertices to disconnect the remaining vertices. It is one of only four 4-connected [simplicial](wikipedia://en/8853373) [well-covered](wikipedia://en/32696387) polyhedra, meaning that all of the [maximal independent sets](wikipedia://en/1793590) of its vertices have the same size. The other three polyhedra with this property are the [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458), the [pentagonal bipyramid](wikipedia://en/728038), and an irregular polyhedron with 12 vertices and 20 triangular faces.

## Applications {#Applications}

In the study of [computational chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_chemistry) and [molecular physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_physics), spheres centered at the vertices of the snub disphenoid form a cluster that, according to numerical experiments, has the minimum possible [Lennard-Jones potential](wikipedia://en/227686) among all eight-sphere clusters.

In the geometry of [chemical compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound), a polyhedron may be visualized as the [atom cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom_cluster) surrounding a central atom. The [dodecahedral molecular geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodecahedral_molecular_geometry) describes the cluster for which it is a snub disphenoid.

:::

## History and naming {#History_and_naming}

The *snub disphenoid* name comes from (Johnson 1966) classification of the [Johnson solid](wikipedia://en/16034), named after American mathematician [Norman W. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_W._Johnson) who listed the convex polyhedra with regular polygonal faces. However, this solid was first studied by (Rausenberger 1915). It was studied again in the paper by (Freudenthal van d. Waerden 1947), which first described the set of eight convex [deltahedra](wikipedia://en/493995), and named it the *Siamese dodecahedron*.

The *dodecadeltahedron* name was given to the same shape by (Bernal 1964), referring to the fact that it is a 12-sided deltahedron. There are other [simplicial dodecahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_polyhedron), such as the [hexagonal bipyramid](wikipedia://en/2081012), but this is the only one that can be realized with equilateral faces. Bernal was interested in the shapes of holes left in irregular close-packed arrangements of spheres, so he used a restrictive definition of deltahedra, in which a deltahedron is a convex polyhedron with triangular faces that can be formed by the centers of a collection of congruent spheres, whose tangencies represent polyhedron edges, and such that there is no room to pack another sphere inside the cage created by this system of spheres. This restrictive definition disallows the [triangular bipyramid](wikipedia://en/646933) (as forming two tetrahedral holes rather than a single hole), [pentagonal bipyramid](wikipedia://en/728038) (because the spheres for its apexes interpenetrate, so it cannot occur in sphere packings), and [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968) (because it has interior room for another sphere). Bernal writes that the snub disphenoid is "a very common [coordination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_geometry) for the [calcium ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_ion) in [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography)". In coordination geometry, it is usually known as the trigonal dodecahedron or simply as the dodecahedron.
