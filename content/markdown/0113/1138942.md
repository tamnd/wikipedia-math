::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Point group"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **point group** is a [mathematical group](wikipedia://en/19447) of [symmetry operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_operation) ([isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)) that have a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) in common. The [coordinate origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) of the Euclidean space is conventionally taken to be a fixed point, and every point group in dimension *d* is then a subgroup of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) O(*d*). Point groups are used to describe the [symmetries](wikipedia://en/44362809) of geometric figures and physical objects such as [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_symmetry).

:::

Each point group can be [represented](wikipedia://en/12695) as sets of [orthogonal matrices](wikipedia://en/105620) *M* that transform point *x* into point *y* according to *y* = *Mx*. Each element of a point group is either a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) ([determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of *M* = 1), or it is a [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) or [improper rotation](wikipedia://en/244324) (determinant of *M* = −1).

The geometric symmetries of [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) are described by [space groups](wikipedia://en/463721), which allow [translations](wikipedia://en/204682) and contain point groups as subgroups. Discrete point groups in more than one dimension come in infinite families, but from the [crystallographic restriction theorem](wikipedia://en/2218040) and [one of Bieberbach's theorems](wikipedia://en/463721#Bieberbach.27s_theorems), each number of dimensions has only a finite number of point groups that are symmetric over some [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) or grid with that number of dimensions. These are the [crystallographic point groups](wikipedia://en/663786).

## Chiral and achiral point groups, reflection groups {#Chiral_and_achiral_point_groups,_reflection_groups}

Point groups can be classified into *[chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiral)* (or purely rotational) groups and *achiral* groups. The chiral groups are subgroups of the [special orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) SO(*d*): they contain only orientation-preserving orthogonal transformations, i.e., those of determinant +1. The achiral groups contain also transformations of determinant −1. In an achiral group, the orientation-preserving transformations form a (chiral) subgroup of index 2.

[Finite Coxeter groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_Coxeter_group) or *reflection groups* are those point groups that are generated purely by a set of reflectional mirrors passing through the same point. A rank *n* Coxeter group has *n* mirrors and is represented by a [Coxeter–Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/5358554). [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502) offers a bracketed notation equivalent to the Coxeter diagram, with markup symbols for rotational and other subsymmetry point groups. Reflection groups are necessarily achiral (except for the trivial group containing only the identity element).

### Two dimensions {#Two_dimensions}

[Point groups in two dimensions](wikipedia://en/3037988), sometimes called **rosette groups**.

They come in two infinite families:

1. [Cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) C<sub>*n*</sub> of *n*-fold rotation groups
1. [Dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267) D<sub>*n*</sub> of *n*-fold rotation and reflection groups

Applying the [crystallographic restriction theorem](wikipedia://en/2218040) restricts *n* to values 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6 for both families, yielding 10 groups.

### Three dimensions {#Three_dimensions}

[Point groups in three dimensions](wikipedia://en/2694525), sometimes called **molecular point groups** after their wide use in studying [symmetries of molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_symmetry).

They come in 7 infinite families of axial groups (also called prismatic), and 7 additional polyhedral groups (also called Platonic). In [Schoenflies notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoenflies_notation),

- Axial groups: C<sub>*n*</sub>, S<sub>2*n*</sub>, C<sub>*n*h</sub>, C<sub>*n*v</sub>, D<sub>*n*</sub>, D<sub>*n*d</sub>, D<sub>*n*h</sub>
- [Polyhedral groups](wikipedia://en/24192415): T, T<sub>d</sub>, T<sub>h</sub>, O, O<sub>h</sub>, I, I<sub>h</sub>

Applying the [crystallographic restriction theorem](wikipedia://en/2218040) to these groups yields the 32 [crystallographic point groups](wikipedia://en/663786).

### Four dimensions {#Four_dimensions}

The four-dimensional point groups (chiral as well as achiral) are listed in Conway and Smith,[^n1] Section 4, Tables 4.1–4.3.

*Image: Coxeter diagram finite rank4 correspondence.png.* Finite isomorphism and correspondences

The following list gives the four-dimensional reflection groups (excluding those that leave a subspace fixed and that are therefore lower-dimensional reflection groups). Each group is specified as a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004), and like the [polyhedral groups](wikipedia://en/24192415) of 3D, it can be named by its related [convex regular 4-polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_regular_4-polytope). Related pure rotational groups exist for each with half the order, and can be represented by the bracket [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502) with a '+' exponent, for example \[3,3,3\]<sup>\+</sup> has three 3-fold gyration points and symmetry order 60. Front-back symmetric groups like \[3,3,3\] and \[3,4,3\] can be doubled, shown as double brackets in Coxeter's notation, for example 3,3,3 with its order doubled to 240.
