# Canonical ensemble

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Canonical ensemble"}

In [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), a **canonical ensemble** is the [statistical ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_ensemble_%28mathematical_physics%29) that represents the possible states of a mechanical system in [thermal equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_equilibrium) with a [heat bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_bath) at a fixed temperature.[^o2] The system can exchange energy with the heat bath, so that the states of the system will differ in total energy.

:::

The principal thermodynamic variable of the canonical ensemble, determining the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of states, is the [absolute temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_temperature) (symbol: $T$). The ensemble typically also depends on mechanical variables such as the number of particles in the system (symbol: $N$) and the system's volume (symbol: $V$), each of which influence the nature of the system's internal states. An ensemble with these three parameters, which are assumed constant for the ensemble to be considered canonical, is sometimes called the **$NVT$ ensemble**.

The canonical ensemble assigns a probability $P$ to each distinct [microstate](wikipedia://en/3066350) given by the following exponential:

$$P = e^{(F - E)/(k T)},$$

where $E$ is the total energy of the microstate, and $k$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant).

The number $F$ is the free energy (specifically, the [Helmholtz free energy](wikipedia://en/255447)) and is assumed to be a constant for a specific ensemble to be considered canonical. However, the probabilities and $F$ will vary if different *N*, *V*, *T* are selected. The free energy $F$ serves two roles: first, it provides a normalization factor for the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) (the probabilities, over the complete set of microstates, must add up to one); second, many important ensemble averages can be directly calculated from the function $F(N, V, T)$.

An alternative but equivalent formulation for the same concept writes the probability as

$$\textstyle P = \frac{1}{Z} e^{-E/(k T)},$$

using the [canonical partition function](wikipedia://en/312252)

$$\textstyle Z = e^{-F/(k T)}$$

rather than the free energy. The equations below (in terms of free energy) may be restated in terms of the canonical partition function by simple mathematical manipulations.

Historically, the canonical ensemble was first described by [Boltzmann](wikipedia://en/544255) (who called it a *holode*) in 1884 in a relatively unknown paper.[^o3] It was later reformulated and extensively investigated by [Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) in 1902.[^o2]

## Applicability of canonical ensemble {#Applicability_of_canonical_ensemble}

The canonical ensemble is the ensemble that describes the possible states of a system that is in thermal equilibrium with a heat bath (the derivation of this fact can be found in Gibbs[^o2]).

The canonical ensemble applies to systems of any size; while it is necessary to assume that the heat bath is very large (i.e., take a [macroscopic limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic_limit)), the system itself may be small or large.

The condition that the system is mechanically isolated is necessary in order to ensure it does not exchange energy with any external object besides the heat bath.[^o2] In general, it is desirable to apply the canonical ensemble to systems that are in direct contact with the heat bath, since it is that contact that ensures the equilibrium. In practical situations, the use of the canonical ensemble is usually justified either 1) by assuming that the contact is mechanically weak, or 2) by incorporating a suitable part of the heat bath connection into the system under analysis, so that the connection's mechanical influence on the system is modeled within the system.

When the total energy is fixed but the internal state of the system is otherwise unknown, the appropriate description is not the canonical ensemble but the [microcanonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1136447). For systems where the particle number is variable (due to contact with a particle reservoir), the correct description is the [grand canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1129074). In [statistical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics) textbooks for interacting particle systems the three ensembles are assumed to be [thermodynamically equivalent](wikipedia://en/2262550): the fluctuations of macroscopic quantities around their average value become small and, as the number of particles tends to infinity, they tend to vanish. In the latter limit, called the thermodynamic limit, the average constraints effectively become hard constraints. The assumption of [ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_ensemble_%28mathematical_physics%29) equivalence dates back to [Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) and has been verified for some models of physical systems with short-range interactions and subject to a small number of macroscopic constraints. Despite the fact that many textbooks still convey the message that ensemble equivalence holds for all physical systems, over the last decades various examples of physical systems have been found for which breaking of ensemble equivalence occurs.[^o5][^o6][^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10]

## Properties {#Properties}

*Uniqueness*: The canonical ensemble is uniquely determined for a given physical system at a given temperature, and does not depend on arbitrary choices such as choice of coordinate system (classical mechanics), or basis (quantum mechanics), or of the zero of energy.[^o2] The canonical ensemble is the only ensemble with constant $N$, $V$, and $T$ that reproduces the [fundamental thermodynamic relation](wikipedia://en/5529757).[^o13]; *Statistical equilibrium* (steady state): A canonical ensemble does not evolve over time, despite the fact that the underlying system is in constant motion. This is because the ensemble is only a function of a conserved quantity of the system (energy).[^o2]; *Thermal equilibrium with other systems*: Two systems, each described by a canonical ensemble of equal temperature, brought into thermal contact[^o14] will each retain the same ensemble and the resulting combined system is described by a canonical ensemble of the same temperature.[^o2]; *Maximum entropy*: For a given mechanical system (fixed $N$, $V$), the canonical ensemble average $-\langle\log P\rangle$ (the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891)) is the maximum possible of any ensemble with the same $\langle E\rangle$.[^o2]; *Minimum free energy*: For a given mechanical system (fixed $N$, $V$) and given value of $T$, the canonical ensemble average $\langle E + kT \log P\rangle$ (the [Helmholtz free energy](wikipedia://en/255447)) is the lowest possible of any ensemble.[^o2] This is easily seen to be equivalent to maximizing the entropy.

:::

## Free energy, ensemble averages, and exact differentials {#Free_energy,_ensemble_averages,_and_exact_differentials}

- The partial derivatives of the function $F(N, V, T)$ give important canonical ensemble average quantities:
  - the average pressure is[^o2] $$\langle p \rangle = -\frac{\partial F} {\partial V},$$
  - the [Gibbs entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_entropy) is[^o2] $$S = -k \langle \log P \rangle = - \frac{\partial F} {\partial T},$$
  - the partial derivative $\partial F/\partial N$ is approximately related to [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628), although the concept of chemical equilibrium does not exactly apply to canonical ensembles of small systems.[^o16]
  - and the average energy is[^o2] $$\langle E \rangle = F + ST.$$
- *Exact differential*: From the above expressions, it can be seen that the function $F(V, T)$, for a given $N$, has the [exact differential](wikipedia://en/405737)[^o2] $$dF = - S \, dT - \langle p\rangle \, dV .$$
- *First law of thermodynamics*: Substituting the above relationship for $\langle E\rangle$ into the exact differential of $F$, an equation similar to the [first law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/166404) is found, except with average signs on some of the quantities:[^o2] $$d\langle E \rangle = T \, dS - \langle p\rangle \, dV .$$
- *[Energy fluctuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_fluctuations)*: The energy in the system has uncertainty in the canonical ensemble. The [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of the energy is[^o2] $$\langle E^2 \rangle - \langle E \rangle^2 = k T^2 \frac{\partial \langle E \rangle}{\partial T}.$$

## Example ensembles {#Example_ensembles}

> *"We may imagine a great number of systems of the same nature, but differing in the configurations and velocities which they have at a given instant, and differing in not merely infinitesimally, but it may be so as to embrace every conceivable combination of configuration and velocities..." J. W. Gibbs* (1903)[^o18]

### Boltzmann distribution (separable system) {#Boltzmann_distribution_(separable_system)}

If a system described by a canonical ensemble can be separated into independent parts (this happens if the different parts do not interact), and each of those parts has a fixed material composition, then each part can be seen as a system unto itself and is described by a canonical ensemble having the same temperature as the whole. Moreover, if the system is made up of multiple *similar* parts, then each part has exactly the same distribution as the other parts.

In this way, the canonical ensemble provides exactly the [Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/4107) (also known as [Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics](wikipedia://en/170167)) for systems of *any number* of particles. In comparison, the justification of the Boltzmann distribution from the [microcanonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1136447) only applies for systems with a large number of parts (that is, in the thermodynamic limit).

The Boltzmann distribution itself is one of the most important tools in applying statistical mechanics to real systems, as it massively simplifies the study of systems that can be separated into independent parts (e.g., [particles in a gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_speed_distribution), [electromagnetic modes in a cavity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck's_law), [molecular bonds in a polymer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer_physics)).

### Ising model (strongly interacting system) {#Ising_model_(strongly_interacting_system)}

In a system composed of pieces that interact with each other, it is usually not possible to find a way to separate the system into independent subsystems as done in the Boltzmann distribution. In these systems it is necessary to resort to using the full expression of the canonical ensemble in order to describe the thermodynamics of the system when it is thermostatted to a heat bath. The canonical ensemble is generally the most straightforward framework for studies of statistical mechanics and even allows one to obtain exact solutions in some interacting model systems.[^o21]

A classic example of this is the [Ising model](wikipedia://en/292744), which is a widely discussed toy model for the phenomena of [ferromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) and of [self-assembled monolayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-assembled_monolayer) formation, and is one of the simplest models that shows a [phase transition](wikipedia://en/54423). [Lars Onsager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Onsager) famously calculated exactly the free energy of an infinite-sized [square-lattice Ising model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-lattice_Ising_model) at zero magnetic field, in the canonical ensemble.[^o22]

## Precise expressions for the ensemble {#Precise_expressions_for_the_ensemble}

The precise mathematical expression for a statistical ensemble depends on the kind of mechanics under consideration—quantum or classical—since the notion of a "microstate" is considerably different in these two cases. In quantum mechanics, the canonical ensemble affords a simple description since [diagonalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_diagonalization) provides a discrete set of [microstates](wikipedia://en/3066350) with specific energies. The classical mechanical case is more complex as it involves instead an integral over canonical [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), and the size of microstates in phase space can be chosen somewhat arbitrarily.

### Quantum mechanical {#Quantum_mechanical}

A statistical ensemble in quantum mechanics is represented by a [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844), denoted by $\hat \rho$. In basis-free notation, the canonical ensemble is the density matrix

$$\hat \rho = \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{kT}(F - \hat H)\right),$$

where $\text{Ĥ}$ is the system's total energy operator ([Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381)), and $\exp()$ is the [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731) operator. The free energy $F$ is determined by the probability normalization condition that the density matrix has a [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of one, $\operatorname{Tr} \hat \rho=1$:

$$e^{-\frac{F}{k T}} = \operatorname{Tr} \exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{kT} \hat H\right).$$

The canonical ensemble can alternatively be written in a simple form using [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542), if the system's [energy eigenstates](wikipedia://en/4930033) and energy eigenvalues are known. Given a complete basis of energy eigenstates $|\psi_{i}\rangle$, indexed by $i$, the canonical ensemble is:

$$\hat \rho = \sum_i e^{\frac{F - E_i}{k T}} |\psi_i\rangle \langle \psi_i |$$

$$e^{-\frac{F}{k T}} = \sum_i e^{\frac{- E_i}{k T}}.$$

where the $E_{i}$ are the energy eigenvalues determined by $\text{Ĥ}|\psi_{i}\rangle = E_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle$. In other words, a set of microstates in quantum mechanics is given by a complete set of stationary states. The density matrix is diagonal in this basis, with the diagonal entries each directly giving a probability.

### Classical mechanical {#Classical_mechanical}

In classical mechanics, a statistical ensemble is instead represented by a [joint probability density function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_probability_density_function) in the system's [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), $\rho(p_{1}, \ldots p_{n}, q_{1}, \ldots q_{n})$, where the $p_{1}, \ldots p_{n}$ and $q_{1}, \ldots q_{n}$ are the [canonical coordinates](wikipedia://en/706311) (generalized momenta and generalized coordinates) of the system's internal degrees of freedom. In a system of particles, the number of degrees of freedom $n$ depends on the number of particles $N$ in a way that depends on the physical situation. For a three-dimensional monoatomic gas (not molecules), $n = 3N$. In diatomic gases there will also be rotational and vibrational degrees of freedom.

The probability density function for the canonical ensemble is:

$$\rho = \frac{1}{h^n C} e^{\frac{F - E}{k T}},$$

where

- $E$ is the energy of the system, a function of the phase $(p_{1}, \ldots q_{n})$,
- $h$ is an arbitrary but predetermined constant with the units of $\operatorname{energy}\times\operatorname{time}$, setting the extent of one microstate and providing correct dimensions to $\rho$.[^o26]
- $C$ is an overcounting correction factor, often used for particle systems where identical particles are able to change place with each other.[^o27]
- $F$ provides a normalizing factor and is also the characteristic state function, the free energy.

Again, the value of $F$ is determined by demanding that $\rho$ is a normalized probability density function:

$$e^{-\frac{F}{k T}} = \int \ldots \int \frac{1}{h^n C} e^{\frac{- E}{k T}} \, dp_1 \ldots dq_n$$

This integral is taken over the entire [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).

In other words, a microstate in classical mechanics is a phase space region, and this region has volume $h^{n}C$. This means that each microstate spans a range of energy, however this range can be made arbitrarily narrow by choosing $h$ to be very small. The phase space integral can be converted into a summation over microstates, once phase space has been finely divided to a sufficient degree.
