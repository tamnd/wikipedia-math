# Hyperbolic sector

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperbolic sector"}

*Image: Hyperbolic\_sector.svg.*

A **hyperbolic sector** is a [region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_%28mathematics%29) of the [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane) bounded by a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) and two [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) from the origin to it. For example, the two points $(a, 1/a)$ and $(b, 1/b)$ on the [rectangular hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052#Rectangular_hyperbola) $xy = 1$, or the corresponding region when this hyperbola is re-scaled and its [orientation](wikipedia://en/2544098) is altered by a [rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_%28geometry%29) leaving the center at the origin, as with the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122#Parametrization). A hyperbolic sector in standard position has $a = 1$ and $b > 1$.

:::

The [signed area](wikipedia://en/39433938) of the hyperbolic sector is used to define the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981), which is analogous to how the area of a [circular sector](wikipedia://en/1967249) defines a [circular angle](wikipedia://en/462705). In other words, the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) is the [argument](wikipedia://en/32842052) of [hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) in the same way that the circular angle is the argument of [circular functions](wikipedia://en/30367).

## Hyperbolic triangle {#Hyperbolic_triangle}

*Image: Cartesian hyperbolic triangle.svg.* **Hyperbolic triangle** (yellow) and hyperbolic sector (red) corresponding to [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) *u*, to the [rectangular hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_hyperbola) (equation *y* = 1/*x*). The legs of the triangle are $\sqrt{2}$ times the [hyperbolic cosine and sine functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function).

When in standard position, a hyperbolic sector determines a **hyperbolic triangle**, the [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) with one [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) at the origin, base on the diagonal ray *y* = *x*, and third vertex on the [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052)

$$xy=1,\,$$

with the hypotenuse being the segment from the origin to the point (*x, y*) on the hyperbola. The length of the base of this triangle is

$$\sqrt 2 \cosh u,\,$$

and the [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241) is

$$\sqrt 2 \sinh u,\,$$

where *u* is the appropriate [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981). The usual definitions of the hyperbolic functions can be seen via the legs of right triangles plotted with [hyperbolic coordinates](wikipedia://en/1264398#Trigonometry). When the length of these legs is divided by the [square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682), they can be graphed as the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) with hyperbolic cosine and sine coordinates.

The analogy between circular and hyperbolic functions was described by [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289) in his *Trigonometry and Double Algebra* (1849).[^o3] [William Burnside](wikipedia://en/251883) used such triangles, projecting from a point on the hyperbola *xy* = 1 onto the main diagonal, in his article "Note on the addition theorem for hyperbolic functions".[^o4]

## Hyperbolic logarithm {#Hyperbolic_logarithm}

*Image: Visual\_proof\_hyperbolic\_sector\_area.svg.* The hyperbolic sector POQ area is equal to logarithm evaluated between S and R.

It is known that f(*x*) = *x*<sup>*p*</sup> has an algebraic [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) except in the case *p* = –1 corresponding to the [quadrature](wikipedia://en/1618671) of the hyperbola. The other cases are given by [Cavalieri's quadrature formula](wikipedia://en/30839740). Whereas quadrature of the parabola had been accomplished by [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) in the third century BC (in *[The Quadrature of the Parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Quadrature_of_the_Parabola)*), the hyperbolic quadrature required the invention in 1647 of a new function: [Gregoire de Saint-Vincent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregoire_de_Saint-Vincent) addressed the problem of computing the areas bounded by a hyperbola. His findings led to the natural logarithm function, once called the **hyperbolic logarithm** since it is obtained by integrating, or finding the area, under the hyperbola.[^o6]

Before 1748 and the publication of *[Introduction to the Analysis of the Infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduction_to_the_Analysis_of_the_Infinite)*, the natural logarithm was known in terms of the area of a hyperbolic sector. [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) changed that when he introduced [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021) such as 10<sup>x</sup>. Euler identified [e](wikipedia://en/9633) as the value of *b* producing a unit of area (under the hyperbola or in a hyperbolic sector in standard position). Then the natural logarithm could be recognized as the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) to the transcendental [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) e<sup>x</sup>.

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** Given 0 \< *a* \< *b* and P = (*a*, 1/*a*), Q = (*b*, 1/*b*), the [signed area](wikipedia://en/39433938) of the hyperbolic sector POQ is log *b*/*a*.[^o8]

proof: In the figure, POQ = POS + PQRS − QOR. Then the equality of areas POS and QOR implies area POQ = area PQRS = $\int_a^b \frac{dx}{x} = \log b - \log a = \log \frac{b}{a}$.

In particular, for a hyperbolic sector in standard position (*a* = 1), the area of the hyperbolic sector is log *b*.

:::

### Standard sector {#Standard_sector}

Given a line of positive [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) *m* > 0, *y* = *mx*, and the main diagonal (*m* = 1), the **standard hyperbolic sector** is limited by *xy* = 1, a standard rectangular hyperbola. The variable line intersects the hyperbola when 1/*x* = *mx* or *x* = *m*<sup>−1/2</sup>.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=inline-head name="Corollary"}

**Corollary:** The area of the standard sector is $\log (m^{-1/2}) = - \frac{1}{2} \log m$.

The negative sign indicates orientation reversal for increasing logarithms and increasing slopes.

:::

## Hyperbolic geometry {#Hyperbolic_geometry}

When [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887)'s book on [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) was published in 1928, it provided a foundation for the subject by reference to [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). To establish hyperbolic measure on a line, Klein noted that the area of a hyperbolic sector provided visual illustration of the concept.[^o12]

Hyperbolic sectors can also be drawn to the hyperbola $y = \sqrt{1 + x^2}$. The area of such hyperbolic sectors has been used to define hyperbolic distance in a geometry textbook.[^o13]
