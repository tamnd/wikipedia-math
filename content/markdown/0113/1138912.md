# Differential of the first kind

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differential of the first kind"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), ***differential of the first kind*** is a traditional term used in the theories of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181) (more generally, [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742)) and [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260) (more generally, [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808)) for everywhere-regular [differential 1-forms](wikipedia://en/221519). Given a complex manifold *M*, a differential of the first kind ω is therefore the same thing as a 1-form that is everywhere [holomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_form).

:::

The dimension of the space of differentials of the first kind, by means of this identification, is the [Hodge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_number)

*h*<sup>1,0</sup>.

The differentials of the first kind, when integrated along paths, give rise to integrals that generalise the [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960) to all curves over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). They include for example the **hyperelliptic integrals** of type

$$\int\frac{x^k \, dx}{\sqrt{Q(x)}}$$

where *Q* is a [square-free polynomial](wikipedia://en/3816655) of any given degree > 4. The allowable power *k* has to be determined by analysis of the possible pole at the [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) on the corresponding [hyperelliptic curve](wikipedia://en/499361). When this is done, one finds that the condition is

*k* ≤ *g* − 1,

or in other words, *k* at most 1 for degree of *Q* 5 or 6, at most 2 for degree 7 or 8, and so on (as *g* = \[(1+ deg *Q*)/2\]).

Quite generally, as this example illustrates, for a [compact Riemann surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Riemann_surface) or [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260), the Hodge number is the [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g*. For the case of [algebraic surfaces](wikipedia://en/648311), this is the quantity known classically as the [irregularity](wikipedia://en/22857258) *q*. It is also, in general, the dimension of the [Albanese variety](wikipedia://en/1814828), which takes the place of the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734).

## Differentials of the second and third kind {#Differentials_of_the_second_and_third_kind}

The traditional terminology also included differentials **of the second kind** and **of the third kind**.

The [Weierstrass zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_zeta_function) was called an *integral of the second kind* in [elliptic function](wikipedia://en/69939) theory; it is a [logarithmic derivative](wikipedia://en/470327) of a [theta function](wikipedia://en/449745), and therefore has [simple poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_pole), with integer residues. The decomposition of a ([meromorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic)) elliptic function into pieces of 'three kinds' parallels the representation as (i) a constant, plus (ii) a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of translates of the Weierstrass zeta function, plus (iii) a function with arbitrary poles but no residues at them.

On the other hand, a meromorphic abelian differential of the *second kind* has traditionally been one with residues at all poles being zero. One of the **third kind** is one where all poles are simple. There is a higher-dimensional analogue available, using the [Poincaré residue](wikipedia://en/7062375).

## Reciprocity laws {#Reciprocity_laws}

Suppose that a compact Riemann surface $S$ is given, along with a collection of cycles $\delta_1,\ldots,\delta_{2g}$ forming a basis of $H_1(S,\mathbb Z)$, issuing from a common base point $s_0\in S$. Let $\omega$ be a holomorphic differential on $S$, and $\eta$ a meromorphic differential with only simple poles, at $s_k\in S$. Letting

$$\Pi_k = \int_{\delta_k}\omega$$

$$N_k=\int_{\delta_k}\eta,$$

we have the Abel reciprocity law for differentials of the first and third kind[^n1]

$$\sum_{k=1}^g (\Pi_kN_{g+k} - \Pi_{g+k}N_k) = 2\pi i \sum_k\operatorname{Res}_{s_k}(\eta)\int_{s_0}^{s_k}\omega.$$

In the special case when $\eta$ is also a differential of the first kind, this recovers [Riemann's first bilinear relation](wikipedia://en/8200934).
