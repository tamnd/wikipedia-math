# Rapidity

*Image: Inverse Hyperbolic Tangent.svg.* Rapidity is the value of $\operatorname{artanh}(v / c)$ for velocity $v$ and speed of light $c$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rapidity"}

In [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), the classical concept of [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) is converted to **rapidity** to accommodate the limit determined by the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). Velocities must be combined by Einstein's [velocity-addition formula](wikipedia://en/1437696#Special_relativity). For low speeds, rapidity and velocity are almost exactly proportional but, for higher velocities, rapidity takes a larger value, with the rapidity of light being infinite.

:::

Mathematically, rapidity can be defined as the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) that differentiates two [frames of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frames_of_reference) in relative motion, each frame being associated with [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) and [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) coordinates.

Using the [inverse hyperbolic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_hyperbolic_function) $\operatorname{artanh}$, the rapidity $w$ corresponding to velocity $v$ is:

$$w = \operatorname{artanh}(\frac{v}{c})$$

where $c$ is the speed of light. Instead of $w$, some authors use $\zeta$, $\eta$, $\xi$, $\phi$, $\varphi$, or $\psi$.

For low speeds, by the [small-angle approximation](wikipedia://en/2009207), $w$ is approximately $v / c$. Since in relativity any velocity $v$ is constrained to the interval $-c < v < c$ the ratio $v / c$ satisfies $-1 < v / c < 1$. The inverse hyperbolic tangent has the unit interval $(-1, 1)$ for its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and the whole [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) for its [image](wikipedia://en/579311); that is, the interval $-c < v < c$ maps onto $-\infty < w < \infty$.

## History {#History}

*Image: Add\_velocity\_Earth\_POV.svg.* Time ($t$) and space ($x$) axes: moving observers have primed or double primed axes

In 1908 [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) explained how the [Lorentz transformation](wikipedia://en/18404) could be seen as simply a [hyperbolic rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_rotation) of the [spacetime coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_time), i.e., a rotation through an imaginary angle.[^o3] This angle therefore represents (in one spatial dimension) a simple additive measure of the velocity between frames.[^o4] The rapidity parameter replacing velocity was introduced in 1910 by [Vladimir Varićak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Varićak)[^o5] and by [E. T. Whittaker](wikipedia://en/1077330).[^o6] The parameter was named *rapidity* by [Alfred Robb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Robb) (1911)[^o7] and this term was adopted by many subsequent authors, such as [Ludwik Silberstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwik_Silberstein) (1914), [Frank Morley](wikipedia://en/589167) (1936) and [Wolfgang Rindler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Rindler) (2001).

### Minkowski diagram {#Minkowski_diagram}

Rapidity is the parameter expressing variability of an event on the [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) which represents the future events one time unit away from the origin O. These events can be expressed $(\sinh w, \cosh w)$ where $\sinh$ is the [hyperbolic sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_sine) and $\cosh$ is the [hyperbolic cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_cosine). Note that as speed and $w$ increase, the axes tilt toward the diagonal. In fact, they remain in a relation of [hyperbolic orthogonality](wikipedia://en/958449) whatever the value of $w$. The appropriate $x$-axis is the *hyperplane of simultaneity* corresponding to rapidity $w$ at the origin.

The hyperbola can be associated with the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122). A moving [reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_frame) sees the spacetime in the same way the rest frame does, so a transformation theory is necessary to explain the adaptation of one to the other. When the unit hyperbola is interpreted as a [one-parameter group](wikipedia://en/754851) that [acts](wikipedia://en/12781) on the future, and correspondingly on the past and elsewhere, then the Minkowski configuration expresses the [relativity of simultaneity](wikipedia://en/3406142) and other features of relativity.

## Lorentz boost {#Lorentz_boost}

The transformations relating reference frames are associated with [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz). To make a moving frame with rapidity $w$ into the rest frame with [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) axes of time and space, one applies a [hyperbolic rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_rotation) of parameter $-w$. Since $\cosh(-w) = \cosh w$ and $\sinh(-w) = -\sinh w$, the following matrix representation of the hyperbolic rotation will bring the moving frame into perpendicularity (though all frames keep hyperbolic orthogonality since that relation is invariant under hyperbolic rotation).

A [Lorentz boost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_boost) is a vector-matrix product

$$\begin{pmatrix} c t' \\ x' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh w & -\sinh w \\ -\sinh w & \cosh w \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} ct \\ x \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf \Lambda (w) \begin{pmatrix} ct \\ x \end{pmatrix}.$$

The matrix $\mathbf{\Lambda}(w)$ is of the type $\begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ q & p \end{pmatrix}$ with $p$ and $q$ satisfying $p^{2} - q^{2} = 1$, so that $(p, q)$ lies on the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122). Such matrices form the [indefinite orthogonal group O(1,1)](wikipedia://en/293609) with one-dimensional Lie algebra spanned by the anti-diagonal unit matrix, showing that the rapidity is the coordinate on this Lie algebra. In [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731) notation, $\mathbf{\Lambda}(w)$ can be expressed as $\mathbf \Lambda (w) = e^{\mathbf Z w}$, where $\mathbf{Z}$ is the negative of the anti-diagonal unit matrix $\mathbf Z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .$ Since $Z^{2}$ is the identity, $Z$ is a [hyperbolic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_unit).

### Velocity addition {#Velocity_addition}

A key property of the matrix exponential is $e^{\mathbf X(s+t)}=e^{\mathbf Xs}e^{\mathbf Xt}$ from which immediately follows that

$$\mathbf{\Lambda}(w_1 + w_2) = \mathbf{\Lambda}(w_1)\mathbf{\Lambda}(w_2).$$

This establishes the useful additive property of rapidity: if $A$, $B$ and $C$ are [frames of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frames_of_reference), then

$$w_{\text{AC}}= w_{\text{AB}} + w_{\text{BC}},$$

where $w_{\operatorname{PQ}}$ denotes the rapidity of a frame of reference $Q$ relative to a frame of reference $P$. The simplicity of this formula contrasts with the complexity of the corresponding [velocity-addition formula](wikipedia://en/1437696#Special_theory_of_relativity).

As we can see from the Lorentz transformation above, the [Lorentz factor](wikipedia://en/732446) identifies with $\cosh w$

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2 / c^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} \equiv \cosh w,$$

so the rapidity $w$ is implicitly used as a hyperbolic angle in the Lorentz transformation expressions using $\gamma$ and $\beta$. We relate rapidities to the [velocity-addition formula](wikipedia://en/1437696#Special_theory_of_relativity)

$$u = \frac{u_1 + u_2}{1 + \frac{u_1 u_2}{c^2}}$$

by recognizing

$$\beta_i = \frac{u_i}{c} = \tanh{w_i}$$

and so

$$\tanh w = \frac{\tanh w_1 + \tanh w_2}{1 + \tanh w_1\tanh w_2} = \tanh(w_1+ w_2)$$

[Proper acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_acceleration) (the acceleration 'felt' by the object being accelerated) is the rate of change of rapidity with respect to [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) (time as measured by the object undergoing acceleration itself). Therefore, the rapidity of an object in a given frame can be viewed simply as the velocity of that object as would be calculated non-relativistically by an inertial guidance system on board the object itself if it accelerated from rest in that frame to its given speed.

### Hyperbolic functions {#Hyperbolic_functions}

The product of $\beta$ and $\gamma$ appears frequently in the equations of special relativity. As a result, some authors define an explicit parameter $\alpha$ for this expression, which is, from above:

$$\alpha = \beta \gamma = \tanh w \cosh w = \sinh w$$

This relationship uses [hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) of the rapidity $w$ to relate these parameters of special relativity, as Minkowski had observed:

$$\alpha = \sinh w$$

$$\beta = \tanh w$$

$$\gamma = \cosh w$$

### Exponential and logarithmic relations {#Exponential_and_logarithmic_relations}

From the above expressions we have

$$e^{w} = \cosh(w) + \sinh(w) = \gamma(1 + \beta) = \frac{ 1 + \beta }{ \sqrt{ 1 - \beta^2} } = \sqrt \frac{1 + \tfrac{v}{c}}{1 - \tfrac{v}{c}},$$

and thus

$$e^{-w} = \cosh(w) - \sinh(w) = \gamma(1 - \beta) = \frac{ 1 - \beta }{ \sqrt{ 1 - \beta^2} } = \sqrt \frac{1 - \tfrac{v}{c}}{1 + \tfrac{v}{c}}.$$

or explicitly

$$w = \ln \left[\gamma(1 + \beta)\right] = -\ln \left[\gamma(1 - \beta)\right] \, .$$

### Doppler effect {#Doppler_effect}

The [Doppler-shift](wikipedia://en/408026) factor, for the longitudinal case with source and receiver moving directly towards or away from each other, that is associated with rapidity $w$ is $k = e^w$.

## In experimental particle physics {#In_experimental_particle_physics}

The energy $E$ and scalar momentum $|\mathbf{p}|$ of a particle of non-zero (rest) mass $m$ are given by:

$$\begin{aligned} E &= \gamma mc^2 \\ \left| \mathbf p \right| &= \gamma mv. \end{aligned}$$

With the definition of $w$

$$w = \operatorname{artanh} \frac{v}{c} = \operatorname{artanh} \beta,$$

and thus with

$$\cosh w = \cosh \left( \operatorname{artanh} \frac{v}{c} \right) = \frac {1}{ \sqrt { 1- \frac{v^2}{c^2} }} = \gamma$$

$$\sinh w = \sinh \left( \operatorname{artanh} \frac{v}{c} \right) = \frac {\frac{v}{c}}{ \sqrt { 1- \frac{v^2}{c^2} }} = \beta \gamma = \alpha ,$$

the energy and scalar momentum can be written as:

$$\begin{aligned} E &= m c^2 \cosh w \\ \left| \mathbf p \right| &= m c \, \sinh w. \end{aligned}$$

So, rapidity can be calculated from measured energy and momentum by

$$w = \operatorname{artanh} \frac{| \mathbf p | c}{E}= \frac{1}{2} \ln \frac{E + | \mathbf p | c}{E - | \mathbf p | c} = \ln \frac{E + | \mathbf p | c}{ mc^2} ~.$$

However, experimental particle physicists often use a modified definition of rapidity relative to a beam axis

$$y = \frac{1}{2} \ln \frac{E + p_z c}{E - p_z c} ,$$

where $p_{z}$ is the component of momentum along the beam axis.[^o15] This is the rapidity of the boost along the beam axis which takes an observer from the lab frame to a frame in which the particle moves only perpendicular to the beam. Related to this is the concept of [pseudorapidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorapidity).

Rapidity relative to a beam axis can also be expressed as

$$y = \ln \frac{E + p_z c}{\sqrt{m^2c^4+p_T^2 c^2} } ~.$$
