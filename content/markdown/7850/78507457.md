# Bayes space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bayes space"}

**Bayes space** is a [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) defined as an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of measures with the same [null-sets](wikipedia://en/21520). Two measures are defined to be equivalent if they are proportional. The basic ideas of Bayes spaces have their roots in [Compositional Data Analysis](wikipedia://en/500654) and the Aitchison geometry.[^o2] Theoretical applications are mainly in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), specifically [functional data analysis](wikipedia://en/2141368) of [density](wikipedia://en/43487) functions, aka density data analysis.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6] Practical applications are in [geochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geochemistry)[^o7], [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19) modelling[^o8], sediment analysis[^o9] and developmental research[^o18]. Alternative approaches to density analysis are based on the [Wasserstein metric](wikipedia://en/6704603), often termed Wasserstein regression, have also been applied to medicine[^o10].

:::

The basic structure of the Bayes space is that of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), with addition and multiplication being defined by perturbation and powering.[^o11] The space is formed over a $\sigma$-finite reference/base measure, denoted $\lambda$ or $P$ depending on whether it is infinite or finite. Densities are considered as [Radon-Nikodym](wikipedia://en/338746) derivatives of the measures with same null-sets as the base measure, and are equivalent if they are proportional. In case of finite base measures, [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) structure can be achieved by defining a [centered log-ratio transformation](wikipedia://en/500654#Linear_transformations) on the measures, mapping them to a subset of $L^2(P)$ consisting of functions integrating to 0.[^o12]

*Image: Visualisation of the clr-transformations.jpg.* Visualisation of clr-transfomrations of densities from [Chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) and [Normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), with truncations to $(0,3)$ and $(-2,5)$ respectively.

For data analysis, density functions are typically estimated using so-called ZB-splines[^o13] to smooth over a [histogram](wikipedia://en/13266) of the data, using [Kernel density estimation](wikipedia://en/2090057), or using a combination of the two.

## Definitions and main results {#Definitions_and_main_results}

Consider a finite base measure $P$ (not necessarily a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437)) on a domain $\Omega$. This may be a uniform distribution on a bounded interval, or it can be a Radon-Nikodym derivative of the Lebesgue measure (the [Gaussian distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), for example). In practice, $\Omega$ is often truncated to a bounded interval. If we take two densities $f,g$ with respect to $P$, they are said to be B-equivalent if there exists a $c>0$ s.t $f(x)= c \cdot g(x)$, denoted $f=_B g$ (the convention $c \cdot \infty = \infty$ is used in cases where a measure is infinite). It can be shown that $(=_B)$ is an equivalence relation. The Bayes space $B(P)$ is defined as the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of all measures with the same null-sets in $\Omega$ as $P$ under the equivalence relation $(=_B)$.

The first challenge to analysing density functions is that $B(P)$ is not [linear space](wikipedia://en/32370) under ordinary addition and multiplication since the ordinary difference between two densities would not be non-negative everywhere. Like in the [Aitchison geometry](wikipedia://en/500654) for finite dimensional data, perturbation and powering is defined for densities:

**Perturbation**

$$(f\oplus g) (x)=_Bf(x)\cdot g(x)$$

**Powering**

$$\alpha \odot f(x)=_B f(x)^\alpha$$

where $f(x),\text{ }g(x)$ are densities in $B(P)$ and $\alpha$ is some [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438). It can be shown using the properties of multiplication and powering of real numbers that $(B(P),\oplus,\odot)$ forms a vector space over the real numbers.

The definition of Bayes space does not strictly require a finite reference measure $P$. If Bayes space is defined over an infinite reference measure $\lambda$, it must be $\sigma$-finite (like the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)). The finite reference measure is, however, necessary for adding Hilbert space structure to a subset of $B(P)$. Consider the subspace

$B^p(P)=\{f\in B(P)|\int_\Omega |\log(f(x))|^p dP(x)<\infty\}$. For $p=2$, this is a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) and isometrically isomorphic to the Hilbert space $L_0^2(P)=\{g\in L^2(P):\int_\Omega g(x) dP(x)=0\}$ via the centered log-ratio (clr) transformation $\text{clr}(f(x))=\log(f(x))-\frac{1}{P(\Omega)}\int \log(f(x)) dP(x)\in L_0^2(P)$. The subspace of log-square integrable functions is termed the Bayes Hilbert space. It can be shown that the clr transformation is a linear isomorphism between the two spaces. Defining an [inner product](wikipedia://en/14856) on $B^2(P)$ as the inner product of the clr transformations will provide the Hilbert space structure for $B^2(P)$, obtaining the centered log-ratio transformation as a linear isometry. Specifically, this results in the Aitchison distance between two densities in the same Bayes Hilbert space

$$d(f,g)=\int (\text{clr}(f(x)-\text{clr}(g(x))^2dP(x)$$

which for densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure on an interval $\Omega=[a,b]$ "simplifies" to $d(f,g)=\int_a^b \left(\left[\text{log}(f(x)-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b \text{log}(f(x)) dx\right]-\left[\text{log}(g(x)-\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b \text{log}(g(x)) dx\right]\right)^2dx$.

## Multivariate densities {#Multivariate_densities}

The measure $P$ does not have to be univariate (one-dimensional), but can also be defined as a [product measure](wikipedia://en/2986564) on [Cartesian products](wikipedia://en/24104095), characterising bivariate (two-dimensional) or multivariate densities. The geometric structure of Hilbert spaces can be used to decompose multivariate densities orthogonally into independent and interaction parts using the concept of "geometric marginals".[^o16][^o17][^o18] This decomposition has relations to [copula theory](wikipedia://en/1793003).[^o17] The geometry in $B^2(P)$ defines norms on densities that can be used to quantify "relative simplicial deviance", which is measure of how much of a bivariate distribution can be explained by the interaction part;[^o16] in the multivariate case the relative simplicial deviance can be generalised to the "information composition".[^o17]
