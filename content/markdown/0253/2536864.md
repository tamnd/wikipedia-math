# Dual graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dual graph"}

*Image: Duals graphs.svg.* The red graph is the dual graph of the blue graph, and *vice versa*.

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) discipline of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **dual graph** of a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) $G$ is a graph that has a [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) for each [face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_%28graph_theory%29) of $G$. The dual graph has an [edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) for each pair of faces in $G$ that are separated from each other by an edge, and a [self-loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-loop) when the same face appears on both sides of an edge. Thus, each edge $e$ of $G$ has a corresponding dual edge, whose endpoints are the dual vertices corresponding to the faces on either side of $e$. The definition of the dual depends on the choice of embedding of the graph $G$, so it is a property of plane graphs (graphs that are already embedded in the plane) rather than [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) (graphs that may be embedded but for which the embedding is not yet known). For planar graphs generally, there may be multiple dual graphs, depending on the choice of planar embedding of the graph.

:::

Historically, the first form of graph [duality](wikipedia://en/609737) to be recognized was the association of the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) into pairs of [dual polyhedra](wikipedia://en/8815). Graph duality is a [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) generalization of the geometric concepts of dual polyhedra and [dual tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_tessellation), and is in turn generalized combinatorially by the concept of a [dual matroid](wikipedia://en/23515837). Variations of planar graph duality include a version of duality for [directed graphs](wikipedia://en/19721986), and duality for graphs embedded onto non-planar two-dimensional surfaces.

These notions of dual graphs should not be confused with a different notion, the edge-to-vertex dual or [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of a graph.

The term *[dual](wikipedia://en/609737)* is used because the property of being a dual graph is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/22510359), meaning that if $H$ is a dual of a [connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph) $G$, then $G$ is a dual of $H$. When discussing the dual of a graph $G$, the graph $G$ itself may be referred to as the "primal graph". Many other graph properties and structures may be translated into other natural properties and structures of the dual. For instance, [cycles](wikipedia://en/168609) are dual to [cuts](wikipedia://en/2180494), [spanning trees](wikipedia://en/455770) are dual to the [complements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_set) of spanning trees, and simple graphs (without [parallel edges](wikipedia://en/1774970) or [self-loops](wikipedia://en/1449083)) are dual to [3-edge-connected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-edge-connected_graph).

Graph duality can help explain the structure of [mazes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze) and of [drainage basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin). Dual graphs have also been applied in [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision), [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), [mesh generation](wikipedia://en/3050716), and the design of [integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit).

## Examples {#Examples}

### Cycles and dipoles {#Cycles_and_dipoles}

The unique planar embedding of a [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) divides the plane into only two regions, the inside and outside of the cycle, by the [Jordan curve theorem](wikipedia://en/267444). However, in an $n$-cycle, these two regions are separated from each other by $n$ different edges. Therefore, the dual graph of the $n$-cycle is a [multigraph](wikipedia://en/1774970) with two vertices (dual to the regions), connected to each other by $n$ dual edges. Such a graph is called a multiple edge, linkage, or sometimes a [dipole graph](wikipedia://en/6412575). Conversely, the dual to an $n$-edge dipole graph is an $n$-cycle.[^o5]

### Dual polyhedra {#Dual_polyhedra}

*Image: Dual Cube-Octahedron.svg.* The cube and regular octahedron are dual graphs of each other

According to [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817), every [polyhedral graph](wikipedia://en/24656270) (the graph formed by the vertices and edges of a three-dimensional [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron)) must be planar and [3-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph), and every 3-vertex-connected planar graph comes from a convex polyhedron in this way. Every three-dimensional convex polyhedron has a [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815); the dual polyhedron has a vertex for every face of the original polyhedron, with two dual vertices adjacent whenever the corresponding two faces share an edge. Whenever two polyhedra are dual, their graphs are also dual. For instance the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) come in dual pairs, with the octahedron dual to the cube, the dodecahedron dual to the icosahedron, and the tetrahedron dual to itself.[^o7] Polyhedron duality can also be extended to duality of higher dimensional [polytopes](wikipedia://en/23471),[^o8] but this extension of geometric duality does not have clear connections to graph-theoretic duality.

### Self-dual graphs {#Self-dual_graphs}

*Image: Self-dual graph.svg.* A self-dual graph

A plane graph is said to be **self-dual** if it is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to its dual graph. The [wheel graphs](wikipedia://en/1655876) provide an infinite family of self-dual graphs coming from [self-dual polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-dual_polyhedron) (the [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045)).[^o13] However, there also exist self-dual graphs that are not polyhedral, such as the one shown. (Servatius Christopher 1992) describe two operations, adhesion and explosion, that can be used to construct a self-dual graph containing a given planar graph; for instance, the self-dual graph shown can be constructed as the adhesion of a tetrahedron with its dual.[^o13]

It follows from [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/148420) that every self-dual graph with $n$ vertices has exactly $2n - 2$ edges.[^o14] Every simple self-dual planar graph contains at least four vertices of degree three, and every self-dual embedding has at least four triangular faces.[^o15]

## Properties {#Properties}

Many natural and important concepts in graph theory correspond to other equally natural but different concepts in the dual graph. Because the dual of the dual of a connected plane graph is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to the primal graph,[^o18] each of these pairings is bidirectional: if concept $X$ in a planar graph corresponds to concept $Y$ in the dual graph, then concept $Y$ in a planar graph corresponds to concept $X$ in the dual.

### Simple graphs versus multigraphs {#Simple_graphs_versus_multigraphs}

The dual of a simple graph need not be simple: it may have [self-loops](wikipedia://en/1449083) (an edge with both endpoints at the same vertex) or multiple edges connecting the same two vertices, as was already evident in the example of dipole multigraphs being dual to cycle graphs. As a special case of the cut-cycle duality discussed below, the [bridges](wikipedia://en/1305071) of a planar graph $G$ are in one-to-one correspondence with the self-loops of the dual graph.[^o20] For the same reason, a pair of parallel edges in a dual multigraph (that is, a length-2 cycle) corresponds to a 2-edge [cutset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutset) in the primal graph (a pair of edges whose deletion disconnects the graph). Therefore, a planar graph is simple if and only if its dual has no 1- or 2-edge cutsets; that is, if it is [3-edge-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-edge-connected_graph). The simple planar graphs whose duals are simple are exactly the 3-edge-connected simple planar graphs.[^o21] This class of graphs includes, but is not the same as, the class of [3-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) simple planar graphs. For instance, the figure showing a self-dual graph is 3-edge-connected (and therefore its dual is simple) but is not 3-vertex-connected.

### Uniqueness {#Uniqueness}

*Image: Noniso dual graphs.svg.* Two red graphs are duals for the blue one, but they are not [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577).

Because the dual graph depends on a particular embedding, the dual graph of a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is not unique, in the sense that the same planar graph can have non-[isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) dual graphs.[^o23] In the picture, the blue graphs are isomorphic but their dual red graphs are not. The upper red dual has a vertex with degree 6 (corresponding to the outer face of the blue graph) while in the lower red graph all degrees are less than 6.

[Hassler Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794) showed that if the graph is [3-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) then the embedding, and thus the dual graph, is unique.[^o24] By [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817), these graphs are exactly the [polyhedral graphs](wikipedia://en/24656270), the graphs of convex polyhedra. A planar graph is 3-vertex-connected if and only if its dual graph is 3-vertex-connected. Moreover, a planar [biconnected graph](wikipedia://en/4989397) has a unique embedding, and therefore also a unique dual, if and only if it is a [subdivision](wikipedia://en/353021) of a 3-vertex-connected planar graph (a graph formed from a 3-vertex-connected planar graph by replacing some of its edges by paths).[^o25] For some planar graphs that are not 3-vertex-connected, such as the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) $K_{2,4}$, the embedding is not unique, but all embeddings are isomorphic. When this happens, correspondingly, all dual graphs are isomorphic.

Because different embeddings may lead to different dual graphs, testing whether one graph is a dual of another (without already knowing their embeddings) is a nontrivial [algorithmic](wikipedia://en/775) problem. For biconnected graphs, it can be solved in polynomial time by using the [SPQR trees](wikipedia://en/11220797) of the graphs to construct a [canonical form](wikipedia://en/515096) for the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) of having a shared mutual dual. For instance, the two red graphs in the illustration are equivalent according to this relation. However, for planar graphs that are not biconnected, this relation is not an equivalence relation and the problem of testing mutual duality is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).[^o26]

### Cuts and cycles {#Cuts_and_cycles}

A [cutset](wikipedia://en/2180494) in an arbitrary connected graph is a subset of edges defined from a partition of the vertices into two subsets, by including an edge in the subset when it has one endpoint on each side of the partition. Removing the edges of a cutset necessarily splits the graph into at least two connected components. A minimal cutset (also called a bond) is a cutset with the property that every proper subset of the cutset is not itself a cut. A minimal cutset of a connected graph necessarily separates its graph into exactly two components, and consists of the set of edges that have one endpoint in each component.[^o28] A [simple cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) is a connected [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#subgraph) in which each vertex of the cycle is incident to exactly two edges of the cycle.

In a connected planar graph $G$, every simple cycle of $G$ corresponds to a minimal cutset in the dual of $G$, and vice versa.[^o30] This can be seen as a form of the [Jordan curve theorem](wikipedia://en/267444): each simple cycle separates the faces of $G$ into the faces in the interior of the cycle and the faces of the exterior of the cycle, and the duals of the cycle edges are exactly the edges that cross from the interior to the exterior.[^o31] The [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) of any planar graph (the size of its smallest cycle) equals the [edge connectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-edge-connected_graph) of its dual graph (the size of its smallest cutset).[^o69]

This duality extends from individual cutsets and cycles to [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) defined from them. The [cycle space](wikipedia://en/262243) of a graph is defined as the family of all subgraphs that have even [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) at each vertex; it can be viewed as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the [two-element finite field](wikipedia://en/11137263), with the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) of two sets of edges acting as the vector addition operation in the vector space. Similarly, the [cut space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_space) of a graph is defined as the family of all cutsets, with vector addition defined in the same way. Then the cycle space of any planar graph and the cut space of its dual graph are isomorphic as vector spaces.[^o32] Thus, the [rank](wikipedia://en/17458495) of a planar graph (the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of its cut space) equals the [cyclotomic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_rank) of its dual (the dimension of its cycle space) and vice versa.[^o23] A [cycle basis](wikipedia://en/24778911) of a graph is a set of simple cycles that form a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of the cycle space (every even-degree subgraph can be formed in exactly one way as a symmetric difference of some of these cycles). For [edge-weighted](wikipedia://en/325806#Weighted_graph) planar graphs (with sufficiently general weights that no two cycles have the same weight) the minimum-weight cycle basis of the graph is dual to the [Gomory–Hu tree](wikipedia://en/24093092) of the dual graph, a collection of nested cuts that together include a [minimum cut](wikipedia://en/3562453) separating each pair of vertices in the graph. Each cycle in the minimum weight cycle basis has a set of edges that are dual to the edges of one of the cuts in the Gomory–Hu tree. When cycle weights may be tied, the minimum-weight cycle basis may not be unique, but in this case it is still true that the Gomory–Hu tree of the dual graph corresponds to one of the minimum weight cycle bases of the graph.[^o32]

In directed planar graphs, simple directed cycles are dual to [directed cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_cut) (partitions of the vertices into two subsets such that all edges go in one direction, from one subset to the other). Strongly oriented planar graphs (graphs whose underlying undirected graph is connected, and in which every edge belongs to a cycle) are dual to [directed acyclic graphs](wikipedia://en/204002) in which no edge belongs to a cycle. To put this another way, the [strong orientations](wikipedia://en/16678715) of a connected planar graph (assignments of directions to the edges of the graph that result in a [strongly connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_connected_graph)) are dual to [acyclic orientations](wikipedia://en/36619881) (assignments of directions that produce a [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002)).[^o33] In the same way, [dijoins](wikipedia://en/72418335) (sets of edges that include an edge from each directed cut) are dual to [feedback arc sets](wikipedia://en/1860374) (sets of edges that include an edge from each cycle).[^o34]

### Spanning trees {#Spanning_trees}

*Image: Circularmazeexample.jpg.* A simple maze in which the maze walls and the free space between the walls form two interdigitating trees

A [spanning tree](wikipedia://en/455770) may be defined as a set of edges that, together with all of the vertices of the graph, forms a connected and acyclic subgraph. But, by cut-cycle duality, if a set $S$ of edges in a planar graph $G$ is acyclic (has no cycles), then the set of edges dual to $S$ has no cuts, from which it follows that the complementary set of dual edges (the duals of the edges that are not in $S$) forms a connected subgraph. Symmetrically, if $S$ is connected, then the edges dual to the complement of $S$ form an acyclic subgraph. Therefore, when $S$ has both properties – it is connected and acyclic – the same is true for the complementary set in the dual graph. That is, each spanning tree of $G$ is complementary to a spanning tree of the dual graph, and vice versa. Thus, the edges of any planar graph and its dual can together be partitioned (in multiple different ways) into two spanning trees, one in the primal and one in the dual, that together extend to all the vertices and faces of the graph but never cross each other. In particular, the [minimum spanning tree](wikipedia://en/41795) of $G$ is complementary to the maximum spanning tree of the dual graph.[^o36] However, this does not work for [shortest path trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path_tree), even approximately: there exist planar graphs such that, for every pair of a spanning tree in the graph and a complementary spanning tree in the dual graph, at least one of the two trees has distances that are significantly longer than the distances in its graph.[^o37]

An example of this type of decomposition into interdigitating trees can be seen in some simple types of [mazes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze), with a single entrance and no disconnected components of its walls. In this case both the maze walls and the space between the walls take the form of a mathematical tree. If the free space of the maze is partitioned into simple cells (such as the squares of a grid) then this system of cells can be viewed as an embedding of a planar graph, in which the tree structure of the walls forms a spanning tree of the graph and the tree structure of the free space forms a spanning tree of the dual graph.[^o38] Similar pairs of interdigitating trees can also be seen in the tree-shaped pattern of streams and rivers within a [drainage basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_basin) and the dual tree-shaped pattern of ridgelines separating the streams.[^o39]

This partition of the edges and their duals into two trees leads to a simple proof of [Euler’s formula](wikipedia://en/148420) $V - E + F = 2$ for planar graphs with $V$ vertices, $E$ edges, and $F$ faces. Any spanning tree and its complementary dual spanning tree partition the edges into two subsets of $V - 1$ and $F - 1$ edges respectively, and adding the sizes of the two subsets gives the equation

$$E = (V - 1) + (F - 1)$$

which may be rearranged to form Euler's formula. According to [Duncan Sommerville](wikipedia://en/8276753), this proof of Euler's formula is due to [K. G. C. Von Staudt’s](wikipedia://en/2186050) *Geometrie der Lage* (Nürnberg, 1847).[^o40]

In nonplanar surface embeddings the set of dual edges complementary to a spanning tree is not a dual spanning tree. Instead this set of edges is the union of a dual spanning tree with a small set of extra edges whose number is determined by the genus of the surface on which the graph is embedded. The extra edges, in combination with paths in the spanning trees, can be used to [generate](wikipedia://en/99945) the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the surface.[^o41]

### Additional properties {#Additional_properties}

Any counting formula involving vertices and faces that is valid for all planar graphs may be transformed by planar duality into an equivalent formula in which the roles of the vertices and faces have been swapped. Euler's formula, which is self-dual, is one example. Another given by [Harary](wikipedia://en/1731731) involves the [handshaking lemma](wikipedia://en/9607933), according to which the sum of the [degrees](wikipedia://en/1374448) of the vertices of any graph equals twice the number of edges. In its dual form, this lemma states that in a plane graph, the sum of the numbers of sides of the faces of the graph equals twice the number of edges.[^o43]

The [medial graph](wikipedia://en/28280731) of a plane graph is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to the medial graph of its dual. Two planar graphs can have isomorphic medial graphs only if they are dual to each other.[^o44]

A planar graph with four or more vertices is maximal (no more edges can be added while preserving planarity) if and only if its dual graph is both 3-vertex-connected and [3-regular](wikipedia://en/980508).[^o45]

A connected planar graph is [Eulerian](wikipedia://en/333219) (has even degree at every vertex) if and only if its dual graph is [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431).[^o68] A [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) in a planar graph $G$ corresponds to a partition of the vertices of the dual graph into two subsets (the interior and exterior of the cycle) whose [induced subgraphs](wikipedia://en/1655188) are both trees. In particular, [Barnette's conjecture](wikipedia://en/28059825) on the Hamiltonicity of cubic bipartite polyhedral graphs is equivalent to the conjecture that every Eulerian maximal planar graph can be partitioned into two induced trees.[^o46]

If a planar graph $G$ has [Tutte polynomial](wikipedia://en/3892303) $T_{G}(x,y)$, then the Tutte polynomial of its dual graph is obtained by swapping $x$ and $y$. For this reason, if some particular value of the Tutte polynomial provides information about certain types of structures in $G$, then swapping the arguments to the Tutte polynomial will give the corresponding information for the dual structures. For instance, the number of strong orientations is $T_{G}(0,2)$ and the number of acyclic orientations is $T_{G}(2,0)$.[^o47] For [bridgeless](wikipedia://en/1305071) planar graphs, [graph colorings](wikipedia://en/426743) with $k$ colors correspond to [nowhere-zero flows](wikipedia://en/9909979) modulo $k$ on the dual graph. For instance, the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949) (the existence of a 4-coloring for every planar graph) can be expressed equivalently as stating that the dual of every bridgeless planar graph has a nowhere-zero 4-flow. The number of $k$-colorings is counted (up to an easily computed factor) by the Tutte polynomial value $T_{G}(1 - k,0)$ and dually the number of nowhere-zero $k$-flows is counted by $T_{G}(0,1 - k)$.[^o48]

An [*st*-planar graph](wikipedia://en/47704642) is a connected planar graph together with a [bipolar orientation](wikipedia://en/40147217) of that graph, an orientation that makes it acyclic with a single source and a single sink, both of which are required to be on the same face as each other. Such a graph may be made into a strongly connected graph by adding one more edge, from the sink back to the source, through the outer face. The dual of this augmented planar graph is itself the augmentation of another *st*-planar graph.[^o52]

## Variations {#Variations}

### Directed graphs {#Directed_graphs}

In a [directed](wikipedia://en/19721986) plane graph, the dual graph may be made directed as well, by orienting each dual edge by a 90° clockwise turn from the corresponding primal edge.[^o52] Strictly speaking, this construction is not a duality of directed planar graphs, because starting from a graph $G$ and taking the dual twice does not return to $G$ itself, but instead constructs a graph isomorphic to the [transpose graph](wikipedia://en/5966396) of $G$, the graph formed from $G$ by reversing all of its edges. Taking the dual four times returns to the original graph.

::: {#o54 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Weak dual"}

### Weak dual {#Weak_dual}

The **weak dual** of a [plane graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_graph) is the [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraphs) of the dual graph whose vertices correspond to the bounded faces of the primal graph. A plane graph is [outerplanar](wikipedia://en/352343) if and only if its weak dual is a [forest](wikipedia://en/48560). For any plane graph $G$, let $G^{+}$ be the plane multigraph formed by adding a single new vertex $v$ in the unbounded face of $G$, and connecting $v$ to each vertex of the outer face (multiple times, if a vertex appears multiple times on the boundary of the outer face); then, $G$ is the weak dual of the (plane) dual of $G^{+}$.[^o55]

:::

### Infinite graphs and tessellations {#Infinite_graphs_and_tessellations}

The concept of duality applies as well to [infinite graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_graph) embedded in the plane as it does to finite graphs. However, care is needed to avoid topological complications such as points of the plane that are neither part of an open region disjoint from the graph nor part of an edge or vertex of the graph. When all faces are bounded regions surrounded by a cycle of the graph, an infinite planar graph embedding can also be viewed as a [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) of the plane, a covering of the plane by closed disks (the tiles of the tessellation) whose interiors (the faces of the embedding) are disjoint open disks. Planar duality gives rise to the notion of a [dual tessellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_tessellation), a tessellation formed by placing a vertex at the center of each tile and connecting the centers of adjacent tiles.

*Image: Delaunay Voronoi.svg.* A Voronoi diagram (red) and Delaunay triangulation (black) of a finite point set (the black points)

The concept of a dual tessellation can also be applied to partitions of the plane into finitely many regions. It is closely related to but not quite the same as planar graph duality in this case. For instance, the [Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/177668) of a finite set of point sites is a partition of the plane into [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) within which one site is closer than any other. The sites on the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the input give rise to unbounded Voronoi polygons, two of whose sides are infinite rays rather than finite line segments. The dual of this diagram is the [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864) of the input, a planar graph that connects two sites by an edge whenever there exists a circle that contains those two sites and no other sites. The edges of the convex hull of the input are also edges of the Delaunay triangulation, but they correspond to rays rather than line segments of the Voronoi diagram. This duality between Voronoi diagrams and Delaunay triangulations can be turned into a duality between finite graphs in either of two ways: by adding an artificial vertex [at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) to the Voronoi diagram, to serve as the other endpoint for all of its rays,[^o58] or by treating the bounded part of the Voronoi diagram as the weak dual of the Delaunay triangulation. Although the Voronoi diagram and Delaunay triangulation are dual, their embedding in the plane may have additional crossings beyond the crossings of dual pairs of edges. Each vertex of the Delaunay triangle is positioned within its corresponding face of the Voronoi diagram. Each vertex of the Voronoi diagram is positioned at the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) of the corresponding triangle of the Delaunay triangulation, but this point may lie outside its triangle.

### Nonplanar embeddings {#Nonplanar_embeddings}

The concept of duality can be extended to [graph embeddings](wikipedia://en/8149170) on two-dimensional [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) other than the plane. The definition is the same: there is a dual vertex for each [connected component](wikipedia://en/6233) of the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of the graph in the manifold, and a dual edge for each graph edge connecting the two dual vertices on either side of the edge. In most applications of this concept, it is restricted to embeddings with the property that each face is a topological disk; this constraint generalizes the requirement for planar graphs that the graph be connected. With this constraint, the dual of any surface-embedded graph has a natural embedding on the same surface, such that the dual of the dual is isomorphic to and isomorphically embedded to the original graph. For instance, the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{7}$ is a [toroidal graph](wikipedia://en/1633057): it is not planar but can be embedded in a torus, with each face of the embedding being a triangle. This embedding has the [Heawood graph](wikipedia://en/1396870) as its dual graph.[^o60]

The same concept works equally well for [non-orientable surfaces](wikipedia://en/187446). For instance, $K_{6}$ can be embedded in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) with ten triangular faces as the [hemi-icosahedron](wikipedia://en/10393682), whose dual is the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) embedded as the [hemi-dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/10393833).[^o61]

Even planar graphs may have nonplanar embeddings, with duals derived from those embeddings that differ from their planar duals. For instance, the four [Petrie polygons](wikipedia://en/18571869) of a cube (hexagons formed by removing two opposite vertices of the cube) form the hexagonal faces of an embedding of the cube in a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800). The dual graph of this embedding has four vertices forming a complete graph $K_{4}$ with doubled edges. In the torus embedding of this dual graph, the six edges incident to each vertex, in [cyclic order](wikipedia://en/434673) around that vertex, cycle twice through the three other vertices. In contrast to the situation in the plane, this embedding of the cube and its dual is not unique; the cube graph has several other torus embeddings, with different duals.[^o60]

Many of the equivalences between primal and dual graph properties of planar graphs fail to generalize to nonplanar duals, or require additional care in their generalization.

Another operation on surface-embedded graphs is the [Petrie dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrie_dual), which uses the [Petrie polygons](wikipedia://en/18571869) of the embedding as the faces of a new embedding. Unlike the usual dual graph, it has the same vertices as the original graph, but generally lies on a different surface.[^o62] Surface duality and Petrie duality are two of the six [Wilson operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilson_operation), and together generate the group of these operations.[^o63]

### Matroids and algebraic duals {#Matroids_and_algebraic_duals}

An **algebraic dual** of a connected graph $G$ is a graph $G^{*}$ such that $G$ and $G^{*}$ have the same set of edges, any [cycle](wikipedia://en/262243) of $G$ is a [cut](wikipedia://en/2180494) of $G^{*}$, and any cut of $G$ is a cycle of $G^{*}$. Every planar graph has an algebraic dual, which is in general not unique (any dual defined by a plane embedding will do). The converse is actually true, as settled by [Hassler Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794) in [Whitney's planarity criterion](wikipedia://en/3126156):[^o65]

A connected graph $G$ is planar if and only if it has an algebraic dual.

The same fact can be expressed in the theory of [matroids](wikipedia://en/244321). If $M$ is the [graphic matroid](wikipedia://en/745714) of a graph $G$, then a graph $G^{*}$ is an algebraic dual of $G$ if and only if the graphic matroid of $G^{*}$ is the [dual matroid](wikipedia://en/23515837) of $M$. Then Whitney's planarity criterion can be rephrased as stating that the [dual matroid](wikipedia://en/23515837) of a graphic matroid $M$ is itself a graphic matroid if and only if the underlying graph $G$ of $M$ is planar. If $G$ is planar, the dual matroid is the graphic matroid of the dual graph of $G$. In particular, all dual graphs, for all the different planar embeddings of $G$, have isomorphic graphic matroids.[^o66]

For nonplanar surface embeddings, unlike planar duals, the dual graph is not generally an algebraic dual of the primal graph. And for a non-planar graph $G$, the dual matroid of the graphic matroid of $G$ is not itself a graphic matroid. However, it is still a matroid whose circuits correspond to the cuts in $G$, and in this sense can be thought of as a combinatorially generalized algebraic dual of $G$.[^o67]

The duality between Eulerian and bipartite planar graphs can be extended to [binary matroids](wikipedia://en/36848165) (which include the [graphic matroids](wikipedia://en/745714) derived from planar graphs): a binary matroid is [Eulerian](wikipedia://en/36854652) if and only if its dual matroid is [bipartite](wikipedia://en/36886954).[^o68]

The two dual concepts of girth and edge connectivity are unified in matroid theory by [matroid girth](wikipedia://en/47482411): the girth of the graphic matroid of a planar graph is the same as the graph's girth, and the girth of the dual matroid (the graphic matroid of the dual graph) is the edge connectivity of the graph.[^o69]

## Applications {#Applications}

Along with its use in graph theory, the duality of planar graphs has applications in several other areas of mathematical and computational study.

In [geographic information systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_information_system), flow networks (such as the networks showing how water flows in a system of streams and rivers) are dual to cellular networks describing [drainage divides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_divide). This duality can be explained by modeling the flow network as a spanning tree on a [grid graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_graph) of an appropriate scale, and modeling the drainage divide as the complementary spanning tree of ridgelines on the dual grid graph.[^o72]

In [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision), digital images are partitioned into small square [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel), each of which has its own color. The dual graph of this subdivision into squares has a vertex per pixel and an edge between pairs of pixels that share an edge; it is useful for applications including clustering of pixels into connected regions of similar colors.[^o73]

In [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), the duality between [Voronoi diagrams](wikipedia://en/177668) and [Delaunay triangulations](wikipedia://en/8864) implies that any [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for constructing a Voronoi diagram can be immediately converted into an algorithm for the Delaunay triangulation, and vice versa.[^o74] The same duality can also be used in [finite element](wikipedia://en/18233581) [mesh generation](wikipedia://en/3050716). [Lloyd's algorithm](wikipedia://en/2607912), a method based on Voronoi diagrams for moving a set of points on a surface to more evenly spaced positions, is commonly used as a way to smooth a finite element mesh described by the dual Delaunay triangulation. This method improves the mesh by making its triangles more uniformly sized and shaped.[^o75]

In the [synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_synthesis) of [CMOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMOS) circuits, the function to be synthesized is represented as a formula in [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844). Then this formula is translated into two [series–parallel multigraphs](wikipedia://en/9938559). These graphs can be interpreted as [circuit diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_diagram) in which the edges of the graphs represent [transistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor), gated by the inputs to the function. One circuit computes the function itself, and the other computes its complement. One of the two circuits is derived by converting the conjunctions and disjunctions of the formula into series and parallel compositions of graphs, respectively. The other circuit reverses this construction, converting the conjunctions and disjunctions of the formula into parallel and series compositions of graphs.[^o76] These two circuits, augmented by an additional edge connecting the input of each circuit to its output, are planar dual graphs.[^o77]

## History {#History}

The duality of convex polyhedra was recognized by [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) in his 1619 book *[Harmonices Mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonices_Mundi)*.[^o79] Recognizable planar dual graphs, outside the context of polyhedra, appeared as early as 1725, in [Pierre Varignon](wikipedia://en/1193511)'s posthumously published work, *Nouvelle Méchanique ou Statique*. This was even before [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)'s 1736 work on the [Seven Bridges of Königsberg](wikipedia://en/192753) that is often taken to be the first work on [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). Varignon analyzed the forces on static systems of [struts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strut) by drawing a graph dual to the struts, with edge lengths proportional to the forces on the struts; this dual graph is a type of [Cremona diagram](wikipedia://en/14523851).[^o80] In connection with the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949), the dual graphs of maps (subdivisions of the plane into regions) were mentioned by [Alfred Kempe](wikipedia://en/9092767) in 1879, and extended to maps on non-planar surfaces by Lothar Heffter in 1891.[^o81] Duality as an operation on abstract planar graphs was introduced by [Hassler Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794) in 1931.[^o82]
