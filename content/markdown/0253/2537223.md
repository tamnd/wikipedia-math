# Uniform polyhedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Uniform polyhedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **uniform polyhedron** has [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) as [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) and is [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive)—there is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) mapping any vertex onto any other. It follows that all vertices are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330). Uniform [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) may be [regular](wikipedia://en/333299) (if also [face-](wikipedia://en/6286120) and [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/6289189)), [quasi-regular](wikipedia://en/8810651) (if also edge-transitive but not face-transitive), or [semi-regular](wikipedia://en/1548894) (if neither edge- nor face-transitive). The faces and vertices don't need to be [convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron), so many of the uniform polyhedra are also [star polyhedra](wikipedia://en/7087334).

:::

There are two infinite classes of uniform polyhedra, together with 75 other polyhedra. They are 2 infinite classes of [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007) and [antiprisms](wikipedia://en/1851), the convex polyhedrons as in 5 [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) and 13 [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847)—2 [quasiregular](wikipedia://en/8810651) and 11 [semiregular](wikipedia://en/1548894)— the non-convex star polyhedra as in 4 [Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler–Poinsot_polyhedra) and 53 [uniform star polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_star_polyhedra)—14 [quasiregular](wikipedia://en/8810651#Nonconvex_examples) and 39 semiregular. There are also many degenerate uniform polyhedra with pairs of edges that coincide, including one found by John Skilling called the [great disnub dirhombidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4145225), Skilling's figure.[^n1]

[Dual polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polyhedra) to uniform polyhedra are [face-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-transitive) (isohedral) and have regular [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719), and are generally classified in parallel with their dual (uniform) polyhedron. The dual of a regular polyhedron is regular, while the dual of an Archimedean solid is a [Catalan solid](wikipedia://en/564393).

The concept of uniform polyhedron is a special case of the concept of [uniform polytope](wikipedia://en/6842392), which also applies to shapes in higher-dimensional (or lower-dimensional) space.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Original Sin in the theory of polyhedra goes back to Euclid, and through Kepler, Poinsot, Cauchy and many others continues to afflict all the work on this topic (including that of the present author). It arises from the fact that the traditional usage of the term "regular polyhedra" was, and is, contrary to syntax and to logic: the words seem to imply that we are dealing, among the objects we call "polyhedra", with those special ones that deserve to be called "regular". But at each stage— Euclid, Kepler, Poinsot, Hess, Brückner, ... —the writers failed to define what are the "polyhedra" among which they are finding the "regular" ones.

(Coxeter Longuet-Higgins Miller 1954) define uniform polyhedra to be [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive) polyhedra with regular faces. They define a polyhedron to be a finite set of polygons such that each side of a polygon is a side of just one other polygon, such that no non-empty proper subset of the polygons has the same property. By a polygon they implicitly mean a polygon in 3-dimensional Euclidean space; these are allowed to be non-convex and intersecting each other.[^n2]

There are some generalizations of the concept of a uniform polyhedron. If the connectedness assumption is dropped, then we get uniform compounds, which can be split as a union of polyhedra, such as the compound of 5 cubes. If we drop the condition that the realization of the polyhedron is non-degenerate, then we get the so-called degenerate uniform polyhedra. These require a more general definition of polyhedra. (Grünbaum 1994) gave a rather complicated definition of a polyhedron, while (McMullen Schulte 2002) gave a simpler and more general definition of a polyhedron: in their terminology, a polyhedron is a 2-dimensional [abstract polytope](wikipedia://en/1211056) with a non-degenerate 3-dimensional realization. Here an abstract polytope is a poset of its "faces" satisfying various condition, a realization is a function from its vertices to some space, and the realization is called non-degenerate if any two distinct faces of the abstract polytope have distinct realizations. Some of the ways they can be degenerate are as follows:

- Hidden faces. Some polyhedra have faces that are hidden, in the sense that no points of their interior can be seen from the outside. These are usually not counted as uniform polyhedra.
- Degenerate compounds. Some polyhedra have multiple edges and their faces are the faces of two or more polyhedra, though these are not compounds in the previous sense since the polyhedra share edges.
- Double covers. Some non-orientable polyhedra have double covers satisfying the definition of a uniform polyhedron. There double covers have doubled faces, edges and vertices. They are usually not counted as uniform polyhedra.
- Double faces. There are several polyhedra with doubled faces produced by Wythoff's construction. Most authors do not allow doubled faces and remove them as part of the construction.
- Double edges. Skilling's figure has the property that it has double edges (as in the degenerate uniform polyhedra) but its faces cannot be written as a union of two uniform polyhedra.

## History {#History}

### Regular convex polyhedra {#Regular_convex_polyhedra}

- The [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) date back to the classical Greeks and were studied by the [Pythagoreans](wikipedia://en/291170), [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) (c. 424 – 348 BC), [Theaetetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theaetetus_%28mathematician%29) (c. 417 BC – 369 BC), [Timaeus of Locri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_of_Locri) (c. 420–380 BC), and [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) (fl. 300 BC). The [Etruscans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etruscans) discovered the regular dodecahedron before 500 BC.[^o12]

### Nonregular uniform convex polyhedra {#Nonregular_uniform_convex_polyhedra}

- The [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280) was known by [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954).
- [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) (287 BC – 212 BC) discovered all of the 13 [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847). His original book on the subject was lost, but [Pappus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/509300) (c. 290 – c. 350 AD) mentioned Archimedes listed 13 polyhedra.
- [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca) (1415 – 1492) rediscovered the five truncations of the Platonic solids—truncated tetrahedron, truncated octahedron, truncated cube, truncated dodecahedron, and truncated icosahedron—and included illustrations and calculations of their metric properties in his book *[De quinque corporibus regularibus](wikipedia://en/65240274)*. He also discussed the cuboctahedron in a different book.[^o14]
- [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473) plagiarized Francesca's work in *[De divina proportione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_divina_proportione)* in 1509, adding the [rhombicuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/26213), calling it an *icosihexahedron* for its 26 faces, which was drawn by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci).
- [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) (1571–1630) was the first to publish the complete list of [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847), in 1619. He also identified the infinite families of uniform [prisms and antiprisms](wikipedia://en/11064788).

### Regular star polyhedra {#Regular_star_polyhedra}

- [Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) (1619) discovered two of the regular [Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler–Poinsot_polyhedra), the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843) and [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878).
- [Louis Poinsot](wikipedia://en/764639) (1809) discovered the other two, the [great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441) and [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898).
- The set of four was proven complete by [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) in 1813 and named by [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034) in 1859.

### Other 53 nonregular star polyhedra {#Other_53_nonregular_star_polyhedra}

- Of the remaining 53, [Edmund Hess](wikipedia://en/9191575) (1878) discovered two (the [dodecadodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4157379) and [great icosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4157391)), Albert Badoureau (1881) discovered 37 (including Hess' two), and Pitsch (1881) independently discovered 18, of which four (the [small icosicosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4157496), the [truncated great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4157430), the [truncated great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/4157466), and the [quasitruncated great stellated dodecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasitruncated_great_stellated_dodecahedron)) had not also been discovered by Hess or Badoureau. Together these gave 41 polyhedra.
- The geometer [H.S.M. Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter) discovered the remaining twelve in collaboration with [J. C. P. Miller](wikipedia://en/20706909) (1930–1932) but did not publish. [M.S. Longuet-Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_S._Longuet-Higgins) and [H.C. Longuet-Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.C._Longuet-Higgins) independently discovered eleven of these (all but the [great dirhombicosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2910030)). Lesavre and Mercier rediscovered five of them in 1947.
- (Coxeter Longuet-Higgins Miller 1954) published the list of uniform polyhedra.
- (Sopov 1970) proved their conjecture that the list was complete.
- In 1974, [Magnus Wenninger](wikipedia://en/8864685) published his book [*Polyhedron models*](wikipedia://en/2915428), which lists all 75 nonprismatic uniform polyhedra, with many previously unpublished names given to them by [Norman Johnson](wikipedia://en/715527).
- (Skilling 1975) independently proved the completeness and showed that if the definition of uniform polyhedron is relaxed to allow edges to coincide then there is just one extra possibility (the [great disnub dirhombidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/4145225)).
- In 1987, [Edmond Bonan](wikipedia://en/39597334) drew all the uniform polyhedra and their duals in 3D with a Turbo Pascal program called **Polyca**. Most of them were shown during the International Stereoscopic Union Congress held in 1993, at the Congress Theatre, Eastbourne, England; and again in 2005 at the Kursaal of Besançon, France.[^o20]
- In 1993, Zvi Har'El (1949–2008)[^o21] produced a complete kaleidoscopic construction of the uniform polyhedra and duals with a computer program called **Kaleido** and summarized it in a paper *Uniform Solution for Uniform Polyhedra*, counting figures 1–80.[^o22]
- Also in 1993, R. Mäder ported this Kaleido solution to [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica) with a slightly different indexing system.[^o23]
- In 2002 Peter W. Messer discovered a minimal set of closed-form expressions for determining the main combinatorial and metrical quantities of any uniform polyhedron (and its dual) given only its [Wythoff symbol](wikipedia://en/3691548).[^o24]

## Uniform star polyhedra {#Uniform_star_polyhedra}

The 57 nonprismatic nonconvex forms, with exception of the [great dirhombicosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2910030), are compiled by Wythoff constructions within [Schwarz triangles](wikipedia://en/2069325).

## Convex forms by Wythoff construction {#Convex_forms_by_Wythoff_construction}

*Image: Wythoffian construction diagram.svg.*

*Image: Polyhedron truncation example3.png.* Example forms from the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) and [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635)

The convex uniform polyhedra can be named by [Wythoff construction](wikipedia://en/2950777) operations on the regular form.

In more detail the convex uniform polyhedron are given below by their Wythoff construction within each symmetry group.

Within the Wythoff construction, there are repetitions created by lower symmetry forms. The cube is a regular polyhedron, and a square prism. The [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) is a regular polyhedron, and a triangular antiprism. The [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) is also a *rectified tetrahedron*. Many polyhedra are repeated from different construction sources, and are colored differently.

The Wythoff construction applies equally to uniform polyhedra and [uniform tilings on the surface of a sphere](wikipedia://en/10393861), so images of both are given. The spherical tilings include the set of [hosohedra](wikipedia://en/2316192) and [dihedra](wikipedia://en/6109457) which are degenerate polyhedra.

These symmetry groups are formed from the reflectional [point groups in three dimensions](wikipedia://en/2694525), each represented by a fundamental triangle (*p* *q* *r*), where *p* > 1, *q* > 1, *r* > 1 and 1/*p* + 1/*q* + 1/*r* \< 1.

- [Tetrahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2904472) (3 3 2) – order 24
- [Octahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2900805) (4 3 2) – order 48
- [Icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844) (5 3 2) – order 120
- [Dihedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_symmetry) (*n* 2 2), for *n* = 3,4,5,... – order 4*n*

The remaining nonreflective forms are constructed by [alternation](wikipedia://en/4349117) operations applied to the polyhedra with an even number of sides.

Along with the prisms and their [dihedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_symmetry), the spherical Wythoff construction process adds two *regular* classes which become degenerate as polyhedra : the *[dihedra](wikipedia://en/6109457)* and the *[hosohedra](wikipedia://en/2316192)*, the first having only two faces, and the second only two vertices. The truncation of the regular *hosohedra* creates the prisms.

Below the convex uniform polyhedra are indexed 1–18 for the nonprismatic forms as they are presented in the tables by symmetry form.

For the infinite set of prismatic forms, they are indexed in four families:

1. [Hosohedra](wikipedia://en/2316192) *H*<sub>\2...</sub> (only as spherical tilings)
1. [Dihedra](wikipedia://en/6109457) *D*<sub>\2...</sub> (only as spherical tilings)
1. [Prisms](wikipedia://en/283007) *P*<sub>\3...</sub> (truncated hosohedra)
1. [Antiprisms](wikipedia://en/1851) *A*<sub>\3...</sub> (snub prisms)

### (3 3 2) T<sub>d</sub> tetrahedral symmetry {#(3_3_2)_Td_tetrahedral_symmetry}

## Wythoff construction operators {#Wythoff_construction_operators}

::: {#o56 .table cue=table}

| Operation | Symbol | [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parent | {p,q} t<sub>0</sub>{p,q} |  | Any regular polyhedron or tiling |
| [Rectified](wikipedia://en/2192366) (r) | r{p,q} t<sub>1</sub>{p,q} |  | The edges are fully truncated into single points. The polyhedron now has the combined faces of the parent and dual. Polyhedra are named by the number of sides of the two regular forms: {p,q} and {q,p}, like cuboctahedron for r{4,3} between a cube and octahedron. |
| Birectified (2r) (also [dual](wikipedia://en/8815)) | 2r{p,q} t<sub>2</sub>{p,q} |  | *Image: Dual Cube-Octahedron.svg.* The birectified (dual) is a further truncation so that the original faces are reduced to points. New faces are formed under each parent vertex. The number of edges is unchanged and are rotated 90 degrees. A birectification can be seen as the dual. |
| [Truncated](wikipedia://en/4224429) (t) | t{p,q} t<sub>0,1</sub>{p,q} |  | Each original vertex is cut off, with a new face filling the gap. Truncation has a degree of freedom, which has one solution that creates a uniform truncated polyhedron. The polyhedron has its original faces doubled in sides, and contains the faces of the dual. *Image: Cube truncation sequence.svg.* |
| Bitruncated (2t) (also truncated dual) | 2t{p,q} t<sub>1,2</sub>{p,q} |  | A bitruncation can be seen as the truncation of the dual. A bitruncated cube is a truncated octahedron. |
| [Cantellated](wikipedia://en/6317514) (rr) (Also [expanded](wikipedia://en/6341070)) | rr{p,q} |  | In addition to vertex truncation, each original edge is *beveled* with new rectangular faces appearing in their place. A uniform cantellation is halfway between both the parent and dual forms. A cantellated polyhedron is named as a rhombi-r{p,q}, like rhombicuboctahedron for rr{4,3}. *Image: Cube cantellation sequence.svg.* |
| Cantitruncated (tr) (Also [omnitruncated](wikipedia://en/26245455)) | tr{p,q} t<sub>0,1,2</sub>{p,q} |  | The truncation and cantellation operations are applied together to create an omnitruncated form which has the parent's faces doubled in sides, the dual's faces doubled in sides, and squares where the original edges existed. |
