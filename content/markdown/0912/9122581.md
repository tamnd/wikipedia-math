# Young–Laplace equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Young–Laplace equation"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the **Young–Laplace equation** () is an equation that describes the [capillary pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary_pressure) difference sustained across the interface between two [static fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_statics), such as [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) and [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air), due to the phenomenon of [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) or [wall tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_tension) acting against the curvature of the surface (although use of the latter is only applicable if assuming that the wall is very thin).

:::

*Image: CurvedSurfaceTension.png.* Surface tension forces acting on a tiny (differential) patch of surface. $\delta\theta_{x}$ and $\delta\theta_{y}$ indicate the amount of bend over the dimensions of the patch. Balancing the tension forces with pressure leads to the Young–Laplace equation

The Young–Laplace equation relates the pressure difference to the shape of the surface or wall and it is fundamentally important in the study of static [capillary surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary_surface). It is a statement of [normal stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_stress) balance for static fluids meeting at an interface, where the interface is treated as a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) (zero thickness):

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta p &= -\gamma \nabla \cdot \hat n \\ &= -2\gamma H_f \\ &= -\gamma \left(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\right) \end{aligned}$$

where $\Delta p$ is the [Laplace pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_pressure), the pressure difference across the fluid interface (the exterior pressure minus the interior pressure), $\gamma$ is the [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) (or [wall tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_tension)), $\hat n$ is the unit normal pointing out of the surface, $H_f$ is the [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210#Mean_curvature_in_fluid_mechanics), and $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the principal [radii of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_curvature_%28mathematics%29). Note that only normal stress is considered, because a static interface is possible only in the absence of tangential stress.[^o2]

The equation is named after [Thomas Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_%28scientist%29), who developed the qualitative theory of surface tension in 1805, and [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783) who completed the mathematical description in the following year. It is sometimes also called the Young–Laplace–Gauss equation, as [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) unified the work of Young and Laplace in 1830, deriving both the differential equation and boundary conditions using [Johann Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/268886)'s [virtual work](wikipedia://en/1211986) principles.[^o3]

## Soap films {#Soap_films}

If the pressure difference is zero, as in a soap film without gravity, the interface will assume the shape of a [minimal surface](wikipedia://en/276734).

## Emulsions {#Emulsions}

The equation also explains the energy required to create an [emulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emulsion). To form the small, highly curved droplets of an emulsion, extra energy is required to overcome the large pressure that results from their small radius.

The Laplace pressure, which is greater for smaller droplets, causes the diffusion of molecules out of the smallest droplets in an emulsion and drives emulsion coarsening via [Ostwald ripening](wikipedia://en/3508610).

## Capillary pressure in a tube {#Capillary_pressure_in_a_tube}

*Image: Spherical meniscus.PNG.* Spherical meniscus with wetting angle less than 90°

In a sufficiently narrow (i.e., low [Bond number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_number)) tube of circular cross-section (radius *a*), the interface between two fluids forms a [meniscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meniscus_%28liquid%29) that is a portion of the surface of a sphere with radius *R*. The pressure jump across this surface is related to the radius and the surface tension γ by

$$\Delta p = \frac{2 \gamma}{R}.$$

This may be shown by writing the Young–Laplace equation in spherical form with a [contact angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_angle) boundary condition and also a prescribed height boundary condition at, say, the bottom of the meniscus. The solution is a portion of a sphere, and the solution will exist *only* for the pressure difference shown above. This is significant because there isn't another equation or law to specify the pressure difference; [existence](wikipedia://en/52564#Well-posedness) of solution for one specific value of the pressure difference prescribes it.

The radius of the sphere will be a function only of the [contact angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_angle), θ, which in turn depends on the exact properties of the fluids and the container material with which the fluids in question are contacting/interfacing:

$$R = \frac{a}{\cos \theta}$$

so that the pressure difference may be written as:

$$\Delta p = \frac{2 \gamma \cos \theta}{a}.$$

*Image: CapillaryAction.svg.* Illustration of capillary rise. Red=contact angle less than 90°; blue=contact angle greater than 90°

In order to maintain [hydrostatic equilibrium](wikipedia://en/51776), the induced **capillary pressure** is balanced by a change in height, *h*, which can be positive or negative, depending on whether the wetting angle is less than or greater than 90°. For a fluid of [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) ρ:

$$\rho g h = \frac{2 \gamma \cos \theta}{a}.$$

where *g* is the [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration). This is sometimes known as the **[Jurin's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurin's_law)** or **Jurin height**[^o7] after [James Jurin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Jurin) who studied the effect in 1718.[^o8]

For a water-filled glass tube in [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air) at [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level):

- γ = 0.0728 J/m<sup>2</sup> at 20 °[C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius)
- *θ* = 20° (0.35 [rad](wikipedia://en/26003))
- *ρ* = 1000 kg/m<sup>3</sup>
- *g* = 9.8 m/s<sup>2</sup>

and so the height of the water column is given by:

$$h\approx {{1.4 \times 10^{-5}} \mathrm{m}^2\over a}.$$

Thus for a 2 mm wide (1 mm radius) tube, the water would rise 14 mm. However, for a capillary tube with radius 0.1 mm, the water would rise 14 cm (about 6 [inches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inch)).

## Capillary action and gravity {#Capillary_action_and_gravity}

When including also the effects of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), for a [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) and for a pressure difference between the fluids equal to Δ*p* at the level *h=0*, there is a balance, when the interface is in equilibrium, between Δ*p*, the [hydrostatic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic_pressure) and the effects of [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension). The **Young–Laplace** equation becomes:

$$\Delta p = \rho g h - \gamma \left[ \frac{1}{R_1(h)} + \frac{1}{R_2(h)}\right]$$

Note that the [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) of the fluid-fluid interface now depends on *h*.

The equation can be [non-dimensionalised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondimensionalization) in terms of its characteristic length-scale, the [capillary length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary_length):

$$L_{c} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\rho g}},$$

and **characteristic pressure**

$$p_{c} = \frac {\gamma} {L_{c}} = \sqrt{ \gamma \rho g}.$$

For clean water at [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure), the **capillary length** is ~2 [mm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter).

The non-dimensional equation then becomes:

$$h^*- \Delta p^*= \left[ \frac{1}{{R_1}^{*}(h)} + \frac{1}{{R_2}^{*}(h)}\right].$$

Thus, the surface shape is determined by only one parameter, the over pressure of the fluid, Δ*p*<sup>\*</sup> and the scale of the surface is given by the **capillary length**. The solution of the equation requires an initial condition for position, and the gradient of the surface at the start point.

### Axisymmetric equations {#Axisymmetric_equations}

The (nondimensional) shape, *r*(*z*) of an [axisymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axisymmetric) surface can be found by substituting general expressions for [principal curvatures](wikipedia://en/2072472) to give the [hydrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic) **Young–Laplace equations**:[^o11]

$$\frac{r''}{(1+r'^2)^{{3}/{2}}} - \frac{1}{r(z) \sqrt{1+r'^2} } = z - \Delta p^*$$

$$\frac{z''}{(1+z'^2)^{3/2}} + \frac{z'}{r (1+z'^2)^{{1}/{2}} } = \Delta p^*- z(r).$$

## Application in medicine {#Application_in_medicine}

In [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine) it is often referred to as the **Law of Laplace**, used in the context of [cardiovascular physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiovascular_physiology),[^o13] and also [respiratory physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_physiology), though the latter use is often erroneous.[^o14]

## History {#History}

[Francis Hauksbee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Hauksbee) performed some of the earliest observations and experiments in 1709[^o16] and these were repeated in 1718 by [James Jurin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Jurin) who observed that the height of fluid in a capillary column was a function only of the cross-sectional area at the surface, not of any other dimensions of the column.[^o8][^o17]

[Thomas Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_%28scientist%29) laid the foundations of the equation in his 1804 paper *An Essay on the Cohesion of Fluids*[^o18] where he set out in descriptive terms the principles governing contact between fluids (along with many other aspects of fluid behaviour). [Pierre Simon Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Simon_Laplace) followed this up in *Mécanique Céleste*[^o19] with the formal mathematical description given above, which reproduced in symbolic terms the relationship described earlier by Young.

Laplace accepted the idea propounded by Hauksbee in his book *Physico-mechanical Experiments* (1709), that the phenomenon was due to a force of attraction that was insensible at sensible distances.[^o20][^o21] The part which deals with the action of a [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) on a [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) and the mutual action of two liquids was not worked out thoroughly, but ultimately was completed by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125).[^o22] [Franz Ernst Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Ernst_Neumann) (1798-1895) later filled in a few details.[^o23][^o17][^o24]
