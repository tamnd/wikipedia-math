# Variational analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Variational analysis"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **variational analysis** is the combination and extension of methods from [convex optimization](wikipedia://en/1674411) and the classical [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) to a more general theory.[^n1] This includes the more general problems of [optimization theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_theory), including topics in [set-valued analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set-valued_analysis), e.g. [generalized derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_derivative).

:::

In the [Mathematics Subject Classification](wikipedia://en/3008091) scheme (MSC2010), the field of "Set-valued and variational analysis" is coded by "49J53".[^o3]

## History {#History}

While this area of mathematics has a long history, the first use of the term "Variational analysis" in this sense was in an eponymous book by [R. Tyrrell Rockafellar](wikipedia://en/26300644) and [Roger J-B Wets](wikipedia://en/26298695).[^n1]

## Existence of minima {#Existence_of_minima}

A classical result is that a [lower semicontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_semicontinuous) function on a [compact set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) attains its minimum. Results from variational analysis such as [Ekeland's variational principle](wikipedia://en/21341034) allow us to extend this result of lower semicontinuous functions on non-compact sets provided that the function has a lower bound and at the cost of adding a small perturbation to the function. A smooth variant is known as the Borwein-Press variational principle.[^o7]

## Generalized derivatives {#Generalized_derivatives}

The classical [Fermat's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_theorem_%28stationary_points%29) says that if a differentiable function attains its minimum at a point, and that point is an interior point of its domain, then its [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) must be zero at that point. For problems where a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) must be minimized subject to constraints which can be expressed in the form of other smooth functions being equal to zero, the method of [Lagrange multipliers](wikipedia://en/159974), another classical result, gives necessary conditions in terms of the derivatives of the function.

The ideas of these classical results can be extended to nondifferentiable [convex functions](wikipedia://en/245568) by generalizing the notion of derivative to that of [subderivative](wikipedia://en/3462862). Further generalization of the notion of the derivative such as the [Clarke generalized gradient](wikipedia://en/72966849) allow the results to be extended to nonsmooth [locally Lipschitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_Lipschitz) functions.[^o9]
