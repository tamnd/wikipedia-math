# Ground expression

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ground expression"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), a **ground term** of a [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102) is a [term](wikipedia://en/24885593) that does not contain any [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109). Similarly, a **ground formula** is a [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_formed_formula) that does not contain any variables.

:::

In [first-order logic with identity](wikipedia://en/10983#Equality_and_its_axioms) with constant symbols $a$ and $b$, the [sentence](wikipedia://en/2129527) $Q(a) \lor P(b)$ is a ground formula. A **ground expression** is a ground term or ground formula.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Consider the following expressions in [first order logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_order_logic) over a [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28mathematical_logic%29) containing the constant symbols $0$ and $1$ for the numbers 0 and 1, respectively, a unary function symbol $s$ for the successor function and a binary function symbol $+$ for addition.

- $s(0), s(s(0)), s(s(s(0))), \ldots$ are ground terms;
- $0 + 1, \; 0 + 1 + 1, \ldots$ are ground terms;
- $0+s(0), \; s(0)+ s(0), \; s(0)+s(s(0))+0$ are ground terms;
- $x + s(1)$ and $s(x)$ are terms, but not ground terms;
- $s(0) = 1$ and $0 + 0 = 0$ are ground formulae.

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definitions"}

## Formal definitions {#Formal_definitions}

What follows is a formal definition for [first-order languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_language). Let a first-order language be given, with $C$ the set of constant symbols, $F$ the set of functional operators, and $P$ the set of [predicate symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_symbol).

### Ground term {#Ground_term}

A **ground term** is a [term](wikipedia://en/24885593) that contains no variables. Ground terms may be defined by logical recursion (formula-recursion):

1. Elements of $C$ are ground terms;
1. If $f \in F$ is an $n$-ary function symbol and $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_n$ are ground terms, then $f\left(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_n\right)$ is a ground term.
1. Every ground term can be given by a finite application of the above two rules (there are no other ground terms; in particular, predicates cannot be ground terms).

Roughly speaking, the [Herbrand universe](wikipedia://en/12274200#Herbrand_universe) is the set of all ground terms.

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ground atom"}

### Ground atom {#Ground_atom}

A **ground predicate**, **ground atom** or **ground literal** is an [atomic formula](wikipedia://en/4472066) all of whose argument terms are ground terms.

If $p \in P$ is an $n$-ary predicate symbol and $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_n$ are ground terms, then $p\left(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots, \alpha_n\right)$ is a ground predicate or ground atom.

Roughly speaking, the [Herbrand base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbrand_base) is the set of all ground atoms, while a [Herbrand interpretation](wikipedia://en/7731831) assigns a [truth value](wikipedia://en/161711) to each ground atom in the base.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement cue=named-heading name="Ground formula"}

### Ground formula {#Ground_formula}

A **ground formula** or **ground clause** is a formula without variables.

Ground formulas may be defined by syntactic recursion as follows:

1. A ground atom is a ground formula.
1. If $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are ground formulas, then $\lnot \varphi$, $\varphi \lor \psi$, and $\varphi \land \psi$ are ground formulas.

Ground formulas are a particular kind of [closed formulas](wikipedia://en/2129527).
