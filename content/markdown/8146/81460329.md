# Commuting graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commuting graph"}

In mathematics, the **commuting graph** of a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799), or in particular of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) in which the vertices are elements of the semigroup and there is an edge between any pair of elements that [commute](wikipedia://en/294390) (that is, there is an edge between vertices $x$ and $y$ if and only if $xy=yx$ in the semigroup). Commuting graphs have been used to study groups and semigroups by seeking relationships between the combinatorial structure of the graph and the algebraic structure of the group or semigroup.

:::

Depending on the author, the vertex set may comprise every element of the semigroup, or only the non-[central](wikipedia://en/7125) elements (since the central elements — those elements of a semigroup that commute with every other element — would always form a [complete](wikipedia://en/85816) subgraph, every vertex of which would be [adjacent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacent_%28graph_theory%29) to every vertex of the whole commuting graph). If the central elements are excluded, the commuting graph is usually only defined for non-[abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) groups and non-commutative semigroups (since in these cases the commuting graph would be empty).

For the purposes of this article, the vertices of the commuting graph are the *non-central* elements unless otherwise noted.

## History {#History}

The concept of a commuting graph was first introduced for groups in 1955,[^o3][^o4] although the term 'commuting graph' was not coined until 1983.[^o5][^o6] They played a implicit role in [Bernd Fischer](wikipedia://en/29628162)'s discovery of the [sporadic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_groups) now known as the [Fischer groups](wikipedia://en/327206).[^o7][^o8]

The study of the commuting graphs of semigroups other than groups was initiated in 2011.[^o9][^o10]

## Properties {#Properties}

### Connectedness and diameters {#Connectedness_and_diameters}

It is possible for a commuting graph to be non-[connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) and thus not to have a finite [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341).

For a finite set $X$, the commuting graph of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $\mathcal{S}(X)$ is connected if and only if $|X|$ and $|X|-1$ are non-[prime](wikipedia://en/23666), and the commuting graph of the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $\mathcal{A}(X)$ is connected if and only if $|X|$, $|X|-1$, and $|X|-2$ are non-prime. When connected, the commuting graphs of $\mathcal{S}(X)$ and $\mathcal{A}(X)$ have diameter at most 5.[^o14]

The commuting graph of the [symmetric inverse semigroup](wikipedia://en/18442482) $\mathcal{I}(X)$ is not connected if and only if $|X|$ is an odd prime. When $|X|$ is not an odd prime, it has diameter 4 or 5, and is known to have diameter 4 when $|X|$ is even and diameter 5 when $|X|$ is a power of an odd prime.[^o15]

For every natural number $n$, there is a finite group whose commuting graph is connected and has diameter equal to $n$.[^o16] But if a finite group has trivial center and its commuting graph is connected, then its diameter is at most 10.[^o17]

The commuting graph of a completely simple semigroup is never connected except when it is a group, and if it not a group, its [connected components](wikipedia://en/246223) are the commuting graphs including central elements of its maximal subgroups[^o18] (which, by the Rees–Suschkewitsch theorem, are isomorphic[^o19]).

### Simple groups {#Simple_groups}

Non-abelian [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_group) are uniquely characterized by their commuting graphs, in the sense that if $G$ is a non-abelian finite simple group and $H$ is a group, and the commuting graphs of $G$ and the commuting graph of $H$ are isomorphic (as graphs), then $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic (as groups). This result was conjectured in 2006[^o21][^o22] and proved by different authors[^o23] for sporadic groups,[^o24] alternating groups,[^o25] and groups of Lie type.[^o26]
