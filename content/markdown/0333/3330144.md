# Spin(7)-manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spin(7)-manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Spin(7)-manifold** is an eight-dimensional [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) whose [holonomy group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holonomy_group) is contained in [Spin(7)](wikipedia://en/411231). Spin(7)-manifolds are [Ricci-flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci-flat) and admit a parallel [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276). They also admit a parallel 4-[form](wikipedia://en/221519), known as the Cayley form, which is a [calibrating form](wikipedia://en/19364294) for a special class of submanifolds called Cayley cycles. In fact, the Riemannian metric and the Cayley form determine each other.

:::

## History {#History}

The fact that Spin(7) might possibly arise as the holonomy group of certain Riemannian 8-manifolds was first suggested by the 1955 [classification theorem](wikipedia://en/4576710) of [Marcel Berger](wikipedia://en/12149363), and this possibility remained consistent with the simplified proof of Berger's theorem given by [Jim Simons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Harris_Simons) in 1962. Although not a single example of such a manifold had yet been discovered, [Edmond Bonan](wikipedia://en/39597334) then showed in 1966 that, if such a manifold did in fact exist, it would carry a parallel 4-form, and that it would necessarily be Ricci-flat.[^o3] The first local examples of 8-manifolds with holonomy Spin(7) were finally constructed around 1984 by [Robert Bryant](wikipedia://en/24204031), and his full proof of their existence appeared in the [Annals of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/2785) in 1987.[^o4] Next, complete (but still noncompact) 8-manifolds with holonomy Spin(7) were explicitly constructed by Bryant and S. Salamon in 1989.[^o5] The first examples of [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_manifold) Spin(7)-manifolds were then constructed by [Dominic Joyce](wikipedia://en/1120202) in 1996.[^o6]

## Equivalent definition {#Equivalent_definition}

By definition, a Spin(7)-manifold is a Riemannian manifold with Spin(7) holonomy. However, it is generally easier to work with an equivalent definition based on the existence of a Cayley form: a differential form with certain properties.[^o9]

There are multiple equivalent ways of defining a Cayley form on an 8-manifold *X*. The most succinct is the following. A **Cayley form** is a 4-form $\Phi\in\Omega^4(X)$ such that at each point $p\in X$, the [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_%28group_theory%29) of $\Phi|_p$ is Spin(7). We also require a notion of positivity. Given a vector *v* and a form $\omega$, let $\iota_v\omega$ denote the [interior product](wikipedia://en/2154963). A Cayley form $\Phi\in\Omega^4(X)$ is called **positive** if the [top-dimensional forms](wikipedia://en/1855811) $\Phi\wedge\Phi$ and $\iota_v\iota_u\Phi\wedge\iota_v\iota_u\Phi\wedge\Phi$ induce the same [orientation](wikipedia://en/187446) whenever *u* and *v* are linearly independent. It is called **negative** if it is not positive.

With the above established, one can define a Spin(7)-structure. Let $X$ be a smooth 8-manifold. A **Spin(7)-structure** on $X$ is a 4-form $\Phi\in\Omega^4(X)$ which restricts to a positive Cayley form on each [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807). In this case, the pair $(X,\Phi)$ is called an **almost Spin(7) manifold**. An almost Spin(7) manifold $(X,\Phi)$ is called a **Spin(7)-manifold** if $\Phi$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/404181).

It turns out that every almost Spin(7) manifold admits a Riemannian metric which is *compatible* with $\Phi$ in the sense that

$$\iota_v\iota_u\Phi\wedge\iota_v\iota_u\Phi\wedge\Phi = 6|u\wedge v|^2\operatorname{vol}$$

for any pair of vectors *v* and *u*. When $\Phi$ is closed, this metric has Spin(7) holonomy.

One can also explicitly construct the metric determined by the Cayley form via Karigiannis's formula.[^o10] The metric is defined up to scaling of the Cayley form.
