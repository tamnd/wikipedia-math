# Euler's theorem in geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euler's theorem in geometry"}

*Image: Euler theorem2.svg.* Euler's theorem:$d=|IO| =\sqrt{R (R-2r)}$

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Euler's theorem** states that the distance *d* between the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) and [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is given by

$$d^2=R (R-2r)$$

or equivalently

$$\frac{1}{R-d} + \frac{1}{R+d} = \frac{1}{r},$$

where $R$ and $r$ denote the circumradius and inradius respectively (the radii of the [circumscribed circle](wikipedia://en/161262) and [inscribed circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_circle) respectively). The theorem is named for [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), who published it in 1765. However, the same result was published earlier by [William Chapple](wikipedia://en/62371614) in 1746.

:::

From the theorem follows the **Euler inequality**:

$$R \ge 2r,$$

which holds with equality only in the [equilateral](wikipedia://en/173285) case.

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

*Image: Вики \_.png.* Proof of Euler's theorem

Let $O$ be the center of the circumcircle of triangle $ABC$, and let $I$ be the center of its incircle.

If the ray $AI$ intersects the circumcircle at the point $L$, then $L$ is the midpoint of arc $BC$. Draw the ray $LO$ and denote its intersection with the circumcircle by $M$.

Then $LM$ is a diameter of the circumcircle. Drop the perpendicular $ID$ from the point $I$ to $AB$. Then $ID = r$. Rewrite Euler's formula in the following form:

$$d^2 - R^2 = -2rR$$

Notice that the left-hand side is the [power](wikipedia://en/2094044) of the point $I$ with respect to the circumcircle.

Therefore, it suffices to prove the equality $LI \cdot IA = 2Rr$.

By the [trident lemma](wikipedia://en/49188683), $LI = LB,$ so it suffices to prove that $LB \cdot IA = 2Rr$.

Now observe that $2R = LM$ and $r = ID,$ that is, the required equality can be rewritten as $LB \cdot IA = LM \cdot ID.$ Rewriting it once more, we obtain $LB / LM = ID / IA$.

This equality follows from the [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377) of triangles $\triangle AID$ and $\triangle MLB$.

Indeed, the angles at $B$ and $D$ in these triangles are right angles, while the angles at $A$ and $M$ are equal because they both [subtend](wikipedia://en/462730) the arc $BL$ (moreover, the ratio $LB / LM = ID / IA$ is equal to the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) of the angle $\angle BAL$).

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Stronger version of the inequality"}

## Stronger version of the inequality {#Stronger_version_of_the_inequality}

A stronger version is

$$\frac{R}{r} \geq \frac{abc+a^3+b^3+c^3}{2abc} \geq \frac{a}{b}+\frac{b}{c}+\frac{c}{a}-1 \geq \frac{2}{3} \left(\frac{a}{b}+\frac{b}{c}+\frac{c}{a} \right) \geq 2,$$

where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are the side lengths of the triangle.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Euler's theorem for the escribed circle"}

## Euler's theorem for the escribed circle {#Euler's_theorem_for_the_escribed_circle}

If $r_a$ and $d_a$ denote respectively the radius of the [escribed circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escribed_circle) opposite to the vertex $A$ and the distance between its center and the center of the circumscribed circle, then $d_a^2=R(R+2r_a)$.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Euler's inequality in absolute geometry"}

## Euler's inequality in absolute geometry {#Euler's_inequality_in_absolute_geometry}

Euler's inequality, in the form stating that, for all triangles inscribed in a given circle, the maximum of the radius of the inscribed circle is reached for the equilateral triangle and only for it, is valid in [absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294).

:::
