# Thin lens

*Image: Lens1.svg.* A lens may be considered a thin lens if its thickness is much less than the radii of curvature of its surfaces ($d \ll |R_{1}|$ and $d \ll |R_{2}|$).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thin lens"}

In [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), a **thin lens** is a [lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29) with a thickness (distance along the [optical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_axis) between the two surfaces of the lens) that is negligible compared to the [radii of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_curvature_%28optics%29) of the lens surfaces. Lenses whose thickness is not negligible are sometimes called *thick lenses*.

:::

The **thin lens approximation** ignores optical effects due to the thickness of lenses and simplifies [ray tracing](wikipedia://en/17003295) calculations. It is often combined with the [paraxial approximation](wikipedia://en/2643603) in techniques such as [ray transfer matrix analysis](wikipedia://en/172317).

## Focal length {#Focal_length}

The focal length, *f*, of a lens in air is given by the [lensmaker's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lensmaker's_equation):

$$\frac{1}{f} = (n-1) \left[ \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{(n-1)d}{n R_1 R_2} \right],$$

where *n* is the [index of refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_of_refraction) of the lens material, *R*<sub>1</sub> and *R*<sub>2</sub> are the [radii of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_curvature_%28optics%29) of the two surfaces, and *d* is the thickness of the lens. Here *R*<sub>1</sub> is taken to be positive if the first surface is convex, and negative if the surface is concave. The signs are reversed for the back surface of the lens: *R*<sub>2</sub> is positive if the surface is concave, and negative if it is convex. This is an arbitrary [sign convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_convention); some authors choose different signs for the radii, which changes the equation for the focal length.

For a thin lens, *d* is much smaller than one of the radii of curvature (either *R*<sub>1</sub> or *R*<sub>2</sub>). In these conditions, the last term of the Lensmaker's equation becomes negligible, and the focal length of a thin lens in air can be approximated by[^o3][^o9]

$$\frac{1}{f} \approx \left(n-1\right)\left[ \frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} \right].$$

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Derivation using Snell's law"}

### Derivation using Snell's law {#Derivation_using_Snell's_law}

*Image: Half lens.svg.* Refraction of a thin planoconvex lens

Consider a thin lens with a first surface of radius $R$ and a flat rear surface, made of material with index of refraction $n$.

Applying [Snell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell's_law), light entering the first surface is refracted according to $\sin i = n \sin r_1$, where $i$ is the angle of incidence on the interface and $r_1$ is the angle of refraction.

For the second surface, $n \sin r_2 = \sin e$, where $r_2$ is the angle of incidence and $e$ is the angle of refraction.

For small angles, $\sin x \approx x$. The geometry of the problem then gives:

$$\begin{aligned} e &\approx n r_2 \\ &= n (i - r_1)\\ &\approx n (i - \frac in) \end{aligned}$$

*Image: Focus of thin half lens.svg.* Focusing by a thin planoconvex lens

If the incoming ray is parallel to the optical axis and distance $h$ from it, then

$$\sin i = \frac hR \implies i \approx \frac hR.$$

Substituting into the expression above, one gets

$$e \approx \frac {h}{R} (n-1).$$

This ray crosses the optical axis at distance $f$, given by

$$\tan e = \frac hf \implies e \approx \frac hf$$

Combining the two expressions gives $\frac 1f = \frac 1R (n - 1)$.[^o6]

It can be shown that if two such lenses of radii $R_1$ and $-R_2$ are placed close together, the inverses of the focal lengths can be added up giving the thin lens formula:

$$\frac 1f = \left(n-1\right)\left( \frac 1{R_1} - \frac 1{R_2} \right)$$

:::

## Image formation {#Image_formation}

*Image: thin\_lens\_rays.svg.* Thin lens ray rules

Certain [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28optics%29) follow simple rules when passing through a thin lens, in the [paraxial ray approximation](wikipedia://en/2643603):

- Any ray that enters parallel to the axis on one side of the lens proceeds towards the [focal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_%28optics%29) $f_2$ on the other side (red in figure).
- Any ray that arrives at the lens after passing through the focal point $f_1$ on the front side, comes out parallel to the axis on the other side (blue).
- Any ray that passes through the center of the lens will not change its direction (green).

If three such rays are traced from the same point on an object in front of the lens (such as the top), their intersection will mark the location of the corresponding point on the image of the object. By following the paths of these rays, the relationship between the object distance *s<sub>o</sub>* and the image distance *s<sub>i</sub>* (these distances are with respect to the lens) can be shown to be

$${1\over s_o} + {1\over s_i} = {1\over f}$$

which is known as the *Gaussian thin-lens equation*. The Gaussian thin-lens equation has a sign convention described as follows:[^o8][^o9]

*Sign convention for Gaussian lens equation*

::: {#o10 .table cue=table name="Sign convention for Gaussian lens equation"}

| Parameter | Meaning | \+ Sign | − Sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| *s<sub>o</sub>* | The distance between an object and a lens. | Real object | Virtual object |
| *s<sub>i</sub>* | The distance between an image and a lens. | Real image | Virtual image |
| *f* | The focal length of a lens. | Converging lens | Diverging lens |
| *y<sub>o</sub>* | The height of an object from the optical axis. | Erect object | Inverted object |
| *y<sub>i</sub>* | The height of an image from the optical axis | Erect image | Inverted image |
| *M<sub>T</sub>* | The transverse magnification in imaging (= the ratio of *y<sub>i</sub>* to *y<sub>o</sub>*). | Image upright as object | Image inverted to object |

:::

*Image: thin\_lens\_graph.svg.* Plots of image distance *s<sub>i</sub>* and magnification *M<sub>T</sub>* vs object distance *s<sub>o</sub>* for the Gaussian lens equation – the dashed curve denotes a virtual image

Plots of image distance *s<sub>i</sub>*, and magnification *M<sub>T</sub>* vs object distance *s<sub>o</sub>* are [rectangular hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052#Rectangular_hyperbola) with the vertical [asymptote](wikipedia://en/3107) being *s<sub>o</sub>* = *f*, and the horizontal asymptote being *s<sub>i</sub>* = *f* and *M<sub>T</sub>* = 0, respectively. Their positive and negative branches denote the real and virtual images, respectively.[^o11][^o12]

There are other sign conventions such as the [Cartesian sign convention](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/geoopt/lenseq.html#c2) where the thin lens equation is written as

$${1\over s_o} + {1\over f} = {1\over s_i}.$$

For a thick lens, the same form of lens equation is applicable with the modification that the image and object distances are measured from the [principal planes](wikipedia://en/4268332) of the lens.[^o13]

## Physical optics {#Physical_optics}

In scalar wave optics, a lens is a part which shifts the phase of the wavefront. Mathematically this can be understood as a multiplication of the wavefront with the following function:[^o15]

$$\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{\lambda} \frac{r^2}{2f}\right).$$
