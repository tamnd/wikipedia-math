# Osculating curve

*Image: Osculating circle.svg.* A curve $C$ containing a point $P$ where the [radius of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_of_curvature_%28mathematics%29) equals $r$, together with the tangent line and the osculating circle touching $C$ at $P$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Osculating curve"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), an **osculating curve** is a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) from a given family that has the highest possible order of [contact](wikipedia://en/396068) with another curve. That is, if $F$ is a family of [smooth curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_curve), $C$ is a smooth curve (not in general belonging to $F$), and $P$ is a point on $C$, then an osculating curve from $F$ at $P$ is a curve from $F$ that passes through $P$ and has as many of its [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) (in succession, from the first derivative) at $P$ equal to the derivatives of $C$ as possible.[^o2][^o3]

:::

The term derives from the Latinate root "osculate", to [kiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiss), because the two curves contact one another in a more intimate way than simple [tangency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangency).[^o4]

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Nested Ellipses.svg.* Osculating ellipses – The spiral is not drawn: we see it as the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of points where the ellipses are especially close to each other.

Examples of osculating curves of different orders include:

- The [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) to a curve $C$ at a point $p$, the osculating curve from the family of [straight lines](wikipedia://en/946975). The tangent line shares its first derivative ([slope](wikipedia://en/29368)) with $C$ and therefore has first-order contact with $C$.[^o2][^o3][^o7]
- The [osculating circle](wikipedia://en/1896705) to $C$ at $p$, the osculating curve from the family of [circles](wikipedia://en/6220). The osculating circle shares both its first and second derivatives (equivalently, its slope and [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770)) with $C$.[^o2][^o3][^o7]
- The osculating parabola to $C$ at $p$, the osculating curve from the family of [parabolas](wikipedia://en/23231), has third order contact with $C$.[^o3][^o7]
- The osculating conic to $C$ at $p$, the osculating curve from the family of [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673), has fourth order contact with $C$.[^o3][^o7]

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The concept of osculation can be generalized to higher-dimensional spaces, and to objects that are not curves within those spaces. For instance an [osculating plane](wikipedia://en/2740897) to a [space curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve) is a plane that has second-order contact with the curve. This is as high an order as is possible in the general case.[^o10]

In one dimension, analytic curves are said to osculate at a point if they share the first three terms of their [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) about that point. This concept can be generalized to [superosculation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superosculation), in which two curves share more than the first three terms of their Taylor expansion.

:::
