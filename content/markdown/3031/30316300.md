# Energy distance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Energy distance"}

**Energy distance** is a [statistical distance](wikipedia://en/5187620) between [probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distributions). If X and Y are independent random vectors in ***R***<sup>d</sup> with [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) (cdf) F and G respectively, then the energy distance between the distributions F and G is defined to be the square root of

:::

$$D^2(F, G) = 2\operatorname E\|X - Y\| - \operatorname E\|X - X'\| - \operatorname E\|Y - Y'\| \geq 0,$$

where (X, X', Y, Y') are independent, the cdf of X and X' is F, the cdf of Y and Y' is G, $\operatorname E$ is the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653), and || . || denotes the [length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm) of a vector. Energy distance satisfies all axioms of a metric thus energy distance characterizes the equality of distributions: D(F,G) = 0 if and **only if** F = G. Energy distance for statistical applications was introduced in 1985 by [Gábor J. Székely](wikipedia://en/28171816), who proved that for real-valued random variables $D^2(F, G)$ is exactly twice the [Cramér distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cramér_distance):[^o2]

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty (F(x) - G(x))^2 \, dx.$$

For a simple proof of this equivalence, see Székely (2002).[^o3]

In higher dimensions, however, the two distances are different because the energy distance is rotation invariant while Cramér's distance is not. (Notice that Cramér's distance is not the same as the [distribution-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution-free) [Cramér–von Mises criterion](wikipedia://en/5509769).)

## Generalization to metric spaces {#Generalization_to_metric_spaces}

One can generalize the notion of energy distance to probability distributions on metric spaces. Let $(M, d)$ be a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) with its [Borel sigma algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra) $\mathcal{B} (M)$. Let $\mathcal{P} (M)$ denote the collection of all [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) on the [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(M, \mathcal{B} (M))$. If μ and ν are probability measures in $\mathcal{P} (M)$, then the energy-distance $D$ of μ and ν can be defined as the square root of

$$D^2(\mu, \nu)= 2 \operatorname E[d(X,Y)] - \operatorname E[d(X,X')] - \operatorname E[d(Y,Y')] .$$

This is not necessarily non-negative, however. If $(M, d)$ is a strongly negative definite kernel, then $D$ is a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), and conversely.[^o5] This condition is expressed by saying that $(M, d)$ has negative type. Negative type is not sufficient for $D$ to be a metric; the latter condition is expressed by saying that $(M, d)$ has strong negative type. In this situation, the energy distance is zero [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) X and Y are identically distributed. An example of a metric of negative type but not of strong negative type is the plane with the [taxicab metric](wikipedia://en/408354). All Euclidean spaces and even separable Hilbert spaces have strong negative type.[^o6]

In the literature on [kernel methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_methods) for [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), these generalized notions of energy distance are studied under the name of maximum mean discrepancy. Equivalence of distance based and kernel methods for hypothesis testing is covered by several authors.[^o7][^o8]

## Energy statistics {#Energy_statistics}

A related statistical concept, the notion of **E-statistic** or **energy-statistic**[^o10] was introduced by [Gábor J. Székely](wikipedia://en/28171816) in the 1980s when he was giving colloquium lectures in Budapest, Hungary and at MIT, Yale, and Columbia. This concept is based on the notion of Newton's [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703).[^o11] The idea is to consider statistical observations as [heavenly bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavenly_body) governed by a statistical [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) which is zero only when an underlying statistical [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) is true. Energy statistics are functions of [distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distances) between statistical observations.

Energy distance and **E-statistic** were considered as **N**-distances and **N-statistic** in Zinger A.A., Kakosyan A.V., Klebanov L.B. Characterization of distributions by means of mean values of some statistics in connection with some probability metrics, Stability Problems for Stochastic Models. Moscow, VNIISI, 1989,47-55. (in Russian), English Translation: A characterization of distributions by mean values of statistics and certain probabilistic metrics A. A. Zinger, A. V. Kakosyan, L. B. Klebanov in Journal of Soviet Mathematics (1992). In the same paper there was given a definition of strongly negative definite kernel, and provided a generalization on metric spaces, discussed above. The book[^o5] gives these results and their applications to statistical testing as well. The book contains also some applications to recovering the measure from its potential.

### Testing for equal distributions {#Testing_for_equal_distributions}

Consider the null hypothesis that two random variables, *X* and *Y*, have the same probability distributions: $\mu = \nu$ . For [statistical samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_sample) from *X* and *Y*:

$x_1, \dots, x_n$ and $y_1, \dots, y_m$,

the following arithmetic averages of distances are computed between the X and the Y samples:

$$A:= \frac{1}{nm} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m \| x_i - y_j \|, B:= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \| x_i - x_j \|, C:= \frac{1}{m^2} \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^m \| y_i - y_j\|.$$

The E-statistic of the underlying null hypothesis is defined as follows:

$$E_{n,m}(X, Y) := 2A - B - C$$

One can prove[^o11][^o15] that $E_{n,m}(X, Y) \geq 0$ and that the corresponding population value is zero if and only if *X* and *Y* have the same distribution ($\mu = \nu$). Under this null hypothesis the [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975)

$$T = \frac{nm}{n+m} E_{n,m}(X,Y)$$

[converges in distribution](wikipedia://en/50723) to a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) of independent standard [normal random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_random_variable). Under the [alternative hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_hypothesis) *T* tends to infinity. This makes it possible to construct a consistent [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test), the energy test for equal distributions.[^o13]

The E-coefficient of inhomogeneity can also be introduced. This is always between 0 and 1 and is defined as

$$H = \frac{D^2(F_X,F_Y)}{ 2 \operatorname{\operatorname E}\|X-Y\|} = \frac{2\operatorname E\|X - Y\| - \operatorname E\|X - X'\| - \operatorname E\|Y - Y'\|} {2 \operatorname{\operatorname E}\|X-Y\|},$$

where $\operatorname E$ denotes the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653). *H* = 0 exactly when *X* and *Y* have the same distribution.

### Goodness-of-fit {#Goodness-of-fit}

A multivariate goodness-of-fit measure is defined for distributions in arbitrary dimension (not restricted by sample size). The energy goodness-of-fit statistic is

$$Q_n = n \left( \frac{2}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname E \|x_i - X\|^\alpha - \operatorname E\|X - X'\|^\alpha - \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \|x_i - x_j\|^\alpha \right),$$

where X and X' are independent and identically distributed according to the hypothesized distribution, and $\alpha \in (0,2)$. The only required condition is that X has finite $\alpha$ moment under the null hypothesis. Under the null hypothesis $\operatorname EQ_n=\operatorname E\|X-X'\|^\alpha$, and the [asymptotic distribution](wikipedia://en/3001287) of Q<sub>n</sub> is a quadratic form of centered Gaussian random variables. Under an alternative hypothesis, Q<sub>n</sub> tends to infinity stochastically, and thus determines a statistically consistent test. For most applications the exponent 1 (Euclidean distance) can be applied. The important special case of testing [multivariate normality](wikipedia://en/50347)[^o15] is implemented in the *energy* package for R. Tests are also developed for heavy tailed distributions such as Pareto ([power law](wikipedia://en/24522)), or [stable distributions](wikipedia://en/1117869) by application of exponents in (0,1).

## Applications {#Applications}

Applications include:

- [Hierarchical clustering](wikipedia://en/477573) (a generalization of Ward's method)[^o18][^o19]
- Testing multivariate normality[^o15]
- Testing the multi-sample hypothesis of equal distributions,[^o20][^o21][^o22]
- [Change point detection](wikipedia://en/10543268)[^o23]
- Multivariate independence:
  - [distance correlation](wikipedia://en/28217722),[^o24]
  - [Brownian covariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_covariance).[^o25]
- [Scoring rules](wikipedia://en/4079010):

Gneiting and Raftery[^o26] apply energy distance to develop a new and very general type of proper scoring rule for probabilistic predictions, the energy score.

- [Robust statistics](wikipedia://en/2885691)[^o27]
- Scenario reduction[^o28]
- Gene selection[^o29]
- Microarray data analysis[^o30][^o31][^o32]
- Material structure analysis[^o33]
- Morphometric and chemometric data[^o34]

Applications of energy statistics are implemented in the open source ***energy*** package[^o35] for [R](wikipedia://en/376707).
