# Bures metric

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bures metric"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of quantum [information geometry](wikipedia://en/487312), the **Bures metric** (named after Donald Bures)[^o2] or **Helstrom metric** (named after [Carl W. Helstrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_W._Helstrom))[^o3] defines an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) distance between [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) operators defining [quantum states](wikipedia://en/30876419). It is a quantum generalization of the [Fisher information metric](wikipedia://en/488687), and is identical to the [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486)[^o4] when restricted to the pure states alone.

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Bures [metric](wikipedia://en/195795) may be defined as

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{2}\mbox{tr}( d \rho G ),$$

where $G$ is the Hermitian 1-form operator implicitly given by

$$\rho G + G \rho = d \rho,$$

which is a special case of a [continuous Lyapunov equation](wikipedia://en/984752).

Some of the applications of the Bures metric include that given a target error, it allows the calculation of the minimum number of measurements to distinguish two different states[^o7] and the use of the [volume element](wikipedia://en/1920694) as a candidate for the [Jeffreys prior](wikipedia://en/1901158) probability density[^o8] for mixed quantum states.

:::

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bures distance"}

## Bures distance {#Bures_distance}

The Bures distance is the finite version of the infinitesimal square distance described above and is given by

$$D_\text{B}(\rho_1,\rho_2)^2 = 2 \left[1 - \sqrt{F(\rho_1, \rho_2)}\right],$$

where $F$ is the [fidelity](wikipedia://en/5698171), and it is defined as[^o11]

$$F(\rho_1,\rho_2) = \Big[\mbox{tr}\Big( \sqrt{ \sqrt{\rho_1}\rho_2\sqrt{\rho_1}}\Big)\Big]^2.$$

Another associated function is the Bures arc also known as Bures angle, Bures length or [quantum angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_angle), defined as

$$D_\text{A}(\rho_1,\rho_2) = \arccos \sqrt{F(\rho_1,\rho_2)},$$

which is a measure of the **statistical distance**[^o12] between quantum states.

:::

## Wootters distance {#Wootters_distance}

When both density operators are diagonal (so that they are just classical probability distributions), then let $\rho_1 = \mathrm{diag}(p_1, \dots)$ and similarly $\rho_2 = \mathrm{diag}(q_1, \dots)$, then the fidelity is

$$\sqrt{F} = \sum_i \sqrt{p_i q_i}$$

with the Bures length becoming the **Wootters distance** $\textstyle \arccos \left(\sum_i \sqrt{p_i q_i}\right)$. The Wootters distance is the geodesic distance between the probability distributions $p, q$ under the [chi-squared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-squared_divergence) metric[^o7] $\textstyle ds^2 = \frac 12 \sum_i \frac{dp_i^2}{p_i}$.

Perform a change of variables with $x_i := \sqrt{p_i}$, then the chi-squared metric becomes $\textstyle ds^2 = \sum_i dx_i^2$. Since $\textstyle \sum_i x_i^2 = \sum_i p_i = 1$, the points $x$ are restricted to move on the positive quadrant of a unit hypersphere. So, the geodesics are just the great circles on the hypersphere, and we also obtain the Wootters distance formula.

If both density operators are pure states, $\psi$, $\phi$, then the fidelity is $\sqrt{F} = \vert\langle \psi \vert \phi \rangle \vert$, and we obtain the quantum version of Wootters distance $\arccos (\vert\langle \psi \vert \phi \rangle \vert)$.[^o14]

In particular, the direct Bures distance between any two orthogonal states is $\sqrt 2$, while the Bures distance summed along the geodesic path connecting them is $\pi/2$.

## Quantum Fisher information {#Quantum_Fisher_information}

The Bures metric can be seen as the quantum equivalent of the Fisher information metric and can be rewritten in terms of the variation of coordinate parameters as

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{2} \mbox{tr}\left( \frac{d \rho}{d \theta^{\mu}} L_{\nu} \right) d \theta^{\mu} d\theta^{\nu},$$

which holds as long as $\rho$ and $\rho+d\rho$ have the same rank. In cases where they do not have the same rank, there is an additional term on the right hand side.[^o16][^o17] $L_\mu$ is the [symmetric logarithmic derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_logarithmic_derivative) operator (SLD) defined from[^o18]

$$\frac{\rho L_{\mu} + L_{\mu} \rho}{2} = \frac{d \rho^{\,}}{d \theta^{\mu}}.$$

In this way, one has

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{2} \mbox{tr}\left[ \rho \frac{L_{\mu} L_{\nu} + L_{\nu} L_{\mu}}{2} \right] d \theta^{\mu} d\theta^{\nu},$$

where the quantum Fisher metric (tensor components) is identified as

$$J_{\mu \nu} = \mbox{tr}\left[ \rho \frac{L_{\mu} L_{\nu} + L_{\nu} L_{\mu}}{2}\right].$$

The definition of the SLD implies that the quantum Fisher metric is 4 times the Bures metric. In other words, given that $g_{\mu\nu}$ are components of the Bures metric tensor, one has

$$J_{\mu\nu}^{ } = 4 g_{\mu \nu}.$$

As it happens with the classical Fisher information metric, the quantum Fisher metric can be used to find the [Cramér–Rao bound](wikipedia://en/581124) of the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059).

::: {#o20 .statement cue=named-heading name="Explicit formulas"}

## Explicit formulas {#Explicit_formulas}

The actual computation of the Bures metric is not evident from the definition, so, some formulas were developed for that purpose. For 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 systems, respectively, the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) of the Bures metric is calculated as[^o21]

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{4}\mbox{tr}\left[ d \rho d \rho + \frac{1}{\det(\rho)}(\mathbf{1}-\rho)d\rho (\mathbf{1}-\rho)d\rho \right],$$

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{4}\mbox{tr}\left[ d \rho d \rho + \frac{3}{1-\mbox{tr} \rho^3} (\mathbf{1}-\rho)d\rho (\mathbf{1}-\rho)d\rho + \frac{3 \det{\rho} }{1-\mbox{tr} \rho^3} (\mathbf{1}-\rho^{-1})d\rho (\mathbf{1}-\rho^{-1})d\rho \right].$$

For general systems, the Bures metric can be written in terms of the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the density matrix $\textstyle \rho=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j|j\rangle\langle j|$ as[^o22][^o23]

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j,k=1}^{n} \frac{|\langle j| d\rho | k\rangle |^2}{\lambda_j+\lambda_k},$$

as an integral,[^o18]

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty\text{tr}[e^{-\rho t}d\rho e^{-\rho t}d\rho]\ dt,$$

or in terms of [Kronecker product](wikipedia://en/712430) and [vectorization](wikipedia://en/7136985),[^o24]

$$[D_\text{B}(\rho, \rho+d\rho)]^2 = \frac{1}{2}\text{vec}[d\rho]^\dagger\big(\rho^*\otimes \mathbf{1}+\mathbf{1}\otimes\rho\big)^{-1}\text{vec}[d\rho],$$

where $^*$ denotes [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918), and $^\dagger$ denotes [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548). This formula holds for invertible density matrices. For non-invertible density matrices, the inverse above is substituted by the [Moore–Penrose inverse](wikipedia://en/331258). Alternatively, the expression can be also computed by performing a limit on a certain mixed and thus invertible state.

:::

## Two-level system {#Two-level_system}

The state of a two-level system can be parametrized with three variables as

$$\rho = \frac{1}{2}( I + \boldsymbol{r\cdot\sigma} ),$$

where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ is the vector of [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) and $\boldsymbol{r}$ is the (three-dimensional) Bloch vector satisfying $r^2 ~\stackrel{\mathrm{def} }{=}~ \boldsymbol{r\cdot r} \le 1$. The components of the Bures metric in this parametrization can be calculated as

$$\mathsf{g} = \frac{\mathsf{I}}{4}+\frac{\boldsymbol{r\otimes r}}{4(1-r^2)} .$$

The Bures measure can be calculated by taking the square root of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) to find

$$dV_\text{B} = \frac{d^3\boldsymbol{r}}{8\sqrt{ 1 - r^2}},$$

which can be used to calculate the Bures volume as

$$V_\text{B} = \iiint_{r^2\leq 1}\frac{d^3\boldsymbol{r}}{8\sqrt{1-r^2}} = \frac{\pi^2}{8} .$$

## Three-level system {#Three-level_system}

The state of a three-level system can be parametrized with eight variables as

$$\rho = \frac{1}{3}( I + \sqrt{3} \sum_{\nu=1}^8\xi_\nu\lambda_\nu),$$

where $\lambda_\nu$ are the eight [Gell-Mann matrices](wikipedia://en/563694) and $\boldsymbol \xi \in\mathbb{R}^8$ the 8-dimensional Bloch vector satisfying certain constraints.
