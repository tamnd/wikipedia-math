# Borel functional calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Borel functional calculus"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Borel functional calculus** is a *[functional calculus](wikipedia://en/965348)* (that is, an assignment of [operators](wikipedia://en/22213) from [commutative algebras](wikipedia://en/245990) to functions defined on their [spectra](wikipedia://en/37291)), which has particularly broad scope.[^o2][^o3][^o4] Thus for instance if *T* is an operator, applying the squaring function *s* → *s*<sup>2</sup> to *T* yields the operator *T*<sup>2</sup>. Using the functional calculus for larger classes of functions, we can for example define rigorously the "square root" of the (negative) [Laplacian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian_operator) $-\Delta$ or the exponential $e^{it \Delta}.$

:::

The 'scope' here means the kind of *function of an operator* which is allowed. The Borel functional calculus is more general than the [continuous functional calculus](wikipedia://en/784808), and its focus is different than the [holomorphic functional calculus](wikipedia://en/3131706).

More precisely, the Borel functional calculus allows for applying an arbitrary [Borel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_function) to a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408), in a way that generalizes applying a [polynomial function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function).

## Motivation {#Motivation}

If *T* is a self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) *H*, then *H* has an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) $\{e_{1}, ..., e_{\ell}\}$ consisting of [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of *T*, that is

$$T e_k = \lambda_k e_k, \qquad 1 \leq k \leq \ell.$$

Thus, for any positive integer *n*,

$$T^n e_k = \lambda_k^n e_k.$$

If only polynomials in *T* are considered, then one gets the [holomorphic functional calculus](wikipedia://en/3131706). The relation also holds for more general functions of *T*. Given a [Borel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_function) *h*, one can define an operator *h*(*T*) by specifying its behavior on the basis:

$$h(T) e_k = h(\lambda_k) e_k.$$

Generally, any self-adjoint operator *T* is [unitarily equivalent](wikipedia://en/187408) to a multiplication operator; this means that for many purposes, *T* can be considered as an operator

$$[T \psi](x) = f(x) \psi(x)$$

acting on *L*<sup>2</sup> of some [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270). The domain of *T* consists of those functions whose above expression is in *L*<sup>2</sup>. In such a case, one can define analogously

$$[h(T) \psi](x) = [h \circ f](x) \psi(x).$$

For many technical purposes, the previous formulation is good enough. However, it is desirable to formulate the functional calculus in a way that does not depend on the particular representation of *T* as a multiplication operator. That's what we do in the next section.

## The bounded functional calculus {#The_bounded_functional_calculus}

Formally, the bounded Borel functional calculus of a self adjoint operator *T* on [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) *H* is a mapping defined on the space of bounded complex-valued Borel functions *f* on the real line,

$$\begin{cases} \pi_T: L^\infty(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}) \to \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\\ f \mapsto f(T) \end{cases}$$

such that the following conditions hold

- $\pi_{T}$ is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450)-preserving and unit-preserving homomorphism from the ring of complex-valued bounded measurable functions on **R**.
- If ξ is an element of *H*, then $$\nu_\xi:E \mapsto \langle \pi_T(\mathbf{1}_E) \xi, \xi \rangle$$ is a [countably additive measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_additive_measure) on the Borel sets *E* of **R**. In the above formula **1**<sub>*E*</sub> denotes the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of *E*. These measures ν<sub>ξ</sub> are called the **spectral measures** of *T*.
- If $\eta$ denotes the mapping *z* → *z* on **C**, then: $$\pi_T \left ([\eta +i]^{-1} \right ) = [T + i]^{-1}.$$

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Any self-adjoint operator *T* has a unique Borel functional calculus.

:::

This defines the functional calculus for *bounded* functions applied to possibly *unbounded* self-adjoint operators. Using the bounded functional calculus, one can prove part of the [Stone's theorem on one-parameter unitary groups](wikipedia://en/893698):

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If *A* is a self-adjoint operator, then

$$U_t = e^{i t A}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}$$

is a 1-parameter strongly continuous unitary group whose [infinitesimal generator](wikipedia://en/17945#The_Lie_algebra_associated_with_a_Lie_group) is *iA*.

:::

As an application, we consider the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874), or equivalently, the [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29) of a quantum mechanical system. In [non-relativistic](wikipedia://en/30001) [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) operator *H* models the total [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) [observable](wikipedia://en/294248) of a quantum mechanical system **S**. The unitary group generated by *iH* corresponds to the time evolution of **S**.

We can also use the Borel functional calculus to abstractly solve some linear [initial value problems](wikipedia://en/498383) such as the heat equation, or Maxwell's equations.

### Existence of a functional calculus {#Existence_of_a_functional_calculus}

The existence of a mapping with the properties of a functional calculus requires proof. For the case of a bounded self-adjoint operator *T*, the existence of a Borel functional calculus can be shown in an elementary way as follows:

First pass from polynomial to [continuous functional calculus](wikipedia://en/784808) by using the [Stone–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858). The crucial fact here is that, for a bounded self adjoint operator *T* and a polynomial *p*,

$$\| p(T) \| = \sup_{\lambda \in \sigma(T)} |p(\lambda)|.$$

Consequently, the mapping

$$p \mapsto p(T)$$

is an isometry and a densely defined homomorphism on the ring of polynomial functions. Extending by continuity defines *f*(*T*) for a continuous function *f* on the spectrum of *T*. The [Riesz-Markov theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesz-Markov_theorem) then allows us to pass from integration on continuous functions to [spectral measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_measure), and this is the Borel functional calculus.

Alternatively, the continuous calculus can be obtained via the [Gelfand transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelfand_transform), in the context of commutative Banach algebras. Extending to measurable functions is achieved by applying Riesz-Markov, as above. In this formulation, *T* can be a [normal operator](wikipedia://en/190167).

Given an operator *T*, the range of the continuous functional calculus *h* → *h*(*T*) is the (abelian) C\*-algebra *C*(*T*) generated by *T*. The Borel functional calculus has a larger range, that is the closure of *C*(*T*) in the [weak operator topology](wikipedia://en/458698), a (still abelian) [von Neumann algebra](wikipedia://en/294185).

## The general functional calculus {#The_general_functional_calculus}

We can also define the functional calculus for not necessarily bounded Borel functions *h*; the result is an operator which in general fails to be bounded. Using the multiplication by a function *f* model of a self-adjoint operator given by the spectral theorem, this is multiplication by the composition of *h* with *f*.

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let *T* be a self-adjoint operator on *H*, *h* a real-valued Borel function on **R**. There is a unique operator *S* such that

$$\operatorname{dom} S = \left\{\xi \in H: h \in L^2_{\nu_\xi}(\mathbb{R}) \right\}$$

$$\langle S \xi, \xi \rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}} h(t) \ d\nu_{\xi} (t), \quad \text{for} \quad \xi \in \operatorname{dom} S$$

:::

The operator *S* of the previous theorem is denoted *h*(*T*).

More generally, a Borel functional calculus also exists for (bounded) normal operators.

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="Resolution of the identity"}

## Resolution of the identity {#Resolution_of_the_identity}

Let $T$ be a self-adjoint operator. If $E$ is a Borel subset of **R**, and $\mathbf{1}_E$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of *E*, then $\mathbf{1}_E(T)$ is a self-adjoint projection on *H*. Then mapping

$$\Omega_T: E \mapsto \mathbf{1}_E(T)$$

is a [projection-valued measure](wikipedia://en/1046155). The measure of **R** with respect to $\Omega_T$ is the identity operator on *H*. In other words, the identity operator can be expressed as the spectral integral

$$I = \Omega_T([-\infty,\infty]) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d\Omega_T.$$

Stone's formula[^o14] expresses the spectral measure $\Omega_T$ in terms of the [resolvent](wikipedia://en/3378540) $R_T(\lambda) \equiv \left(T-\lambda I \right)^{-1}$:

$$\frac{1}{2\pi i} \lim_{\epsilon \to 0^+} \int_a^b \left[ R_T(\lambda+i\epsilon)) - R_T(\lambda-i\epsilon) \right] \, d\lambda = \Omega_T((a,b)) + \frac{1}{2}\left[ \Omega_T(\{a\}) + \Omega_T(\{b\}) \right].$$

Depending on the source, the **resolution of the identity** is defined, either as a projection-valued measure $\Omega_T$,[^o15] or as a one-parameter family of projection-valued measures $\{\Sigma_\lambda\}$ with $-\infty < \lambda < \infty$.[^o16]

In the case of a discrete measure (in particular, when *H* is finite-dimensional), $I = \int 1\,d\Omega_T$ can be written as

$$I = \sum_{i} \left | i \right \rangle \left \langle i \right |$$

in the Dirac notation, where each $|i\rangle$ is a normalized eigenvector of *T*. The set $\{ |i\rangle \}$ is an orthonormal basis of *H*.

In physics literature, using the above as heuristic, one passes to the case when the spectral measure is no longer discrete and write the resolution of identity as

$$I = \int\!\! di~ |i\rangle \langle i|$$

and speak of a "continuous basis", or "continuum of basis states", $\{ |i\rangle \}$ Mathematically, unless rigorous justifications are given, this expression is purely formal.

:::
