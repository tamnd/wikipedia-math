# Poincaré map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poincaré map"}

*Image: Forced Duffing equation Poincaré section.png.* A two-dimensional Poincaré section of the forced [Duffing equation](wikipedia://en/2987943)

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems), a **first recurrence map** or **Poincaré map**, named after [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), is the intersection of a [periodic orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit) in the [state space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space) of a [continuous dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dynamical_system) with a certain lower-dimensional subspace, called the **Poincaré section**, [transversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29) to the [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735) of the system. More precisely, one considers a periodic orbit with initial conditions within a section of the space, which leaves that section afterwards, and observes the point at which this orbit first returns to the section. One then creates a [map](wikipedia://en/516931) to send the first point to the second, hence the name *first recurrence map*. The transversality of the Poincaré section means that periodic orbits starting on the subspace flow through it and not parallel to it.

:::

A Poincaré map can be interpreted as a [discrete dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_dynamical_system) with a state space that is one dimension smaller than the original continuous dynamical system. Because it preserves many properties of periodic and quasiperiodic orbits of the original system and has a lower-dimensional state space, it is often used for analyzing the original system in a simpler way. In practice this is not always possible as there is no general method to construct a Poincaré map.

A Poincaré map differs from a [recurrence plot](wikipedia://en/546120) in that space, not time, determines when to plot a point. For instance, the locus of the Moon when the Earth is at [perihelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelion) is a recurrence plot; the locus of the Moon when it passes through the plane perpendicular to the Earth's orbit and passing through the Sun and the Earth at perihelion is a Poincaré map. It was used by [Michel Hénon](wikipedia://en/958653) to study the motion of stars in a [galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy), because the path of a star projected onto a plane looks like a tangled mess, while the Poincaré map shows the structure more clearly.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Poincare map.svg.* In the Poincaré section *S*, the Poincaré map *P* projects a point *x* onto the point *P*(*x*).

Let (**R**, *M*, *φ*) be a [global dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_dynamical_system), with **R** the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), *M* the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) and *φ* the [evolution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_function). Let γ be a [periodic orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit) through a point *p* and *S* be a local differentiable and transversal section of *φ* through *p*, called a **Poincaré section** through *p*.

Given an open and connected [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) $U \subset S$ of *p*, a [function](wikipedia://en/185427)

$$P: U \to S$$

is called **Poincaré map** for the orbit γ on the **Poincaré section** *S* through the point *p* if

- *P*(*p*) = *p*
- *P*(*U*) is a neighborhood of *p* and *P*:*U* → *P*(*U*) is a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564)
- for every point *x* in *U*, the [positive semi-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semi-orbit) of *x* intersects *S* for the first time at *P*(*x*)

:::

## Example {#Example}

Consider the following system of differential equations in polar coordinates, $(\theta, r)\in \mathbb{S}^1\times \mathbb{R}^+$:

$$\begin{cases} \dot{\theta} = 1\\ \dot{r} = (1-r^2)r \end{cases}$$

The flow of the system can be obtained by integrating the equation: for the $\theta$ component we simply have $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + t$ while for the $r$ component we need to separate the variables and integrate:

$$\int \frac{1}{(1-r^2)r} dr = \int dt \Longrightarrow \log\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}\right) = t+c$$

Inverting last expression gives

$$r(t) = \sqrt{\frac{e^{2(t+c)}}{1+e^{2(t+c)}}}$$

and since

$$r(0)=\sqrt{\frac{e^{2c}}{1+e^{2c}}}$$

we find

$$r(t) = \sqrt{\frac{e^{2t}r_0^2}{1+r_0^2(e^{2t}-1)}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{1+e^{-2t}\left(\frac{1}{r_0^2}-1\right)}}$$

The flow of the system is therefore

$$\Phi_t(\theta, r) = \left(\theta_0 + t, \sqrt{\frac{1}{1+e^{-2t}\left(\frac{1}{r_0^2}-1\right)}}\right)$$

The behaviour of the flow is the following:

- The angle $\theta$ increases monotonically and at constant rate.
- The radius $r$ tends to the equilibrium $\bar{r}=1$ for every value.

Therefore, the solution with initial data $(\theta_0, r_0\neq 1)$ draws a spiral that tends towards the radius 1 circle.

We can take as Poincaré section for this flow the positive horizontal axis, namely $\Sigma = \{(\theta, r) \ : \ \theta =0 \}$: obviously we can use $r$ as coordinate on the section. Every point in $\Sigma$ returns to the section after a time $t=2\pi$ (this can be understood by looking at the evolution of the angle): we can take as Poincaré map the restriction of $\Phi$ to the section $\Sigma$ computed at the time $2\pi$, $\Phi_{2\pi}|_{\Sigma}$. The Poincaré map is therefore :$\Psi(r) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{1+e^{-4\pi}\left(\frac{1}{r^2}-1\right)}}$

The behaviour of the orbits of the discrete dynamical system $(\Sigma, \mathbb{Z}, \Psi)$ is the following:

- The point $r=1$ is fixed, so $\Psi^n(1)=1$ for every $n$.
- Every other point tends monotonically to the equilibrium, $\Psi^n(z) \to 1$ for $n\to \pm \infty$.

:::

## Poincaré maps and stability analysis {#Poincaré_maps_and_stability_analysis}

Poincaré maps can be interpreted as a [discrete dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_dynamical_system). The [stability](wikipedia://en/5493795) of a periodic orbit of the original system is closely related to the stability of the fixed point of the corresponding Poincaré map.

Let (**R**, *M*, *φ*) be a [differentiable dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_dynamical_system) with periodic orbit γ through *p*. Let

$$P: U \to S$$

be the corresponding Poincaré map through *p*. We define

$$P^{0} := \operatorname{id}_{U}$$

$$P^{n+1} := P \circ P^n$$

$$P^{-n-1} := P^{-1} \circ P^{-n}$$

and

$$P(n, x) := P^n(x)$$

then (**Z**, *U*, *P*) is a discrete dynamical system with state space *U* and evolution function

$$P: \mathbb{Z} \times U \to U.$$

Per definition this system has a fixed point at *p*.

The periodic orbit γ of the continuous dynamical system is [stable](wikipedia://en/5493795) if and only if the fixed point *p* of the discrete dynamical system is stable.

The periodic orbit γ of the continuous dynamical system is [asymptotically stable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotically_stable) if and only if the fixed point *p* of the discrete dynamical system is asymptotically stable.
