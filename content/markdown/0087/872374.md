# Parastatistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Parastatistics"}

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) and [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), **parastatistics** is a hypothetical alternative[^o2] to the established [particle statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_statistics) models ([Bose–Einstein statistics](wikipedia://en/188935), [Fermi–Dirac statistics](wikipedia://en/159225) and [Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics](wikipedia://en/170167)). Other alternatives include [anyonic statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anyonic_statistics) and [braid statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_statistics), both of these involving lower spacetime dimensions. [Herbert S.&nbsp;Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_S.&nbsp;Green)[^o3] is credited with the creation of parastatistics in 1953.[^o4][^o5] The particles predicted by parastatistics have not been experimentally observed.

:::

## Formalism {#Formalism}

Consider the [operator algebra](wikipedia://en/455987) of a system of $N$ identical particles. This is a [\*-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star-algebra). There is an $S_N$ group ([symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of order $N$) [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) upon the operator algebra with the intended interpretation of [permuting](wikipedia://en/44027) the $N$ particles. Quantum mechanics requires focus on [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) having a physical meaning, and the observables would have to be [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under all possible permutations of the $N$ particles. For example, in the case $N = 2, \, R_2 - R_1$ cannot be an observable because it changes sign if we switch the two particles, but the distance $|R_2 - R_1|$ between the two particles is a legitimate observable.

In other words, the observable algebra would have to be a \*-[subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) invariant under the action of $S_N$ (noting that this does not mean that every element of the operator algebra invariant under $S_N$ is an observable). This allows different [superselection sectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superselection_sector), each parameterized by a [Young diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_diagram) of $S_N$.

In particular:

- For $N$ identical **parabosons** of order $p$ (where $p$ is a positive integer), permissible Young diagrams are all those with $p$ or fewer rows.
- For $N$ identical **parafermions** of order $p$, permissible Young diagrams are all those with $p$ or fewer columns.
- If $p = 1$, this reduces to Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac statistics respectively.
- If $p$ is arbitrarily large (infinite), this reduces to Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics.

## Trilinear relations {#Trilinear_relations}

There are [creation and annihilation operators](wikipedia://en/701991) satisfying the trilinear commutation relations[^o4]

$$\big[a_k, [a_l^\dagger, a_m]_{\pm}\big]_- = [a_k, a_l^\dagger]_{\mp} a_m \pm a_l^\dagger [a_k, a_m]_{\mp} \pm [a_k, a_m]_{\mp} a_l^\dagger + a_m [a_k, a_l^\dagger]_{\mp} = 2\delta_{kl}a_m,$$

$$\big[a_k, [a_l^\dagger, a_m^\dagger]_{\pm}\big]_- = [a_k, a_l^\dagger]_{\mp} a_m^\dagger \pm a_l^\dagger [a_k, a_m^\dagger]_{\mp} \pm [a_k, a_m^\dagger]_{\mp} a_l^\dagger + a_m^\dagger [a_k, a_l^\dagger]_{\mp} = 2\delta_{kl} a_m^\dagger \pm 2\delta_{km} a_l^\dagger,$$

$$\big[a_k, [a_l, a_m]_{\pm}\big]_- = [a_k, a_l]_{\mp} a_m \pm a_l [a_k, a_m]_{\mp} \pm [a_k, a_m]_{\mp} a_l + a_m [a_k, a_l]_{\mp} = 0.$$

## Quantum field theory {#Quantum_field_theory}

A paraboson field of order *p*, $\phi(x) = \sum_{i=1}^p \phi^{(i)}(x)$, where if *x* and *y* are [spacelike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacelike)-separated points, $[\phi^{(i)}(x), \phi^{(i)}(y)] = 0$ and $\{\phi^{(i)}(x), \phi^{(j)}(y)\} = 0$ if $i \neq j$, where \[⋅, ⋅\] is the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193), and {⋅, ⋅} is the [anticommutator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutator). Note that this disagrees with the [spin–statistics theorem](wikipedia://en/352827), which is for [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) and not parabosons. There might be a group such as the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) *S<sub>p</sub>* acting upon the *φ*<sup>(*i*)</sup>s. [Observables](wikipedia://en/294248) would have to be operators which are [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under the group in question. However, the existence of such a symmetry is not essential.

A parafermion field $\psi(x) = \sum_{i=1}^p \psi^{(i)}(x)$ of order *p*, where if *x* and *y* are [spacelike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacelike)-separated points, $\{\psi^{(i)}(x), \psi^{(i)}(y)\} = 0$ and $[\psi^{(i)}(x), \psi^{(j)}(y)] = 0$ if $i \neq j$. The same comment about [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) would apply together with the requirement that they have even [grading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) under the grading where the *ψ*s have odd grading.

The *parafermionic and parabosonic algebras* are generated by elements that obey the commutation and anticommutation relations. They generalize the usual *fermionic algebra* and the *bosonic algebra* of quantum mechanics.[^o9] The [Dirac algebra](wikipedia://en/10290343) and the [Duffin–Kemmer–Petiau algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duffin–Kemmer–Petiau_algebra) appear as special cases of the parafermionic algebra for order *p* = 1 and *p* = 2 respectively.[^o10]

### Explanation {#Explanation}

Note that if *x* and *y* are spacelike-separated points, *φ*(*x*) and *φ*(*y*) neither commute nor anticommute unless *p* = 1. The same comment applies to *ψ*(*x*) and *ψ*(*y*). So, if we have *n* spacelike-separated points *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>,

$$\phi(x_1) \cdots \phi(x_n) |\Omega\rangle$$

corresponds to creating *n* identical parabosons at *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>. Similarly,

$$\psi(x_1) \cdots \psi(x_n) |\Omega\rangle$$

corresponds to creating *n* identical parafermions. Because these fields neither commute nor anticommute,

$$\phi(x_{\pi(1)}) \cdots \phi(x_{\pi(n)}) |\Omega\rangle$$

and

$$\psi(x_{\pi(1)}) \cdots \psi(x_{\pi(n)}) |\Omega\rangle$$

give distinct states for each permutation π in *[S<sub>n</sub>](wikipedia://en/28901)*.

We can define a permutation operator $\mathcal{E}(\pi)$ by

$$\mathcal{E}(\pi)\big[\phi(x_1) \cdots \phi(x_n) |\Omega\rangle\big] = \phi(x_{\pi^{-1}(1)}) \cdots \phi(x_{\pi^{-1}(n)}) |\Omega\rangle$$

and

$$\mathcal{E}(\pi)\big[\psi(x_1) \cdots \psi(x_n) |\Omega\rangle\big] = \psi(x_{\pi^{-1}(1)}) \cdots \psi(x_{\pi^{-1}(n)}) |\Omega\rangle$$

respectively. This can be shown to be well-defined as long as $\mathcal{E}(\pi)$ is only restricted to states spanned by the vectors given above (essentially the states with *n* identical particles). It is also [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999). Moreover, $\mathcal{E}$ is an operator-valued [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of the symmetric group *S<sub>n</sub>*, and as such, we can interpret it as the action of *S<sub>n</sub>* upon the *n*-particle Hilbert space itself, turning it into a [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788).
