# Complete group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complete group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ is said to be **complete** if every [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of $G$ is [inner](wikipedia://en/61839), and it is centerless; that is, it has a trivial [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) and trivial [center](wikipedia://en/7125).

:::

Equivalently, a group is complete if the [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugation_%28group_theory%29) map, $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(G)$ (sending an element $g$ to conjugation by $g$), is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397): [injectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) implies that only conjugation by the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) is the identity automorphism, meaning the group is centerless, while [surjectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) implies it has no outer automorphisms.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

As an example, all the [symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/28901), $S_{n}$, are complete except when $n \in \{2, 6$}. For the case $n = 2$, the group has a non-trivial center, while for the case $n = 6$, there is an [outer automorphism](wikipedia://en/13763478#exceptional_outer_automorphism).

The automorphism group of a [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) is an [almost simple group](wikipedia://en/25027899); for a non-[abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) simple group $G$, the automorphism group of $G$ is complete.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

A complete group is always [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to its [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) (via sending an element to conjugation by that element), although the converse need not hold: for example, the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of 8 elements is isomorphic to its automorphism group, but it is not complete. For a discussion, see (Robinson 1996, section 13.5).

:::

## Extensions of complete groups {#Extensions_of_complete_groups}

Assume that a group $G$ is a group extension given as a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of groups

$$1 \longrightarrow N \longrightarrow G \longrightarrow G' \longrightarrow 1$$

with [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28group_theory%29), $N$, and quotient, $G'$. If the kernel, $N$, is a complete group then the extension splits: $G$ is isomorphic to the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015), $N \times G'$. A proof using [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) and exact sequences can be given in a natural way: The action of $G$ (by [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugation_%28group_theory%29)) on the [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918), $N$, gives rise to a group homomorphism, $\varphi : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(N) \cong N$. Since $\operatorname{Out}(N) = 1$ and $N$ has trivial center the homomorphism $\varphi$ is surjective and has an obvious section given by the inclusion of $N$ in $G$. The kernel of $\varphi$ is the [centralizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) $C_{G}(N)$ of $N$ in $G$, and so $G$ is at least a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246), $C_{G}(N) \rtimes N$, but the action of $N$ on $C_{G}(N)$ is trivial, and so the product is direct.

This can be restated in terms of elements and internal conditions: If $N$ is a normal, complete [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of a group $G$, then $G = C_{G}(N) \times N$ is a direct product. The proof follows directly from the definition: $N$ is centerless giving $C_{G}(N) \cap N$ is trivial. If $g$ is an element of $G$ then it induces an automorphism of $N$ by conjugation, but $N = \operatorname{Aut}(N)$ and this conjugation must be equal to conjugation by some element $n$ of $N$. Then conjugation by $gn^{-1}$ is the identity on $N$ and so $gn^{-1}$ is in $C_{G}(N)$ and every element, $g$, of $G$ is a product $(gn^{-1})n$ in $C_{G}(N)N$.
