# Serge Lang

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Serge Lang"}

**Serge Lang** (; May 19, 1927 – September 12, 2005) was a French-American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and activist who taught at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) for most of his career. He is known for his work in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and for his mathematics textbooks, including the influential [*Algebra*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_%28Lang%29). He received the [Frank Nelson Cole Prize](wikipedia://en/1372177) in 1960 and was a member of the [Bourbaki group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourbaki_group).

:::

As an activist, Lang campaigned against the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War), and also successfully fought against the nomination of the political scientist [Samuel P. Huntington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_P._Huntington) to the [National Academies of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academies_of_Science). Later in his life, Lang was an [HIV/AIDS denialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDS_denialist). He claimed that [HIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV) had not been proven to cause [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDS) and protested Yale's research into HIV/AIDS.[^o3]

## Early life {#Early_life}

Lang was born in [Saint-Germain-en-Laye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Germain-en-Laye), close to [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989), in 1927. He had a twin brother who became a basketball coach and a sister who became an actress.[^o5] Lang moved with his family to [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California) as a teenager, where he graduated in 1943 from [Beverly Hills High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beverly_Hills_High_School). After completing his undergraduate education at the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology) in 1946, he received a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in mathematics from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in 1951. He held faculty positions at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago), [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) (from 1955, leaving in 1971 in a dispute), and [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University).

Lang studied at Princeton University, writing his thesis titled "On [quasi algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/2934213)" under the supervision of [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351),[^o6] and then worked on the geometric analogues of [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695) and [diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/502205). Later he moved into [diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558) and [transcendental number theory](wikipedia://en/1157698), proving the [Schneider–Lang theorem](wikipedia://en/28883744). A break in research while he was involved in trying to meet 1960s student activism halfway caused him (by his own description) difficulties in picking up the threads afterwards. He wrote on [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000) and [modular units](wikipedia://en/35197301), the idea of a "distribution" on a [profinite group](wikipedia://en/24266), and [value distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_distribution_theory). He made a number of conjectures in diophantine geometry: [Mordell–Lang conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell–Lang_conjecture), [Bombieri–Lang conjecture](wikipedia://en/3142105), [Lang–Trotter conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lang–Trotter_conjecture), and the [Lang conjecture on analytically hyperbolic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lang_conjecture_on_analytically_hyperbolic_varieties). He introduced the [Lang map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lang_map),[^o8] the [Katz–Lang finiteness theorem](wikipedia://en/37661448), and the [Lang–Steinberg theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lang–Steinberg_theorem) (cf. [Lang's theorem](wikipedia://en/42146937)) in algebraic groups.

## Mathematical books {#Mathematical_books}

Lang was a prolific writer of mathematical texts, often completing one on his summer vacation. Most are at the graduate level. He wrote [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) texts and also prepared a book on [group cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) for Bourbaki. Lang's *Algebra*, a graduate-level introduction to [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), was a highly influential text that ran through numerous updated editions. His Steele prize citation stated, "Lang's *Algebra* changed the way graduate algebra is taught...It has affected all subsequent graduate-level algebra books." It contained ideas of his teacher, Artin; some of the most interesting passages in *Algebraic Number Theory* also reflect Artin's influence and ideas that might otherwise not have been published in that or any form.

## Awards as expositor {#Awards_as_expositor}

Lang was noted for his eagerness for contact with students. He was described as a passionate teacher who would throw chalk at students who he believed were not paying attention. One of his colleagues recalled: "He would rant and rave in front of his students. He would say, 'Our two aims are truth and clarity, and to achieve these I will shout in class.'"[^o14] He won a [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) for Mathematical Exposition (1999) from the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822). In 1960, he won the sixth [Frank Nelson Cole Prize](wikipedia://en/1372177) in Algebra for his paper "Unramified class field theory over function fields in several variables" (*[Annals of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/2785)*, Series 2, volume 64 (1956), pp. 285–325).

## Activism {#Activism}

Lang spent much of his professional time engaged in political activism. He was a staunch [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist) and active in [opposition to the Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_to_US_involvement_in_the_Vietnam_War), volunteering for the 1966 anti-war campaign of [Robert Scheer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Scheer) (the subject of his book *The Scheer Campaign*). Lang later quit his position at Columbia in 1971 in protest over the university's treatment of anti-war protesters.

Lang engaged in several efforts to challenge anyone he believed was spreading misinformation or misusing science or mathematics to further their own goals. He attacked the 1977 Survey of the American Professoriate, an opinion questionnaire that [Seymour Martin Lipset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seymour_Martin_Lipset) and [E. C. Ladd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._C._Ladd) had sent to thousands of college professors in the United States. Lang said it contained numerous biased and loaded questions.[^o12] This led to a public and highly acrimonious conflict as detailed in his book *The File : Case Study in Correction (1977-1979)*.[^o13]

In 1986, Lang mounted what the *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times)* described as a "one-man challenge" against the nomination of political scientist [Samuel P. Huntington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_P._Huntington) to the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences).[^o14] Lang described Huntington's research, in particular his use of mathematical equations to demonstrate that [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa) was a "satisfied society", as "pseudoscience", arguing that it gave "the illusion of science without any of its substance." Despite support for Huntington from the Academy's social and behavioral scientists, Lang's challenge was successful, and Huntington was twice rejected for Academy membership. Huntington's supporters argued that Lang's opposition was political rather than scientific in nature.[^o15] Lang's detailed description of these events, "Academia, Journalism, and Politics: A Case Study: The Huntington Case", occupies the first 222 pages of his 1998 book *Challenges*.[^o16]

Lang kept his political correspondence and related documentation in extensive "files". He would send letters or publish articles, wait for responses, engage the writers in further correspondence, collect all these writings together and point out what he considered contradictions. He often mailed these files to mathematicians and other interested parties throughout the world.[^o5] Some of the files were published in his books *Challenges*[^o17] and *The File : Case Study in Correction (1977-1979)*.[^o13] His extensive file criticizing Nobel laureate [David Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Baltimore) was published in the journal *Ethics and Behaviour* in January 1993[^o18] and in his book *Challenges*.[^o17] Lang fought the decision by Yale University to hire [Daniel Kevles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kevles), a [historian of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science), because Lang disagreed with Kevles' analysis in *The Baltimore Case*.

In the last twelve years of his life, Lang challenged the scientific consensus on the connection between HIV and AIDS. He argued that existing data did not support the conclusion that [HIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIV) causes [AIDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDS).[^o19] A portion of *Challenges* is devoted to this issue.[^o17]
