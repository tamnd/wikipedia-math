# Modulo (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Modulo"}

In mathematics, the term ***modulo*** ("with respect to a modulus of", the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) [ablative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ablative) of *modulus* which itself means "a small measure") is often used to assert that two distinct mathematical objects can be regarded as equivalent—if their difference is accounted for by an additional factor. It was initially introduced into [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) in the context of [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) in 1801.[^o2] Since then, the term has gained many meanings—some exact and some imprecise (such as equating "modulo" with "except for").[^o3] For the most part, the term often occurs in statements of the form:

:::

*A* is the same as *B* modulo *C*

which is often equivalent to "*A* is the same as *B* [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) *C*", and means

*A* and *B* are the same—except for differences accounted for or explained by *C*.

## History {#History}

*Modulo* is a [mathematical jargon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_jargon) that was introduced into [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) in the book *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)* by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) in 1801.[^o5] Given the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *a*, *b* and *n*, the expression "*a* ≡ *b* (mod *n*)", pronounced "*a* is congruent to *b* modulo *n*", means that *a* − *b* is an integer multiple of *n*, or equivalently, *a* and *b* both share the same remainder when divided by *n*. It is the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) [ablative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ablative) of *modulus*, which itself means "a small measure."[^o6]

The term has gained many meanings over the years—some exact and some imprecise. The most general precise definition is simply in terms of an [equivalence](wikipedia://en/9259) (or [congruence](wikipedia://en/48167)) relation *R*, where *a* is *equivalent* (or *congruent*) to *b* modulo *R* if *aRb*.

## Usage {#Usage}

### Original use {#Original_use}

Gauss originally intended to use "modulo" as follows: given the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *a*, *b* and *n*, the expression *a* ≡ *b* (mod *n*) (pronounced "*a* is congruent to *b* modulo *n*") means that *a* − *b* is an integer multiple of *n*, or equivalently, *a* and *b* both leave the same remainder when divided by *n*. For example:

13 is congruent to 63 modulo 10

means that

13 − 63 is a multiple of 10 (equiv., 13 and 63 differ by a multiple of 10).

### Computing {#Computing}

In [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), the term can be used in several ways:

- In [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing), it is typically the [modulo](wikipedia://en/1352428) operation: given two numbers (either integer or real), *a* and *n*, *a* modulo *n* is the [remainder](wikipedia://en/502897) of the numerical [division](wikipedia://en/53696) of *a* by *n*, under certain constraints.
- In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) as applied to functional programming, "operating modulo" is special jargon which refers to mapping a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) to a category by highlighting or defining remainders.[^o10]

### Structures {#Structures}

The term "modulo" can be used differently—when referring to different mathematical structures. For example:

- Two members *a* and *b* of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) are congruent modulo a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918), [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *ab*<sup>−1</sup> is a member of the normal subgroup (see [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) and [isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorem) for more).
- Two members of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) or an algebra are congruent modulo an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977), if the difference between them is in the ideal.
  - Used as a verb, the act of [factoring](wikipedia://en/11526) out a normal subgroup (or an ideal) from a group (or ring) is often called "*modding out* the..." or "we now *mod out* the...".
- Two subsets of an [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679) are **equal modulo finite sets** precisely if their [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) is finite, that is, you can remove a finite piece from the first subset, then add a finite piece to it, and get the second subset as a result.
- A [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of maps leads to the definition of a [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) as being one space modulo another; thus, for example, that a [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) is the space of [closed forms](wikipedia://en/221519) modulo exact forms.

::: {#o13 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Modding out"}

### Modding out {#Modding_out}

In general, ***modding out*** is a somewhat informal term that means declaring things equivalent that otherwise would be considered distinct. For example, suppose the sequence 1 4 2 8 5 7 is to be regarded as the same as the sequence 7 1 4 2 8 5, because each is a cyclicly-shifted version of the other:

$$\begin{array}{ccccccccccccc} & 1 & & 4 & & 2 & & 8 & & 5 & & 7 \\ \searrow & & \searrow & & \searrow & & \searrow & & \searrow & & \searrow & & \searrow \\ & 7 & & 1 & & 4 & & 2 & & 8 & & 5 \end{array}$$

In that case, one is *"modding out by cyclic shifts*".
