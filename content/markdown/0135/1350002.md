# Essential spectrum

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Essential spectrum"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **essential spectrum** of a [bounded operator](wikipedia://en/455961) (or, more generally, of a [densely defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Densely_defined) [closed linear operator](wikipedia://en/1422584#Closed_linear_operators)) is a certain subset of its [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137), defined by a condition of the type that says, roughly speaking, "fails badly to be invertible".

:::

## Of self-adjoint operators {#Of_self-adjoint_operators}

In formal terms, let $X$ be a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) and let $T$ be a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408) on $X$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

The **essential spectrum** of $T$, usually denoted $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)$, is the set of all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that

$$T-\lambda I_X$$

is not a [Fredholm operator](wikipedia://en/279722), where $I_X$ denotes the [identity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_operator) on $X$, so that $I_X(x)=x$, for all $x \in X$. (An operator is Fredholm if it is bounded, and its [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) and [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) are finite-dimensional.)

The definition of essential spectrum $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)$ will remain unchanged if we allow it to consist of all those [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ (instead of just real numbers) such that the above condition holds. This is due to the fact that the spectrum of a self-adjoint operator is real.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

The essential spectrum is always [closed](wikipedia://en/47279), and it is a subset of the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) $\sigma(T)$. As mentioned above, since $T$ is self-adjoint, the spectrum is contained on the real axis.

The spectrum can be partitioned into two parts. One part is the essential spectrum. The other part is the [discrete spectrum](wikipedia://en/61992522), which is the set of points $\lambda \in \sigma(T)$ such that it is an [isolated point](wikipedia://en/390557), and $\ker(\lambda I_X - T)$ is a finite dimensional subspace. That is, it is an isolated eigenvalue of finite algebraic multiplicity (normal eigenvalues).

The essential spectrum is invariant under compact perturbations. That is, if $K$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6690773) self-adjoint operator on $X$, then the essential spectra of $T$ and that of $T+K$ coincide, i.e. $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T+K)$. This explains why it is called the *essential spectrum*: [Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) (1910) originally defined the essential spectrum of a certain differential operator to be the spectrum independent of boundary conditions.

:::

### The discrete spectrum {#The_discrete_spectrum}

The essential spectrum $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)$ is a subset of the spectrum $\sigma(T)$ and its complement is called the [discrete spectrum](wikipedia://en/61992522), so

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(T) = \sigma(T) \setminus \sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T).$$

If $T$ is self-adjoint, then, by definition, a number $\lambda$ is in the *discrete spectrum* $\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}$ of $T$ if it is an isolated eigenvalue of finite multiplicity, meaning that the dimension of the space

$$\ \mathrm{span} \{ \psi \in X : T\psi = \lambda\psi \}$$

has finite but non-zero dimension and that there is an $\varepsilon>0$ such that $\mu \in \sigma(T)$ and $|\mu-\lambda|<\varepsilon$ imply that $\mu$ and $\lambda$ are equal. (For general, non-self-adjoint operators $S$ on [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989), by definition, a complex number $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ is in the [discrete spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_spectrum) $\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(S)$ if it is a [normal eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_eigenvalue); or, equivalently, if it is an isolated point of the spectrum and the rank of the corresponding [Riesz projector](wikipedia://en/61861688) is finite.)

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Weyl's criterion"}

### Weyl's criterion {#Weyl's_criterion}

Define the following:

- A vector is a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) iff it has norm 1.
- A sequence of vectors $(\psi_n)_n$ converge (strongly) to 0 iff $\lim_n \|\psi_n\| = 0$. This is written as $\psi_n \to 0$.
- A sequence of vectors $(\psi_n)_n$ [converge weakly](wikipedia://en/2119266) to 0 iff $\lim_n \langle\psi_n, v\rangle = 0$ for any $v \in X$. This is written as $\psi_n \xrightarrow{w} 0$.

Under these definitions, we have the following characterization of the spectrum $\sigma(T)$ of the operator $T$:

> A number $\lambda$ is in $\sigma(T)$ if and only if there exists a sequence of unit vectors $(\psi_n)_n$ with $(T - \lambda) \psi_n \to 0$.

If $\lambda$ is on the discrete spectrum, then since $\lambda$ is isolated in $\sigma(T)$, any sequence of unit vectors $(\psi_n)_n$ with $(T - \lambda) \psi_n \to 0$ must converge to $\ker(\lambda I-T)$, and since $\ker(\lambda I-T)$ is finite-dimensional, $\psi_n$ must have a convergent subsequence by compactness of the unit sphere of $\ker(\lambda I-T)$. Therefore, $\psi_n \not\xrightarrow{w} 0$.

Weyl's criterion states that the converse is true as well:[^o10]

> A number $\lambda$ is in $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)$ if and only if there exists a sequence of unit vectors $(\psi_n)_n$ with $(T - \lambda) \psi_n \to 0$, and $\psi_n \xrightarrow{w} 0$.

Such a sequence is called a **singular sequence** or **Weyl sequence**. By sparsifying the sequence and applying [Gram–Schmidt process](wikipedia://en/82361), the sequence can be made orthonormal.

:::

::: {#o12 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Let $T: L^2[0, 1] \to L^2[0, 1]$ be the [multiplication operator](wikipedia://en/976642) (or the [position operator](wikipedia://en/4838571)) defined by $(Tf)(x) = xf(x)$. The [essential range](wikipedia://en/18380182) of $x \mapsto x$ is $[0, 1]$, so the spectrum is $\sigma(T) = [0, 1]$. For any $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, we can explicitly construct a singular sequence as a sequence of increasingly narrow and sharp rectangular functions that are supported on disjoint sets. For example, let $\lambda = 0$, then we can construct $\psi_n$ to be the rectangular function on $[2^{-n}, 2^{-n+1}]$ of height $\sqrt{2^n}$. They are orthonormal, with $\|(T-\lambda)\psi_n\| = O(1/2^{2n}) \to 0$. Note that the sequence increasingly resembles the [Dirac delta "function"](wikipedia://en/37021) at 0, even though it does not converge.

Let $T: L^2(\mathbb{R}) \to L^2(\mathbb{R})$ be the [momentum operator](wikipedia://en/2288549) defined by extending $T = -i\frac{d}{dx}$ for compactly supported smooth functions. Its essential spectrum is the entire real line. Physicists say that each $k \in \mathbb{R}$ is an eigenvalue of $T$ with eigenfunction $e^{ikx}$. However, this is not technically correct, since $e^{ikx}$ has infinite L2-norm. Nevertheless, it is possible to make a similar rigorous statement. While $e^{ikx}$ is not in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, it can be approached by a Weyl sequence in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$. The construction is essentially the same, by constructing a sequence approaching the Dirac delta at $k$ in [momentum space](wikipedia://en/36740699), then performing a [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) to position space.

Let $T: H^2(\mathbb{R}^n) \to H^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ be the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706) $T = -\Delta$, where $H^2$ is the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964). Its essential spectrum is $[0, \infty)$. For each $\lambda \in [0, \infty)$, and any unit vector $\hat k$, the construction of the Weyl sequence for the "eigenfunction" $e^{i\sqrt{\lambda}\hat k \cdot x}$ is similar.[^o10]

:::

## Of densely defined operators {#Of_densely_defined_operators}

### Preliminary concepts {#Preliminary_concepts}

Let $X$ be a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), and let $T$ be a [densely defined operator](wikipedia://en/13510193) on $X$. That is, it is of type $T:\,D(T)\to X$, where $D(T)$ is a dense subspace of $X$. Let the spectrum of $T$ be $\sigma(T)$, defined by

$$\sigma(T) = \{\lambda : (\lambda I - T) \text{ has no bounded inverse}\}$$

The complement of $\sigma(T)$ is the [resolvent set](wikipedia://en/8721698) of $T$.

::: {#o16 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

There are several definitions of the essential spectrum of $T$, which are not necessarily the same. Each of these definitions is of the form

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T) = \{\lambda : (\lambda I - T) \text{ is not nice}\}$$

There are at least 5 different levels of niceness, increasing in strength. Each increase in strength shrinks the set of nice $\lambda$, thus expands the essential domain.[^o17]

Let $A$ denote an operator of type $A: D(T)\to X$. Let $\ker A$ be its [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915), $\operatorname{coker} A$ be its [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715), $\operatorname{ran} A$ be its range. We say that $A$ is:

1. [Normally solvable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_solvability), if $A$ is a [closed operator](wikipedia://en/64713671), and $\operatorname{ran} A$ is a closed set. This can be checked via the [closed range theorem](wikipedia://en/23483982).
1. Semi-Fredholm, if furthermore, $\ker A$ is finite-dimensional [inclusive-or](wikipedia://en/7962) $\operatorname{coker} A$ is finite-dimensional.
1. [Fredholm](wikipedia://en/279722), if furthermore, $\ker A$ is finite-dimensional and $\operatorname{coker} A$ is finite-dimensional.
1. Fredholm with index zero, if furthermore, $\ker A$ and $\operatorname{coker} A$ has the same dimension.
1. If furthermore, there exists a [deleted neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deleted_neighborhood) of zero that is a subset of the resolvent set.
   - In other words, zero is not a limit point of $\sigma(A)$.
1. Has bounded inverse, if there exists a bounded linear operator $A^{-1}: X \to D(T)$, such that $A, A^{-1}$ are inverses of each other.

Now, set $A = (\lambda I - T)$. Then conditions 1 to 5 defines 5 essential spectra $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T)$, $1\le k\le 5$, and condition 6 defines the spectrum $\sigma(T)$. It is clear that conditions 1 to 5 increases in strength. One can also show that condition 6 is stronger than condition 5. Thus,

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},1}(T) \subseteq \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},2}(T) \subseteq \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},3}(T) \subseteq \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},4}(T) \subseteq \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}(T) \subseteq \sigma(T) \subseteq \mathbb{C},$$

Any of these inclusions may be strict.

Different authors defined the essential spectra differently, resulting in different terminologies. For example, Kato used $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},2}$, Wolf used $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},3}$, Schechter used $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},4}$, [Browder](wikipedia://en/10201445) used $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}$. Thus, $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}$ is also called the **Browder essential spectrum**, etc.[^o18]

:::

### More definitions {#More_definitions}

There are even more definitions of the essential spectrum.[^o17]

The following definition states that the essential spectrum is the part of the spectrum that is stable under compact perturbation:

$$w(T) := \bigcap_{B \text{ is compact}} \sigma(T+B)$$

Another definition states that:

$$\sigma_l(T) = \sigma(T) \setminus \{\text{isolated eigenvalues of }T\text{ with finite multiplicity}\}$$

Given $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$, it is an isolated eigenvalue of $T$ with finite multiplicity if and only if $\ker(\lambda I - T)$ has positive finite dimension, and $\lambda$ is an [isolated point](wikipedia://en/390557) of $\sigma(T)$.

### Equalities {#Equalities}

#### Banach space case {#Banach_space_case}

1. If $T$ is not closed, then $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},1}(T) = \mathbb{C}$. Because of this, the essential spectrum is uninteresting for these, and we will assume thenceforth that $T$ is closed.
1. If $T$ is bounded and either [hypernormal](wikipedia://en/21498423) or [Toeplitz](wikipedia://en/14598730), then $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},4}(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}(T)$.
1. If $T$ is bounded and $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},2}(T)$, then $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},2}(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}(T)$.
1. $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T') = \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T)$ for all $k=1,2,3,4,5$, where $T'$ is the [transpose operator](wikipedia://en/1442470) of $T$.
1. Define the *radius* of the essential spectrum by $r_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T) = \max \{ |\lambda| : \lambda\in\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T) \}.$ Even though the spectra may be different, the radius is the same for all $k=1,2,3,4,5$.
1. The essential spectrum $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T)$ is invariant under compact perturbations for $k=1,2,3,4$, but not for $k=5$. That is, for $k=1,2,3,4$ and any compact operator $B$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T+B) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T)$. The 4th essential spectrum is in fact the maximal possible that is stable under compact perturbations, in the sense that $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},4}(T) = w(T)$. (D.E. Edmunds and W.D. Evans, 1987).
1. $$\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}, 5}(T) = \sigma_l (T).$$
1. $\sigma(T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}(T)\bigsqcup\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(T)$, where $\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}(T)$ is the [discrete spectrum](wikipedia://en/61992522) of $T$.

The definition of the set $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},2}(T)$ is equivalent to Weyl's criterion: $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},2}(T)$ is the set of all $\lambda$ for which there exists a singular sequence.

#### Hilbert space case {#Hilbert_space_case}

If $X$ is a Hilbert space, and $T$ is self-adjoint, then all the above definitions of the essential spectrum coincide, except $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},1}(T)$. Concretely, we have[^o17]

$$\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},1}(T) \subseteq \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},2}(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},3}(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},4}(T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},5}(T)$$

The issue is that $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},1}(T)$ does not include isolated eigenvalues of infinite multiplicity. For example, if $T = I$ and $X$ is infinite-dimensional, then $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},1}(T)$ is empty, whereas $\sigma(T) = \{1\}$. This is because 1 is an eigenvalue of the identity operator with infinite multiplicity.

If $X$ is a Hilbert space, then $\sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T^*) = \overline{ \sigma_{\mathrm{ess},k}(T) }$ for all $k=1,2,3,4,5$.
