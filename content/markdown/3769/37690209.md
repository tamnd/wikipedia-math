# Sofic group

*Image: Sofic.svg.* Two balls of radius 2 in the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) $D_4$. Both balls contain 7 of the 8 group elements and are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) as [edge-colored graphs](wikipedia://en/690647). Furthermore, for *any* radius $r$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, we can use the approximating graph $G_0$ as the full Cayley graph itself, where the set $W$ of all vertices satisfies $|W|/|V| = 8/8 = 1 > 1-\varepsilon$. Since all 8 vertices have isomorphic $r$-balls (by the vertex-transitivity of Cayley graphs), $D_4$ is sofic.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sofic group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **sofic group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) whose [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) is an initially [subamenable](wikipedia://en/657430) graph, or equivalently a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of an [ultraproduct](wikipedia://en/273642) of finite-rank [symmetric groups](wikipedia://en/28901) such that every two distinct elements of the group have distance 1.[^n1] They were introduced by [Gromov](wikipedia://en/771562) as a common generalization of [amenable](wikipedia://en/657430) and [residually finite](wikipedia://en/5780875) groups.[^n2] The name "sofic", from [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) סופי 'finite', was later applied by [Weiss](wikipedia://en/40423267),[^n3] following his use of the word as a generalization of finiteness in [sofic subshifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofic_subshift). A **non-sofic group** is a group that is not a sofic group.

:::

The class of sofic groups is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under the operations of taking subgroups, [extensions](wikipedia://en/345807) by amenable groups, and [free products](wikipedia://en/360036). A [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) is sofic if it is the [limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of a sequence of sofic groups. The limit of a sequence of amenable groups (that is, an initially subamenable group) is necessarily sofic, but there exist sofic groups that are not initially subamenable groups.[^n4]

Gromov proved that Sofic groups are [surjunctive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjunctive).[^n1] That is, they obey a form of the [Garden of Eden theorem](wikipedia://en/1144206) for [cellular automata](wikipedia://en/54342) defined over the group ([dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) whose states are mappings from the group to a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) and whose state transitions are [translation-invariant and continuous](wikipedia://en/18903091)) stating that every injective automaton is surjective and therefore also [reversible](wikipedia://en/30766907).[^n5]

## Existence of non-sofic groups {#Existence_of_non-sofic_groups}

In 2026, artificial intelligence research company [OpenAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI) announced a machine-checkable [proof by construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_construction) of the existence of a non-sofic countable discrete group.[^o9][^o10] The existence of such groups was previously an open question. Researchers in the field have raised the possibility that key ideas in the proof were taken from their unpublished research, about which they had non-public conversation with OpenAI's chat bots.[^o11][^o12] [Similar concerns](wikipedia://en/84186116) were raised about OpenAI's announcement of a solution to the Navier-Stokes [Millenium Prize Problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millenium_Prize_Problem).
