# Regularization perspectives on support vector machines

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regularization perspectives on support vector machines"}

Within [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), r**egularization perspectives on support-vector machines** provide a way of interpreting [support-vector machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support-vector_machine) (SVMs) in the context of other regularization-based machine-learning algorithms. SVM algorithms categorize binary data, with the goal of fitting the [training set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Training_set) data in a way that minimizes the average of the hinge-loss function and L2 norm of the learned weights. This strategy avoids [overfitting](wikipedia://en/173332) via [Tikhonov regularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikhonov_regularization) and in the L2 norm sense and also corresponds to minimizing the bias and variance of our estimator of the weights. Estimators with lower [mean squared error](wikipedia://en/201816) predict better or generalize better when given unseen data.

:::

Specifically, [Tikhonov regularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikhonov_regularization) algorithms produce a decision boundary that minimizes the average training-set error and constrain the [decision boundary](wikipedia://en/2714979) not to be excessively complicated or overfit the training data via a L2 norm of the weights term. The training and test-set errors can be measured without bias and in a fair way using accuracy, precision, Auc-Roc, precision-recall, and other metrics.

Regularization perspectives on support-vector machines interpret SVM as a special case of Tikhonov regularization, specifically Tikhonov regularization with the [hinge loss](wikipedia://en/33100241) for a loss function. This provides a theoretical framework with which to analyze SVM algorithms and compare them to other algorithms with the same goals: to [generalize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalize) without [overfitting](wikipedia://en/173332). SVM was first proposed in 1995 by [Corinna Cortes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corinna_Cortes) and [Vladimir Vapnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Vapnik), and framed geometrically as a method for finding [hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/99862) that can separate [multidimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multidimensional) data into two categories.[^o2] This traditional geometric interpretation of SVMs provides useful intuition about how SVMs work, but is difficult to relate to other [machine-learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine-learning) techniques for avoiding overfitting, like [regularization](wikipedia://en/2009061), [early stopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_stopping), [sparsity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparsity) and [Bayesian inference](wikipedia://en/49571). However, once it was discovered that SVM is also a [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) of Tikhonov regularization, regularization perspectives on SVM provided the theory necessary to fit SVM within a broader class of algorithms.[^o3][^o4][^o5] This has enabled detailed comparisons between SVM and other forms of Tikhonov regularization, and theoretical grounding for why it is beneficial to use SVM's loss function, the hinge loss.[^o6]

## Theoretical background {#Theoretical_background}

In the [statistical learning theory](wikipedia://en/1053303) framework, an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) is a strategy for choosing a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f \colon \mathbf X \to \mathbf Y$ given a training set $S = \{(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)\}$ of inputs $x_i$ and their labels $y_i$ (the labels are usually $\pm1$). [Regularization](wikipedia://en/2009061) strategies avoid [overfitting](wikipedia://en/173332) by choosing a function that fits the data, but is not too complex. Specifically:

$$f = \underset{f\in\mathcal{H}} \operatorname{argmin}\left\{\frac 1 n \sum_{i=1}^n V(y_i, f(x_i)) + \lambda\|f\|^2_\mathcal{H}\right\},$$

where $\mathcal{H}$ is a [hypothesis space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_space)[^o8] of functions, $V \colon \mathbf Y \times \mathbf Y \to \mathbb R$ is the loss function, $\|\cdot\|_\mathcal H$ is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on the hypothesis space of functions, and $\lambda \in \mathbb R$ is the [regularization parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularization_parameter).[^o9]

When $\mathcal{H}$ is a [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/651196), there exists a [kernel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_function) $K \colon \mathbf X \times \mathbf X \to \mathbb R$ that can be written as an $n \times n$ [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric) [positive-definite](wikipedia://en/10273855) [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $\mathbf K$. By the [representer theorem](wikipedia://en/35887507),[^o10]

$$f(x_i) = \sum_{j=1}^n c_j \mathbf K_{ij}, \text{ and } \|f\|^2_{\mathcal H} = \langle f, f\rangle_\mathcal H = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n c_i c_jK(x_i, x_j) = c^T \mathbf K c.$$

## Special properties of the hinge loss {#Special_properties_of_the_hinge_loss}

*Image: Hinge and Misclassification Loss.png.* Hinge and misclassification loss functions

The simplest and most intuitive loss function for categorization is the misclassification loss, or 0–1 loss, which is 0 if $f(x_i) = y_i$ and 1 if $f(x_i) \neq y_i$, i.e. the [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299) on $-y_if(x_i)$. However, this loss function is not [convex](wikipedia://en/245568), which makes the regularization problem very difficult to minimize computationally. Therefore, we look for convex substitutes for the 0–1 loss. The hinge loss, $V\big(y_i, f(x_i)\big) = \big(1 - yf(x)\big)_+$, where $(s)_+ = \max(s, 0)$, provides such a [convex relaxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_relaxation). In fact, the hinge loss is the tightest convex [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) to the 0–1 misclassification loss function,[^o5] and with infinite data returns the [Bayes](wikipedia://en/49569)-optimal solution:[^o6][^o12]

$$f_b(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & p(1\mid x) > p(-1\mid x), \\ -1, & p(1\mid x) < p(-1\mid x). \end{cases}$$

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The Tikhonov regularization problem can be shown to be equivalent to traditional formulations of SVM by expressing it in terms of the hinge loss.[^o14] With the hinge loss

$$V\big(y_i, f(x_i)\big) = \big(1 - yf(x)\big)_+,$$

where $(s)_+ = \max(s, 0)$, the regularization problem becomes

$$f = \underset{f\in\mathcal{H}} \operatorname{argmin} \left\{\frac 1 n \sum_{i=1}^n \big(1 - yf(x)\big)_+ + \lambda\|f\|^2_\mathcal{H}\right\}.$$

Multiplying by $1/(2\lambda)$ yields

$$f = \underset{f\in\mathcal{H}} \operatorname{argmin} \left\{C\sum_{i=1}^n \big(1 - yf(x)\big)_+ + \frac{1}{2}\|f\|^2_\mathcal{H}\right\}$$

with $C = 1/(2\lambda n)$, which is equivalent to the standard SVM minimization problem.
