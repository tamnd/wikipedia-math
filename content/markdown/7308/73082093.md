# Dimension doubling theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dimension doubling theorem"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **dimension doubling theorems** are two results about the [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) of an [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436). In their core both statements say, that the dimension of a set $A$ under a Brownian motion doubles [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908).

:::

The first result is due to [Henry P. McKean jr](wikipedia://en/35051081) and hence called **McKean's theorem** (1955). The second theorem is a refinement of McKean's result and called **Kaufman's theorem** (1969) since it was proven by [Robert Kaufman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rober_Kaufman_%28mathematician%29).[^o2][^o3]

## Dimension doubling theorems {#Dimension_doubling_theorems}

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ be a probability space. For a $d$-dimensional Brownian motion $W(t)$ and a set $A\subset [0,\infty)$ we define the image of $A$ under $W$, i.e.

$$W(A):=\{W(t): t\in A\}\subset \mathbb{R}^d.$$

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="McKean's theorem"}

### McKean's theorem {#McKean's_theorem}

Let $W(t)$ be a Brownian motion in dimension $d\geq 2$. Let $A\subset [0,\infty)$, then

$$\dim W(A)=2\dim A$$

$P$-almost surely.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Kaufman's theorem"}

### Kaufman's theorem {#Kaufman's_theorem}

Let $W(t)$ be a Brownian motion in dimension $d\geq 2$. Then $P$-almost surely, for any set $A\subset [0,\infty)$, we have

$$\dim W(A)=2\dim A.$$

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Difference of the theorems"}

### Difference of the theorems {#Difference_of_the_theorems}

The difference of the theorems is the following: in McKean's result the $P$-[null sets](wikipedia://en/21520), where the statement is not true, depends on the choice of $A$. Kaufman's result on the other hand is true for all choices of $A$ simultaneously. This means Kaufman's theorem can also be applied to random sets $A$.

:::
