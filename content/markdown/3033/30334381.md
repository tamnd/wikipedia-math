# Fictitious domain method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fictitious domain method"}

In [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), the **fictitious domain method (FD)** is a numerical technique designed to solve [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) on complex geometries by embedding the physical domain into a larger and simpler computational domain. The method consists of extending the equations beyond the physical boundaries and enforcing the interface conditions through a [distributed Lagrange multiplier](wikipedia://en/159974) in order to recover the correct solution within the original [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of interest.[^o2]

*Image: Fitted mesh.png.* Example of a fitted mesh for a circular obstacle

*Image: Unfitted mesh-overlapping domains.png.* Example of a background unfitted mesh with an overlapping circular obstacle

This method belongs to the more general family of unfitted methods (also known as embedded or immersed), which allow solving [interface problems](wikipedia://en/436779) on complex or evolving domains without generating a mesh that conforms to the domain’s boundaries.[^o3] For this reason, the construction of two independent [meshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh) is considered in the fictitious domain method: one for the unfitted background domain and another overlapping mesh that conforms to the interface, but which can move freely during the simulation. As with the [immersed boundary method](wikipedia://en/17943941)[^o4] and the [level-set method](wikipedia://en/1203063),[^o5] the fictitious domain method is particularly effective for problems with evolving geometries since there is no need to rebuild the mesh but just to move the overlapping one.[^o13]

:::

The [Dirichlet boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/423504) on immersed closed interfaces are imposed weakly[^o6] by the introduction of the distributed Lagrange multiplier which enforces the boundary condition over the whole overlapping region.[^o2]

A particular application of the fictitious domain method is in [fluid–structure interaction](wikipedia://en/6061729) (FSI) problems, where a fluid and an immersed solid interact one each other, exchanging forces through the interface.[^o7] In this context, the solid is considered to be embedded within the [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924) domain and the coupling conditions at the interface are enforced weakly over the whole fictitious fluid region.[^o8]

## Weak imposition of Dirichlet boundary conditions {#Weak_imposition_of_Dirichlet_boundary_conditions}

Let $\Omega_f \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ be a fluid domain in which we want to solve the [Stokes equation](wikipedia://en/2047298) for [incompressible Newtonian fluids](wikipedia://en/363903). We want to impose the [Dirichlet boundary condition](wikipedia://en/423504) $\textbf{u} = \textbf{g}$ on the boundary of an immersed circle described by the interface $\Gamma = \partial \Omega_{c}$. In this context, the fictitious domain method allows us to solve the Stokes equation over the whole domain $\Omega = \Omega_f \cup \Omega_{c}$, and to impose weakly the constraint $\textbf{u} = \textbf{g}$ by means of the [Lagrange multiplier](wikipedia://en/159974) $\mathbf{\lambda}$.

*Image: Fictitious domain method simulation.png.* Velocity field around a fixed circular obstacle

The fictitious domain [weak formulation](wikipedia://en/3368981) reads:[^o10]

$$\begin{cases} \displaystyle \int_{\Omega} \mu \nabla \textbf{u} : \nabla\textbf{v} - \int_{\Omega} p \nabla \cdot \textbf{v} + \langle \mathbf{\lambda} , \textbf{v} \rangle_{\Omega_{c}} = 0 \quad &\forall \textbf{v} \in H^1_0(\Omega) \\[1ex] \displaystyle \int_{\Omega} q \ \nabla \cdot \textbf{u}_f = 0 \quad &\forall q \in L^2_0(\Omega) \\[1ex] \displaystyle \langle \textbf{u} - \textbf{g} , \mathbf{\eta} \rangle_{\Omega_{c}} = 0 \quad &\forall \mathbf{\eta} \in \Lambda \end{cases}$$

The last equation of the system guarantees that, in a weak sense, the interface condition $\textbf{u} = \textbf{g}$ is satisfied over the whole domain $\Omega_{c}$, and in particular on the interface $\Gamma$, while in $\Omega_f$ the equation is left untouched.[^o6]

The Lagrange multiplier $\mathbf{\lambda}$ acts on the fluid as an external force defined only in $\Omega_{c}$ that enforces the fictitious fluid velocity $\textbf{u}$ to fulfil the constraint inside the circle.[^o8]

## Fluid-structure interaction application {#Fluid-structure_interaction_application}

As it is typically done in [fluid-structure interaction problems](wikipedia://en/6061729), we consider a fluid domain $\Omega_f \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, where the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395) are usually solved, and $\mathcal{B} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ representing the region occupied by the structure.[^o7] The fluid is described in an [Eulerian framework](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_Coordinates) while the solid is described in a [Lagrangian formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_coordinates). This means that the solid domain $\mathcal{B}$ can be expressed as the image of a fixed reference configuration $\mathcal{B}^*$ through the [Lagrangian map](wikipedia://en/23371726) $x = \text{X}(s,t)$.[^o7]

The solid structure interacts with the fluid through its interface $\Gamma = \partial \mathcal{B}$, and from the mathematical point of view, it is required that the following kinematic condition is satisfied at the interface $\Gamma$:

$$\textbf{u}(x,t) = \dfrac{\partial \text{X}(s,t)}{\partial t} \Big|_{x = \text{X}(s,t)}$$

where $\textbf{u}$ is the fluid velocity and $x = \text{X}(s,t)$ is the position of the Lagrangian coordinate $s$ at time $t$. The latter kinematic condition can be seen as a specific [Dirichlet boundary condition](wikipedia://en/423504) at the interface $\Gamma$ which involves both the unknowns $\textbf{u}$ and $\text{X}$ of the problem.[^o7]

Therefore, in the spirit of the fictitious domain method, we can extend the fluid equation to the whole fixed computational domain $\Omega = \Omega_f \cup \mathcal{B}$ and impose weakly the kinematic coupling condition through the Lagrange multiplier $\mathbf{\lambda}$, requiring that the fictitious velocity $\textbf{u}$ matches the time derivative of the [Lagrangian map](wikipedia://en/23371726) $\text{X}$ in a weak sense inside the solid domain $\mathcal{B}$.**[^o7]**

*Image: ADINA R & D FSI Heart Valve Analysis.gif.* Numerical simulation of a heart valve

This requirement leads to the following [weak formulation](wikipedia://en/3368981):**[^o7]**

$$\begin{cases} a_f(\textbf{u}, \textbf{v}) - b(p,\textbf{v}) + \displaystyle \langle \mathbf{\lambda} , \textbf{v}(\text{X}) \rangle_{\mathcal{B}^*} = F_f(\textbf{v}) \quad &\forall \textbf{v} \in H^1_0(\Omega) \\[1ex] b(q,\textbf{u})= 0 \quad &\forall q \in L^2_0(\Omega) \\[1ex] a_s(\text{X}, \text{Y}) - \displaystyle \langle \mathbf{\lambda} , \text{Y} \rangle_{\mathcal{B}^*} = F_s(\text{Y}) \quad &\forall \text{Y} \in H^1(\mathcal{B^*}) \\[1ex] \displaystyle \langle \textbf{u}(\text{X}) - \dfrac{\partial \text{X}}{\partial t} , \mathbf{\eta} \rangle_{\mathcal{B}^*} = 0 \quad &\forall \mathbf{\eta} \in \Lambda \end{cases}$$

where $a_f(\cdot,\cdot)$ and $b(\cdot,\cdot)$ are the classical [bilinear forms](wikipedia://en/449477) coming from the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395), while $a_s(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the bilinear form associated with the [elastic model](wikipedia://en/458866) describing the solid.**[^o7]**

The kinematic condition is represented in a weak sense by the last equation, and the Lagrange multiplier $\mathbf{\lambda}$ acts as a body force on both the fluid and solid equations in order to fulfil this condition.[^o8]

## Numerical aspects {#Numerical_aspects}

The fictitious domain method proves to be effective for the numerical simulation of moving obstacles, as it employs two independent [meshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh): a fixed background grid and an independent, overlapping mesh that describes the obstacle geometry.[^o13] As we have seen in the previous applications, in order to weakly enforce the interface condition, we have to compute the [duality pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duality_pairing) $\displaystyle \langle \mathbf{\lambda} , \textbf{v}\rangle_{\Omega_{ob}}$ over the obstacle domain $\Omega_{ob}$.**[^o7]**[^o8][^o10]

The [Lagrange multiplier](wikipedia://en/159974) $\mathbf{\lambda}$ is defined over $\Omega_{ob}$ while the [test function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) $\mathbf{v}$ is defined over the whole computational domain $\Omega$. Therefore, upon [finite element method](wikipedia://en/18233581) [discretization](wikipedia://en/330017), the discrete Lagrange multiplier $\mathbf{\lambda}_h$ is defined on the overlapping mesh, whereas the discrete test function $\mathbf{v}_h$ lives on the background mesh.[^o14] As a result, computing the above coupling term requires the evaluation of [basis functions](wikipedia://en/50909) defined on the background mesh at locations that belong to the overlapping mesh, which may be change over time. This task is computationally non-trivial and may introduce [numerical errors](wikipedia://en/12076692) and possibly wrong solutions.**[^o14]**

This issue can be handled with different strategies:[^o15]

- Augmented [quadrature rule](wikipedia://en/170089): sub-triangulate the overlapping region in order to adopt an enhanced quadrature formula capable of accurately computing the coupling term. While exact, this approach can be computationally expensive.[^o15]
- Inexact [quadrature rule](wikipedia://en/170089): maintain the original quadrature used for the overlapping mesh, accepting a quadrature error that is controlled by the mesh size $h$.[^o14]
- [Collocation method](wikipedia://en/5463596): project $\mathbf{\lambda}_h$ over the discrete space of $\mathbf{v}_h$ in order to have both the functions defined on the same mesh.[^o8]
