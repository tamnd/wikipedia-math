# Step detection

*Image: step signal.png.* Examples of signals that may contain steps corrupted by noise. (a) [DNA copy-number ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copy-number_variation) from [microarray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_microarray) data, (b) [cosmic ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray) intensity from a [neutron monitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_monitor), (c) rotation speed against time of [R. Sphaeroides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodobacter_sphaeroides) [flagellar motor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_motors), and (d) red pixel intensity from a single scan line of a digital image.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Step detection"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), **step detection** (also known as **step smoothing**, **step filtering**, **shift detection**, **jump detection** or **[edge detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_detection)**) is the process of finding abrupt changes (steps, jumps, shifts) in the mean level of a [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) or signal. It is usually considered as a special case of the statistical method known as [change detection](wikipedia://en/10543268) or change point detection. Often, the step is small and the time series is corrupted by some kind of [noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise), and this makes the problem challenging because the step may be hidden by the noise. Therefore, statistical and/or signal processing algorithms are often required.

:::

The step detection problem occurs in multiple scientific and engineering contexts, for example in [statistical process control](wikipedia://en/593680)[^o2] (the [control chart](wikipedia://en/435754) being the most directly related method), in exploration [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics) (where the problem is to segment a [well-log](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_logging) recording into [stratigraphic zones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratigraphy)[^o11]), in [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics) (the problem of separating [microarray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microarray) data into similar [copy-number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copy-number_variation) regimes[^o3]), and in [biophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysics) (detecting state transitions in a [molecular machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_machine) as recorded in time-position traces[^o4]). For 2D signals, the related problem of [edge detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_detection) has been studied intensively for [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing).[^o5]

::: {#o7 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Algorithms"}

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

When the step detection must be performed as and when the data arrives, then [online algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_algorithm) are usually used,[^o8] and it becomes a special case of [sequential analysis](wikipedia://en/3509530). Such algorithms include the classical [CUSUM](wikipedia://en/15191637) method applied to changes in mean. [^o9]

By contrast, *offline* algorithms are applied to the data potentially long after it has been received. Most offline algorithms for step detection in digital data can be categorised as *top-down*, *bottom-up*, *sliding window*, or *global* methods.

### Top-down {#Top-down}

These algorithms start with the assumption that there are no steps and introduce possible candidate steps one at a time, testing each candidate to find the one that minimizes some criteria (such as the least-squares fit of the estimated, underlying piecewise constant signal). An example is the *stepwise jump placement* algorithm, first studied in geophysical problems,[^o11] that has found recent uses in modern biophysics.[^o12]

### Bottom-up {#Bottom-up}

Bottom-up algorithms take the "opposite" approach to top-down methods, first assuming that there is a step in between every sample in the digital signal, and then successively merging steps based on some criteria tested for every candidate merge.

### Sliding window {#Sliding_window}

By considering a small "window" of the signal, these algorithms look for evidence of a step occurring within the window. The window "slides" across the time series, one time step at a time. The evidence for a step is tested by statistical procedures, for example, by use of the two-sample [Student's t-test](wikipedia://en/536080). Alternatively, a [nonlinear filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_filter) such as the [median filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_filter) is applied to the signal. Filters such as these attempt to remove the noise whilst preserving the abrupt steps.

### Global {#Global}

Global algorithms consider the entire signal in one go, and attempt to find the steps in the signal by some kind of optimization procedure. Algorithms include [wavelet](wikipedia://en/50903) methods,[^o16] and [total variation denoising](wikipedia://en/26792343) which uses methods from [convex optimization](wikipedia://en/1674411). Where the steps can be modelled as a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876), then [Hidden Markov Models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_Markov_Models) are also often used (a popular approach in the biophysics community[^o17]). When there are only a few unique values of the mean, then [k-means clustering](wikipedia://en/1860407) can also be used.

:::

## Linear versus nonlinear signal processing methods for step detection {#Linear_versus_nonlinear_signal_processing_methods_for_step_detection}

Because steps and (independent) noise have theoretically infinite [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_%28signal_processing%29) and so overlap in the [Fourier basis](wikipedia://en/52247), [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324) approaches to step detection generally do not use classical smoothing techniques such as the [low pass filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_pass_filter). Instead, most algorithms are explicitly nonlinear or time-varying.[^o19]

## Step detection and piecewise constant signals {#Step_detection_and_piecewise_constant_signals}

Because the aim of step detection is to find a series of instantaneous jumps in the mean of a signal, the wanted, underlying, mean signal is [piecewise constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_constant). For this reason, step detection can be profitably viewed as the problem of recovering a piecewise constant signal corrupted by noise. There are two complementary models for piecewise constant signals: as [0-degree splines](wikipedia://en/457680) with a few knots, or as [level sets](wikipedia://en/559622) with a few unique levels. Many algorithms for step detection are therefore best understood as either 0-degree spline fitting, or level set recovery, methods.

### Step detection as level set recovery {#Step_detection_as_level_set_recovery}

When there are only a few unique values of the mean, clustering techniques such as [k-means clustering](wikipedia://en/1860407) or [mean-shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean-shift) are appropriate. These techniques are best understood as methods for finding a level set description of the underlying piecewise constant signal.

### Step detection as 0-degree spline fitting {#Step_detection_as_0-degree_spline_fitting}

Many algorithms explicitly fit 0-degree splines to the noisy signal in order to detect steps (including stepwise jump placement methods[^o11][^o12]), but there are other popular algorithms that can also be seen to be spline fitting methods after some transformation, for example [total variation denoising](wikipedia://en/26792343).[^o23]

### Generalized step detection by piecewise constant denoising {#Generalized_step_detection_by_piecewise_constant_denoising}

All the algorithms mentioned above have certain advantages and disadvantages in particular circumstances, yet, a surprisingly large number of these step detection algorithms are special cases of a more general algorithm.[^o19] This algorithm involves the minimization of a global functional:[^o25]

::: {#o26 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$H[m]=\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{j=1}^N \Lambda(x_i-m_j,m_i-m_j,x_i-x_j,i-j) \tag{1}$$

:::

Here, *x*<sub>*i*</sub> for *i* = 1, ...., *N* is the discrete-time input signal of length *N*, and *m*<sub>*i*</sub> is the signal output from the algorithm. The goal is to minimize *H*\[*m*\] with respect to the output signal *m*. The form of the function $\scriptstyle \Lambda$ determines the particular algorithm. For example, choosing:

$$\Lambda = \frac12 \left|x_i-m_j\right|^2 I(i-j=0) + \gamma\left|m_i-m_j\right| I(i-j=1)$$

where *I*(*S*) = 0 if the condition *S* is false, and one otherwise, obtains the [total variation denoising](wikipedia://en/26792343) algorithm with regularization parameter $\gamma$. Similarly:

$$\Lambda=\min \left\{\frac12 \left|m_i-m_j\right|^2,W\right\}$$

leads to the [mean shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_shift) algorithm, when using an adaptive step size Euler integrator initialized with the input signal *x*.[^o25] Here *W* > 0 is a parameter that determines the support of the mean shift kernel. Another example is:

$$\Lambda = \frac{1-\exp(-\beta|m_i-m_j|^2/2)}{\beta} \cdot I(|i-j|\le W)$$

leading to the [bilateral filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_filter), where $\scriptstyle \beta>0$ is the tonal kernel parameter, and *W* is the spatial kernel support. Yet another special case is:

$$\Lambda= \frac12 \left|x_i-m_j\right|^2 I(i-j=0) + \gamma \left|m_i-m_j\right|^0 I(i-j=1)$$

specifying a group of algorithms that attempt to greedily fit 0-degree splines to the signal.[^o11][^o12] Here, $\scriptstyle \left|x\right|^0$ is defined as zero if *x* = 0, and one otherwise.

Many of the functionals in equation ((1)) defined by the particular choice of $\scriptstyle \Lambda$ are [convex](wikipedia://en/245568): they can be minimized using methods from [convex optimization](wikipedia://en/1674411). Still others are non-convex but a range of algorithms for minimizing these functionals have been devised.[^o25]

### Step detection using the Potts model {#Step_detection_using_the_Potts_model}

A classical variational method for step detection is the [Potts model](wikipedia://en/1162226). It is given by the non-convex [optimization problem](wikipedia://en/1126536)

$$u^* = \arg\min_{u \in\mathbb{R}^N} \gamma \| \nabla u \|_0 + \| u-x\|_p^p$$

The term $\| \nabla u \|_0 = \# \{ i : u_i \neq u_{i+1} \}$ penalizes the number of jumps and the term $\| u-x\|_p^p = \sum_{i=1}^N |u_i - x_i|^p$ measures fidelity to data *x*. The parameter γ > 0 controls the tradeoff between regularity and [data fidelity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_fidelity). Since the minimizer $u^*$ is piecewise constant the steps are given by the non-zero locations of the gradient $\nabla u^*$. For $p = 2$ and $p= 1$ there are fast algorithms which give an exact solution of the Potts problem in $O(N^2)$. [^o28][^o29][^o30][^o31]
