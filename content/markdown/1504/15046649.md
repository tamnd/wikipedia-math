# Samuel Simon Snyder

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Samuel Simon Snyder"}

**Samuel Simon Snyder** (August 18, 1911 – December 28, 2007[^o3][^o4]) was a cryptographer for the United States Government.[^o8] His pioneering work in early computers led directly to the development of the computer as we know it, and laid the foundation for many aspects of the modern computing industry.[^o8] He is known for having broken every Japanese encrypted message with his partners in the Signal Intelligence Service during World War II and for having developed the [MARC standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MARC_standards).[^o4]

:::

## Career {#Career}

Snyder was an alumnus of [George Washington University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_University), where, at the height of the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression), he attended night school, working on various government jobs during the day.[^o4] While still at the university, Snyder started his career in 1934 with the [Signal Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_Intelligence_Service) as one of the first 10 employees,.[^o4][^o8] He worked at the National Security Agency until 1964.[^o3] He graduated from George Washington University in 1939 with a B.S. in chemistry.[^o4]

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), Snyder coordinated teams and worked with [William F. Friedman](wikipedia://en/175115) to break Japanese army cryptosystems. He also developed a more systematic approach to using sorting machines for cryptanalysis.[^o8] Near the end of the war, he and his partners were able to break every Japanese encrypted message, which, according to the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939), "is believed to have directly contributed to shortening the war by at least one year".[^o4] Snyder also determined whether computers that decoded Axis information at the Signal Intelligence Service were useful for other purposes, and concluded that they were integral to the agency; as a result, the NSA became the "leading computing industry on earth", according to his eldest son.[^o4]

Later at NSA he worked on one of the early code breaking computers called ABNER[^o6] and other computing systems such as Harvest, one of the earliest general-purpose computers made with IBM.[^o8][^o7] After spending 30 years at the National Security Agency, he worked at the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress),[^o8] where in 1964 he became an information systems specialist.[^o8] He was the coordinator of the Library of Congress's information systems from 1964 to 1966.[^o7][^o3] There, he helped to create the [MARC standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MARC_standards), a machine readable cataloging system that became an international standard electronic database system for libraries and for data sharing in research.[^o4][^o8]

From 1967 to 1970, he worked at the [Research Analysis Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Analysis_Corporation).[^o3]

Snyder coauthored the book "Man and the Computer", which was published in 1972 with Ashley Montagu, as well as a classified history of the NSA.

Mr. Snyder was inducted into the [NSA Hall of Honor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NSA_Hall_of_Honor) in 2007,[^o8] and he held a Defense Department [Meritorious Civilian Service Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meritorious_Civilian_Service_Award) and The Washington Post's "Ideal Father of the Year" award for 1949.[^o4]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Samuel Snyder married Patricia Yakerson Snyder in 1935; Patricia died in 1996.[^o7]

He left behind 4 children, 9 grandchildren, and 5 great-grandchildren.[^o4] Snyder's eldest son, named Sol and a professor at [Johns Hopkins School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_School_of_Medicine) as of 2008, remembered Samuel Snyder's "knack for math and science" and that he spent his last years "basking in his passion for music".[^o4] Sol's elder sister Elaine Hodges (d. 2006) [^o7], then 12 years old, described Samuel Snyder as a "mathematician, artist, scientist, house cleaner, sewer, dog harness maker, dog bather, can play the clarinet, saxophone, piccolo, story writer, \[and the\] best father in the world" in a letter to the Washington Post asking that Snyder be named the Post’s “Ideal Father”; he was awarded that designation in 1949.[^o4]
