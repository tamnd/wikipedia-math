# Hückel method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hückel method"}

The **Hückel method** or **Hückel molecular orbital theory**, proposed by [Erich Hückel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Hückel) in 1930, is a simple [method for calculating molecular orbitals as linear combinations of atomic orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCAO_MO_Method). The theory predicts the [molecular orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital) for [π-electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_electrons) in [π-delocalized molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugated_system), such as [ethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene), [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene), [butadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butadiene), and [pyridine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyridine).[^o2][^o3][^o4] It provides the theoretical basis for [Hückel's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hückel's_rule) that cyclic, planar molecules or ions with $4n+2$ π-electrons are [aromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromaticity). It was later extended to [conjugated molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugated_system) such as [pyridine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyridine), [pyrrole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrrole) and [furan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furan) that contain atoms other than carbon and hydrogen ([heteroatoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heteroatom)).[^o5] A more dramatic extension of the method to include σ-electrons, known as the [extended Hückel method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_Hückel_method) (EHM), was developed by [Roald Hoffmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Hoffmann). The extended Hückel method gives some degree of quantitative accuracy for organic molecules in general (not just planar systems) and was used to provide computational justification for the [Woodward–Hoffmann rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodward–Hoffmann_rules).[^o6] To distinguish the original approach from Hoffmann's extension, the Hückel method is also known as the **simple Hückel method** (SHM).

:::

Although undeniably a cornerstone of organic chemistry, Hückel's concepts were undeservedly unrecognized for two decades. [Pauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling) and Wheland characterized his approach as "cumbersome" at the time, and their competing [resonance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonance_%28chemistry%29) was relatively easier to understand for chemists without fundamental physics background, even if they couldn't grasp the concept of [quantum superposition](wikipedia://en/82728) and confused it with [tautomerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautomerism). His lack of communication skills contributed: when [Robert Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Robinson_%28chemist%29) sent him a friendly request, he responded arrogantly that he is not interested in organic chemistry.[^o7]

In spite of its simplicity, the Hückel method in its original form makes qualitatively accurate and chemically useful predictions for many common molecules and is therefore a powerful and widely taught educational tool. It is described in many introductory [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry) and [physical organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_organic_chemistry) textbooks, and organic chemists in particular still routinely apply Hückel theory to obtain a very approximate, [back-of-the-envelope](wikipedia://en/550392) understanding of π-bonding.

## Hückel characteristics {#Hückel_characteristics}

The method has several characteristics:

- It limits itself to [conjugated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugated_system) molecules.
- Only [π electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_electron) molecular orbitals are included because these determine much of the chemical and spectral properties of these molecules. The [σ electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_electron) are assumed to form the framework of the molecule and σ connectivity is used to determine whether two π orbitals interact. However, the orbitals formed by σ electrons are ignored and assumed not to interact with π electrons. This is referred to as σ-π separability. It is justified by the [orthogonality](wikipedia://en/102221) of σ and π orbitals in planar molecules. For this reason, the Hückel method is limited to systems that are planar or nearly so.
- The method is based on applying the [variational method](wikipedia://en/37487265) to [linear combination of atomic orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCAO_MO_Method) and making simplifying assumptions regarding the overlap, resonance and Coulomb integrals of these atomic orbitals. It does not attempt to solve the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874), and neither the functional form of the basis atomic orbitals nor details of the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) are involved.
- For hydrocarbons, the method takes atomic connectivity as the only input; empirical parameters are only needed when heteroatoms are introduced.
- The method predicts how many energy levels exist for a given molecule, which levels are [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_energy_level) and it expresses the molecular orbital energies in terms of two parameters, called α, the energy of an electron in a 2p orbital, and β, the interaction energy between two 2p orbitals (the extent to which an electron is stabilized by allowing it to delocalize between two orbitals). The usual [sign convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_convention) is to let both α and β be negative numbers. To understand and compare systems in a qualitative or even semi-quantitative sense, explicit numerical values for these parameters are typically not required.
- In addition the method also enables calculation of [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) for each atom in the π framework, the fractional [bond order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_order) between any two atoms, and the overall [molecular dipole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole#Molecular_dipoles).

The theory predicts two energy levels for [ethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene) with its two [π electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_electron) filling the low-energy [HOMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOMO) and the high energy [LUMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LUMO) remaining empty. In [butadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butadiene) the 4 π-electrons occupy 2 low energy molecular orbitals, out of a total of 4, and for [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene) 6 energy levels are predicted, two of them [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_energy_level).

For linear and cyclic systems (with *N* atoms), general solutions exist:[^o14]

*Image: Frostcircle.svg.* Frost circle mnemonic for 1,3-cyclopenta-5-dienyl anion

- Linear system ([polyene/polyenyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyene)): $E_k = \alpha + 2\beta \cos \frac{(k+1)\pi}{N+1}\quad (k=0,1,\ldots, N-1)$.
  - Energy levels are all distinct.
- Cyclic system, [Hückel topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius–Hückel_concept) ([annulene/annulenyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annulene)): $E_k = \alpha + 2\beta \cos \frac{2k\pi}{N}\quad(k=0,1,\ldots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor)$.
  - Energy levels $k = 1,\ldots, \lceil N/2 \rceil-1$ are each doubly degenerate.
- Cyclic system, [Möbius topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius–Hückel_concept) (*hypothetical for N* \< 8[^o15]): $E_k = \alpha + 2\beta' \cos \frac{(2k+1)\pi}{N},\ \beta'=\beta\cos(\pi/N)\quad(k=0,1,\ldots,\lceil N/2\rceil-1)$.
  - Energy levels $k = 0,\ldots, \lfloor N/2 \rfloor-1$ are each doubly degenerate.

The energy levels for cyclic systems can be predicted using the Frost circle mnemonic (named after the American chemist Arthur Atwater Frost). A circle centered at α with radius 2β is inscribed with a regular *N-*gon with one vertex pointing down; the *y*-coordinate of the vertices of the polygon then represent the orbital energies of the \[*N*\]annulene/annulenyl system.[^o16] Related mnemonics exists for linear and Möbius systems.[^o17]

### The values of α and β {#The_values_of_α_and_β}

The value of α is the energy of an electron in a 2p orbital, relative to an unbound electron at infinity. This quantity is negative, since the electron is stabilized by being electrostatically bound to the positively charged nucleus. For carbon this value is known to be approximately –11.4 eV. Since Hückel theory is generally only interested in energies relative to a reference localized system, the value of α is often immaterial and can be set to zero without affecting any conclusions.

Roughly speaking, β physically represents the energy of stabilization experienced by an electron allowed to delocalize in a π molecular orbital formed from the 2p orbitals of adjacent atoms, compared to being localized in an isolated 2p atomic orbital. As such, it is also a negative number, although it is often spoken of in terms of its absolute value. The value for |β| in Hückel theory is roughly constant for structurally similar compounds, but not surprisingly, structurally dissimilar compounds will give very different values for |β|. For example, using the π [bond energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_energy) of ethylene (65 kcal/mole) and comparing the energy of a doubly-occupied π orbital (2α + 2β) with the energy of electrons in two isolated p orbitals (2α), a value of |β| = 32.5 kcal/mole can be inferred. On the other hand, using the resonance energy of benzene (36 kcal/mole, derived from heats of hydrogenation) and comparing benzene (6α + 8β) with a hypothetical "non-aromatic 1,3,5-cyclohexatriene" (6α + 6β), a much smaller value of |β| = 18 kcal/mole emerges. These differences are not surprising, given the substantially shorter [bond length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_length) of ethylene (1.33 Å) compared to benzene (1.40 Å). The shorter distance between the interacting p orbitals accounts for the greater energy of interaction, which is reflected by a higher value of |β|. Nevertheless, heat of hydrogenation measurements of various [polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycyclic_aromatic_hydrocarbon) like [naphthalene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naphthalene) and anthracene all imply values of |β| between 17 and 20 kcal/mol.

However, even for the same compound, the correct assignment of |β| can be controversial. For instance, it is argued that the resonance energy measured experimentally via heats of hydrogenation is diminished by the distortions in bond lengths that must take place going from the single and double bonds of "non-aromatic 1,3,5-cyclohexatriene" to the delocalized bonds of benzene. Taking this distortion energy into account, the value of |β| for delocalization without geometric change (called the "vertical resonance energy") for benzene is found to be around 37 kcal/mole. On the other hand, experimental measurements of electronic spectra have given a value of |β| (called the "spectroscopic resonance energy") as high as 3 eV (~70 kcal/mole) for benzene.[^o19] Given these subtleties, qualifications, and ambiguities, Hückel theory should not be called upon to provide accurate quantitative predictions – only semi-quantitative or qualitative trends and comparisons are reliable and robust.

### Other successful predictions {#Other_successful_predictions}

With this caveat in mind, many predictions of the theory have been experimentally verified:

- The [HOMO–LUMO gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOMO–LUMO_gap), in terms of the β constant, correlates directly with the respective [molecular electronic transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_electronic_transition) observed with [UV/VIS spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UV/VIS_spectroscopy). For linear [polyenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyene), the energy gap is given as:

$$\Delta E = -4\beta \sin \frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}$$

from which a value for β can be obtained between −60 and −70 [kcal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie)/[mol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29) (−250 to −290 [kJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilojoule)/mol).[^o21]

- The predicted molecular orbital energies as stipulated by [Koopmans' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koopmans'_theorem) correlate with [photoelectron spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectron_spectroscopy).[^o22]
- The Hückel [delocalization energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delocalization_energy) correlates with the experimental [heat of combustion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_combustion). This energy is defined as the difference between the total predicted π energy (in [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene) 8β) and a hypothetical π energy in which all [ethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene) units are assumed isolated, each contributing 2β (making benzene 3 × 2β = 6β).
- Molecules with molecular orbitals paired up such that only the sign differs (for example α ± β) are called *[alternant hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternant_hydrocarbon)* and have in common small [molecular dipole moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole#Molecular_dipoles). This is in contrast to non-alternant hydrocarbons, such as [azulene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azulene) and [fulvene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulvene) that have large [dipole moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole#Molecular_dipoles). The Hückel theory is more accurate for alternant hydrocarbons.
- For [cyclobutadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclobutadiene) the theory predicts that the two high-energy electrons occupy a degenerate pair of molecular orbitals (following from [Hund's rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hund's_rules)) that are neither stabilized nor destabilized. Hence the square molecule would be a very reactive triplet [diradical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diradical) (the ground state is actually rectangular without degenerate orbitals). In fact, all cyclic conjugated hydrocarbons with a total of 4*n* π-electrons share this molecular orbital pattern, and this forms the basis of [Hückel's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hückel's_rule).
- [Dewar reactivity numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dewar_reactivity_number) deriving from the Hückel approach correctly predict the reactivity of aromatic systems with [nucleophiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleophiles) and [electrophiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophiles).
- The benzyl cation and anion serve as simple models for arenes with electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups, respectively. The π-electron population correctly implies the *meta*- and *ortho-*/*para-*selectivity for electrophilic aromatic substitution of π electron-poor and π electron-rich arenes, respectively.

### Application in optical activity analysis {#Application_in_optical_activity_analysis}

The analysis of the [optical activity](wikipedia://en/39774) of a molecule depends to a certain extent on the study of its [chiral](wikipedia://en/32703814) characteristics. However, for achiral molecules applying [pseudoscalars](wikipedia://en/475035) to simplify the calculations of optical activity cannot be achieved due to the lack of spatial average.[^o24]

Instead of traditional chiroptical solution measurements, Hückel theory helps focus on oriented π systems by separating from σ electrons especially in the planar, $C_{\mathrm{2v}}$-symmetric cases. Transition dipole moments derived by multiplying each wavefunction of individual planar molecule one by one, contribute to the directions of the most optical activity, where sit at the bisectors of two orthogonal ones. Despite the zero value for the trace of the tensor, cis-butadiene shows considerable off diagonal component which was computed as the first optical activity evaluation of achiral molecule.[^o25]

Transition electric dipole, [magnetic dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipole) and electric quadrupole moments interactions result in optical rotation(OR), which can be described by both tensor components and chemical geometries. The in phase overlap of two molecular orbitals yield negative charge while depleting charge out of phase. The movement can be interpreted quantitatively by corresponding π and π\* orbitals coefficients.

## Delocalization energy, π-bond orders, and π-electron populations {#Delocalization_energy,_π-bond_orders,_and_π-electron_populations}

The delocalization energy, π-bond orders, and π-electron population are chemically significant parameters that can be gleaned from the orbital energies and coefficients that are the direct outputs of Hückel theory.[^o27] These are quantities strictly derived from theory, as opposed to measurable physical properties, though they correlate with measurable qualitative and quantitative properties of the [chemical species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_species). Delocalization energy is defined as the difference in energy between that of the most stable localized [Lewis structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_structure) and the energy of the molecule computed from Hückel theory orbital energies and occupancies. Since all energies are relative, we set $\alpha=0$ [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) to simplify discussion. The energy of the localized structure is then set to be 2β for every two-electron localized π-bond. The Hückel energy of the molecule is $\sum_i n_iE_i$, where the sum is over all Hückel orbitals, $n_i$ is the occupancy of orbital *i*, set to be 2 for doubly-occupied orbitals, 1 for singly-occupied orbitals, and 0 for unoccupied orbitals, and $E_i$ is the energy of orbital *i*. Thus, the delocalization energy, conventionally a positive number, is defined as

$$E_{\mathrm{deloc.}}=\Bigg|\Big(\sum_i n_iE_i\Big)-2\beta\times(\#\ \mathrm{of}\ \mathrm{localized}\ \pi\ \mathrm{bonds})\Bigg|.$$

*Image: EAS directing group models.png.* π-Electron populations of benzyl cation and benzyl anion can be used to rationalize the directing group effects of electron-withdrawing and -donating substituents in electrophilic aromatic substitution.

In the case of benzene, the occupied orbitals have energies (again setting $\alpha=0$) 2β, β, and β. This gives the Hückel energy of benzene as $2\times2\beta+2\times\beta+2\times\beta=8\beta$. Each Kekulé structure of benzene has three double bonds, so the localized structure is assigned an energy of $2\beta\times 3=6\beta$. The delocalization energy, measured in units of $|\beta|$, is then $|8\beta-6\beta|=2|\beta|$.

The π-bond orders derived from Hückel theory are defined using the orbital coefficients of the Hückel MOs. The π-bond order between atoms *j* and *k* is defined as

$$\mathrm{BO}_\pi(j,k)= \sum_i n_ic^{(i)}_jc^{(i)}_k,$$

where $n_i$ is again the orbital occupancy of orbital *i* and $c_j^{(i)}$ and $c_k^{(i)}$ are the coefficients on atoms *j* and *k*, respectively, for orbital *i*. For benzene, the three occupied MOs, expressed as linear combinations of AOs $\phi_i$, are:[^o28]

$\Psi(A_{2u})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3+\phi_4+\phi_5+\phi_6)$, \[$E=2\beta$\];

$\Psi(E_{1g}^{(x)})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{12}}(2\phi_1+\phi_2-\phi_3-2\phi_4-\phi_5+\phi_6)$, \[$E=\beta$\];

$\Psi(E_{1g}^{(y)})=\frac{1}{2}(\phi_2+\phi_3-\phi_5-\phi_6)$, \[$E=\beta$\].

Perhaps surprisingly, the π-bond order formula gives a bond order of

$$2\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\Bigg)+2\Bigg(\frac{2}{\sqrt{12}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{12}}\Bigg)+2(0)\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big)=\frac{2}{3}$$

for the bond between carbons 1 and 2.[^o29] The resulting total (σ + π) bond order of $1\frac{2}{3}$ is the same between any other pair of adjacent carbon atoms. This is more than the naive π-bond order of $\frac{1}{2}$ (for a total bond order of $1\frac{1}{2}$) that one might guess when simply considering the Kekulé structures and the usual definition of bond order in valence bond theory. The Hückel definition of bond order attempts to quantify any additional stabilization that the system enjoys resulting from delocalization. In a sense, the Hückel bond order suggests that there are four π-bonds in benzene instead of the three that are implied by the Kekulé-type Lewis structures. The "extra" bond is attributed to the additional stabilization that results from the aromaticity of the benzene molecule. (This is only one of several definitions for non-integral bond orders, and other definitions will lead to different values that fall between 1 and 2.)

The π-electron population is calculated in a very similar way to the bond order using the orbital coefficients of the Hückel MOs. The π-electron population on atom *j* is defined as

$$n_\pi(j)= \sum_i n_i[c^{(i)}_j]^2.$$

The associated Hückel Coulomb charge is defined as $q_j=N_\pi(j)-n_\pi(j)$, where $N_\pi(j)$ is the number of π-electrons contributed by a neutral, sp<sup>2</sup>-hybridized atom *j* (we always have $N_\pi=1$ for carbon).

For carbon 1 on benzene, this yields a π-electron population of

$$2\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\Bigg)+2\Bigg(\frac{2}{\sqrt{12}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\frac{2}{\sqrt{12}}\Bigg)+2(0)(0)=1.$$

Since each carbon atom contributes one π-electron to the molecule, this gives a Coulomb charge of 0 for carbon 1 (and all other carbon atoms), as expected.

In the cases of benzyl cation and benzyl anion shown above,

$q_j(\mathrm{CH}_2^+)=N_\pi(j)-n_\pi(j)=1-0.43=+0.57$ and $q_j(\mathrm{CH}_2^-)=N_\pi(j)-n_\pi(j)=1-1.57=-0.57$,

$q_j(\mathrm{C}_{o,p}^+)=N_\pi(j)-n_\pi(j)=1-0.86=+0.14$ and $q_j(\mathrm{C}_{o,p}^-)=N_\pi(j)-n_\pi(j)=1-1.14=-0.14$.

## Mathematics behind the Hückel method {#Mathematics_behind_the_Hückel_method}

The mathematics of the Hückel method is based on the [Ritz method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritz_method). In short, given a basis set of *n* [normalized](wikipedia://en/145343#Normalization_condition) atomic orbitals $\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^n$, an *ansatz* molecular orbital $\psi_g= N(c_1\phi_1+\cdots+c_n\phi_n)$ is written down, with normalization constant *N* and coefficients $c_i$ which are to be determined. In other words, we are assuming that the molecular orbital (MO) can be written as a linear combination of atomic orbitals, a conceptually intuitive and convenient approximation (the [linear combination of atomic orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_combination_of_atomic_orbitals) or LCAO approximation). The [variational theorem](wikipedia://en/37487265) states that given an eigenvalue problem $\hat{H}| \psi^{(i)} \rangle=E^{(i)} | \psi^{(i)} \rangle$ with smallest eigenvalue $E^{(0)}$ and corresponding wavefunction $\psi^{(0)}$, any normalized trial wavefunction $\psi_g$ (i.e., $\langle \psi_g | \psi_g \rangle = \int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \psi_g^*\,\psi_g\,dV = 1$ holds) will satisfy

$$\mathcal{E}[\psi_g]=\langle \psi_g | \hat{H} | \psi_g \rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^3} \psi_g^*\,\hat{H}\psi_g\,dV\geq E^{(0)},$$

with equality holding if and only if $\psi_g=\psi^{(0)}$. Thus, by minimizing $E(c_1,\ldots,c_n)=\mathcal{E}[\psi_g]$ with respect to coefficients $c_i$ for normalized trial wavefunctions $\psi_g(c_1,\ldots, c_n)$, we obtain a closer approximation of the true ground-state wavefunction and its energy.

To start, we apply the normalization condition to the *ansatz* and expand to get an expression for *N* in terms of the $c_i$. Then, we substitute the *ansatz* into the expression for *E* and expand, yielding

$E(c_1,\ldots, c_n)=N^2\Big[ \sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2H_{ii}+\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}c_ic_jH_{ij}\Big]$, where $N=\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2S_{ii}+\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n} c_ic_jS_{ij}\Big]^{-1/2}$,

$S_{ij}=\langle \phi_{i} | \phi_{j} \rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}\phi_{i}^*\,\phi_{j}\,dV$, and $H_{ij}=\langle \phi_{i} |\hat{H} | \phi_{j}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^3}\phi_{i}^*\,\hat{H}\phi_{j}\,dV$.

In the remainder of the derivation, we will assume that the atomic orbitals are real. (For the simple case of the Hückel theory, they will be the 2p*<sub>z</sub>* orbitals on carbon.) Thus, $S_{ij}=S_{ji}^*=S_{ji}$, and because the Hamiltonian operator is [hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian), $H_{ij}=H_{ji}^*=H_{ji}$. Setting $\partial{E}/\partial{c_i}=0$ for $i=1,\ldots, n$ to minimize *E* and collecting terms, we obtain a system of *n* simultaneous equations

$$\sum_{j=1}^n c_j(H_{ij}-E S_{ij})=0\quad(i=1,\cdots, n).$$

When $i\neq j$, $S_{ij}$ and $H_{ij}$ are called the **overlap** and **resonance** (or **exchange**) **integrals**, respectively, while $H_{ii}$ is called the **Coulomb integral**, and $S_{ii}=1$ simply expresses the fact that the $\phi_i$ are normalized. The *n* × *n* matrices $[S_{ij}]$ and $[H_{ij}]$ are known as the **overlap** and **Hamiltonian matrices**, respectively.

By a well-known result from [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), nontrivial solutions $(c_1, c_2,\ldots,c_n)$ to the above [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) can only exist if the coefficient matrix $[H_{ij}-E S_{ij}]$ is [singular](wikipedia://en/245528). Hence, $E$ must have a value such that the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the coefficient matrix vanishes:

$\mathrm{det}([H_{ij}-E S_{ij}])=0$. (\*)

This determinant expression is known as the **secular determinant** and gives rise to a [generalized eigenvalue problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenvalue_problem). The variational theorem guarantees that the lowest value of $E$ that gives rise to a nontrivial (that is, not all zero) solution vector $(c_1, c_2,\ldots, c_n)$ represents the best LCAO approximation of the energy of the most stable π orbital; higher values of $E$ with nontrivial solution vectors represent reasonable estimates of the energies of the remaining π orbitals.

The Hückel method makes a few further simplifying assumptions concerning the values of the $S_{ij}$ and $H_{ij}$. In particular, it is first assumed that distinct $\phi_i$ have zero overlap. Together with the assumption that $\phi_i$ are normalized, this means that the overlap matrix is the *n* × *n* identity matrix: $[S_{ij}]=\mathbf{I}_n$. Solving for *E* in (\*) then reduces to finding the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian matrix.

Second, in the simplest case of a planar, unsaturated hydrocarbon, the Hamiltonian matrix $\mathbf{H}=[H_{ij}]$ is parameterized in the following way:

$H_{ij} = \begin{cases} \alpha, & i=j;\\ \beta, & i,j \ \ \text{adjacent};\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$ (\*\*)

To summarize, we are assuming that: **(1)** the energy of an electron in an isolated C(2p*<sub>z</sub>*) orbital is $H_{ii}=\alpha$; **(2)** the energy of interaction between C(2p*<sub>z</sub>*) orbitals on adjacent carbons *i* and *j* (i.e., *i* and *j* are connected by a σ-bond) is $H_{ij}=\beta$; **(3)** orbitals on carbons not joined in this way are assumed not to interact, so *$H_{ij}=0$* for nonadjacent *i* and *j*; and, as mentioned above, **(4)** the spatial overlap of electron density between different orbitals, represented by non-diagonal elements of the overlap matrix, is ignored by setting $S_{ij}=0\ \ (i\neq j)$, *even when the orbitals are adjacent*.

This neglect of orbital overlap is an especially severe approximation. In actuality, orbital overlap is a prerequisite for orbital interaction, and it is impossible to have $H_{ij}=\beta$ while $S_{ij}=0$. For typical bond distances (1.40 Å) as might be found in [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene), for example, the true value of the overlap for C(2p*<sub>z</sub>*) orbitals on adjacent atoms *i* and *j* is about $S_{ij}=0.21$; even larger values are found when the bond distance is shorter (e.g., $S_{ij}=0.27$ ethylene).[^o31] A major consequence of having nonzero overlap integrals is the fact that, compared to non-interacting isolated orbitals, bonding orbitals are not energetically stabilized by nearly as much as antibonding orbitals are destabilized. The orbital energies derived from the Hückel treatment do not account for this asymmetry (*see* Hückel solution for ethylene (below) *for details*).

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{H}$ are the Hückel molecular orbital energies $E_1,\ldots, E_n$, expressed in terms of $\alpha$ and $\beta$, while the eigenvectors are the Hückel MOs $\Psi_1,\ldots,\Psi_n$, expressed as linear combinations of the atomic orbitals $\phi_i$. Using the expression for the normalization constant *N* and the fact that $[S_{ij}]=\mathbf{I}_n$, we can find the normalized MOs by incorporating the additional condition

$$\sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2=1.$$

The Hückel MOs are thus uniquely determined when eigenvalues are all distinct. When an eigenvalue is degenerate (two or more of the $E_i$ are equal), the [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) corresponding to the degenerate energy level has dimension greater than 1, and the normalized MOs at that energy level are then *not* uniquely determined. When that happens, further assumptions pertaining to the coefficients of the degenerate orbitals (usually ones that make the MOs orthogonal and mathematically convenient[^o32]) have to be made in order to generate a concrete set of molecular orbital functions.

If the substance is a planar, unsaturated hydrocarbon, the coefficients of the MOs can be found without appeal to empirical parameters, while orbital energies are given in terms of only $\alpha$ and $\beta$. On the other hand, for systems containing heteroatoms, such as [pyridine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyridine) or [formaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formaldehyde), values of correction constants $h_{\mathrm{X}}$ and $k_{\mathrm{X-Y}}$ have to be specified for the atoms and bonds in question, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in (\*\*) are replaced by $\alpha+ h_{\mathrm{X}}\beta$ and $k_{\mathrm{X-Y}}\beta$, respectively.

## Hückel solution for ethylene in detail {#Hückel_solution_for_ethylene_in_detail}

*Image: Ethylene-LUMO-Spartan-3D-balls.png.* Molecular orbitals ethylene $E = \alpha - \beta$

In the Hückel treatment for [ethylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethylene), we write the Hückel MOs $\Psi\,$ as a linear combination of the atomic orbitals (2p orbitals) on each of the carbon atoms:

$$\ \Psi = c_1 \phi_1 + c_2 \phi_2.$$

*Image: Ethylene-HOMO-Spartan-3D-balls.png.* Molecular orbitals ethylene $E = \alpha + \beta$

Applying the result obtained by the Ritz method, we have the system of equations

$$\begin{bmatrix} H_{11} - ES_{11} & H_{12} - ES_{12} \\ H_{21} - ES_{21} & H_{22} - ES_{22} \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}= 0,$$

where:

$H_{ij} = \langle \phi_i | \hat{H} | \phi_j \rangle$ and

$$S_{ij} = \langle \phi_i|\phi_j\rangle.$$

(Since 2p*<sub>z</sub>* atomic orbital can be expressed as a pure real function, the \* representing complex conjugation can be dropped.) The Hückel method assumes that all overlap integrals (including the normalization integrals) equal the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890), $S_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\,$, all Coulomb integrals $H_{ii}\,$ are equal, and the resonance integral $H_{ij}\,$ is nonzero when the atoms *i* and *j* are bonded. Using the standard Hückel variable names, we set

$$H_{11} = H_{22} = \alpha \,,$$

$$H_{12} = H_{21} = \beta \,,$$

$S_{11} = S_{22} = 1 \,$, and

$$S_{12} = S_{21} = 0 \,.$$

The Hamiltonian matrix is

$$\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ \beta & \alpha \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

The matrix equation that needs to be solved is then

$$\begin{bmatrix} \alpha - E & \beta \\ \beta & \alpha - E \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}= 0,$$

or, dividing by $\beta$,

$$\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\alpha - E}{\beta} & 1 \\ 1 & \frac{\alpha - E}{\beta} \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}= 0.$$

Setting $x:=\frac{\alpha - E}{\beta}$, we obtain

$\begin{bmatrix} x & 1 \\ 1 & x \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \end{bmatrix}= 0$. (\*\*\*)

This homogeneous system of equations has nontrivial solutions for $c_1,c_2$ (solutions besides the physically meaningless $c_1=c_2=0$) [iff](wikipedia://en/14922) the matrix is singular and the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) is zero:

$$\begin{vmatrix} x & 1 \\ 1 & x \\ \end{vmatrix} = 0.$$

Solving for $x$,

$x^2-1 = 0\,$, or

$$x = \pm 1\,.$$

Since $E = \alpha - x \beta$, the energy levels are

$E = \alpha - \pm 1 \times \beta$, or

$$E = \alpha \mp \beta.$$

The coefficients can then be found by expanding (\*\*\*):

$c_2 = -x c_1\,$and

$$c_1 = -x c_2\,.$$

Since the matrix is singular, the two equations are linearly dependent, and the solution set is not uniquely determined until we apply the normalization condition. We can only solve for $c_2$ in terms of $c_1$:

$c_2 = -\pm 1 \times c_1\,$, or

$$c_2 = \mp c_1\,.$$

After [normalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalisable_wave_function) with $c_1^2+c_2^2=1$, the numerical values of $c_1$ and $c_2$ can be found:

$c_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $c_2 =\mp \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$.

Finally, the Hückel molecular orbitals are

$$\Psi_{\mp} = c_1\phi_1+c_2\phi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_1 \mp \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi_2 = \frac{\phi_1 \mp \phi_2}{\sqrt{2}} \,.$$

The constant β in the energy term is negative; therefore, $E_+=\alpha + \beta$ with $\Psi_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\phi_1 + \phi_2)\,$ is the lower energy corresponding to the [HOMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HOMO) energy and $E_- = \alpha - \beta$ with $\Psi_- = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\phi_1 - \phi_2)\,$ is the [LUMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LUMO) energy.

If, contrary to the Hückel treatment, a positive value for $S:=S_{12}=S_{21}$ were included, the energies would instead be

$$E_{\pm}=\frac{\alpha\pm\beta}{1\pm S},$$

while the corresponding orbitals would take the form

$$\Psi_{\pm} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pm 2S}}\phi_1\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pm 2S}}\phi_2.$$

An important consequence of setting $S>0$ is that the bonding (in-phase) combination is always stabilized to a lesser extent than the antibonding (out-of-phase) combination is destabilized, relative to the energy of the free 2p orbital. Thus, in general, 2-center 4-electron interactions, where both the bonding and antibonding orbitals are occupied, are destabilizing overall. This asymmetry is ignored by Hückel theory. In general, for the orbital energies derived from Hückel theory, the sum of stabilization energies for the bonding orbitals is equal to the sum of destabilization energies for the antibonding orbitals, as in the simplest case of ethylene shown here and the case of butadiene shown below.

## Hückel solution for 1,3-butadiene {#Hückel_solution_for_1,3-butadiene}

*Image: Butadiene-pi-MOs-Spartan-3D-balls.png.* Butadiene molecular orbitals

The Hückel MO theory treatment of [1,3-butadiene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butadiene) is largely analogous to the treatment of ethylene, shown in detail above, though we must now find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 4 × 4 Hamiltonian matrix. We first write the molecular orbital $\Psi\,$ as a linear combination of the four atomic orbitals $\phi_i$ (carbon 2p orbitals) with coefficients $c_i$:

$$\ \Psi = c_1 \phi_1 + c_2 \phi_2 + c_3 \phi_3 + c_4 \phi_4.$$

The Hamiltonian matrix is

$$\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix} \alpha & \beta & 0 & 0 \\ \beta & \alpha & \beta & 0 \\ 0 & \beta & \alpha & \beta \\ 0 & 0 & \beta & \alpha \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

In the same way, we write the secular equations in matrix form as

$$\begin{bmatrix} \alpha - E & \beta & 0 & 0 \\ \beta & \alpha - E & \beta & 0 \\ 0 & \beta & \alpha - E & \beta \\ 0 & 0 & \beta & \alpha - E \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \\ c_3 \\ c_4 \\ \end{bmatrix}= 0,$$

which leads to

$$\Big(\frac{\alpha-E}{\beta}\Big)^4-3\Big(\frac{\alpha-E}{\beta}\Big)^2+1=0$$

and

$E_{1,2,3,4} = \alpha + \frac{ \sqrt{5} \pm 1}{2} \beta, \alpha - \frac{ \sqrt{5} \mp1}{2} \beta$, or approximately,

$E_{1,2,3,4}\approx\alpha+1.618\beta,\alpha+0.618\beta,\alpha-0.618\beta,\alpha-1.618\beta$, where 1.618... and 0.618... are the [golden ratios](wikipedia://en/12386) $\varphi$ and $1/\varphi$.

The orbitals are given by

$$\Psi_1 \approx 0.372\phi_1+0.602\phi_2+0.602\phi_3+0.372\phi_4,$$

$$\Psi_2 \approx 0.602\phi_1+0.372\phi_2-0.372\phi_3-0.602\phi_4,$$

$\Psi_3 \approx 0.602\phi_1-0.372\phi_2-0.372\phi_3+0.602\phi_4$, and

$$\Psi_4 \approx 0.372\phi_1-0.602\phi_2+0.602\phi_3-0.372\phi_4.$$
