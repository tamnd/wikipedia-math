# Reciprocals of primes

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Reciprocals of primes"}

The [reciprocals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29) of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) have been of interest to mathematicians for various reasons. They [do not have a finite sum](wikipedia://en/249208), as [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) proved in 1737.

:::

As [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_numbers), the reciprocals of primes have [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447) representations. In his later years, [George Salmon](wikipedia://en/589203) (1819–1904) concerned himself with the repeating periods of these decimal representations of reciprocals of primes.[^o2]

Contemporaneously, [William Shanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shanks) (1812–1882) calculated numerous reciprocals of primes and their repeating periods, and published two papers "On Periods in the Reciprocals of Primes" in 1873[^o3] and 1874.[^o4] In 1874 he also published a table of primes, and the periods of their reciprocals, up to 20,000 (with help from and "communicated by the Rev. George Salmon"), and pointed out the errors in previous tables by three other authors.[^o5]

File:Shanks's table of primes just below 20000 and their decimal periods.png

Rules for calculating the periods of repeating decimals from rational fractions were given by [James Whitbread Lee Glaisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Whitbread_Lee_Glaisher) in 1878.[^o6] For a prime $p$, the period of its reciprocal divides $p - 1$.[^o7]

The sequence of recurrence periods of the reciprocal primes appears in the 1973 Handbook of Integer Sequences.

## Full reptend primes {#Full_reptend_primes}

A *full reptend prime*, *full repetend prime*, *proper prime*[^o11] or *long prime* in [base](wikipedia://en/701207) *b* is an [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* such that the [Fermat quotient](wikipedia://en/22834255)

$$q_p(b) = \frac{b^{p - 1} - 1}{p}$$

(where *p* does not [divide](wikipedia://en/49492) *b*) gives a [cyclic number](wikipedia://en/1672489) with *p* − 1 digits. Therefore, the base *b* expansion of $1/p$ repeats the digits of the corresponding cyclic number infinitely.

## Unique primes {#Unique_primes}

A prime *p* (where *p* ≠ 2, 5 when working in base 10) is called **unique** if there is no other prime *q* such that the [period length](wikipedia://en/13612447#Fractions_with_prime_denominators) of the decimal expansion of its [reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29), 1/*p*, is equal to the period length of the reciprocal of *q*, 1/*q*.[^o13] For example, 3 is the only prime with period 1, 11 is the only prime with period 2, 37 is the only prime with period 3, 101 is the only prime with period 4, so they are unique primes. The next larger unique prime is 9091 with period 10, though the next larger period is 9 (its prime being 333667). Unique primes were described by [Samuel Yates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Yates) in 1980.[^o14] A prime number *p* is unique if and only if there exists an *n* such that

$$\frac{\Phi_n(10)}{\gcd(\Phi_n(10), n)}$$

is a power of *p*, where $\Phi_n(b)$ denotes the $n$th [cyclotomic polynomial](wikipedia://en/171992) evaluated at $b$. The value of *n* is then the period of the decimal expansion of 1/*p*.[^o15]

At present, more than fifty decimal unique primes or [probable primes](wikipedia://en/160560) are known. However, there are only twenty-three unique primes below 10<sup>100</sup>.

The decimal unique primes are

3, 11, 37, 101, 9091, 9901, 333667, 909091, ... .
