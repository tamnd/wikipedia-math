# Sectrix of Maclaurin

*Image: Sectrix of Maclaurin.gif.* Sectrix of Maclaurin: example with $q_{0} = PI/2$ and $K = 3$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sectrix of Maclaurin"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **sectrix of Maclaurin** is defined as the [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) swept out by the point of [intersection of two lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_of_two_lines) which are each revolving at constant rates about different points called **poles**. Equivalently, a sectrix of Maclaurin can be defined as a curve whose equation in [biangular coordinates](wikipedia://en/8349310) is linear. The name is derived from the [trisectrix of Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/2738114) (named for [Colin Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/7824)), which is a prominent member of the family, and their [sectrix](wikipedia://en/2909893) property, which means they can be used to divide an angle into a given number of equal parts. Unlike a proper sectrix curve, however, there is no one curve of this family that can divide angles into arbitrary numbers of parts; instead, different rates of revolution are needed for different numbers of parts. There are special cases known as **arachnida** or **araneidans** because of their [spider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spider)-like shape, and **Plateau curves** after [Joseph Plateau](wikipedia://en/331154) who studied them.

:::

## Equations in polar coordinates {#Equations_in_polar_coordinates}

We are given two lines rotating about two poles $P$ and $P_1$. By translation and rotation we may assume $P = (0,0)$ and $P_1 = (a, 0)$. At time $t$, the line rotating about $P$ has angle $\theta = \kappa t + \alpha$ and the line rotating about $P_1$ has angle $\theta_1 = \kappa_1 t + \alpha_1$, where $\kappa$, $\alpha$, $\kappa_1$ and $\alpha_1$ are constants. Eliminate $t$ to get $\theta_1 = q \theta + \theta_0$ where $q = \kappa_1 / \kappa$ and $\theta_0 = \alpha_1 - q \alpha$. We assume $q$ is rational, otherwise the curve is not [algebraic](wikipedia://en/253260) and is dense in the plane. Let $Q$ be the point of intersection of the two lines and let $\psi$ be the angle at $Q$, so $\psi = \theta_1 - \theta$. If $r$ is the distance from $P$ to $Q$ then, by the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222),

$${r \over \sin \theta_1} = {a \over \sin \psi}\!$$

so

$$r = a \frac {\sin \theta_1}{\sin \psi} = a \frac {\sin [q \theta + \theta_0]}{\sin [(q-1) \theta + \theta_0]}\!$$

is the equation in [polar coordinates](wikipedia://en/25120).

The case $\theta_0 = 0$ and $q = n$ where $n$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) greater than 2 gives arachnida or araneidan curves

$$r = a \frac {\sin n \theta}{\sin (n-1) \theta}\!$$

The case $\theta_0 = 0$ and $q = -n$ where $n$ is an integer greater than 1 gives alternate forms of arachnida or araneidan curves

$$r = a \frac {\sin n \theta}{\sin (n+1) \theta}\!$$

A similar derivation to that above gives

$$r_1 = (-a) \frac {\sin [(1/q) \theta_1 - \theta_0 /q]}{\sin [(1/q-1) \theta_1 - \theta_0 /q]}\!$$

as the polar equation (in $r_1$ and $\theta_1$) if the origin is shifted to the right by $a$. Note that this is the earlier equation with a change of parameters; this to be expected from the fact that two poles are interchangeable in the construction of the curve.

## Equations in the complex plane, rectangular coordinates and orthogonal trajectories {#Equations_in_the_complex_plane,_rectangular_coordinates_and_orthogonal_trajectories}

Let $q = m/n$ where $m$ and $n$ are integers and the fraction is in [lowest terms](wikipedia://en/14822). In the notation of the previous section, we have $\theta_1 = q \theta + \theta_0$ or $n \theta_1 = m \theta + n \theta_0$. If $z = x + iy$ then $\theta = \arg(z),\ \theta_1 = \arg(z-a)$, so the equation becomes $n\ \arg(z-a) = m\ \arg(z) + n\ \theta_0$ or $m\ \arg(z) - n\ \arg(z-a) = \arg(z^m (z-a)^{-n} ) = const$. This can also be written

$$\frac{\operatorname{Re}(z^m (z-a)^{-n})}{\operatorname{Im}(z^m (z-a)^{-n})} = const.$$

from which it is relatively simple to derive the Cartesian equation given m and n. The function $w = z^m (z-a)^{-n}$ is analytic so the [orthogonal trajectories](wikipedia://en/2564849) of the family $arg(w) = const.$ are the curves $|w| = const$, or $\frac{|z|^m}{|z-a|^n} = const.$

## Parametric equations {#Parametric_equations}

Let $q = m/n$ where $m$ and $n$ are integers, and let $\theta = n p$ where $p$ is a parameter. Then converting the polar equation above to [parametric equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_equations) produces

$$x = a \frac {\sin [m p + \theta_0] \cos n p}{\sin [(m - n) p + \theta_0]}, y = a \frac {\sin [m p + \theta_0] \sin n p}{\sin [(m - n) p + \theta_0]}\!.$$

Applying the [angle addition rule for sine](wikipedia://en/25804104#Angle_sum_and_difference_identities) produces

$$x = a \frac {\sin [m p + \theta_0] \cos n p}{\sin [(m - n) p + \theta_0]} = a + a \frac {\cos [m p + \theta_0] \sin n p}{\sin [(m - n) p + \theta_0]} = {a \over 2} + {a \over 2} \frac {\sin [(m + n) p + \theta_0]}{\sin [(m - n) p + \theta_0]}\!.$$

So if the origin is shifted to the right by $a/2$ then the parametric equations are

$$x = {a \over 2} \cdot \frac {\sin [(m + n) p + \theta_0]}{\sin [(m - n) p + \theta_0]}, y = a \frac {\sin [m p + \theta_0] \sin n p}{\sin [(m - n) p + \theta_0]}\!.$$

These are the equations for Plateau curves when $\theta_0 = 0$, or

$$x = {a \over 2} \frac {\sin (m + n) p}{\sin (m - n) p}, y = a \frac {\sin m p \sin n p}{\sin (m - n) p}\!.$$

## Inversive triplets {#Inversive_triplets}

The [inverse](wikipedia://en/15040455) with respect to the circle with radius a and center at the origin of

$$r = a \frac {\sin [q \theta + \theta_0]}{\sin [(q-1) \theta + \theta_0]}$$

is

$$r = a \frac {\sin [(q-1) \theta + \theta_0]}{\sin [q \theta + \theta_0]} = a \frac {\sin [(1-q) \theta - \theta_0]}{\sin [((1-q)-1) \theta - \theta_0]}.$$

This is another curve in the family. The inverse with respect to the other pole produces yet another curve in the same family and the two inverses are in turn inverses of each other. Therefore, each curve in the family is a member of a triple, each of which belongs to the family and is an inverse of the other two. The values of q in this family are

$$q,\ \frac{1}{q},\ 1-q, \frac{1}{1-q},\ \frac{q-1}{q},\ \frac{q}{q-1}.$$

## Sectrix properties {#Sectrix_properties}

Let $q = m/n$ where $m$ and $n$ are integers in lowest terms and assume $\theta_0$ is [constructible with compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions). (The value of $\theta_0$ is usually 0 in practice so this is not normally an issue.) Let $\varphi$ be a given angle and suppose that the sectrix of Maclaurin has been drawn with poles $P$ and $P_1$ according to the construction above. Construct a ray from $P_1$ at angle $\varphi+\theta_0$ and let $Q$ be the point of intersection of the ray and the sectrix and draw $PQ$. If $\theta$ is the angle of this line then

$$\varphi+\theta_0=\theta_1=q \theta + \theta_0$$

so $\theta = \frac{n\varphi}{m}$. By repeatedly subtracting $\theta$ and $\varphi$ from each other as in the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377), the angle $\varphi/m$ can be constructed. Thus, the curve is an *m*-sectrix, meaning that with the aid of the curve an arbitrary angle can be divided by any integer. This is a generalization of the concept of a [trisectrix](wikipedia://en/2909893) and examples of these will be found below.

Now draw a ray with angle $\varphi$ from $P$ and $Q'$ be the point of intersection of this ray with the curve. The angle of $P'Q'$ is

$$\theta_1=q\theta+\theta_0=q\varphi+\theta_0$$

and subtracting $\theta_0$ gives an angle of

$$q\varphi = \frac{m\varphi}{n}.$$

Applying the Euclidean Algorithm again gives an angle of $\varphi/n$ showing that the curve is also an *n*-sectrix.

Finally, draw a ray from $P$ with angle $\pi/2-\varphi-\theta_0$ and a ray from $P'$ with angle $\pi/2+\varphi+\theta_0$, and let $C$ be the point of intersection. This point is on the perpendicular bisector of $PP'$ so there is a circle with center $C$ containing $P$ and $P'$. $\angle PCP' = 2(\varphi+\theta_0)$ so any point on the circle forms an angle of $\varphi+\theta_0$ between $P$ and $P'$. (This is, in fact, one of the [Apollonian circles](wikipedia://en/5023947) of *P* and *P**.) Let $Q''$ be the point intersection of this circle and the curve. Then*** $\varphi+\theta_0 = \angle PQ''P' = \psi = \theta_1-\theta = (q-1)\theta+\theta_0$ so

$$\varphi = \frac{(m-n)\theta}{n},\ \theta=\frac{n\varphi}{m-n}.$$

Applying a Euclidean algorithm a third time gives an angle of $\varphi/(m-n)$, showing that the curve is an (*m*−*n*)-sectrix as well.

## Specific cases {#Specific_cases}

### *q* = 0 {#q_=_0}

This is the curve

$$r = a \frac {\sin \theta_0}{\sin (-\theta + \theta_0)}$$

which is a line through $(a, 0)$.

### *q* = 1 {#q_=_1}

This is a circle containing the origin and $(a, 0)$. It has polar equation

$$r = a \frac {\sin (\theta + \theta_0)}{\sin \theta_0}.$$

It is the inverse with respect to the origin of the *q* = 0 case. The orthogonal trajectories of the family of circles is the family $\frac{|z-a|}{|z|} = const.$ These form the [Apollonian circles](wikipedia://en/5023947) with poles $(0, 0)$ and $(a, 0)$.

### *q* = -1 {#q_=_-1}

These curves have polar equation

$$r = a \frac {\sin (-\theta + \theta_0)}{\sin (-2\theta + \theta_0)},$$

complex equation $arg(z(z-a)) = const.$ In rectangular coordinates this becomes $x^2 - y^2 - x = c(2xy - y)$ which is a conic. From the polar equation it is evident that the curves has asymptotes at $\theta = \theta_0 /2$ and $\theta_0 /2 + \pi/2$ which are at right angles. So the conics are, in fact, rectangular hyperbolas. The center of the hyperbola is always $(a/2, 0)$. The orthogonal trajectories of this family are given by $|z||z-a| = c$ which is the family of [Cassini ovals](wikipedia://en/2040906) with foci $(0, 0)$ and $(a, 0)$.

### Trisectrix of Maclaurin {#Trisectrix_of_Maclaurin}

In the case where $q=3$ (or $q=1/3$ by switching the poles) and $\theta_0 = 0$, the equation is

$$r= a \frac{\sin 3\theta}{\sin 2\theta} = {a \over 2} \frac{4 \cos^2 \theta - 1} {\cos \theta} = {a \over 2} (4 \cos \theta - \sec \theta).$$

This is the [Trisectrix of Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/2738114) which is specific case whose generalization is the sectrix of Maclaurin. The construction above gives a method that this curve may be used as a trisectrix.

### Limaçon trisectrix and rose {#Limaçon_trisectrix_and_rose}

In the case where $q=3/2$ (or $q=2/3$ by switching the poles) and $\theta_0 = 0$, the equation is

$$r=a \frac {\sin \tfrac{3}{2}\theta}{\sin \tfrac{1}{2}\theta} = a(3\cos^2 \tfrac{1}{2}\theta - \sin^2 \tfrac{1}{2}\theta) = a(1+2\cos\theta).$$

This is the [Limaçon trisectrix](wikipedia://en/18635261).

The equation with the origin take to be the other pole is the [rose](wikipedia://en/727472) curve that has the same shape

$$r=-a \frac {\sin \tfrac{2}{3}\theta}{\sin -\tfrac{1}{3}\theta} = 2a\cos \tfrac{1}{3}\theta.$$

The 3 in the numerator of *q* and the construction above give a method that the curve may be used as a trisectrix.
