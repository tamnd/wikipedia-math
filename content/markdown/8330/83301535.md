# Nut graph (graph theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nut graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **nut graph** is a [finite simple](wikipedia://en/7445) [graph](wikipedia://en/12401) with at least two vertices whose [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) has a [nullity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullity_%28linear_algebra%29) equal to one and whose kernel is spanned by a vector with no zero entries.[^o2][^o3] This class of graphs was introduced by [Irene Sciriha](wikipedia://en/67781978) and [Iván Gutman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iván_Gutman) in 1998.[^o2] Nut graphs arise frequently in [spectral graph theory](wikipedia://en/534914) and [chemical graph theory](wikipedia://en/14222708).[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $G$ be a finite simple graph with vertex set $V(G)=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ and adjacency matrix $A(G)$. The graph $G$ is a nut graph if $n\geq 2$, $\dim\ker A(G)=1$, and every nonzero vector $x=[x_1,\ldots,x_n]^T$ satisfying $A(G)x=0$ has $x_i\ne 0$ for every $i$.[^o2]

The condition $\dim\ker A(G)=1$ is equivalent to saying that $0$ is an [eigenvalue](wikipedia://en/2161429#Matrices) of $A(G)$ of multiplicity one. A vector with no zero coordinates is called a *full vector*.[^o7] Thus, a nut graph is a graph whose adjacency matrix has a one-dimensional kernel spanned by a full vector.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The smallest nut graphs have seven vertices,[^o10] and the three such graphs are called the Sciriha graphs.[^o11] Nut graphs exist on every order $n\geq 7$.[^o12]

The $n$-[antiprism graph](wikipedia://en/45012210) is a nut graph when $n$ is not divisible by $3$.[^o10] The [Frucht graph](wikipedia://en/23784721) is a cubic polyhedral nut graph.[^o13]

:::

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Every nut graph is connected, non-bipartite, and without [leaf vertices](wikipedia://en/638899#Vertex_degree).[^o2] Deletion of any vertex from a nut graph results in a non-singular graph.[^o16]

:::

::: {#o18 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Constructions"}

## Constructions {#Constructions}

Several graph operations are known that produce larger nut graphs from smaller ones.[^o2]

The coalescence of two vertex-rooted graphs $(G_1,v_1)$ and $(G_2,v_2)$ is obtained from the disjoint union of $G_1$ and $G_2$ by identifying $v_1$ and $v_2$ as a single vertex. If $G_1$ and $G_2$ are nut graphs, then their coalescence at arbitrary chosen vertices is again a nut graph.[^o16]

:::

## Recognition and generation {#Recognition_and_generation}

Nut graphs can be recognized by determining the [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) or [nullity](wikipedia://en/17458663) of the adjacency matrix and checking whether a vector spanning the nullspace has a zero coordinate.[^o3]

Nutgen is a generator for nut graphs.[^o20] Nutgen has been used to generate all non-isomorphic nut graphs up to 13 vertices and all chemical nut graphs up to 22 vertices. Further enumerations include nut graphs among [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508) [polyhedral graphs](wikipedia://en/24656270) up to 34 vertices, nut graphs among [fullerene graphs](wikipedia://en/10628) up to 250 vertices, and regular nut graphs for degrees up to 8. Catalogues of these nut graphs are available in the House of Graphs.[^o21] The generation of nut graphs up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/247577) can be performed by an algorithm based on the canonical construction path method.[^o3]

::: {#o23 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Nut graphs have been experimentally realized using [coaxial cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaxial_cable) networks.[^o24]

:::

## Generalisations {#Generalisations}

The notion of a nut graph has been extended to [signed graphs](wikipedia://en/2472618).[^o26]

There are directed analogues of nut graphs. For a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986), the adjacency matrix need not be symmetric, so the right kernel and left kernel may differ. A digraph whose right kernel is spanned by a full vector is called dextro-nut, and a digraph whose left kernel is spanned by a full vector is called laevo-nut. A digraph satisfying both conditions is called bi-nut. A digraph is ambi-nut if it is bi-nut and its kernel and co-kernel are spanned by the same full vector.[^o27]
