# Broken stick problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Broken stick problem"}

*Image: 4 views of the broken stick problem.svg.* Four views of the broken stick problem. Upper left: A line segment broken into three parts by two random points $x$ and $x$. Upper right: A triangle having side lengths equal to the three parts. Lower left: [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740)'s visualization using the three part lengths as barycentric coordinates in an equilateral triangle. The blue shaded [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743), of 1/4 the area, corresponds to the partitions that form triangles. Lower right: [Richard Hamming](wikipedia://en/99326)'s visualization using the two split points as Cartesian coordinates in a unit square. The blue shaded [crossed square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossed_square), of 1/4 the area, corresponds to the partitions that form triangles.

In [geometric probability](wikipedia://en/733613), the **broken stick problem** asks for the probability that one can form a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) from the three parts of a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) that has been split randomly into three parts. Three line segments form a triangle if and only if they obey the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941): each segment's length is less than the sum of the other two lengths. The problem was posed by [Emanuel Czuber](wikipedia://en/34422782) in 1884. As with other problems in geometric probability, the answer depends on how one formalizes this random split using a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543).

:::

For two split points that are chosen [uniformly](wikipedia://en/1699223) and independently in the given line segment, the three lengths can be represented as the three [barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinates_%28mathematics%29) of a point in an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) whose height is the length of the original line segment, or equivalently as the three distances of the point from the sides of the triangle. (This is an application of [Viviani's theorem](wikipedia://en/10460511).) For this two-dimensional interpretation of the problem, found by [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), the uniform distribution of the two split points corresponds to a uniform distribution of points within the equilateral triangle, and a pair of split points that forms a triangle corresponds to a point within the [medial triangle](wikipedia://en/3339743) of the given triangle. The area of the medial triangle is $\tfrac14$ the area of the outer equilateral triangle, and so for this distribution of split points, the probability of forming a triangle is $\tfrac14$.

An alternative two-dimensional interpretation uses the two split points as the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of a point in the [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301); the region of the unit square in which the two splits form three segments of a triangle is a [crossed square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossed_square) with its vertices at the midpoints of the unit square, again having area $\tfrac14$ of the total.

An alternative probability distribution chooses the first split point uniformly at random, chooses one of the two resulting line segments randomly (with probability $\tfrac12$ of choosing each segment), and then chooses a second split point uniformly at random within the chosen segment. For this model of the problem, the probability of forming a triangle is $\tfrac16$.

A generalization of the problem asks whether a line segment broken into $k$ pieces can form a $k$-gon.
