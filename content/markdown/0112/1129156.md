# Twelfth root of two

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Twelfth root of two"}

The **twelfth root of two** or $\sqrt[12]{2}$ (or [equivalently](wikipedia://en/235029#Identities_and_properties) $2^{1/12}$) is an [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158) [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) approximately equal to 1.0594631. It is important in Western [music theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory), where it represents the [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) ([musical interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_interval)) of a [semitone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitone) (Minor second on C.mid) in [12-tone equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve-tone_equal_temperament). This number was first proposed in relation to [musical tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_tuning) in the 16th and 17th centuries. It allows measurement and comparison of different intervals (frequency ratios) as consisting of different numbers of a single interval, the equal-tempered semitone (for example, a minor third is 3 semitones, a major third is 4 semitones, and a perfect fifth is 7 semitones). Semitones are divided into 100 [cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_%28music%29) (1 cent = $\sqrt[1200]{2}=2^{1/1200}$).

:::

## Numerical value {#Numerical_value}

The [twelfth root](wikipedia://en/235029) of [two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two) to 20 significant figures is \1.0594630943592952646. The [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) begins \[1: 16, 1, 4, 2, 7, 1, 1, 2, 2, 7, 4, 1, 2, 1, ...\], so a simple rational approximation is $\tfrac{18}{17}$.

## The equal-tempered chromatic scale {#The_equal-tempered_chromatic_scale}

A [musical interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28music%29) is a ratio of frequencies and the [equal-tempered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_temperament) chromatic scale divides the [octave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave) (which has a ratio of 2:1) into 12 equal parts. Each note has a frequency that is 2${}^{\tfrac{1}{12}}$ times that of the one below it.[^o6]

Applying this value successively to the tones of a chromatic scale, starting from **A** above [middle **C**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_C) (known as [A<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A440_%28pitch_standard%29)) with a frequency of 440 Hz, produces the following sequence of [pitches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_%28music%29):

The final **A** (A<sub>5</sub>: 880 Hz) is exactly twice the frequency of the lower **A** (A<sub>4</sub>: 440 Hz), that is, one octave higher.

### Other tuning scales {#Other_tuning_scales}

Other tuning scales use slightly different interval ratios:

- The [just](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_intonation) or [Pythagorean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_tuning) perfect fifth is 3/2, and the difference between the equal-tempered perfect fifth and the just is a [grad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grad_%28musical_interval%29), the 12th root of the [Pythagorean comma](wikipedia://en/177721) ($3 / 2^{19/12}$).
- The equal-tempered [Bohlen–Pierce scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohlen–Pierce_scale) uses the interval of the 13th root of three ($\sqrt[13]{3}$).
- Stockhausen's *[Studie II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studie_II)* (1954) makes use of the 25th root of five ($\sqrt[25]{5}$), a compound major third divided into 5×5 parts.
- The delta scale is based on ≈$\sqrt[50]{3/2}$.
- The [gamma scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_scale) is based on ≈$\sqrt[20]{3/2}$.
- The [beta scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_scale) is based on ≈$\sqrt[11]{3/2}$.
- The [alpha scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_scale) is based on ≈$\sqrt[9]{3/2}$.

## Pitch adjustment {#Pitch_adjustment}

*Image: Monochord ET.png.* One octave of 12-tet on a monochord (linear)

*Image: Pitch class space star.svg.* The [chromatic circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_circle) depicts equal distances between notes (logarithmic)

Since the frequency ratio of a semitone is close to 106% ($100\sqrt[12]{2} \approx 105.946$), increasing or decreasing the playback speed of a recording by 6% will shift the pitch up or down by about a semitone, or "half-step". Upscale [reel-to-reel magnetic tape recorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reel-to-reel_audio_tape_recording) typically have pitch adjustments of up to ±6%, generally used to match the playback or recording pitch to other music sources having slightly different tunings (or possibly recorded on equipment that was not running at quite the right speed). Modern recording studios utilize digital [pitch shifting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_shift) to achieve similar results, ranging from [cents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_%28music%29) up to several half-steps. Reel-to-reel adjustments also affect the tempo of the recorded sound, while digital shifting does not.

## History {#History}

Historically this number was proposed for the first time in relation to musical tuning in 1580 (drafted, rewritten 1610) by [Simon Stevin](wikipedia://en/72586).[^o11] [Vincenzo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Galilei) may have been the first European to suggest 12-tone equal temperament, in 1581.[^n1] The 12th root of two was first calculated in 1584 by the Chinese mathematician and musician [Zhu Zaiyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhu_Zaiyu) using an abacus to reach 24 decimal places accurately,[^n1] calculated circa 1605 by Flemish mathematician [Simon Stevin](wikipedia://en/72586),[^n1] in 1636 by the French mathematician [Marin Mersenne](wikipedia://en/72947), and in 1691 by German musician [Andreas Werckmeister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Werckmeister).[^o12]
