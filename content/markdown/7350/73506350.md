# Cubicity

*Image: A graph with cubicity 2.svg.* A graph with cubicity $2$, realized as the intersection graph of axis-parallel unit $2$-cubes, i.e. axis-parallel unit squares, in the plane.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cubicity"}

In the mathematical field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), **cubicity** is a [graph invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_invariant) defined to be the smallest dimension such that a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) can be realized as the [intersection graph](wikipedia://en/7726759) of axis-parallel [unit cubes](wikipedia://en/407763) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697).[^o2] Cubicity was introduced by [Fred S. Roberts](wikipedia://en/26225243) in 1969, along with a related invariant called [boxicity](wikipedia://en/11167471) that considers the smallest dimension needed to represent a graph as the intersection graph of axis-parallel [rectangles](wikipedia://en/1688550) in Euclidean space.[^o3]

:::

*Image: Indifference graph = unit interval graph.svg.* An [indifference graph](wikipedia://en/42535190) with cubicity $1$, realized as the intersection graph of unit $1$-cubes, i.e. unit intervals, on the real [number line](wikipedia://en/287188).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

This article only considers [simple, undirected graphs](wikipedia://en/325806), with finite and non-empty vertex sets.[^o6][^o7]

The *cubicity* of a graph $G$, denoted by $\operatorname{cub}(G)$, is the smallest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $k$ such that $G$ can be represented as the intersection graph of axis-parallel [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) unit $k$-cubes in $k$-dimensional Euclidean space, $\mathrm{E}^k$.[^o8][^o9][^o10]

For $k \geq 1$, a graph $G$ can have such a representation in $\mathrm{E}^k$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $G$ is the intersection of $k$ indifference graphs on the same vertex set as $G$.[^n1]

The cubicity of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) is defined to be zero.[^n2]

:::

## Relations to certain graph classes, upper bound {#Relations_to_certain_graph_classes,_upper_bound}

For a graph $G$, $\operatorname{cub}(G) = 0$ if and only if $G$ is complete.[^n3]

For a graph $G$, $\operatorname{cub}(G) = 1$ if and only if $G$ is a [unit interval graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_interval_graph) that is not complete.[^n4]

For $n \in \mathbb{N}^*$, $\operatorname{cub}(K_{1,n}) = \lfloor \log_2 (2n-1) \rfloor$, where $K_{1,n}$ denotes the [star graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_graph), a tree with one internal vertex and $n$ leaves, and where $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ denotes the [floor function](wikipedia://en/54267).[^n5]

For $p \in \mathbb{N}^*$, $\operatorname{cub}(K_{p(2)}) = p$, where $K_{p(2)}$ denotes the [complete multipartite graph](wikipedia://en/39469099) with $p$ parts of [cardinal](wikipedia://en/6173) $2$.[^n4][^n3]

For a graph $G$ on $n$ vertices, $\operatorname{cub}(G) \leq \lfloor 2n/3 \rfloor$. Moreover, this upper bound is best possible in terms of $n$.[^n4][^n6]

## Relations to other graph dimensions {#Relations_to_other_graph_dimensions}

### Relations to boxicity: bounds {#Relations_to_boxicity:_bounds}

The cubicity of a graph $G$ is closely related to its boxicity, denoted by $\operatorname{box}(G).$ The definition of boxicity is essentially the same as that of cubicity, but with axis-parallel [boxes](wikipedia://en/1688550) instead of axis-parallel unit cubes.

Since a cube is a special case of a box, the cubicity of a graph $G$ is always an upper bound for its boxicity, i.e., $~ \operatorname{box}(G) \leq \operatorname{cub}(G).$

In the other direction, it can be shown that for a graph $G$ on $n$ vertices, $~ \operatorname{cub}(G) \leq \lceil \log_2 n \rceil \operatorname{box}(G), ~$ where $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ denotes the [ceiling function](wikipedia://en/54267). Moreover, this upper bound is tight.[^o23]

### Relations to sphericity {#Relations_to_sphericity}

The [sphericity](wikipedia://en/73506123) of a graph $G,$ denoted by $\operatorname{sph}(G),$ is defined in the same way as cubicity but with [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859) instead of axis-parallel unit cubes.

For certain graphs, cubicity exceeds sphericity; the five-pointed star, $K_{1,5},$ is an example: $~ \operatorname{cub}(K_{1,5}) = 3 > \operatorname{sph}(K_{1,5}) = 2.$[^o25]

In the other direction, graphs $G$ can be constructed so that $~ \operatorname{sph}(G) > \operatorname{cub}(G) = k, ~$ for $k \in \{ 2,3 \}.$[^o26]
