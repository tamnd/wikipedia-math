# False attribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="False attribution"}

**False attribution** may refer to:

:::

- Misattribution in general, when a quotation or work is accidentally, traditionally, or based on bad information attributed to the wrong person or group.
- A specific [fallacy](wikipedia://en/53986) where an advocate appeals to an irrelevant, unqualified, unidentified, biased, or fabricated source in support of an argument.[^o2]

## Incorrect identification of source {#Incorrect_identification_of_source}

One particular case of misattribution is the [Matthew effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_effect). A [quotation](wikipedia://en/25199) is often attributed to someone more famous than the real author. This leads the quotation to be more famous, but the real author to be forgotten (see also: [obliteration by incorporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obliteration_by_incorporation) and [Churchillian Drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchillian_Drift)).[^o4]

Such misattributions may originate as a sort of fallacious argument, if use of the quotation is meant to be persuasive, and attachment to a more famous person (whether intentionally or through misremembering) would lend it more authority.

In Jewish biblical studies, an entire group of falsely-attributed books is known as the [pseudepigrapha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudepigrapha).

## Fallacy {#Fallacy}

A fraudulent advocate may go so far as to fabricate a source in order to support a claim. For example, the "Levitt Institute" was a fake organisation created in 2009 solely for the purposes of (successfully) fooling the [Australian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia) media into reporting that [Sydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney) was Australia’s most naive city.[^o6]

Contextomy ([quoting out of context](wikipedia://en/4154982)) is a type of false attribution.[^o7]
