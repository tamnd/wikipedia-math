# Point process notation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Point process notation"}

In [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **point process notation** comprises the range of [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) used to symbolically represent [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) [objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) known as [point processes](wikipedia://en/2147685), which are used in related fields such as [stochastic geometry](wikipedia://en/22037813), [spatial statistics](wikipedia://en/9467349) and [continuum percolation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_percolation_theory) and frequently serve as [mathematical models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_models) of random phenomena, representable as points, in time, space or both.

:::

The notation varies due to the histories of certain mathematical fields and the different interpretations of point processes,[^o2][^o3][^o4] and borrows notation from mathematical areas of study such as [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) and [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553).[^o2]

## Interpretation of point processes {#Interpretation_of_point_processes}

The notation, as well as the terminology, of point processes depends on their setting and interpretation as mathematical objects which under certain assumptions can be interpreted as random [sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequences) of points, random [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of points or [random counting measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_counting_measure).[^o2]

### Random sequences of points {#Random_sequences_of_points}

In some mathematical frameworks, a given point process may be considered as a sequence of points with each point randomly positioned in *d*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>*d*</sup>[^o2] as well as some other more abstract [mathematical spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_space). In general, whether or not a random sequence is equivalent to the other interpretations of a point process depends on the underlying mathematical space, but this holds true for the setting of finite-dimensional Euclidean space **R**<sup>*d*</sup>.[^o7]

### Random set of points {#Random_set_of_points}

A point process is called *simple* if no two (or more points) coincide in location with [probability one](wikipedia://en/351908). Given that often point processes are simple and the order of the points does not matter, a collection of random points can be considered as a random set of points[^o2][^o9] The theory of random sets was independently developed by [David Kendall](wikipedia://en/4631370) and [Georges Matheron](wikipedia://en/4415402). In terms of being considered as a random set, a sequence of random points is a random closed set if the sequence has no [accumulation points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point#Types_of_limit_points) with probability one[^o10]

A point process is often denoted by a single letter,[^o2][^o11][^o12] for example ${N}$, and if the point process is considered as a random set, then the corresponding notation:[^o2]

$$x\in {N},$$

is used to denote that a random point $x$ is an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) of (or [belongs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29#Notation_and_terminology) to) the point process ${N}$. The theory of random sets can be applied to point processes owing to this interpretation, which alongside the random sequence interpretation has resulted in a point process being written as:

$$\{x_1, x_2,\dots \}=\{x\}_i,$$

which highlights its interpretation as either a random sequence or random closed set of points.[^o2] Furthermore, sometimes an uppercase letter denotes the point process, while a lowercase denotes a point from the process, so, for example, the point $\textstyle x$ (or $\textstyle x_i$) belongs to or is a point of the point process $\textstyle X$, or with set notation, $\textstyle x\in X$.[^o12]

### Random measures {#Random_measures}

To denote the number of points of ${N}$ located in some [Borel set](wikipedia://en/43327) $B$, it is sometimes written [^o11]

$$\Phi(B) =\#( B \cap {N}),$$

where $\Phi(B)$ is a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) and $\#$ is a [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786), which gives the number of points in some set. In this [mathematical expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_expression) the point process is denoted by:

$${N}.$$

On the other hand, the symbol:

$$\Phi$$

represents the number of points of ${N}$ in $B$. In the context of random measures, one can write:

$$\Phi(B)=n$$

to denote that there is the set $B$ that contains $n$ points of ${N}$. In other words, a point process can be considered as a [random measure](wikipedia://en/10040846) that assigns some non-negative integer-valued [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) to sets.[^o2] This interpretation has motivated a point process being considered just another name for a *random counting measure*[^o14] and the techniques of random measure theory offering another way to study point processes,[^o2][^o15] which also induces the use of the various notations used in [integration](wikipedia://en/15532#Terminology_and_notation) and measure theory.

## Dual notation {#Dual_notation}

The different interpretations of point processes as random sets and counting measures is captured with the often used notation [^o2][^o4][^o12][^o17] in which:

- ${N}$ denotes a set of random points.
- ${N}(B)$ denotes a random variable that gives the number of points of ${N}$ in $B$ (hence it is a random counting measure).

Denoting the counting measure again with $\#$, this dual notation implies:

$${N}(B) =\#(B \cap {N}).$$

## Sums {#Sums}

If $f$ is some [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) on **R**<sup>*d*</sup>, then the sum of $f(x)$ over all the points $x$ in ${N}$ can be written in a number of ways [^o2][^o4] such as:

$$f(x_1) + f(x_2)+ \cdots$$

which has the random sequence appearance, or with set notation as:

$$\sum_{x\in {N}}f(x)$$

or, equivalently, with integration notation as:

$$\int_{\textbf{R}^d} f(x) {N}(dx)$$

which puts an emphasis on the interpretation of ${N}$ being a random counting measure. An alternative integration notation may be used to write this integral as:

$$\int_{\textbf{R}^d} f \, d{N}$$

The dual interpretation of point processes is illustrated when writing the number of ${N}$ points in a set $B$ as:

$${N}(B)= \sum_{x\in {N}}1_B(x)$$

where the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) $1_B(x) =1$ if the point $x$ is exists in $B$ and zero otherwise, which in this setting is also known as a [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002).[^o17] In this expression the random measure interpretation is on the [left-hand side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-hand_side) while the random set notation is used is on the right-hand side.

## Expectations {#Expectations}

The [average](wikipedia://en/60167) or [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of a sum of functions over a point process is written as:[^o2][^o4]

$$E\left[\sum_{x\in {N}}f(x)\right] \qquad \text{or} \qquad \int_{\textbf{N}}\sum_{x\in {N}}f(x) P(d{N}),$$

where (in the random measure sense) $P$ is an appropriate [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) defined on the space of [counting measures](wikipedia://en/191786) $\textbf{N}$. The expected value of ${N}(B)$ can be written as:[^o2]

$$E[{N}(B)]=E\left( \sum_{x\in {N}}1_B(x)\right) \qquad \text{or} \qquad \int_{\textbf{N}}\sum_{x\in {N}}1_B(x) P(d{N}).$$

which is also known as the first [moment measure](wikipedia://en/40969212) of ${N}$. The expectation of such a random sum, known as a *shot noise process* in the theory of point processes, can be calculated with [Campbell's theorem](wikipedia://en/34929672#Campbell's_theorem:_general_point_process).[^o3]

## Uses in other fields {#Uses_in_other_fields}

Point processes are employed in other mathematical and statistical disciplines, hence the notation may be used in fields such [stochastic geometry](wikipedia://en/22037813), [spatial statistics](wikipedia://en/9467349) or [continuum percolation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_percolation_theory), and areas which use the methods and theory from these fields.
