# Compound probability distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Compound probability distribution"}

In [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a **compound probability distribution** (also known as a **[mixture distribution](wikipedia://en/708242)** or **contagious distribution**) is the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) that results from assuming that a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) is distributed according to some parametrized distribution, with (some of) the parameters of that distribution themselves being random variables. If the parameter is a [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503), the resulting mixture is also called a **scale mixture**.

:::

The compound distribution ("unconditional distribution") is the result of [marginalizing](wikipedia://en/506077) (integrating) over the *latent* random variable(s) representing the parameter(s) of the parametrized distribution ("conditional distribution").

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **compound probability distribution** is the probability distribution that results from assuming that a random variable $X$ is distributed according to some parametrized distribution $F$ with an unknown parameter $\theta$ that is again distributed according to some other distribution $G$. The resulting distribution $H$ is said to be the distribution that results from compounding $F$ with $G$. The parameter's distribution $G$ is also called the **mixing distribution** or **latent distribution**. Technically, the *unconditional* distribution $H$ results from *[marginalizing](wikipedia://en/506077)* over $G$, i.e., from integrating out the unknown parameter(s) $\theta$. Its [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) is given by:

$$p_H(x) = {\displaystyle \int\limits p_F(x|\theta)\,p_G(\theta) \operatorname{d}\!\theta}$$

The same formula applies analogously if some or all of the variables are vectors.

From the above formula, one can see that a compound distribution essentially is a special case of a [marginal distribution](wikipedia://en/506077): The *[joint distribution](wikipedia://en/879637)* of $x$ and $\theta$ is given by $p(x,\theta)=p(x|\theta)p(\theta)$, and the compound results as its marginal distribution: ${\textstyle p(x) = \int p(x,\theta) \operatorname{d}\!\theta }$. If the domain of $\theta$ is discrete, then the distribution is again a special case of a [mixture distribution](wikipedia://en/708242).

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

### General {#General}

The compound distribution $H$ will depend on the specific expression of each distribution, as well as which parameter of $F$ is distributed according to the distribution $G$, and the parameters of $H$ will include any parameters of $G$ that are not marginalized, or integrated, out. The [support](wikipedia://en/381013) of $H$ is the same as that of $F$, and if the latter is a two-parameter distribution parameterized with the mean and variance, some general properties exist.

### Mean and variance {#Mean_and_variance}

The compound distribution's first two [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) are given by the [law of total expectation](wikipedia://en/312397) and the [law of total variance](wikipedia://en/312408):

$$\operatorname{E}_H[X] = \operatorname{E}_G\bigl[\operatorname{E}_F[X|\theta]\bigr]$$

$$\operatorname{Var}_H(X) = \operatorname{E}_G\bigl[\operatorname{Var}_F(X|\theta)\bigr] + \operatorname{Var}_G\bigl(\operatorname{E}_F[X|\theta]\bigr)$$

If the mean of $F$ is distributed as $G$, which in turn has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ the expressions above imply $\operatorname{E}_H[X] = \operatorname{E}_G[\theta] = \mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}_H(X) = \operatorname{Var}_F(X|\theta) + \operatorname{Var}_G(Y) = \tau^2 + \sigma^2$, where $\tau^2$ is the variance of $F$.

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

let $F$ and $G$ be probability distributions parameterized with mean and variance as

$$\begin{aligned} x &\sim \mathcal{F}(\theta,\tau^2) \\ \theta &\sim \mathcal{G}(\mu,\sigma^2) \end{aligned}$$

then denoting the probability density functions as $f(x|\theta) = p_F(x|\theta)$ and $g(\theta) = p_G(\theta)$ respectively, and $h(x)$ being the probability density of $H$ we have

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}_H[X] = \int_F x h(x)dx &= \int_F x \int_G f(x|\theta) g(\theta) d\theta dx \\ &= \int_G \int_F x f(x|\theta) dx\ g(\theta) d\theta \\ &= \int_G \operatorname{E}_F[X|\theta] g(\theta) d\theta \end{aligned}$$

and we have from the parameterization $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{G}$ that

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}_F[X|\theta] &= \int_F x f(x|\theta)dx = \theta \\ \operatorname{E}_G[\theta] &= \int_G \theta g(\theta)d\theta = \mu \end{aligned}$$

and therefore the mean of the compound distribution $\operatorname{E}_H[X] = \mu$ as per the expression for its first moment above.

The variance of $H$ is given by $\operatorname{E}_H[X^2] - (\operatorname{E}_H[X])^2$, and

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}_H[X^2] = \int_F x^2 h(x)dx &= \int_F x^2 \int_G f(x|\theta) g(\theta) d\theta dx \\ &= \int_G g(\theta)\int_F x^2 f(x|\theta) dx\ d\theta \\ &= \int_G g(\theta)(\tau^2+\theta^2)d\theta\\ &= \tau^2\int_G g(\theta)d\theta+\int_Gg(\theta)\theta^2d\theta\\ &= \tau^2+(\sigma^2+\mu^2), \end{aligned}$$

given the fact that $\int_F x^2 f(x\mid \theta) dx=\operatorname{E}_F[X^2\mid \theta]=\operatorname{Var}_F(X\mid\theta)+(\operatorname{E}_F[X\mid \theta])^2$ and $\int_G \theta^2 g(\theta)d\theta=\operatorname{E}_G[\theta^2 ]=\operatorname{Var}_G(\theta) + (\operatorname{E}_G[\theta])^2$. Finally we get

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}_H(X) &= \operatorname{E}_H[X^2] - (\operatorname{E}_H[X])^2 \\ &= \tau^2 + \sigma^2 \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Testing {#Testing}

Distributions of common [test statistics](wikipedia://en/1416975) result as compound distributions under their null hypothesis, for example in [Student's t-test](wikipedia://en/536080) (where the test statistic results as the ratio of a [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) and a [chi-squared](wikipedia://en/113424) random variable), or in the [F-test](wikipedia://en/318976) (where the test statistic is the ratio of two [chi-squared](wikipedia://en/113424) random variables).

### Overdispersion modeling {#Overdispersion_modeling}

Compound distributions are useful for modeling outcomes exhibiting [overdispersion](wikipedia://en/4190350), i.e., a greater amount of variability than would be expected under a certain model. For example, count data are commonly modeled using the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144), whose variance is equal to its mean. The distribution may be generalized by allowing for variability in its [rate parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_parameter), implemented via a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079), which results in a marginal [negative binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/45177). This distribution is similar in its shape to the Poisson distribution, but it allows for larger variances. Similarly, a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) may be generalized to allow for additional variability by compounding it with a [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) for its success probability parameter, which results in a [beta-binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/7377730).

### Bayesian inference {#Bayesian_inference}

Besides ubiquitous marginal distributions that may be seen as special cases of compound distributions, in [Bayesian inference](wikipedia://en/49571), compound distributions arise when, in the notation above, *F* represents the distribution of future observations and *G* is the [posterior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_distribution) of the parameters of *F*, given the information in a set of observed data. This gives a [posterior predictive distribution](wikipedia://en/35052447). Correspondingly, for the [prior predictive distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_predictive_distribution), *F* is the distribution of a new data point while *G* is the [prior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_distribution) of the parameters.

### Convolution {#Convolution}

[Convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of probability distributions (to derive the probability distribution of sums of random variables) may also be seen as a special case of compounding; here the sum's distribution essentially results from considering one summand as a random [location parameter](wikipedia://en/17516) for the other summand.[^o16]

## Computation {#Computation}

Compound distributions derived from [exponential family](wikipedia://en/339174) distributions often have a closed form. If analytical integration is not possible, numerical methods may be necessary.

Compound distributions may relatively easily be investigated using [Monte Carlo methods](wikipedia://en/56098), i.e., by generating random samples. It is often easy to generate random numbers from the distributions $p(\theta)$ as well as $p(x|\theta)$ and then utilize these to perform *[collapsed Gibbs sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapsed_Gibbs_sampling)* to generate samples from $p(x)$.

A compound distribution may usually also be approximated to a sufficient degree by a [mixture distribution](wikipedia://en/708242) using a finite number of mixture components, allowing to derive approximate density, distribution function etc.[^o16]

[Parameter estimation](wikipedia://en/1565926) ([maximum-likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum-likelihood_estimation) or [maximum-a-posteriori](wikipedia://en/1792433) estimation) within a compound distribution model may sometimes be simplified by utilizing the [EM-algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EM-algorithm).[^o19]

## Examples {#Examples}

- **Gaussian scale mixtures**:[^o22][^o23]
  - Compounding a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) with [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) distributed according to an [inverse gamma distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_gamma_distribution) (or equivalently, with [precision](wikipedia://en/24467258) distributed as a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079)) yields a non-standardized **[Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375)**.[^o24] This distribution has the same symmetrical shape as a normal distribution with the same central point, but has greater variance and [heavy tails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_tail).
  - Compounding a [Gaussian (or normal) distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) with variance distributed according to an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) (or with standard deviation according to a [Rayleigh distribution](wikipedia://en/262296)) yields a **[Laplace distribution](wikipedia://en/1106213)**. More generally, compounding a Gaussian (or normal) distribution with variance distributed according to a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) yields a **[variance-gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/6534096)**.
  - Compounding a [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) with variance distributed according to an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) whose rate parameter is itself distributed according to a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) yields a **[Normal-exponential-gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/26619534)**. (This involves two compounding stages. The variance itself then follows a [Lomax distribution](wikipedia://en/32253318); see below.)
  - Compounding a [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) with standard deviation distributed according to a [(standard) inverse uniform distribution](wikipedia://en/37560131#Inverse_uniform_distribution) yields a **[Slash distribution](wikipedia://en/23470822)**.
  - Compounding a [Gaussian (normal) distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) with a [Kolmogorov distribution](wikipedia://en/16714#Kolmogorov_distribution) yields a **[logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/542666)**.[^o25][^o22]
- **other Gaussian mixtures**:
  - Compounding a [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) with [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) distributed according to another [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) yields (again) a **[Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution)**.
  - Compounding a [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) with [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) distributed according to a shifted [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) yields an **[exponentially modified Gaussian distribution](wikipedia://en/34299105)**.
- Compounding a [Bernoulli distribution](wikipedia://en/199189) with probability of success $p$ distributed according to a distribution $X$ that has a defined expected value yields a Bernoulli distribution with success probability $E[X]$. An interesting consequence is that the dispersion of $X$ does not influence the dispersion of the resulting compound distribution.
- Compounding a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) with probability of success distributed according to a [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) yields a **[beta-binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/7377730)**. It possesses three parameters, a parameter $n$ (number of samples) from the binomial distribution and [shape parameters](wikipedia://en/9210865) $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from the beta distribution.[^o26][^o27]
- Compounding a [multinomial distribution](wikipedia://en/1045553) with probability vector distributed according to a [Dirichlet distribution](wikipedia://en/1117833) yields a **[Dirichlet-multinomial distribution](wikipedia://en/9169137)**.
- Compounding a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with [rate parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_parameter) distributed according to a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) yields a **[negative binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/45177)**.[^o28][^o29]
- Compounding a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with rate parameter distributed according to an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) yields a **[geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922)**.
- Compounding an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) with its [rate parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_parameter) distributed according to a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) yields a **[Lomax distribution](wikipedia://en/32253318)**.[^o30]
- Compounding a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) with [inverse scale parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_parameter) distributed according to another [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) yields a three-parameter **[beta prime distribution](wikipedia://en/3126406#Compound_gamma_distribution)**.[^o31]
- Compounding a [half-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/15906455) with its [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503) distributed according to a [Rayleigh distribution](wikipedia://en/262296) yields an **[exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906)**. This follows immediately from the [Laplace distribution](wikipedia://en/1106213) resulting as a [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) scale mixture; see above. The roles of conditional and mixing distributions may also be exchanged here; consequently, compounding a [Rayleigh distribution](wikipedia://en/262296) with its scale parameter distributed according to a [half-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/15906455) *also* yields an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906).
- A [Gamma(k=2,θ) - distributed](wikipedia://en/207079) random variable whose [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503) θ again is [uniformly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) distributed marginally yields an **[exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906)**.

:::

## Similar terms {#Similar_terms}

The notion of "compound distribution" as used e.g. in the definition of a [Compound Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/949562) or [Compound Poisson process](wikipedia://en/1082294) is different from the definition found in this article. The meaning in this article corresponds to what is used in e.g. [Bayesian hierarchical modeling](wikipedia://en/42734031).

The special case for compound probability distributions where the parametrized distribution $F$ is the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) is also called [mixed Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/70277231).
