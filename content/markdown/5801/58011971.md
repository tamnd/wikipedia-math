# ACE model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="ACE model"}

The **ACE model** is a [statistical model](wikipedia://en/27576) commonly used to analyze the results of [twin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_studies) and [adoption studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption_studies). This classic [behaviour genetic](wikipedia://en/24235330) model aims to partition the [phenotypic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotypic) [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) into three categories: [additive genetic variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_genetic_effects) (A), common (or shared) environmental factors (C), and specific (or nonshared) environmental factors plus [measurement error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_error) (E).[^o2] It is widely used in [genetic epidemiology](wikipedia://en/16017459) and [behavioural genetics](wikipedia://en/24235330).[^o3][^o4] The basic ACE model relies on several assumptions, including the absence of [assortative mating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assortative_mating),[^o5] that there is no [genetic dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_dominance) or [epistasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistasis),[^o6] that all genetic effects are additive, and the absence of [gene-environment interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene-environment_interaction).[^o4] In order to address these limitations, several variants of the ACE model have been developed, including an ACE-β model, which emphasizes the identification of causal effects,[^o4] and the ACDE model, which accounts for the effects of [genetic dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_%28genetics%29).[^o7]

:::
