# History of trigonometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="History of trigonometry"}

Early study of triangles can be traced to [Egyptian mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mathematics) ([Rhind Mathematical Papyrus](wikipedia://en/9210114)) and [Babylonian mathematics](wikipedia://en/3861353) during the 2nd millennium BC. Systematic study of [trigonometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric) functions began in [Hellenistic mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_mathematics), reaching [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India) as part of [Hellenistic astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_astronomy).[^n1] In [Indian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy), the study of trigonometric functions flourished in the [Gupta period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_period), especially due to [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204) (6th century AD), who discovered the [sine function, cosine function, and versine function.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_function)[^n2]

:::

During the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), the study of trigonometry continued in [Islamic mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematics), building on earlier foundations from [Indian](wikipedia://en/1848052) (notably [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204)'s sine and cosine concepts) and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) ([Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600)' chord table) sources.[^o4] Mathematicians such as [al-Khwarizmi](wikipedia://en/649861) and [Abu al-Wafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_al-Wafa) made important contributions, but the field was fundamentally transformed by [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](wikipedia://en/33642424) (1201–1274).

Al-Tusi was the first to treat trigonometry as an independent mathematical discipline, separate from astronomy.[^o5] He fully established all six trigonometric functions (sine, cosine, tangent, cotangent, secant, cosecant) with complete proofs, and formulated the plane and spherical law of sines, the form still taught in schools and universities today.[^o6] He also rewrote and corrected Ptolemy's Almagest, replacing chords with sine and tangent tables and adding practical, real-world examples.[^o7]

Through [translations of Arabic and Greek texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_translations_of_the_12th_century), this knowledge reached the [Latin West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_West). However, during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), [Regiomontanus](wikipedia://en/25929) (c. 1464) translated al-Tusi's work into Latin without crediting him, effectively erasing al-Tusi's name from European historiography.[^o8] Consequently, for centuries, European textbooks mistakenly attributed the development of trigonometry to Regiomontanus and earlier Greek sources, sidelining al-Tusi's central role.

Modern scholarship has since restored al-Tusi's credit: he is now recognized in academic literature as the "Master of Trigonometry", the one who gave the field its modern, independent form.[^o9]

The development of modern trigonometry shifted during the western [Age of Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment), beginning with 17th-century mathematics ([Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [James Stirling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Stirling_%28mathematician%29)) and reaching its modern form with [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) (1748).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The term "trigonometry" was derived from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) τρίγωνον *trigōnon*, "triangle" and μέτρον *metron*, "measure".[^o11]

The word sine is derived, indirectly, from the Sanskrit word *jya-ardha* used by Indian mathematicians, was shortened to *jiva* and was later adopted into Arabic as *jiba*, a phonetically similar but meaningless word, before being translated into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) as *sinus* and was finally imported as *sine* in English. (See [Jyā, koti-jyā and utkrama-jyā](wikipedia://en/26397073) and Sine and cosine#Etymology.) Particularly [Fibonacci](wikipedia://en/17949)'s *[sinus rectus arcus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinus_rectus_arcus)* proved influential in establishing the term.[^o12][^o13][^o14] [^n3][^n4]

The word *tangent* comes from Latin tangens meaning "touching", since the line[^o17] *touches* the circle of unit radius, whereas *secant* stems from Latin secans "cutting" since the line[^o17] *cuts* the circle (see the figure at [Pythagorean identities](wikipedia://en/25804104#Pythagorean_identities)).[^o18]

The prefix "[co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co_%28function_prefix%29)-" (in "cosine", "cotangent", "cosecant") is found in [Edmund Gunter](wikipedia://en/1399676)'s *Canon triangulorum* (1620), which defines the *cosinus* as an abbreviation for the *sinus complementi* (sine of the [complementary angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_angle)) and proceeds to define the *cotangens* similarly.[^o19][^o20]

The words "minute" and "second" are derived from the Latin phrases *[partes minutae primae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partes_minutae_primae)* and *[partes minutae secundae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partes_minutae_secundae)*.[^o21] These roughly translate to "first small parts" and "second small parts".

## Ancient {#Ancient}

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Trigonometry in ancient Egypt"}

### Ancient Near East {#Ancient_Near_East}

The ancient [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mathematics) and [Babylonians](wikipedia://en/3861353) had known of theorems on the ratios of the sides of similar triangles for many centuries. However, as pre-Hellenic societies lacked the concept of an angle measure, they were limited to studying the sides of triangles instead.[^o25]

The [Babylonian astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomer) kept detailed records on the rising and setting of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star), the motion of the [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet), and the solar and lunar [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse), all of which required familiarity with [angular](wikipedia://en/1196) distances measured on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239).[^o26] Based on one interpretation of the [Plimpton 322](wikipedia://en/2983547) [cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform_script) tablet (c. 1900 BC), some have even asserted that the ancient Babylonians had a table of secants but does not work in this context as without using circles and angles in the situation modern trigonometric notations will not apply.[^n5] There is, however, much debate as to whether it is a table of [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172), a solution of quadratic equations, or a [trigonometric table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_trigonometric_tables).[^o28]

The Egyptians, on the other hand, used a primitive form of trigonometry for building [pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid) in the 2nd millennium BC.[^o26] The [Rhind Mathematical Papyrus](wikipedia://en/9210114), written by the Egyptian scribe [Ahmes](wikipedia://en/386234) (c. 1680–1620 BC), contains the following problem related to trigonometry:[^o26]

"If a pyramid is 250 cubits high and the side of its base 360 cubits long, what is its *[seked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seked)*?" Ahmes' solution to the problem is the ratio of half the side of the base of the pyramid to its height, or the run-to-rise ratio of its face. In other words, the quantity he found for the *seked* is the cotangent of the angle to the base of the pyramid and its face.[^o26]

:::

### Classical antiquity {#Classical_antiquity}

*Image: Trigonometric Chord.svg.* The chord of an angle subtends the arc of the angle.

Ancient [Greek and Hellenistic mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) made use of the [chord](wikipedia://en/297947). Given a circle and an arc on the circle, the chord is the line that subtends the arc. A chord's perpendicular bisector passes through the center of the circle and bisects the angle. One half of the bisected chord is the sine of one half the bisected angle, that is,[^n6]

$$\mathrm{chord}\ \theta = 2 r\sin \frac{\theta}{2},$$

and consequently the sine function is also known as the *half-chord*. Due to this relationship, a number of trigonometric identities and theorems that are known today were also known to [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_civilization) mathematicians, but in their equivalent chord form.[^o31][^o36]

Although there is no trigonometry in the works of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) and [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844), in the strict sense of the word, there are theorems presented in a geometric way (rather than a trigonometric way) that are equivalent to specific trigonometric laws or formulas.[^o25] For instance, propositions twelve and thirteen of book two of the *Elements* are the [laws of cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_cosines) for obtuse and acute angles, respectively. Theorems on the lengths of chords are applications of the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222). And Archimedes' theorem on broken chords is equivalent to formulas for sines of sums and differences of angles.[^o25] To compensate for the lack of a [table of chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_chords), mathematicians of [Aristarchus](wikipedia://en/74857)' time would sometimes use the statement that, in modern notation, sin *α*/sin *β* \< *α*/*β* \< tan *α*/tan *β* whenever 0° \< β \< α \< 90°, now known as [Aristarchus's inequality](wikipedia://en/31152478).[^o34]

The first-known trigonometric table was apparently compiled by [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) of [Nicaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicaea) (180 – 125 BC), who is now consequently known as "the father of trigonometry."[^o32]His table of chords, analogous to modern tables of sine values, was the first to tabulate the corresponding arc and chord values for a series of angles.[^n7][^o32]

Although it is not known when the systematic use of the 360° circle came into mathematics, it is known that the systematic introduction of the 360° circle came a little after [Aristarchus of Samos](wikipedia://en/74857) composed *[On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Sizes_and_Distances_of_the_Sun_and_Moon)* (c. 260 BC), since he measured an angle in terms of a fraction of a quadrant.[^o34] It seems that the systematic use of the 360° circle is largely due to Hipparchus and his [table of chords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_chords). Hipparchus may have taken the idea of this division from [Hypsicles](wikipedia://en/12815034) who had earlier divided the day into 360 parts, a division of the day that may have been suggested by Babylonian astronomy.[^o35] In ancient astronomy, the zodiac had been divided into twelve "signs" or thirty-six "decans". A seasonal cycle of roughly 360 days could have corresponded to the signs and decans of the zodiac by dividing each sign into thirty parts and each decan into ten parts.[^o21] It is due to the Babylonian [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170) that each degree is divided into sixty minutes and each minute is divided into sixty seconds.[^o21]

*Image: Menelaus-thm.png.* [Menelaus' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus'_theorem)

[Menelaus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/46301) (c. 100 AD) wrote in three books his *Sphaerica*. In Book I, he established a basis for spherical triangles analogous to the Euclidean basis for plane triangles.[^o36] He established a theorem that is without Euclidean analogue, that two spherical triangles are congruent if corresponding angles are equal, but he did not distinguish between congruent and symmetric spherical triangles.[^o36] Another theorem that he establishes is that the sum of the angles of a spherical triangle is greater than 180°.[^o36] Book II of *Sphaerica* applies spherical geometry to astronomy. And Book III contains the "theorem of Menelaus".[^o36] He further gave his famous "rule of six quantities".[^n8]

Later, [Claudius Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) (c. 90 – c. 168 AD) expanded upon Hipparchus' *Chords in a Circle* in his *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*, or the *Mathematical Syntaxis*. The Almagest is primarily a work on astronomy, and astronomy relies on trigonometry. [Ptolemy's table of chords](wikipedia://en/30684123) gives the lengths of chords of a circle of diameter 120 as a function of the number of degrees *n* in the corresponding arc of the circle, for *n* ranging from 1/2 to 180 by increments of 1/2.[^o38] The thirteen books of the *Almagest* are the most influential and significant trigonometric work of all antiquity.[^o39] A theorem that was central to Ptolemy's calculation of chords was what is still known today as [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971), that the sum of the products of the opposite sides of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) is equal to the product of the diagonals. A special case of Ptolemy's theorem appeared as proposition 93 in Euclid's *Data*. Ptolemy's theorem leads to the equivalent of the four sum-and-difference formulas for sine and cosine that are today known as Ptolemy's formulas, although Ptolemy himself used chords instead of sine and cosine.[^o39] Ptolemy further derived the equivalent of the half-angle formula

$\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) = \frac{1 - \cos(x)}{2}.$[^o39]

Ptolemy used these results to create his trigonometric tables, but whether these tables were derived from Hipparchus' work cannot be determined.[^o39] Ptolemy also provided an equivalent of the law of sines, stating that the sides of a triangle are proportional to the chords of twice their opposite angles. This relation appears in his 2nd-century work, the [Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060), where it was used in astronomical and geometric calculations.[^o40]

Neither the tables of Hipparchus nor those of Ptolemy have survived to the present day, although descriptions by other ancient authors leave little doubt that they once existed.[^n9]

### Indian mathematics {#Indian_mathematics}

Some of the early and very significant developments of trigonometry were in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_India). Influential works from the 4th–5th century AD, known as the [Siddhantas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddhanta) (of which there were five, the most important of which is the [Surya Siddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya_Siddhanta)[^n10]) first defined the sine as the modern relationship between half an angle and half a chord, while also defining the cosine, [versine](wikipedia://en/555782), and [inverse sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_sine).[^n11]

The [Shulba Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulba_Sutras) contained no trigonometric functions or explicit angle measurements. According to [T. A. Sarasvati Amma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._A._Sarasvati_Amma), they established geometric principles—including the theorem of the square on the diagonal and the properties of similar figures—that later became fundamental to Indian geometry and influenced the later development of sine tables.[^o45] [Indian mathematician](wikipedia://en/1848052) and [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy), [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204) (476–550 AD), collected and expanded upon the developments of the Siddhantas in an important work called the *[Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya)*.[^n12] The *Siddhantas* and the *Aryabhatiya* contain the earliest surviving tables of sine values and [versine](wikipedia://en/555782) (1 − cosine) values, in 3.75° intervals from 0° to 90°, to an accuracy of 4 decimal places.[^n13] They used the words *[jya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jya)* for sine, *[kojya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kojya)* for cosine, *[utkrama-jya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utkrama-jya)* for versine, and *otkram jya* for inverse sine. The words *[jya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jya)* and *[kojya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kojya)* eventually became *sine* and *cosine* respectively after a mistranslation described above. Some historians of mathematics have argued that the sine table given in the *Āryabhaṭiya* was an adaptation of earlier such tables constructed by mathematicians and astronomers of ancient Greece.[^o48][^o49] However, this hypothesis has been refuted due to significant mathematical discrepancies and inadequacies in those arguments. Scholars have instead demonstrated its independent origin.[^o50][^o51][^o52][^o53] Takao Hayashi, for instance, re-examined Aryabhata's sine table and observed that specific (rounding) differences indicate its originality, stating that Aryabhata computed the values himself using a traditional Indian rule (*ardhādhikena*).[^o54][^o55]

In the [Pañcasiddhāntikā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancha-siddhantika) (c. 6th century), [Varahamihira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varahamihira) gave trigonometric identities for the half-chord (*Jyā, koti-jyā and utkrama-jyā*) and cosine (*koṭi-jyā*), corresponding in modern notation to:[^o56]

$$\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$$

$$\sin x = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)$$

$$\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) = \frac{1 - \cos x}{2}$$

The text also contains a 24-entry sine table based on the *Surya Siddhanta* and rules for linear interpolation between tabulated values.[^o57][^o56][^o58]

In the 7th century, [Bhaskara I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhaskara_I) produced a [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhaskara_I's_sine_approximation_formula) for calculating the sine of an acute angle without the use of a table. He also gave the following approximation formula for sin(*x*), which had a relative error of less than 1.9%:

$$\sin x \approx \frac{16x (\pi - x)}{5 \pi^2 - 4x (\pi - x)}, \qquad \left(0\leq x\leq\pi\right).$$

Later in the 7th century, [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) redeveloped the formula

$$\ 1 - \sin^2(x) = \cos^2(x) = \sin^2\left (\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right )$$

(also derived earlier, as mentioned above) and the [Brahmagupta interpolation formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta_interpolation_formula) for computing sine values.[^n5] Statements equivalent to the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) appear in the astronomical and trigonometric work of [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206). In his *[Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta)*, Brahmagupta expresses the circumradius of a triangle as the product of two sides divided by twice the [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241); the law of sines can be derived by alternately expressing the altitude as the sine of one or the other base angle times its opposite side, then equating the two resulting variants.[^o60] An equation even closer to the modern law of sines appears in Brahmagupta's *[Khaṇḍakhādyaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khaṇḍakhādyaka)*, in a method for finding the distance between the Earth and a planet following an [epicycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicycle); however, whether Brahmagupta treated the law of sines as an independent subject or used it systematically for solving triangles isn't known.\<ref>Van Brummelen, Glen. (2009). "The Mathematics of the Heavens and the Earth". Princeton University Press. p. 109–111. isbn:978-0-691-12973-0 Brahmagupta. (1934). "The Khandakhadyaka: An Astronomical Treatise of Brahmagupta". University of Calcutta\</re [Madhava](wikipedia://en/2793007) (c. 1400) made early strides in the [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) of trigonometric functions and their [infinite series](wikipedia://en/15287) expansions. He developed the concepts of the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) and [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448), and produced the [power series](wikipedia://en/26149716) expansions of sine, cosine, tangent, and arctangent.[^o63] Using the Taylor series approximations of sine and cosine, he produced a sine table to 12 decimal places of accuracy and a cosine table to 9 decimal places of accuracy. He also gave the power series of [π](wikipedia://en/23601) and the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196), [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815), [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007), and [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of a circle in terms of trigonometric functions. His works were expanded by his followers at the [Kerala School](wikipedia://en/3099132) up to the 16th century.[^o63][^o64]

The Indian text the [Yuktibhāṣā](wikipedia://en/5864214) contains proof for the expansion of the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) and [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) functions and the derivation and proof of the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) for [inverse tangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_tangent), discovered by Madhava. The Yuktibhāṣā also contains rules for finding the sines and the cosines of the sum and difference of two angles.

### Chinese mathematics {#Chinese_mathematics}

*Image: Guo Shoujing-beijing.JPG.* [Guo Shoujing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guo_Shoujing) (1231–1316)

In [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China), [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204)'s table of sines were translated into the [Chinese mathematical](wikipedia://en/1524543) book of the *[Kaiyuan Zhanjing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise_on_Astrology_of_the_Kaiyuan_Era)*, compiled in 718 AD during the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty).[^n14] Although the Chinese excelled in other fields of mathematics such as solid geometry, [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677), and complex algebraic formulas, early forms of trigonometry were not as widely appreciated as in the earlier Greek, Hellenistic, Indian and Islamic worlds.[^n15] Instead, the early Chinese used an empirical substitute known as *chong cha*, while practical use of plane trigonometry in using the sine, the tangent, and the secant were known.[^n14] However, this embryonic state of trigonometry in China slowly began to change and advance during the [Song dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty) (960–1279), where Chinese mathematicians began to express greater emphasis for the need of spherical trigonometry in calendrical science and astronomical calculations.[^n14] The [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath) Chinese scientist, mathematician and official [Shen Kuo](wikipedia://en/1102000) (1031–1095) used trigonometric functions to solve mathematical problems of chords and arcs.[^n14] Joseph W.Dauben writes that in Shen's formula "technique of intersecting circles", he created an approximation of the arc *s* of a circle given the diameter *d*, [sagitta](wikipedia://en/24947530) *v*, and length *c* of the chord subtending the arc, the length of which he approximated as[^n16]

$$s = c + \frac{2v^2}{d}.$$

Sal Restivo writes that Shen's work in the lengths of arcs of circles provided the basis for [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405) developed in the 13th century by the mathematician and astronomer [Guo Shoujing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guo_Shoujing) (1231–1316).[^n17] As the historians L. Gauchet and Joseph Needham state, Guo Shoujing used spherical trigonometry in his calculations to improve the [calendar system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_calendar) and [Chinese astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_astronomy).[^n14][^o76] Along with a later 17th-century Chinese illustration of Guo's mathematical proofs, Needham states that:

> Guo used a quadrangular spherical pyramid, the basal quadrilateral of which consisted of one equatorial and one ecliptic arc, together with two [meridian arcs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc), one of which passed through the [summer solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_solstice) point...By such methods he was able to obtain the du lü (degrees of equator corresponding to degrees of ecliptic), the ji cha (values of chords for given ecliptic arcs), and the cha lü (difference between chords of arcs differing by 1 degree).[^n18]

Despite the achievements of Shen and Guo's work in trigonometry, another substantial work in Chinese trigonometry would not be published again until 1607, with the dual publication of *[Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)* by Chinese official and astronomer [Xu Guangqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Guangqi) (1562–1633) and the Italian Jesuit [Matteo Ricci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteo_Ricci) (1552–1610).[^n19]

## Medieval {#Medieval}

### Islamic world {#Islamic_world}

*Image: Image-Al-Kitāb al-muḫtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-ğabr wa-l-muqābala.jpg.* Page from *[The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Compendious_Book_on_Calculation_by_Completion_and_Balancing)* by [Muhammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Mūsā_al-Khwārizmī) (c. AD 820)

Previous works from India and Greece were later translated and expanded in the [medieval Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age) by [Muslim mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam) of mostly [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Iranian_scientists_and_scholars) and [Arab descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Arab_scientists_and_scholars), who enunciated a large number of theorems which freed the subject of trigonometry from dependence upon the complete [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278), as was the case in Hellenistic mathematics due to the application of [Menelaus' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menelaus'_theorem). According to E. S. Kennedy, it was after this development in Islamic mathematics that "the first real trigonometry emerged, in the sense that only then did the object of study become the [spherical](wikipedia://en/650405) or plane [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654), its sides and [angles](wikipedia://en/1196)."[^o81]

The sin and cos functions of trigonometry, were important mathematical concepts, imported from the [Gupta period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_period) of [Indian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy) namely the [*jyā* and *koṭi-jyā*](wikipedia://en/26397073) functions via translation of texts like the *[Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya)* and *[Surya Siddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surya_Siddhanta)*, from Sanskrit to Arabic, and then from Arabic to Latin, and later to other European languages.[^o82]

Methods dealing with spherical triangles were also known, particularly the method of [Menelaus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/46301), who developed "Menelaus' theorem" to deal with spherical problems.[^o36][^o83] However, E. S. Kennedy points out that while it was possible in pre-Islamic mathematics to compute the magnitudes of a spherical figure, in principle, by use of the table of chords and Menelaus' theorem, the application of the theorem to spherical problems was very difficult in practice.[^o84] In order to observe holy days on the [Islamic calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar) in which timings were determined by [phases of the moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_the_moon), astronomers initially used Menelaus' method to calculate the place of the [moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) and [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star), though this method proved to be clumsy and difficult. It involved setting up two intersecting [right triangles](wikipedia://en/39420); by applying Menelaus' theorem it was possible to solve one of the six sides, but only if the other five sides were known. To tell the time from the [sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun)'s [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude), for instance, repeated applications of Menelaus' theorem were required. For medieval [Islamic astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_medieval_Islam), there was an obvious challenge to find a simpler trigonometric method.[^o85]

In the early 9th century AD, [Muhammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Mūsā_al-Khwārizmī) produced accurate sine and cosine tables. He was also a pioneer in [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405). In 830 AD, [Habash al-Hasib al-Marwazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habash_al-Hasib_al-Marwazi) discovered the tangent and the cotangent and produced the first table of these [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367).[^o90][^o95] [Muhammad ibn Jābir al-Harrānī al-Battānī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Jābir_al-Harrānī_al-Battānī) (Albatenius) (853–929 AD) discovered the secant and the cosecant, and produced the first table of cosecants for each degree from 1° to 90°.[^o86]

By the 10th century AD, in the work of [Abū al-Wafā' al-Būzjānī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abū_al-Wafā'_al-Būzjānī), all six [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) were used.[^n20] Abu al-Wafa had sine tables in 0.25° increments, to 8 decimal places of accuracy, and accurate tables of tangent values.[^n20] He also developed the following trigonometric formula:[^o89]

$\ \sin(2x) = 2 \sin(x) \cos(x)$ (a special case of Ptolemy's angle-addition formula; see above)

In his original text, Abū al-Wafā' states: "If we want that, we multiply the given sine by the cosine [minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_of_arc), and the result is half the sine of the double".[^o89] Abū al-Wafā also established the angle addition and difference identities presented with complete proofs:[^o89]

$$\sin(\alpha \pm \beta) = \sqrt{\sin^2 \alpha - (\sin \alpha \sin \beta)^2} \pm \sqrt{\sin^2 \beta- (\sin \alpha\sin \beta)^2}$$

$$\sin(\alpha \pm \beta) = \sin \alpha \cos \beta \pm \cos \alpha \sin \beta$$

For the second one, the text states: "We multiply the sine of each of the two arcs by the cosine of the other *minutes*. If we want the sine of the sum, we add the products, if we want the sine of the difference, we take their difference".[^o89]

Systematizing the existing concepts, he also discovered the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222#Curvature) for spherical trigonometry:[^o90]

$$\frac{\sin A}{\sin a} = \frac{\sin B}{\sin b} = \frac{\sin C}{\sin c}.$$

Also in the late 10th and early 11th centuries AD, the Egyptian astronomer [Ibn Yunus](wikipedia://en/998087) performed many careful trigonometric calculations and demonstrated the following [trigonometric identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity):[^o91]

$$\cos a \cos b = \frac{\cos(a+b) + \cos(a-b)}{2}$$

[Al-Jayyani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Jayyani) (989–1079) of [al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) wrote *The book of unknown arcs of a sphere*, which is considered "the first treatise on [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405)". It "contains formulae for [right-handed triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_right_triangles), the general law of sines, and the solution of a [spherical triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle) by means of the polar triangle." This treatise later had a "strong influence on European mathematics", and his "definition of [ratios](wikipedia://en/87837) as numbers" and "method of solving a spherical triangle when all sides are unknown" are likely to have influenced [Regiomontanus](wikipedia://en/25929).[^o92]

The method of [triangulation](wikipedia://en/207174) was first developed by Muslim mathematicians, who applied it to practical uses such as [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying)[^o93] and [Islamic geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geography), as described by [Abu Rayhan Biruni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Rayhan_Biruni) in the early 11th century. Biruni himself introduced triangulation techniques to [measure the size of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Rayhan_Biruni#Geodesy_and_geography) and the distances between various places. In the late 11th century, [Omar Khayyám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayyám) (1048–1131) solved [cubic equations](wikipedia://en/180787) using approximate numerical solutions found by interpolation in trigonometric tables. In the 13th century, [Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naṣīr_al-Dīn_al-Ṭūsī) was the first to treat trigonometry as a mathematical discipline independent from astronomy, and he developed spherical trigonometry into its present form.[^o95] He listed the six distinct cases of a right-angled triangle in spherical trigonometry, and in his *Book on the Complete Quadrilateral*, he stated the law of sines for plane and spherical triangles, discovered the [law of tangents](wikipedia://en/319484) for spherical triangles, and provided proofs for both these laws.[^o96] [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](wikipedia://en/33642424) has been described as the creator of trigonometry as a mathematical discipline in its own right.[^o97][^o98][^o99]

The [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890), in geometric form, can be found as propositions II.12–13 in [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107) (c. 300 BC),[^o100] but was not used for the solution of triangles per se. Medieval Islamic mathematicians developed a method for finding the third side of an arbitrary triangle given two sides and the included angle based on the same concept but more similar to the modern formulation of the law of cosines. A sketch of the method can be found in Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī's *Book on the Complete Quadrilateral* (c. 1250),[^o101] and the same method is described in more detail in [Jamshīd al-Kāshī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jamshīd_al-Kāshī)'s *Key of Arithmetic* (1427).[^o102] Al-Kāshī also computed the [sine of 1°](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_of_1°) accurate to 8 [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) digits, and constructed the most accurate trigonometric tables to date, accurate to four [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) places (equivalent to 8 decimal places) for each 1° of arc. Al-Kāshī presumably worked on [Ulugh Beg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulugh_Beg)'s even more comprehensive trigonometric tables, with five-place (sexagesimal) entries for each minute of arc.

[Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](wikipedia://en/33642424) (1201–1274) was the first to treat [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) as an independent mathematical discipline, separate from astronomy, in his *Treatise on the Quadrilateral* (c. 1260). He fully established all six trigonometric functions (sine, cosine, tangent, cotangent, secant, cosecant) with proofs, and formulated the [plane and spherical law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) – the form still taught today.[^o103][^o104]

### European renaissance {#European_renaissance}

In 1342, [Levi ben Gershon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_ben_Gershon) (Gersonides) wrote *On Sines, Chords and Arcs*, also proving the sine law for plane triangles (though after al-Tusi) and giving five-figure [sine tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_table).[^o106]

A simplified trigonometric table, the "*[toleta de marteloio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toleta_de_marteloio)*", was used by sailors in the [Mediterranean Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea) during the 14th–15th centuries to calculate [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) courses. It is described by [Ramon Llull](wikipedia://en/69677) of [Mallorca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mallorca) in 1295, and laid out in the 1436 atlas of [Venetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) captain [Andrea Bianco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Bianco).

[Regiomontanus](wikipedia://en/25929) was the first mathematician in **Europe** to treat trigonometry as a distinct mathematical discipline,[^n21] in his *De triangulis omnimodis* written in 1464, as well as his later *Tabulae directionum* which included the tangent function, unnamed. However, his work relied on Latin translations of earlier Arabic texts, including al-Tusi's *Treatise on the Quadrilateral*.[^o108] Modern scholarship has since restored al-Tusi's priority as the first to establish trigonometry as an independent discipline.[^o109]

## Modern {#Modern}

The *Opus palatinum de triangulis* of [Georg Joachim Rheticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Joachim_Rheticus), a student of [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus), was probably the first in Europe to define trigonometric functions directly in terms of right triangles instead of circles, with tables for all six trigonometric functions; this work was finished by Rheticus' student [Valentin Otho](wikipedia://en/14447713) in 1596.[^o12]

The term “trigonometry” first appeared as the title of the book Trigonometria (1595) by [Bartholomaeus Pitiscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomaeus_Pitiscus). He is also credited with deriving several trigonometric identities, including the multiple-angle formulas for sine and cosine, such as $sin 2x$,$sin 3x$,$cos 2x$, and $cos 3x$.[^o12]

In the 17th century, [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [James Stirling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Stirling_%28mathematician%29) developed the general Newton–Stirling interpolation formula for trigonometric functions.

In 1707, de Moivre derived an equation from which one can deduce:

$$\cos x = \tfrac{1}{2} (\cos(nx) + i\sin(nx))^{1/n} + \tfrac{1}{2}(\cos(nx) - i\sin(nx))^{1/n}$$

which he was able to prove for all positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *n*.[^o111][^o112] In 1722, he presented equations from which one can deduce the better known form of [de Moivre's Formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Moivre's_Formula):

$(\cos x + i\sin x)^n = \cos(nx) + i\sin(nx). \,$[^o113][^o114]

*[Introductio in analysin infinitorum](wikipedia://en/19777721)* (1748) by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) was mostly responsible for establishing the analytic treatment of trigonometric functions in Europe, deriving their infinite series and presenting [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613).

$$e^{i x} = \cos x + i \sin x$$

Euler used the near-modern abbreviations *sin.*, *cos.*, *tang.*, *cot.*, *sec.*, and *cosec.* Prior to this, [Roger Cotes](wikipedia://en/236798) had computed the derivative of sine in his *Harmonia Mensurarum* (1722).[^o115] Johann Bernoulli found the relation between $\sin^{-1}(z)$ and $\operatorname{Log} y$ in 1702.[^o12] Also in the 18th century, [Brook Taylor](wikipedia://en/210429) defined the general Taylor series and gave the series expansions and approximations for all six trigonometric functions. The works of [James Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gregory_%28astronomer_and_mathematician%29) in the 17th century and [Colin Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/7824) in the 18th century were also very influential in the development of trigonometric series.

In the 19th century, [Joseph Fourier](wikipedia://en/25767401) discovered the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) during his attempts to find solutions to the [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415), paving the way for [Fourier](wikipedia://en/11659) and [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147).
