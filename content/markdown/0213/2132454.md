# Evolutionary graph theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Evolutionary graph theory"}

**Evolutionary graph theory** is an area of research lying at the intersection of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), and [mathematical biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_biology). Evolutionary graph theory is an approach to studying how [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) affects [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution) of a [population](wikipedia://en/22949). That the underlying topology can substantially affect the results of the evolutionary process is seen most clearly in a paper by [Erez Lieberman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erez_Lieberman), Christoph Hauert and [Martin Nowak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Nowak).[^o2]

:::

## Mathematical framework {#Mathematical_framework}

In evolutionary graph theory, individuals occupy [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) of a weighted [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) and the weight w<sub>i j</sub> of an [edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) from vertex *i* to vertex *j* denotes the probability of *i* replacing *j*. The weight corresponds to the biological notion of [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_%28biology%29) where fitter types propagate more readily.

One property studied on graphs with two types of individuals is the *fixation probability*, which is defined as the probability that a single, randomly placed mutant of type A will replace a population of type B. According to the *isothermal theorem*, a graph has the same fixation probability as the corresponding [Moran process](wikipedia://en/21700432) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is isothermal, thus the sum of all weights that lead into a vertex is the same for all vertices. Thus, for example, a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) with equal weights describes a Moran process. The fixation probability is

$$\begin{aligned} \rho_M = \frac{1-r^{-1}} { 1-r^{-N} } \end{aligned}$$

where *r* is the relative fitness of the invading type.

### Selection amplifiers and suppressors {#Selection_amplifiers_and_suppressors}

Graphs can be classified into amplifiers of selection and suppressors of selection. If the fixation probability of a single advantageous mutation $\rho_G$ is higher than the fixation probability of the corresponding [Moran process](wikipedia://en/21700432) $\rho_M$ then the graph is an amplifier, otherwise a suppressor of selection. One example of the suppressor of selection is a linear process where only vertex *i-1* can replace vertex *i* (but not the other way around). In this case the fixation probability is $\rho_G = 1/N$ (where *N* is the number of vertices) since this is the probability that the mutation arises in the first vertex which will eventually replace all the other ones. Since $\rho_G < \rho_M$ for all *r* greater than 1, this graph is by definition a suppressor of selection.

### Alternative formulations {#Alternative_formulations}

Evolutionary graph theory may also be studied in a dual formulation, as a [coalescing random walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalescing_random_walk), or as a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895). We may consider the mutant population on a graph as a [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451) between absorbing barriers representing mutant extinction and mutant fixation. For highly symmetric graphs, we can then use martingales to find the *fixation probability* as illustrated by Monk (2018).

Also [evolutionary games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_game_theory) can be studied on graphs where again an edge between *i* and *j* means that these two individuals will play a game against each other.

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

Closely related stochastic processes include the [voter model](wikipedia://en/35998199), which was introduced by Clifford and Sudbury (1973) and independently by Holley and Liggett (1975), and which has been studied extensively.
