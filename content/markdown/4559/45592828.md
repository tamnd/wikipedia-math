# Regular skew apeirohedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular skew apeirohedron"}

*Image: Mucube internal.png.* The [mucube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mucube) is a regular skew apeirohedron.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **regular skew apeirohedron** is an infinite [regular skew polyhedron](wikipedia://en/19193037). They have either [skew](wikipedia://en/6545461) regular [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) or skew regular [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719).

:::

## History {#History}

In 1926 [John Flinders Petrie](wikipedia://en/23915649) took the concept of [regular skew polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon), polygons whose vertices are not all in the same plane, and extended it to polyhedra. While [apeirohedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_tiling) are typically required to tile the 2-dimensional plane, Petrie considered cases where the faces were still convex but were not required to lie flat in the plane, they could have a skew polygon [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719).

Petrie discovered two regular skew apeirohedra, the mucube and the muoctahedron.[^o3] [Harold Scott MacDonald Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.S.M._Coxeter) derived a third, the mutetrahedron, and proved that these three were complete. Under Coxeter and Petrie's definition, requiring convex faces and allowing a skew vertex figure, the three were not only the only skew apeirohedra in 3-dimensional Euclidean space, but they were the only skew polyhedra in 3-space as there Coxeter showed there were no finite cases.

In 1967[^o4] Garner investigated regular skew apeirohedra in [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space) with Petrie and Coxeters definition, discovering 31[^o5] regular skew apeirohedra with compact or paracompact symmetry.

In 1977[^o6][^o3] [Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) generalized skew polyhedra to allow for skew faces as well. Grünbaum discovered an additional 23[^o7] skew apeirohedra in 3-dimensional Euclidean space and 3 in 2-dimensional space which are skew by virtue of their faces. 12 of Grünbaum's polyhedra were formed using the blending operation on 2-dimensional apeirohedra, and the other 11 were pure, i.e. could not be formed by a non-trivial blend. Grünbaum conjectured that this new list was complete for the parameters considered.

In 1985[^o8][^o3] Dress found an additional pure regular skew apeirohedron in 3-space, and proved that with this additional skew apeirohedron the list was complete.

## Regular skew apeirohedra in Euclidean 3-space {#Regular_skew_apeirohedra_in_Euclidean_3-space}

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Petrie–Coxeter polyhedra"}

### Petrie–Coxeter polyhedra {#Petrie–Coxeter_polyhedra}

The three Euclidean solutions in 3-space are {4,6$|$4}, {6,4$|$4}, and {6,6$|$3}. [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) named them mucube, muoctahedron, and mutetrahedron respectively for multiple cube, octahedron, and tetrahedron.[^o12]

1. **Mucube**: {4,6|4}: 6 [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) about each vertex (related to [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318), constructed by cubic cells, removing two opposite faces from each, and linking sets of six together around a faceless [cube](wikipedia://en/6285).)
1. **Muoctahedron**: {6,4|4}: 4 [hexagons](wikipedia://en/59733) about each vertex (related to [bitruncated cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3736299), constructed by [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654) with their square faces removed and linking hole pairs of holes together.)
1. **Mutetrahedron**: {6,6|3}: 6 hexagons about each vertex (related to [quarter cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3757507), constructed by [truncated tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/279651) cells, removing triangle faces, and linking sets of four around a faceless [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606).)

Coxeter gives these regular skew apeirohedra {2q,2r|p} with [extended chiral symmetry](wikipedia://en/32088502) \[(*p*,*q*,*p*,*r*)\]<sup>\+</sup> which he says is isomorphic to his [abstract group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_group) (2*q*,2*r*|2,*p*). The related honeycomb has the extended symmetry \[(*p*,*q*,*p*,*r*)\].[^o13]

### Grünbaum-Dress polyhedra {#Grünbaum-Dress_polyhedra}

#### Skew honeycombs {#Skew_honeycombs}

There are 3 regular skew apeirohedra of full rank, also called **regular skew honeycombs**, that is skew apeirohedra in 2-dimensions. As with the [finite skew polyhedra](wikipedia://en/19193037#Finite_regular_skew_polyhedra) of full rank, all three of these can be obtained by applying the [Petrie dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrie_dual) to planar polytopes, in this case the three regular tilings.[^o18][^o19][^o20]

Alternatively they can be constructed using the apeir operation on regular polygons.[^o21] While the Petrial is used the classical construction, it does not generalize well to higher ranks. In contrast, the apeir operation is used to construct higher rank skew honeycombs.[^o22]

The apeir operation takes the generating mirrors of the polygon, $\rho_{0}$ and $\rho_{1}$, and uses them as the mirrors for the vertex figure of a polyhedron, the new vertex mirror $w$ is then a point located where the initial vertex of the polygon (or anywhere on the mirror $\rho_{1}$ other than its intersection with $\rho_{0}$). The new initial vertex is placed at the intersection of the mirrors $\rho_{0}$ and $\rho_{1}$. Thus the apeir polyhedron is generated by $\langle w, \rho_{0}, \rho_{0}\rangle$.[^o23]

#### Blended apeirohedra {#Blended_apeirohedra}

*Image: Blended triangular tiling.png.* $\{3,6\}\#$ with the edges of one face highlighted in red

For any two regular polytopes, $P$ and $Q$, a new polytope can be made by the following process:

- Start with the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of the vertices of $P$ with the vertices of $Q$.
- Add edges between any two vertices $p_{0} \times q_{0}$ and $p_{1} \times q_{1}$ if there is an edge between $p$${}_{0}$ and $p$${}_{1}$ in $P$ and an edge between $q$${}_{0}$ and $q$${}_{1}$ in $Q$. (If $Q$ has no edges then add a virtual edge connecting its vertex to itself.)
- Similarly add faces to every set of vertices all incident on the same face in both $P$ and $Q$. (If $Q$ has no faces then add a virtual face connecting its edge to itself.)
- Repeat as such for all ranks of proper elements.
- From the resulting polytope, select one connected component.

For regular polytopes the last step is guaranteed to produce a unique result. This new polytope is called the **blend** of $P$ and $Q$ and is represented $P\#Q$.

Equivalently the blend can be obtained by positioning $P$ and $Q$ in orthogonal spaces and taking composing their generating mirrors pairwise.

Blended polyhedra in 3-dimensional space can be made by blending 2-dimensional polyhedra with 1-dimensional polytopes. The only 2-dimensional polyhedra are the 6 honeycombs (3 Euclidean tilings and 3 skew honeycombs):

- [Triangular tiling](wikipedia://en/2863858): $\{3, 6$
- [Square tiling](wikipedia://en/2863809): $\{4, 4$
- [Hexagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/2863719): $\{6, 3$
- Petrial triangular tiling: $\{3, 6\}^{\pi}$
- Petrial square tiling: $\{4, 4\}^{\pi}$
- Petrial hexagonal tiling: $\{6, 3\}^{\pi}$

The only 1-dimensional polytopes are:

- The [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860):
- The [apeirogon](wikipedia://en/5658261): $\{\infty$

Each pair between these produces a valid distinct regular skew apeirohedron in 3-dimensional Euclidean space, for a total of 12[^o7] blended skew apeirohedra.

Since the skeleton of the square tiling is [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431), two of these blends, $\{4, 4\}\#$ and $\{4, 4\}^{\pi}\#$, are combinatrially equivalent to their non-blended counterparts.

#### Pure apeirohedra {#Pure_apeirohedra}

A polytope is considered pure if it cannot be expressed as a non-trivial blend of two polytopes. A blend is considered trivial if it contains the result as one of the components. Alternatively a pure polytope is one whose symmetry group contains no non-trivial [subrepresentation](wikipedia://en/55943952).[^o27]

There are 12 regular pure apeirohedra in 3 dimensions. Three of these are the [Petrie-Coxeter polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrie-Coxeter_polyhedra):

- $$\{4,6 | 4$$
- $$\{6,4 | 4$$
- $$\{6,6 | 3$$

Three more are obtained as the [Petrials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrial) of the Petrie-Coxeter polyhedra:

- $$\{4,6 | 4\}^{\pi} = \{\infty, 4\}_{6,4}$$
- $$\{6,4 | 4\}^{\pi} = \{\infty, 6\}_{4,4}$$
- $$\{6,6 | 3\}^{\pi} = \{\infty, 6\}_{6,3}$$

Three additional pure apeirohedra can be formed with finite skew polygons as faces:

These 3 are closed under the [Wilson operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilson_operation). Meaning that each can be constructed from any other by some combination of the Petrial and dual operations. $\{6,6\}_{4}$ is [self-dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-dual_polyhedron) and $\{6,4\}_{6}$ is self-Petrial.

## Regular skew apeirohedra in hyperbolic 3-space {#Regular_skew_apeirohedra_in_hyperbolic_3-space}

*Image: Hyperbolic skew apeirohedron 4,6 5.png.* The compact skew apeirohedron $\{4,6 | 5$

In 1967, C. W. L. Garner identified 31 hyperbolic skew apeirohedra with [regular skew polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon) [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719), found by extending the [Petrie-Coxeter polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrie-Coxeter_polyhedra) to hyperbolic space.[^o29]

These represent 14 compact and 17[^o5] paracompact regular skew polyhedra in hyperbolic space, constructed from the symmetry of a subset of linear and cyclic [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004) graphs of the form \[(*p*,*q*,*p*,*r*)\], These define *regular skew polyhedra* {2*q*,2*r*|*p*} and dual {2*r*,2*q*|*p*}. For the special case of linear graph groups *r* = 2, this represents the Coxeter group \[*p*,*q*,*p*\]. It generates regular skews {2*q*,*4*|*p*} and {4,2*q*|*p*}. All of these exist as a subset of faces of the [convex uniform honeycombs in hyperbolic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_uniform_honeycombs_in_hyperbolic_space).

The skew apeirohedron shares the same [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851) vertex figure with the honeycomb, but only the zig-zag edge faces of the vertex figure are realized, while the other faces make holes.
