# YΔ- and ΔY-transformation

*Image: DY YD.svg.* Depiction of the ΔY- and YΔ-transformations applied to a graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="YΔ- and ΔY-transformation"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), **ΔY-** and **YΔ-transformations** (also written **delta-wye** and **wye-delta**) are a pair of operations on [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806). A ΔY-transformation replaces a [triangle](wikipedia://en/27071812) by a vertex of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_degree) three; and conversely, a YΔ-transformation replaces a vertex of degree three by a triangle. The names for the operations derive from the shapes of the involved subgraphs, which look respectively like the letter Y and the Greek capital letter [Δ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_%28letter%29).

:::

A YΔ-transformation may create [parallel edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-edge), even if applied to a [simple graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph). For this reason ΔY- and YΔ-transformations are most naturally considered as operations on [multigraphs](wikipedia://en/1774970). On multigraphs both operations preserve the edge count and are exact inverses of each other. In the context of simple graphs it is common to combine a YΔ-transformation with a subsequent *normalization step* that reduces parallel edges to a single edge. This may no longer preserve the number of edges, nor be exactly reversible via a ΔY-transformation.

*Image: DY YD multigraph.svg.* The YΔ-transformation can lead to multi-edges. Removing the multi-edges in a normalization step and applying a ΔY-transformation will then not result in the original graph.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let $G$ be a graph (potentially a multigraph).

Suppose $G$ contains a triangle $\Delta$ with vertices $x_1,x_2,x_3$ and edges $e_{12},e_{23},e_{31}$. A *ΔY-transformation* of $G$ at $\Delta$ deletes the edges $e_{12},e_{23},e_{31}$ and adds a new vertex $y$ adjacent to each of $x_1,x_2,x_3$.

Conversely, if $y$ is a vertex of degree three with neighbors $x_1,x_2,x_3$, then a *YΔ-transformation* of $G$ at $y$ deletes $y$ and adds three new edges $e_{12},e_{23},e_{31}$, where $e_{ij}$ connects $x_i$ and $x_j$. If the resulting graph should be a simple graph, then any resulting parallel edges are to be replaced by a single edge.

:::

## Relevance {#Relevance}

ΔY- and YΔ-transformations are a tool both in pure graph theory as well as applications.

Both operations preserve a number of natural [topological properties](wikipedia://en/1631772) of graphs. For example, applying a YΔ-transformation to a 3-vertex of a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314), or a ΔY-transformation to a triangular face of a planar graph, results again in a planar graph.[^o5] This was used in the original proof of [Steinitz's theorem](wikipedia://en/19762817), showing that every [3-connected](wikipedia://en/7566175) planar graph is the [edge graph](wikipedia://en/24656270) of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470). Applying ΔY- and YΔ-transformations to a [linkless graph](wikipedia://en/772031) results again in a linkless graph.[^o9] This fact is used to compactly describe the [forbidden minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbidden_minor_characterization) of the associated graph classes as ΔY-families generated from a small number of graphs (see the section on ΔY-families below).

A particularly relevant application exists in [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) in the study of [three-phase power systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-phase_electric_power#Three-wire_and_four-wire_circuits) (see [Y-Δ transform (electrical engineering)](wikipedia://en/268145)).[^o6] In this context they are also known as **star-triangle transformations** and are a special case of [star-mesh transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star-mesh_transform).

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="YΔ-family"}

## ΔY-families {#ΔY-families}

*Image: Petersen\_family.svg.* The seven members of the Petersen family with edges denoting an application of a single YΔ- or ΔY-transformations

The **ΔY-family** generated by a graph $G$ is the smallest family of graphs that contains $G$ and is closed under YΔ- and ΔY-transformations. Equivalently, it is constructed from $G$ by recursively applying these transformations until no new graph is generated. If $G$ is a finite graph it generates a finite ΔY-family, all members of which have the same edge count.

The ΔY-family generated by several graphs is the smallest family that contains all these graphs and is closed under YΔ- and ΔY-transformation.

Some notable families are generated in this way:

- the [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family) is generated from the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_6$. It consists of the seven forbidden minors for the class of linkless graphs.[^o9]
- the [Heawood family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heawood_family) is generated from $K_7$ and $K_{3,3,1,1}$. It consists of 78 graphs, each of which is a forbidden minors for the class of [4-flat graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-flat_graph).[^o10]

:::

## YΔY-reducible graphs {#YΔY-reducible_graphs}

*Image: Apex\_rhombic\_dodecahedron.svg.* The graph formed by connecting a vertex to every degree-three vertex of a [rhombic dodecahedron graph](wikipedia://en/607631) is linkless but not YΔY-reducible.

A graph is **YΔY-reducible** if it can be reduced to a single vertex by a sequence of ΔY- or YΔ-transformations and the following normalization steps:

- removing a [loop](wikipedia://en/1449083),
- removing a [parallel edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-edge),
- removing a vertex of degree one,
- *smoothing out* a vertex of degree two, i.e., replacing it by a single edge between its two former neighbors.

The YΔY-reducible graphs form a minor closed family and therefore have a [forbidden minor characterization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbidden_minor_characterization) (by the [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769)). The graphs of the [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family) constitute some (but not all) of the excluded minors.[^o13] In fact, already more than 68 billion excluded minor are known.[^o14]

The class of YΔY-reducible graphs lies between the classes of planar graphs and linkless graphs: each planar graph is YΔY-reducible, while each YΔY-reducible graph is linkless. Both inclusions are strict: $K_5$ is not planar but YΔY-reducible, while the graph in the figure is not YΔY-reducible but linkless.[^o13]

:::
