# Strongly chordal graph

*Image: Strongly chordal graphs.svg.* In the first graph, the 8-cycle has odd edges 3-6 and 2-7, and the 6-cycles each also have one of those two edges as an odd edge. In the second graph, although the graph without the red edge is [chordal](wikipedia://en/744165), the red edge must be added to make an odd chord for the 6-cycle formed by all vertices excluding 4 and 5.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strongly chordal graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) area of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) $G$ is **strongly chordal** if it is a [chordal graph](wikipedia://en/744165) and every [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) of even length (≥ 6) in $G$ has an *odd chord*, i.e., an edge that connects two [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) that are an odd [distance](wikipedia://en/1020021) (>1) apart from each other in the cycle.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterizations"}

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

Strongly chordal graphs have a [forbidden subgraph characterization](wikipedia://en/13547826) as the graphs that do not contain an induced cycle of length greater than three or an [*n*-sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-sun) (*n* ≥ 3) as an [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188).[^o5] An *n*-sun is a chordal graph with 2*n* vertices, partitioned into two subsets *U* = {*u*<sub>1</sub>, *u*<sub>2</sub>,...} and *W* = {*w*<sub>1</sub>, *w*<sub>2</sub>,...}, such that each vertex *w<sub>i</sub>* in *W* has exactly two neighbors, *u*<sub>*i*</sub> and *u*<sub>(*i* + 1) mod *n*</sub>. An *n*-sun cannot be strongly chordal, because the cycle *u*<sub>1</sub>*w*<sub>1</sub>*u*<sub>2</sub>*w*<sub>2</sub>... has no odd chord.

Strongly chordal graphs may also be characterized as the graphs having a strong perfect elimination ordering, an ordering of the vertices such that the neighbors of any vertex that come later in the ordering form a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) and such that, for each *i* \< *j* \< *k* \< *l*, if the *i*th vertex in the ordering is adjacent to the *k*th and the *l*th vertices, and the *j*th and *k*th vertices are adjacent, then the *j*th and *l*th vertices must also be adjacent.[^o6]

A graph is strongly chordal [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) every one of its induced subgraphs has a simple vertex, a vertex whose neighbors have neighborhoods that are linearly ordered by inclusion.[^o7] Also, a graph is strongly chordal if and only if it is chordal and every cycle of length five or more has a 2-chord triangle, a triangle formed by two chords and an edge of the cycle.[^o8]

A graph is strongly chordal if and only if each of its induced subgraphs is a [dually chordal graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dually_chordal_graph).[^o9]

Strongly chordal graphs may also be characterized in terms of the number of [complete subgraphs](wikipedia://en/524466) each edge participates in.[^o10] Yet another characterization is given in.[^o11]

## Recognition {#Recognition}

It is possible to determine whether a graph is strongly chordal in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time), by repeatedly searching for and removing a simple vertex. If this process eliminates all vertices in the graph, the graph must be strongly chordal; otherwise, if this process finds a subgraph without any more simple vertices, the original graph cannot be strongly chordal. For a strongly chordal graph, the order in which the vertices are removed by this process is a strong perfect elimination ordering.[^o13]

Alternative algorithms are now known that can determine whether a graph is strongly chordal and, if so, construct a strong perfect elimination ordering more efficiently, in time O(min(*n*<sup>2</sup>, (*n* + *m*) log *n*)) for a graph with *n* vertices and *m* edges.[^o14]

## Subclasses {#Subclasses}

An important subclass (based on [phylogeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylogeny)) is the class of $k$-[leaf powers](wikipedia://en/49639194), the graphs formed from the leaves of a tree by connecting two leaves by an edge when their distance in the tree is at most $k$. A leaf power is a graph that is a $k$-leaf power for some $k$. Since powers of strongly chordal graphs are strongly chordal and trees are strongly chordal, it follows that leaf powers are strongly chordal. They form a proper subclass of strongly chordal graphs, which in turn includes the [cluster graphs](wikipedia://en/50931136) as the 2-leaf powers.[^o16] Another important subclass of strongly chordal graphs are [interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423). In [^o17] it is shown that interval graphs and the larger class of rooted directed path graphs are leaf powers.

## Algorithmic problems {#Algorithmic_problems}

Since strongly chordal graphs are both [chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/744165) and [dually chordal graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dually_chordal_graph), various NP-complete problems such as Independent Set, Clique, Coloring, [Clique Cover](wikipedia://en/17113364), [Dominating Set](wikipedia://en/1747972), and Steiner Tree can be solved efficiently for strongly chordal graphs. Graph isomorphism is isomorphism-complete for strongly chordal graphs.[^o19] Hamiltonian Circuit remains NP-complete for strongly chordal [split graphs](wikipedia://en/9928681).[^o20]
