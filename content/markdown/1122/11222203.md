# Spectrum bias

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spectrum bias"}

In [biostatistics](wikipedia://en/3878), **spectrum bias** refers to the phenomenon that the performance of a [diagnostic test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagnostic_test) may vary in different clinical settings because each setting has a different mix of patients.[^o2] Because the performance may be dependent on the mix of patients, performance at one clinic may not be predictive of performance at another clinic.[^o3] These differences are interpreted as a kind of *bias*. Mathematically, the spectrum bias is a [sampling bias](wikipedia://en/17692) and not a traditional [statistical bias](wikipedia://en/184099); this has led some authors to refer to the phenomenon as *spectrum effects*,[^o4] whilst others maintain it is a bias if the true performance of the test differs from that which is 'expected'.[^o3] Usually the performance of a diagnostic test is measured in terms of its [sensitivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_%28tests%29) and [specificity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specificity_%28tests%29) and it is changes in these that are considered when referring to spectrum bias. However, other performance measures such as the [likelihood ratios](wikipedia://en/935451) may also be affected by spectrum bias.[^o3]

:::

Generally spectrum bias is considered to have three causes.[^o3] The first is due to a change in the case-mix of those patients with the target disorder (disease) and this affects the [sensitivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_%28tests%29). The second is due to a change in the case-mix of those without the target disorder (disease-free) and this affects the [specificity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specificity_%28tests%29). The third is due to a change in the prevalence, and this affects both the [sensitivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_%28tests%29) and [specificity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specificity_%28tests%29).[^o5] This final cause is not widely appreciated, but there is mounting [empirical evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence)[^o5][^o6] as well as theoretical arguments[^o7] which suggest that it does indeed affect a test's performance.

Examples where the [sensitivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_%28tests%29) and [specificity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specificity_%28tests%29) change between different sub-groups of patients may be found with the [carcinoembryonic antigen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinoembryonic_antigen) test[^o8] and [urinary dipstick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urine_test_strip) tests.[^o9]

Diagnostic test performances reported by some studies may be artificially overestimated if it is a case-control design where a healthy population ('fittest of the fit') is compared with a population with advanced disease ('sickest of the sick'); that is two extreme populations are compared, rather than typical healthy and diseased populations.[^o10]

If properly analyzed, recognition of [heterogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneity_%28statistics%29) of subgroups can lead to insights about the test's performance in varying populations.[^o4]
