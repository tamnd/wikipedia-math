# Nancy Kopell

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Nancy Kopell"}

**Nancy Jane Kopell** (born November 8, 1942, [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City)) is an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and professor at [Boston University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_University). She is co-director of the Center for Computational Neuroscience and Neural Technology (CompNet). She organized and directs the Cognitive Rhythms Collaborative (CRC). Kopell received her B.A. from Cornell University in 1963 and her Ph.D. from Berkeley in 1967. She held visiting positions at the Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique in France (1970), MIT (1975, 1976–1977), and the California Institute of Technology (1976).

:::

The focus of her research is the field of applied [biomathematics](wikipedia://en/662088) and includes use of mathematical models to analyze the physiological mechanisms of brain dynamics. The techniques Kopell uses include extensions of [invariant manifold theory](wikipedia://en/5494713), [averaging theory](wikipedia://en/18340), and [geometric methods](wikipedia://en/18973446) for singularly perturbed equations. From the peak of her career in 1990, she has contributed to over 200 published research articles in the field of biomathematics. Her current interests include topics such as: how does the brain produce its dynamics ([physiological mechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology)), how do brain rhythms take part in cognition (sensory processing, attention, memory, motor control), and how can pathologies of brain dynamics help to understand symptoms of neurological diseases ([Parkinson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease), [schizophrenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia), [epilepsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epilepsy)) as well as altered states of consciousness ([anesthesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anesthesia)). She collaborates widely with experimentalists and clinicians in order to conduct research on these topics.[^o5]

Kopell is a [1990 MacArthur Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Fellows_Program).

## Biography {#Biography}

### Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Kopell was born on November 8, 1942, and grew up on Pelham Parkway in the [Bronx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bronx). Her father was an accountant and her mother and older sister also majored in mathematics.[^o8] As a child, she had a severe eye problem which taught her how to cope with being "different." This ability to cope would help her successfully deal with feelings of marginalization she would later experience as a female scientist. She attributes her professional success to this ability as well as the support she received from mentors throughout her career. Her high school teachers encouraged her to go into the mathematics field.[^o9]

During her undergraduate education at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University), she registered for a mathematics honors program, and was the only female participant.[^o9] Kopell graduated from Cornell with an A.B. in 1963.[^o10]

She then decided to attend graduate school in order to "find an alternative to the more traditional life her family expected for her."[^o9] Kopell originally applied to programs on the East Coast and was admitted to all of them except for one; however, after being encouraged by another student, she chose to attend school on the West Coast instead.[^o9] She was admitted to the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), with a fellowship and graduated with an M.A. and a Ph.D. in 1967.[^o10] At Berkeley, she did well on her exams and was known as the "bright female."[^o9] However, she struggled in the early stages of her dissertation work and switched supervisors to [Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294).[^o9] Smale suggested a problem which Kopell almost singlehandedly solved, leading to her thesis in the field of dynamical systems which catapulted her career.[^o9]

### Academic career {#Academic_career}

After completing her graduate education, Kopell accepted an instructorship at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology).[^o9] There, she met collaborator Lou Howard, with whom she published several articles. She later met her husband, Gabriel Stolzenberg, at Northeastern University.[^o9]

While Kopell did her thesis work in theoretical mathematics, she later switched to applied mathematics.

In 1969, she joined [Northeastern University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_University) as faculty, becoming a full professor in 1978. In 1986, she became a professor of mathematics at Boston University and in 2009 she became the first woman at [Boston University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_University) to be named a [William Fairfield Warren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fairfield_Warren) Distinguished Professor.[^o12][^o13] She was awarded a [MacArthur Foundation Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Fellows_Program) in 1990 for her work developing methods of dynamical systems to attack problems of applied mathematics.[^o13] She is currently Director and Co-Founder of the Cognitive Rhythms Collaborative, which consists of a group of over two dozen labs, mostly in the Boston Area, working on brain dynamics and their cognitive implications. She is also Co-Director of the Center for Computational Neuroscience and Neural Technology (CompNet).[^o14][^o15]

Kopell is a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. She was recently selected to be an honorary member of the London Mathematical Society, a distinction given to one or two mathematicians per year worldwide. She has been awarded Sloan Guggenheim, and McArthur Fellowships, and has an honorary Ph.D. from the [New Jersey Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey_Institute_of_Technology). She has given the Weldon Memorial Prize Lecture (Oxford), the [von Neumann Lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Prize), and the Josiah Willard Gibbs Lecture (AMS), as well as multiple other named lectureships.[^o5]
