# Functor represented by a scheme

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Functor represented by a scheme"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **functor represented by a scheme** *X* is a set-valued [contravariant functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_functor) on the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) such that the value of the functor at each scheme *S* is (up to natural [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942), or one-to-one correspondence) the set of all [morphisms](wikipedia://en/7482797) $S \to X$. The functor *F* is then said to be naturally equivalent to the **functor of points** of *X*; and the scheme *X* is said to *[represent](wikipedia://en/844461)* the functor *F*, and to *classify* geometric objects over *S* given by *F*.[^o2]

:::

A functor producing certain geometric objects over *S* might be represented by a scheme *X*. For example, the functor taking *S* to the set of all [line bundles](wikipedia://en/361366) over *S* (or more precisely *n-*dimensional [linear systems](wikipedia://en/1186886)) is represented by the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $X = \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$. Another example is the [Hilbert scheme](wikipedia://en/6612581) *X* of a scheme *Y*, which represents the functor sending a scheme *S* to the set of closed subschemes of $Y\times S$ which are [flat families](wikipedia://en/542520) over *S*.[^o3]

In some applications, it may not be possible to find a scheme that represents a given functor. This led to the notion of a [stack](wikipedia://en/13185596), which is [not quite a functor](wikipedia://en/41702055) but can still be treated as if it were a geometric space. (A [Hilbert scheme](wikipedia://en/6612581) is a scheme rather than a stack, because, very roughly speaking, [deformation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) is simpler for closed schemes.)

Some moduli problems are solved by giving [formal solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_solution) (as opposed to polynomial algebraic solutions) and in that case, the resulting functor is represented by a [formal scheme](wikipedia://en/18829483). Such a formal scheme is then said to be [algebraizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraizable) if there is a scheme that can represent the same functor, up to some isomorphisms.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

The notion is an analog of a [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), where each principal *G*-bundle over a space *S* is (up to natural [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828)) the pullback of the universal bundle $EG \to BG$ along some map $S\to BG$. To give a principal *G*-bundle over *S* is the same as to give a map (called a classifying map) from *S* to the classifying space $BG$.

A similar phenomenon in algebraic geometry is given by a [linear system](wikipedia://en/1186886): to give a morphism from a base variety *S* to a projective space $X = \mathbb{P}^n$ is equivalent to giving a [basepoint-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basepoint-free) linear system (or equivalently a line bundle) on *S*. That is, the projective space *X* represents the functor which gives all line bundles over *S*.

[Yoneda's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoneda's_lemma) says that a scheme *X* determines and is determined by its functor of points.[^o5]

## Functor of points {#Functor_of_points}

Let *X* be a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754). Its *functor of points* is the functor

> Hom(−,*X*) : (Affine schemes)<sup>op</sup> ⟶ Sets

sending an affine scheme *Y* to the set of [scheme maps](wikipedia://en/7482797) $Y \to X$.[^o8]

A scheme is determined up to isomorphism by its functor of points. This is a stronger version of the [Yoneda lemma](wikipedia://en/34359), which says that a *X* is determined by the map Hom(−,*X*) : Schemes<sup>op</sup> → Sets.

Conversely, a functor *F* : (Affine schemes)<sup>op</sup> → Sets is the functor of points of some scheme if and only if *F* is a sheaf with respect to the [Zariski topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_site) on (Affine schemes), and *F* admits an open cover by affine schemes.[^o9][^o10]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Points as characters {#Points_as_characters}

Let *X* be a scheme over the base ring *B*. If *x* is a set-theoretic point of *X*, then the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) $k(x)$ is the residue field of the [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) $\mathcal{O}_{X, x}$ (i.e., the quotient by the maximal ideal). For example, if *X* is an affine scheme Spec(*A*) and *x* is a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, then the residue field of *x* is the [function field](wikipedia://en/381301) of the closed subscheme $\operatorname{Spec}(A/\mathfrak{p})$.

For simplicity, suppose $X = \operatorname{Spec}(A)$. Then the inclusion of a set-theoretic point *x* into *X* corresponds to the ring homomorphism:

$$A \to k(x)$$

(which is $A \to A_{\mathfrak{p}} \to k(\mathfrak{p})$ if $x =\mathfrak{p}$.)

The above should be compared to the [spectrum of a commutative Banach algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_a_commutative_Banach_algebra).

### Points as sections {#Points_as_sections}

By the universal property of [fiber product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_product), each *R*-point of a scheme *X* determines a morphism of *R*-schemes

$$\operatorname{Spec}(R) \to X_R \overset{\mathrm{def}}= X \times_{\operatorname{Spec}(B)} \operatorname{Spec}(R);$$

i.e., a section of the projection $X_R \to \operatorname{Spec}(R)$. If *S* is a subset of *X*(*R*), then one writes $|S| \subset X_R$ for the set of the images of the sections determined by elements in *S*.[^o15]

### Spec of the ring of dual numbers {#Spec_of_the_ring_of_dual_numbers}

Let $D = \operatorname{Spec}(k[t]/(t^2))$, the Spec of the [ring of dual numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_dual_numbers) over a field *k* and *X* a scheme over *k*. Then each $D \to X$ amounts to the tangent vector to *X* at the point that is the image of the [closed point](wikipedia://en/33086408) of the map.[^o2] In other words, $X(D)$ is the set of tangent vectors to *X*.

:::

## Universal object {#Universal_object}

Let $F$ be the functor represented by a scheme $X$. Under the [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) $F(X) \cong \text{Hom}(X, X)$, there is a unique element of $F(X)$ that corresponds to the identity map $\text{id}_X : X \to X$. This unique element is known as the [universal object](wikipedia://en/59405) or the universal family (when the objects being classified are families). The universal object acts as a template from which all other elements in $F(S)$ for any scheme $S$ can be derived via pullback along a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) from $S$ to $X$.[^o2]
