# Coefficient of relationship

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coefficient of relationship"}

The **coefficient of relationship** is a measure of the degree of [consanguinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consanguinity) (or biological relationship) between two individuals. The term [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) of relationship was defined by [Sewall Wright](wikipedia://en/1700593) in 1922, and was derived from his definition of the coefficient of [inbreeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inbreeding) of 1921. The measure is most commonly used in [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics) and [genealogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genealogy). A [coefficient of inbreeding](wikipedia://en/8543254) can be calculated for an individual, and is typically one-half the coefficient of relationship between the parents.

:::

In general, the higher the level of inbreeding the closer the coefficient of relationship between the parents approaches a value of 1, expressed as a percentage, and approaches a value of 0 for individuals with arbitrarily remote common ancestors.

## Coefficient of relationship {#Coefficient_of_relationship}

The coefficient of relationship $r$ between two people B and C is obtained by a summation of coefficients calculated for every line by which they are connected to their [common ancestors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identical_ancestors_point). Each such line connects the two people via a common ancestor, passing through no person who is not a common ancestor more than once. A path coefficient between an ancestor A and an offspring O separated by $n$ generations is given as:

$$p_{AO} = 2^{-n} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1 + f_A}{1 + f_O}}$$

where $f_A$ and $f_O$ are the [coefficients of inbreeding](wikipedia://en/8543254) for A and O, respectively.

The coefficient of relationship $r_{BC}$ is now obtained by summing over all path coefficients:

$$r_{BC} = \sum p_{AB} \cdot p_{AC}$$

By assuming that the pedigree can be traced back to a sufficiently remote population of perfectly random-bred stock (*f*<sub>A</sub> = 0 for all *A* in the sum) the definition of *r* may be simplified to

$$r_{BC} = \sum_p 2^{-L(p)}$$

where *p* enumerates all paths connecting B and C with unique common ancestors (i.e. all paths terminate at a common ancestor and may not pass through a common ancestor to a common ancestor's ancestor), and *L*(*p*) is the length of the path *p*.

To give an (artificial) example: Assuming that two people share the same 32 ancestors of *n* = 5 generations ago, but do not have any common ancestors at four or fewer generations ago, their coefficient of relationship would be

$r = 2^n \cdot 2^{-2n} = 2^{-n}$, which for *n* = 5, is, $2^{-5} = \frac{1}{32}$, equal to 0.03125 or approximately 3%.

People for which the same situation applies for their 1024 ancestors of ten generations ago would have a coefficient of *r* = 2<sup>−10</sup> = 0.1%. If follows that the value of *r* can be given to an accuracy of a few percent if the family tree of both people is known for a depth of five generations, and to an accuracy of a tenth of a percent if the known depth is at least ten generations. The contribution to *r* from common ancestors of 20 generations ago (corresponding to roughly 500 years in human genealogy, or the contribution from common descent from a [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) population) falls below one [part-per-million](wikipedia://en/145865).

## Human relationships {#Human_relationships}

*Image: Coefficient of relatedness.png.* Diagram of common family relationships, where the area of each colored circle is scaled according to the coefficient of relatedness. All relatives of the same relatedness are included together in one of the gray ellipses. Legal [degrees of relationship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_relationship) can be found by counting the number of solid-line connections between the self and a relative.

The coefficient of relationship is sometimes used to express [degrees of kinship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_kinship) in numeric terms in human [genealogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genealogy).

In human relationships, the value of the coefficient of relationship is usually calculated based on the knowledge of a full family tree extending to a comparatively small number of generations, perhaps of the order of three or four. As explained above, the value for the coefficient of relationship so calculated is thus a lower bound, with an actual value that may be up to a few percent higher. The value is accurate to within 1% if the full family tree of both individuals is known to a depth of seven generations.

A first-degree relative (FDR) is a person's [parent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parent) (father or mother), [sibling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sibling) (brother or sister) or [child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offspring) (son or daughter).[^o4] It constitutes a category of family members that largely overlaps with the term [nuclear family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_family), but without spouses.[^o5] If the persons are [related by blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consanguinity), the first degree relatives share approximately 50% of their genes. First-degree relatives are a common measure used to diagnose risks for common diseases by analyzing family history.[^o6]

A second-degree relative (SDR) is someone who shares 25% of a person's genes. It includes [uncles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncle), [aunts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aunt), [nephews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nephew), [nieces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niece), [grandparents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandparent), [grandchildren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandchild), [half-siblings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-sibling) and double-first cousins.[^o7][^o8][^o9]

Third-degree relatives are a segment of the [extended family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_family) and includes first cousins, great-grandparents and great-grandchildren.[^o10] Third-degree relatives are generally defined by the expected amount of genetic overlap that exists between two people, with the third-degree relatives of an individual sharing approximately 12.5% of their genes.[^o11] The category includes great-grandparents, great-grandchildren, granduncles, grandaunts, grandnephews, grandnieces, first cousins,[^o12] half-uncles, half-aunts, half-nieces and half-nephews.

In a clinical sense, marriage between two family members who have *r* = 3.125% (2<sup>−5</sup>) or higher qualifies as [consanguineous marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consanguineous_marriage). Most [incest laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest_laws) concern the relationships where *r* = 25% (2<sup>−2</sup>) or higher, although many ignore the rare case of double first cousins. Some jurisdictions also prohibit sexual relations or marriage [between cousins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cousin_marriage) of various degree, or individuals related only through [adoption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adoption) or [affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affinity_%28law%29). Whether there is any likelihood of conception is generally considered irrelevant.

## Kinship coefficient {#Kinship_coefficient}

The **kinship coefficient** is a simple measure of relatedness, defined as the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) that a pair of randomly sampled homologous [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alleles) are [identical by descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_by_descent).[^o18] More simply, it is the probability that an allele selected randomly from an individual, i, and an allele selected at the same [autosomal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autosomal) locus from another individual, j, are identical and from the same ancestor.

::: {#o19 .table cue=table}

| Relationship | Kinship coefficient |
|---|---|
| self | 1/2 |
| mother / father / daughter / son | 1/4 |
| sister / brother | 1/4 |
| grandmother / grandfather / granddaughter / grandson | 1/8 |
| aunt / uncle / niece / nephew | 1/8 |
| first cousin | 1/16 |
| half-sister / half-brother | 1/8 |
| half-first cousin | 1/32 |
| double-first cousin | 1/8 |
| Several of the most common family relationships and their corresponding kinship coefficient. |  |

:::

The coefficient of relatedness is equal to twice the kinship coefficient.[^o20]

### Calculation {#Calculation}

The kinship coefficient between two individuals, i and j, is represented as Φ<sub>*ij*</sub>. The kinship coefficient between a non-inbred individual and itself, Φ<sub>*ii*</sub>, is equal to 1/2. This is due to the fact that humans are [diploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ploidy), meaning the only way for the randomly chosen alleles to be identical by descent is if the same allele is chosen twice (probability 1/2). Similarly, the relationship between a parent and a child is found by the chance that the randomly picked allele in the child is from the parent (probability 1/2) and the probability of the allele that is picked from the parent being the same one passed to the child (probability 1/2). Since these two events are independent of each other, they are multiplied Φ<sub>*ij*</sub> = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.[^o22][^o23]

:::
