# Einstein manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Einstein manifold"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), an **Einstein manifold** is a [Riemannian](wikipedia://en/144652) or [pseudo-Riemannian](wikipedia://en/287555) [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) whose [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) is proportional to the [metric](wikipedia://en/195795). They are named after [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) because this condition is equivalent to saying that the metric is a solution of the [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum) [Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540) (with [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992)), although both the dimension and the signature of the metric can be arbitrary, thus not being restricted to [Lorentzian manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_manifold) (including the four-dimensional Lorentzian manifolds usually studied in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024)). Einstein manifolds in four Euclidean dimensions are studied as [gravitational instantons](wikipedia://en/2618270).

:::

If $M$ is the underlying $n$-dimensional [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), and $g$ is its [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795), the Einstein condition means that

$$\mathrm{Ric} = kg$$

for some constant $k$, where $\operatorname{Ric}$ denotes the [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) of $g$. Einstein manifolds with $k = 0$ are called [Ricci-flat manifolds](wikipedia://en/644797).

## The Einstein condition and Einstein's equation {#The_Einstein_condition_and_Einstein's_equation}

In local coordinates the condition that $(M, g)$ be an Einstein manifold is simply

$$R_{ab} = kg_{ab} .$$

Taking the trace of both sides reveals that the constant of proportionality $k$ for Einstein manifolds is related to the [scalar curvature](wikipedia://en/285622) $R$ by

$$R = nk ,$$

where $n$ is the dimension of $M$.

In [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), [Einstein's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein's_equation) with a [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992) $\Lambda$ is

$$R_{ab} - \frac{1}{2}g_{ab}R + g_{ab}\Lambda = \kappa T_{ab},$$

where $\kappa$ is the [Einstein gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_gravitational_constant).[^o3] The [stress–energy tensor](wikipedia://en/70671) $T_{ab}$ gives the matter and energy content of the underlying spacetime. In [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum) (a region of spacetime devoid of matter) $T_{ab} = 0$, and Einstein's equation can be rewritten in the form (assuming that $n \neq 2$):

$$R_{ab} = \frac{2\Lambda}{n-2}\,g_{ab} .$$

Therefore, vacuum solutions of Einstein's equation are (Lorentzian) Einstein manifolds with $k$ proportional to the cosmological constant.

## Examples {#Examples}

Simple examples of Einstein manifolds include:

- All 2D manifolds admit Einstein metrics. In fact, in this dimension, a metric is Einstein if and only if it has constant Gauss curvature. The classical uniformization theorem for Riemann surfaces guarantees that there is such a metric in every conformal class on any 2-manifold.
- Any manifold with [constant sectional curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_sectional_curvature) is an Einstein manifold—in particular:
  - [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), which is flat, is a simple example of Ricci-flat, hence Einstein metric.
  - The [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782), $S^n$, with the round metric is Einstein with $k=n-1$.
  - [Hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) with the canonical metric is Einstein with $k = -(n - 1)$.
- [Complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977), $\mathbf{CP}^n$, with the [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486), have $k = 2n + 2.$
- [Calabi–Yau manifolds](wikipedia://en/188932) admit an Einstein metric that is also [Kähler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_metric), with Einstein constant $k=0$. Such metrics are not unique, but rather come in families; there is a Calabi–Yau metric in every Kähler class, and the metric also depends on the choice of complex structure. For example, there is a 60-parameter family of such metrics on [K3](wikipedia://en/646116), 57 parameters of which give rise to Einstein metrics which are not related by isometries or rescalings.
- [Kähler–Einstein metrics](wikipedia://en/7335270) exist on a variety of compact [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742) due to the existence results of [Shing-Tung Yau](wikipedia://en/303137), and the later study of [K-stability](wikipedia://en/62719320) especially in the case of [Fano manifolds](wikipedia://en/70175623).
- Irreducible symmetric spaces, as classified by Élie Cartan, are always Einstein. Among these spaces, the compact ones all have positive Einstein constant $k$. Examples of these include the Grassmannians $Gr (k, \mathbb{R}^\ell)$, $Gr (k, \mathbb{C}^\ell)$, and $Gr (k, \mathbb{H}^\ell)$. Every such compact space has a so-called non-compact dual, which instead has negative Einstein constant $k$. These dual pairs are related in manner that is exactly parallel to the relationship between spheres and hyperbolic spaces.

One necessary condition for [closed](wikipedia://en/669475), [oriented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented), [4-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1362795) to be Einstein is satisfying the [Hitchin–Thorpe inequality](wikipedia://en/22283794). However, this necessary condition is very far from sufficient, as further obstructions have been discovered by LeBrun, Sambusetti, and others.

## Applications {#Applications}

Four dimensional Riemannian Einstein manifolds are also important in mathematical physics as [gravitational instantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_instantons) in [quantum theories of gravity](wikipedia://en/25312). The term "gravitational instanton" is sometimes restricted to Einstein 4-manifolds whose [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848) is anti-self-dual, and it is very often assumed that the metric is asymptotic to the standard metric on a finite quotient Euclidean 4-space (and are therefore [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_metric) but [non-compact](wikipedia://en/6042)). In differential geometry, simply connected self-dual Einstein 4-manifolds are coincide with the 4-dimensional, reverse-oriented [hyperkähler manifolds](wikipedia://en/646120) in the Ricci-flat case, but are sometimes called [quaternion Kähler manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternion_Kähler_manifold) otherwise.

Higher-dimensional Lorentzian Einstein manifolds are used in modern theories of gravity, such as [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), [M-theory](wikipedia://en/20406) and [supergravity](wikipedia://en/432632). Hyperkähler and quaternion Kähler manifolds (which are special kinds of Einstein manifolds) also have applications in physics as target spaces for [nonlinear σ-models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_σ-model) with [supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/224636).

Compact Einstein manifolds have been much studied in differential geometry, and many examples are known, although constructing them is often challenging. Compact Ricci-flat manifolds are particularly difficult to find: in the monograph on the subject by the pseudonymous author [Arthur Besse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Besse), readers are offered a meal in a [starred restaurant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelin_star) in exchange for a new example.[^o8]
