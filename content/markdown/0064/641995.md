# Asymptotic analysis

*Image: Asymptotic approximation.jpg.* Figure 1. Aysmptotic approximation: The function f(x)=x is an asymptotic approximation for the function f(x)=x+e^-x for large positive values of x.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Asymptotic analysis"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), **asymptotic analysis**, also known as **asymptotics**, is the development and application of methods that generate an approximate [analytical solution](wikipedia://en/61478) to a mathematical problem when a variable or parameter assumes a value that is large, small or near a specified value.[^n1]

:::

An example of asymptotic analysis is function approximation. For example, the function $\widetilde{y}(x)=x$ accurately approximates the function $y(x)=x+e^{-x}$ for large positive $x$ values (Figure 1). For any desired accuracy, there is a corresponding range of $x$ values where this accuracy occurs. In this case, a chosen accuracy with a relative error of less than 1% occurs when the $x$ values are greater than 3.4.

The [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692) is an example of an important asymptotic result. For any real number $x$, the prime counting function, denoted as $\operatorname{\pi}(x)$, is the number of prime numbers less than or equal to $x$. The function, $x / \ln(x)$, approximates the prime counting function for large numbers $x$. As in the preceding example, as the number $x$ becomes increasingly larger, the approximating function becomes increasingly more accurate, leading to an increasingly smaller relative error. This asymptotic relationship is expressed using this notation:

$$\pi(x)\sim\frac{x}{\ln(x)} \quad (x \to \infty)$$

Asymptotic analysis impacts on many areas of mathematics. Mathematicians use asymptotic analysis for computing [function](wikipedia://en/185427) approximations, [implicit functions](wikipedia://en/250896), [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532), [iterated functions](wikipedia://en/1448702), [series](wikipedia://en/15287) summation, [partial sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum), solutions of [difference equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_equation). solutions of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) and properties of [computer algorithms](wikipedia://en/2230).[^n2][^n3][^n4][^n5]

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Formally, given functions $f\,(x)$ and $g(x)$, we define a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931)

$$f(x) \sim g(x) \quad (\text{as } x\to\infty)$$

[if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) (de Bruijn 1981, §1.4)

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = 1.$$

The symbol $\sim$ is the [tilde](wikipedia://en/212390). The relation is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on the set of functions of $x$; the functions $f$ and $g$ are said to be *asymptotically equivalent*. The [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of $f$ and $g$ can be any set for which the limit is defined: e.g. real numbers, complex numbers, positive integers.

The same notation is also used for other ways of passing to a limit: e.g. $x \to 0$, $x \downarrow 0$, $\lvert x\rvert \to 0$. The way of passing to the limit is often not stated explicitly, if it is clear from the context.

Although the above definition is common in the literature, it is problematic if $g(x)$ is zero infinitely often as $x$ goes to the limiting value. For that reason, some authors use an alternative definition. The alternative definition, in [little-o notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little-o_notation), is that $f \sim g$ if and only if

$$f(x)=g(x)(1+o(1)).$$

This definition is equivalent to the prior definition if $g(x)$ is not zero in some [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of the limiting value.[^n5]

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The zero function, $f(z)=0$, can never be equivalent to any other function.[^n6]

The [little-o](wikipedia://en/44578) relation has the [partial ordering property](wikipedia://en/23572) defined as if $f(x)=o(g(x))$ and $g(x)=o(h(x))$ then $f(x)=o(h(x))$.[^n6]

Asymptotic equivalence has [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458), [symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459) and [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463) properties.

If these functions are equivalent, $f(x)\sim g(x) \, (x\to L)$, then the exponentiated functions to the real power $r$ are also equivalent:[^n7]

$$(f(x))^{r} \sim (g(x))^{r} \ (x \to L)$$

If these functions are equivalent $f(x) \sim g(x) \, (x \to L)$, and these functions are equivalent $h(x) \sim k(x) \, (x \to L)$, then the quotients are equivalent:[^n7]

$$\frac{f(x)}{h(x)} \sim \frac{g(x)}{k(x)} \ (x \to L)$$

These properties allow asymptotically equivalent functions to be freely exchanged in many algebraic expressions.

Asymptotically equivalent functions remain asymptotically equivalent under [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) if requirements related to convergence are met. There are more stringent requirements for asymptotically equivalent functions to remain asymptotically equivalent under [differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation).[^n8]

:::

## Examples of asymptotic formulas {#Examples_of_asymptotic_formulas}

- [Factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) $$n! \sim \sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^n$$ —this is [Stirling's approximation](wikipedia://en/151783)
- [Partition function](wikipedia://en/312250) For a positive integer *n*, the partition function, *p*(*n*), gives the number of ways of writing the integer *n* as a sum of positive integers, where the order of addends is not considered. $$p(n)\sim \frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}} e^{\pi\sqrt{\frac{2n}{3}}}$$
- [Airy function](wikipedia://en/406896) The Airy function, Ai(*x*), is a solution of the differential equation $y'' - xy = 0$; it has many applications in physics. $$\operatorname{Ai}(x) \sim \frac{e^{-\frac{2}{3} x^\frac{3}{2}}}{2\sqrt{\pi} x^{1/4}}$$
- [Hankel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hankel_functions) $$\begin{aligned} H_\alpha^{(1)}(z) &\sim \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}} e^{ i\left(z - \frac{2\pi\alpha - \pi}{4}\right)} \\ H_\alpha^{(2)}(z) &\sim \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}} e^{-i\left(z - \frac{2\pi\alpha - \pi}{4}\right)} \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Asymptotic expansion {#Asymptotic_expansion}

An [asymptotic expansion](wikipedia://en/642090) of a function $f(x)$ is in practice an expression of that function in terms of a [series](wikipedia://en/15287), the [partial sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) of which do not necessarily converge, but such that taking any initial partial sum provides an asymptotic formula for $f$. The idea is that successive terms provide an increasingly accurate description of the order of growth of $f$.

In symbols, it means we have $f \sim g_1,$ but also $f - g_1 \sim g_2$ and $f - g_1 - \cdots - g_{k-1} \sim g_{k}$ for each fixed *k*. In view of the definition of the $\sim$ symbol, the last equation means $f - (g_1 + \cdots + g_k) = o(g_k)$ in the [little o notation](wikipedia://en/44578#Little-o_notation), i.e., $f - (g_1 + \cdots + g_k)$ is much smaller than $g_k.$

The relation $f - g_1 - \cdots - g_{k-1} \sim g_{k}$ takes its full meaning if $g_{k+1} = o(g_k)$ for all *k*, which means the $g_k$ form an [asymptotic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_scale). In that case, some authors may [abusively](wikipedia://en/1698066) write $f \sim g_1 + \cdots + g_k$ to denote the statement $f - (g_1 + \cdots + g_k) = o(g_k).$ One should however be careful that this is not a standard use of the $\sim$ symbol, and that it does not correspond to the definition given in .

In the present situation, this relation $g_{k} = o(g_{k-1})$ actually follows from combining steps *k* and *k*−1; by subtracting $f - g_1 - \cdots - g_{k-2} = g_{k-1} + o(g_{k-1})$ from $f - g_1 - \cdots - g_{k-2} - g_{k-1} = g_{k} + o(g_{k}),$ one gets $g_{k} + o(g_{k})=o(g_{k-1}),$ i.e. $g_{k} = o(g_{k-1}).$

In case the asymptotic expansion does not converge, for any particular value of the argument there will be a particular partial sum which provides the best approximation and adding additional terms will decrease the accuracy. This optimal partial sum will usually have more terms as the argument approaches the limit value.

### Examples of asymptotic expansions {#Examples_of_asymptotic_expansions}

- [Gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) $$\frac{e^x}{x^x \sqrt{2\pi x}} \Gamma(x+1) \sim 1+\frac{1}{12x}+\frac{1}{288x^2}-\frac{139}{51840x^3}-\cdots \ (x \to \infty)$$
- [Exponential integral](wikipedia://en/648614) $$xe^xE_1(x) \sim \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^nn!}{x^n} \ (x \to \infty)$$
- [Error function](wikipedia://en/177111) $$\sqrt{\pi}x e^{x^2}\operatorname{erfc}(x) \sim 1+\sum_{n=1}^\infty (-1)^n \frac{(2n-1)!!}{n!(2x^2)^n} \ (x \to \infty)$$ where $m!!$ is the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208).

### Worked example {#Worked_example}

Asymptotic expansions often occur when an ordinary series is used in a formal expression that forces the taking of values outside of its domain of convergence. For example, we might start with the [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012)

$$\frac{1}{1-w}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty w^n$$

The expression on the left is valid on the entire [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) $w \ne 1$, while the right hand side converges only for $|w|< 1$. Multiplying by $e^{-w/t}$ and integrating both sides yields

$$\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-\frac{w}{t}}}{1 - w} \, dw = \sum_{n=0}^\infty t^{n+1} \int_0^\infty e^{-u} u^n \, du$$

The integral on the left hand side can be expressed in terms of the [exponential integral](wikipedia://en/648614). The integral on the right hand side, after the substitution $u=w/t$, may be recognized as the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316). Evaluating both, one obtains the asymptotic expansion

$$e^{-\frac{1}{t}} \operatorname{Ei}\left(\frac{1}{t}\right) = \sum _{n=0}^\infty n! \; t^{n+1}$$

Here, the right hand side is clearly not convergent for any non-zero value of *t*. However, by keeping *t* small, and truncating the series on the right to a finite number of terms, one may obtain a fairly good approximation to the value of $\operatorname{Ei}(1/t)$. Substituting $x = -1/t$ and noting that $\operatorname{Ei}(x) = -E_1(-x)$ results in the asymptotic expansion given earlier in this article.

## Asymptotic distribution {#Asymptotic_distribution}

In [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711), an [asymptotic distribution](wikipedia://en/3001287) is a hypothetical distribution that is in a sense the "limiting" distribution of a sequence of distributions. A distribution is an ordered set of random variables $Z_{i}$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, for some positive integer $n$. An asymptotic distribution allows $i$ to range without bound, that is, $n$ is infinite.

A special case of an asymptotic distribution is when the late entries go to zero—that is, the $Z_{i}$ go to 0 as $i$ goes to infinity. Some instances of "asymptotic distribution" refer only to this special case.

This is based on the notion of an [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic) function which cleanly approaches a constant value (the *asymptote*) as the independent variable goes to infinity; "clean" in this sense meaning that for any desired closeness epsilon there is some value of the independent variable after which the function never differs from the constant by more than epsilon.

An [asymptote](wikipedia://en/3107) is a straight line that a curve approaches but never meets or crosses. Informally, one may speak of the curve meeting the asymptote "at infinity" although this is not a precise definition. In the equation $y = \frac{1}{x},$ *y* becomes arbitrarily small in magnitude as *x* increases.

## Applications {#Applications}

Asymptotic analysis is used in several [mathematical sciences](wikipedia://en/25750436). In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), asymptotic theory provides limiting approximations of the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of [sample statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_statistic), such as the [likelihood ratio](wikipedia://en/45035) [statistic](wikipedia://en/26703) and the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of the [deviance](wikipedia://en/432609). Asymptotic theory does not provide a method of evaluating the finite-sample distributions of sample statistics, however. Non-asymptotic bounds are provided by methods of [approximation theory](wikipedia://en/1092713).

Examples of applications are the following.

- In [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), asymptotic analysis is used to build [numerical methods](wikipedia://en/1678715) to approximate [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) solutions.
- In [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711) and [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), asymptotics are used in analysis of long-run or large-sample behaviour of random variables and estimators.
- In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), the [analysis of algorithms](wikipedia://en/2230) evaluate the performance of algorithms and is expressed in terms of [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578).
- The behavior of [physical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system), an example being [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481).
- In [accident analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accident_analysis) when identifying the causation of crash through count modeling with large number of crash counts in a given time and space.

Asymptotic analysis is a key tool for exploring the [ordinary](wikipedia://en/32742753) and [partial](wikipedia://en/52564) differential equations which arise in the [mathematical modelling](wikipedia://en/20590) of real-world phenomena.[^n9] An illustrative example is the derivation of the [boundary layer equations](wikipedia://en/224301#Boundary_layer_equations) from the full [Navier-Stokes equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navier-Stokes_equations) governing fluid flow. In many cases, the asymptotic expansion is in power of a small parameter, $\varepsilon$: in the boundary layer case, this is the [nondimensional](wikipedia://en/8267) ratio of the boundary layer thickness to a typical [length scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_scale) of the problem. Indeed, applications of asymptotic analysis in mathematical modelling often center around a nondimensional parameter which has been shown, or assumed, to be small through a consideration of the scales of the problem at hand.[^n9]

Asymptotic expansions typically arise in the approximation of certain integrals ([Laplace's method](wikipedia://en/642006), [saddle-point method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle-point_method), [method of steepest descent](wikipedia://en/27082137)) or in the approximation of probability distributions ([Edgeworth series](wikipedia://en/641132)). The [Feynman graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_graphs) in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) are another example of asymptotic expansions which often do not converge.

### Asymptotic versus Numerical Analysis {#Asymptotic_versus_Numerical_Analysis}

De Bruijn illustrates the use of asymptotics in the following dialog between Dr. N.A., a [numerical analyst](wikipedia://en/21506), and Dr. A.A., an asymptotic analyst:

> N.A.: I want to evaluate my function $f(x)$ for large values of $x$, with a relative error of at most 1%.
>
> A.A.: $f(x)=x^{-1}+\mathrm O(x^{-2}) \qquad (x\to\infty)$.
>
> N.A.: I am sorry, I don't understand.
>
> A.A.: $|f(x)-x^{-1}|<8x^{-2} \qquad (x>10^4).$
>
> N.A.: But my value of $x$ is only 100.
>
> A.A.: Why did you not say so? My evaluations give
>
> > $$|f(x)-x^{-1}|<57000x^{-2} \qquad (x>100).$$
>
> N.A.: This is no news to me. I know already that $0<f(100)<1$.
>
> A.A.: I can gain a little on some of my estimates. Now I find that
>
> > $$|f(x)-x^{-1}|<20x^{-2} \qquad (x>100).$$
>
> N.A.: I asked for 1%, not for 20%.
>
> A.A.: It is almost the best thing I possibly can get. Why don't you take larger values of $x$?
>
> N.A.: !!! I think it's better to ask my electronic computing machine.
>
> Machine: f(100) = 0.01137 42259 34008 67153
>
> A.A.: Haven't I told you so? My estimate of 20% was not far off from the 14% of the real error.
>
> N.A.: !!! . . . !
>
> Some days later, Miss N.A. wants to know the value of f(1000), but her machine would take a month of computation to give the answer. She returns to her Asymptotic Colleague, and gets a fully satisfactory reply.[^n2]p. 19
