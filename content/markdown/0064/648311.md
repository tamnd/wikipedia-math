# Algebraic surface

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic surface"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **algebraic surface** is an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) of [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251) two. Thus, an algebraic surface is a solution of a set of polynomial equations, in which there are two independent directions at every point. An example of an algebraic surface is the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), which is determined by the single polynomial equation $x^2+y^2+z^2=1.$ Studying the intrinsic geometry of algebraic surfaces is a central topic in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). The theory is much more complicated than for [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260) (one-dimensional cases), and was developed substantially by the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424) in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It remains an active field of research.

:::

In the simplest cases, algebraic surfaces are studied as algebraic varieties over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). For example, the familiar sphere (for real $x,y,z$), becomes a complex ([affine](wikipedia://en/320468)) [quadric surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadric_surface), which simultaneously incorporates the sphere and [hyperboloids](wikipedia://en/145844) of one and two sheets, and this allows some complications (such as the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954): whether the surface is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), or [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected)) to be deferred somewhat. Higher [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_%28algebraic_geometry%29) surfaces include, for example, the [Kummer surface](wikipedia://en/647758). The classification of algebraic surfaces is much more intricate than the classification of [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260), which have dimension one, and is already quite complicated.

Algebraic surfaces, like algebraic curves, may possess singularities, which are points where there is no [tangent plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_plane). A singularity may be a self-crossing point or a point where the number of "free" dimensions may drop, such as at a [cusp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cusp_%28algebraic_geometry%29). [Catastrophe theory](wikipedia://en/434288) is strongly related with the classification of surface singularities. Likewise, algebraic surfaces may be defined over other [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) than the complex numbers. This article focuses primarily on the complex case.

## Classification by the Kodaira dimension {#Classification_by_the_Kodaira_dimension}

In the case of dimension one, varieties are classified by only the [topological genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_genus), but, in dimension two, one needs to distinguish the [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) $p_a$ and the [geometric genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) $p_g$ because one cannot distinguish birationally only the topological genus. Then, [irregularity](wikipedia://en/22857258) is introduced for the classification of varieties. A summary of the results (in detail, for each kind of surface refers to each redirection), follows:

Examples of algebraic surfaces include (κ is the [Kodaira dimension](wikipedia://en/1866873)):

- κ = −∞: the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155), [quadrics](wikipedia://en/145570) in **P**<sup>3</sup>, [cubic surfaces](wikipedia://en/526126), [Veronese surface](wikipedia://en/1569217), [del Pezzo surfaces](wikipedia://en/732323), [ruled surfaces](wikipedia://en/705158)
- κ = 0 : [K3 surfaces](wikipedia://en/646116), [abelian surfaces](wikipedia://en/974181), [Enriques surfaces](wikipedia://en/3094760), [hyperelliptic surfaces](wikipedia://en/8358276)
- κ = 1: [elliptic surfaces](wikipedia://en/3073462)
- κ = 2: [surfaces of general type](wikipedia://en/2368356).

For more examples see the [list of algebraic surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_algebraic_surfaces).

The first five examples are in fact [birationally equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birationally_equivalent). That is, for example, a cubic surface has a [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) isomorphic to that of the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350), being the [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in two indeterminates. The Cartesian product of two curves also provides examples.

## Birational geometry of surfaces {#Birational_geometry_of_surfaces}

The [birational geometry](wikipedia://en/382733) of algebraic surfaces is rich, because of [blowing up](wikipedia://en/1817790) (also known as a [monoidal transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoidal_transformation)), under which a point is replaced by the *curve* of all limiting tangent directions coming into it (a [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578)). Certain curves may also be blown *down*, but there is a restriction (self-intersection number must be −1).

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Castelnuovo's Theorem"}

### Castelnuovo's Theorem {#Castelnuovo's_Theorem}

One of the fundamental theorems for the birational geometry of surfaces is [**Castelnuovo's theorem**](wikipedia://en/45626463). This states that any birational map between algebraic surfaces is given by a finite sequence of blowups and blowdowns.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The [**Nakai criterion**](wikipedia://en/1186896#Intersection_theorem) says that:

A Divisor *D* on a surface *S* is ample if and only if *D<sup>2</sup> > 0* and for all irreducible curve *C* on *S* *D•C > 0*.

Ample divisors have a nice property such as it is the pullback of some hyperplane bundle of projective space, whose properties are very well known. Let $\mathcal{D}(S)$ be the abelian group consisting of all the divisors on *S*. Then due to the [intersection theorem](wikipedia://en/384327)

$$\mathcal{D}(S)\times\mathcal{D}(S)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}:(X,Y)\mapsto X\cdot Y$$

is viewed as a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478). Let

$$\mathcal{D}_0(S):=\{D\in\mathcal{D}(S)|D\cdot X=0,\text{for all } X\in\mathcal{D}(S)\}$$

then $\mathcal{D}/\mathcal{D}_0(S):=Num(S)$ becomes to be a **numerical equivalent class group** of *S* and

$$Num(S)\times Num(S)\mapsto\mathbb{Z}=(\bar{D},\bar{E})\mapsto D\cdot E$$

also becomes to be a quadratic form on $Num(S)$, where $\bar{D}$ is the image of a divisor *D* on *S*. (In the below the image $\bar{D}$ is abbreviated with *D*.)

For an ample line bundle *H* on *S*, the definition

$$\{H\}^\perp:=\{D\in Num(S)|D\cdot H=0\}.$$

is used in the surface version of the **Hodge index theorem**:

for $D\in\{\{H\}^\perp|D\ne0\}, D\cdot D < 0$, i.e. the restriction of the intersection form to $\{H\}^\perp$ is a negative definite quadratic form.

This theorem is proven using the Nakai criterion and the Riemann-Roch theorem for surfaces. The Hodge index theorem is used in Deligne's proof of the [Weil conjecture](wikipedia://en/244705).

Basic results on algebraic surfaces include the [Hodge index theorem](wikipedia://en/1242892), and the division into five groups of birational equivalence classes called the [classification of algebraic surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_algebraic_surfaces). The *general type* class, of [Kodaira dimension](wikipedia://en/1866873) 2, is very large (degree 5 or larger for a non-singular surface in **P**<sup>3</sup> lies in it, for example).

There are essential three [Hodge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_number) invariants of a surface. Of those, *h*<sup>1,0</sup> was classically called the **irregularity** and denoted by *q*; and *h*<sup>2,0</sup> was called the **geometric genus** *p*<sub>*g*</sub>. The third, *h*<sup>1,1</sup>, is not a [birational invariant](wikipedia://en/2605247), because [blowing up](wikipedia://en/1817790) can add whole curves, with classes in *H*<sup>1,1</sup>. It is known that [Hodge cycles](wikipedia://en/898010) are algebraic and that [algebraic equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_equivalence) coincides with [homological equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homological_equivalence), so that *h*<sup>1,1</sup> is an upper bound for ρ, the rank of the [Néron-Severi group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Néron-Severi_group). The [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) *p*<sub>*a*</sub> is the difference

geometric genus − irregularity.

This explains why the irregularity got its name, as a kind of 'error term'.

## Riemann-Roch theorem for surfaces {#Riemann-Roch_theorem_for_surfaces}

The [Riemann-Roch theorem for surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-Roch_theorem_for_surfaces) was first formulated by [Max Noether](wikipedia://en/2482337). The families of curves on surfaces can be classified, in a sense, and give rise to much of their interesting geometry.
