# Conifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), a **conifold** is a generalization of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). Unlike manifolds, conifolds can contain [conical singularities](wikipedia://en/54244#Conical), i.e. points whose neighbourhoods look like [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) over a certain base. In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), in particular in [flux compactifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactification_%28physics%29#Flux_compactification) of [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), the base is usually a five-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) real manifold, since the typically considered conifolds are complex 3-dimensional (real 6-dimensional) spaces.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Conifolds are important objects in [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305): [Brian Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Greene) explains the [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) of conifolds in Chapter 13 of his book *[The Elegant Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elegant_Universe)*—including the fact that the space can tear near the cone, and its [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) can change. This possibility was first noticed by (Candelas Dale Lutken Schimmrigk 1988) and employed by (Green Hübsch 1988) to prove that conifolds provide a connection between all (then) known Calabi–Yau compactifications in string theory; this partially supports a conjecture by (Reid 1987) whereby conifolds connect all possible Calabi–Yau complex 3-dimensional spaces.

A well-known example of a conifold is obtained as a deformation limit of a quintic - i.e. a [quintic hypersurface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintic_hypersurface) in the [projective space](wikipedia://en/737155) $\mathbb{CP}^4$. The space $\mathbb{CP}^4$ has complex dimension equal to four, and therefore the space defined by the quintic (degree five) equations:

$$z_1^5+z_2^5+z_3^5+z_4^5+z_5^5-5\psi z_1z_2z_3z_4z_5 = 0$$

in terms of homogeneous coordinates $z_i$ on $\mathbb{CP}^4$, for any fixed complex $\psi$, has complex dimension three. This family of [quintic hypersurfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintic_hypersurface) is the most famous example of [Calabi–Yau manifolds](wikipedia://en/188932). If the [complex structure](wikipedia://en/509742) [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065) $\psi$ is chosen to become equal to one, the manifold described above becomes singular since the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of the quintic [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in the equation vanish when all coordinates $z_i$ are equal or their [ratios](wikipedia://en/87837) are certain fifth roots of unity. The neighbourhood of this singular point looks like a [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) whose base is [topologically](wikipedia://en/29954) just $S^2 \times S^3$.

In the context of [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), the geometrically singular conifolds can be shown to lead to completely smooth physics of strings. The divergences are "smeared out" by [D3-branes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-branes) wrapped on the shrinking three-sphere in [Type IIB string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_IIB_string_theory) and by [D2-branes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D-branes) wrapped on the shrinking two-sphere in [Type IIA string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_IIA_string_theory), as originally pointed out by (Strominger 1995). As shown by (Greene Morrison Strominger 1995), this provides the string-theoretic description of the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954)-change via the conifold transition originally described by (Candelas Green Hübsch 1990), who also invented the term "conifold" and the diagram

*Image: 3FoldConifoldTransition.pdf.*

for the purpose. The two topologically distinct ways of smoothing a conifold are thus shown to involve replacing the singular vertex (node) by either a 3-sphere (by way of deforming the complex structure) or a 2-sphere (by way of a "small resolution"). It is believed that nearly all [Calabi–Yau manifolds](wikipedia://en/188932) can be connected via these "critical transitions", resonating with Reid's conjecture.
