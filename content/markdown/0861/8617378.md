# Normality test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Normality test"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **normality tests** are used to determine if a [data set](wikipedia://en/8495) is well-modeled by a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) and to compute how likely it is for a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) underlying the data set to be normally distributed.

:::

More precisely, the tests are a form of [model selection](wikipedia://en/3664073), and can be interpreted several ways, depending on one's [interpretations of probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_probability):

- In [descriptive statistics](wikipedia://en/8187) terms, one measures a [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821) of a normal model to the data – if the fit is poor then the data are not well modeled in that respect by a normal distribution, without making a judgment on any underlying variable.
- In [frequentist statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequentist_statistics) [statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing), data are tested against the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) that it is normally distributed.
- In [Bayesian statistics](wikipedia://en/404412), one does not "test normality" per se, but rather computes the likelihood that the data come from a normal distribution with given parameters *μ*,*σ* (for all *μ*,*σ*), and compares that with the likelihood that the data come from other distributions under consideration, most simply using a [Bayes factor](wikipedia://en/824552) (giving the relative likelihood of seeing the data given different models), or more finely taking a [prior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_distribution) on possible models and parameters and computing a [posterior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_distribution) given the computed likelihoods.

A normality test is used to determine whether sample data has been drawn from a normally distributed population (within some tolerance). A number of statistical tests, such as the [Student's *t*-test](wikipedia://en/536080) and the [one-way](wikipedia://en/14344439) and [two-way analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/33580814) (ANOVA), require a normally distributed sample population.

## Graphical methods {#Graphical_methods}

An informal approach to testing normality is to compare a [histogram](wikipedia://en/13266) of the sample data to a normal probability curve. The empirical distribution of the data (the histogram) should be bell-shaped and resemble the normal distribution.[^o3] This might be difficult to see if the sample is small. In this case one might proceed by regressing the data against the [quantiles](wikipedia://en/25184) of a normal distribution with the same mean and variance as the sample. Lack of fit to the regression line suggests a departure from normality (see Anderson Darling coefficient and minitab).

Another graphical tool for assessing normality is the [normal probability plot](wikipedia://en/543119), a [quantile-quantile plot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantile-quantile_plot) (QQ plot) of the standardized data against the [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution). Here the [correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson_product-moment_correlation_coefficient) between the sample data and normal quantiles (a measure of the goodness of fit) measures how well the data are modeled by a normal distribution. For normal data the points plotted in the QQ plot should fall approximately on a straight line, indicating high positive correlation.[^o4] These plots are easy to interpret and also have the benefit that outliers are easily identified.

## Back-of-the-envelope test {#Back-of-the-envelope_test}

Simple [back-of-the-envelope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back-of-the-envelope) test takes the [sample maximum and minimum](wikipedia://en/21602806) and computes their [z-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-score), or more properly [t-statistic](wikipedia://en/22474343) (number of sample standard deviations that a sample is above or below the sample mean), and compares it to the [68–95–99.7 rule](wikipedia://en/9606881): if one has a 3*σ* event (properly, a 3*s* event) and substantially fewer than 300 samples, or a 4*s* event and substantially fewer than 15,000 samples, then a normal distribution will understate the maximum magnitude of deviations in the sample data.

This test is useful in cases where one faces [kurtosis risk](wikipedia://en/7069430) – where large deviations matter – and has the benefit that it is very easy to compute and to communicate: non-statisticians can easily grasp that 6*σ* events are very rare in normal distributions.

## Frequentist tests {#Frequentist_tests}

Tests of univariate normality include the following:

- [D'Agostino's K-squared test](wikipedia://en/11686201),
- [Jarque–Bera test](wikipedia://en/30865916),
- [Anderson–Darling test](wikipedia://en/3479881),
- [Cramér–von Mises criterion](wikipedia://en/5509769),
- [Kolmogorov–Smirnov test](wikipedia://en/16714): this test only works if the mean and the variance of the normal distribution are assumed known under the null hypothesis,
- [Lilliefors test](wikipedia://en/2690426): based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, adjusted for when also estimating the mean and variance from the data,
- [Shapiro–Wilk test](wikipedia://en/2690983), and
- [Pearson's chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226676).

A 2011 study concludes that Shapiro–Wilk has the best [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_a_test) for a given significance, followed closely by Anderson–Darling when comparing the Shapiro–Wilk, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Lilliefors, and Anderson–Darling tests.[^o7]

Some published works recommend the Jarque–Bera test,[^o8][^o9] but the test has weakness. In particular, the test has low power for distributions with short tails, especially for bimodal distributions.[^o10] Some authors have declined to include its results in their studies because of its poor overall performance.[^o11]

Historically, the third and fourth [standardized moments](wikipedia://en/202713) ([skewness](wikipedia://en/28212) and [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848)) were some of the earliest tests for normality. The [Lin–Mudholkar test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin–Mudholkar_test) specifically targets asymmetric alternatives.[^o12] The [Jarque–Bera test](wikipedia://en/30865916) is itself derived from [skewness](wikipedia://en/28212) and [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848) estimates. [Mardia's multivariate skewness and kurtosis tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mardia's_test) generalize the moment tests to the multivariate case.[^o13] Other early [test statistics](wikipedia://en/1416975) include the ratio of the [mean absolute deviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_absolute_deviation) to the standard deviation and of the range to the standard deviation.[^o14]

More recent tests of normality include the energy test[^o15] (Székely and Rizzo) and the tests based on the [empirical characteristic function](wikipedia://en/50621216) (ECF) (e.g. Epps and Pulley,[^o16] Henze–Zirkler,[^o17] [BHEP test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BHEP_test)[^o18]). The energy and the ECF tests are powerful tests that apply for testing univariate or [multivariate normality](wikipedia://en/50347) and are statistically consistent against general alternatives.

The normal distribution has the [highest entropy](wikipedia://en/3504168) of any distribution for a given standard deviation. There are a number of normality tests based on this property, the first attributable to Vasicek.[^o19]

## Bayesian tests {#Bayesian_tests}

[Kullback–Leibler divergences](wikipedia://en/1115052) between the whole posterior distributions of the slope and variance do not indicate non-normality. However, the ratio of expectations of these posteriors and the expectation of the ratios give similar results to the Shapiro–Wilk statistic except for very small samples, when non-informative priors are used.[^o21]

Spiegelhalter suggests using a [Bayes factor](wikipedia://en/824552) to compare normality with a different class of distributional alternatives.[^o22] This approach has been extended by Farrell and Rogers-Stewart.[^o23]

## Applications {#Applications}

One application of normality tests is to the [residuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics) from a [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) model.[^o26] If they are not normally distributed, the residuals should not be used in Z tests or in any other tests derived from the normal distribution, such as [t tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T_test), [F tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F_test) and [chi-squared tests](wikipedia://en/226680). If the residuals are not normally distributed, then the dependent variable or at least one [explanatory variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explanatory_variable) may have the wrong functional form, or important variables may be missing, etc. Correcting one or more of these [systematic errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_error) may produce residuals that are normally distributed; in other words, non-normality of residuals is often a model deficiency rather than a data problem.[^o27]
