# Vaught conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Vaught conjecture"}

The **Vaught conjecture** is a [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) in the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) originally proposed by [Robert Lawson Vaught](wikipedia://en/3550212) in 1961. It states that the number of [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_%28logic%29) of a [first-order](wikipedia://en/10983) [complete theory](wikipedia://en/10086335) in a countable language is finite or ℵ<sub>0</sub> or 2${}^{\aleph_{0}}$. [Morley](wikipedia://en/2921675) showed that the number of countable models is finite or ℵ<sub>0</sub> or ℵ<sub>1</sub> or 2${}^{\aleph_{0}}$, which solves the conjecture except for the case of ℵ<sub>1</sub> models when the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) fails. For this remaining case, (txt) has announced a counterexample to the Vaught conjecture and the [topological Vaught conjecture](wikipedia://en/13797188#Topological_Vaught_conjecture). As of 2021, the counterexample has not been verified.

:::

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Statement of the conjecture"}

## Statement of the conjecture {#Statement_of_the_conjecture}

Let $T$ be a first-order, countable, complete theory with infinite models. Let $I(T, \alpha)$ denote the number of models of *T* of cardinality $\alpha$ up to isomorphism—the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/3820897) of the theory $T$. Morley [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) that if *I*(*T*, ℵ<sub>0</sub>) is infinite then it must be ℵ<sub>0</sub> or ℵ<sub>1</sub> or the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901). The Vaught conjecture is the statement that it is not possible for $\aleph_{0} < I(T,\aleph_{0}) < 2^{\aleph_{0}}$. The conjecture is a trivial consequence of the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705); so this axiom is often excluded in work on the conjecture. Alternatively, there is a sharper form of the conjecture that states that any countable complete *T* with uncountably many countable models will have a perfect set of uncountable models (as pointed out by [John Steel](wikipedia://en/2290967), in "On Vaught's conjecture". [Cabal Seminar](wikipedia://en/2275193) 76–77 (Proc. Caltech-UCLA Logic Sem., 1976–77), pp. 193–208, Lecture Notes in Math., 689, Springer, Berlin, 1978, this form of the Vaught conjecture is equiprovable with the original).

### Original formulation {#Original_formulation}

The original formulation by Vaught was not stated as a conjecture, but as a problem: *Can it be proved, without the use of the continuum hypothesis, that there exists a complete theory having exactly* ℵ<sub>1</sub> *non-isomorphic denumerable models?* By the result by Morley mentioned at the beginning, a positive solution to the conjecture essentially corresponds to a negative answer to Vaught's problem as originally stated.

:::

## Vaught's theorem {#Vaught's_theorem}

Vaught proved that the number of countable models of a complete theory cannot be 2. It can be any finite number other than 2, for example:

- Any complete theory with a finite model has no countably infinite models.
- The theories with just one countable model are the [ω-categorical theories](wikipedia://en/26628083). There are many examples of these, such as the theory of an infinite set, or the theory of a [dense](wikipedia://en/6320997) unbounded [total order](wikipedia://en/30330).
- [Ehrenfeucht](wikipedia://en/13405819) gave the following example of a theory with 3 countable models: the language has a relation ≥ and a countable number of constants *c*<sub>0</sub>, *c*<sub>1</sub>, ... with axioms stating that ≥ is a dense unbounded total order, and *c*<sub>0</sub> \< *c*<sub>1</sub> \< *c*<sub>2</sub> \< ... The three models differ according to whether this [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) is unbounded, or [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_sequence), or is bounded but does not converge.
- Ehrenfeucht's example can be modified to give a theory with any finite number *n* ≥ 3 of models by adding *n* − 2 unary relations *P*<sub>*i*</sub> to the language, with axioms stating that for every *x* exactly one of the *P*<sub>*i*</sub> is true, the values of *y* for which *P*<sub>*i*</sub>(*y*) is true are dense, and *P*<sub>1</sub> is true for all *c*<sub>*i*</sub>. Then the models for which the sequence of elements *c*<sub>*i*</sub> converge to a limit *c* split into *n* − 2 cases depending on for which *i* the relation *P*<sub>*i*</sub>(*c*) is true.

The idea of the proof of Vaught's theorem is as follows. If there are at most countably many countable models, then there is a smallest one: the [atomic model](wikipedia://en/3005202), and a largest one, the [saturated model](wikipedia://en/535836), which are different if there is more than one model. If they are different, the saturated model must realize some [*n*-type](wikipedia://en/3741564) omitted by the atomic model. Then one can show that an atomic model of the theory of structures realizing this *n*-type (in a language expanded by finitely many constants) is a third model, not isomorphic to either the atomic or the saturated model. In the example above with 3 models, the atomic model is the one where the sequence is unbounded, the saturated model is the one where the sequence converges, and an example of a type not realized by the atomic model is an element greater than all elements of the sequence.

:::

## Topological Vaught conjecture {#Topological_Vaught_conjecture}

The topological Vaught conjecture is the statement that whenever a [Polish group](wikipedia://en/396622#Polish_groups) [acts](wikipedia://en/12781) continuously on a [Polish space](wikipedia://en/396622), there are either countably many [orbits](wikipedia://en/12781#Orbits_and_stabilizers) or continuum many orbits. The topological Vaught conjecture is more general than the original Vaught conjecture: Given a countable language we can form the space of all structures on the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) for that language. If we equip this with the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) generated by first-order formulas, then it is known from [A. Gregorczyk](wikipedia://en/24824350), [A. Mostowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Mostowski), [C. Ryll-Nardzewski](wikipedia://en/1926283), "Definability of sets of models of axiomatic theories" (*Bulletin of the Polish Academy of Sciences (series Mathematics, Astronomy, Physics)*, vol. 9 (1961), pp. 163–7) that the resulting space is Polish. There is a continuous action of the infinite [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) (the collection of all permutations of the natural numbers with the topology of point-wise convergence) that gives rise to the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) of isomorphism. Given a complete first-order theory *T*, the set of structures satisfying *T* is a minimal, [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) invariant set, and hence Polish in its own right.

:::
