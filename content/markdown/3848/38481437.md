# Petersen's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Petersen's theorem"}

*Image: Petersen-graph-factors.svg.* A [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) (red edges), in the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926). Since the Petersen graph is [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508) and [bridgeless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgeless_graph), it meets the conditions of Petersen's theorem.

*Image: Sylvester counter.svg.* A cubic (but not bridgeless) graph with no perfect matching, showing that the bridgeless condition in Petersen's theorem cannot be omitted

In the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) discipline of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), **Petersen's theorem**, named after [Julius Petersen](wikipedia://en/76861), is one of the earliest results in graph theory and can be stated as follows:

:::

> **Petersen's Theorem.** Every [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508), [bridgeless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgeless_graph) graph contains a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031).[^o2]

In other words, if a graph has exactly three edges at each vertex, and every edge belongs to a cycle, then it has a set of edges that touches every vertex exactly once.

::: {#o4 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

We show that for every cubic, bridgeless graph $G = (V, E)$ we have that for every set $U \subseteq V$ the number of connected components in the graph [induced](wikipedia://en/1655188) by $V - U$ with an odd number of vertices is at most the cardinality of $U$. Then by [Tutte's theorem on perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/1655060) $G$ contains a perfect matching.

Let $G_{i}$ be a component with an odd number of vertices in the graph induced by the vertex set $V - U$. Let $V_{i}$ denote the vertices of $G_{i}$ and let $m_{i}$ denote the number of edges of $G$ with one vertex in $V_{i}$ and one vertex in $U$. By a simple double counting argument we have that

$$\sum\nolimits_{v\in V_i} \deg_G(v) = 2|E_i| + m_i ,$$

where $E_{i}$ is the set of edges of $G_{i}$ with both vertices in $V_{i}$. Since

$$\sum\nolimits_{v \in V_i} \deg_G(v)= 3 |V_i|$$

is an odd number and $2|E_{i}|$ is an even number it follows that $m_{i}$ has to be an odd number. Moreover, since $G$ is bridgeless we have that $m_{i} \ge 3$.

Let $m$ be the number of edges in $G$ with one vertex in $U$ and one vertex in the graph induced by $V - U$. Every component with an odd number of vertices contributes at least 3 edges to $m$, and these are unique, therefore, the number of such components is at most $m/3$. In the worst case, $U$ is an independent set, and therefore $m \le 3|U|$. We get

$$|U|\geq\frac{1}{3}m\geq |\{\text{components with an odd number of vertices}\}| ,$$

which shows that the condition of [Tutte's theorem on perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/1655060) holds.

:::

## History {#History}

The theorem is due to [Julius Petersen](wikipedia://en/76861), a Danish mathematician. It can be considered as one of the first results in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). The theorem appears first in the 1891 article "*Die Theorie der regulären graphs*".[^o2] By today's standards Petersen's proof of the theorem is complicated. A series of simplifications of the proof culminated in the proofs by (Frink 1926) and (König 1936).

In modern textbooks Petersen's theorem is covered as an application of [Tutte's theorem on perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/1655060).

::: {#o9 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

- In a cubic graph with a perfect matching, the edges that are not in the perfect matching form a [2-factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-factor). By [orienting](wikipedia://en/36619818) the 2-factor, the edges of the perfect matching can be extended to [paths](wikipedia://en/638889) of length three, say by taking the outward-oriented edges. This shows that every cubic, bridgeless graph decomposes into edge-disjoint paths of length three.[^o10]
- Petersen's theorem can also be applied to show that every [maximal planar graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_planar_graph) can be decomposed into a set of edge-disjoint paths of length three. In this case, the [dual graph](wikipedia://en/2536864) is cubic and bridgeless, so by Petersen's theorem it has a matching, which corresponds in the original graph to a pairing of adjacent triangle faces. Each pair of triangles gives a path of length three that includes the edge connecting the triangles together with two of the four remaining triangle edges.[^o11]
- By applying Petersen's theorem to the dual graph of a [triangle mesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_mesh) and connecting pairs of triangles that are not matched, one can decompose the mesh into cyclic [strips of triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_strip). With some further transformations it can be turned into a single strip, and hence gives a method for transforming a triangle mesh such that its dual graph becomes [hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/244437).[^n1]

:::

## Extensions {#Extensions}

### Each edge belongs to some perfect matching in cubic bridgeless graphs {#Each_edge_belongs_to_some_perfect_matching_in_cubic_bridgeless_graphs}

Schönberger strengthened Petersen's theorem in 1934 by showing that each edge of any cubic bridgeless graph belongs to some perfect matching.

### Number of perfect matchings in cubic bridgeless graphs {#Number_of_perfect_matchings_in_cubic_bridgeless_graphs}

It was conjectured by [Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938) and [Plummer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_D._Plummer) that the number of [perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/44031) contained in a [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508), [bridgeless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgeless_graph) graph is exponential in the number of the vertices of the graph $n$.[^n2] The conjecture was first proven for [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431), cubic, bridgeless graphs by (Voorhoeve 1979), later for [planar](wikipedia://en/24314), cubic, bridgeless graphs by (Chudnovsky Seymour 2012). The general case was settled by (Esperet Kardoš King Králʼ 2011), where it was shown that every cubic, bridgeless graph contains at least $2^{n/3656}$ perfect matchings.

### Algorithmic versions {#Algorithmic_versions}

(Biedl Bose Demaine Lubiw 2001) discuss efficient versions of Petersen's theorem. Based on Frink's proof[^o23] they obtain an $O(n \log^{4} n)$ algorithm for computing a perfect matching in a cubic, bridgeless graph with $n$ vertices. If the graph is furthermore [planar](wikipedia://en/24314) the same paper gives an $O(n)$ algorithm. Their $O(n \log^{4} n)$ time bound can be improved based on subsequent improvements to the time for maintaining the set of bridges in a dynamic graph.[^n3] Further improvements, reducing the time bound to $O(n \log^{2} n)$ or (with additional [randomized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) [data structures](wikipedia://en/8519)) $O(n \log n (\log \log n)^{3})$, were given by (Diks Stanczyk 2010).

### Higher degree {#Higher_degree}

If *G* is a regular graph of degree *d* whose [edge connectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-edge-connected_graph) is at least *d* − 1, and *G* has an even number of vertices, then it has a perfect matching. More strongly, every edge of *G* belongs to at least one perfect matching. The condition on the number of vertices can be omitted from this result when the degree is odd, because in that case (by the [handshaking lemma](wikipedia://en/9607933)) the number of vertices is always even.[^o27]
