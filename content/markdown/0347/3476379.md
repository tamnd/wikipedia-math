# Zero element

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zero element"}

Zero

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **zero element** is one of several generalizations of [the number zero](wikipedia://en/34513) to other [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364). These alternate meanings may or may not reduce to the same thing, depending on the context.

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Zero vector"}

## Additive identities {#Additive_identities}

An *[additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225)* is the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) in an [additive group](wikipedia://en/2974) or [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652). It corresponds to the element $0$ such that for all $x$ in the group, $0+x=x+0=x$. Some examples of additive identity include:

- The **zero vector** under [vector addition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_addition): the vector whose components are all $0$; in a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) its norm (length) is also $0$. Often denoted as $\mathbf{0}$ or $\vec{0}$.[^o4]
- The **zero function** or **zero map** defined by $z(x)=0$, under [pointwise addition](wikipedia://en/4786797) $(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)$
- The *[empty set](wikipedia://en/9566)* under [set union](wikipedia://en/47949)
- An *[empty sum](wikipedia://en/209748)* or *empty [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889)*
- An *[initial object](wikipedia://en/59405)* in a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) (an empty coproduct, and so an identity under [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889))

:::

## Absorbing elements {#Absorbing_elements}

An *[absorbing element](wikipedia://en/14206224)* in a multiplicative [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) or [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468) generalises the property $0\cdot x=0$. Examples include:

- The *[empty set](wikipedia://en/9566)*, which is an absorbing element under [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of sets, since $\varnothing\times S=\varnothing$
- The **zero function** or **zero map** defined by $z(x)=0$ under [pointwise multiplication](wikipedia://en/4786797) $(f\cdot g)(x)=f(x)\cdot g(x)$

Many absorbing elements are also additive identities, including the empty set and the zero function. Another important example is the distinguished element $0$ in a *[field](wikipedia://en/10603)* or *[ring](wikipedia://en/48404)*, which is both the additive identity and the multiplicative absorbing element, and whose [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126) is the smallest ideal.

## Zero objects {#Zero_objects}

A *[zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object)* in a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) is both an [initial and terminal object](wikipedia://en/59405) (and so an identity under both [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) and [products](wikipedia://en/237868)). For example, the trivial structure (containing only the identity) is a zero object in categories where morphisms must map identities to identities. Specific examples include:

- The *[trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059)*, containing only the identity (a zero object in the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092))
- The **zero module**, containing only the identity (a zero object in the category of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over a ring)

## Zero morphisms {#Zero_morphisms}

A *[zero morphism](wikipedia://en/320638)* in a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) is a generalised absorbing element under [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947): any morphism composed with a zero morphism gives a zero morphism. Specifically, if $0_{XY}:X\to Y$ is the zero morphism among morphisms from $X$ to $Y$, and $f:A\to X$ and $g:Y\to B$ are arbitrary morphisms, then $g\circ 0_{XY}=0_{XB}$ and $0_{XY}\circ f=0_{AY}$.

If a category has a zero object $0$, then there are canonical morphisms $X\to 0$ and $0\to Y$, and composing them gives a zero morphism $0_{XY}:X\to Y$. In the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), for example, zero morphisms are morphisms which always return group identities, thus generalising the function $z(x)=0$.

## Least elements {#Least_elements}

A *[least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element)* in a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) or [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) may sometimes be called a zero element, and written either as $0$ or $\bot$.

## Zero module {#Zero_module}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **zero module** is the [module](wikipedia://en/276410) consisting of only the additive [identity](wikipedia://en/14962) for the module's [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) function. In the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), this identity is [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29), which gives the name *zero module*. That the zero module is in fact a module is simple to show; it is closed under addition and [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) trivially.

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Zero ideal"}

## Zero ideal {#Zero_ideal}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **zero ideal** in a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ is the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) $0$ consisting of only the additive identity (or [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29) element). The fact that this is an ideal follows directly from the definition.

:::

## Zero matrix {#Zero_matrix}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a *[zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888)* is a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with all its entries being [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29). It is alternately denoted by the symbol $O$.[^o13] Some examples of zero matrices are

$$0_{1,1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\ 0_{2,2} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\ 0_{2,3} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} ,\$$

The set of *m* × *n* matrices with entries in a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *K* forms a module $K_{m,n}$. The zero matrix $0_{K_{m,n}}$ in $K_{m,n}$ is the matrix with all entries equal to $0_K$, where $0_K$ is the additive identity in *K*.

$$0_{K_{m,n}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0_K & 0_K & \cdots & 0_K \\ 0_K & 0_K & \cdots & 0_K \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 0_K & 0_K & \cdots & 0_K \end{bmatrix}$$

The zero matrix is the additive identity in $K_{m,n}$. That is, for all $A \in K_{m,n}$:

$$0_{K_{m,n}}+A = A + 0_{K_{m,n}} = A$$

There is exactly one zero matrix of any given size $m\times n$ (with entries from a given ring), so when the context is clear, one often refers to *the* zero matrix. In a [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541), the zero matrix serves the role of both an additive identity and an absorbing element. In general, the zero element of a ring is unique, and typically denoted as $0$ without any subscript to indicate the parent ring. Hence the examples above represent zero matrices over any ring.

The zero matrix also represents the [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) which sends all vectors to the zero vector.

## Zero tensor {#Zero_tensor}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **zero tensor** is a [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965), of any order, all of whose components are [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29). The zero tensor of order $1$ is sometimes known as the zero vector.

Taking a [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of any tensor with any zero tensor results in another zero tensor. Among tensors of a given type, the zero tensor of that type serves as the additive identity among those tensors.

:::
