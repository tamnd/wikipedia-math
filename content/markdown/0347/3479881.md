# Anderson–Darling test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Anderson–Darling test"}

The **Anderson–Darling test** is a [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing) of whether a given sample of data is drawn from a given [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). In its basic form, the test assumes that there are no parameters to be estimated in the distribution being tested, in which case the test and its set of [critical values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_value_%28statistics%29) is distribution-free. However, the test is most often used in contexts wherein a family of distributions is being tested, in which case the parameters of that family need to be estimated and account must be taken of this in adjusting either the test-statistic or its critical values. When applied to testing whether a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) adequately describes a set of data, it is one of the most powerful statistical tools for detecting most departures from [normality](wikipedia://en/21462).[^o2][^o3] ***K*-sample Anderson–Darling tests** are available for testing whether several collections of observations can be modelled as coming from a single population, where the [distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) does not have to be specified.

:::

In addition to its use as a test of fit for distributions, it can be used in parameter estimation as the basis for a form of [minimum distance estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_distance_estimation) procedure.

The test is named after [Theodore Wilbur Anderson](wikipedia://en/22021377) (1918–2016) and [Donald A. Darling](wikipedia://en/38298648) (1915–2014), who invented it in 1952.[^o4]

## The single-sample test {#The_single-sample_test}

The Anderson–Darling and [Cramér–von Mises statistics](wikipedia://en/5509769) belong to the class of quadratic [EDF](wikipedia://en/2690455) statistics (tests based on the [empirical distribution function](wikipedia://en/2690455)).[^o3] If the hypothesized distribution is $F$, and empirical (sample) cumulative distribution function is $F_n$, then the quadratic EDF statistics measure the distance between $F$ and $F_n$ by

$$n \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} {\left(F_n(x) - F(x)\right)}^2 \, w(x) \, dF(x),$$

where $n$ is the number of elements in the sample, and $w(x)$ is a weighting function. When the weighting function is $w(x)=1$, the statistic is the [Cramér–von Mises statistic](wikipedia://en/5509769). The Anderson–Darling (1954) test[^o6] is based on the distance

$$A^2 = n \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{{\left(F_n(x) - F(x)\right)}^2}{F(x) \left(1 - F(x)\right)} \, dF(x),$$

which is obtained when the [weight function](wikipedia://en/245523) is $w(x) = {\left[F(x) \left(1 - F(x)\right)\right]}^{-1}$. Thus, compared with the [Cramér–von Mises distance](wikipedia://en/5509769), the Anderson–Darling distance places more weight on observations in the tails of the distribution.

### Basic test statistic {#Basic_test_statistic}

The Anderson–Darling test assesses whether a [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) comes from a specified distribution. It makes use of the fact that, when given a hypothesized underlying distribution and assuming the data does arise from this distribution, the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF) of the data can be transformed to what should follow a [uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29). The data can be then tested for uniformity with a distance test (Shapiro, 1980). The formula for the [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975) $A$ to assess if data $\{Y_1<\cdots <Y_n\}$ (note that the data must be put in order) comes from a CDF $F$ is

$$A^2 = -n-S \,,$$

where

$$S=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{2i-1}{n}\left[\ln( F(Y_i)) + \ln\left(1-F(Y_{n+1-i})\right)\right].$$

The test statistic can then be compared against the critical values of the theoretical distribution. In this case, no parameters are estimated in relation to the cumulative distribution function $F$.

## Tests for families of distributions {#Tests_for_families_of_distributions}

Essentially the same test statistic can be used in the test of fit of a family of distributions, but then it must be compared against the critical values appropriate to that family of theoretical distributions and dependent also on the method used for parameter estimation.

### Test for normality {#Test_for_normality}

Empirical testing has found[^o10] that the Anderson–Darling test is not quite as good as the [Shapiro–Wilk test](wikipedia://en/2690983), but is better than other tests. Stephens[^o2] found $A^2$ to be one of the best [empirical distribution function](wikipedia://en/2690455) statistics for detecting most departures from normality.

The computation differs based on what is known about the distribution:[^o11]

- Case 0: The mean $\mu$ and the variance $\sigma^2$ are both known.
- Case 1: The variance $\sigma^2$ is known, but the mean $\mu$ is unknown.
- Case 2: The mean $\mu$ is known, but the variance $\sigma^2$ is unknown.
- Case 3: Both the mean $\mu$ and the variance $\sigma^2$ are unknown.

The *n* observations, $X_i$, for $i=1,\ldots n$, of the variable $X$ must be sorted such that $X_1 \leq X_2 \leq \dots \leq X_n$ and the notation in the following assumes that *X<sub>i</sub>* represent the ordered observations. Let

$$\hat{\mu} = \begin{cases} \mu, & \text{if the mean is known.} \\ \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i = 1}^n X_i, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

$$\hat{\sigma}^2 = \begin{cases} \sigma^2, & \text{if the variance is known.} \\ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i = 1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2, & \text{if the variance is not known, but the mean is.} \\ \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i = 1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

The values $X_i$ are standardized to create new values $Y_i$, given by

$$Y_i = \frac{X_i-\hat{\mu}}{\hat{\sigma}}.$$

With the standard normal CDF $\Phi$, $A^2$ is calculated using

$$A^2 = -n -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(2i - 1\right) \left[\ln \Phi(Y_i) + \ln(1-\Phi(Y_{n+1-i}))\right].$$

An alternative expression in which only a single observation is dealt with at each step of the summation is:

$$A^2 = -n -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n\left[(2i-1)\ln\Phi(Y_i)+(2(n-i)+1)\ln(1-\Phi(Y_i))\right].$$

A modified statistic can be calculated using

$$A^{*2} = \begin{cases} A^2\left(1+\frac{0.75}{n}+\frac{2.25}{n^2}\right), & \text{if the variance and the mean are both unknown.} \\ A^2, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

[^o3]. If $A^{2}$ or $A^{*2}$ exceeds a given critical value, then the hypothesis of normality is rejected with some significance level. The critical values are given in the table below for values of $A^{*2}$.[^o2][^o3] [^o12]

::: {#o13 .table cue=table}

| Case | *n* | 15% | 10% | 5% | \2.5% | 1% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | ≥ 5 | \1.621 | \1.933 | \2.492 | \3.070 | \3.857 |
| 1 | ≥ 20 | \0.782 | \0.894 | \1.087 | \1.285 | \1.551 |
| 2 | ≥ 20 | \1.430 | \1.743 | \2.308 | \2.898 | \3.702 |
| 3 |  | \0.561 | \0.631 | \0.752 | \0.873 | \1.035 |

:::

Note 1: If $\hat{\sigma}$ = 0 or any $\Phi(Y_i)=$(0 or 1) then $A^2$ cannot be calculated and is undefined.

Note 2: The above formula for the modified statistic $A^{*2}$ is taken from D'Agostino (1986, p. 123). Care is required in comparisons across different sources as often the specific adjustment formula is not stated.

Note 3: Stephens[^o2] notes that the test becomes better when the parameters are computed from the data, even if they are known.

Note 4: Marsaglia & Marsaglia[^o12] provide a more accurate result for Case 0 at 85% and 99%.

### Tests for other distributions {#Tests_for_other_distributions}

Above, it was assumed that the variable $X_i$ was being tested for normal distribution. Any other family of distributions can be tested, but the test for each family is implemented by using a different modification of the basic test statistic, with reference to critical values specific to that family of distributions. The modifications of the statistic and tables of critical values are given by Stephens (1986)[^o3] for the exponential, extreme-value, Weibull, gamma, logistic, Cauchy, and von Mises distributions. Tests for the (two-parameter) [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476) can be implemented by transforming the data using a [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) and using the above test for normality. Details for the required modifications to the test statistic and for the critical values for the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) and the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) have been published by Pearson & Hartley (1972, Table 54). Details for these distributions, with the addition of the [Gumbel distribution](wikipedia://en/270827), are also given by Shorack & Wellner (1986, p239). Details for the [logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/542666) are given by Stephens (1979). A test for the (two parameter) [Weibull distribution](wikipedia://en/206948) can be obtained by making use of the fact that the logarithm of a Weibull variate has a [Gumbel distribution](wikipedia://en/270827).

## Non-parametric *k*-sample tests {#Non-parametric_k-sample_tests}

Fritz Scholz and Michael A. Stephens (1987) discuss a test, based on the Anderson–Darling measure of agreement between distributions, for whether a number of random samples with possibly different sample sizes may have arisen from the same distribution, where this distribution is unspecified.[^o16] The [R package](wikipedia://en/376707) kSamples and the [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) package [Scipy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciPy) implements this [rank test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_test) for comparing k samples among several other such rank tests.[^o17][^o18]

For $k$ samples the statistic can be computed as follows under the assumption that the distribution function $F_i$ of $i$-th sample is continuous

$$A^2_{kN} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{n_i} \sum_{j=1}^{N-1} \frac{(NM_{ij} - jn_i)^2}{j(N-j)}$$

where

- $n_i$ is the number of observations in the $i$-th sample
- $N$ is the total number of observations in all samples
- $Z_1 < \cdots < Z_N$ is the pooled ordered sample
- $M_{ij}$ is the number of observations in the $i$-th sample that are not greater than $Z_j$.[^o16]
