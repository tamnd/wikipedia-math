# Berkovich space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Berkovich space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Berkovich space**, introduced by (txt), is a version of an analytic space over a [non-Archimedean field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-archimedean_field) (e.g. [*p*-adic field](wikipedia://en/51423)), refining [Tate](wikipedia://en/245933)'s notion of a [rigid analytic space](wikipedia://en/5828941).

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

In the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) case, [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) begins by defining the complex affine space to be $\mathbb{C}^n.$ For each $U\subset\mathbb{C}^n,$ we define $\mathcal{O}_U,$ the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) on $U$ to be the ring of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110), i.e. functions on $U$ that can be written as a convergent [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) in a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) of each point.

We then define a local model space for $f_{1}, \ldots, f_{n}\in\mathcal{O}_U$ to be

$$X:=\{x\in U:f_{1}(x)= \cdots =f_{n}(x)=0\}$$

with $\mathcal{O}_X=\mathcal{O}_U/(f_{1}, \ldots,f_{n}).$ A **[complex analytic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analytic_space)** is a locally ringed $\mathbb{C}$-space $(Y, \mathcal{O}_Y)$ which is locally isomorphic to a local model space.

When $k$ is a [complete](wikipedia://en/21156575) non-Archimedean field, we have that $k$ is [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected). In such a case, if we continue with the same definition as in the complex case, we wouldn't get a good analytic theory. Berkovich gave a definition which gives nice analytic spaces over such $k$, and also gives back the usual definition over $\mathbb{C}.$

In addition to defining analytic functions over non-Archimedean fields, Berkovich spaces also have a nice underlying [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450).

## Berkovich spectrum {#Berkovich_spectrum}

A **[seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067)** on a ring $A$ is a non-constant function $|\!-\!|: A \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ such that

$$\begin{aligned} |0|&=0 \\ |1|&=1 \\ |f+g| &\leqslant |f|+|g| \\ |fg| &\leqslant |f||g| \end{aligned}$$

for all $f, g \in A$. It is called **multiplicative** if $|fg| =|f||g|$ and is called a **norm** if $|f| = 0$ implies $f = 0$.

If $A$ is a normed ring with norm $\|\!-\!\|$ then the **Berkovich spectrum** of $A$, denoted $\mathcal{M}(A)$, is the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of multiplicative seminorms on $A$ that are bounded by the norm of $A$.

The Berkovich spectrum is equipped with the weakest [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) such that for any $f \in A$ the map

$$\begin{cases} \varphi_{f}:\mathcal{M}(A)\to\mathbb{R} \\ |\cdot| \mapsto |f| \end{cases}$$

is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29).

The Berkovich spectrum of a normed ring $A$ is [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) if $A$ is [non-zero](wikipedia://en/1872874) and is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) if $A$ is complete.

If $x$ is a point of the spectrum of $A$ then the elements $f$ with $|f|_x = 0$ form a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) of $A$. The [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the quotient by this prime ideal is a normed field, whose completion is a complete field with a multiplicative norm; this field is denoted by $\mathcal{H}(x)$ and the image of an element $f\in A$ is denoted by $f(x)$. The field $\mathcal{H}(x)$ is generated by the image of $A$.

Conversely a bounded map from $A$ to a complete normed field with a multiplicative norm that is generated by the image of $A$ gives a point in the spectrum of $A$.

The spectral radius of $f,$

$$\rho(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty} \left \|f^n \right \|^{\frac{1}{n}}$$

is equal to

$$\sup_{x\in\mathcal{M}(A)}|f|_{x}.$$

### Examples {#Examples}

- The spectrum of a field complete with respect to a valuation is a single point corresponding to its valuation.
- If $A$ is a [commutative C\*-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_C*-algebra) then the Berkovich spectrum is the same as the [Gelfand spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelfand_spectrum). A point of the Gelfand spectrum is essentially a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) to $\mathbb{C}$, and its absolute value is the corresponding seminorm in the Berkovich spectrum.
- [Ostrowski's theorem](wikipedia://en/3360343) shows that any multiplicative seminorm on the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) (with the usual absolute value) is one of the following four types: $|-|_{\infty, \varepsilon} (0 < \varepsilon \leq 1), |-|_0, |-|_{p, \varepsilon} (0 < \varepsilon < 1)$ and $|-|_{p,0}$ ($p$ a prime). Here $|-|_{\infty, \varepsilon} = |-|_{\infty}^\varepsilon$, $|-|_{p, \varepsilon}$ is the $p$-adic norm for which $|p| = \varepsilon$, $|-|_{p, 0}$ is the seminorm induced by the trivial norm $|-|_{0}$ on $\mathbb{Z}/p$, and $|-|_{0}$ is the trivial norm on $\mathbb{Z}$, i.e., the norm which sends all nonzero elements to 1. For each $p$ (prime or infinity) we get a branch which is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to a real [interval](wikipedia://en/49172), the branches meet at the point corresponding to the trivial valuation. The open neighborhoods of the trivial valuations are such that they contain all but finitely many branches, and their intersection with each branch is open.

:::

## Berkovich affine space {#Berkovich_affine_space}

If $k$ is a field with a [valuation](wikipedia://en/347049), then the ***n*-dimensional Berkovich affine space** over $k$, denoted $\mathbb{A}^n_k$, is the set of multiplicative seminorms on $k[x_1, \ldots,x_n]$ extending the norm on $k$.

The Berkovich affine space is equipped with the weakest topology such that for any $f\in k$ the map $\varphi_{f}: \mathbb{A}^n \to\mathbb{R}$ taking $|\cdot|\in\mathbb{A}^n$ to $| f|$ is continuous. This is not a Berkovich spectrum, but is an increasing union of the Berkovich spectra of [rings of power series that converge in some ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_restricted_power_series) (so it is locally compact).

We define an analytic function on an open subset $U\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}$ as a map

$$f:U\to\prod_{x\in U}\mathcal{H}(x)$$

with $f(x)\in\mathcal{H}(x)$, which is a local limit of rational functions, i.e., such that every point $x\in U$ has an open neighborhood $U'\subset U$ with the following property:

$$\forall \varepsilon > 0\,\exists g, h \in\mathcal{k}[x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}]: \qquad \forall x' \in U' \left( h(x') \neq 0 \ \,\land\ \left|f(x')-\frac{g(x')}{h(x')}\right| < \varepsilon \right).$$

Continuing with the same definitions as in the complex case, one can define the ring of analytic functions, local model space, and analytic spaces over any field with a valuation (one can also define similar objects over normed rings). This gives reasonable objects for fields complete with respect to a nontrivial valuation and the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}.$

In the case where $k=\mathbb{C},$ this will give the same objects as described in the motivation section.

These analytic spaces are not all analytic spaces over non-Archimedean fields.

### Berkovich affine line {#Berkovich_affine_line}

The **1-dimensional Berkovich affine space** is called the **Berkovich affine line**. When $k$ is an [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018) non-Archimedean field, complete with respects to its valuation, one can describe all the points of the affine line.

There is a canonical [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) $k\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}^1_k$ .

The space $\mathbb{A}^{1}$ is a locally compact, [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), and uniquely [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) topological space which contains $k$ as a [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [subspace](wikipedia://en/910926).

One can also define the Berkovich projective line $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ by adjoining to $\mathbb{A}^{1}$, in a suitable manner, a point at infinity. The resulting space is a compact, Hausdorff, and uniquely path-connected topological space which contains $\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)$ as a dense subspace.
