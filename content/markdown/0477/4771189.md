# Hyperboloid model

*Image: HyperboloidProjection.png.* Red circular arc is geodesic in [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836); it projects to the brown geodesic on the green hyperboloid.

*Image: (7,3)-hyperboloid-tiling.webm.* Animation of partial {7,3} hyperbolic tiling of the hyperboloid rotated into the Poincaré perspective.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperboloid model"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **hyperboloid model**, also known as the **Minkowski model** after [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537), is a model of *n*-dimensional [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) in which points are represented by points on the forward sheet *S*<sup>\+</sup> of a two-sheeted [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844) in (*n*+1)-dimensional [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) or by the displacement [vectors](wikipedia://en/10962546) from the origin to those points, and *m*-planes are represented by the intersections of (*m*+1)-planes passing through the origin in Minkowski space with *S*<sup>\+</sup> or by [wedge products](wikipedia://en/221537) of *m* vectors. Hyperbolic space is [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) isometrically in Minkowski space; that is, the hyperbolic distance function is inherited from Minkowski space, analogous to the way spherical distance is inherited from [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) when the [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) is embedded in (*n*+1)-dimensional Euclidean space.

:::

Other models of hyperbolic space can be thought of as [map projections](wikipedia://en/51784) of *S*<sup>\+</sup>: the [Beltrami–Klein model](wikipedia://en/4792791) is the [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) of *S*<sup>\+</sup> through the origin onto a plane perpendicular to a vector from the origin to specific point in *S*<sup>\+</sup> analogous to the [gnomonic projection](wikipedia://en/1331480) of the sphere; the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) is a projection of *S*<sup>\+</sup> through a point on the other sheet *S*<sup>−</sup> onto perpendicular plane, analogous to the [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) of the sphere; the [Gans model](wikipedia://en/241291#The_Gans_model) is the orthogonal projection of *S*<sup>\+</sup> onto a plane perpendicular to a specific point in *S*<sup>\+</sup>, analogous to the [orthographic projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthographic_map_projection); the [band model](wikipedia://en/58129622) of the hyperbolic plane is a conformal “cylindrical” projection analogous to the [Mercator projection](wikipedia://en/20771) of the sphere; [Lobachevsky coordinates](wikipedia://en/49331727#Lobachevsky_coordinates) are a cylindrical projection analogous to the [equirectangular projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equirectangular_projection) (longitude, latitude) of the sphere.

## Minkowski quadratic form {#Minkowski_quadratic_form}

If (*x*<sub>0</sub>, *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>) is a vector in the (*n* + 1)-dimensional coordinate space **R**<sup>*n*+1</sup>, the **Minkowski [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478)** is defined to be

$$Q(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n) = -x_0^2 + x_1^2 + \ldots + x_n^2.$$

The vectors *v* ∈ **R**<sup>*n*+1</sup> such that *Q*(*v*) = −1 form an *n*-dimensional [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844) *S* consisting of two [connected components](wikipedia://en/6233), or *sheets*: the forward, or future, sheet *S*<sup>\+</sup>, where *x*<sub>0</sub>>0 and the backward, or past, sheet *S*<sup>−</sup>, where *x*<sub>0</sub>\<0. The points of the *n*-dimensional hyperboloid model are the points on the forward sheet *S*<sup>\+</sup>.

The metric on the hyperboloid is

$$ds^2 = Q(dx_0, dx_1, \ldots, dx_n) = -dx_0^2 + dx_1^2 + \ldots + dx_n^2.$$

The **Minkowski [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477)** *B* is the [polarization](wikipedia://en/2047965) of the Minkowski quadratic form *Q*,

$$B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = (Q(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) - Q(\mathbf{u}) - Q(\mathbf{v})) / 2 .$$

(This is sometimes also written using scalar product notation $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}.$) Explicitly,

$$B((x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n), (y_0, y_1, \ldots, y_n)) = -x_0y_0 + x_1 y_1 + \ldots + x_n y_n .$$

The **hyperbolic distance** between two points *u* and *v* of *S*<sup>\+</sup> is given by the formula

$$d(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \operatorname{arcosh}(-B(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})) ,$$

where $\operatorname{arcosh}$ is the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) of [hyperbolic cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_cosine).

### Choice of metric signature {#Choice_of_metric_signature}

The bilinear form $B$ also functions as the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) over the space. In *n*+1 dimensional Minkowski space, there are two choices for the metric with opposite [signature](wikipedia://en/463225), in the 3-dimensional case either (+, −, −) or (−, +, +).

If the signature (−, +, +) is chosen, then the [scalar square](wikipedia://en/10962546) of chords between distinct points on the same sheet of the hyperboloid will be positive, which more closely aligns with conventional definitions and expectations in mathematics. Then *n*-dimensional hyperbolic space is a [Riemannian space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_space) and distance or length can be defined as the square root of the scalar square. If the signature (+, −, −) is chosen, scalar square between distinct points on the hyperboloid will be negative, so various definitions of basic terms must be adjusted, which can be inconvenient. Nonetheless, the signature (+, −, −, −) is also common for describing [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) in physics. (Cf. [Sign convention#Metric signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_convention#Metric_signature).)

## Straight lines {#Straight_lines}

A straight line in hyperbolic *n*-space is modeled by a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) on the hyperboloid. A geodesic on the hyperboloid is the (non-empty) intersection of the hyperboloid with a two-dimensional [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) (a plane through the origin) of the *n*+1-dimensional Minkowski space. If we take **u** and **v** to be basis vectors of that linear subspace with

$$B (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u}) = 1$$

$$B (\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v}) = -1$$

$$B (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = B (\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u}) = 0$$

and use *w* as a real parameter for points on the geodesic, then

$$\mathbf{u} \sinh w + \mathbf{v} \cosh w$$

will be a point on the geodesic.[^o25]

More generally, a *k*-dimensional "flat" in the hyperbolic *n*-space will be modeled by the (non-empty) intersection of the hyperboloid with a *k*+1-dimensional linear subspace (including the origin) of the Minkowski space.

## Isometries {#Isometries}

The [indefinite orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/293609) O(1,*n*), also called the (*n*+1)-dimensional [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489), is the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) (*n*+1)×(*n*+1) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) which preserve the Minkowski bilinear form. In a different language, it is the group of linear [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of the [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space). In particular, this group preserves the hyperboloid *S*. Recall that indefinite orthogonal groups have four connected components, corresponding to reversing or preserving the orientation on each subspace (here 1-dimensional and *n*-dimensional), and form a [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928). The subgroup of O(1,*n*) that preserves the sign of the first coordinate is the **[orthochronous Lorentz group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthochronous_Lorentz_group)**, denoted O<sup>\+</sup>(1,*n*), and has two components, corresponding to preserving or reversing the orientation of the spatial subspace. Its subgroup SO<sup>\+</sup>(1,*n*) consisting of matrices with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) one is a connected Lie group of dimension *n*(*n*+1)/2 which acts on *S*<sup>\+</sup> by linear automorphisms and preserves the hyperbolic distance. This action is transitive and the stabilizer of the vector (1,0,...,0) consists of the matrices of the form

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\ 0 & & & \\[-4mu] \vdots & & A & \\ 0 & & & \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

Where $A$ belongs to the compact [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) SO(*n*) (generalizing the [rotation group SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29) for *n* = 3). It follows that the *n*-dimensional [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) can be exhibited as the [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325) and a [Riemannian symmetric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_symmetric_space) of rank 1,

$$\mathbb{H}^n=\mathrm{SO}^{+}(1,n)/\mathrm{SO}(n).$$

The group SO<sup>\+</sup>(1,*n*) is the full group of orientation-preserving isometries of the *n*-dimensional hyperbolic space.

In more concrete terms, SO<sup>\+</sup>(1,*n*) can be split into *n*(*n*−1)/2 rotations (formed with a regular Euclidean [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005) in the lower-right block) and *n* hyperbolic translations, which take the form

$$\begin{pmatrix} \cosh \alpha & \sinh \alpha & 0 & \cdots \\[2mu] \sinh \alpha & \cosh \alpha & 0 & \cdots \\[2mu] 0 & 0 & 1 & \\[-7mu] \vdots & \vdots & & \ddots \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\alpha$ is the distance translated (along the *x*-axis in this case), and the 2nd row/column can be exchanged with a different pair to change to a translation along a different axis. The general form of a translation in 3 dimensions along the vector $(w, x, y, z)$ is:

$$\begin{pmatrix} w & x & y & z \\[2mu] x & \ \dfrac{x^2}{w+1}+1 & \dfrac{yx}{w+1} & \dfrac{zx}{w+1} \\[2mu] y & \dfrac{xy}{w+1} & \,\dfrac{y^2}{w+1}+1 & \dfrac{zy}{w+1} \\[2mu] z & \dfrac{xz}{w+1} & \dfrac{yz}{w+1} & \dfrac{z^2}{w+1}+1 \end{pmatrix}_{\vphantom|},$$

where $\textstyle w = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2+1}$. This extends naturally to more dimensions, and is also the simplified version of a [Lorentz boost](wikipedia://en/18404#Proper_transformations) when you remove the relativity-specific terms.

### Examples of groups of isometries {#Examples_of_groups_of_isometries}

The group of all isometries of the hyperboloid model is O<sup>\+</sup>(1,*n*). Any group of isometries is a subgroup of it.

#### Reflections {#Reflections}

For two points $\mathbf p, \mathbf q \in \mathbb{H}^n, \mathbf p \neq \mathbf q$, there is a unique reflection exchanging them.

Let $\mathbf u = \frac {\mathbf p - \mathbf q}{\sqrt{Q(\mathbf p - \mathbf q)}}$. Note that $Q(\mathbf u) = 1$, and therefore $u \notin \mathbb{H}^n$.

Then

$$\mathbf x \mapsto \mathbf x - 2 B(\mathbf x, \mathbf u) \mathbf u$$

is a reflection that exchanges $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf q$. This is equivalent to the following matrix:

$$R = I - 2 \mathbf u \mathbf u^{\operatorname{T}} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & I \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

(note the use of [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464) notation).

Then $\{I, R\}$ is a group of isometries. All such subgroups are [conjugate](wikipedia://en/49176#Conjugacy_of_subgroups_and_general_subsets).

#### Rotations and reflections {#Rotations_and_reflections}

$$S = \left \{ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & A \\ \end{pmatrix} : A \in O(n) \right \}$$

is the group of rotations and reflections that preserve $(1, 0, \dots, 0)$. The function $A \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & A \\ \end{pmatrix}$ is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) from [O(*n*)](wikipedia://en/173954) to this group. For any point $p$, if $X$ is an isometry that maps $(1, 0, \dots, 0)$ to $p$, then $XSX^{-1}$ is the group of rotations and reflections that preserve $p$.

#### Translations {#Translations}

For any real number $t$, there is a translation

$$L_t = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh t & \sinh t & 0 \\ \sinh t & \cosh t & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & I \\ \end{pmatrix} = e^\begin{pmatrix} 0 & t & 0 \\ t & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

(The expression on the RHS is a [matrix exponential](wikipedia://en/672731).) This is a translation of distance $t$ in the positive x direction if $t \ge 0$ or of distance $-t$ in the negative x direction if $t \le 0$. Any translation of distance $t$ is conjugate to $L_t$ and $L_{-t}$. The set $\left \{L_t : t \in \mathbb R \right \}$ is the group of translations through the x-axis, and a group of isometries is conjugate to it [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is a group of isometries through a line.

For example, let's say we want to find the group of translations through a line $\overline{\mathbf p \mathbf q}$. Let $X$ be an isometry that maps $(1, 0, \dots, 0)$ to $p$ and let $Y$ be an isometry that fixes $p$ and maps $X L_{d(\mathbf p, \mathbf q)} [1, 0, \dots, 0]^{\operatorname{T}}$ to $q$. An example of such a $Y$ is a reflection exchanging $X L_{d(\mathbf p, \mathbf q)} [1, 0, \dots, 0]^{\operatorname{T}}$ and $q$ (assuming they are different), because they are both the same distance from $p$. Then $YX$ is an isometry mapping $(1, 0, \dots, 0)$ to $p$ and a point on the positive x-axis to $q$. $(YX)L_t(YX)^{-1}$ is a translation through the line $\overline{\mathbf p \mathbf q}$ of distance $|t|$. If $t \ge 0$, it is in the $\overrightarrow{\mathbf p \mathbf q}$ direction. If $t \le 0$, it is in the $\overrightarrow{\mathbf q \mathbf p}$ direction. $\left \{(YX)L_t(YX)^{-1} : t \in \mathbb R \right \}$ is the group of translations through $\overline{\mathbf p \mathbf q}$.

#### Symmetries of horospheres {#Symmetries_of_horospheres}

Let *H* be some [horosphere](wikipedia://en/13487128) such that points of the form $(w, x, 0, \dots, 0)$ are inside of it for arbitrarily large *x*. For any vector *b* in $\mathbb R^{n-1}$

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 + \tfrac12 \|\mathbf b\|^2 & - \tfrac12 \|\mathbf b\|^2 & \mathbf b^{\operatorname{T}} \\ \tfrac12 \|\mathbf b\|^2 & 1 - \tfrac12 \|\mathbf b\|^2 & \mathbf b^{\operatorname{T}} \\ \mathbf b & -\mathbf b & I \end{pmatrix} = e^\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & \mathbf b^{\operatorname{T}} \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbf b^{\operatorname{T}} \\ \mathbf b & -\mathbf b & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

is a hororotation that maps *H* to itself. The set of such hororotations is the group of hororotations preserving *H*. All hororotations are conjugate to each other.

For any $A$ in O(*n*−1)

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & A \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

is a rotation or reflection that preserves *H* and the x-axis. These hororotations, rotations, and reflections generate the group of symmetries of *H*. The symmetry group of any horosphere is conjugate to it. They are isomorphic to the [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164) E(*n*−1).

## History {#History}

In several papers between 1878–1885, [Wilhelm Killing](wikipedia://en/1531824)[^o12][^o13][^o14] used the representation he attributed to [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459) for [Lobachevskian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobachevskian_geometry). In particular, he discussed quadratic forms such as $k^{2}t^{2}+u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2}=k^{2}$ or in arbitrary dimensions $k^{2}x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}=k^{2}$, where $k$ is the reciprocal measure of curvature, $k^{2}=\infty$ denotes [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), $k^{2}>0$ [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020), and $k^{2}<0$ hyperbolic geometry.

According to Jeremy Gray (1986),[^o15] [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) used the hyperboloid model in his personal notes in 1880. Poincaré published his results in 1881, in which he discussed the invariance of the quadratic form $\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}-\zeta^{2}=-1$.[^o16] Gray shows where the hyperboloid model is implicit in later writing by Poincaré.[^o17]

Also [Homersham Cox](wikipedia://en/55620821) in 1882[^o18][^o19] used Weierstrass coordinates (without using this name) satisfying the relation $z^{2}-x^{2}-y^{2}=1$ as well as $w^{2}-x^{2}-y^{2}-z^{2}=1$.

Further exposure of the model was given by [Alfred Clebsch](wikipedia://en/1369957) and [Ferdinand Lindemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Lindemann) in 1891 discussing the relation $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-4k^{2}x_{3}^{2}=-4k^{2}$ and $x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}-4k^{2}x_{4}^{2}=-4k^{2}$.[^o20]

Weierstrass coordinates were also used by Gérard (1892),[^o21] [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989) (1899),[^o22] [Frederick S. Woods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_S._Woods) (1903)\],[^o23] [Heinrich Liebmann](wikipedia://en/55621425) (1905).[^o24]

The hyperboloid was explored as a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) by [Alexander Macfarlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Macfarlane) in his *Papers in Space Analysis* (1894). He noted that points on the hyperboloid could be written as

$$\cosh A + \alpha \sinh A,$$

where α is a [basis vector](wikipedia://en/18420) orthogonal to the hyperboloid axis. For example, he obtained the [hyperbolic law of cosines](wikipedia://en/20757063) through use of his [Algebra of Physics](wikipedia://en/1071730).[^o25]

H. Jansen made the hyperboloid model the explicit focus of his 1909 paper "Representation of hyperbolic geometry on a two sheeted hyperboloid".[^o26] In 1993 W.F. Reynolds recounted some of the early history of the model in his article in the [American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly).[^o27]

Being a commonplace model by the twentieth century, it was identified with the *Geschwindigkeitsvectoren* (velocity vectors) by [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) in his 1907 Göttingen lecture 'The Relativity Principle'. Scott Walter, in his 1999 paper "The Non-Euclidean Style of Minkowskian Relativity"[^o28] recalls Minkowski's awareness, but traces the lineage of the model to [Hermann Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Helmholtz) rather than Weierstrass and Killing.

In the early years of relativity the hyperboloid model was used by [Vladimir Varićak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Varićak) to explain the physics of velocity. In his speech to the German mathematical union in 1912 he referred to Weierstrass coordinates.[^o29]
