# Industrial-grade prime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Industrial-grade prime"}

**Industrial-grade primes** (the term is apparently due to [Henri Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Cohen_%28number_theorist%29)[^o2]) are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) for which [primality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality) has not been certified (i.e. rigorously proven), but they have undergone [probable prime](wikipedia://en/160560) tests such as the [Miller–Rabin primality test](wikipedia://en/188999), which has a positive, but negligible, failure rate, or the [Baillie–PSW primality test](wikipedia://en/3609890), which no composites are known to pass.

:::

Industrial-grade primes are sometimes used instead of certified primes in [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms) such as [RSA encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_encryption), which require the user to generate large [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_numbers). [Certifying the primality](wikipedia://en/183751) of large numbers (over 100 digits for instance) is significantly harder than showing they are industrial-grade primes. The latter can be done almost instantly with a [failure rate](wikipedia://en/1336960) so low that it is highly unlikely to ever fail in practice. In other words, the number is believed to be prime with very high, but not absolute, confidence.
