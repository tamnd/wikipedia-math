# Born rule

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Born rule"}

The **Born rule** is a postulate of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) that gives the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) that a [measurement of a quantum system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_in_quantum_mechanics) will yield a given result. In one commonly used application, it states that the [probability density](wikipedia://en/43487) for finding a particle at a given position is proportional to the square of the amplitude of the system's [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) at that position. It was formulated and published by German physicist [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born) in July 1926.[^o2]

:::

## Details {#Details}

The Born rule states that an [observable](wikipedia://en/294248), measured in a system with normalized [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) $|\psi\rangle$ (see [Bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542)), corresponds to a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408) $A$ whose [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) is discrete if:

- the measured result will be one of the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) $\lambda$ of $A$, and
- the probability of measuring a given eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ will equal $\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$, where $P_i$ is the projection onto the [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda_i$.

(In the case where the eigenspace of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda_i$ is one-dimensional and spanned by the normalized eigenvector $|\lambda_i\rangle$, $P_i$ is equal to $|\lambda_i\rangle\langle\lambda_i|$, so the probability $\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$ is equal to $\langle\psi|\lambda_i\rangle\langle\lambda_i|\psi\rangle$. Since the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $\langle\lambda_i|\psi\rangle$ is known as the *[probability amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude)* that the state vector $|\psi\rangle$ assigns to the eigenvector $|\lambda_i\rangle$, it is common to describe the Born rule as saying that probability is equal to the amplitude-squared (really the amplitude times its own [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918)). Equivalently, the probability can be written as $\big|\langle\lambda_i|\psi\rangle\big|^2$.)

In the case where the spectrum of $A$ is not wholly discrete, the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495#Possible_absence_of_eigenvectors) proves the existence of a certain [projection-valued measure (PVM)](wikipedia://en/1046155) $Q$, the spectral measure of $A$. In this case:

- the probability that the result of the measurement lies in a measurable set $M$ is given by $\langle\psi|Q(M)|\psi\rangle$.

For example, a single structureless particle can be described by a wave function $\psi$ that depends upon position coordinates $(x, y, z)$ and a time coordinate $t$. The Born rule implies that the probability density function $p$ for the result of a measurement of the particle's position at time $t_0$ is:

$$p(x, y, z, t_0) = |\psi(x, y, z, t_0)|^2.$$

The Born rule can also be employed to calculate probabilities (for measurements with discrete sets of outcomes) or probability densities (for continuous-valued measurements) for other observables, like momentum, energy, and angular momentum.

In some applications, this treatment of the Born rule is generalized using [positive-operator-valued measures (POVM)](wikipedia://en/3793084). A POVM is a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) whose values are [positive semi-definite operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definiteness_of_a_matrix) on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). POVMs are a generalization of von Neumann measurements and, correspondingly, quantum measurements described by POVMs are a generalization of quantum measurements described by self-adjoint observables. In rough analogy, a POVM is to a PVM what a [mixed state](wikipedia://en/30876419#Mixed_states) is to a [pure state](wikipedia://en/30876419#Pure_states). Mixed states are needed to specify the state of a subsystem of a larger system (see [purification of quantum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purification_of_quantum_state)); analogously, POVMs are necessary to describe the effect on a subsystem of a projective measurement performed on a larger system. POVMs are the most general kind of measurement in quantum mechanics and can also be used in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267).[^o4] They are extensively used in the field of [quantum information](wikipedia://en/25274).

In the simplest case of a POVM with a finite number of elements acting on a finite-dimensional [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), a POVM is a set of [positive semi-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definiteness_of_a_matrix) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) $\{F_i\}$ on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ that sum to the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718),:[^o5]

$$\sum_{i=1}^n F_i = I.$$

The POVM element $F_i$ is associated with the measurement outcome $i$, such that the probability of obtaining it when making a measurement on the quantum state $\rho$ is given by:

$$p(i) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho F_i),$$

where $\operatorname{tr}$ is the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) operator. This is the POVM version of the Born rule. When the quantum state being measured is a pure state $|\psi\rangle$ this formula reduces to:

$$p(i) = \operatorname{tr}\big(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| F_i\big) = \langle\psi|F_i|\psi\rangle.$$

The Born rule, together with the [unitarity](wikipedia://en/225999) of the time evolution operator $e^{-i\hat{H}t}$ (or, equivalently, the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/14381) $\hat{H}$ being [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682)), implies the [unitarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarity_%28physics%29) of the theory: a wave function that is time-evolved by a unitary operator will remain properly normalized. (In the more general case where one considers the time evolution of a [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844), proper normalization is ensured by requiring that the time evolution is a [trace-preserving, completely positive map](wikipedia://en/1013550).)

## History {#History}

The Born rule was formulated by Born in a 1926 paper.[^o7] In this paper, Born solves the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) for a scattering problem and, inspired by [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) and [Einstein's probabilistic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein's_thought_experiments#Background:_Einstein_and_the_quantum) for the [photoelectric effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectric_effect),[^o8] concludes, in a footnote, that the Born rule gives the only possible interpretation of the solution. (The main body of the article says that the amplitude "gives the probability" \[*bestimmt die Wahrscheinlichkeit*\], while the footnote added in proof says that the probability is proportional to the square of its magnitude.) In 1954, together with [Walther Bothe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Bothe), Born was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics) for this and other work.[^o8] [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) discussed the application of [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) to Born's rule in his 1932 book.[^o9]

### Derivation from more basic principles {#Derivation_from_more_basic_principles}

[Gleason's theorem](wikipedia://en/6796998) shows that the Born rule can be derived from the usual mathematical representation of measurements in quantum physics. [Andrew M. Gleason](wikipedia://en/4244871) first proved the theorem in 1957,[^o12] prompted by a question posed by [George W. Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey).[^o13][^o14] This theorem was historically significant for the role it played in showing that wide classes of [hidden-variable theories](wikipedia://en/211602) are inconsistent with quantum physics.[^o15]

Several other researchers have also tried to derive the Born rule from more basic principles. A number of derivations have been proposed in the context of the [many-worlds interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation). These include the decision-theory approach pioneered by [David Deutsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Deutsch)[^o16] and later developed by [Hilary Greaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilary_Greaves)[^o17] and David Wallace;[^o18] and an "envariance" approach by [Wojciech H. Zurek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wojciech_H._Zurek).[^o19] These proofs have, however, been criticized as circular.[^o20] In 2018, an approach based on self-locating uncertainty was suggested by Charles Sebens and [Sean M. Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_M._Carroll);[^o21] this has also been criticized.[^o22] In 2019, Lluís Masanes, Thomas Galley, and Markus Müller proposed a derivation based on postulates including the possibility of state estimation.[^o23][^o24] In 2021, [Simon Saunders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Saunders) produced a branch counting derivation of the Born rule. The crucial feature of this approach is to define the branches so that they all have the same magnitude or [2-norm](wikipedia://en/990534). The ratios of the numbers of branches thus defined give the probabilities of the various outcomes of a measurement, in accordance with the Born rule.[^o25]

It has also been claimed that [pilot-wave theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot-wave_theory) can be used to statistically derive the Born rule, though this remains controversial.[^o26]

Within the [QBist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Bayesianism) interpretation of quantum theory, the Born rule is seen as an extension of the normative principle of [coherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_%28philosophical_gambling_strategy%29), which ensures self-consistency of probability assessments across a whole set of such assessments. It can be shown that an agent gambling on the outcomes of measurements on a sufficiently quantum-like system, refusing to use the Born rule when placing bets, is vulnerable to a [Dutch book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_book).[^o27]
