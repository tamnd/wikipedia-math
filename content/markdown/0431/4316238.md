# Lebesgue's decomposition theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lebesgue's decomposition theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more precisely in [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), the **Lebesgue decomposition theorem**[^n1] provides a way to decompose a measure into two distinct parts based on their relationship with another measure.

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Formal Statement"}

## Formal Statement {#Formal_Statement}

The theorem states that if $(\Omega,\Sigma)$ is a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) and $\mu$ and $\nu$ are [σ-finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-finite_measure) [signed measures](wikipedia://en/2985811) on $\Sigma$, then there exist two uniquely determined σ-finite signed measures $\nu_0$ and $\nu_1$ such that:[^n2][^n3]

- $$\nu=\nu_0+\nu_1\,$$
- $\nu_0\ll\mu$ (that is, $\nu_0$ is [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_continuity_%28measure_theory%29) with respect to $\mu$)
- $\nu_1\perp\mu$ (that is, $\nu_1$ and $\mu$ are [singular](wikipedia://en/4304500)).

### Refinement {#Refinement}

Lebesgue's decomposition theorem can be refined in a number of ways. First, as the **Lebesgue–Radon–Nikodym theorem**. That is, let $(\Omega,\Sigma)$ be a measure space, $\mu$ a [σ-finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-finite_measure) [positive measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_measure) on $\Sigma$ and $\lambda$ a [complex measure](wikipedia://en/3057532) on $\Sigma$.[^n4]

- There is a unique pair of complex measures on $\Sigma$ such that $$\lambda = \lambda_a + \lambda_s, \quad \lambda_a \ll \mu, \quad \lambda_s \perp \mu.$$ If $\lambda$ is positive and finite, then so are $\lambda_a$ and $\lambda_s$.
- There is a unique $h \in L^1(\mu)$ such that $$\lambda_a (E) = \int_E h d\mu, \quad E \in \Sigma.$$

The first assertion follows from the Lebesgue decomposition, the second is known as the [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746). That is, the function $h$ is a Radon–Nikodym derivative that can be expressed as

$$h = \frac{d\lambda_a}{d\mu}.$$

An alternative refinement is that of the decomposition of a regular [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322)[^n5][^n6][^n7]

$$\nu = \nu_{ac} + \nu_{sc} + \nu_{pp},$$

where

- $\nu_{ac} \ll \mu$ is the **absolutely continuous** part
- $\nu_{sc} \perp \mu$ is the **singular continuous** part
- $\nu_{pp}$ is the **pure point** part (a [discrete measure](wikipedia://en/8912350)).

The absolutely continuous measures are classified by the [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746), and discrete measures are easily understood. Hence (singular continuous measures aside), Lebesgue decomposition gives a very explicit description of measures. The [Cantor measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor_measure) (the [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) whose [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) is the [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819)) is an example of a singular continuous measure.

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

### Lévy–Itō decomposition {#Lévy–Itō_decomposition}

The analogous decomposition for a [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) is the [Lévy–Itō decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lévy–Itō_decomposition): given a [Lévy process](wikipedia://en/1025748) *X,* it can be decomposed as a sum of three independent [Lévy processes](wikipedia://en/1025748) $X=X^{(1)}+X^{(2)}+X^{(3)}$ where:

- $X^{(1)}$ is a [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) with drift, corresponding to the absolutely continuous part;
- $X^{(2)}$ is a [compound Poisson process](wikipedia://en/1082294), corresponding to the pure point part;
- $X^{(3)}$ is a [square integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_integrable) pure jump [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536) that almost surely has a countable number of jumps on a finite interval, corresponding to the singular continuous part.
