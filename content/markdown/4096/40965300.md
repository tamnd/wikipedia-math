# Concatenation theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Concatenation theory"}

**Concatenation theory**, also called **string theory**, **character-string theory**, or **theoretical [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860)**, studies [character strings](wikipedia://en/27701) over finite alphabets of characters, signs, symbols, or marks. String theory is foundational for [formal linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_linguistics), computer science, logic, and metamathematics, especially proof theory.[^o2] A [generative grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar) can be seen as a recursive definition in string theory.

:::

The most basic operation on strings is [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474): connecting two strings to form a longer string whose length is the sum of the lengths of those two strings. ABCDE is the concatenation of AB with CDE, in symbols, ABCDE = AB ^ CDE. Strings and concatenation of strings can be treated as an algebraic system with some properties resembling those of the addition of integers. In modern mathematics, this system is called a [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166).

In 1956 [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731) wrote: "Like any branch of mathematics, theoretical syntax may, and ultimately must, be studied by the axiomatic method".[^o3] Church was evidently unaware that string theory already had two axiomatizations from the 1930s: one by [Hans Hermes](wikipedia://en/41139401) and one by [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920).[^o4] Coincidentally, the first English presentation of Tarski's 1933 axiomatic foundations of string theory appeared in 1956 – the same year that Church called for such axiomatizations.[^o5] As Tarski himself noted using other terminology, serious difficulties arise if strings are construed as tokens rather than types in the sense of [Peirce's type-token distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peirce's_type-token_distinction).
