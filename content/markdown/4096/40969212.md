# Moment measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Moment measure"}

In [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a **moment measure** is a [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) quantity, [function](wikipedia://en/185427) or, more precisely, [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) that is defined in relation to [mathematical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_objects) known as [point processes](wikipedia://en/2147685), which are types of [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) often used as [mathematical models](wikipedia://en/20590) of physical phenomena representable as [randomly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) positioned [points](wikipedia://en/593693) in [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time), [space](wikipedia://en/27667) or both. Moment measures generalize the idea of (raw) [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), hence they arise often in the study of point processes and related fields.[^o2]

:::

An example of a moment measure is the **first moment measure** of a point process, often called the **mean measure** or **intensity measure**, which gives the [expected](wikipedia://en/9653) or average number of points of the point process being located in some region of space.[^o3] In other words, if the number of points of a point process located in some region of space is a random variable, then the first moment measure corresponds to the first moment of this random variable.[^o4]

Moment measures feature prominently in the study of point processes[^o2][^o5][^o6] as well as the related fields of [stochastic geometry](wikipedia://en/22037813)[^o4] and [spatial statistics](wikipedia://en/9467349)[^o6][^o7] whose applications are found in numerous [scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) disciplines such as [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), and [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications).[^o4][^o5][^o8]

## Point process notation {#Point_process_notation}

Point processes are mathematical objects that are defined on some underlying [mathematical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_space). Since these processes are often used to represent collections of points randomly scattered in physical space, time or both, the underlying space is usually *d*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) denoted here by $\textstyle \textbf{R}^{ d}$, but they can be defined on more [abstract](wikipedia://en/454446) mathematical spaces.[^o2]

Point processes have a number of interpretations, which is reflected by the various types of [point process notation](wikipedia://en/40765261).[^o4][^o8] For example, if a point $\textstyle x$ belongs to or is a member of a point process, denoted by $\textstyle {N}$, then this can be written as:[^o4]

$$\textstyle x\in {N},$$

and represents the point process being interpreted as a random [set](wikipedia://en/26691). Alternatively, the number of points of $\textstyle {N}$ located in some [Borel set](wikipedia://en/43327) $\textstyle B$ is often written as:[^o3][^o4][^o7]

$$\textstyle {N}(B),$$

which reflects a [random measure](wikipedia://en/10040846) interpretation for point processes. These two notations are often used in parallel or interchangeably.[^o3][^o4][^o7]

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### *n*-th power of a point process {#n-th_power_of_a_point_process}

For some [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $\textstyle n=1,2,\dots$, the $\textstyle n$-th power of a point process $\textstyle {N}$ is defined as:[^o3]

$${N}^n(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)= \prod_{i=1}^n{N}(B_i)$$

where $\textstyle B_1,...,B_n$ is a collection of not necessarily disjoint Borel sets (in $\textstyle \textbf{R}^{ d}$), which form a $\textstyle n$-fold [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of sets denoted by $B_1\times\cdots\times B_n$. The symbol $\textstyle \Pi$ denotes standard [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845#Capital_Pi_notation).

The notation $\textstyle {N}(B_i)$ reflects the interpretation of the point process $\textstyle {N}$ as a random measure.[^o4]

The $\textstyle n$-th power of a point process $\textstyle {N}$ can be equivalently defined as:[^o4]

$${N}^{n}(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)= \sum_{(x_1,\dots, x_n)\in {N} } \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}_{B_i}(x_i)$$

where [summation](wikipedia://en/246160) is performed over all $\textstyle n$-[tuples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuples) of (possibly repeating) points, and $\textstyle \mathbf{1}$ denotes an [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) such that $\textstyle \mathbf{1}_{B_1}$ is a [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002). This definition can be contrasted with the definition of the [*n*-factorial power of a point process](wikipedia://en/40990955#n-th_factorial_power_of_a_point_process) for which each *n*-[tuples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuples) consists of *n* distinct points.

### *n*-th moment measure {#n-th_moment_measure}

The $\textstyle n$-th moment measure is defined as:

$$M^n(B_1\times\ldots\times B_n)=E [{N}^n(B_1\times\ldots\times B_n)],$$

where the *E* denotes the [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653) ([operator](wikipedia://en/22213#Probability_theory)) of the point process $\textstyle {N}$. In other words, the *n*-th moment measure is the expectation of the *n*-th power of some point process.

The $\textstyle n\,$th moment measure of a point process $\textstyle {N}$ is equivalently defined[^o4] as:

$$\int_{\textbf{R}^{n d}}f(x_1,\dots,x_n) M^n(dx_1,\dots,dx_n)=E \left[ \sum_{(x_1,\dots,x_n)\in {N} } f(x_1,\dots,x_n) \right],$$

where $\textstyle f$ is any [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) on $\textstyle \textbf{R}^{n d}$ and the sum is over $\textstyle n$-[tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) of points for which repetition is allowed.

### First moment measure {#First_moment_measure}

For some Borel set *B*, the first moment of a point process *N* is:

$$M^1(B)=E [{N}(B)],$$

where $\textstyle M^1$ is known, among other terms, as the *intensity measure*[^o4] or *mean measure*,[^o15] and is interpreted as the expected or average number of points of $\textstyle {N}$ found or located in the set $\textstyle B$.

### Second moment measure {#Second_moment_measure}

The second moment measure for two Borel sets $\textstyle A$ and $\textstyle B$ is:

$$M^2(A\times B)=E [{N}(A){N}(B)],$$

which for a single Borel set $\textstyle B$ becomes

$$M^2(B\times B)=(E [{N}(B)])^2+\text{Var}[{N}(B)],$$

where $\textstyle \text{Var}[{N}(B)]$ denotes the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of the random variable $\textstyle {N}(B)$.

The previous variance term alludes to how moments measures, like moments of random variables, can be used to calculate quantities like the variance of point processes. A further example is the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) of a point process $\textstyle {N}$ for two Borel sets $\textstyle A$ and $\textstyle B$, which is given by:[^o3]

$$\text{Cov}[{N}(A),{N}(B)]=M^2(A\times B)-M^1(A)M^1(B)$$

:::

## Example: Poisson point process {#Example:_Poisson_point_process}

For a general [Poisson point process](wikipedia://en/41597450) with intensity measure $\textstyle \Lambda$ the first moment measure is:[^o3]

$$M^1(B)=\Lambda(B),$$

which for a [homogeneous Poisson point process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_Poisson_point_process) with [constant](wikipedia://en/48779551) intensity $\textstyle \lambda$ means:

$$M^1(B)=\lambda|B|,$$

where $\textstyle |B|$ is the length, area or volume (or more generally, the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)) of $\textstyle B$.

For the Poisson case with measure $\textstyle \Lambda$ the second moment measure defined on the product set $(B \times B)$ is:[^o6]

$$M^2(B \times B)=\Lambda(B)+\Lambda(B)^2.$$

which in the homogeneous case reduces to

$$M^2(B \times B)=\lambda|B|+(\lambda|B|)^2.$$
