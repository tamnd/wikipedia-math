# Coordinate systems for the hyperbolic plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coordinate systems for the hyperbolic plane"}

In the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291), as in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), each point can be uniquely identified by two [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). Several qualitatively different ways of coordinatizing the plane in hyperbolic geometry are used.

:::

This article tries to give an overview of several coordinate systems in use for the two-dimensional hyperbolic plane.

In the descriptions below the constant [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) of the plane is −1. [Sinh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function#Sinh), [cosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function#Cosh) and [tanh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function#Tanh) are [hyperbolic functions](wikipedia://en/56567).

## Polar coordinate system {#Polar_coordinate_system}

*Image: Examples of Polar Coordinates.svg.* Points in the polar coordinate system with pole *O* and polar axis *L*. In green, the point with radial coordinate 3 and angular coordinate 60 degrees or (3, 60°). In blue, the point (4, 210°).

The **polar coordinate system** is a [two-dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931) in which each [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29) on a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) is determined by a [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from a reference point and an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) from a reference direction.

The reference point (analogous to the origin of a [Cartesian system](wikipedia://en/7706)) is called the *pole*, and the [ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) from the pole in the reference direction is the *polar axis*. The distance from the pole is called the *radial coordinate* or *radius*, and the angle is called the *angular coordinate*, or *polar angle*.

From the [hyperbolic law of cosines](wikipedia://en/20757063), we get that the distance between two points given in polar coordinates is

$$\operatorname{dist} (\langle r_1, \theta_1 \rangle, \langle r_2, \theta_2 \rangle) = \operatorname{arcosh} \, \left( \cosh r_1 \cosh r_2 - \sinh r_1 \sinh r_2 \cos (\theta_2 - \theta_1) \right) \,.$$

Let $r=r_1=r_2,\theta=\theta_2 - \theta_1$, differentiating at $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{\theta}}=0$:

$$\begin{aligned}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{\theta}}\operatorname{dist} (\langle r, \theta_1 \rangle, \langle r, \theta_1+\theta \rangle)\right|_{\theta=0}&=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{\theta}}\operatorname{arcosh} \, \left( \cosh^2 r - \sinh^2 r \cos (\theta) \right)\right|_{\theta=0}\\ &=\sinh(r)\end{aligned}$$

we get the corresponding [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795): $(\mathrm{d} s)^2 = (\mathrm{d} r)^2 + \sinh^2 r \, (\mathrm{d} \theta)^2 \,.$

The straight lines are described by equations of the form

$$\theta = \theta_0 \pm \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \text{ or } \quad \tanh r = \tanh r_0 \sec (\theta - \theta_0)$$

where *r*<sub>0</sub> and θ<sub>0</sub> are the coordinates of the nearest point on the line to the pole.

## Quadrant model system {#Quadrant_model_system}

The [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) is closely related to a model of the hyperbolic plane in the quadrant *Q* = {(*x,y*): *x* > 0, *y* > 0}. For such a point the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) $v = \sqrt {x y}$ and the [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) $u = \ln \sqrt {x/y}$ produce a point (*u,v*) in the upper half-plane. The hyperbolic metric in the quadrant depends on the Poincaré half-plane metric. The [motions](wikipedia://en/1229416) of the Poincaré model carry over to the quadrant; in particular the left or right shifts of the real axis correspond to [hyperbolic rotations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_rotation) of the quadrant. Due to the study of ratios in physics and economics where the quadrant is the universe of discourse, its points are said to be located by [hyperbolic coordinates](wikipedia://en/1264398).

## Cartesian-style coordinate systems {#Cartesian-style_coordinate_systems}

In hyperbolic geometry [rectangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangles) do not exist. The sum of the angles of a quadrilateral in hyperbolic geometry is always less than 4 [right angles](wikipedia://en/76956) (see [Lambert quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3515519)). Also in hyperbolic geometry there are no equidistant lines (see [hypercycles](wikipedia://en/7516582)). This all has influences on the coordinate systems.

There are however different coordinate systems for hyperbolic plane geometry. All are based on choosing a real (non [ideal](wikipedia://en/4995922)) point (the [Origin](wikipedia://en/1313432)) on a chosen directed line (the *x*-axis) and after that many choices exist.

### Axial coordinates {#Axial_coordinates}

Axial coordinates *x*<sub>*a*</sub> and *y*<sub>*a*</sub> are found by constructing a *y*-axis perpendicular to the *x*-axis through the origin.[^o6]

Like in the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), the coordinates are found by dropping perpendiculars from the point onto the *x* and *y*-axes. *x*<sub>*a*</sub> is the distance from the foot of the perpendicular on the *x*-axis to the origin (regarded as positive on one side and negative on the other); *y*<sub>*a*</sub> is the distance from the foot of the perpendicular on the *y*-axis to the origin.

*Image: Circles about the origin in hyperbolic axial coordinates.gif.* Circles about the origin in hyperbolic axial coordinates.

Every point and almost all [ideal points](wikipedia://en/4995922) have axial coordinates, but not every pair of real numbers corresponds to a point.

If $\tanh^2 (x_a) + \tanh^2 (y_a) = 1$ then $P(x_a , y_a)$ is an ideal point.

If $\tanh^2 (x_a) + \tanh^2 (y_a) > 1$ then $P(x_a , y_a)$ is not a point at all.

The distance of a point $P(x_a , y_a)$ to the *x*-axis is $\operatorname {artanh} \left( \tanh(y_a) \cosh(x_a) \right)$. To the *y*-axis it is $\operatorname{artanh} \left( \tanh(x_a) \cosh(y_a) \right)$.

The relationship of axial coordinates to polar coordinates (assuming the origin is the pole and that the positive *x*-axis is the polar axis) is

$$x = \operatorname {artanh} \, (\tanh r \cos \theta)$$

$$y = \operatorname {artanh} \, (\tanh r \sin \theta)$$

$$r = \operatorname {artanh} \, (\sqrt{\tanh^2 x + \tanh^2 y} \, )$$

$$\theta = 2 \operatorname {arctan} \, \left( \frac{\tanh y}{\tanh x + \sqrt{\tanh^2 x + \tanh^2 y} } \right) \,.$$

::: {#o8 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Lobachevsky coordinates"}

### Lobachevsky coordinates {#Lobachevsky_coordinates}

The Lobachevsky coordinates *x*<sub>*ℓ*</sub> and *y*<sub>*ℓ*</sub> are found by dropping a perpendicular onto the *x*-axis. *x*<sub>*ℓ*</sub> is the distance from the foot of the perpendicular to the *x*-axis to the origin (positive on one side and negative on the other, the same as in axial coordinates).[^o6]

*y*<sub>*ℓ*</sub> is the distance along the perpendicular of the given point to its foot (positive on one side and negative on the other).

$$x_l = x_a \ , \ \tanh(y_l) = \tanh(y_a) \cosh(x_a) \ , \ \tanh(y_a) = \frac{\tanh(y_l)}{\cosh(x_l)}.$$

The Lobachevsky coordinates are useful for integration for length of curves[^o9] and area between lines and curves.

Lobachevsky coordinates are named after [Nikolai Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/648527), one of the discoverers of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).

*Image: Circles in Lobachevsky hyperbolic coordinates.png.* Circles about the origin of radius 1, 5 and 10 in the Lobachevsky hyperbolic coordinates.

*Image: Circles of fixed radius in Lobachevsky hyperbolic coordinates.png.* Circles about the points (0,0), (0,1), (0,2) and (0,3) of radius 3.5 in the Lobachevsky hyperbolic coordinates.

Construct a Cartesian-like coordinate system as follows. Choose a line (the *x*-axis) in the hyperbolic plane (with a standardized curvature of −1) and label the points on it by their distance from an origin (*x*=0) point on the *x*-axis (positive on one side and negative on the other). For any point in the plane, one can define coordinates *x* and *y* by dropping a perpendicular onto the *x*-axis. *x* will be the label of the foot of the perpendicular. *y* will be the distance along the perpendicular of the given point from its foot (positive on one side and negative on the other). Then the distance between two such points will be

$$\operatorname{dist} (\langle x_1, y_1 \rangle, \langle x_2, y_2 \rangle) = \operatorname{arcosh} \left( \cosh y_1 \cosh (x_2 - x_1) \cosh y_2 - \sinh y_1 \sinh y_2 \right) \,.$$

This formula can be derived from the formulas about [hyperbolic triangles](wikipedia://en/1283865).

The corresponding metric tensor is: $(\mathrm{d} s)^2 = \cosh^2 y \, (\mathrm{d} x)^2 + (\mathrm{d} y)^2$.

In this coordinate system, straight lines are either perpendicular to the *x*-axis (with equation *x* = a constant) or described by equations of the form

$$\tanh y = A \cosh x + B \sinh x \quad \text{ when } \quad A^2 < 1 + B^2$$

where *A* and *B* are real parameters which characterize the straight line.

The relationship of Lobachevsky coordinates to polar coordinates (assuming the origin is the pole and that the positive *x*-axis is the polar axis) is

$$x = \operatorname {artanh} \, (\tanh r \cos \theta)$$

$$y = \operatorname {arsinh} \, (\sinh r \sin \theta)$$

$$r = \operatorname {arcosh} \, (\cosh x \cosh y)$$

$$\theta = 2 \operatorname {arctan} \, \left( \frac{\sinh y}{\sinh x \cosh y + \sqrt{\cosh^2 x \cosh^2 y - 1} } \right) \,.$$

### Horocycle-based coordinate system {#Horocycle-based_coordinate_system}

*Image: Horocycle-based coordinate system.svg.* Horocycle-based coordinate system

Another coordinate system represents each hyperbolic point $P$ by two real numbers, defined relative to some given [horocycle](wikipedia://en/11084460). These numbers are the hyperbolic distance $x_h$ from $P$ to the horocycle, and the (signed) [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) $y_h$ along the horocycle between a fixed reference point $O$ and $P_h$, where $P_h$ is the closest point on the horocycle to $P$.[^o12]

:::

## Model-based coordinate systems {#Model-based_coordinate_systems}

Model-based coordinate systems use one of the [models of hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291#models_of_hyperbolic_plane) and take the Euclidean coordinates inside the model as the hyperbolic coordinates.

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Beltrami coordinates"}

### Beltrami coordinates {#Beltrami_coordinates}

The Beltrami coordinates of a point are the [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) of the point when the point is mapped in the [Beltrami–Klein model](wikipedia://en/4792791) of the hyperbolic plane, the *x*-axis is mapped to the segment (−1,0) − (1,0) and the origin is mapped to the centre of the boundary circle.[^o6]

The following equations hold:

$$x_b = \tanh (x_a), \ y_b = \tanh(y_a)$$

:::

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Poincaré coordinates"}

### Poincaré coordinates {#Poincaré_coordinates}

The Poincaré coordinates of a point are the Cartesian coordinates of the point when the point is mapped in the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) of the hyperbolic plane,[^o6] the *x*-axis is mapped to the segment (−1,0) − (1,0) and the origin is mapped to the centre of the boundary circle.

The Poincaré coordinates, in terms of the Beltrami coordinates, are:

$$x_p = \frac{x_b}{1+\sqrt{1 -x_b^2 - y_b^2 }}, \ \ y_p = \frac{y_b}{1+\sqrt{1 -x_b^2 - y_b^2 }}$$

:::

::: {#o19 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Weierstrass coordinates"}

### Weierstrass coordinates {#Weierstrass_coordinates}

The Weierstrass coordinates of a point are the Cartesian coordinates of the point when the point is mapped in the [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189) of the hyperbolic plane, the *x*-axis is mapped to the (half) [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) $(t , 0 , \sqrt{t^2+1} )$ and the origin is mapped to the point (0,0,1).[^o6]

The point P with axial coordinates (*x*<sub>*a*</sub>, *y*<sub>*a*</sub>) is mapped to

$$\left( \frac{\tanh x_a}{\sqrt{1 -\tanh^2 x_a - \tanh^2 y_a }} \ , \ \frac{\tanh y_a}{\sqrt{1 -\tanh^2 x_a - \tanh^2 y_a }} \ , \ \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 -\tanh^2 x_a - \tanh^2 y_a }} \right)$$

:::

### Hyperbolic barycentric coordinates {#Hyperbolic_barycentric_coordinates}

From [Gyrovector space#Triangle centers](wikipedia://en/20295286#Triangle_centers)

The study of [triangle centers](wikipedia://en/12401488) traditionally is concerned with Euclidean geometry, but triangle centers can also be studied in hyperbolic geometry. Using gyrotrigonometry, expressions for trigonometric barycentric coordinates can be calculated that have the same form for both euclidean and hyperbolic geometry. In order for the expressions to coincide, the expressions must *not* encapsulate the specification of the anglesum being 180 degrees.[^o23][^o24][^o25]
