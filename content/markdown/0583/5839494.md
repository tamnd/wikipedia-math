# Decorrelation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Decorrelation"}

**Decorrelation** is a general term for any process that is used to reduce [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) within a signal, or [cross-correlation](wikipedia://en/714163) within a set of signals, while preserving other aspects of the signal. A frequently used method of decorrelation is the use of a matched [linear filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_filter) to reduce the [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) of a signal as far as possible. Since the minimum possible autocorrelation for a given signal energy is achieved by equalising the power spectrum of the signal to be similar to that of a [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182) signal, this is often referred to as [signal whitening](wikipedia://en/8145581).

:::

## Process {#Process}

### Signal processing {#Signal_processing}

Most decorrelation algorithms are [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), but there are also [non-linear](wikipedia://en/146103) decorrelation algorithms.

Many data compression algorithms incorporate a decorrelation stage. For example, many [transform coders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transform_coder) first apply a fixed linear transformation that would, on average, have the effect of decorrelating a typical signal of the class to be coded, prior to any later processing. This is typically a [Karhunen–Loève transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karhunen–Loève_transform), or a simplified approximation such as the [discrete cosine transform](wikipedia://en/59962).

By comparison, [sub-band coders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-band_coder) do not generally have an explicit decorrelation step, but instead exploit the already-existing reduced correlation within each of the sub-bands of the signal, due to the relative flatness of each sub-band of the power spectrum in many classes of signals.

[Linear predictive coders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_predictive_coder) can be modelled as an attempt to decorrelate signals by subtracting the best possible linear prediction from the input signal, leaving a whitened residual signal.

Decorrelation techniques can also be used for many other purposes, such as reducing [crosstalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosstalk) in a multi-channel signal, or in the design of [echo cancellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echo_canceller).

In [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing) decorrelation techniques can be used to enhance or stretch, [colour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colour) differences found in each [pixel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel) of an image. This is generally termed as 'decorrelation stretching'.[^o4]

### Neuroscience {#Neuroscience}

In [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience), decorrelation is used in the analysis of the [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network) in the human visual system. The raw inputs from [cone cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_cell) and [rod cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_cell) undergo many steps of processing before it is handled by the [visual cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_cortex). These steps generally perform decorrelation, both spatial ([surround suppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surround_suppression) in the retina) and temporal (handling of movement in the [lateral geniculate nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_geniculate_nucleus)).[^o6]

### Cryptography {#Cryptography}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), decorrelation is used in cipher design (see [Decorrelation theory](wikipedia://en/12930296)) and in the design of [hardware random number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardware_random_number_generator#Software_whitening).
