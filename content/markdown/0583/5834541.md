# Schröder's equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schröder's equation"}

*Image: Ernst schroeder.jpg.* [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015) (1841–1902) in 1870 formulated his eponymous equation.

**Schröder's equation**,[^o2][^o3][^o4] named after [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015), is a [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377) with one [independent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable): given the function $h$, find the function $\Psi$ such that

Schröder's equation is an eigenvalue equation for the [composition operator](wikipedia://en/2702039) $C_{h}$ that sends a function $f$ to $f(h(.))$.

:::

If $a$ is a [fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_point_iteration) of $h$, meaning $h(a) = a$, then either $\Psi(a) = 0$ (or $\infty$) or $s = 1$. Application of the chain rule shows that the [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) $s$ is given by $s = h'(a)$, provided that $\Psi(a)$ is finite and $\Psi'(a)$ does not vanish or diverge.

## Functional significance {#Functional_significance}

For $a = 0$, if $h$ is analytic on the unit disk, fixes $0$, and $0 < |h'(0)| < 1$, then [Gabriel Koenigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Koenigs) showed in 1884 that there is an analytic (non-trivial) $\Psi$ satisfying Schröder's equation. This is one of the first steps in a long line of theorems fruitful for understanding composition operators on analytic function spaces, cf. [Koenigs function](wikipedia://en/33973912).

Equations such as Schröder's are suitable to encoding [self-similarity](wikipedia://en/28782), and have thus been extensively utilized in studies of [nonlinear dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_dynamics) (often referred to colloquially as [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295)). It is also used in studies of [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664), as well as the [renormalization group](wikipedia://en/291462).[^o7][^o8]

An equivalent transpose form of Schröder's equation for the inverse $\Phi = \Psi^{-1}$ of Schröder's conjugacy function is $h(\Phi(y)) = \Phi(sy)$. The change of variables $\alpha(x) = \log(\Psi(x))/\log(s)$ (the [Abel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_function)) further converts Schröder's equation to the older [Abel equation](wikipedia://en/5834526), $\alpha(h(x)) = \alpha(x) + 1$. Similarly, the change of variables $\Psi(x) = \log(\varphi(x))$ converts Schröder's equation to [Böttcher's equation](wikipedia://en/34458555), $\varphi(h(x)) = (\varphi(x))^{s}$.

Moreover, for the velocity,[^o8] $\beta(x) = \Psi/\Psi'$, *[Julia](wikipedia://en/249107)'s equation*, $\beta(f(x)) = f'(x)\beta(x)$, holds.

The $n$-th power of a solution of Schröder's equation provides a solution of Schröder's equation with eigenvalue $s^{n}$, instead. In the same vein, for an invertible solution $\Psi(x)$ of Schröder's equation, the (non-invertible) function $\Psi(x) k(\log \Psi(x))$ is also a solution, for *any* periodic function $k(x)$ with period $\log(s)$. All solutions of Schröder's equation are related in this manner.

## Solutions {#Solutions}

Schröder's equation was solved analytically if $a$ is an attracting (but not superattracting) fixed point, that is $0 < |h'(a)| < 1$ by [Gabriel Koenigs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Xavier_Paul_Koenigs) (1884).[^o10][^o11]

In the case of a superattracting fixed point, $|h'(a)| = 0$, Schröder's equation is unwieldy, and had best be transformed to [Böttcher's equation](wikipedia://en/34458555).[^o12]

There are a good number of particular solutions dating back to Schröder's original 1870 paper.[^o2]

The series expansion around a fixed point and the relevant convergence properties of the solution for the resulting orbit and its analyticity properties are cogently summarized by [Szekeres](wikipedia://en/2563194).[^o13] Several of the solutions are furnished in terms of [asymptotic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_series), cf. [Jabotinsky matrix](wikipedia://en/14320253).

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Phase-space Orbit of-Logistic map.jpg.* First five half periods of the phase-space orbit of the *s* = 4 chaotic logistic map $h(x)$, interpolated holographically through Schröder's equation. The velocity $v = dh_{t}/dt$ plotted against $h_{t}$. Chaos is evident in the orbit sweeping all $x$s at all times.

It is used to analyse discrete dynamical systems by finding a new coordinate system in which the system (orbit) generated by *h*(*x*) looks simpler, a mere dilation.

More specifically, a system for which a discrete unit time step amounts to $x \to h(x)$, can have its smooth [orbit](wikipedia://en/252250) (or [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735)) reconstructed from the solution of the above Schröder's equation, its [conjugacy equation](wikipedia://en/5506149).

That is, $h(x) = \Psi^{-1}(s \Psi(x)) \equiv h_{1}(x)$.

In general, *all of its functional iterates* (its *regular iteration [group](wikipedia://en/754851)*, see [iterated function](wikipedia://en/1448702)) are provided by the **orbit**

for $t$ real — not necessarily positive or integer. (Thus a full [continuous group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_group).) The set of $h_{n}(x)$, i.e., of all positive integer iterates of $h(x)$ ([semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799)) is called the *splinter* (or Picard sequence) of $h(x)$.

However, *all iterates* (fractional, infinitesimal, or negative) of $h(x)$ are likewise specified through the coordinate transformation $\Psi(x)$ determined to solve Schröder's equation: a holographic continuous interpolation of the initial discrete recursion $x \to h(x)$ has been constructed;[^o17] in effect, the entire [orbit](wikipedia://en/252250).

For instance, the [functional square root](wikipedia://en/7207519) is $h_{1/2}(x) = \Psi^{-1}(s^{1/2} \Psi(x))$, so that $h_{1/2}(h_{1/2}(x)) = h(x)$, and so on.

For example,[^o18] special cases of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) such as the chaotic case $h(x) = 4x(1 - x)$ were already worked out by Schröder in his original article[^o2] (p. 306),

$\Psi(x) = (\arcsin \sqrt{x})^{2}$, $s = 4$, and hence $h_{t}(x) = \sin^{2}(2^{t} \arcsin \sqrt{x})$.

In fact, this solution is seen to result as motion dictated by a sequence of switchback potentials,[^o19] $V(x) \propto x(x - 1) (n\pi + \arcsin \sqrt{x})^{2}$, a generic feature of continuous iterates effected by Schröder's equation.

A nonchaotic case he also illustrated with his method, $h(x) = 2x(1 - x)$, yields

$\Psi(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ln(1 - 2x)$, and hence $h_{t}(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2}((1 - 2x)^{2^{t}} - 1)$.

Likewise, for the [Beverton–Holt model](wikipedia://en/11185796), $h(x) = x/(2 - x)$, one readily finds[^o17] $\Psi(x) = x/(1 - x)$, so that[^o20]

$$h_t(x)= \Psi^{-1}\big(2^{-t} \Psi(x)\big) = \frac{x}{2^t + x(1 - 2^t)}.$$
