# Point groups in three dimensions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Point groups in three dimensions"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **[point group](wikipedia://en/1138942) in three dimensions** is an [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694) in three dimensions that leaves the origin fixed, or correspondingly, an isometry group of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). It is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) O(3), the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) that leave the origin fixed, or correspondingly, the group of [orthogonal matrices](wikipedia://en/105620). O(3) itself is a subgroup of the [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164) E(3) of all isometries.

:::

[Symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186) of geometric objects are isometry groups. Accordingly, analysis of isometry groups is analysis of possible [symmetries](wikipedia://en/53741). All isometries of a [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) (finite) 3D object have one or more common fixed points. We follow the usual convention by choosing the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) as one of them.

The symmetry group of an object is sometimes also called its **full symmetry group**, as opposed to its **proper symmetry group**, the intersection of its full symmetry group with [E<sup>\+</sup>(3)](wikipedia://en/652164#Direct_and_indirect_isometries), which consists of all [*direct isometries*](wikipedia://en/652164#Direct_and_indirect_isometries), i.e., isometries preserving [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29). For a bounded object, the proper symmetry group is called its **rotation group**. It is the intersection of its full symmetry group with [SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29), the full rotation group of the 3D space. The rotation group of a bounded object is equal to its full symmetry group [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the object is [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160).

The point groups that are generated purely by a finite set of reflection mirror planes passing through the same point are the [finite Coxeter groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_Coxeter_group), represented by [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502).

The point groups in three dimensions are widely used in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), especially to describe the symmetries of a [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) and of [molecular orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital) forming [covalent bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond), and in this context they are also called **[molecular point groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_character_tables_for_chemically_important_3D_point_groups)**.

## 3D isometries that leave the origin fixed {#3D_isometries_that_leave_the_origin_fixed}

The symmetry group operations ([symmetry operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_operation)) are the isometries of [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) **R**<sup>3</sup> that leave the origin fixed, forming the group O(3). These operations can be categorized as:

- The direct (orientation-preserving) symmetry operations, which form the group SO(3):
  - The identity operation, denoted by *E* or the identity matrix *I*.
  - Rotation about an axis through the origin by an angle θ. Rotation by θ = 360°/*n* for any positive integer *n* is denoted *C*<sub>*n*</sub> (from the [Schoenflies notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoenflies_notation) for the group *C*<sub>*n*</sub> that it [generates](wikipedia://en/99945)). The identity operation, also written *C*<sub>1</sub>, is a special case of the rotation operator.
- The indirect (orientation-reversing) operations:
  - Inversion, denoted i or *C*<sub>i</sub>, that is, rotation by 180° about a coordinate axis followed by a reflection in the orthogonal coordinate plane. The matrix notation is *−I*.
  - Reflection in a plane through the origin, denoted σ.
  - [Improper rotation](wikipedia://en/244324), also called rotation-reflection: rotation about an axis by an angle θ, combined with reflection in the plane through the origin perpendicular to the axis. Rotation-reflection by θ = 360°/*n* for any positive integer *n* is denoted *S*<sub>*n*</sub> (from the [Schoenflies notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoenflies_notation) for the group *S*<sub>*n*</sub> that it [generates](wikipedia://en/99945) if *n* is even).

Inversion is a special case of rotation-reflection (i = *S*<sub>2</sub>), as is reflection (σ = *S*<sub>1</sub>), so these operations are often classified as improper rotations.

A [circumflex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumflex) is sometimes added to the symbol to indicate an operator, as in *Ĉ*<sub>*n*</sub> and *Ŝ*<sub>*n*</sub>.

## Conjugacy {#Conjugacy}

When comparing the symmetry type of two objects, the origin is chosen for each separately, i.e., they need not have the same center. Moreover, two objects are considered to be of the same symmetry type if their symmetry groups are conjugate subgroups of O(3) (two subgroups *H*<sub>1</sub>, *H*<sub>2</sub> of a group *G* are [*conjugate*](wikipedia://en/49176#Conjugacy_of_subgroups_and_general_subsets), if there exists *g* ∈ *G* such that *H*<sub>1</sub> = *g*<sup>−1</sup>*H*<sub>2</sub>*g* ).

For example, two 3D objects have the same symmetry type:

- if both have mirror symmetry, but with respect to a different mirror plane
- if both have 3-fold rotational symmetry, but with respect to a different axis.

In the case of multiple mirror planes and/or axes of rotation, two symmetry groups are of the same symmetry type if and only if there is a rotation mapping the whole structure of the first symmetry group to that of the second. (In fact there will be more than one such rotation, but not an infinite number as when there is only one mirror or axis.) The conjugacy definition would also allow a mirror image of the structure, but this is not needed, the structure itself is achiral. For example, if a symmetry group contains a 3-fold axis of rotation, it contains rotations in two opposite directions. (The structure ***is*** chiral for 11 pairs of ***[space groups](wikipedia://en/463721)*** with a screw axis.)

## Infinite isometry groups {#Infinite_isometry_groups}

There are many **infinite isometry groups**; for example, the "[cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327)" (meaning that it is generated by one element – not to be confused with a [torsion group](wikipedia://en/3568695)) generated by a rotation by an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) of turns about an axis. We may create non-cyclical [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) by adding more rotations around the same axis. The set of points on a circle at [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) numbers of degrees around the circle illustrates a point group requiring an infinite number of [generators](wikipedia://en/99945). There are also non-abelian groups generated by rotations around different axes. These are usually (generically) [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735). They will be infinite unless the rotations are specially chosen.

All the infinite groups mentioned so far are not [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_%28topology%29) as [topological subgroups](wikipedia://en/42315) of O(3). We now discuss topologically closed subgroups of O(3).

*Image: Blender-meta-ball.png.* An unmarked [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) has O(3) symmetry.

The whole O(3) is the symmetry group of [spherical symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_symmetry); [SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29) is the corresponding rotation group. The other infinite isometry groups consist of all [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) about an axis through the origin, and those with additionally reflection in the planes through the axis, and/or reflection in the plane through the origin, perpendicular to the axis. Those with reflection in the planes through the axis, with or without reflection in the plane through the origin perpendicular to the axis, are the symmetry groups for the two types of [cylindrical symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_symmetry). Any 3D shape (subset of **R**<sup>3</sup>) having infinite rotational symmetry must also have mirror symmetry for every plane through the axis. Physical objects having infinite rotational symmetry will also have the symmetry of mirror planes through the axis, but vector fields may not, for instance the velocity vectors of a cone rotating about its axis, or the magnetic field surrounding a wire.[^o6]

There are seven continuous groups which are all in a sense limits of the finite isometry groups. These so called *limiting point groups* or *[Curie limiting groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_limiting_groups)* are named after [Pierre Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Curie) who was the first to investigate them.[^o6][^o7] The seven infinite series of axial groups lead to five limiting groups (two of them are duplicates), and the seven remaining point groups produce two more continuous groups. In international notation, the list is ∞, ∞2, ∞/m, ∞mm, ∞/mm, ∞∞, and ∞∞m.[^o8] Not all of these are possible for physical objects, for example objects with ∞∞ symmetry also have ∞∞m symmetry. See below for other designations and more details.

:::

## Finite isometry groups {#Finite_isometry_groups}

Symmetries in 3D that leave the origin fixed are fully characterized by symmetries on a sphere centered at the origin. For finite 3D point groups, see also [spherical symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/2871265).

Up to conjugacy, the set of finite 3D point groups consists of:

- , which have at most one more-than-2-fold rotation axis; they are the finite symmetry groups on an infinite [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29), or equivalently, those on a finite cylinder. They are sometimes called the axial or prismatic point groups.
- , which have multiple 3-or-more-fold rotation axes; these groups can also be characterized as point groups having multiple 3-fold rotation axes. The possible combinations are:
  - Four 3-fold axes (the three [tetrahedral symmetries](wikipedia://en/2904472) *T*, *T*<sub>h</sub>, and *T*<sub>d</sub>)
  - Four 3-fold axes and three 4-fold axes ([octahedral symmetries](wikipedia://en/2900805) *O* and *O*<sub>h</sub>)
  - Ten 3-fold axes and six 5-fold axes ([icosahedral symmetries](wikipedia://en/2877844) *I* and *I*<sub>h</sub>)

According to the [crystallographic restriction theorem](wikipedia://en/2218040), only a limited number of point groups are compatible with discrete [translational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry): 27 from the 7 infinite series, and 5 of the 7 others. Together, these make up the 32 so-called [crystallographic point groups](wikipedia://en/663786).

## The seven infinite series of axial groups {#The_seven_infinite_series_of_axial_groups}

The infinite series of axial or prismatic groups have an index *n*, which can be any integer; in each series, the *n*th symmetry group contains *n*-fold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) about an axis, i.e., symmetry with respect to a rotation by an angle 360°/*n*. *n*=1 covers the cases of no rotational symmetry at all. There are four series with no other axes of rotational symmetry (see [cyclic symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_symmetries)) and three with additional axes of 2-fold symmetry (see [dihedral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihedral_symmetry)). They can be understood as [point groups in two dimensions](wikipedia://en/3037988) extended with an axial coordinate and reflections in it. They are related to the [frieze groups](wikipedia://en/375503);[^o11] they can be interpreted as frieze-group patterns repeated *n* times around a cylinder.

The following table lists several notations for point groups: [Hermann–Mauguin notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann–Mauguin_notation) (used in [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography)), [Schönflies notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schönflies_notation) (used to describe [molecular symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_symmetry)), [orbifold notation](wikipedia://en/2915678), and [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502). The latter three are not only conveniently related to its properties, but also to the order of the group. The orbifold notation is a unified notation, also applicable for [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214) and [frieze groups](wikipedia://en/375503). The crystallographic groups have *n* restricted to 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6; removing crystallographic restriction allows any positive integer. The series are:

For odd *n* we have Z<sub>2*n*</sub> = Z<sub>*n*</sub> × Z<sub>2</sub> and Dih<sub>2*n*</sub> = Dih<sub>*n*</sub> × Z<sub>2</sub>.

The groups *C*<sub>*n*</sub> (including the trivial *C*<sub>1</sub>) and *D*<sub>*n*</sub> are chiral, the others are achiral.

The terms horizontal (h) and vertical (v), and the corresponding subscripts, refer to the additional mirror plane, that can be parallel to the rotation axis (vertical) or perpendicular to the rotation axis (horizontal).

The simplest nontrivial axial groups are equivalent to the abstract group Z<sub>2</sub>:

- *C*<sub>i</sub> (equivalent to *S*<sub>2</sub>) – **[inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_point) symmetry**
- *C*<sub>2</sub> (equivalent to *D*<sub>1</sub>) – **2-fold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096)**
- *C*<sub>s</sub> (equivalent to *C*<sub>1h</sub>and *C*<sub>1v</sub>) – **[reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873)**, also called **[bilateral symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28biology%29#Bilateral_symmetry)**.

*Image: Uniaxial.png.* Patterns on a cylindrical band illustrating the case *n* = 6 for each of the 7 infinite families of point groups. The symmetry group of each pattern is the indicated group.

The second of these is the first of the uniaxial groups ([cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327)) *C*<sub>*n*</sub> of order *n* (also applicable in 2D), which are generated by a single rotation of angle 360°/*n*. In addition to this, one may add a mirror plane perpendicular to the axis, giving the group *C*<sub>*n*h</sub> of order 2*n*, or a set of *n* mirror planes containing the axis, giving the group *C*<sub>*n*v</sub>, also of order 2*n*. The latter is the symmetry group for a regular *n*-sided [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045). A typical object with symmetry group *C*<sub>*n*</sub> or *D*<sub>*n*</sub> is a [propeller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propeller).

If both horizontal and vertical reflection planes are added, their intersections give *n* axes of rotation through 180°, so the group is no longer uniaxial. This new group of order 4*n* is called *D*<sub>*n*h</sub>. Its subgroup of rotations is the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) *D*<sub>*n*</sub> of order 2*n*, which still has the 2-fold rotation axes perpendicular to the primary rotation axis, but no mirror planes.

Note: in 2D, *D*<sub>*n*</sub> includes reflections, which can also be viewed as flipping over flat objects without distinction of frontside and backside; but in 3D, the two operations are distinguished: *D*<sub>*n*</sub> contains "flipping over", not reflections.

There is one more group in this family, called *D*<sub>*n*d</sub> (or *D*<sub>*n*v</sub>), which has vertical mirror planes containing the main rotation axis, but instead of having a horizontal mirror plane, it has an isometry that combines a reflection in the horizontal plane and a rotation by an angle 180°/*n*. *D*<sub>*n*h</sub> is the symmetry group for a "regular" *n*-gonal [prism](wikipedia://en/283007) and also for a "regular" *n*-gonal [bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153). *D*<sub>*n*d</sub> is the symmetry group for a "regular" *n*-gonal [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851), and also for a "regular" *n*-gonal [trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/719845). *D*<sub>*n*</sub> is the symmetry group of a partially rotated ("twisted") prism.

The groups *D*<sub>2</sub> and *D*<sub>2h</sub> are noteworthy in that there is no special rotation axis. Rather, there are three perpendicular 2-fold axes. *D*<sub>2</sub> is a subgroup of all the polyhedral symmetries (see below), and *D*<sub>2h</sub> is a subgroup of the polyhedral groups *T*<sub>h</sub> and *O*<sub>h</sub>. *D*<sub>2</sub> occurs in molecules such as [twistane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistane) and in [homotetramers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotetramer) such as [Concanavalin A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concanavalin_A). The elements of *D*<sub>2</sub> are in 1-to-2 correspondence with the rotations given by the [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) [Lipschitz quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_quaternion).

The group *S*<sub>*n*</sub> is generated by the combination of a reflection in the horizontal plane and a rotation by an angle 360°/n. For *n* odd this is equal to the group generated by the two separately, *C*<sub>*n*h</sub> of order 2*n*, and therefore the notation *S*<sub>*n*</sub> is not needed; however, for *n* even it is distinct, and of order *n*. Like *D*<sub>*n*d</sub> it contains a number of [improper rotations](wikipedia://en/244324) without containing the corresponding rotations.

All symmetry groups in the 7 infinite series are different, except for the following four pairs of mutually equal ones:

- *C*<sub>1h</sub> and *C*<sub>1v</sub>: group of order 2 with a single reflection (*C*<sub>s</sub> )
- *D*<sub>1</sub> and *C*<sub>2</sub>: group of order 2 with a single 180° rotation
- *D*<sub>1h</sub> and *C*<sub>2v</sub>: group of order 4 with a reflection in a plane and a 180° rotation through a line in that plane
- *D*<sub>1d</sub> and *C*<sub>2h</sub>: group of order 4 with a reflection in a plane and a 180° rotation through a line perpendicular to that plane.

*S*<sub>2</sub> is the group of order 2 with a single inversion (*C*<sub>i</sub> ).

"Equal" is meant here as the same up to conjugacy in space. This is stronger than "up to algebraic isomorphism". For example, there are three different groups of order two in the first sense, but there is only one in the second sense. Similarly, e.g. *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> is algebraically isomorphic with Z<sub>2*n*</sub>.

The groups may be constructed as follows:

- *C*<sub>*n*</sub>. Generated by an element also called *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, which corresponds to a rotation by angle 2π/*n* around the axis. Its elements are E (the identity), *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>2</sup>, ..., *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>*n*−1</sup>, corresponding to rotation angles 0, 2π/*n*, 4π/*n*, ..., 2(*n* − 1)π/*n*.
- *S*<sub>2*n*</sub>. Generated by element *C*<sub>2*n*</sub>σ<sub>h</sub>, where σ<sub>h</sub> is a reflection in the direction of the axis. Its elements are the elements of *C*<sub>*n*</sub> with *C*<sub>2*n*</sub>σ<sub>h</sub>, *C*<sub>2*n*</sub><sup>3</sup>σ<sub>h</sub>, ..., *C*<sub>2*n*</sub><sup>2*n*−1</sup>σ<sub>h</sub> added.
- *C*<sub>*n*h</sub>. Generated by element *C*<sub>*n*</sub> and reflection σ<sub>h</sub>. Its elements are the elements of group *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, with elements σ<sub>h</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*</sub>σ<sub>h</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>2</sup>σ<sub>h</sub>, ..., *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>*n*−1</sup>σ<sub>h</sub> added.
- *C*<sub>*n*v</sub>. Generated by element *C*<sub>*n*</sub> and reflection σ<sub>v</sub> in a direction in the plane perpendicular to the axis. Its elements are the elements of group *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, with elements σ<sub>v</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*</sub>σ<sub>v</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>2</sup>σ<sub>v</sub>, ..., *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>*n*−1</sup>σ<sub>v</sub> added.
- *D*<sub>*n*</sub>. Generated by element *C*<sub>*n*</sub> and 180° rotation U = σ<sub>h</sub>σ<sub>v</sub> around a direction in the plane perpendicular to the axis. Its elements are the elements of group *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, with elements U, *C*<sub>*n*</sub>U, *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>2</sup>U, ..., *C*<sub>*n*</sub><sup>*n* − 1</sup>U added.
- *D*<sub>*n*d</sub>. Generated by elements *C*<sub>2*n*</sub>σ<sub>h</sub> and σ<sub>v</sub>. Its elements are the elements of group *C*<sub>*n*</sub> and the additional elements of *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> and *C*<sub>*n*v</sub>, with elements *C*<sub>2*n*</sub>σ<sub>h</sub>σ<sub>v</sub>, *C*<sub>2*n*</sub><sup>3</sup>σ<sub>h</sub>σ<sub>v</sub>, ..., *C*<sub>2*n*</sub><sup>2*n* − 1</sup>σ<sub>h</sub>σ<sub>v</sub> added.
- *D*<sub>*n*h</sub>. Generated by elements *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, σ<sub>h</sub>, and σ<sub>v</sub>. Its elements are the elements of group *C*<sub>*n*</sub> and the additional elements of *C*<sub>*n*h</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*v</sub>, and *D*<sub>*n*</sub>.

Groups with continuous axial rotations are designated by putting ∞ in place of *n*. Note however that *C*${}_{\infty}$ here is not the same as the [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) (also sometimes designated *C*${}_{\infty}$), which is isomorphic to the integers. The following table gives the five continuous axial rotation groups. They are limits of the finite groups only in the sense that they arise when the main rotation is replaced by rotation by an arbitrary angle, so not necessarily a rational number of degrees as with the finite groups. Physical objects can only have *C*${}_{\infty v}$ or *D*${}_{\infty h}$ symmetry, but vector fields can have the others.

## The seven remaining point groups {#The_seven_remaining_point_groups}

The remaining point groups are said to be of very high or [polyhedral](wikipedia://en/23470) symmetry because they have more than one rotation axis of order greater than 2. Here, *C*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes an axis of rotation through 360°/n and *S*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes an axis of improper rotation through the same. ## Rotation groups {#Rotation_groups}

The rotation groups, i.e., the finite subgroups of SO(3), are: the cyclic groups *C*<sub>*n*</sub> (the rotation group of a canonical [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045)), the dihedral groups *D*<sub>*n*</sub> (the rotation group of a uniform [prism](wikipedia://en/283007), or canonical [bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153)), and the rotation groups *T*, *O* and *I* of a regular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635)/[cube](wikipedia://en/6285) and [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084)/[dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407).

In particular, the dihedral groups *D*<sub>3</sub>, *D*<sub>4</sub> etc. are the rotation groups of plane regular polygons embedded in three-dimensional space, and such a figure may be considered as a degenerate regular prism. Therefore, it is also called a *[dihedron](wikipedia://en/6109457)* (Greek: solid with two faces), which explains the name *dihedral group*.

- An object having symmetry group *C*<sub>*n*</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*h</sub>, *C*<sub>*n*v</sub> or *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> has rotation group *C*<sub>*n*</sub>.
- An object having symmetry group *D*<sub>*n*</sub>, *D*<sub>*n*h</sub>, or *D*<sub>*n*d</sub> has rotation group *D*<sub>*n*</sub>.
- An object having a polyhedral symmetry (*T*, *T*<sub>d</sub>, *T*<sub>h</sub>, *O*, *O*<sub>h</sub>, *I* or *I*<sub>h</sub>) has as its rotation group the corresponding one without a subscript: *T*, *O* or *I*.

The rotation group of an object is equal to its full symmetry group if and only if the object is [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160). In other words, the chiral objects are those with their symmetry group in the list of rotation groups.

## Correspondence between rotation groups and other groups {#Correspondence_between_rotation_groups_and_other_groups}

### Groups containing inversion {#Groups_containing_inversion}

The rotation group SO(3) is a subgroup of O(3), the full point rotation group of the 3D Euclidean space. Correspondingly, O(3) is the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of SO(3) and the [inversion](wikipedia://en/12487536) group *C*<sub>i</sub> (where inversion is denoted by its [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) −*I*):

O(3) = SO(3) × { *I* , −*I* }

Thus there is a 1-to-1 correspondence between all direct isometries and all indirect isometries, through inversion. Also there is a 1-to-1 correspondence between all groups *H* of direct isometries in SO(3) and all groups *K* of isometries in O(3) that contain inversion:

*K* = *H* × { *I* , −*I* }

*H* = *K* ∩ SO(3)

where the isometry ( *A*, *I* ) is [identified](wikipedia://en/298428) with *A*.

For finite groups, the correspondence is:

::: {#o24 .table cue=table}

| Rotation group *H* | Parity of *n* | Group containing inversion *K* |
|---|---|---|
| *C*<sub>*n*</sub> | even | *C*<sub>*n*h</sub> |
|  | odd | *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> |
| *D*<sub>*n*</sub> | even | *D*<sub>*n*h</sub> |
|  | odd | *D*<sub>*n*d</sub> |
| *T* |  | *T*<sub>h</sub> |
| *O* |  | *O*<sub>h</sub> |
| *I* |  | *I*<sub>h</sub> |

:::

### Groups containing indirect isometries but no inversion {#Groups_containing_indirect_isometries_but_no_inversion}

If a group of direct isometries *H* has a subgroup *L* of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) 2, then there is a corresponding group that contains indirect isometries but no inversion:

$$M = L \cup ( (H \smallsetminus L) \times \{ -I \})$$

For example, *H* = *C*<sub>4</sub> corresponds to *M* = *S*<sub>4</sub>.

Thus *M* is obtained from *H* by inverting the isometries in $H \smallsetminus L$. This group *M* is, when considered as an [abstract group](wikipedia://en/41890#Abstract_groups), isomorphic to *H*. Conversely, for all point groups *M* that contain indirect isometries but no inversion we can obtain a rotation group *H* by inverting the indirect isometries.

For finite groups, the correspondence is:

| Rotation group *H* | Index-2 subgroup *L* | Parity of *n* | Group containing indirect isometries *M* |
|---|---|---|---|
| *C*<sub>2*n*</sub> | *C*<sub>*n*</sub> | even | *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> |
|  |  | odd | *C*<sub>*n*h</sub> |
| *D*<sub>2*n*</sub> | *D*<sub>*n*</sub> | even | *D*<sub>*n*h</sub> |
|  |  | odd | *D*<sub>*n*d</sub> |
| *D*<sub>*n*</sub> | *C*<sub>*n*</sub> | any | *C*<sub>*n*v</sub> |
| *O* | *T* |  | *T*<sub>d</sub> |

:::

## Normal subgroups {#Normal_subgroups}

In 2D, the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of *k*-fold [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) *C*<sub>*k*</sub> is for every positive integer *k* a normal subgroup of O(2) and SO(2). Accordingly, in 3D, for every axis the cyclic group of *k*-fold rotations about that axis is a normal subgroup of the group of all rotations about that axis. Since any subgroup of index two is normal, the group of rotations (*C*<sub>*n*</sub>) is normal both in the group (*C*<sub>*n*v</sub>) obtained by adding to (*C*<sub>*n*</sub>) reflection planes through its axis and in the group (*C*<sub>*n*h</sub>) obtained by adding to (*C*<sub>*n*</sub>) a reflection plane perpendicular to its axis.

## Maximal symmetries {#Maximal_symmetries}

There are two discrete point groups with the property that no discrete point group has it as proper subgroup: *O*<sub>h</sub> and *I*<sub>h</sub>. Their largest common subgroup is *T*<sub>h</sub>. The two groups are obtained from it by changing 2-fold rotational symmetry to 4-fold, and adding 5-fold symmetry, respectively.

There are two crystallographic point groups with the property that no crystallographic point group has it as proper subgroup: *O*<sub>h</sub> and *D*<sub>6h</sub>. Their maximal common subgroups, depending on orientation, are *D*<sub>3d</sub> and *D*<sub>2h</sub>.

## The groups arranged by abstract group type {#The_groups_arranged_by_abstract_group_type}

Below the groups explained above are arranged by abstract group type.

The smallest abstract groups that are *not* any symmetry group in 3D, are the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) (of order 8), Z<sub>3</sub> × Z<sub>3</sub> (of order 9), the [dicyclic group](wikipedia://en/154963) Dic<sub>3</sub> (of order 12), and 10 of the 14 groups of order 16.

The column "# of order 2 elements" in the following tables shows the total number of isometry subgroups of types *C*<sub>2</sub>, *C*<sub>i</sub>, *C*<sub>s</sub>. This total number is one of the characteristics helping to distinguish the various abstract group types, while their isometry type helps to distinguish the various isometry groups of the same abstract group.

Within the possibilities of isometry groups in 3D, there are infinitely many abstract group types with 0, 1 and 3 elements of order 2, there are two with 4*n* + 1 elements of order 2, and there are three with 4*n* + 3 elements of order 2 (for each *n* ≥ 8 ). There is never a positive even number of elements of order 2.

### Symmetry groups in 3D that are dihedral as abstract group {#Symmetry_groups_in_3D_that_are_dihedral_as_abstract_group}

In 2D [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) *D*<sub>*n*</sub> includes reflections, which can also be viewed as flipping over flat objects without distinction of front- and backside.

However, in 3D the two operations are distinguished: the symmetry group denoted by *D*<sub>*n*</sub> contains *n* 2-fold axes perpendicular to the *n*-fold axis, not reflections. *D*<sub>*n*</sub> is the [rotation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29) of the *n*-sided [prism](wikipedia://en/283007) with regular base, and *n*-sided [bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153) with regular base, and also of a regular, *n*-sided [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851) and of a regular, *n*-sided [trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/719845). The group is also the full symmetry group of such objects after making them [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160) by an identical chiral marking on every face, for example, or some modification in the shape.

The abstract group type is [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) Dih<sub>*n*</sub>, which is also denoted by *D*<sub>*n*</sub>. However, there are three more infinite series of symmetry groups with this abstract group type:

- *C*<sub>*n*v</sub> of order 2*n*, the symmetry group of a regular *n*-sided [pyramid](wikipedia://en/3307045)
- *D*<sub>*n*d</sub> of order 4*n*, the symmetry group of a regular *n*-sided [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851)
- *D*<sub>*n*h</sub> of order 4*n* for odd *n*. For *n* = 1 we get *D*<sub>2</sub>, already covered above, so *n* ≥ 3.

Note the following property:

Dih<sub>4*n*+2</sub> $\cong$ Dih<sub>2*n*+1</sub> × Z<sub>2</sub>

Thus we have, with bolding of the 12 crystallographic point groups, and writing *D*<sub>1d</sub> as the equivalent *C*<sub>2h</sub>:

## Fundamental domain {#Fundamental_domain}

*Disdyakis triacontahedron*

The [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) of a point group is a [conic solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_solid). An object with a given symmetry in a given orientation is characterized by the fundamental domain. If the object is a surface it is characterized by a surface in the fundamental domain continuing to its radial bordal faces or surface. If the copies of the surface do not fit, radial faces or surfaces can be added. They fit anyway if the fundamental domain is bounded by reflection planes.

For a polyhedron this surface in the fundamental domain can be part of an arbitrary plane. For example, in the [disdyakis triacontahedron](wikipedia://en/725111) one full face is a fundamental domain of [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844). Adjusting the orientation of the plane gives various possibilities of combining two or more adjacent faces to one, giving various other polyhedra with the same symmetry. The polyhedron is convex if the surface fits to its copies and the radial line perpendicular to the plane is in the fundamental domain.

Also the surface in the fundamental domain may be composed of multiple faces.

## Binary polyhedral groups {#Binary_polyhedral_groups}

The map Spin(3) → SO(3) is the double cover of the rotation group by the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231) in 3 dimensions. (This is the only connected cover of SO(3), since Spin(3) is simply connected.) By the [lattice theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theorem), there is a [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) between subgroups of Spin(3) and subgroups of SO(3) (rotational point groups): the image of a subgroup of Spin(3) is a rotational point group, and the preimage of a point group is a subgroup of Spin(3). (Note that Spin(3) has alternative descriptions as the special unitary group [SU(2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%282%29) and as the group of [unit quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions). Topologically, this [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) is the [3-dimensional sphere **S**<sup>3</sup>](wikipedia://en/39792).)

The preimage of a finite point group is called a **binary polyhedral group**, presented as ⟨*l*,*m*,*n*⟩, and is called by the same name as its point group, with the prefix **binary**, with double the order of the related [polyhedral group](wikipedia://en/24192415) (*l*,*m*,*n*). For instance, the preimage of the [icosahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_group) (2,3,5) is the [binary icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/3195145), ⟨2,3,5⟩.

The binary polyhedral groups are:

- $A_n$: [binary cyclic group](wikipedia://en/14423377) of an (*n* + 1)-gon, order 2*n*
- $D_n$: [binary dihedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_dihedral_group) of an *n*-gon, ⟨2,2,*n*⟩, order 4*n*
- $E_6$: [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586), ⟨2,3,3⟩, order 24
- $E_7$: [binary octahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997598), ⟨2,3,4⟩, order 48
- $E_8$: [binary icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/3195145), ⟨2,3,5⟩, order 120

These are classified by the [ADE classification](wikipedia://en/648042), and the quotient of **C**<sup>2</sup> by the action of a binary polyhedral group is a [Du Val singularity](wikipedia://en/22921783).[^o40]

For point groups that reverse orientation, the situation is more complicated, as there are two [pin groups](wikipedia://en/1794902), so there are two possible binary groups corresponding to a given point group.

Note that this is a covering of *groups,* not a covering of *spaces* – the sphere is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected), and thus has no [covering spaces](wikipedia://en/250323). There is thus no notion of a "binary polyhedron" that covers a 3-dimensional polyhedron. Binary polyhedral groups are discrete subgroups of a Spin group, and under a representation of the spin group act on a vector space, and may stabilize a polyhedron in this representation – under the map Spin(3) → SO(3) they act on the same polyhedron that the underlying (non-binary) group acts on, while under [spin representations](wikipedia://en/10160091) or other representations they may stabilize other polyhedra.

This is in contrast to [projective polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_polyhedra) – the sphere does cover [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) (and also [lens spaces](wikipedia://en/885725)), and thus a tessellation of projective space or lens space yields a distinct notion of polyhedron.

:::
