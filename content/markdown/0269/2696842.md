# Milnor map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Milnor map"}

In mathematics, **Milnor maps** are named in honor of [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632), who introduced them to [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) in his book ***Singular Points of Complex Hypersurfaces*** ([Princeton University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University_Press), 1968) and earlier lectures. The most studied Milnor maps are actually [fibrations](wikipedia://en/316826), and the phrase **Milnor fibration** is more commonly encountered in the mathematical literature. These were introduced to study isolated singularities by constructing numerical [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638) related to the topology of a smooth [deformation](wikipedia://en/679351) of the singular space.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $f(z_0,\dots,z_n)$ be a non-constant [polynomial function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function) of $n+1$ [complex variables](wikipedia://en/5759) $z_0,\dots,z_n$ where the vanishing locus of

$$f(z)\ \text{ and }\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i}(z)$$

is only at the origin, meaning the associated [variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $X = V(f)$ is not [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_variety) at the origin. Then, for $K = X \cap S^{2n+1}_{\varepsilon}$ (a sphere inside $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$ of radius $\varepsilon > 0$) the **Milnor fibration**[^o4]<sup>pg 68</sup> associated to $f$ is defined as the map

$$\phi\colon (S_\varepsilon^{2n+1}\setminus K) \to S^1\ \text{ sending } \ x \mapsto \frac{f(x)}{|f(x)|},$$

which is a locally trivial [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map) [fibration](wikipedia://en/316826) for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$. Originally this was proven as a theorem by Milnor, but was later taken as the definition of a Milnor fibration. Note this is a well defined map since

$$f(x) = |f(x)|\cdot e^{2\pi i \operatorname{Arg}(f(x))},$$

where $\operatorname{Arg}(f(x))$ is the [argument of a complex number](wikipedia://en/14699765).

### Historical motivation {#Historical_motivation}

One of the original motivations for studying such maps was in the study of [knots](wikipedia://en/153008) constructed by taking an $\varepsilon$-ball around a singular point of a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691), which is isomorphic to a real 4-dimensional ball, and looking at the knot inside the boundary, which is a 1-[manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) inside of a 3-[sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). Since this concept could be generalized to [hypersurfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_hypersurface) with isolated singularities, Milnor introduced the subject and proved his theorem.

### In algebraic geometry {#In_algebraic_geometry}

Another closed related notion in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) is the Milnor fiber of an isolated hypersurface singularity. This has a similar setup, where a polynomial $f$ with $f = 0$ having a singularity at the origin, but now the polynomial

$$f_t\colon \mathbb{C}^{n+1} \to \mathbb{C}\ \text{ sending }\ (z_0,\ldots,z_n)\mapsto f(z_0,\ldots,z_n) - t$$

is considered. Then, the **algebraic Milnor fiber** is taken as one of the polynomials $f_{t\neq 0}$.

## Properties and Theorems {#Properties_and_Theorems}

### Parallelizability {#Parallelizability}

One of the basic structure theorems about Milnor fibers is they are [parallelizable manifolds](wikipedia://en/3108161)[^o4]<sup>pg 75</sup>.

### Homotopy type {#Homotopy_type}

Milnor fibers are special because they have the [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type) of a [bouquet of spheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouquet_of_spheres)[^o4]<sup>pg 78</sup>. The number of these spheres is the [Milnor number](wikipedia://en/18882483). In fact, the number of spheres can be computed using the formula

$$\mu(f) = \text{dim}_\mathbb{C}\frac{\mathbb{C}[[z_0,\ldots,z_n]]}{\operatorname{Jac}(f)},$$

where the quotient ideal is the [Jacobian ideal](wikipedia://en/29321367), defined by the partial derivatives $\partial f / \partial z_i$. These spheres deformed to the algebraic Milnor fiber are the [Vanishing cycles](wikipedia://en/5859204) of the fibration[^o4]<sup>pg 83</sup>. Computing the eigenvalues of their monodromy is computationally challenging and requires advanced techniques such as [b-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-function)[^o11]<sup>pg 23</sup>.

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Milnor's fibration theorem"}

### Milnor's fibration theorem {#Milnor's_fibration_theorem}

Milnor's Fibration Theorem states that, for every $f$ such that the origin is a [singular point](wikipedia://en/4230456) of the hypersurface $V_f$ (in particular, for every non-constant [square-free polynomial](wikipedia://en/3816655) $f$ of two variables, the case of plane curves), then for $\epsilon$ sufficiently small,

$$\dfrac{f}{|f|}\colon \left(S^{2n+1}_{\varepsilon} \setminus V_f \right) \to S^1$$

is a fibration. Each fiber is a non-compact [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) of real dimension $2n$. Note that the closure of each fiber is a compact [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) with boundary. Here the boundary corresponds to the intersection of $V_f$ with the $(2n+1)$-sphere (of sufficiently small radius) and therefore it is a real manifold of dimension $(2n-1)$. Furthermore, this compact manifold with boundary, which is known as the *Milnor fiber* (of the isolated singular point of $V_f$ at the origin), is diffeomorphic to the intersection of the closed $(2n+2)$-ball (bounded by the small $(2n+1)$-sphere) with the (non-singular) hypersurface $V_g$ where $g=f-e$ and $e$ is any sufficiently small non-zero [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826). This small piece of hypersurface is also called a *Milnor fiber*.

Milnor maps at other radii are not always fibrations, but they still have many interesting properties. For most (but not all) polynomials, the **Milnor map at infinity** (that is, at any sufficiently large radius) is again a fibration.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

The Milnor map of $f(z,w)=z^2+w^3$ at any radius is a fibration; this construction gives the [trefoil knot](wikipedia://en/404773) its structure as a [fibered knot](wikipedia://en/1358592).

:::
