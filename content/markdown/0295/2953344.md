# Stagnation pressure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stagnation pressure"}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), **stagnation pressure**, also referred to as **total pressure**, is what the pressure would be if all the kinetic energy of the fluid were to be converted into pressure in a reversible manner.;[^o3] it is defined as the sum of the free-stream [static pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_pressure) and the free-stream [dynamic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_pressure).[^o2]

:::

The Bernoulli equation applicable to [incompressible flow](wikipedia://en/461477) shows that the stagnation pressure is equal to the dynamic pressure and static pressure combined.[^o3] In [compressible flows](wikipedia://en/219144), stagnation pressure is also equal to total pressure as well, provided that the fluid entering the stagnation point is brought to rest [isentropically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isentropic_flow).[^o3]

Stagnation pressure is sometimes referred to as [pitot pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitot_pressure) because the two pressures are equal.

## Magnitude {#Magnitude}

The magnitude of stagnation pressure can be derived from [Bernoulli equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli's_principle)[^o5][^o3] for [incompressible flow](wikipedia://en/461477) and no height changes. For any two points 1 and 2:

$$P_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v_1^2 = P_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v_2^2$$

The two points of interest are 1) in the freestream flow at relative speed $v$ where the pressure is called the "static" pressure, (for example well away from an airplane moving at speed $v$); and 2) at a "stagnation" point where the fluid is at rest with respect to the measuring apparatus (for example at the end of a pitot tube in an airplane).

Then

$$P_\text{static} + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2 = P_\text{stagnation} + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho (0)^2$$

or[^o6]

$$P_\text{stagnation}=P_\text{static} + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2$$

where:

$P_\text{stagnation}$ is the stagnation pressure

$\rho\;$ is the fluid density

$v$ is the speed of fluid

$P_\text{static}$ is the static pressure

So the stagnation pressure is increased over the static pressure, by the amount $\tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2$ which is called the "dynamic" or "ram" pressure because it results from fluid motion. In our airplane example, the stagnation pressure would be atmospheric pressure plus the dynamic pressure.

In [compressible flow](wikipedia://en/219144) however, the fluid density is higher at the stagnation point than at the static point. Therefore, $\tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2$ can't be used for the dynamic pressure. For many purposes in compressible flow, the stagnation [enthalpy](wikipedia://en/10274) or [stagnation temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagnation_temperature) plays a role similar to the stagnation pressure in incompressible flow.[^o7]

## Compressible flow {#Compressible_flow}

Stagnation pressure is the static pressure a gas retains when brought to rest [isentropically](wikipedia://en/375900) from [Mach number](wikipedia://en/20051) *M*.[^o9]

$$\frac{p_t}{p} = \left(1 + \frac{\gamma -1}{2} M^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\,$$

or, assuming an [isentropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isentropic) process, the stagnation pressure can be calculated from the ratio of stagnation temperature to static temperature:

$$\frac{p_t}{p} = \left(\frac{T_t}{T}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\,$$

where:

$p_t$ is the stagnation pressure

$p$ is the static pressure

$T_t$ is the stagnation temperature

$T$ is the static temperature

$\gamma$ is the [ratio of specific heats](wikipedia://en/1949009)

The above derivation holds only for the case when the gas is assumed to be [calorically perfect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_gas) (specific heats and the ratio of the specific heats $\gamma$ are assumed to be constant with temperature).
