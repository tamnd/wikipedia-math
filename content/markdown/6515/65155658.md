# Sincere favorite criterion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sincere favorite criterion"}

The **sincere favorite** or **no favorite-betrayal criterion** is a [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_system_criterion) of some voting systems that says voters should have no incentive to vote for someone else over their favorite.[^o2] It protects voters from having to engage in [lesser-evil voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser-evil_voting) or a strategy called "decapitation" (removing the "head" off a ballot).[^o3]

:::

Most [rated voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rated_voting) systems satisfy the criterion while most [ranked voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranked_voting_systems) systems fail this criterion. [^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7][^o8] Lesser-evil-voting is particularly prevalent in [plurality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurality_voting)-based voting systems like [ranked choice voting (RCV)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting),[^o7][^o9][^o10] [traditional runoffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-round_system),[^o5] [partisan primaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partisan_primary), and [first-past-the-post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-past-the-post_voting).[^o5] Lesser-evil voting is typically associated with [center-squeeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center-squeeze) in these systems.

[Duverger's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duverger's_law) says that systems vulnerable to this strategy will typically (though not always) develop [two party-systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-party_system), as voters will abandon minor-party candidates to support stronger major-party candidates.[^o11]

::: {#o13 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A voting rule satisfies the sincere favorite criterion if there is never a need to "betray" a perfect candidate—i.e. if a voter will never achieve a worse result by honestly ranking their favorite candidate first.[^o2]

:::

## Arguments for {#Arguments_for}

The [Center for Election Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Center_for_Election_Science) argues systems that violate the favorite betrayal criterion strongly incentivize voters to cast dishonest ballots, which can make voters feel unsatisfied or frustrated with the results despite having the opportunity to participate in the election.[^o15][^o16][^o17][^o18]

Other commentators have argued that failing the favorite-betrayal criterion can increase the effectiveness of [misinformation campaigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misinformation), by allowing major-party candidates to sow doubt as to whether voting honestly for one's favorite is actually the best strategy.[^o19]

## Compliant methods {#Compliant_methods}

### Rated voting {#Rated_voting}

Because [rated voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rated_voting) methods are not affected by [Arrow's theorem](wikipedia://en/89425), they can be both [spoilerproof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoilerproof) (satisfy [IIA](wikipedia://en/259105)) and ensure [positive vote weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_response) at the same time. Taken together, these properties imply that increasing the rating of a favorite candidate can never change the result, *except* by causing the favorite candidate to win; therefore, giving a favorite candidate the maximum level of support is always the optimal strategy.

Examples of systems that are both spoilerproof and monotonic include [score voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Score_voting), [approval voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approval_voting), and [highest medians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_median_voting_rules).

### Anti-plurality voting {#Anti-plurality_voting}

Interpreted as a ranked voting method where every candidate but the last ranked gets one point, [anti-plurality voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-plurality_voting) passes the sincere favorite criterion. Because there is no incentive to rank one's favorite last, and the method otherwise does not care where the favorite is ranked, the method passes.

Anti-plurality voting thus shows that the sincere favorite criterion is distinct from [independence of irrelevant alternatives](wikipedia://en/259105), and that ranked voting methods do not necessarily fail the criterion.

## Non-compliant methods {#Non-compliant_methods}

### Instant-runoff voting {#Instant-runoff_voting}

This example shows that instant-runoff voting violates the favorite betrayal criterion. Assume there are four candidates: Amy, Bert, Cindy, and Dan. This election has 41 voters with the following preferences:

::: {#o25 .table cue=table}

| \# of voters | Preferences |
|---|---|
| **10** | **Amy > Bert > Cindy > Dan** |
| 6 | Bert > Amy > Cindy > Dan |
| 5 | Cindy > Bert > Amy > Dan |
| 20 | Dan > Amy > Cindy > Bert |

:::

#### Sincere voting {#Sincere_voting}

Assuming all voters vote in a sincere way, Cindy is awarded only 5 first place votes and is eliminated first. Her votes are transferred to Bert. In the second round, Amy is eliminated with only 10 votes. Her votes are transferred to Bert as well. Finally, Bert has 21 votes and wins against Dan, who has 20 votes.

::: {#o27 .table cue=table}

| Votes in round/ Candidate | 1st | 2nd | 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amy | 10 | *10* | – |
| Bert | 6 | 11 | **21** |
| Cindy | *5* | – | – |
| Dan | 20 | 20 | *20* |

:::

**Result**: **Bert** wins against Dan, after Cindy and Amy were eliminated.

#### Favorite betrayal {#Favorite_betrayal}

Now assume two of the voters who favor Amy (marked bold) realize the situation and insincerely vote for Cindy instead of Amy:

::: {#o29 .table cue=table}

| \# of voters | Ballots |
|---|---|
| **2** | **Cindy > Amy > Bert > Dan** |
| 8 | Amy > Bert > Cindy > Dan |
| 6 | Bert > Amy > Cindy > Dan |
| 5 | Cindy > Bert > Amy > Dan |
| 20 | Dan > Amy > Cindy > Bert |

:::

In this scenario, Cindy has 7 first place votes and so Bert is eliminated first with only 6 first place votes. His votes are transferred to Amy. In the second round, Cindy is eliminated with only 7 votes. Her votes are transferred to Amy as well. Finally, Amy has 21 votes and wins against Dan, who has 20 votes.

::: {#o30 .table cue=table}

| Votes in round/ Candidate | 1st | 2nd | 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amy | 8 | 14 | **21** |
| Bert | *6* | – | – |
| Cindy | 7 | *7* | – |
| Dan | 20 | 20 | *20* |

:::

**Result**: **Amy** wins against Dan, after Bert and Cindy has been eliminated.

By listing Cindy ahead of their true favorite, Amy, the two insincere voters obtained a more preferred outcome (causing their favorite candidate to win). There was no way to achieve this without raising another candidate ahead of their sincere favorite. Thus, instant-runoff voting fails the favorite betrayal criterion.
