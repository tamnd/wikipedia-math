# Krylov subspace

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Krylov subspace"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the order-*r* **Krylov subspace** generated by an *n*-by-*n* [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) *A* and a vector *b* of dimension *n* is the [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) [spanned](wikipedia://en/56353) by the [images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_%28matrix%29) of *b* under the first *r* powers of *A* (starting from $A^0=I$), that is,[^o2][^o3]

:::

$$\mathcal{K}_r(A,b) = \operatorname{span} \, \{ b, Ab, A^2b, \ldots, A^{r-1}b \}.$$

## Background {#Background}

The concept is named after Russian applied mathematician and naval engineer [Aleksey Krylov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksey_Krylov), who published a paper about the concept in 1931.[^o5]

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- $$\mathcal{K}_r(A,b), A\,\mathcal{K}_r(A,b)\subset \mathcal{K}_{r+1}(A,b).$$
- Let $r_0 = \operatorname{dim} \operatorname{span} \, \{ b, Ab, A^2b, \ldots \}$. Then $\{ b, Ab, A^2b, \ldots, A^{r-1}b \}$ are linearly independent unless $r>r_0$, $\mathcal{K}_r(A,b) \subset \mathcal{K}_{r_0}(A,b)$ for all $r$, and $\operatorname{dim} \mathcal{K}_{r_0}(A,b) = r_0$. So $r_0$ is the maximal dimension of the Krylov subspaces $\mathcal{K}_r(A,b)$.
- The maximal dimension satisfies $r_0\leq 1 + \operatorname{rank} A$ and $r_0 \leq n$.
- Consider $\dim \operatorname{span} \, \{ I, A, A^2, \ldots \} = \deg\,p(A)$, where $p(A)$ is the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667107) of $A$. We have $r_0\leq \deg\,p(A)$. Moreover, for any $A$, there exists a $b$ for which this bound is tight, i.e. $r_0 = \deg\,p(A)$.
- $\mathcal{K}_r(A,b)$ is a cyclic submodule generated by $b$ of the [torsion](wikipedia://en/3039330) $k[x]$-module $(k^n)^A$, where $k^n$ is the linear space on $k$.
- $k^n$ can be decomposed as the direct sum of Krylov subspaces.

:::

::: {#o9 .application cue=heading name="Use"}

## Use {#Use}

Krylov subspaces are used in algorithms for finding approximate solutions to high-dimensional [linear algebra problems](wikipedia://en/113087).[^o3] Many [linear dynamical system](wikipedia://en/4786318) tests in [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039), especially those related to [controllability](wikipedia://en/100638) and [observability](wikipedia://en/871280), involve checking the rank of the Krylov subspace. These tests are equivalent to finding the span of the [Gramians](wikipedia://en/987959) associated with the system/output maps so the uncontrollable and unobservable subspaces are simply the orthogonal complement to the Krylov subspace.[^o10]

Modern [iterative methods](wikipedia://en/15237) such as [Arnoldi iteration](wikipedia://en/1134614) can be used for finding one (or a few) eigenvalues of large [sparse matrices](wikipedia://en/341015) or solving large systems of linear equations. They try to avoid matrix-matrix operations, but rather multiply vectors by the matrix and work with the resulting vectors. Starting with a vector $b$, one computes $A b$, then one multiplies that vector by $A$ to find $A^2 b$ and so on. All algorithms that work this way are referred to as Krylov subspace methods; they are among the most successful methods currently available in numerical linear algebra. These methods can be used in situations where there is an algorithm to compute the matrix-vector multiplication without there being an explicit representation of $A$, giving rise to [matrix-free methods](wikipedia://en/8515187).

:::

## Issues {#Issues}

Because the vectors usually soon become almost [linearly dependent](wikipedia://en/101863) due to the properties of [power iteration](wikipedia://en/5975550), methods relying on Krylov subspace frequently involve some [orthogonalization](wikipedia://en/201611) scheme, such as [Lanczos iteration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanczos_iteration) for [Hermitian matrices](wikipedia://en/189682) or [Arnoldi iteration](wikipedia://en/1134614) for more general matrices.

## Existing methods {#Existing_methods}

The best known Krylov subspace methods are the [Conjugate gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_gradient), [IDR(s)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDR%28s%29) (Induced dimension reduction), [GMRES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GMRES) (generalized minimum residual), [BiCGSTAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BiCGSTAB) (biconjugate gradient stabilized), [QMR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi_minimal_residual) (quasi minimal residual), [TFQMR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TFQMR) (transpose-free QMR) and [MINRES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MINRES) (minimal residual method).
