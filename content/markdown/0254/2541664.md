# Equidistribution theorem

*Image: equidistribution\_theorem.svg.* Illustration of filling the unit interval (horizontal axis) with the first *n* terms using the equidistribution theorem with four common irrational numbers, for *n* from 0 to 999 (vertical axis). The 113 distinct bands for $\pi$ are due to the closeness of its value to the rational number 355/113. Similarly, the 7 distinct groups are due to $\pi$ being approximately 22/7.\[ (click for detailed view)\]

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equidistribution theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **equidistribution theorem** is the statement that the sequence

:::

*a*, 2*a*, 3*a*, ... mod 1

is [uniformly distributed](wikipedia://en/1030420) on the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$, when *a* is an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689). It is a special case of the [ergodic theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_theorem) where one takes the normalized angle measure $\mu=\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}$.

## History {#History}

While this theorem was proved in 1909 and 1910 separately by [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), [Wacław Sierpiński](wikipedia://en/34122) and [Piers Bohl](wikipedia://en/13428603), variants of this theorem continue to be studied to this day.

In 1916, Weyl proved that the sequence *a*, 2<sup>2</sup>*a*, 3<sup>2</sup>*a*, ... mod 1 is uniformly distributed on the unit interval. In 1937, [Ivan Vinogradov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Vinogradov) proved that the sequence *p*<sub>*n*</sub> *a* mod 1 is uniformly distributed, where *p*<sub>*n*</sub> is the *n*th [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). Vinogradov's proof was a byproduct of the [odd Goldbach conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_Goldbach_conjecture), that every sufficiently large odd number is the sum of three primes.

[George Birkhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Birkhoff), in 1931, and [Aleksandr Khinchin](wikipedia://en/1029748), in 1933, proved that the generalization *x* + *na*, for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) *x*, is equidistributed on any [Lebesgue measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_measurable) subset of the unit interval. The corresponding generalizations for the Weyl and Vinogradov results were proven by [Jean Bourgain](wikipedia://en/1024131) in 1988.

Specifically, Khinchin showed that the identity

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n f( (x+ka) \bmod 1 ) = \int_0^1 f(y)\,dy$$

holds for almost all *x* and any Lebesgue integrable function ƒ. In modern formulations, it is asked under what conditions the identity

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^n f( (x+b_ka) \bmod 1 ) = \int_0^1 f(y)\,dy$$

might hold, given some general [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) *b*<sub>*k*</sub>.

One noteworthy result is that the sequence 2<sup>*k*</sup>*a* mod 1 is uniformly distributed for almost all, but not all, irrational *a*. Similarly, for the sequence *b*<sub>*k*</sub> = 2<sup>*k*</sup>a, for every irrational *a*, and almost all *x*, there exists a function ƒ for which the sum diverges. In this sense, this sequence is considered to be a **universally bad averaging sequence**, as opposed to *b*<sub>*k*</sub> = *k*, which is termed a **universally good averaging sequence**, because it does not have the latter shortcoming.

A powerful general result is [Weyl's criterion](wikipedia://en/1030420#Weyl's_criterion), which shows that equidistribution is equivalent to having a non-trivial estimate for the [exponential sums](wikipedia://en/468937) formed with the sequence as exponents. For the case of multiples of *a*, Weyl's criterion reduces the problem to summing finite [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630).
