# Zero-sum problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zero-sum problem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **zero-sum problems** are certain kinds of [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial) problems about the structure of a [finite abelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_abelian_group). Concretely, given a finite abelian group *G* and a positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n*, one asks for the smallest value of *k* such that every sequence of elements of *G* of size *k* contains *n* terms that sum to [0](wikipedia://en/14962).

:::

The classic result in this area is the 1961 theorem of [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284), [Abraham Ginzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Ginzburg), and [Abraham Ziv](wikipedia://en/45030219).[^o2] They proved that for the group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ of integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) *n*,

$$k = 2n - 1.$$

Explicitly this says that any [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) of 2*n* − 1 integers has a subset of size *n* the sum of whose elements is a multiple of *n*, but that the same is not true of multisets of size 2*n* − 2. (Indeed, the lower bound is easy to see: the multiset containing *n* − 1 copies of 0 and *n* − 1 copies of 1 contains no *n*-subset summing to a multiple of *n*.) This result is known as the **Erdős–Ginzburg–Ziv theorem** after its discoverers. It may also be deduced from the [Cauchy–Davenport theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy–Davenport_theorem).[^o3]

More general results than this theorem exist, such as [Olson's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olson's_theorem), [Kemnitz's conjecture](wikipedia://en/22407758) (proved by [Christian Reiher](wikipedia://en/20113579) in 2003[^o4]), and the [weighted EGZ theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_EGZ_theorem) (proved by [David J. Grynkiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_J._Grynkiewicz) in 2005[^o5]).
