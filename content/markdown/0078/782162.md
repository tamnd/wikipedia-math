# Cone (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cone"}

*Image: Cone.svg.* Cone of a circle. The original space *X* is in blue, and the collapsed end point *v* is in green.

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), especially [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the **cone** **of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450)** $X$ is intuitively obtained by stretching *X* into a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) and then collapsing one of its end faces to a point. The cone of X is denoted by $CX$ or by $\operatorname{cone}(X)$.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Formally, the cone of *X* is defined as:

$$CX = (X \times [0,1])\cup_p v\ =\ \varinjlim \bigl( (X \times [0,1]) \hookleftarrow (X\times \{0\}) \xrightarrow{p} v\bigr),$$

where $v$ is a [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28topology%29) (called the vertex of the cone) and $p$ is the [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) to that point. In other words, it is the result of [attaching](wikipedia://en/1240842) the [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) $X \times [0,1]$ by its face $X\times\{0\}$ to a point $v$ along the projection $p: \bigl( X\times\{0\} \bigr)\to v$.

If $X$ is a non-empty [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subspace of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the cone on $X$ is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of segments from $X$ to any fixed point $v \not\in X$ such that these segments intersect only in $v$ itself. That is, the topological cone agrees with the [geometric cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_cone) for compact spaces when the latter is defined. However, the topological cone construction is more general.

The cone is a special case of a [join](wikipedia://en/3127114): $CX \simeq X\star \{v\} =$ the join of $X$ with a single point $v\not\in X$.*[^o6]*p. 76

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Here we often use a [geometric cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_cone) ($C X$ where $X$ is a non-empty [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subspace of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)). The considered spaces are compact, so we get the same result up to homeomorphism.

- The cone over a point *p* of the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) is a line-segment in $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\{p\} \times [0,1]$.
- The cone over two points {0, 1} is a "V" shape with endpoints at {0} and {1}.
- The cone over a [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) *I* of the real line is a filled-in [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) (with one of the edges being *I*), otherwise known as a 2-simplex (see the final example).
- The cone over a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) *P* is a pyramid with base *P*.
- The cone over a [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) is the solid [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) of classical geometry (hence the concept's name).
- The cone over a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) given by

$$\{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x^2 + y^2 = 1 \mbox{ and } z=0\}$$

is the curved surface of the solid cone:

$$\{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x^2 + y^2 = (z-1)^2 \mbox{ and } 0\leq z\leq 1\}.$$

This in turn is homeomorphic to the closed [disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disc_%28mathematics%29).

More general examples:*[^o6]*p. 77

- The cone over an [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) is homeomorphic to the closed (*n* + 1)-[ball](wikipedia://en/160556).
- The cone over an [*n*-ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-ball) is also homeomorphic to the closed (*n* + 1)-[ball](wikipedia://en/160556).
- The cone over an *n*-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) is an (*n* + 1)-simplex.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

All cones are [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected_space) since every point can be connected to the vertex point. Furthermore, every cone is [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible) to the vertex point by the [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250)

$$h_t(x,s) = (x, (1-t)s).$$

The cone is used in algebraic topology precisely because it [embeds](wikipedia://en/52119) a space as a [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of a contractible space.

When *X* is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) (essentially, when *X* can be embedded in Euclidean space), then the cone $CX$ can be visualized as the collection of lines joining every point of *X* to a single point. However, this picture fails when *X* is not compact or not Hausdorff, as generally the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) on $CX$ will be [finer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finer_topology) than the set of lines joining *X* to a point.

:::

## Cone functor {#Cone_functor}

The map $X\mapsto CX$ induces a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) $C\colon \mathbf{Top}\to\mathbf{Top}$ on the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) **Top**. If $f \colon X \to Y$ is a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29), then $Cf \colon CX \to CY$ is defined by

$$(Cf)([x,t])=[f(x),t],$$

where square brackets denote [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260).

## Reduced cone {#Reduced_cone}

If $(X,x_0)$ is a [pointed space](wikipedia://en/1236666), there is a related construction, the **reduced cone**, given by

$$(X\times [0,1]) / (X\times \left\{0\right\} \cup\left\{x_0\right\}\times [0,1])$$

where we take the basepoint of the reduced cone to be the equivalence class of $(x_0,0)$. With this definition, the natural inclusion $x\mapsto (x,1)$ becomes a based map. This construction also gives a functor, from the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of pointed spaces to itself.
