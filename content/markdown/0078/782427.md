# Cone

*Image: Cone 3d.png.* A right circular cone and an oblique circular cone

*Image: DoubleCone.png.* A double cone, not infinitely extended

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Cone"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **cone** is a [three-dimensional figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_figure) that tapers smoothly from a [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) base (typically a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220)) to a point not contained in the base, called the *[apex](wikipedia://en/6022079)* or *[vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417)*.

:::

A cone is formed by a set of [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860), [half-lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29), or [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) connecting a common point, the apex, to all of the points on a base. In the case of line segments, the cone does not extend beyond the base, while in the case of half-lines, it extends infinitely far. In the case of lines, the cone extends infinitely far in both directions from the apex, in which case it is sometimes called a *double cone*. Each of the two halves of a double cone split at the apex is called a *nappe*.

Depending on the author, the base may be restricted to a circle, any one-dimensional [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) in the plane, any closed [one-dimensional figure](wikipedia://en/25584215), or any of the above plus all the enclosed points. If the enclosed points are included in the base, the cone is a [solid object](wikipedia://en/507960); otherwise it is an [open surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_surface), a [two-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional) object in three-dimensional space. In the case of a solid object, the boundary formed by these lines or partial lines is called the *lateral surface*; if the lateral surface is [unbounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbounded_set), it is a *[conical surface](wikipedia://en/706244)*.

The [axis](wikipedia://en/701096) of a cone is the straight line passing through the apex about which the cone has a [circular symmetry](wikipedia://en/2217599). In common usage in elementary geometry, cones are assumed to be *right circular*, i.e., with a circle base [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the axis.[^o3] If the cone is right circular the intersection of a plane with the lateral surface is a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673). In general, however, the base may be any shape[^o4] and the apex may lie anywhere (though it is usually assumed that the base is bounded and therefore has finite [area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area_%28geometry%29), and that the apex lies outside the plane of the base). Contrasted with right cones are *oblique cones*, in which the axis passes through the centre of the base non-perpendicularly.

Depending on context, *cone* may refer more narrowly to either a [convex cone](wikipedia://en/3558266) or [projective cone](wikipedia://en/4068867). Cones can be generalized to [higher dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398#Additional_dimensions).

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Elliptical cone"}

## Further terminology {#Further_terminology}

The perimeter of the base of a cone is called the *directrix*, and each of the line segments between the directrix and apex is a *generatrix* or *generating line* of the lateral surface. (For the connection between this sense of the term *directrix* and the [directrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directrix_%28conic_section%29) of a conic section, see [Dandelin spheres](wikipedia://en/683726).)

The *base radius* of a circular cone is the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of its base; often this is simply called the radius of the cone. The *aperture* of a right circular cone is the maximum angle between two generatrix lines; if the generatrix makes an angle *θ* to the axis, the aperture is 2*θ*. In [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), the angle *θ* is called the *half-angle* of the cone, to distinguish it from the aperture.

*Image: Acta Eruditorum - I geometria, 1734 – BEIC 13446956.jpg.* Illustration from *Problemata mathematica...* published in [Acta Eruditorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acta_Eruditorum), 1734

*Image: Cut cone unparallel.JPG.* A cone truncated by an inclined plane

A cone with a region including its apex cut off by a plane is called a *truncated cone*; if the [truncation](wikipedia://en/4224429) plane is parallel to the cone's base, it is called a *[frustum](wikipedia://en/89405)*.[^o3] An *[elliptical cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_cone)* is a cone with an [elliptical](wikipedia://en/9277) base.[^o3] A *generalized cone* is the surface created by the set of lines passing through a vertex and every point on a boundary (see [Visual hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_hull)).

:::

## Measurements and equations {#Measurements_and_equations}

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Volume of a cone"}

### Volume {#Volume}

*Image: visual\_proof\_cone\_volume.svg.* }

The [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) $V$ of any conic solid, regardless of the shape of its base, is one third of the product of the area of the base $A_B$ and the height $h$[^o11]

$$V = \frac{1}{3}A_B h.$$

In modern mathematics, this formula can easily be computed using calculus — if $A_B = k \cdot h$, where $k$ is a coefficient, the integral

$$\int_0^{h} k x^2 \, dx = \tfrac{1}{3} k h^3$$

Without using calculus, the formula can be proven by comparing the cone to a pyramid and applying [Cavalieri's principle](wikipedia://en/21697672) – specifically, comparing the cone to a (vertically scaled) right square pyramid, which forms one third of a cube. This formula cannot be proven without using such infinitesimal arguments – unlike the 2-dimensional formulae for polyhedral area, though similar to the area of the circle – and hence admitted less rigorous proofs before the advent of calculus, with the ancient Greeks using the [method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284). This is essentially the content of [Hilbert's third problem](wikipedia://en/152703) – more precisely, not all polyhedral pyramids are *scissors congruent* (can be cut apart into finite pieces and rearranged into the other), and thus volume cannot be computed purely by using a decomposition argument.[^o12]

:::

### Center of mass {#Center_of_mass}

The [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) of a conic solid of uniform density lies one-quarter of the way from the center of the base to the vertex, on the straight line joining the two.

### Right circular cone {#Right_circular_cone}

#### Volume {#Volume}

For a circular cone with radius $r$ and height $h$, the base is a circle of area $\pi r^2$ thus the formula for volume is:[^o16]

$$V = \frac{1}{3} \pi r^2 h$$

::: {#o18 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Slant height"}

#### Slant height {#Slant_height}

The [slant height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slant_height) of a right circular cone is the distance from any point on the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) of its base to the apex via a line segment along the surface of the cone. It is given by $\sqrt{r^2+h^2}$, where $r$ is the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the base and $h$ is the height. This can be proved by the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).

:::

::: {#o20 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Surface area of a cone"}

#### Surface area {#Surface_area}

The [lateral surface](wikipedia://en/16402429) area of a right circular cone is $LSA = \pi r \ell$ where $r$ is the radius of the circle at the bottom of the cone and $\ell$ is the slant height of the cone.[^o21] The surface area of the bottom circle of a cone is the same as for any circle, $\pi r^2$. Thus, the total surface area of a right circular cone can be expressed as each of the following:

- Radius and height

$$\pi r^2+\pi r \sqrt{r^2+h^2}$$

(the area of the base plus the area of the lateral surface; the term $\sqrt{r^2+h^2}$ is the slant height)

$$\pi r \left(r + \sqrt{r^2+h^2}\right)$$

where $r$ is the radius and $h$ is the height.

*Image: Cone\_surface\_area.svg.* Total surface area of a right circular cone, given radius 𝑟 and slant height ℓ

- Radius and slant height

$$\pi r^2+\pi r \ell$$

$$\pi r(r+\ell)$$

where $r$ is the radius and $\ell$ is the slant height.

- Circumference and slant height

$$\frac {c^2} {4 \pi} + \frac {c\ell} 2$$

$$\left(\frac c 2\right)\left(\frac c {2\pi} + \ell\right)$$

where $c$ is the circumference and $\ell$ is the slant height.

- Apex angle and height

$$\pi h^2 \tan \frac{\theta}{2} \left(\tan \frac{\theta}{2} + \sec \frac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

$$-\frac{\pi h^2 \sin \frac{\theta}{2}}{\sin \frac{\theta}{2}-1}$$

where $\theta$ is the apex angle and $h$ is the height.

:::

#### Circular sector {#Circular_sector}

The [circular sector](wikipedia://en/1967249) is obtained by unfolding the surface of one nappe of the cone:

- radius *R*

$$R = \sqrt{r^2+h^2}$$

- arc length *L*

$$L = c = 2\pi r$$

- central angle *φ* in radians

$$\varphi = \frac{L}{R} = \frac{2\pi r}{\sqrt{r^2+h^2}}$$

#### Equation form {#Equation_form}

The surface of a cone can be parameterized as

$$f(\theta,h) = (h \cos\theta, h \sin\theta, h ),$$

where $\theta \in [0,2\pi)$ is the angle "around" the cone, and $h \in \mathbb{R}$ is the "height" along the cone.

A right solid circular cone with height $h$ and aperture $2\theta$, whose axis is the $z$ coordinate axis and whose apex is the origin, is described parametrically as

$$F(s,t,u) = \left(u \tan s \cos t, u \tan s \sin t, u \right)$$

where $s,t,u$ range over $[0,\theta)$, $[0,2\pi)$, and $[0,h]$, respectively.

In [implicit](wikipedia://en/250896) form, the same solid is defined by the inequalities

$$\{ F(x,y,z) \leq 0, z\geq 0, z\leq h\},$$

where

$$F(x,y,z) = (x^2 + y^2)(\cos\theta)^2 - z^2 (\sin \theta)^2.\,$$

More generally, a right circular cone with vertex at the origin, axis parallel to the vector $d$, and aperture $2\theta$, is given by the implicit [vector](wikipedia://en/32640) equation $F(u) = 0$ where

$$F(u) = (u \cdot d)^2 - (d \cdot d) (u \cdot u) (\cos \theta)^2$$

$$F(u) = u \cdot d - |d| |u| \cos \theta$$

where $u=(x,y,z)$, and $u \cdot d$ denotes the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093).

## Projective geometry {#Projective_geometry}

*Image: Australia Square building in George Street Sydney.jpg.* In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) is simply a cone whose apex is at infinity, which corresponds visually to a cylinder in perspective appearing to be a cone towards the sky.

In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) is simply a cone whose apex is at infinity.[^o25] Intuitively, if one keeps the base fixed and takes the limit as the apex goes to infinity, one obtains a cylinder, the angle of the side increasing as [arctan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctan), in the limit forming a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956). This is useful in the definition of [degenerate conics](wikipedia://en/392586), which require considering the [cylindrical conics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_conic).

According to [G. B. Halsted](wikipedia://en/3992708), a cone is generated similarly to a [Steiner conic](wikipedia://en/46462753) only with a projectivity and [axial pencils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pencil_%28mathematics%29) (not in perspective) rather than the projective ranges used for the Steiner conic:

"If two copunctual non-costraight axial pencils are projective but not perspective, the meets of correlated planes form a 'conic surface of the second order', or 'cone'."[^o26]

::: {#o28 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The definition of a cone may be extended to higher dimensions; see [convex cone](wikipedia://en/3558266). In this case, one says that a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) *C* in the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a cone (with apex at the origin) if for every vector *x* in *C* and every nonnegative real number *a*, the vector *ax* is in *C*.[^o4] In this context, the analogues of circular cones are not usually special; in fact one is often interested in [polyhedral cones](wikipedia://en/3558266#Polyhedral_and_finitely_generated_cones).

An even more general concept is the [topological cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_cone), which is defined in arbitrary topological spaces.

:::
