# Continuous functional calculus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Continuous functional calculus"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675) and [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184) theory, the **continuous functional calculus** is a [functional calculus](wikipedia://en/965348) which allows the application of a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) to [normal elements](wikipedia://en/21454688) of a C\*-algebra.

:::

In advanced theory, the applications of this functional calculus are so natural that they are often not even mentioned. It is no overstatement to say that the continuous functional calculus makes *the* difference between C\*-algebras and general [Banach algebras](wikipedia://en/4665), in which only a [holomorphic functional calculus](wikipedia://en/3131706) exists.

## Motivation {#Motivation}

If one wants to extend the [natural functional calculus for polynomials](wikipedia://en/965348) on the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/4665#Spectral_theory) $\sigma(a)$ of an element $a$ of a Banach algebra $\mathcal{A}$ to a functional calculus for continuous functions $C(\sigma(a))$ on the spectrum, it seems obvious to [approximate](wikipedia://en/336271) a continuous function by [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) according to the [Stone-Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858), to insert the element into these polynomials and to show that this [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of elements [converges](wikipedia://en/26551602) to $\mathcal{A}$. The continuous functions on $\sigma(a) \subset \mathbb{C}$ are approximated by polynomials in $z$ and $\overline{z}$, i.e. by polynomials of the form $p(z, \overline{z}) = \sum_{k,l=0}^N c_{k,l} z^k\overline{z}^l \; \left( c_{k,l} \in \mathbb{C} \right)$. Here, $\overline{z}$ denotes the [complex conjugation](wikipedia://en/173918), which is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) on the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).[^n1] To be able to insert $a$ in place of $z$ in this kind of polynomial, [Banach \*-algebras](wikipedia://en/4665#Banach_*-algebras) are considered, i.e. Banach algebras that also have an involution \*, and $a^*$ is inserted in place of $\overline{z}$. In order to obtain a [homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) ${\mathbb C}[z,\overline{z}]\rightarrow\mathcal{A}$, a restriction to normal elements, i.e. elements with $a^*a = aa^*$, is necessary, as the polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[z,\overline{z}]$ is [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390). If $(p_n(z,\overline{z}))_n$ is a sequence of polynomials that converges [uniformly](wikipedia://en/50652) on $\sigma(a)$ to a continuous function $f$, the convergence of the sequence $(p_n(a,a^*))_n$ in $\mathcal{A}$ to an element $f(a)$ must be ensured. A detailed analysis of this convergence problem shows that it is necessary to resort to C\*-algebras. These considerations lead to the so-called continuous functional calculus.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="continuous functional calculus"}

**continuous functional calculus.**

Let $a$ be a normal element of the C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ with [unit element](wikipedia://en/14962) $e$ and let $C (\sigma(a))$ be the commutative C\*-algebra of continuous functions on $\sigma(a)$, the spectrum of $a$. Then there exists exactly one [\*-homomorphism](wikipedia://en/293426#*-algebra) $\Phi_a \colon C (\sigma(a)) \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$ with $\Phi_a (\boldsymbol{1}) = e$ for $\boldsymbol{1}(z) = 1$ and $\Phi_a(\operatorname{Id}_{\sigma(a)}) = a$ for the [identity](wikipedia://en/298428).[^n2]

The mapping $\Phi_a$ is called the continuous functional calculus of the normal element $a$. Usually it is suggestively set $f(a) := \Phi_a(f)$.[^n3]

:::

Due to the \*-homomorphism property, the following calculation rules apply to all functions $f,g \in C(\sigma(a))$ and [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) $\lambda,\mu \in \mathbb{C}$:[^n4]

| $(\lambda f + \mu g)(a) = \lambda f(a) + \mu g(a) \qquad$ | (linear) |
|---|---|
| $(f \cdot g)(a) = f (a) \cdot g(a)$ | (multiplicative) |
| $\overline{f}(a) =\colon \; (f^*)(a) = (f(a))^*$ | (involutive) |

One can therefore imagine actually inserting the normal elements into continuous functions; the obvious algebraic operations behave as expected.

The requirement for a unit element is not a significant restriction. If necessary, a [unit element can be adjoined](wikipedia://en/3405004#Adjoining_an_identity_element_%28Dorroh_extension%29), yielding the enlarged C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}_1$. Then if $a \in \mathcal{A}$ and $f \in C(\sigma (a))$ with $f(0) = 0$, it follows that $0 \in \sigma (a)$ and $f(a)\in \mathcal{A} \subset \mathcal{A}_1$.[^n5]

The existence and uniqueness of the continuous functional calculus are proven separately:

- *Existence:* Since the spectrum of $a$ in the C\*-[subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) $C^*(a,e)$ generated by $a$ and $e$ is the same as it is in $\mathcal{A}$, it suffices to show the statement for $\mathcal{A} = C^*(a,e)$.[^n6] The actual construction is almost immediate from the [Gelfand representation](wikipedia://en/364372): it suffices to assume $\mathcal{A}$ is the C\*-algebra of continuous functions on some compact space $X$ and define $\Phi_a(f) = f \circ x$.[^n7]
- *Uniqueness:* Since $\Phi_a(\boldsymbol{1})$ and $\Phi_a(\operatorname{Id}_{\sigma(a)})$ are fixed, $\Phi_a$ is already uniquely defined for all polynomials $p(z, \overline{z}) = \sum_{k,l=0}^N c_{k,l} z^k\overline{z}^l \; \left( c_{k,l} \in \mathbb{C} \right)$, since $\Phi_a$ is a \*-homomorphism. These form a [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) subalgebra of $C(\sigma(a))$ by the Stone-Weierstrass theorem. Thus $\Phi_a$ is unique.[^n7]

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the continuous functional calculus for a normal operator $T$ is often of interest, i.e. the case where $\mathcal{A}$ is the C\*-algebra $\mathcal{B}(H)$ of [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961) on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$. In the literature, the continuous functional calculus is often only proved for [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) in this setting. In this case, the proof does not need the Gelfand representation.[^n8]

:::

## Further properties of the continuous functional calculus {#Further_properties_of_the_continuous_functional_calculus}

The continuous functional calculus $\Phi_a$ is an [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) into the C\*-subalgebra $C^*(a,e)$ generated by $a$ and $e$, that is:[^n7]

- $\left\| \Phi_a(f) \right\| = \left\| f \right\|_{\sigma(a)}$ for all $f \in C(\sigma(a))$; $\Phi_a$ is therefore continuous.
- $$\Phi_a \left( C(\sigma(a)) \right) = C^*(a, e) \subseteq \mathcal{A}$$

Since $a$ is a normal element of $\mathcal{A}$, the C\*-subalgebra generated by $a$ and $e$ is commutative. In particular, $f(a)$ is normal and all elements of a functional calculus commutate.[^n4]

The [holomorphic functional calculus](wikipedia://en/3131706) is [extended](wikipedia://en/1589032#Extension_of_a_function) by the continuous functional calculus in an unambiguous way.[^n9] Therefore, for polynomials $p(z,\overline{z})$ the continuous functional calculus corresponds to the natural functional calculus for polynomials: $\Phi_a(p(z, \overline{z})) = p(a, a^*) = \sum_{k,l=0}^N c_{k, l} a^k(a^*)^l$ for all $p(z, \overline{z}) = \sum_{k,l=0}^N c_{k,l} z^k\overline{z}^l$ with $c_{k,l} \in \mathbb{C}$.[^n3]

For a sequence of functions $f_n \in C(\sigma(a))$ that converges uniformly on $\sigma(a)$ to a function $f \in C(\sigma(a))$, $f_n(a)$ converges to $f(a)$.[^n10] For a [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) $f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n z^n$, which converges [absolutely](wikipedia://en/61532) [uniformly](wikipedia://en/50652) on $\sigma(a)$, therefore $f(a) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_na^n$ holds.[^n11]

If $f \in \mathcal{C}(\sigma(a))$ and $g\in \mathcal{ C}(\sigma(f(a)))$, then $(g \circ f)(a) = g(f(a))$ holds for their [composition](wikipedia://en/195947).[^n5] If $a,b \in \mathcal{A}_N$ are two normal elements with $f(a) = f(b)$ and $g$ is the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) of $f$ on both $\sigma(a)$ and $\sigma(b)$, then $a = b$, since $a = (f \circ g) (a) = f(g(a)) = f(g(b)) = (f \circ g) (b) = b$.[^n12]

The *spectral mapping theorem* applies: $\sigma(f(a)) = f(\sigma(a))$ for all $f \in C(\sigma(a))$.[^n7]

If $ab = ba$ holds for $b \in \mathcal{A}$, then $f(a)b = bf(a)$ also holds for all $f \in C ( \sigma (a))$, i.e. if $b$ commutates with $a$, then also with the corresponding elements of the continuous functional calculus $f(a)$.[^n13]

Let $\Psi \colon \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathcal{B}$ be an unital \*-homomorphism between C\*-algebras $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$. Then $\Psi$ commutates with the continuous functional calculus. The following holds: $\Psi(f(a)) = f(\Psi(a))$ for all $f \in C(\sigma(a))$. In particular, the continuous functional calculus commutates with the Gelfand representation.[^n4]

With the spectral mapping theorem, functions with certain properties can be directly related to certain properties of elements of C\*-algebras:[^n14]

- $f(a)$ is [invertible](wikipedia://en/45199) if and only if $f$ [has no zero](wikipedia://en/264210) on $\sigma(a)$.[^n15] Then $f(a)^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{f} (a)$ holds.[^n16]
- $f(a)$ is [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) if and only if $f$ is [real-valued](wikipedia://en/22284647), i.e. $f(\sigma(a)) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.
- $f(a)$ is [positive](wikipedia://en/68445647) ($f(a) \geq 0$) if and only if $f \geq 0$, i.e. $f(\sigma(a)) \subseteq [0,\infty )$.
- $f(a)$ is [unitary](wikipedia://en/20402624) if all values of $f$ lie in the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177), i.e. $f(\sigma(a)) \subseteq \mathbb{T} = \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C} \mid \left\| \lambda \right\| = 1 \}$.
- $f(a)$ is a [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) if $f$ only takes on the values $0$ and $1$, i.e. $f(\sigma(a)) \subseteq \{ 0, 1 \}$.

These are based on statements about the spectrum of certain elements, which are shown in the Applications section.

In the special case that $\mathcal{A}$ is the C\*-algebra of bounded operators $\mathcal{B}(H)$ for a Hilbert space $H$, [eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429) $v \in H$ for the eigenvalue $\lambda \in \sigma(T)$ of a normal operator $T \in \mathcal{B}(H)$ are also eigenvectors for the eigenvalue $f(\lambda) \in \sigma(f(T))$ of the operator $f(T)$. If $Tv = \lambda v$, then $f(T)v = f(\lambda)v$ also holds for all $f \in \sigma(T)$.[^n17]

## Applications {#Applications}

The following applications are typical and very simple examples of the numerous applications of the continuous functional calculus:

### Spectrum {#Spectrum}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a C\*-algebra and $a \in \mathcal{A}_N$ a normal element. Then the following applies to the spectrum $\sigma(a)$:[^n14]

- $a$ is self-adjoint if and only if $\sigma(a) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.
- $a$ is unitary if and only if $\sigma(a) \subseteq \mathbb{T} = \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C} \mid \left\| \lambda \right\| = 1 \}$.
- $a$ is a projection if and only if $\sigma(a) \subseteq \{ 0, 1 \}$.

*Proof.*[^n3] The continuous functional calculus $\Phi_a$ for the normal element $a \in \mathcal{A}$ is a \*-homomorphism with $\Phi_a (\operatorname{Id}) = a$ and thus $a$ is self-adjoint/unitary/a projection if $\operatorname{Id} \in C( \sigma(a))$ is also self-adjoint/unitary/a projection. Exactly then $\operatorname{Id}$ is self-adjoint if $z = \text{Id}(z) = \overline{\text{Id}}(z) = \overline{z}$ holds for all $z \in \sigma(a)$, i.e. if $\sigma(a)$ is real. Exactly then $\text{Id}$ is unitary if $1 = \text{Id}(z) \overline{\operatorname{Id}}(z) = z \overline{z} = |z|^2$ holds for all $z \in \sigma(a)$, therefore $\sigma(a) \subseteq \{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C} \ | \ \left\| \lambda \right\| = 1 \}$. Exactly then $\text{Id}$ is a projection if and only if $(\operatorname{Id}(z))^2 = \operatorname{Id}}(z) = \overline{\operatorname{Id}(z)$, that is $z^2 = z = \overline{z}$ for all $z \in \sigma(a)$, i.e. $\sigma(a) \subseteq \{ 0,1 \}$

### Roots {#Roots}

Let $a$ be a positive element of a C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}$. Then for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$ there exists a uniquely determined positive element $b \in \mathcal{A}_+$ with $b^n =a$, i.e. a unique $n$-th root.[^n18]

*Proof.* For each $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the root function $f_n \colon \mathbb{R}_0^+ \to \mathbb{R}_0^+, x \mapsto \sqrt[n]x$ is a continuous function on $\sigma (a) \subseteq \mathbb{R}_0^+$. If $b \; \colon = f_n (a)$ is defined using the continuous functional calculus, then $b^n = (f_n(a))^n = (f_n^n)(a) = \operatorname{Id}_{\sigma(a)}(a)=a$ follows from the properties of the calculus. From the spectral mapping theorem follows $\sigma(b) = \sigma(f_n(a)) = f_n(\sigma(a)) \subseteq [0,\infty)$, i.e. $b$ is positive.[^n18] If $c \in \mathcal{A}_+$ is another positive element with $c^n = a = b^n$, then $c = f_n (c^n) = f_n(b^n) = b$ holds, as the root function on the positive real numbers is an inverse function to the function $z \mapsto z^n$.[^n12]

If $a \in \mathcal{A}_{sa}$ is a self-adjoint element, then at least for every odd $n \in \mathbb{N}$ there is a uniquely determined self-adjoint element $b \in \mathcal{A}_{sa}$ with $b^n = a$.[^n19]

Similarly, for a positive element $a$ of a C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}$, each $\alpha \geq 0$ defines a uniquely determined positive element $a^\alpha$ of $C^*(a)$, such that $a^\alpha a^\beta = a^{\alpha + \beta}$ holds for all $\alpha, \beta \geq 0$. If $a$ is invertible, this can also be extended to negative values of $\alpha$.[^n18]

### Absolute value {#Absolute_value}

If $a \in \mathcal{A}$, then the element $a^*a$ is positive, so that the absolute value can be defined by the continuous functional calculus $|a| = \sqrt{a^*a}$, since it is continuous on the positive real numbers.[^n20]

Let $a$ be a self-adjoint element of a C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}$, then there exist positive elements $a_+,a_- \in \mathcal{A}_+$, such that $a = a_+ - a_-$ with $a_+ a_- = a_- a_+ = 0$ holds. The elements $a_+$ and $a_-$ are also referred to as the [positive and negative parts](wikipedia://en/3344921).[^n21] In addition, $|a| = a_+ + a_-$ holds.[^n22]

*Proof.* The functions $f_+(z) = \max(z,0)$ and $f_-(z) = -\min(z, 0)$ are continuous functions on $\sigma(a) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $\operatorname{Id} (z) = z = f_+(z) -f_-(z)$ and $f_+(z)f_-(z) = f_-(z)f_+(z) = 0$. Put $a_+ = f_+(a)$ and $a_- = f_-(a)$. According to the spectral mapping theorem, $a_+$ and $a_-$ are positive elements for which $a = \operatorname{Id}(a) = (f_+ - f_-) (a) = f_+(a) - f_-(a) = a_+ - a_-$ and $a_+ a_- = f_+(a)f_-(a) = (f_+f_-)(a) = 0 = (f_-f_+)(a) = f_-(a)f_+(a) = a_- a_+$ holds.[^n21] Furthermore, $f_+(z) + f_-(z) = |z| = \sqrt{z^* z} = \sqrt{z^2}$, such that $a_+ + a_- = f_+(a) + f_-(a) = |a| = \sqrt{a^* a} = \sqrt{a^2}$ holds.[^n22]

### Unitary elements {#Unitary_elements}

If $a$ is a self-adjoint element of a C\*-algebra $\mathcal{A}$ with unit element $e$, then $u = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} a}$ is unitary, where $\mathrm{i}$ denotes the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358). Conversely, if $u \in \mathcal{A}_U$ is a unitary element, with the restriction that the spectrum is a [proper subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the unit circle, i.e. $\sigma(u) \subsetneq \mathbb{T}$, there exists a self-adjoint element $a \in \mathcal{A}_{sa}$ with $u = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} a}$.[^n23]

*Proof.*[^n23] It is $u = f(a)$ with $f \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C},\ x \mapsto \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x}$, since $a$ is self-adjoint, it follows that $\sigma(a) \subset \mathbb{R}$, i.e. $f$ is a function on the spectrum of $a$. Since $f\cdot \overline{f} = \overline{f}\cdot f = 1$, using the functional calculus $uu^* = u^*u = e$ follows, i.e. $u$ is unitary. Since for the other statement there is a $z_0 \in \mathbb{T}$, such that $\sigma(u) \subseteq \{ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} z} \mid z_0 \leq z \leq z_0 + 2 \pi \}$ the function $f(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} z}) = z$ is a real-valued continuous function on the spectrum $\sigma(u)$ for $z_0 \leq z \leq z_0 + 2 \pi$, such that $a = f(u)$ is a self-adjoint element that satisfies $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} a} = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i} f(u)} = u$.

### Spectral decomposition theorem {#Spectral_decomposition_theorem}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be an unital C\*-algebra and $a \in \mathcal{A}_N$ a normal element. Let the spectrum consist of $n$ pairwise [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) subsets $\sigma_k \subset \mathbb{C}$ for all $1 \leq k \leq n$, i.e. $\sigma(a)=\sigma_1 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup \sigma_n$. Then there exist projections $p_1, \ldots, p_n \in \mathcal{A}$ that have the following properties for all $1 \leq j,k \leq n$:[^n24]

- For the spectrum, $\sigma(p_k) = \sigma_k$ holds.
- The projections commutate with $a$, i.e. $p_ka=ap_k$.
- The projections are [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221), i.e. $p_jp_k=\delta_{jk} p_k$.
- The sum of the projections is the unit element, i.e. $\sum_{k=1}^n p_k = e$.

In particular, there is a decomposition $a = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k$ for which $\sigma(a_k) = \sigma_k$ holds for all $1 \leq k \leq n$.

*Proof.*[^n24] Since all $\sigma_k$ are closed, the [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/240790) $\chi_{\sigma_k}$ are continuous on $\sigma(a)$. Now let $p_k := \chi_{\sigma_k} (a)$ be defined using the continuous functional. As the $\sigma_k$ are pairwise disjoint, $\chi_{\sigma_j} \chi_{\sigma_k} = \delta_{jk} \chi_{\sigma_k}$ and $\sum_{k=1}^n \chi_{\sigma_k} = \chi_{\cup_{k=1}^n \sigma_k} = \chi_{\sigma(a)} = \textbf{1}$ holds and thus the $p_k$ satisfy the claimed properties, as can be seen from the properties of the continuous functional equation. For the last statement, let $a_k = a p_k = \operatorname{Id} (a) \cdot \chi_{\sigma_k} (a) = (\operatorname{Id} \cdot \chi_{\sigma_k}) (a)$.
