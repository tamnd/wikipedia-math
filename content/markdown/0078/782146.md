# Automorphic form

*Image: Dedekind Eta.jpg.* The [Dedekind eta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_eta-function) is an automorphic form in the complex plane.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Automorphic form"}

In [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), an **automorphic form** is a well-behaved function from a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) $G$ to the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (or complex [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370)) which is invariant under the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of a [discrete subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_subgroup) $\Gamma < G$ of the topological group. Automorphic forms are a generalization of the idea of [periodic functions](wikipedia://en/76848) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) to general topological groups.

:::

[Modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000) are [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) automorphic forms defined over the groups [SL(2, **R**)](wikipedia://en/11105238) or [PSL(2, **R**)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSL2%28R%29), with the discrete subgroup being the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) or one of its [congruence subgroups](wikipedia://en/382694); in this sense the theory of automorphic forms is an extension of the theory of modular forms. More generally, one can use the [adelic](wikipedia://en/247978) approach as a way of dealing with the whole family of [congruence subgroups](wikipedia://en/382694) at once. From this point of view, an automorphic form over the group $G(\mathbb{A}_F)$, for an algebraic group $G$ and an [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) field $F$, is a complex-valued function on $G(\mathbb{A}_F)$ that is left invariant under $G(F)$ and satisfies certain smoothness and growth conditions.

[Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) first discovered automorphic forms as generalizations of [trigonometric](wikipedia://en/30367) and [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939). Through the [Langlands conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langlands_conjectures), automorphic forms play an important role in modern number theory.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the notion of **factor of automorphy** arises for a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on a [complex-analytic manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex-analytic_manifold). Suppose a group $G$ acts on a complex-analytic manifold $X$. Then, $G$ also acts on the space of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) from $X$ to the complex numbers. A function $f$ is termed an *automorphic form* if the following holds:

$$f(g\cdot x) = j_g(x)f(x)$$

where $j_g(x)$ is an everywhere nonzero holomorphic function. Equivalently, an automorphic form is a function whose divisor is invariant under the action of $G$.

The *factor of automorphy* for the automorphic form $f$ is the function $j$. An *automorphic function* is an automorphic form for which $j$ is the identity.

An automorphic form is a function *F* on *G* (with values in some fixed finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V*, in the vector-valued case), subject to three kinds of conditions:

1. to transform under translation by elements $\gamma \in \Gamma$ according to the given [factor of automorphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_of_automorphy) *j*;
1. to be an [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) of certain [Casimir operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimir_operator) on *G*; and
1. to satisfy a "moderate growth" asymptotic condition a [height function](wikipedia://en/3141855).

It is the first of these that makes *F* *automorphic*, that is, satisfy a [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377) relating *F*(*g*) with *F*(*γg*) for $\gamma \in \Gamma$. In the vector-valued case the specification can involve a finite-dimensional [group representation](wikipedia://en/12695) ρ acting on the components to 'twist' them. The Casimir operator condition says that some [Laplacians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) have *F* as eigenfunction; this ensures that *F* has excellent analytic properties, but whether it is actually a complex-analytic function depends on the particular case. The third condition is to handle the case where *G*/Γ is not [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) but has [cusps](wikipedia://en/610165).

The formulation requires the general notion of *factor of automorphy* *j* for Γ, which is a type of 1-[cocycle](wikipedia://en/248635#Cochain_complexes) in the language of [group cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635). The values of *j* may be complex numbers, or in fact complex square matrices, corresponding to the possibility of vector-valued automorphic forms. The cocycle condition imposed on the factor of automorphy is something that can be routinely checked, when *j* is derived from a [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix), by means of the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113).

A more straightforward but technically advanced definition using [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695), constructs automorphic forms and their correspondent functions as embeddings of [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673) to their underlying [global field](wikipedia://en/288358) extensions. In this formulation, automorphic forms are certain finite invariants, mapping from the [idele class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idele_class_group) under the [Artin reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_reciprocity_law). Herein, the analytical structure of its [L-function](wikipedia://en/245283) allows for generalizations with various [algebro-geometric](wikipedia://en/1997) properties; and the resultant [Langlands program](wikipedia://en/171905). To oversimplify, automorphic forms in this general perspective, are analytic functionals quantifying the invariance of [number fields](wikipedia://en/28730822) in an abstract sense, therefore indicating the ['primitivity'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) of their [fundamental structure](wikipedia://en/150907). It is a mathematical tool for analyzing the invariant constructs of many numerical structure.

Examples of automorphic forms in an explicit unabstracted state are difficult to obtain, though some have directly analytical properties:

\- The [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458) (which is a prototypical [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000)) over certain [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634) as [Abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974).

\- Specific generalizations of [Dirichlet L-functions](wikipedia://en/400092) as [class field-theoretic](wikipedia://en/242695) objects.

\- Generally any [harmonic analytic](wikipedia://en/14147) object as a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) over [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673) which is invariant on its [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907) (or [idele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idele)).

As a general principle, automorphic forms can be thought of as [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) on [abstract structures](wikipedia://en/19616384), which are invariant with respect to a generalized analogue of their [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) (or an abstracted [irreducible fundamental representation](wikipedia://en/649743)). As mentioned, automorphic functions can be seen as generalizations of modular forms (as therefore [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225)), constructed by some [zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) analogue on an [automorphic](wikipedia://en/56661772) structure. In the simplest sense, automorphic forms are [modular forms](wikipedia://en/286000) defined on general [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945); because of their symmetry properties. Therefore, in simpler terms, a general function which analyzes the invariance of a structure with respect to its prime ['morphology'](wikipedia://en/24457573).

## History {#History}

Before this very general setting was proposed (around 1960), there had already been substantial developments of automorphic forms other than modular forms. The case of Γ a [Fuchsian group](wikipedia://en/471365) had already received attention before 1900 (see below). The [Hilbert modular forms](wikipedia://en/2333880) (also called Hilbert-Blumenthal forms) were proposed not long after that, though a full theory was long in coming. The [Siegel modular forms](wikipedia://en/7721927), for which *G* is a [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977), arose naturally from considering [moduli spaces](wikipedia://en/361609) and [theta functions](wikipedia://en/449745). The post-war interest in several complex variables made it natural to pursue the idea of automorphic form in the cases where the forms are indeed complex-analytic. Much work was done, in particular by [Ilya Piatetski-Shapiro](wikipedia://en/802738), in the years around 1960, in creating such a theory. The theory of the [Selberg trace formula](wikipedia://en/981694), as applied by others, showed the considerable depth of the theory. [Robert Langlands](wikipedia://en/25986) showed how (in generality, many particular cases being known) the [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261) could be applied to the calculation of dimensions of automorphic forms; this is a kind of *post hoc* check on the validity of the notion. He also produced the general theory of [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/5562599), which corresponds to what in [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) terms would be the 'continuous spectrum' for this problem, leaving the [cusp form](wikipedia://en/610165) or discrete part to investigate. From the point of view of number theory, the cusp forms had been recognised, since [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717), as the heart of the matter.

## Automorphic representations {#Automorphic_representations}

The subsequent notion of an "automorphic representation" has proved of technical value when dealing with *G* an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924), treated as an [adelic algebraic group](wikipedia://en/1168608). It does not completely include the automorphic form idea introduced above, in that the [adelic](wikipedia://en/247978) approach is a way of dealing with the whole family of [congruence subgroups](wikipedia://en/382694) at once. Inside an *L*<sup>2</sup> space for a quotient of the adelic form of *G*, an automorphic representation is a representation that is an infinite [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of representations of [p-adic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_group), with specific [enveloping algebra](wikipedia://en/363551) representations for the [infinite prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_prime)(s). One way to express the shift in emphasis is that the [Hecke operators](wikipedia://en/521837) are here in effect put on the same level as the Casimir operators; which is natural from the point of view of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), though not so obviously for the number theory. It is this concept that is basic to the formulation of the [Langlands philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langlands_philosophy).

## Poincaré on discovery and his work on automorphic functions {#Poincaré_on_discovery_and_his_work_on_automorphic_functions}

One of [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740)'s first discoveries in mathematics, dating to the 1880s, was automorphic forms. He named them Fuchsian functions, after the mathematician [Lazarus Fuchs](wikipedia://en/2817087), because Fuchs was known for being a good teacher and had researched on differential equations and the theory of functions. Poincaré actually developed the concept of these functions as part of his doctoral thesis. Under Poincaré's definition, an automorphic function is one which is analytic in its domain and is invariant under a discrete [infinite group](wikipedia://en/4587030) of linear fractional transformations. Automorphic functions then generalize both [trigonometric](wikipedia://en/30367) and [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939).

Poincaré explains how he discovered Fuchsian functions: For fifteen days I strove to prove that there could not be any functions like those I have since called Fuchsian functions. I was then very ignorant; every day I seated myself at my work table, stayed an hour or two, tried a great number of combinations and reached no results. One evening, contrary to my custom, I drank black coffee and could not sleep. Ideas rose in crowds; I felt them collide until pairs interlocked, so to speak, making a stable combination. By the next morning I had established the existence of a class of Fuchsian functions, those which come from the [hypergeometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_series); I had only to write out the results, which took but a few hours.
