# Cis (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cis"}

Cisoidal oscillation

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), $\mathbf{cis}$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) defined by $\operatorname{cis} x = \cos x + i \sin x$,[^o11] where $\cos$ is the [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) function, $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) and $\sin$ is the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function. $x$ is the [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) of the complex number (angle between line to point and x-axis in [polar form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_form)). It is mathematically equivalent to [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613), $e^{ix}$, which offers an even shorter notation for $\cos x + i \sin x$, but `cis(x)` is widely used as a name for this function in [software libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_%28computing%29).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

The $\operatorname{cis}$ notation is a shorthand for the combination of functions on the right-hand side of [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613):

$$e^{ix} = \cos x + i\sin x,$$

where $i^{2} = -1$. So,

$\operatorname{cis} x = \cos x + i\sin x,$[^o32][^o14][^o31][^o43]

i.e. "$\operatorname{cis}$" is an [acronym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acronym) for "$\cos i \sin$".

It connects [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) with [exponential functions](wikipedia://en/9678) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) via Euler's formula. While the [domain of definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_of_definition) is usually $x \in \mathbb{R}$, [complex values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_value) $z \in \mathbb{C}$ are possible as well:

$$\operatorname{cis} z = \cos z + i\sin z,$$

so the $\operatorname{cis}$ function can be used to extend Euler's formula to a more [general complex version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_complex_exponential_function).[^o45]

The function is mostly used as a convenient shorthand notation to simplify some expressions,[^o22][^o37][^o38] for example in conjunction with [Fourier](wikipedia://en/52247) and [Hartley transforms](wikipedia://en/596556),[^o13][^o15][^o16] or when exponential functions shouldn't be used for some reason in math education.

In information technology, the function sees dedicated support in various high-performance math libraries (such as [Intel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel)'s [Math Kernel Library](wikipedia://en/23820248) (MKL)[^o23] or MathCW[^o44]), available for many compilers and programming languages (including [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29), [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++),[^o24] [Common Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Lisp),[^o25][^o26] [D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_%28programming_language%29),[^o27] [Haskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskell_%28programming_language%29),[^o30] [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29),[^o28] and [Rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_%28programming_language%29)[^o29]). Depending on the platform, the [fused operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fused_operation) is about twice as fast as calling the sine and cosine functions individually.[^o27][^o31]

## Mathematical identities {#Mathematical_identities}

### Derivative {#Derivative}

$\frac{d}{dz}\operatorname{cis} z = i\operatorname{cis} z = ie^{iz}$[^o32][^o33]

### Integral {#Integral}

$\int\operatorname{cis} z \,dz = -i\operatorname{cis} z = -ie^{iz}$[^o32]

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

These follow directly from [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613).

$$\cos(x) = \frac{\operatorname{cis}(x) + \operatorname{cis}(-x)}{2} = \frac{e^{ix} + e^{-ix}}{2}$$

$$\sin(x) = \frac{\operatorname{cis}(x) - \operatorname{cis}(-x)}{2i}= \frac{e^{ix} - e^{-ix}}{2i}$$

$\operatorname{cis}(x+y) = \operatorname{cis} x\,\operatorname{cis} y$[^o34]

$$\operatorname{cis}(x-y) = {\operatorname{cis} x \over \operatorname{cis} y}$$

$$\operatorname{cis}(nx) = \operatorname{cis}(x)^n$$

$$\operatorname{cis} \left( {x \over n} \right) = \sqrt[n]{\operatorname{cis}(x)}$$

The identities above hold if $x$ and $y$ are any complex numbers. If $x$ and $y$ are real, then

$|\operatorname{cis} x - \operatorname{cis} y| \le |x-y|.$[^o34]

## History {#History}

The $\operatorname{cis}$ notation was first coined by [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in *Elements of Quaternions* (1866)[^o17][^o18] and subsequently used by [Irving Stringham](wikipedia://en/24044076) (who also called it "[sector](wikipedia://en/1967249) of $x$") in works such as *Uniplanar Algebra* (1893),[^o19][^o20] [James Harkness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Harkness_%28mathematician%29) and [Frank Morley](wikipedia://en/589167) in their *Introduction to the Theory of Analytic Functions* (1898),[^o20][^o21] or by [George Ashley Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Ashley_Campbell) (who also referred to it as "cisoidal oscillation") in his works on [transmission lines](wikipedia://en/41811) (1901) and [Fourier integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_integral) (1928).[^o40][^o41][^o42]

In 1942, inspired by the $\operatorname{cis}$ notation, [Ralph V. L. Hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_V._L._Hartley) introduced the $\operatorname{cas}$ (for *cosine-and-sine*) function for the real-valued [Hartley kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_kernel), a meanwhile established shortcut in conjunction with [Hartley transforms](wikipedia://en/596556):[^o35][^o36]

$$\operatorname{cas} x = \cos x + \sin x.$$

## Motivation {#Motivation}

The $\operatorname{cis}$ notation is sometimes used to emphasize one method of viewing and dealing with a problem over another.[^o39] The mathematics of trigonometry and exponentials are related but not exactly the same; exponential notation emphasizes the whole, whereas $\operatorname{cis} x$ and $\cos x + i \sin x$ notations emphasize the parts. This can be rhetorically useful to mathematicians and engineers when discussing this function, and further serve as a [mnemonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemonic) (for $\cos + i \sin$).[^o42]

The $\operatorname{cis}$ notation is convenient for math students whose knowledge of trigonometry and complex numbers permit this notation, but whose conceptual understanding does not yet permit the notation $e^{ix}$. The usual proof that $\operatorname{cis} x = e^{ix}$ requires [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), which the student may not have studied before encountering the expression $\cos x + i \sin x$.

This notation was more common when typewriters were used to convey mathematical expressions.
