# Davenport constant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Davenport constant"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Davenport constant** $D(G)$ is an [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) studied in [additive combinatorics](wikipedia://en/18243392), quantifying the size of nonunique factorizations. Given a [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) $G$, $D(G)$ is defined as the smallest number such that every [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of elements of that length contains a non-empty subsequence adding up to 0. In symbols, this is[^o2]

:::

$$D(G) = \min\left\{ N : \forall\left(\{g_n\}_{n=1}^N \in G^N\right)\left(\exists\{n_k\}_{k=1}^K : \sum_{k=1}^K{g_{n_k}} = 0\right) \right\}.$$

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

- The Davenport constant for the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $G = \mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ is $n$. To see this, note that the sequence of a fixed [generator](wikipedia://en/99945), repeated $n-1$ times, contains no subsequence with sum 0. Thus $D(G)\ge n$. On the other hand, if $\{g_k\}_{k=1}^n$ is an arbitrary sequence, then two of the sums in the sequence $\left\{\sum_{k=1}^K{g_k}\right\}_{K=0}^n$ are equal. The difference of these two sums also gives a subsequence with sum 0.[^n1]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Consider a [finite abelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_abelian_group) $G=\oplus_i C_{d_i}$, where the $d_1|d_2|\dots|d_r$ are [invariant factors](wikipedia://en/9886801). Then $$D(G) \ge M(G) = 1-r+\sum_i{d_i}.$$ The lower bound is [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by noting that the sequence consisting of $d_1$ copies of $(1,0,\dots,0)$, $d_2$ copies of $(0,1,\dots,0)$, etc., contains no subsequence with sum 0.[^o8]
- $D=M$ for [$p$-groups](wikipedia://en/24664) or when $r$ is 1 or 2.
- $D=M$ for certain groups including all groups of the form $C_2\oplus C_{2n}\oplus C_{2nm}$ and $C_3\oplus C_{3n}\oplus C_{3nm}$.
- There are infinitely many examples with $r$ at least 4 where $D$ does not equal $M$; it is not known whether there are any with $r=3$.[^o8]
- Let $\exp(G)$ be the [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent_of_a_group) of $G$. Then[^o12] $$\frac{D(G)}{\exp(G)} \leq 1+\log\left(\frac{|G|}{\exp(G)}\right).$$

:::

::: {#o10 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The original motivation for studying Davenport's constant was the problem of non-unique factorization in [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field). Let $\mathcal{O}$ be the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) in a number field, $G$ its [class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_group). Then every element $\alpha\in\mathcal{O}$, which factors into at least $D(G)$ non-trivial [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977), is properly divisible by an element of $\mathcal{O}$. This observation implies that Davenport's constant determines by how much the lengths of different factorization of some element in $\mathcal{O}$ can differ.[^o11]

The upper bound mentioned above plays an important role in Ahlford, Granville and Pomerance's proof of the existence of infinitely many [Carmichael numbers](wikipedia://en/7723).[^o12]

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variants"}

## Variants {#Variants}

Olson's constant $O(G)$ uses the same definition, but requires the elements of $\{g_n\}_{n=1}^N$ to be distinct.[^o15]

- Balandraud proved that $O(C_p)$ equals the smallest $k$ such that $\frac{k(k+1)}{2} \geq p$.
- For $p>6000$ we have $$O(C_p\oplus C_p) = p-1+O(C_p).$$ On the other hand, if $G=C_p^r$ with $r\ge p$, then Olson's constant equals the Davenport constant.[^o16]

:::
