# Factorial moment

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Factorial moment"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **factorial moment** is a mathematical quantity defined as the [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653) or average of the [falling factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falling_factorial) of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685). Factorial moments are useful for studying [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563)-valued random variables,[^o2] and arise in the use of [probability-generating functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function) to derive the moments of discrete random variables.

:::

Factorial moments serve as analytic tools in the mathematical field of combinatorics, which is the study of discrete mathematical structures.[^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For a natural number $r$, the $r$-th factorial moment of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) on the real or complex numbers, or, in other words, a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ with that probability distribution, is[^o6]

$$\operatorname{E}\bigl[(X)_r\bigr] = \operatorname{E}\bigl[ X(X-1)(X-2)\cdots(X-r+1)\bigr],$$

where the $E$ is the [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653) ([operator](wikipedia://en/22213#Probability_theory)) and

$$(x)_r := \underbrace{x(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-r+1)}_{r \text{ factors}} \equiv \frac{x!}{(x-r)!}$$

is the [falling factorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falling_factorial), which gives rise to the name, although the notation $(x)_{r}$ varies depending on the mathematical field. Of course, the definition requires that the expectation is meaningful, which is the case if $(X)_{r} \ge 0$ or $E[|(X)_{r}|] < \infty$.

If $X$ is the number of successes in $n$ trials, and $p_{r}$ is the probability that any $r$ of the $n$ trials are all successes, then[^o7]

$$\operatorname{E}\bigl[(X)_r\bigr] = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-r+1)p_r$$

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Poisson distribution {#Poisson_distribution}

If a random variable $X$ has a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with parameter *λ*, then the factorial moments of $X$ are

$$\operatorname{E}\bigl[(X)_r\bigr] =\lambda^r,$$

which are simple in form compared to [its moments](wikipedia://en/23009144#Higher_moments), which involve [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292).

### Binomial distribution {#Binomial_distribution}

If a random variable $X$ has a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) with success probability $p \in$0,1 and number of trials $n$, then the factorial moments of $X$ are[^o12]

$$\operatorname{E}\bigl[(X)_r\bigr] = \binom{n}{r} p^r r! = (n)_r p^r,$$

where by convention, $\textstyle{\binom{n}{r}}$ and $(n)_r$ are understood to be zero if *r* > *n*.

### Hypergeometric distribution {#Hypergeometric_distribution}

If a random variable $X$ has a [hypergeometric distribution](wikipedia://en/180841) with population size $N$, number of success states $K \in \{0,...,N$} in the population, and draws $n \in \{0,...,N$}, then the factorial moments of $X$ are [^o12]

$$\operatorname{E}\bigl[(X)_r\bigr] = \frac{\binom{K}{r}\binom{n}{r}r!}{\binom{N}{r}} = \frac{(K)_r (n)_r}{(N)_r}.$$

### Beta-binomial distribution {#Beta-binomial_distribution}

If a random variable $X$ has a [beta-binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/7377730) with parameters $\alpha > 0$, $\beta > 0$, and number of trials $n$, then the factorial moments of $X$ are

$$\operatorname{E}\bigl[(X)_r\bigr] = \binom{n}{r}\frac{B(\alpha+r,\beta)r!}{B(\alpha,\beta)} = (n)_r \frac{B(\alpha+r,\beta)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}$$

:::

## Calculation of moments {#Calculation_of_moments}

The *r*th raw moment of a random variable *X* can be expressed in terms of its factorial moments by the formula

$$\operatorname{E}[X^r] = \sum_{j=1}^r \left\{ {r \atop j} \right\} \operatorname{E}[(X)_j],$$

where the curly braces denote [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292).
