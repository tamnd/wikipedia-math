# Porphyry of Tyre

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Porphyry of Tyre"}

**Porphyry** (; Πορφύριος; c. 234 – c. 305) was a [Phoenician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenicia)[^n1][^n2] [Neoplatonic philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism) born in [Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyre,_Lebanon), [Roman Phoenicia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenice_%28Roman_province%29)[^o5] during [Roman rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire).[^o5][^n3] He edited and published the *[Enneads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enneads)*, the only collection of the work of [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus), his teacher.

:::

He wrote original works in the [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) on a wide variety of topics, ranging from [music theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory) to [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer) to [vegetarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetarianism). His *[Isagoge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isagoge)* or *Introduction*, an introduction to logic and philosophy, was the standard textbook on [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) throughout the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) in its [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) and [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) translations.[^n4] Porphyry was, and still is, also well known for his [anti-Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Christian_sentiment) polemics,[^o18] and for arguing that the [Book of Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Daniel) was [pseudepigrapha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudepigrapha) written during the [Maccabean period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maccabean_period).[^o8][^o9] Through works such as *Philosophy from Oracles* and *[Against the Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_the_Christians)* (which was banned by [Constantine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_the_Great)),[^n5] he was involved in a controversy with [early Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christianity).[^n6]

## Life {#Life}

The *[Suda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suda)* (a 10th-century Byzantine encyclopedia based on many sources now lost) reports that Porphyry was born in [Tyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyre_%28Lebanon%29), however, other sources report that he was born in [Batanaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batanaea), present-day [Syria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syria).[^o13][^o14][^o15][^o16] His parents named him **Malchus** (Μάλχος; cf. [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic) *malkā* 'king'). However, he changed it to "[Basileus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basileus)" "King", and into his [nickname](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickname) "Porphyrius" "\[clad\] in purple" later in his life.[^o18] In his work *The Life of Plotinus,* he refers to [Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic) as his "native tongue."[^o17] Under [Cassius Longinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassius_Longinus_%28philosopher%29), in Athens, he studied [grammar](wikipedia://en/12569) and rhetoric, and became acquainted with [Middle Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Platonism).[^o18]

In 262, he went to [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rome), attracted by the reputation of [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus), and for six years devoted himself to the practice of [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism), during which time he severely modified his diet, at one point becoming suicidal.[^o55] On the advice of Plotinus he went to live in [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily) for five years to recover his mental health. On returning to Rome, he lectured on [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) and completed an edition of the writings of Plotinus (who had died in the meantime) together with a biography of his teacher. [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus) is mentioned in ancient Neoplatonic writings as his disciple, but this is most likely only meant to indicate that he was the dominant figure in the next generation of philosophers succeeding him.[^o19] The two men differed publicly on the issue of [theurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theurgy).

In his later years, he married Marcella, a student of philosophy and a widow with seven children.[^o20] The *Theosophia Tubingensis*, a late-antique [Christian apologetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_apologetics) text, presents his wife as Jewish, though this claim may have originated from Porphyry's own positive statements about [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism), according to [Pieter W. van der Horst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter_Willem_van_der_Horst).[^o21]

There are around sixty works connected to Porphyry's name, some in fragments or lost. Some pieces of his work are still being reconstructed today.[^o22] Little more is known of his life, and the date of his death is uncertain.

## Works {#Works}

### *Introduction* (*Isagoge*) {#Introduction_(Isagoge)}

*Image: AverroesAndPorphyry.JPG.* Imaginary debate between [Averroes](wikipedia://en/47836) (1126–1198 AD) and Porphyry (234–c. 305 AD). Monfredo de Monte Imperiali *Liber de herbis*, 14th century.[^n7]

Porphyry is best known for his contributions to philosophy. Apart from writing the *Aids to the Study of the Intelligibles* (Ἀφορμαὶ πρὸς τὰ νοητά; *Sententiae ad Intelligibilia Ducentes*), a basic summary of Neoplatonism, he is especially appreciated for his Introduction to Categories—also known in Latin as *Introductio in Praedicamenta* or *[Isagoge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isagoge) et in Aristotelis Categorias Commentarium*—a very short work often considered to be a commentary on [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s *[Categories](wikipedia://en/1692652)*, hence the title. According to (Barnes 2003), however, the correct title is simply *Introduction* (Εἰσαγωγή *Isagoge*), and the book is an introduction not to the *Categories* in particular, but to logic in general, as it includes the theories of predication, definition, and proof. The *Introduction* describes how qualities attributed to things may be classified, famously breaking down the philosophical [concept of substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_theory) into the five components *genus*, *species*, *difference*, *property*, and *accident*. Porphyry's discussion of *accident* sparked a long-running debate on the application of [*accident* and *essence*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essentialism).[^n8]

As Porphyry's most influential contribution to philosophy, the *Introduction to Categories* incorporated Aristotle's logic into Neoplatonism, in particular the doctrine of the [categories of being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_being) interpreted in terms of entities (in later philosophy, "[universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_%28metaphysics%29)"). [Boethius](wikipedia://en/195684)' *Isagoge*, a Latin translation of Porphyry's *Introduction*, became a standard medieval textbook in European schools and universities, which set the stage for medieval philosophical-theological developments of logic and the [problem of universals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_universals). In medieval textbooks, the all-important *[Arbor porphyriana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyrian_tree)* ("Porphyrian Tree") illustrates his logical classification of substance. To this day, taxonomy benefits from concepts in Porphyry's Tree, in classifying living organisms (see [cladistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cladistics)). Porphyry's invention of the "Porphyrian Tree" is noted as the first proper commentary made on Aristotle's work.

*Image: Porphyrian Trees Gallery small.png.* A replica of the *[Arbor porphyriana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbor_porphyriana)* (Porphyrian tree) used to comment on Aristotle's work by Purchotius (1730), Boethius (6th century), and Ramon Llull (ca. 1305).

The *Introduction* was translated into Arabic by [Abd-Allāh ibn al-Muqaffaʿ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Muqaffa') from a Syriac version. With the Arabicized name *Isāghūjī* (إيساغوجي) it long remained the standard introductory logic text in the Muslim world and influenced the study of theology, philosophy, grammar, and jurisprudence. Besides the adaptations and epitomes of this work, many independent works on logic by Muslim philosophers have been entitled Isāghūjī.

### *Philosophy from Oracles* (*De Philosophia ex Oraculis Haurienda*) {#Philosophy_from_Oracles_(De_Philosophia_ex_Oraculis_Haurienda)}

Porphyry is also known as an opponent of [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) and defender of [Paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism); his precise contribution to the philosophical approach to traditional religion may be discovered in the fragments of *Philosophy from Oracles* (Περὶ τῆς ἐκ λογίων φιλοσοφίας; *De Philosophia ex Oraculis Haurienda*), which was originally three books in length. There is debate as to whether it was written in his youth (as Eunapius reports[^o55]) or closer in time to the persecutions of Christians under [Diocletian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocletian) and [Galerius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galerius).

Whether or not Porphyry was the pagan philosopher's opponent in [Lactantius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactantius)' *Divine Institutes*, written at the time of the persecutions, has long been discussed. The fragments of the *Philosophy from Oracles* are only quoted by Christians, especially [Eusebius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius_of_Caesarea), [Theodoret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodoret), [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine), and [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus). The fragments contain oracles identifying proper sacrificial procedure, the nature of [astrological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological) fate, and other topics relevant to Greek and Roman religion in the third century. Whether this work contradicts his treatise defending [vegetarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetarianism), which also warned the philosopher to avoid animal sacrifice, is disputed among scholars.[^n9] Due to Porphyry's work being incomplete or lost, the understanding of the piece could be misconstrued.[^o22]

### *Against the Christians* (*Adversus Christianos*) {#Against_the_Christians_(Adversus_Christianos)}

*Image: Porphyrios Sucevita Fresco.jpg.* Porphyry, a detail of the *Tree of Jesse*, 1535, [Sucevița Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucevița_Monastery).

During his retirement in [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily), Porphyry wrote *[Against the Christians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_the_Christians)* (Κατὰ Χριστιανῶν; *Adversus Christianos*) which consisted of fifteen books. Some thirty Christian apologists, such as [Methodius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodius_of_Olympus), [Eusebius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eusebius), [Apollinaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollinaris_of_Laodicea), [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo), [Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome), etc., responded to his challenge. In fact, everything known about Porphyry's arguments is found in these refutations, largely because [Theodosius II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_II) ordered [every copy burned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_burning#Christian_book_burnings) in AD 435 and again in 448.[^n10][^n11][^n12]

[Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo) and the 5th-century ecclesiastical historian [Socrates of Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates_of_Constantinople) assert that Porphyry was once a Christian.[^n13] It is said, however, that while Porphyry did engage with Christianity, he did not believe it. Augustine made comments to Porphyry as he said he was the "most learned of the philosophers, as the most bitter enemy of the Christians".[^o35]

### Other works {#Other_works}

Porphyry was opposed to the [theurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theurgy) of his disciple [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus). Much of Iamblichus' mysteries is dedicated to the defense of mystic theurgic divine possession against the critiques of Porphyry. French philosopher [Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot) maintains that for Porphyry, spiritual exercises are an essential part of spiritual development.[^n14]

Porphyry was, like [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275), an advocate of [vegetarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetarianism) on spiritual and ethical grounds. These two philosophers are perhaps the most famous vegetarians of classical antiquity. He wrote the *[On Abstinence from Animal Food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Abstinence_from_Animal_Food)* (Περὶ ἀποχῆς ἐμψύχων; *De Abstinentia ab Esu Animalium*), advocating against the consumption of animals, and he is cited with approval in vegetarian literature up to the present day. He believed that everything was created for mutual advantage, and vegetarianism was a way to preserve universal harmony of nature.[^o38]

Porphyry also wrote widely on [music theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory),[^o39] [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), religion, and philosophy. He produced a *History of Philosophy* (*Philosophos Historia*) with *[vitae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biography)* of philosophers that included a life of his teacher, Plotinus. His life of Plato from book iv exists only in quotes by [Cyril of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_of_Alexandria). His book *Vita Pythagorae* on the life of [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) is not to be confused with the book of the same name by [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus). His commentary on [Ptolemy's *Harmonics*](wikipedia://en/23979#Music)[^o56] (*Eis ta Harmonika Ptolemaiou hypomnēma*) is an important source for the history of ancient harmonic theory.

Porphyry also wrote about [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer). Apart from several lost texts known only from quotations by other authors, two texts survive at least in large parts: the *Homeric Questions* (*Homēriká zētḗmata*, largely a philological comment on the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*) and *[On the Cave of the Nymphs in the Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Cave_of_the_Nymphs_in_the_Odyssey)* (*Peri tou en Odysseia tōn nymphōn antrou*).

Porphyry's commentary on [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)* was used as a source by [Pappus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/509300).[^o54]

## On Judaism and Christianity {#On_Judaism_and_Christianity}

In his work *Life of Pythagoras*, Porphyry quotes the writer [Antonius Diogenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonius_Diogenes), who wrote that Pythagoras visited the [Egyptians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptians), [Arabs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabs), [Chaldeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyrians), and [Hebrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews), from whom he learned the science of dreams.[^o21] Eusebius likewise records that Porphyry mentioned "the wisdom of the Hebrews" alongside other nations known for their wisdom, attributing to [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo) the statement: "Only Chaldeans and Hebrews found wisdom in the pure worship of a self-born God."[^o21]

In *De antro nympharum*, Porphyry quotes Numenius on the Jewish belief that "the souls rest on water, which is divinely animated," based on [Genesis 1:2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesis_1:2), whose traditional author, [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses), is referred to as "the [Prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet)." Elsewhere, in *Ad Gaurum* 11, Moses is instead styled "the theologian of the Hebrews," introduced in connection with [Genesis 2:7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesis_2:7), which states that "when the human body was formed and had received all of its bodily workmanship, God breathed the spirit into it to act as a living soul."[^o21] In *De abstinentia*, he presents an abridgement of [Josephus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephus)'s *[The Jewish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jewish_War)* 2.119–161, describing the [Essene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essenes) sect as *semnotate*, rendered "most august," and adds the [bird-nest law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiluach_haken) from [Deuteronomy 22](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuteronomy_22):6–7 (or possibly drawing instead on Josephus, *[Against Apion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Against_Apion)* 2.213).[^o21]

Porphyry accused Christians of becoming "zealots for the strange mythologies of the Jews, which are in ill repute among all men," citing [Genesis 2:17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesis_2_%28Bible%29) as an example and asking why God would deny man not only the knowledge of evil, but of good as well."[^o21] Elsewhere, however, Augustine reports that Porphyry credited the Jews with a [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Judaism) superior to the [Christian one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_theology).[^o21] He also has [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus) justly condemned by "right-thinking judges", a motif shared with the [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud).[^o21]
