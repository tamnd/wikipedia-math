# Curvature of a measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Curvature of a measure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **curvature of a measure** defined on the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) **R**<sup>2</sup> is a quantification of how much the measure's "distribution of mass" is "curved". It is related to notions of [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446). In the form presented below, the concept was introduced in 1995 by the [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Mark S. Melnikov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_S._Melnikov); accordingly, it may be referred to as the **Melnikov curvature** or **Menger-Melnikov curvature**. Melnikov and Verdera (1995) established a powerful connection between the curvature of measures and the [Cauchy kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_integral_formula).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *μ* be a [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) on the Euclidean plane **R**<sup>2</sup>. Given three (distinct) points *x*, *y* and *z* in **R**<sup>2</sup>, let *R*(*x*, *y*, *z*) be the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the Euclidean [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) that joins all three of them, or +∞ if they are [collinear](wikipedia://en/946975). The [Menger curvature](wikipedia://en/11830372) *c*(*x*, *y*, *z*) is defined to be

$$c(x, y, z) = \frac{1}{R(x, y, z)},$$

with the natural convention that *c*(*x*, *y*, *z*) = 0 if *x*, *y* and *z* are collinear. It is also conventional to extend this definition by setting *c*(*x*, *y*, *z*) = 0 if any of the points *x*, *y* and *z* coincide. The **Menger-Melnikov curvature** *c*<sup>2</sup>(*μ*) of *μ* is defined to be

$$c^{2} (\mu) = \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}} c(x, y, z)^{2} \, \mathrm{d} \mu (x) \mathrm{d} \mu (y) \mathrm{d} \mu (z).$$

More generally, for *α* ≥ 0, define *c*<sup>2*α*</sup>(*μ*) by

$$c^{2 \alpha} (\mu) = \iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}} c(x, y, z)^{2 \alpha} \, \mathrm{d} \mu (x) \mathrm{d} \mu (y) \mathrm{d} \mu (z).$$

One may also refer to the curvature of *μ* at a given point *x*:

$$c^{2} (\mu; x) = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}} c(x, y, z)^{2} \, \mathrm{d} \mu (y) \mathrm{d} \mu (z),$$

in which case

$$c^{2} (\mu) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}} c^{2} (\mu; x) \, \mathrm{d} \mu (x).$$

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [trivial measure](wikipedia://en/10122069) has zero curvature.
- A [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) *δ*<sub>*a*</sub> supported at any point *a* has zero curvature.
- If *μ* is any measure whose [support](wikipedia://en/6591796) is contained within a Euclidean line *L*, then *μ* has zero curvature. For example, one-dimensional [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on any line (or line segment) has zero curvature.
- The Lebesgue measure defined on all of **R**<sup>2</sup> has infinite curvature.
- If *μ* is the uniform one-dimensional [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109) on a circle *C*<sub>*r*</sub> or radius *r*, then *μ* has curvature 1/*r*.

:::

## Relationship to the Cauchy kernel {#Relationship_to_the_Cauchy_kernel}

In this section, **R**<sup>2</sup> is thought of as the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) **C**. Melnikov and Verdera (1995) showed the precise relation of the [boundedness](wikipedia://en/455961) of the Cauchy kernel to the curvature of measures. They proved that if there is some constant *C*<sub>0</sub> such that

$$\mu(B_{r} (x)) \leq C_{0} r$$

for all *x* in **C** and all *r* > 0, then there is another constant *C*, depending only on *C*<sub>0</sub>, such that

$$\left| 6 \int_{\mathbb{C}} | \mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon} (\mu) (z) |^{2} \, \mathrm{d} \mu (z) - c_{\varepsilon}^{2} (\mu) \right| \leq C \| \mu \|$$

for all *ε* > 0. Here *c*<sub>*ε*</sub> denotes a truncated version of the Menger-Melnikov curvature in which the integral is taken only over those points *x*, *y* and *z* such that

$$| x - y | > \varepsilon;$$

$$| y - z | > \varepsilon;$$

$$| z - x | > \varepsilon.$$

Similarly, $\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}$ denotes a truncated Cauchy integral operator: for a measure *μ* on **C** and a point *z* in **C**, define

$$\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon} (\mu) (z) = \int \frac{1}{\xi - z} \, \mathrm{d} \mu (\xi),$$

where the integral is taken over those points *ξ* in **C** with

$$| \xi - z | > \varepsilon.$$
