# Complex conjugate root theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complex conjugate root theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **complex conjugate root theorem** states that if *P* is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in one variable with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), and *a* + *bi* is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of *P* with *a* and *b* being [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), then its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) *a* − *bi* is also a root of *P*.[^o2]

:::

It follows from this (and the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414)) that, if the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of a real [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135), it must have at least one real root.[^o3] That fact can also be [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by using the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884).

## Examples and consequences {#Examples_and_consequences}

- The polynomial $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has two imaginary roots: $\pm i$
- Any real [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) of odd degree has at least one real [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue). For example, if the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/105620), then 1 or −1 is an eigenvalue.
- The polynomial

$$x^3 - 7x^2 + 41x - 87$$

has roots of

$$3,\, 2 + 5i,\, 2 - 5i,$$

and thus can be factored as

$$(x - 3)(x - 2 - 5i)(x - 2 + 5i).$$

In computing the product of the last two factors, the [imaginary parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) cancel, and we get

$$(x - 3)(x^2 - 4x + 29).$$

The non-real factors come in pairs which when multiplied give [quadratic polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) with real coefficients. Since every polynomial with [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) coefficients can be factored into 1st-degree factors (that is one way of stating the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414)), it follows that every polynomial with real coefficients can be factored into factors of degree no higher than 2: just 1st-degree and quadratic factors.

- If the roots are $a+bi$ and $a-bi$, they form a quadratic

$$x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + b^2).$$

If the third root is $c$, this becomes

$$(x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + b^2))(x-c)$$

$$=x^3 + x^2(-2a-c) + x(2ac+a^2+b^2) - c(a^2 + b^2).$$

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary on odd-degree polynomials"}

### Corollary on odd-degree polynomials {#Corollary_on_odd-degree_polynomials}

It follows from the present theorem and the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414) that if the degree of a real polynomial is odd, it must have at least one real root.[^o3]

This can be proved as follows.

- Since non-real complex roots come in conjugate pairs, there are an [even number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) of them;
- But a polynomial of odd degree has an odd number of roots ([fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414));
- Therefore, some of them must be real.

This requires some care in the presence of [multiple roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_root); but a complex root and its conjugate do have the same [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) (and this [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634) is not hard to prove). It can also be worked around by considering only [irreducible polynomials](wikipedia://en/188725); any real polynomial of odd degree must have an irreducible factor of odd degree, which (having no multiple roots) must have a real root by the reasoning above.

This [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) can also be proved directly by using the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884).

:::

## Proof {#Proof}

One proof of the theorem is as follows:[^o3]

Consider the polynomial

$$P(z) = a_0 + a_1z + a_2z^2 + \cdots + a_nz^n$$

where all *a*<sub>*r*</sub> are real. Suppose some [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) *ζ* is a root of *P*, that is $P(\zeta) = 0$. It needs to be shown that

$$P\big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big) = 0$$

as well.

If *P*(*ζ*) = 0, then

$$a_0 + a_1\zeta + a_2\zeta^2 + \cdots + a_n\zeta^n = 0$$

which can be put as

$$\sum_{r=0}^n a_r\zeta^r = 0.$$

Now

$$P\big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big) = \sum_{r=0}^n a_r \big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big)^r$$

and given the [properties of complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation#Properties),

$$\sum_{r=0}^n a_r\big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big)^r = \sum_{r=0}^n a_r \overline{\zeta^r} = \sum_{r=0}^n \overline{a_r\zeta^r} = \overline{\sum_{r=0}^n a_r\zeta^r}.$$

Since

$$\overline{\sum_{r=0}^n a_r\zeta^r} = \overline{0},$$

it follows that

$$\sum_{r=0}^n a_r\big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big)^r = \overline{0} = 0.$$

That is,

$$P\big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big) = a_0 + a_1\overline{\zeta} + a_2\big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big)^2 + \cdots + a_n\big(\, \overline{\zeta} \,\big)^n = 0.$$

Note that this works only because the *a*<sub>*r*</sub> are real, that is, $\overline{a_r} = a_r$. If any of the coefficients were non-real, the roots would not necessarily come in conjugate pairs. In addition, one can show that for any $\zeta \in \mathbb{C}$, it holds that $P(\overline{\zeta})=\overline{P(\zeta)}$ even if $P(\zeta) \neq 0$.

:::
