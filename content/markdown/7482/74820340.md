# Random generalized Lotka–Volterra model

*Image: RGLV example dynamics.png.* Example dynamics in the unique fixed point and multiple attractors phases with $S=64$ species. For both simulations $(\mu_\alpha,\gamma,K,\sigma_K) = (4,0,1,0)$. For UFP, $\sigma_\alpha = 1$; for MA, $\sigma_\alpha = 2$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Random generalized Lotka–Volterra model"}

The **random generalized Lotka–Volterra model** (rGLV) is an [ecological model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_model) and [random set of coupled ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/7185428) where the parameters of the [generalized Lotka–Volterra equation](wikipedia://en/24018375) are sampled from a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543), analogously to [quenched disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quenched_disorder). The rGLV models dynamics of a community of species in which each species' abundance grows towards a [carrying capacity](wikipedia://en/47544) but is depleted due to [competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competition_%28biology%29) from the presence of other species. It is often analyzed in the [many-species limit](wikipedia://en/2262550) using tools from [statistical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics), in particular from [spin glass](wikipedia://en/148555) theory.

:::

The rGLV has been used as a tool to analyze [emergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence) macroscopic behavior in [microbial communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbial_communities) with dense, strong interspecies interactions. The model has served as a context for theoretical investigations studying [diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diversity_%28ecology%29)-[stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_%28ecology%29) relations in community ecology[^o10] and properties of static and dynamic [coexistence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coexistence_theory).[^o11][^o2] Dynamical behavior in the rGLV has been mapped experimentally in community microcosms.[^o12] The rGLV model has also served as an object of interest for the spin glass and disordered systems physics community to develop new techniques and numerical methods.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7]

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The random generalized Lotka–Volterra model is written as the system of [coupled ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753),[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13]

$$\frac{\mathrm dN_i}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{r_i}{K_i}N_i \left(K_i - N_i - \sum_{j (\neq i)} \alpha_{ij} N_j\right), \qquad i = 1,\dots,S,$$

where $N_i$ is the abundance of species $i$, $S$ is the number of species, $K_i$ is the carrying capacity of species $i$ in the absence of interactions, $r_i$ sets a timescale, and $\alpha$ is a [random matrix](wikipedia://en/1648765) whose entries are [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) with mean $\langle \alpha_{ij}\rangle = \mu_\alpha/S$, variance $\mathrm{var}(\alpha_{ij}) = \sigma_\alpha^2/S$, and [correlations](wikipedia://en/157057) $\mathrm{corr}(\alpha_{ij}, \alpha_{ji}) =\gamma$ for $i \neq j$ where $-1\leq \gamma \leq 1$. The [interaction matrix](wikipedia://en/22174129), $\alpha$, may be parameterized as,

$$\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\mu_\alpha}{S} + \frac{\sigma_\alpha}{\sqrt{S}} a_{ij},$$

where $a_{ij}$ are standard random variables (i.e., zero mean and unit variance) with $\langle a_{ij} a_{ji}\rangle = \gamma$ for $i \neq j$. The matrix entries may have any distribution with common finite first and second moments and will yield identical results in the large $S$ limit due to the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406). The carrying capacities may also be treated as random variables with $\langle K_i \rangle = K,\,\operatorname{var}(K_i) =\sigma_K^2.$ Analyses by statistical physics-inspired methods have revealed [phase transitions](wikipedia://en/54423) between different qualitative behaviors of the model in the [many-species limit](wikipedia://en/2262550). In some cases, this may include transitions between the existence of a [unique globally-attractive fixed point](wikipedia://en/1326107) and [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295), persistent fluctuations.

:::

## Steady-state abundances in the thermodynamic limit {#Steady-state_abundances_in_the_thermodynamic_limit}

In the [thermodynamic limit](wikipedia://en/2262550) (i.e., the community has a very large number of species) where a unique globally-attractive fixed point exists, the distribution of species abundances can be computed using the [cavity method](wikipedia://en/4263491) while assuming the system is [self-averaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-averaging). The self-averaging assumption means that the distribution of any one species' abundance between samplings of model parameters matches the distribution of species abundances within a single sampling of model parameters. In the cavity method, an additional [mean-field](wikipedia://en/555768) species $i = 0$ is introduced and the response of the system is approximated linearly. The cavity calculation yields a [self-consistent equation](wikipedia://en/555768) describing the distribution of species abundances as a mean-field random variable, $N_0$. When $\sigma_K =0$, the mean-field equation is,[^o10]

$$0 = N_0 \left( K - \mu_\alpha m- N_0 +\sqrt{q\left(\mu_\alpha^2 + \gamma \sigma_\alpha^2\right)} Z + \sigma_\alpha^2 \gamma\chi N_0\right),$$

where $m = \langle N_0\rangle ,\,q=\langle N_0^2\rangle, \,\chi = \langle \partial N_0/\partial K_0\rangle$, and $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is a [standard normal random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_random_variable). Only [ecologically uninvadable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasibility) solutions are taken (i.e., the largest solution for $N_0$ in the quadratic equation is selected). The relevant susceptibility and moments of $N_0$, which has a [truncated normal distribution](wikipedia://en/11753597), are determined self-consistently.

## Dynamical phases {#Dynamical_phases}

In the thermodynamic limit where there is an asymptotically large number of species (i.e., $S \to \infty$), there are three distinct [phases](wikipedia://en/54423): one in which there is a unique fixed point (UFP), another with a multiple [attractors](wikipedia://en/191094) (MA), and a third with unbounded growth. In the MA phase, depending on whether species abundances are replenished at a small rate, may approach arbitrarily small population sizes, or are removed from the community when the population falls below some cutoff, the resulting dynamics may be chaotic with persistent fluctuations or approach an initial conditions-dependent steady state.[^o10]

The transition from the UFP to MA phase is signaled by the cavity solution becoming unstable to disordered perturbations. When $\sigma_K = 0$, the [phase transition boundary](wikipedia://en/54423) occurs when the parameters satisfy,

$$\sigma_\alpha = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1+\gamma}.$$

In the $\sigma_K > 0$ case, the phase boundary can still be calculated analytically, but no closed-form solution has been found; numerical methods are necessary to solve the self-consistent equations determining the phase boundary.

The transition to the unbounded growth phase is signaled by the divergence of $\langle N_0 \rangle$ as computed in the cavity calculation.

## Dynamical mean-field theory {#Dynamical_mean-field_theory}

The cavity method can also be used to derive a [dynamical mean-field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_mean-field_theory) model for the dynamics. The cavity calculation yields a self-consistent equation describing the dynamics as a [Gaussian process](wikipedia://en/302944) defined by the self-consistent equation (for $\sigma_K = 0$),[^o6]

$$\frac{\mathrm dN_0}{\mathrm d t} = N_0(t) \left[ K_0 - N_0(t) - \mu_\alpha m(t) - \sigma_\alpha \eta(t) + \gamma \sigma_\alpha^2 \int_0^t\mathrm dt'\, \chi(t,t') N_0(t') \right],$$

where $m(t) = \langle N_0(t)\rangle$, $\eta$ is a zero-mean Gaussian process with [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) $\langle \eta(t)\eta(t')\rangle = \langle N_0(t)N_0(t')\rangle$, and $\chi(t,t') = \langle \left.\delta N_0(t)/\delta K_0(t')\right|_{K_0(t') = K_0} \rangle$ is the dynamical susceptibility defined in terms of a [functional derivative](wikipedia://en/301504) of the dynamics with respect to a time-dependent perturbation of the carrying capacity.

Using dynamical mean-field theory, it has been shown that at long times, the dynamics exhibit aging in which the characteristic time scale defining the decay of correlations increases linearly in the duration of the dynamics. That is, $C_N(t,t+t\tau) \to f(\tau)$ when $t$ is large, where $C_N(t,t') = \langle N(t)N(t')\rangle$ is the autocorrelation function of the dynamics and $f(\tau)$ is a common scaling collapse function.[^o6][^o17]

When a small immigration rate $\lambda \ll 1$ is added (i.e., a small constant is added to the right-hand side of the equations of motion) the dynamics reach a [time transitionally invariant](wikipedia://en/329898) state. In this case, the dynamics exhibit jumps between $O(1)$ and $O(\lambda)$ abundances.[^o18]
