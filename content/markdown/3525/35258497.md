# Telephone number (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Telephone number"}

*Image: K4 matchings.svg.* The [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{4}$ has ten matchings, corresponding to the value $T(4) = 10$ of the fourth telephone number.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **telephone numbers** or the **involution numbers** form a [sequence of integers](wikipedia://en/51421) that count the ways $n$ people can be connected by person-to-person telephone calls. These numbers also describe the number of [matchings](wikipedia://en/581797) (the [Hosoya index](wikipedia://en/14221581)) of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on $n$ vertices, the number of [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) on $n$ elements that are [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450), the sum of absolute values of [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) of the [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial), the number of standard [Young tableaux](wikipedia://en/683368) with $n$ cells, and the sum of the degrees of the [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901). Involution numbers were first studied in 1800 by [Heinrich August Rothe](wikipedia://en/35235049), who gave a [recurrence equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurrence_equation) by which they may be calculated,[^o2] giving the values (starting from $n = 0$)

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

[John Riordan](wikipedia://en/11751393) provides the following explanation for these numbers: suppose that $n$ people subscribe to a telephone service that can connect any two of them by a call, but cannot make a single call connecting more than two people. How many different patterns of connection are possible? For instance, with three subscribers, there are three ways of forming a single telephone call, and one additional pattern in which no calls are being made, for a total of four patterns.[^o5] For this reason, the numbers counting how many patterns are possible are sometimes called the telephone numbers.[^o6][^o7]

Every pattern of pairwise connections between $n$ people defines an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450), a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of the people that is its own inverse. In this permutation, each two people who call each other are swapped, and the people not involved in calls remain fixed in place. Conversely, every possible involution has the form of a set of pairwise swaps of this type. Therefore, the telephone numbers also count involutions. The problem of counting involutions was the original [combinatorial enumeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_enumeration) problem studied by Rothe in 1800[^o2] and these numbers have also been called involution numbers.[^o8][^o9]

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a subset of the edges of a graph that touches each vertex at most once is called a [matching](wikipedia://en/581797). Counting the matchings of a given graph is important in [chemical graph theory](wikipedia://en/14222708), where the graphs model molecules and the number of matchings is the [Hosoya index](wikipedia://en/14221581). The largest possible Hosoya index of an $n$-vertex graph is given by the [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816), for which any pattern of pairwise connections is possible; thus, the Hosoya index of a complete graph on $n$ vertices is the same as the $n$-th telephone number.[^o10]

*Image: Young tableaux for 541 partition.svg.* A standard Young tableau

A [Ferrers diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrers_diagram) is a geometric shape formed by a collection of $n$ squares in the plane, grouped into a [polyomino](wikipedia://en/72634) with a horizontal top edge, a vertical left edge, and a single monotonic chain of edges from top right to bottom left. A standard [Young tableau](wikipedia://en/683368) is formed by placing the numbers from 1 to $n$ into these squares in such a way that the numbers increase from left to right and from top to bottom throughout the tableau. According to the [Robinson–Schensted correspondence](wikipedia://en/1901026), permutations correspond one-for-one with ordered pairs of standard Young tableaux. Inverting a permutation corresponds to swapping the two tableaux, and so the self-inverse permutations correspond to single tableaux, paired with themselves.[^o11] Thus, the telephone numbers also count the number of Young tableaux with $n$ squares.[^o2] In [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), the Ferrers diagrams correspond to the [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of permutations, and the Young tableaux with a given shape form a basis of the irreducible representation with that shape. Therefore, the telephone numbers give the sum of the degrees of the irreducible representations.[^o12]

floatright

In the [mathematics of chess](wikipedia://en/30681859), the telephone numbers count the number of ways to place $n$ rooks on an $n \times n$ [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) in such a way that no two rooks attack each other (the so-called [eight rooks puzzle](wikipedia://en/10474#other_pieces)), and in such a way that the configuration of the rooks is symmetric under a diagonal reflection of the board. Via the [Pólya enumeration theorem](wikipedia://en/4396962), these numbers form one of the key components of a formula for the overall number of "essentially different" configurations of $n$ mutually non-attacking rooks, where two configurations are counted as essentially different if there is no symmetry of the board that takes one into the other.[^o13]

:::

## Mathematical properties {#Mathematical_properties}

### Recurrence {#Recurrence}

The telephone numbers satisfy the [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806)

$$T(0) = T(1) = 1,$$

$$T(n) = T(n-1) + (n-1)T(n-2), \quad \mathrm{for~} n \ge 2,$$

first published in 1800 by [Heinrich August Rothe](wikipedia://en/35235049), by which they may easily be calculated.[^o2] One way to explain this recurrence is to partition the $T(n)$ connection patterns of the $n$ subscribers to a telephone system into the patterns in which the first person is not calling anyone else, and the patterns in which the first person is making a call. There are $T(n - 1)$ connection patterns in which the first person is disconnected, explaining the first term of the recurrence. If the first person is connected to someone, there are $n - 1$ choices for that person, and $T(n - 2)$ patterns of connection for the remaining $n - 2$ people, explaining the second term of the recurrence.[^o18]

::: {#o17 .statement cue=named-heading name="Summation formula and approximation"}

### Summation formula and approximation {#Summation_formula_and_approximation}

The telephone numbers may be expressed exactly as a [summation](wikipedia://en/246160)

$$T(n) = \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\binom{n}{2k}(2k-1)!! = \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{n!}{2^k (n-2k)! k!}.$$

In each term of the first sum, $k$ gives the number of matched pairs, the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) $\tbinom{n}{2k}$ counts the number of ways of choosing the $2k$ elements to be matched, and the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208)

$$(2k-1)!!=\frac{(2k)!}{2^k\,k!}$$

is the product of the odd integers up to its argument and counts the number of ways of completely matching the $2k$ selected elements.[^o2][^o18] It follows from the summation formula and [Stirling's approximation](wikipedia://en/151783) that, [asymptotically](wikipedia://en/641995),[^o2][^o18][^o19]

$$T(n) \sim \left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n/2} \frac{e^{\sqrt{n}}}{(4e)^{1/4}}\,.$$

:::

### Generating function {#Generating_function}

The [exponential generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_generating_function) of the telephone numbers is[^o18][^o21]

$$G(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{T(n)x^n}{n!}=\exp\left(x+\frac{x^2}{2}\right).$$

In other words, the telephone numbers may be read off as the coefficients of the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of $\exp(x + x^{2}/2)$ and, in particular, the $n$-th telephone number is the value at zero of the $n$-th derivative of this function. The exponential generating function can be derived in a number of ways; for example, taking the recurrence relation for $T(n)$ above, multiplying it by $x^{n-1} / (n - 1)!$, and summing over $n \ge 1$ gives

$$\sum_n T(n) \frac{x^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} = \sum_n T(n-1) \frac{x^{n-1}}{(n-1)!} + x \sum_n T(n-2) \frac{x^{n-2}}{(n-2)!}\,\mathrm{,~which~is}$$

$$G'(x) = G(x) + x G(x)\,.$$

The general solution to this differential equation is $G(x) \propto \exp(x + x^{2}/2)$, and $T(0) = 1$ shows that the constant of proportionality is 1.

This function is closely related to the exponential generating function of the [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial), which are the [matching polynomials](wikipedia://en/24956783) of the complete graphs.[^o21] The sum of absolute values of the coefficients of the $n$-th (probabilist's) Hermite polynomial is the $n$-th telephone number, and the telephone numbers can also be realized as certain special values of the Hermite polynomials:[^o8][^o21]

$$T(n)=\frac{\mathit{He}_n(i)}{i^n}.$$

### Prime factors {#Prime_factors}

For large values of $n$, the $n$-th telephone number is divisible by a large [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948), $2^{n/4 + O(1)}$. More precisely, the [2-adic order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_order) (the number of factors of two in the [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization)) of $T(4k)$ and of $T(4k + 1)$ is $k$; for $T(4k + 2)$ it is $k + 1$, and for $T(4k + 3)$ it is $k + 2$.[^o23]

For any prime number $p$, one can test whether there exists a telephone number divisible by $p$ by computing the recurrence for the sequence of telephone numbers, modulo $p$, until either reaching zero or [detecting a cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_detection).
