# Affine geometry of curves

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Affine geometry of curves"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the **affine geometry of curves** is the study of [curves](wikipedia://en/89246) in an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), and specifically the properties of such curves which are [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under the [special affine group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_affine_group) $\mbox{SL}(n,\mathbb{R}) \ltimes \mathbb{R}^n.$

:::

In the classical [Euclidean geometry of curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry_of_curves), the fundamental tool is the [Frenet&ndash;Serret frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frenet&ndash;Serret_frame). In affine geometry, the Frenet–Serret frame is no longer well-defined, but it is possible to define another canonical [moving frame](wikipedia://en/439082) along a curve which plays a similar decisive role. The theory was developed in the early 20th century, largely from the efforts of [Wilhelm Blaschke](wikipedia://en/728998) and [Jean Favard](wikipedia://en/9995378).

## The affine frame {#The_affine_frame}

Let **x**(*t*) be a curve in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Assume, as one does in the Euclidean case, that the first *n* derivatives of **x**(*t*) are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) so that, in particular, **x**(*t*) does not lie in any lower-dimensional affine subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then the curve parameter *t* can be normalized by setting [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468)

$$\det \begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{x}}, &\ddot{\mathbf{x}}, &\dots, &{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)} \end{bmatrix} = \pm 1.$$

Such a curve is said to be parametrized by its *[affine arclength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_arclength)*. For such a parameterization,

$$t\mapsto [\mathbf{x}(t),\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t),\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}(t)]$$

determines a mapping into the special affine group, known as a special affine frame for the curve. That is, at each point of the quantities $\mathbf{x},\dot{\mathbf{x}},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}$ define a special [affine frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_frame) for the affine space $\mathbb{R}^n$, consisting of a point **x** of the space and a special linear basis $\dot{\mathbf{x}},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}$ attached to the point at **x**. The [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) of the [Maurer&ndash;Cartan form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurer&ndash;Cartan_form) along this map gives a complete set of affine structural invariants of the curve. In the plane, this gives a single scalar invariant, the [affine curvature](wikipedia://en/4688641) of the curve.

## Discrete invariant {#Discrete_invariant}

The normalization of the curve parameter *s* was selected above so that

$$\det \begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{x}}, &\ddot{\mathbf{x}}, &\dots, &{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)} \end{bmatrix} = \pm 1.$$

If *n*≡0 (mod 4) or *n*≡3 (mod 4) then the sign of this determinant is a discrete invariant of the curve. A curve is called **dextrorse** (right winding, frequently *weinwendig* in German) if it is +1, and **sinistrorse** (left winding, frequently *hopfenwendig* in German) if it is −1.

In three-dimensions, a right-handed [helix](wikipedia://en/179924) is dextrorse, and a left-handed helix is sinistrorse.

## Curvature {#Curvature}

Suppose that the curve **x** in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is parameterized by affine arclength. Then the **[affine curvatures](wikipedia://en/4688641)**, *k*<sub>1</sub>, …, *k*<sub>*n*−1</sub> of **x** are defined by

$$\mathbf{x}^{(n+1)} = k_1\dot{\mathbf{x}} +\cdots + k_{n-1}\mathbf{x}^{(n-1)}.$$

That such an expression is possible follows by computing the derivative of the determinant

$$0=\det \begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{x}}, &\ddot{\mathbf{x}}, &\dots, &{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)} \end{bmatrix}\dot{}\, = \det \begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{x}}, &\ddot{\mathbf{x}}, &\dots, &{\mathbf{x}}^{(n+1)} \end{bmatrix}$$

so that **x**<sup>(*n*+1)</sup> is a linear combination of **x**′, …, **x**<sup>(*n*−1)</sup>.

Consider the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859)

$$A = \begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{x}}, &\ddot{\mathbf{x}}, &\dots, &{\mathbf{x}}^{(n)} \end{bmatrix}$$

whose columns are the first *n* derivatives of **x** (still parameterized by special affine arclength). Then,

$$\dot{A} = \begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\ 0&0&1&0&\cdots&0&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\ 0&0&0&0&\cdots&1&0\\ 0&0&0&0&\cdots&0&1\\ k_1&k_2&k_3&k_4&\cdots&k_{n-1}&0 \end{bmatrix}A = CA.$$

In concrete terms, the matrix *C* is the [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) of the Maurer–Cartan form of the special linear group along the frame given by the first *n* derivatives of **x**.
