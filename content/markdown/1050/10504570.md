# Lift (mathematics)

*Image: Lifting diagram.svg.* The morphism *h* is a lift of *f* ([commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484))

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lift"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), given a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) *f*: *X* → *Y* and a morphism *g*: *Z* → *Y*, a **lift** or **lifting** of *f* to *Z* is a morphism *h*: *X* → *Z* such that *f* = *g* ∘ *h* (in terms of the [composition operator](wikipedia://en/2702039)). We say that *f* [*factors through*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematical_jargon#factor_through) *h*.

:::

Lifts are ubiquitous; for example, the definition of [fibrations](wikipedia://en/316826) (see [Homotopy lifting property](wikipedia://en/522786)) and the valuative criteria of [separated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separated_morphism) and [proper maps](wikipedia://en/1190262) of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) are formulated in terms of existence and (in the last case) [uniqueness](wikipedia://en/29759120) of certain lifts.

In [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) and the [Hom functor](wikipedia://en/2741037) are [adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor-hom_adjunction); however, they might not always lift to an [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215). This leads to the definition of the [Tor functor](wikipedia://en/856347) and the [Ext functor](wikipedia://en/840758).

## Covering space {#Covering_space}

A basic example in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) is lifting a [path](wikipedia://en/1189553) in one [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) to a path in a [covering space](wikipedia://en/250323).[^o3] For example, consider mapping opposite points on a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) to the same point, a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) from the sphere covering the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). A path in the projective plane is a continuous map from the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) \[0,1\]. We can lift such a path to the sphere by choosing one of the two sphere points mapping to the first point on the path, then maintain continuity. In this case, each of the two starting points forces a unique path on the sphere, the lift of the path in the projective plane. Thus in the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) with continuous maps as morphisms, we have

$$\begin{aligned} f\colon\, &[0,1] \to \mathbb{RP}^2 &&\ \text{ (projective plane path)} \\ g\colon\, &S^2 \to \mathbb{RP}^2 &&\ \text{ (covering map)} \\ h\colon\, &[0,1] \to S^2 &&\ \text{ (sphere path)} \end{aligned}$$

## Algebraic logic {#Algebraic_logic}

The notations of [first-order predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_predicate_logic) are streamlined when [quantifiers](wikipedia://en/43507260) are relegated to established domains and ranges of [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931). [Gunther Schmidt](wikipedia://en/44939247) and Michael Winter have illustrated the method of lifting traditional logical expressions of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) to calculus of relations in their book *Relational Topology*.[^o5] They aim "to lift concepts to a relational level making them point free as well as quantifier free, thus liberating them from the style of first order predicate logic and approaching the clarity of algebraic reasoning."

For example, a [partial function](wikipedia://en/23577) *M* corresponds to the inclusion $M^T ; M \subseteq I$ where $I$ denotes the identity relation on the range of *M*. "The notation for quantification is hidden and stays deeply incorporated in the typing of the relational operations (here transposition and composition) and their rules."

## Circle maps {#Circle_maps}

For maps of a circle, the definition of a lift to the real line is slightly different (a common application is the calculation of [rotation number](wikipedia://en/3928569)). Given a map on a circle, $T:\text{S}\rightarrow\text{S}$, a lift of $T$, $F_T$, is any map on the real line, $F_T:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$, for which there exists a projection (or, [covering map](wikipedia://en/250323)), $\pi: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \text{S}$, such that $\pi \circ F_T = T \circ \pi$.[^o7]
