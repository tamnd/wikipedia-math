# Lifting-line theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lifting-line theory"}

The **Lanchester–Prandtl lifting-line theory**[^o2] is a mathematical model in [aerodynamics](wikipedia://en/2819) that predicts [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29) distribution over a three-dimensional [wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wing) from the wing's [geometry](wikipedia://en/169191).[^o3] The theory was expressed independently[^o4] by [Frederick W. Lanchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_W._Lanchester) in 1907,[^o5] and by [Ludwig Prandtl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Prandtl) in 1918–1919[^o6] after working with [Albert Betz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Betz) and [Max Munk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Munk). In this model, the vortex bound to the wing develops along the whole wingspan because it is shed as a vortex-sheet from the trailing edge, rather than just as a single vortex from the wing-tips.[^o7][^o8]

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

*Image: 1915ca\_laminar\_fluegel(cropped).jpg.* Airfoils in two dimensions are easier to understand, but they do not directly map to three-dimensional finite wings

It is difficult to predict analytically the overall amount of lift that a wing of given geometry will generate. When analyzing a three-dimensional [finite wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_wing), a traditional approach [slices the wing into cross-sections](wikipedia://en/5744114) and analyzes each cross-section independently as a wing in a two-dimensional world. Each of these slices is called an [airfoil](wikipedia://en/290053), and it is easier to understand an airfoil than a complete three-dimensional wing.

One might expect that understanding the full wing simply involves adding up the independently calculated forces from each airfoil segment. However, this approximation is grossly incorrect: on a real wing, the lift from each infinitesimal wing section is strongly affected by the airflow over neighboring wing sections. Lifting-line theory corrects some of the errors in the naive two-dimensional approach by including some interactions between the wing slices.

::: {#o11 .statement cue=named-heading name="Principle and derivation"}

## Principle and derivation {#Principle_and_derivation}

Lifting line theory supposes wings that are [long and thin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_%28aeronautics%29) with negligible [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage), akin to a thin bar (the eponymous "lifting line") of [span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingspan) $2s$ driven through the fluid. From the [Kutta–Joukowski theorem](wikipedia://en/8606325), the lift $L(y)$ on a 2-dimensional segment of the wing at distance $y$ from the [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage) is proportional to the [circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulation_%28fluid_dynamics%29) $\Gamma(y)$ about the bar at $y$. When the aircraft is stationary on the ground, these circulations are all equal, but when the craft is in motion, they vary with $y$. By [Helmholtz's theorems](wikipedia://en/477513), the generation of spatially-varying circulation must correspond to shedding [an equal-strength vortex filament downstream from the wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_vortex).[^o12]

In the lifting line theory, the resulting vortex line is presumed to remain [bound to the wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_vortex), so that it changes the effective vertical angle of the incoming [freestream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freestream) air.

The vertical motion induced by a vortex line of strength $\gamma$ on air a distance $r$ away is $\tfrac{\gamma}{4\pi r}$, so that the entire vortex system induces a freestream vertical motion at position $y$ of

$$w(y)=\int_{-s}^s{\frac{d\Gamma(\tilde{y})}{4\pi(y-\tilde{y})}},$$

where the integral is understood in the sense of a [Cauchy principal value](wikipedia://en/408195). This flow changes the effective [angle of attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_attack) at $y$; if the circulation response of the airfoils comprising the wing are understood over a range of attack angles, then one can develop an [integral equation](wikipedia://en/474234) to determine $\Gamma(y)$.[^n1]

Formally, there is some angle of orientation such that the airfoil at position $y$ develops no lift. For airstreams of velocity $V$ oriented at an angle $\alpha$ relative to the liftless angle, the airfoil will develop some circulation $V\cdot C(y,\alpha)$; for small $\alpha$, [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) approximates that circulation as $V\cdot\tfrac{\partial C}{\partial\alpha}(y,0)\cdot\alpha$. If the airfoil is ideal and has chord $c(y)$, then [theory](wikipedia://en/4265190) predicts that

$$C(y,\alpha)=2\pi c(y)\sin(\alpha)\text{,}$$

but real airfoils may be less efficient.[^o14][^o15]

Suppose the freestream flow attacks the airfoil at position $y$ at angle $\alpha(y)$ (relative to the liftless angle for the airfoil at position $y$ — thus a uniform flow across a wing may still have varying $\alpha(y)$). By the [small-angle approximation](wikipedia://en/2009207), the effective angle of attack at $y$ of the combined freestream and vortex system is $\alpha(y)+\tfrac{w(y)}{V}$. Combining the above formulae,

::: {#o16 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma(y)&=V\cdot\frac{\partial C}{\partial\alpha}(y,0)\left(\alpha(y)+\frac{w(y)}{V}\right) \\ &=\partial_\alpha C(y,0)\left(V\alpha(y)+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-s}^s{\frac{\Gamma'(\tilde{y})\,dy}{y-\tilde{y}}}\right)\text{.} \end{aligned} \tag{1}$$

:::

All the quantities in this equation except $V$ and $\Gamma$ are geometric properties of the wing, and so an engineer can (in principle) solve for $\Gamma(y)$ given a fixed $V$. As in the derivation of [thin-airfoil theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-airfoil_theory), a common approach is to expand $\Gamma$ as a Fourier series along the wing, and then keep only the first few terms.[^n2][^o18][^o19]

Once the velocity $V$, circulation $\Gamma$, and fluid density $\rho$ are known, the lift generated by the wing is assumed to be the net lift produced by each airfoil with the prescribed circulation...

$$L=\rho V\int_{-s}^s{\Gamma(y)\,dy}$$

...and the drag is likewise the total across airfoils:

$$D=\rho V\int_{-s}^s{\Gamma(y)\alpha(y)\,dy}\text{.}$$

From these quantities and the aspect ratio $A\text{‍}R$, the [span efficiency factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_efficiency_number#Comparison_with_span_efficiency_factor)

$$e=\frac{1}{\pi\mathrm{A\!R}}\cdot\frac{L^2}{\rho VD}$$

may be computed.[^o20][^o21][^o15]

:::

## Effects of control inputs {#Effects_of_control_inputs}

[Control surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_control_surfaces) deflection changes the shape of each airfoil slice, which can produce a different angle-of-no-lift for that airfoil, as well as a different angle-of-attack response. These do not require substantial modification to the theory, only changing $\partial_{\alpha}C(y,0)$ and $\alpha(y)$ in 1. However, a body with rapidly moving wings, such as a rolling aircraft or flapping bird, experiences a vertical flow across the wing due to the wing's change in orientation, which appears as a missing term in the theory.

### Rolling wings {#Rolling_wings}

When the aircraft is rolling at rate $p$ about the fuselage, an airfoil at ([signed](wikipedia://en/7951270)) position $y$ experiences a vertical airflow at rate $py$, which correspondingly adds $\tfrac{py}{V}$ to the effective angle of attack. Thus 1 becomes:

$$\Gamma(y)=\partial_\alpha C(y,0)\left(V\alpha(y)+py+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{-s}^s{\frac{\Gamma'(\tilde{y})\,dy}{y-\tilde{y}}}\right)\text{,}$$

which correspondingly modifies both the lift and the induced drag.[^o24] This "drag force" comprises the main production of thrust for flapping wings.[^o24]

## Elliptical wings {#Elliptical_wings}

The [efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_efficiency) $e$ is theoretically optimized in an elliptical wing with no twist, in which

$$\begin{aligned} y&=s\cos\theta \\ c(y)&=\mathrm{A\!R}\,s\sin\theta\text{,} \end{aligned}$$

where $\theta$ is an alternate parameterization of station along the wing. For such a wing,

$$e = 1\text{,}$$

which yields the equation for the elliptic induced drag coefficient:

$$C_{D_{induced}} = \frac{C_L^2}{\pi A\!R}$$

According to lifting-line theory, any wing planform can achieve the same efficiency through twist (a position-varying increase in [pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_%28aviation%29)) relative to the fuselage.[^o19]

## Useful approximations {#Useful_approximations}

A useful approximation for the 3D [lift coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_coefficient) for elliptical circulation distribution is

$$\ C_{L} = \partial_\alpha C(0,0)\left(1+\frac{2}{\mathrm{A\!R}}\right)^{-1}\alpha$$

Note that this equation becomes the [thin airfoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin_Airfoil_Theory) equation if *AR* goes to infinity.[^o27]

## Limitations {#Limitations}

The lifting line theory does not take into account [compression of the air](wikipedia://en/219144) by the wings, [viscous flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_viscous_flow) within the fuselage's [boundary layer](wikipedia://en/224301), or wing shapes other than the long, straight and thin, such as [swept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swept_wing) or low–[aspect-ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_%28aeronautics%29) wings. The theory also presupposes that flow around the wings is in [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady_flow), and does not address bodies that are quickly accelerated relative to the freestream air.
