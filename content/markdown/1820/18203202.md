# Measurement of a Circle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Measurement of a Circle"}

*Image: A page from Archimedes' Measurement of a Circle.jpg.* A page from Archimedes' Measurement of a Circle

***Measurement of a Circle*** or ***Dimension of the Circle*** ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): Κύκλου μέτρησις, *Kuklou metrēsis*)[^o2] is a [treatise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise) that consists of three propositions, probably made by [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844), ca. 250 BCE.[^o3][^o4] The treatise is only a fraction of what was a longer work.[^o5][^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=named-heading name="Propositions"}

## Propositions {#Propositions}

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=named-heading name="Proposition one"}

### Proposition one {#Proposition_one}

*Image: Area of circle and triangle.svg.* The circle and the triangle are equal in area.

Proposition one states: The area of any circle is equal to a right-angled triangle in which one of the sides about the right angle is equal to the radius, and the other to the circumference of the circle. Any [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) with a [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) *c* and a [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) *r* is equal in [area](wikipedia://en/1209) with a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) with the two [legs](wikipedia://en/2568071) being *c* and *r*. This proposition is proved by the [method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284).[^o11]

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=named-heading name="Proposition two"}

### Proposition two {#Proposition_two}

Proposition two states:

> The area of a circle is to the square on its diameter as 11 to 14.

This proposition could not have been placed by Archimedes, for it relies on the outcome of the third proposition.[^o11]

:::

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=named-heading name="Proposition three"}

### Proposition three {#Proposition_three}

Proposition three states:

> The ratio of the circumference of any circle to its diameter is greater than $3\tfrac{10}{71}$ but less than $3\tfrac{1}{7}$.

This approximates what we now call the [mathematical constant](wikipedia://en/48779551) [π](wikipedia://en/23601). He found these bounds on the value of π by [inscribing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribe) and [circumscribing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribe) a circle with two [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) 96-sided [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306).[^o16]

#### Approximation to square roots {#Approximation_to_square_roots}

This proposition also contains accurate approximations to the [square root of 3](wikipedia://en/9126665) (one larger and one smaller) and other larger non-perfect [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208); however, Archimedes gives no explanation as to how he found these numbers.[^o6] He gives the upper and lower bounds to $\sqrt{3}$ as $\tfrac{1351}{780}$ > $\sqrt{3}$ > $\tfrac{265}{153}$.[^o11] However, these bounds are familiar from the study of [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877) and the convergents of an associated [simple continued fraction](wikipedia://en/46802), leading to much speculation as to how much of this number theory might have been accessible to Archimedes. Discussion of this approach goes back at least to [Thomas Fantet de Lagny](wikipedia://en/54353631), FRS (compare [Chronology of computation of &pi;](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_computation_of_&pi;)) in 1723, but was treated more explicitly by [Hieronymus Georg Zeuthen](wikipedia://en/1732797). In the early 1880s, [Friedrich Otto Hultsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Otto_Hultsch) (1833–1906) and [Karl Heinrich Hunrath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Heinrich_Hunrath) (b. 1847) noted how the bounds could be found quickly by means of simple binomial bounds on square roots close to a perfect square modelled on Elements II.4, 7; this method is favoured by [Thomas Little Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Little_Heath). Although only one route to the bounds is mentioned, in fact there are two others, making the bounds almost inescapable however the method is worked. But the bounds can also be produced by an iterative geometrical construction suggested by Archimedes' [Stomachion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomachion) in the setting of the regular dodecagon. In this case, the task is to give rational approximations to the tangent of π/12.
