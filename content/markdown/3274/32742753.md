# Ordinary differential equation

*Image: Parabolic trajectory.svg.* The [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) of a [projectile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile) launched from a [cannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon) follows a curve determined by an ordinary differential equation that is derived from Newton's second law.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ordinary differential equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **ordinary differential equation** (**ODE**) is a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) (DE) dependent on only a single independent [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109). As with any other DE, its unknown(s) consists of one (or more) [function](wikipedia://en/185427)(s) and involves the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of those functions.[^o2] The term "ordinary" is used in contrast with [*partial* differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) (PDEs) which may be with respect to more than one independent variable,[^o3] and, less commonly, in contrast with [*stochastic* differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_differential_equations) (SDEs) where the modeled process is random.[^o4]

:::

## Differential equations {#Differential_equations}

A [linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868) is a differential equation that is defined by a [linear polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polynomial) in the unknown function and its derivatives, that is an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) of the form

$$a_0(x)y +a_1(x)y' + a_2(x)y'' +\cdots +a_n(x)y^{(n)}+b(x)=0,$$

where $a_0(x),\ldots,a_n(x)$ and $b(x)$ are functions that do not need to be linear, and $y',\ldots, y^{(n)}$ are the successive derivatives of the unknown function $y$ of the variable $x$.[^o6]

Among ordinary differential equations, linear differential equations play a prominent role for several reasons. Most [elementary](wikipedia://en/10412) and [special](wikipedia://en/23709110) functions that are encountered in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969) are solutions of linear differential equations (see [Holonomic function](wikipedia://en/5817043)). When physical phenomena are modeled with non-linear equations, they are generally approximated by linear differential equations for an easier solution. The few non-linear ODEs that can be solved explicitly are generally solved by transforming the equation into an equivalent linear ODE (see, for example [Riccati equation](wikipedia://en/304999)).[^o7]

Some ODEs can be solved explicitly in terms of known functions and [integrals](wikipedia://en/2823). When that is not possible, the equation for computing the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of the solutions may be useful. For applied problems, [numerical methods for ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/272829) can supply an approximation of the solution.[^o8]

## Background {#Background}

Ordinary differential equations (ODEs) arise in many contexts of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [social](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social) and [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science). Mathematical descriptions of change use differentials and derivatives. Various differentials, derivatives, and functions become related via equations, such that a differential equation is a result that describes dynamically changing phenomena, evolution, and variation. Often, quantities are defined as the rate of change of other quantities (for example, derivatives of displacement with respect to time), or [gradients](wikipedia://en/12461) of quantities, which is how they enter differential equations.[^o10]

Specific mathematical fields include [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [analytical mechanics](wikipedia://en/454450). Scientific fields include much of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) (celestial mechanics), [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904) (weather modeling), [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) (reaction rates),[^o11] [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) (infectious diseases, genetic variation), [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology) and [population modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_modeling) (population competition), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) (stock trends, interest rates and the market equilibrium price changes).

Many mathematicians have studied differential equations and contributed to the field, including [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz), the [Bernoulli family](wikipedia://en/199226), [Riccati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riccati), [Clairaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Claude_Clairaut), [d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Alembert), and [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler).

A simple example is [Newton's second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_second_law) of motion—the relationship between the displacement $x$ and the time $t$ of an object under the force $F$, is given by the differential equation

$$m \frac{\mathrm{d}^2 x(t)}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = F(x(t))\,$$

which constrains the [motion of a particle](wikipedia://en/862717) of constant mass $m$. In general, $F$ is a function of the position $x(t)$ of the particle at time $t$. The unknown function $x(t)$ appears on both sides of the differential equation, and is indicated in the notation $F(x(t))$.[^o12][^o13][^o14][^o15]

## Definitions {#Definitions}

In what follows, $y$ represents an unknown function $y=f(x)$ of the [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) $x$. The [notation for differentiation](wikipedia://en/10265555) varies depending upon the author and upon which notation is most useful for the task at hand. In this context, the [Leibniz's notation](wikipedia://en/410712) $\frac{dy}{dx},\frac{d^2y}{dx^2},\ldots, \frac{d^ny}{dx^n}$ is more useful for differentiation and [integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_%28mathematics%29), whereas [Lagrange's notation](wikipedia://en/10265555#Lagrange's_notation) $y',y'',\ldots,y^{(n)}$ is more useful for representing [higher-order derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_derivative) compactly, and [Newton's notation](wikipedia://en/10265555#Newton's_notation) $(\dot y, \ddot y, \overset{...}{y})$ is often used in physics for representing derivatives of low order with respect to time.

::: {#o19 .definition cue=heading name="General definition"}

### General definition {#General_definition}

An *ordinary differential equation* of order $n$ is an expression of the form

$$F\left(x, y, y', y'',\ \ldots,\ y^{(n)}\right) = 0,$$

where $F$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427)—typically a [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) one— of $n+2$ variables, $x$ is an [independent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable), $y$ is an unknown function of $x$, and $y', y'', \ldots, y^{(n)}$ are the $n$ first derivative of $y$. The term "ordinary" is used to distinguish duch an equation from a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564); it is commonly omitted when no confusion may occur, as in the remainder of this article.

By the [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005), most differential equations can, at least theoretically, be put in the form

$$y^{(n)}=F\left (x,y,y',\ldots, y^{(n-1)} \right ),$$

which is called an *[explicit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_function) differential equation*[^o20][^o21] or a differential equation in *normal form*.[^o22]

There are further classifications:

::: {#o23 .entry cue=term name="Autonomous"}

**[Autonomous](wikipedia://en/312398)**

A differential equation is *autonomous* if it does not depend on the independent variable $x$.

:::

::: {#o24 .entry cue=term name="Linear"}

**[Linear](wikipedia://en/379868)**

A differential equation is *linear* if $F$ can be written as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of the dependent variable $y$ and its derivatives; that is,

$$y^{(n)} = a_0y +\sum_{i=1}^{n - 1} a_i(x) y^{(i)} + r(x)$$

where $a_i(x)$ and $r(x)$ are continuous functions of $x$.[^o20][^o25][^o26] The equation has *constant coefficient* if all the $a_i(x)$ are [constant functions](wikipedia://en/320861). The function $r(x)$ is called the *nonhomogeneous term*, *source term*, *forcing term*, or *inhomogeneity*, leading to further classification.[^o25][^o27]

:::

::: {#o28 .entry cue=term name="Homogeneous"}

**[Homogeneous](wikipedia://en/6954092)**

A linear differential equation is *homogeneous* if $r(x)=0$. In this case, there is always the "[trivial solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_solution)" $y=0$.

:::

::: {#o29 .entry cue=term name="Nonhomogeneous (or inhomogeneous)"}

**[Nonhomogeneous (or inhomogeneous)](wikipedia://en/6954092#Homogeneous_linear_differential_equations)**

A linear differential equation is *nonhomogeneous* if $r(x)\neq 0$.

:::

::: {#o30 .entry cue=term name="Non-linear"}

**[Non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_differential_equation)**

A differential equation that is not linear.

:::

:::

### System of ODEs {#System_of_ODEs}

A number of coupled differential equations form a system of equations. If $\mathbf{y}$ is a vector whose elements are functions; $\mathbf{y}(x) = [y_1(x),y_2(x),\ldots, y_m(x)]$, and $\mathbf{F}$ is a [vector-valued function](wikipedia://en/3675281) of $\mathbf{y}$ and its derivatives, then

$$\mathbf{y}^{(n)} = \mathbf{F}\left(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n-1)} \right)$$

is an *explicit system of ordinary differential equations* of *order* $n$ and *dimension* $m$. In [column vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector) form:

$$\begin{pmatrix} y_1^{(n)} \\ y_2^{(n)} \\ \vdots \\ y_m^{(n)} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} f_1 \left (x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n-1)} \right ) \\ f_2 \left (x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n-1)} \right ) \\ \vdots \\ f_m \left (x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n-1)} \right) \end{pmatrix}$$

These are not necessarily linear. The *implicit* analogue is:

$$\mathbf{F} \left(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n)} \right) = \boldsymbol{0}$$

where $\boldsymbol{0}=(0,0,\ldots,0)$ is the [zero vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_vector). In matrix form

$$\begin{pmatrix} f_1(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n)}) \\ f_2(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n)}) \\ \vdots \\ f_m(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}',\mathbf{y}'',\ldots, \mathbf{y}^{(n)}) \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

For a system of the form $\mathbf{F} \left(x,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}'\right) = \boldsymbol{0}$, some sources also require that the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) $\frac{\partial\mathbf{F}(x,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})}{\partial \mathbf{v}}$ be [non-singular](wikipedia://en/245528) in order to call this an implicit ODE \[system\]; an implicit ODE system satisfying this Jacobian non-singularity condition can be transformed into an explicit ODE system. In the same sources, implicit ODE systems with a singular Jacobian are termed [differential algebraic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_algebraic_equation) (DAEs). This distinction is not merely one of terminology; DAEs have fundamentally different characteristics and are generally more involved to solve than (nonsingular) ODE systems.[^o32][^o33][^o34] Presumably for additional derivatives, the [Hessian matrix](wikipedia://en/412108) and so forth are also assumed non-singular according to this scheme, although note that any ODE of order greater than one can be (and usually is) rewritten as system of ODEs of first order,[^o35] which makes the Jacobian singularity criterion sufficient for this taxonomy to be comprehensive at all orders.

The behavior of a system of ODEs can be visualized through the use of a [phase portrait](wikipedia://en/4519252).

### Solutions {#Solutions}

Given a differential equation

$$F\left(x, y, y', \ldots, y^{(n)} \right) = 0$$

a function $u:I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, where $I$ is an interval, is called a *solution* or [integral curve](wikipedia://en/1322061) for $F$, if $u$ is $n$-times differentiable on $I$, and

$$F(x,u,u',\ \ldots,\ u^{(n)})=0 \quad x \in I.$$

Given two solutions $u:J\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ and $v:I\subset\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $u$ is called an *extension* of $v$ if $I\subset J$ and

$$u(x) = v(x) \quad x \in I.\,$$

A solution that has no extension is called a *maximal solution*. A solution defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$ is called a *global solution*.

A *general solution* of an $n$th-order equation is a solution containing $n$ arbitrary independent [constants of integration](wikipedia://en/147939). A *particular solution* is derived from the general solution by setting the constants to particular values, often chosen to fulfill set '[initial conditions](wikipedia://en/498383) or [boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/436779)'.[^o38] A [singular solution](wikipedia://en/506289) is a solution that cannot be obtained by assigning definite values to the arbitrary constants in the general solution.[^o39]

In the context of linear ODE, the terminology *particular solution* can also refer to any solution of the ODE (not necessarily satisfying the initial conditions), which is then added to the *homogeneous* solution (a general solution of the homogeneous ODE), which then forms a general solution of the original ODE. This is the terminology used in the [guessing method](wikipedia://en/32742753#The_guessing_method) section in this article, and is frequently used when discussing the [method of undetermined coefficients](wikipedia://en/1654076) and [variation of parameters](wikipedia://en/1036303).

:::

### Solutions of finite duration {#Solutions_of_finite_duration}

For non-linear autonomous ODEs it is possible under some conditions to develop solutions of finite duration,[^o41] meaning here that from its own dynamics, the system will reach the value zero at an ending time and stays there in zero forever after. These finite-duration solutions can't be analytical functions on the whole real line, and because they will be non-Lipschitz functions at their ending time, they are not included in the uniqueness theorem of solutions of Lipschitz differential equations.

As example, the equation

$$y'= -\text{sgn}(y)\sqrt{|y|},\,\,y(0)=1$$

admits the finite duration solution:

$$y(x)=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\frac{x}{2}+\left|1-\frac{x}{2}\right|\right)^2$$

:::

## Theories {#Theories}

### Singular solutions {#Singular_solutions}

The theory of [singular solutions](wikipedia://en/506289) of ordinary and [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) was a subject of research from the time of Leibniz, but only since the middle of the nineteenth century has it received special attention. A valuable but little-known work on the subject is that of [Houtain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Houtain) (1854). [Darboux](wikipedia://en/1142530) (from 1873) was a leader in the theory, and in the geometric interpretation of these solutions he opened a field worked by various writers, notably [Casorati](wikipedia://en/5697549) and [Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034). To the latter is due (1872) the theory of singular solutions of differential equations of the first order as accepted circa 1900.

### Reduction to quadratures {#Reduction_to_quadratures}

The primitive attempt in dealing with differential equations had in view a reduction to [quadratures](wikipedia://en/1618671), that is, expressing the solutions in terms of known function and their integrals. This is possible for linear equations with constant coefficients, it appeared in the 19th century that this is generally impossible in other cases. Hence, analysts began the study (for their own) of functions that are solutions of differential equations, thus opening a new and fertile field. [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy) was the first to appreciate the importance of this view. Thereafter, the real question was no longer whether a solution is possible by quadratures, but whether a given differential equation suffices for the definition of a function, and, if so, what are the characteristic properties of such functions.

### Fuchsian theory {#Fuchsian_theory}

Two memoirs by [Fuchs](wikipedia://en/2817087)[^o46] inspired a novel approach, subsequently elaborated by Thomé and [Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048). (WHO?)Collet was a prominent contributor beginning in 1869. His method for integrating a non-linear system was communicated to Bertrand in 1868. [Clebsch](wikipedia://en/1369957) (1873) attacked the theory along lines parallel to those in his theory of [Abelian integrals](wikipedia://en/1138853). As the latter can be classified according to the properties of the fundamental curve that remains unchanged under a rational transformation, Clebsch proposed to classify the transcendent functions defined by differential equations according to the invariant properties of the corresponding surfaces $f=0$ under rational one-to-one transformations.

### Lie's theory {#Lie's_theory}

From 1870, [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010)'s work put the theory of differential equations on a better foundation. He showed that the integration theories of the older mathematicians can, using [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), be referred to a common source, and that ordinary differential equations that admit the same [infinitesimal transformations](wikipedia://en/658068) present comparable integration difficulties. He also emphasized the subject of [transformations of contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_transformation).

Lie's group theory of differential equations has been certified, namely: (1) that it unifies the many ad hoc methods known for solving differential equations, and (2) that it provides powerful new ways to find solutions. The theory has applications to both ordinary and partial differential equations.[^o48]

A general solution approach uses the symmetry property of differential equations, the continuous [infinitesimal transformations](wikipedia://en/658068) of solutions to solutions ([Lie theory](wikipedia://en/1595681)). Continuous [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), [Lie algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebras), and [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) are used to understand the structure of linear and non-linear (partial) differential equations for generating integrable equations, to find its [Lax pairs](wikipedia://en/5527029), recursion operators, [Bäcklund transform](wikipedia://en/701077), and finally finding exact analytic solutions to DE.

Symmetry methods have been applied to differential equations that arise in mathematics, physics, engineering, and other disciplines.

### Sturm–Liouville theory {#Sturm–Liouville_theory}

Sturm–Liouville theory is a theory of a special type of second-order linear ordinary differential equation. Their solutions are based on [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) and corresponding [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of linear operators defined via second-order [homogeneous linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087). The problems are identified as Sturm–Liouville problems (SLP) and are named after [J. C. F. Sturm](wikipedia://en/1181554) and [J. Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810), who studied them in the mid-1800s. SLPs have an infinite number of eigenvalues, and the corresponding eigenfunctions form a complete, orthogonal set, which makes orthogonal expansions possible. This is a key idea in applied mathematics, physics, and engineering.[^o50] SLPs are also useful in the analysis of certain partial differential equations.

## Existence and uniqueness of solutions {#Existence_and_uniqueness_of_solutions}

There are several theorems that establish existence and uniqueness of solutions to [initial value problems](wikipedia://en/498383) involving ODEs both locally and globally. The two main theorems are

{| class="wikitable"

\|- ! Theorem ! Assumption ! Conclusion |- |[Peano existence theorem](wikipedia://en/10090547) ||$F$ [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) ||local existence only |- |[Picard–Lindelöf theorem](wikipedia://en/666177) ||$F$ [Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous) ||local existence and uniqueness |}

In their basic form both of these theorems only guarantee local results, though the latter can be extended to give a global result, for example, if the conditions of [Grönwall's inequality](wikipedia://en/1770447) are met.

Also, uniqueness theorems like the Lipschitz one above do not apply to [DAE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_algebraic_equation) systems, which may have multiple solutions stemming from their (non-linear) algebraic part alone.[^o52]

::: {#o54 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Local existence and uniqueness theorem simplified"}

### Local existence and uniqueness theorem simplified {#Local_existence_and_uniqueness_theorem_simplified}

The theorem can be stated simply as follows.[^o55] For the equation and initial value problem:

$$y' = F(x,y)\,,\quad y_0 = y(x_0)$$

if $F$ and $\partial F/\partial y$ are continuous in a closed rectangle

$$R = [x_0-a,x_0+a] \times [y_0-b,y_0+b]$$

in the $x-y$ plane, where $a$ and $b$ are [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) (symbolically: $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$) and $\times$ denotes the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095), square brackets denote [closed intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_notation), then there is an interval

$$I = [x_0-h,x_0+h] \subset [x_0-a,x_0+a]$$

for some $h\in\mathbb{R}$ where *the* solution to the above equation and initial value problem can be found. That is, there is a solution and it is unique. Since there is no restriction on $F$ to be linear, this applies to non-linear equations that take the form $F(x,y)$, and it can also be applied to systems of equations.

:::

### Global uniqueness and maximum domain of solution {#Global_uniqueness_and_maximum_domain_of_solution}

When the hypotheses of the Picard–Lindelöf theorem are satisfied, then local existence and uniqueness can be extended to a global result. More precisely:[^o57]

For each initial condition $(x_0,y_0)$ there exists a unique maximum (possibly infinite) open interval

$$I_{\max} = (x_-,x_+), x_\pm \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{\pm \infty\}, x_0 \in I_{\max}$$

such that any solution that satisfies this initial condition is a [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) of the solution that satisfies this initial condition with domain $I_\max$.

In the case that $x_\pm \neq \pm\infty$, there are exactly two possibilities

- explosion in finite time: $\limsup_{x \to x_\pm} \|y(x)\| \to \infty$
- leaves domain of definition: $\lim_{x \to x_\pm} y(x)\ \in \partial \bar{\Omega}$

where $\Omega$ is the open set in which $F$ is defined, and $\partial \bar{\Omega}$ is its boundary.

Note that the maximum domain of the solution

- is always an interval (to have uniqueness)
- may be smaller than $\mathbb{R}$
- may depend on the specific choice of $(x_0,y_0)$.

::: {#o58 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example.**

$$y' = y^2$$

This means that $F(x,y)=y^2$, which is $C^1$ and therefore locally Lipschitz continuous, satisfying the Picard–Lindelöf theorem.

Even in such a simple setting, the maximum domain of solution cannot be all $\mathbb{R}$ since the solution is

$$y(x) = \frac{y_0}{(x_0-x)y_0+1}$$

which has maximum domain:

$$\begin{cases}\mathbb{R} & y_0 = 0 \\[4pt] \left (-\infty, x_0+\frac{1}{y_0} \right ) & y_0 > 0 \\[4pt] \left (x_0+\frac{1}{y_0},+\infty \right ) & y_0 < 0 \end{cases}$$

This shows clearly that the maximum interval may depend on the initial conditions. The domain of $y$ could be taken as being $\mathbb{R} \setminus (x_0+ 1/y_0),$ but this would lead to a domain that is not an interval, so that the side opposite to the initial condition would be disconnected from the initial condition, and therefore not uniquely determined by it.

The maximum domain is not $\mathbb{R}$ because

$$\lim_{x \to x_\pm} \|y(x)\| \to \infty,$$

which is one of the two possible cases according to the above theorem.

:::

## Reduction of order {#Reduction_of_order}

Differential equations are usually easier to solve if the [order](wikipedia://en/1424309#Order) of the equation can be reduced.

### Reduction to a first-order system {#Reduction_to_a_first-order_system}

Any explicit differential equation of order $n$,

$$F\left(x, y, y', y'',\ \ldots,\ y^{(n-1)}\right) = y^{(n)}$$

can be written as a system of $n$ first-order differential equations by defining a new family of unknown functions

$$y_i = y^{(i-1)}.\!$$

for $i=1,2,\ldots,n$. The $n$-dimensional system of first-order coupled differential equations is then

$$\begin{array}{rcl} y_1'&=&y_2\\ y_2'&=&y_3\\ &\vdots&\\ y_{n-1}'&=&y_n\\ y_n'&=&F(x,y_1,\ldots,y_n). \end{array}$$

more compactly in vector notation:

$$\mathbf{y}'=\mathbf{F}(x,\mathbf{y})$$

where

$$\mathbf{y}=(y_1,\ldots,y_n),\quad \mathbf{F}(x,y_1,\ldots,y_n)=(y_2,\ldots,y_n,F(x,y_1,\ldots,y_n)).$$

## Summary of exact solutions {#Summary_of_exact_solutions}

Some differential equations have solutions that can be written in an exact and closed form. Several important classes are given here.

In the table below, $P(x)$, $Q(x)$, $P(y)$, $Q(y)$, and $M(x,y)$, $N(x,y)$ are any [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable) functions of $x$, $y$; $b$ and $c$ are real given constants; $C_1,C_2,\ldots$ are arbitrary constants ([complex](wikipedia://en/5826) in general). The differential equations are in their equivalent and alternative forms that lead to the solution through integration.

In the integral solutions, $\lambda$ and $\varepsilon$ are dummy variables of integration (the continuum analogues of indices in [summation](wikipedia://en/246160)), and the notation $\int^x F(\lambda)\, d\lambda$ just means to integrate $F(\lambda)$ with respect to $\lambda$, then *after* the integration substitute $\lambda=x$, without adding constants (explicitly stated).

|---|---|---|
| First-order, homogeneous[^o55] $\frac{dy}{dx} = F \left( \frac y x \right )$ | Set *y = ux*, then solve by separation of variables in *u* and *x*. | $\ln (Cx) = \int^{y/x} \frac{d\lambda}{F(\lambda) - \lambda}$ |
| First-order, separable[^o64] $\begin{aligned} yM(xy) + xN(xy)\,\frac{dy}{dx} &= 0 \\ yM(xy)\,dx + xN(xy)\,dy &= 0 \end{aligned}$ | Separation of variables (divide by $xy$). | $\ln (Cx) = \int^{xy} \frac{N(\lambda)\,d\lambda}{\lambda [N(\lambda)-M(\lambda)] }$ If $N=M$, the solution is $xy=C$. |
| [Exact differential](wikipedia://en/2013002), first-order[^o55] $\begin{aligned} M(x,y) \frac{dy}{dx} + N(x,y) &= 0 \\ M(x,y)\,dy + N(x,y)\,dx &= 0 \end{aligned}$ where $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$ | Integrate throughout. | $\begin{aligned} F(x,y) &= \int^x M(\lambda,y)\,d\lambda + \int^y Y(\lambda)\,d\lambda \\ &= \int^y N(x,\lambda)\,d\lambda + \int^x X(\lambda)\,d\lambda=C \end{aligned}$ where $Y(y)= N(x,y)-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int^x M(\lambda,y)\,d\lambda$ and $X(x)= M(x,y)-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int^y N(x,\lambda)\,d\lambda$ |
| [Inexact differential](wikipedia://en/52000501), first-order[^o55] $\begin{aligned} M(x,y) \frac{dy}{dx} + N(x,y) &= 0 \\ M(x,y)\,dy + N(x,y)\,dx &= 0 \end{aligned}$ where $\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \neq \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}$ | [Integration factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integration_factor) $\mu(x,y)$ satisfying $\frac{\partial (\mu M)}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial (\mu N)}{\partial x}$ | If $\mu(x,y)$ can be found in a suitable way, then $\begin{aligned} F(x,y) = &\int^x \mu(\lambda,y)M(\lambda,y)\,d\lambda + \int^y Y(\lambda)\,d\lambda \\ = &\int^y \mu(x,\lambda)N(x,\lambda)\,d\lambda + \int^x X(\lambda)\,d\lambda = C \end{aligned}$ where $Y(y)= N(x,y)-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int^x \mu (\lambda,y)M(\lambda,y)\,d\lambda$ and $X(x)= M(x,y)-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int^y \mu (x,\lambda)N(x,\lambda)\,d\lambda$ |

:::

### General second-order equations {#General_second-order_equations}

::: {#o70 .table cue=table}

| Differential equation | Solution method | General solution |
|---|---|---|
| Second-order, autonomous[^o71] $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = F(y)$ | Multiply both sides of equation by $2 \frac{dy}{dx}$, substitute $2 \frac{dy}{dx} \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx} \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2 = 2 \frac{dy}{dx} F(y)$, then integrate twice. | $x = \pm \int^y \frac{ d \lambda}{\sqrt{2 \int^\lambda F(\varepsilon) \, d \varepsilon + C_1}} + C_2$ |

:::

### Linear to the nth order equations {#Linear_to_the_nth_order_equations}

::: {#o73 .table cue=table}

| Differential equation | Solution method | General solution |
|---|---|---|
| First-order, linear, inhomogeneous, function coefficients[^o55] $\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x)$ | Integrating factor: $e^{\int^x P(\lambda)\,d\lambda}.$ | $y = e^{- \int^x P(\lambda) \, d\lambda}\left[\int^x e^{\int^\lambda P(\varepsilon) \, d\varepsilon}Q(\lambda) \, d\lambda +C \right]$ |
| Second-order, linear, inhomogeneous, function coefficients $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}+2p(x)\frac{dy}{dx}+\left(p(x)^2+p'(x)\right)y=q(x)$ | Integrating factor: $e^{\int^x P(\lambda)\,d\lambda}$ | $y = e^{- \int^x P(\lambda) \, d\lambda}\left[\int^x\left(\int^\xi e^{\int^\lambda P(\varepsilon) \, d\varepsilon}Q(\lambda) \, d\lambda \right)d\xi +C_1x+C_2\right]$ |
| Second-order, linear, inhomogeneous, constant coefficients[^o74] $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + b\frac{dy}{dx} + cy = r(x)$ | Complementary function $y_c$: assume $y_c(x)=e^{\alpha x}$, substitute and solve polynomial in $\alpha$, to find the [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) functions $e^{\alpha_j x}$. Particular integral $y_p$: in general the [method of variation of parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_variation_of_parameters), though for very simple $r(x)$ inspection may work.[^o55] | $y = y_c + y_p$ If $b^2>4c$, then $y_c = C_1e^{ -\frac x2\,\left(b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4c}\right)} + C_2e^{-\frac x2\,\left(b - \sqrt{b^2 - 4c}\right)}$ If $b^2=4c$, then $y_c = (C_1x + C_2)e^{-\frac{bx}{2}}$ If $b^2<4c$, then $y_c = e^{ -\frac{bx}{2}} \left[ C_1 \sin\left( x\,\frac{\sqrt{4c-b^2}}{2}\right) + C_2\cos\left( x\,\frac{\sqrt{4c-b^2}}{2}\right)\right]$ |
| $n$th-order, linear, inhomogeneous, constant coefficients[^o74] $\sum_{j=0}^n b_j \frac{d^j y}{dx^j} = r(x)$ | Complementary function $y_c$: assume $y_c(x)=e^{\alpha x}$, substitute and solve polynomial in $\alpha$, to find the [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) functions $e^{\alpha_j x}$. Particular integral $y_p$: in general the [method of variation of parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_variation_of_parameters), though for very simple $r(x)$ inspection may work.[^o55] | $y = y_c + y_p$ Since $\alpha_j$ are the solutions of the [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $n$: $\prod_{j=1}^n ( \alpha - \alpha_j) = 0$, then: for $\alpha_j$ all different, $y_c = \sum_{j=1}^n C_j e^{\alpha_j x}$ for each root $\alpha_j$ repeated $k_j$ times, $y_c = \sum_{j=1}^n \left( \sum_{\ell=1}^{k_j} C_{j,\ell} x^{\ell-1}\right )e^{\alpha_j x}$ for some $\alpha_j$ complex, then setting $\alpha_j = \chi_j + i\gamma_j$, and using [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613), allows some terms in the previous results to be written in the form $C_j e^{\alpha_j x} = C_j e^{\chi_j x}\cos(\gamma_j x + \varphi_j)$ where $\varphi_j$ is an arbitrary constant (phase shift). |

:::

## Guessing solutions {#Guessing_solutions}

If an ODE satisfies the conditions of the [Picard-Lindelof theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard-Lindelof_theorem), then it has a unique solution, and it suffices to correctly *guess* and verify that solution. Several less-than-completely rigorous methods to guess the solution exist, most notably the [method of undetermined coefficients](wikipedia://en/1654076).[^o76]
