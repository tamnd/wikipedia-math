# Fundamental sequence (set theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fundamental sequence"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), a mathematical discipline, a **fundamental sequence** is a [cofinal](wikipedia://en/1633290) [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfinite_ordinal) all below a given [limit ordinal](wikipedia://en/373137). Depending on author, fundamental sequences may be restricted to ω-sequences only[^o2] or permit fundamental sequences of length [$\mathrm{\omega}_1$](wikipedia://en/20860540).[^o3] The $n^{\text{th}}$ element of the fundamental sequence of $\alpha$ is commonly denoted $\alpha[n]$,[^o3] although it may be denoted $\alpha_n$[^o4] or $\{\alpha\}(n)$.[^o5] Additionally, some authors may allow fundamental sequences to be defined on [successor ordinals](wikipedia://en/375256).[^o6] The term dates back to (at the latest) [Veblen's construction](wikipedia://en/16124925) of [normal functions](wikipedia://en/404306) $\varphi_\alpha$, while the concept dates back to [Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599)'s 1904 attempt to construct a set of [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) $\aleph_1$.[^o7]

:::

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given an ordinal $\alpha$, a **fundamental sequence** for $\alpha$ is a sequence $(\alpha[n])_{n\in\mathbb N}$ such that $\forall(n\in\mathbb N)(\alpha[n]<\alpha)$ and $\textrm{sup}\{\alpha[n]\mid n\in\mathbb N\}=\alpha$.[^o2] An additional restriction may be that the sequence of ordinals must be [strictly increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_increasing).[^o10]

:::

::: {#o12 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The following is a common assignment of fundamental sequences to all limit ordinals less than [$\varepsilon_0$](wikipedia://en/3981747).[^o13][^o5][^o4]

- $$\omega^{\alpha+1}[n]=\omega^\alpha\cdot(n+1)$$
- $\omega^\alpha[n]=\omega^{\alpha[n]}$ for limit ordinals $\alpha$
- $(\omega^{\alpha_1}+\ldots+\omega^{\alpha_k})[n]=\omega^{\alpha_1}+\ldots+(\omega^{\alpha_k}[n])$ for $\alpha_1 \geq \dots \geq \alpha_k$.

This is very similar to the system used in the [Wainer hierarchy](wikipedia://en/24947285#The_Wainer_hierarchy).[^o10]

:::

## Usage {#Usage}

Fundamental sequences arise in some settings of definitions of [large countable ordinals](wikipedia://en/4376814), definitions of [hierarchies of fast-growing functions](wikipedia://en/24947285), and [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478). [Bachmann](wikipedia://en/47768191) defined a hierarchy of functions $\phi_\alpha$ in 1950, providing a system of names for ordinals up to what is now known as the [Bachmann–Howard ordinal](wikipedia://en/12732453), by defining fundamental sequences for namable ordinals below $\omega_1$.[^o15] This system was subsequently simplified by [Feferman](wikipedia://en/355401) and [Aczel](wikipedia://en/24089125) to reduce the reliance on fundamental sequences.[^o16]

The [fast-growing hierarchy](wikipedia://en/24947285), [Hardy hierarchy](wikipedia://en/25264191), and [slow-growing hierarchy](wikipedia://en/25264092) of functions are all defined via a chosen system of fundamental sequences up to a given ordinal. The fast-growing hierarchy is closely related to the Hardy hierarchy, which is used in proof theory along with the slow-growing hierarchy to majorize the provably [computable functions](wikipedia://en/1139338) of a given theory.[^o13][^o17] Fundamental sequences also have applications in [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414) and the [Turing jump](wikipedia://en/1681091).[^o18]

### Additional conditions {#Additional_conditions}

A system of fundamental sequences up to $\alpha$ is said to have the **Bachmann property** if for all ordinals $\alpha,\beta$ in the domain of the system and for all $n\in\mathbb N$, $\alpha[n]<\beta<\alpha\implies\alpha[n]<\beta[0]$. If a system of fundamental sequences has the Bachmann property, all the functions in its associated fast-growing hierarchy are monotone, and $f_\beta$ eventually dominates $f_\alpha$ when $\alpha<\beta$.[^o10]
