# Landau's problems

*Image: Edmund Landau.jpg.* [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230), German mathematician

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Landau's problems"}

At the 1912 [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842), [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230) listed four basic problems about [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666). These problems were characterised in his speech as "unattackable at the present state of mathematics" and are now known as **Landau's problems**. They are as follows:

:::

1. [Goldbach's conjecture](wikipedia://en/44758): Can every even [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) greater than 2 be written as the sum of two primes?
1. [Twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture): Are there infinitely many primes *p* such that *p* + 2 is prime?
1. [Legendre's conjecture](wikipedia://en/2620450): Does there always exist at least one prime between consecutive [perfect squares](wikipedia://en/213919)?
1. Are there infinitely many primes *p* such that *p* − 1 is a perfect square? In other words: Are there infinitely many primes of the form *n*<sup>2</sup> + 1?

, all four problems are unresolved.

## Progress toward solutions {#Progress_toward_solutions}

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Goldbach's conjecture"}

### Goldbach's conjecture {#Goldbach's_conjecture}

[Goldbach's weak conjecture](wikipedia://en/147164), every odd number greater than 5 can be expressed as the sum of three primes, is a consequence of [Goldbach's conjecture](wikipedia://en/44758). [Ivan Vinogradov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Vinogradov) proved it for large enough *n* ([Vinogradov's theorem](wikipedia://en/4633030)) in 1937,[^o5] and [Harald Helfgott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Helfgott) extended this to a full proof of Goldbach's weak conjecture in 2013.[^o6][^o7][^o8]

[Chen's theorem](wikipedia://en/1400840), another weakening of Goldbach's conjecture, proves that for all sufficiently large *n*, $2n=p+q$ where *p* is prime and *q* is either prime or [semiprime](wikipedia://en/339542).[^o9] Bordignon, Johnston, and Starichkova,[^o10] correcting and improving on Yamada,[^o11] proved an explicit version of Chen's theorem: every even number greater than $e^{e^{32.7}} \approx 1.4\cdot10^{69057979807814}$ is the sum of a prime and a product of at most two primes. Bordignon and Starichkova[^o12] reduce this to $e^{e^{15.85}} \approx 3.6\cdot10^{3321634}$ assuming the [Generalized Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/152567) (GRH) for [Dirichlet L-functions](wikipedia://en/400092). Johnston and Starichkova give a version working for all *n* ≥ 4 at the cost of using a number which is the product of at most 395 primes rather than a prime or semiprime; under GRH they improve 395 to 31.[^o13]

[Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Montgomery_%28mathematician%29) and [Vaughan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Charles_Vaughan_%28mathematician%29) showed that the exceptional set of even numbers not expressible as the sum of two primes has a [density](wikipedia://en/1819983) zero, although the set is not proven to be finite.[^o14] The best current bounds on the exceptional set is $E(x) < x^{0.72}$ (for large enough *x*) due to [Pintz](wikipedia://en/22191053),[^o15][^o16] and $E(x) \ll x^{0.5}\log^3 x$ under [RH](wikipedia://en/19344125), due to [Goldston](wikipedia://en/2393553).[^o17]

[Linnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Linnik) proved that large enough even numbers could be expressed as the sum of two primes and some ([ineffective](wikipedia://en/250237)) constant *K* of powers of 2.[^o18] Following many advances (see Pintz[^o19] for an overview), [Pintz](wikipedia://en/22191053) and [Ruzsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Z._Ruzsa)[^o20] improved this to *K* = 8. Assuming the GRH, this can be improved to *K* = 7.[^o21]

:::

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Twin prime conjecture"}

### Twin prime conjecture {#Twin_prime_conjecture}

In 2013 [Yitang Zhang](wikipedia://en/39441844) showed[^o24] that there are infinitely many prime pairs with gap bounded by 70 million, and this result has been improved to gaps of length 246 by a collaborative effort of the [Polymath Project](wikipedia://en/34858344).[^o25] Under the generalized [Elliott–Halberstam conjecture](wikipedia://en/1898427) this was improved to 6, extending earlier work by [Maynard](wikipedia://en/42512102)[^o26] and [Goldston](wikipedia://en/2393553), [Pintz](wikipedia://en/22191053) and [Yıldırım](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cem_Yıldırım).[^o27]

In 1966 [Chen](wikipedia://en/352985) showed that there are infinitely many primes *p* (later called [Chen primes](wikipedia://en/2331143)) such that *p* + 2 is either a prime or a semiprime.

:::

::: {#o29 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Legendre's conjecture"}

### Legendre's conjecture {#Legendre's_conjecture}

It suffices to check that each prime gap starting at *p* is smaller than $2 \sqrt p$. A table of maximal prime gaps shows that the [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) holds to 2<sup>64</sup> ≈ 1.8.[^o30] A [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) near that size would require a prime gap a hundred million times the size of the average gap.

Järviniemi,[^o31] improving on work by Heath-Brown[^o32] and by Matomäki,[^o33] shows that there are at most $x^{7/100+\varepsilon}$ exceptional primes followed by gaps larger than $\sqrt{2p}$; in particular,

$$\sum_{\stackrel{p_{n+1}-p_n > \sqrt{p_n}^{1/2}}{p_n \leq x}}p_{n+1}-p_n\ll x^{0.57+\varepsilon}.$$

A result due to [Ingham](wikipedia://en/5380138) shows that there is a prime between $n^3$ and $(n+1)^3$ for every large enough *n*.[^o34]

:::

::: {#o36 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Near-square prime conjecture"}

### Near-square primes {#Near-square_primes}

Landau's fourth problem asked whether there are infinitely many primes which are of the form $p=n^2+1$ for integer *n*. (The list of known primes of this form is .) The existence of infinitely many such primes would follow as a consequence of other number-theoretic conjectures such as the [Bunyakovsky conjecture](wikipedia://en/12511846) and [Bateman–Horn conjecture](wikipedia://en/1085053).

One example of near-square primes are [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_primes). [Henryk Iwaniec](wikipedia://en/9338319) showed that there are infinitely many numbers of the form $n^2+1$ with at most two prime factors.[^o37][^o38] [Ankeny](wikipedia://en/7677262)[^o39] and [Kubilius](wikipedia://en/22469208)[^o40] proved that, assuming the [extended Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_Riemann_hypothesis) for [*L*-functions](wikipedia://en/245283) on [Hecke characters](wikipedia://en/3149443), there are infinitely many primes of the form $p=x^2+y^2$ with $y=O(\log p)$. Landau's conjecture is for the stronger $y=1$. The best unconditional result is due to Harman and Lewis[^o41] and it gives $y=O(p^{0.119})$. In 2024, [Ben Green](wikipedia://en/1927952) and Mehtaab Sawhney proved that infinitely many primes are of the form $p^2+nq^2$ for any given $n\equiv0$ or $n\equiv4}\pmod{6$, noting that "the argument could surely be modified in a straightforward manner to handle arbitrary positive definite [binary quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/1867129) over $\mathbb{Z}$".[^o42]

Grimmelt & Merikoski,[^o43] improving on previous works,[^o44][^o45][^o46][^o47][^o48][^o49] showed that there are infinitely many numbers of the form $n^2+1$ with greatest prime factor at least $n^{1.312}$. Replacing the exponent with 2 would yield Landau's conjecture.

The [Friedlander–Iwaniec theorem](wikipedia://en/9351265) shows that infinitely many primes are of the form $x^2+y^4$.[^o50]

Baier and Zhao[^o51] prove that there are infinitely many primes of the form $p=an^2+1$ with $a < p^{5/9+\varepsilon}$; the exponent can be improved to $1/2+\varepsilon$ under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for L-functions and to $\varepsilon$ under a certain [Elliott-Halberstam](wikipedia://en/1898427) type hypothesis.

The [Brun sieve](wikipedia://en/18731525) establishes an upper bound on the density of primes having the form $p=n^2+1$: there are $O(\sqrt x/\log x)$ such primes up to $x$. Hence [almost all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotically_almost_surely) numbers of the form $n^2+1$ are composite.

:::
