# Complemented group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complemented group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the realm of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the term **complemented group** is used in two distinct, but similar ways.

:::

In (Hall 1937), a complemented group is one in which every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) has a group-theoretic [complement](wikipedia://en/11436087). Such [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) are called **completely factorizable groups** in the Russian literature, following (Baeva 1953) and (Černikov 1953).

The following are equivalent for any [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) *G*:

- *G* is complemented
- *G* is a subgroup of a [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of groups of [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525) [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) (a special type of [Z-group](wikipedia://en/15757039))
- *G* is a [supersolvable group](wikipedia://en/4966866) with [elementary abelian](wikipedia://en/4843202) [Sylow subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_subgroup) (a special type of [A-group](wikipedia://en/4938898)), (Hall 1937, Theorem 1 and 2).

Later, in (Zacher 1953), a group is said to be complemented if the [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) is a [complemented lattice](wikipedia://en/753521), that is, if for every subgroup *H* there is a subgroup *K* such that *H* ∩ *K* = 1 and ⟨*H*, *K*⟩ is the whole group. Hall's definition required in addition that *H* and *K* permute, that is, that *HK* = {*hk* : *h* in *H*, *k* in *K*} form a subgroup. Such groups are also called **K-groups** in the Italian and [lattice theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory) literature, such as (Schmidt 1994, 114–121). The [Frattini subgroup](wikipedia://en/540707) of a K-group is [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059); if a group has a [core-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core-free) [maximal subgroup](wikipedia://en/1472649) that is a K-group, then it itself is a K-group; hence subgroups of K-groups need not be K-groups, but [quotient groups](wikipedia://en/11526) and direct products of K-groups are K-groups, (Schmidt 1994, 115–116). In (Costantini Zacher 2004) it is shown that every [finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_group) [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) is a complemented group. Note that in the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445), *K*-group is more used to mean a group whose proper subgroups only have composition factors amongst the known finite simple groups.

An example of a group that is not complemented (in either sense) is the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order *p*<sup>2</sup>, where *p* is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). This group only has one nontrivial subgroup *H*, the cyclic group of order *p*, so there can be no other subgroup *L* to be the complement of *H*.
