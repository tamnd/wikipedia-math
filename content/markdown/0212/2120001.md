# Retraction (topology)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Retraction"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **retraction** is a [continuous mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_mapping) from a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) into a [subspace](wikipedia://en/910926) that preserves the position of all points in that subspace.[^o2] The subspace is then called a **retract** of the original space. A **deformation retraction** is a mapping that captures the idea of *continuously shrinking* a space into a subspace.

:::

An **absolute neighborhood retract** (**ANR**) is a particularly [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved) type of topological space. For example, every [topological manifold](wikipedia://en/2119193) is an ANR. Every ANR has the [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type) of a very simple topological space, a [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Retract {#Retract}

Let *X* be a topological space and *A* a subspace of *X*. Then a continuous map

$$r\colon X \to A$$

is a **retraction** if the [restriction](wikipedia://en/185427#Restrictions_and_extensions) of *r* to *A* is the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069) on *A*; that is, $r(a) = a$ for all *a* in *A*. Equivalently, denoting by

$$\iota\colon A \hookrightarrow X$$

the [inclusion](wikipedia://en/492516), a retraction is a continuous map *r* such that

$$r \circ \iota = \operatorname{id}_A,$$

that is, the composition of *r* with the inclusion is the identity of *A*. Note that, by definition, a retraction maps *X* [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onto) *A*. A subspace *A* is called a **retract** of *X* if such a retraction exists. For instance, any non-empty space retracts to a point in the obvious way (any constant map yields a retraction). If *X* is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), then *A* must be a [closed subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subset) of *X*.

If $r: X \to A$ is a retraction, then the composition $\iota \circ r$ is an [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) continuous map from *X* to *X*. Conversely, given any idempotent continuous map $s: X \to X,$ we obtain a retraction onto the image of *s* by restricting the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264).

### Deformation retract and strong deformation retract {#Deformation_retract_and_strong_deformation_retract}

A continuous map

$$F\colon X \times [0, 1] \to X$$

is a *deformation retraction* of a space *X* onto a subspace *A* if, for every *x* in *X* and *a* in *A*,

$$F(x,0) = x, \quad F(x,1) \in A ,\quad \mbox{and} \quad F(a,1) = a.$$

In other words, a deformation retraction is a [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) between a retraction (strictly, between its composition with the inclusion) and the identity map on *X*. The subspace *A* is called a **deformation retract** of *X*. A deformation retraction is a special case of a [homotopy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalence).

A retract need not be a deformation retract. For instance, having a single point as a deformation retract of a space *X* would imply that *X* is [path connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_connected) (and in fact that *X* is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414)).

*Note:* An equivalent definition of deformation retraction is the following. A continuous map $r: X \to A$ is itself called a deformation retraction if it is a retraction and its composition with the inclusion is homotopic to the identity map on *X*. In this language, a deformation retraction still carries with it a homotopy between the identity map on *X* and itself, but we refer to the map $r$ rather than the homotopy as a deformation retraction.

If, in the definition of a deformation retraction, we add the requirement that

$$F(a,t) = a$$

for all *t* in \[0, 1\] and *a* in *A*, then *F* is called a **strong deformation retraction**. In other words, a strong deformation retraction leaves points in *A* fixed throughout the homotopy. (Some authors, such as [Hatcher](wikipedia://en/1112366), take this as the definition of deformation retraction.)

As an example, the [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) *$S^{n}$* is a strong deformation retract of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1} \backslash \{0\};$ as strong deformation retraction one can choose the map

$$F(x,t)=(1-t)x+t{x\over \|x\|}.$$

Note that the condition of being a strong deformation retract is *strictly [stronger](wikipedia://en/1042164#strong)* than being a deformation retract. For instance, let *X* be the [closed infinite broom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_infinite_broom). It is the subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$ consisting of closed line segments connecting the origin and the point $(1,1/n)$ for *n* a positive integer, together with the closed line segment connecting the origin with $(0,1)$. Let X have the subspace topology inherited from the [Euclidean topology](wikipedia://en/1482085) on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Now let *A* be the subspace of *X* consisting of the line segment connecting the origin with $(1,0)$. Then *A* is a deformation retract of *X* but not a strong deformation retract of *X*.[^o7]

### Cofibration and neighborhood deformation retract {#Cofibration_and_neighborhood_deformation_retract}

A map *f*: *A* → *X* of topological spaces is a ([Hurewicz](wikipedia://en/362385)) **[cofibration](wikipedia://en/1697216)** if it has the [homotopy extension property](wikipedia://en/4029308) for maps to any space. This is one of the central concepts of [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831). A cofibration *f* is always injective, in fact a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) to its image.[^o10] If *X* is Hausdorff (or a [compactly generated](wikipedia://en/899382) [weak Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/19500167)), then the image of a cofibration *f* is closed in *X*.

Among all closed inclusions, cofibrations can be characterized as follows. The inclusion of a closed subspace *A* in a space *X* is a cofibration if and only if *A* is a **neighborhood deformation retract** of *X*, meaning that there is a continuous map $u: X \rightarrow [0, 1]$ with $A = u^{-1}\!\left(0\right)$ and a homotopy $H: X \times [0, 1] \rightarrow X$ such that $H(x,0) = x$ for all $x \in X,$ $H(a,t) = a$ for all $a \in A$ and $t \in [0, 1],$ and $H\left(x,1\right) \in A$ if $u(x) < 1$.[^o11]

For example, the inclusion of a subcomplex in a CW complex is a cofibration.

## Properties {#Properties}

- One basic property of a retract *A* of *X* (with retraction $r: X \to A$) is that every continuous map $f: A \rightarrow Y$ has at least one extension $g: X \rightarrow Y,$ namely $g = f \circ r$.
- If a subspace is a retract of a space, then the inclusion induces an injection between fundamental groups.
- Deformation retraction is a particular case of homotopy equivalence. In fact, two spaces are homotopy equivalent [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are both homeomorphic to deformation retracts of a single larger space.
- Any topological space that deformation retracts to a point is contractible and vice versa. However, there exist contractible spaces that do not strongly deformation retract to a point.[^o14]

:::

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="No-retraction theorem"}

## No-retraction theorem {#No-retraction_theorem}

The [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of the [*n*-dimensional ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-ball), that is, the (*n*−1)-sphere, is not a retract of the ball. (See Brouwer fixed-point theorem.)

:::

## Absolute neighborhood retract (ANR) {#Absolute_neighborhood_retract_(ANR)}

A closed subset $X$ of a topological space $Y$ is called a **neighborhood retract** of $Y$ if $X$ is a retract of some open subset of $Y$ that contains $X$.

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a class of topological spaces, closed under homeomorphisms and passage to closed subsets. Following [Borsuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Borsuk) (starting in 1931), a space *$X$* is called an **absolute retract** for the class $\mathcal{C}$, written $\operatorname{AR} \left(\mathcal{C}\right),$ if *$X$* is in $\mathcal{C}$ and whenever *$X$* is a closed subset of a space $Y$ in $\mathcal{C}$, $X$ is a retract of $Y$. A space $X$ is an **absolute neighborhood retract** for the class $\mathcal{C}$, written $\operatorname{ANR} \left(\mathcal{C}\right),$ if $X$ is in $\mathcal{C}$ and whenever $X$ is a closed subset of a space $Y$ in $\mathcal{C}$, $X$ is a neighborhood retract of $Y$.

Various classes $\mathcal{C}$ such as [normal spaces](wikipedia://en/48629) have been considered in this definition, but the class $\mathcal{M}$ of [metrizable spaces](wikipedia://en/19738) has been found to give the most satisfactory theory. For that reason, the notations AR and ANR by themselves are used in this article to mean $\operatorname {AR} \left({\mathcal {M}}\right)$ and $\operatorname {ANR} \left({\mathcal {M}}\right)$.[^o18]

A metrizable space is an AR if and only if it is contractible and an ANR.[^o19] By [Dugundji](wikipedia://en/36501408), every locally convex metrizable [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $V$ is an AR; more generally, every nonempty [convex subset](wikipedia://en/6292) of such a vector space $V$ is an AR.[^o20] For example, any [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) ([complete](wikipedia://en/7832) or not) is an AR. More concretely, Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{n},$ the [unit cube](wikipedia://en/407763) $I^{n},$and the [Hilbert cube](wikipedia://en/216389) $I^{\omega}$ are ARs.

ANRs form a remarkable class of "[well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well_behaved)" topological spaces. Among their properties are:

- Every open subset of an ANR is an ANR.
- By [Hanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olof_Hanner), a metrizable space that has an [open cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) by ANRs is an ANR.[^o21] (That is, being an ANR is a **[local property](wikipedia://en/1462457)** for metrizable spaces.) It follows that every topological manifold is an ANR. For example, the sphere *$S^{n}$* is an ANR but not an AR (because it is not contractible). In infinite dimensions, Hanner's theorem implies that every Hilbert cube manifold as well as the (rather different, for example not [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632)) [Hilbert manifolds](wikipedia://en/2504412) and [Banach manifolds](wikipedia://en/2504397) are ANRs.
- Every locally finite [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022) is an ANR.[^o22] An arbitrary CW complex need not be metrizable, but every CW complex has the homotopy type of an ANR (which is metrizable, by definition).[^o23]
- Every ANR *X* is **[locally contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_contractible)** in the sense that for every open neighborhood $U$ of a point $x$ in $X$, there is an open neighborhood $V$ of $x$ contained in $U$ such that the inclusion $V \hookrightarrow U$ is homotopic to a [constant map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_map). A [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_dimension) metrizable space is an ANR if and only if it is locally contractible in this sense.[^o24] For example, the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subset of the real line that is not an ANR, since it is not even [locally connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_connected).
- Counterexamples: Borsuk found a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ that is an ANR but not strictly locally contractible.[^o25] (A space is **strictly locally contractible** if every open neighborhood $U$ of each point $x$ contains a contractible open neighborhood of $x$.) Borsuk also found a compact subset of the Hilbert cube that is locally contractible (as defined above) but not an ANR.[^o26]
- Every ANR has the homotopy type of a CW complex, by [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/385179) and [Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632).[^o27] Moreover, a locally compact ANR has the homotopy type of a locally finite CW complex; and, by West, a compact ANR has the homotopy type of a finite CW complex.[^o28] In this sense, ANRs avoid all the homotopy-theoretic pathologies of arbitrary topological spaces. For example, the [Whitehead theorem](wikipedia://en/976793) holds for ANRs: a map of ANRs that induces an isomorphism on [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) (for all choices of base point) is a homotopy equivalence. Since ANRs include topological manifolds, Hilbert cube manifolds, Banach manifolds, and so on, these results apply to a large class of spaces.
- Many [mapping spaces](wikipedia://en/78365422) are ANRs. In particular, let *Y* be an ANR with a closed subspace *A* that is an ANR, and let *X* be any compact metrizable space with a closed subspace *B*. Then the space $\left(Y, A\right)^{\left(X, B\right)}$ of maps of [pairs](wikipedia://en/19067071) $\left(X, B\right) \rightarrow \left(Y, A\right)$ (with the [compact-open topology](wikipedia://en/726335) on the [mapping space](wikipedia://en/340630)) is an ANR.[^o29] It follows, for example, that the [loop space](wikipedia://en/2174332) of any CW complex has the homotopy type of a CW complex.
- By Cauty, a metrizable space $X$ is an ANR if and only if every open subset of $X$ has the homotopy type of a CW complex.[^o30]
- By Cauty, there is a [metric linear space](wikipedia://en/45752#Topological_structure) *$V$* (meaning a topological vector space with a [translation-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_invariant) metric) that is not an AR. One can take $V$ to be [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) and an [F-space](wikipedia://en/200440) (that is, a complete metric linear space).[^o31] (By Dugundji's theorem above, $V$ cannot be locally convex.) Since $V$ is contractible and not an AR, it is also not an ANR. By Cauty's theorem above, $V$ has an open subset $U$ that is not homotopy equivalent to a CW complex. Thus there is a metrizable space $U$ that is strictly locally contractible but is not homotopy equivalent to a CW complex. It is not known whether a compact (or locally compact) metrizable space that is strictly locally contractible must be an ANR.
