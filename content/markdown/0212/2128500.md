# Pascal's rule

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pascal's rule"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Pascal's rule** (or **Pascal's formula**) is a [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) about [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668). The binomial coefficients are the numbers that appear in [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497). Pascal's rule states that for [positive integers](wikipedia://en/21474) *n* and *k*,

$${n-1\choose k} + {n-1\choose k-1} = {n\choose k},$$

where $\tbinom{n}{k}$ is the binomial coefficient, namely the coefficient of the $x^{k}$ term in the [expansion](wikipedia://en/4173350) of $(1 + x)^{n}$. There is no restriction on the relative sizes of $n$ and $k$;[^o2] in particular, the above identity remains valid when $n < k$ since $\tbinom{n}{k} = 0$ whenever $n < k$.

:::

Together with the boundary conditions $\tbinom{n}{0} = \tbinom{n}{n}= 1$ for all nonnegative integers *n*, Pascal's rule determines that

$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!},$$

for all integers $0 \le k \le n$. In this sense, Pascal's rule is the [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806) that defines the binomial coefficients.

Pascal's rule can also be generalized to apply to [multinomial coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinomial_coefficient).

## Combinatorial proof {#Combinatorial_proof}

*Image: Pascal's rule 4c1 plus 4c2 equals 5c2.svg.* Illustrates combinatorial proof: $\binom 4 1+\binom 4 2=\binom 5 2.$

[Pascal's](wikipedia://en/4068) rule has an intuitive combinatorial meaning, that is clearly expressed in this counting proof.[^o7]p. 44

*Proof*. Recall that $\tbinom{n}{k}$ equals the number of [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) with *k* elements from a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) with *n* elements. Suppose one particular element is uniquely labeled *X* in a set with *n* elements.

To construct a subset of *k* elements containing *X*, include X and choose *k* − 1 elements from the remaining *n* − 1 elements in the set. There are $\tbinom{n-1}{k-1}$ such subsets.

To construct a subset of *k* elements *not* containing *X*, choose *k* elements from the remaining *n* − 1 elements in the set. There are $\tbinom{n-1}{k}$ such subsets.

Every subset of *k* elements either contains *X* or not. The total number of subsets with *k* elements in a set of *n* elements is the sum of the number of subsets containing *X* and the number of subsets that do not contain *X*, $\tbinom{n-1}{k-1} + \tbinom{n-1}{k}$.

This equals $\tbinom{n}{k}$; therefore, $\tbinom{n}{k} = \tbinom{n-1}{k-1} + \tbinom{n-1}{k}$.

## Algebraic proof {#Algebraic_proof}

Alternatively, the algebraic derivation of the binomial case follows.

$$\begin{aligned} { n-1 \choose k } + { n-1 \choose k-1} & = \frac{(n-1)!}{k! (n - 1 - k)!} + \frac{(n-1)!}{(k - 1)!(n - k)!} \\ & = (n-1)! \left[ \frac{n - k}{k!(n - k)!} + \frac{k}{k!(n - k)!}\right] \\ & = (n-1)! \frac{n}{k!(n - k)!} \\ & = \frac{n!}{k!(n - k)!} \\ & = \binom{n}{k}. \end{aligned}$$

An alternative algebraic proof using the alternative definition of binomial coefficients: $\tbinom{n}{k} = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n - k + 1)}{k!}$. Indeed

$$\begin{aligned} { n-1 \choose k } + { n-1 \choose k-1} & = \frac{(n-1)\cdots((n-1)-k + 1)}{k!} + \frac{(n-1)\cdots((n-1)-(k-1)+1)}{(k - 1)!} \\ & = \frac{(n-1)\cdots(n-k)}{k!} + \frac{(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{(k - 1)!} \\ & = \frac{(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{(k-1)!}\left[ \frac{n - k}{k} + 1\right] \\ & = \frac{(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{(k-1)!} \cdot \frac{n}{k} \\ & = \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n - k + 1)}{k!} \\ & = \binom{n}{k}. \end{aligned}$$

Since $\tbinom{z}{k} = \frac{z(z-1)\cdots(z - k + 1)}{k!}$ is used as the extended definition of the binomial coefficient when *z* is a complex number, thus the above alternative algebraic proof shows that Pascal's rule holds more generally when *n* is replaced by any complex number.

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Pascal's rule can be generalized to multinomial coefficients.[^o7]p. 144 For any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *p* such that $p \ge 2$, $k_1, k_2, k_3, \dots, k_p \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}\!,$ and $n = k_1 + k_2 + k_3 + \cdots + k_p \ge 1$,

$${n-1\choose k_1-1,k_2,k_3, \dots, k_p}+{n-1 \choose k_1,k_2-1,k_3,\dots, k_p}+\cdots+{n-1\choose k_1,k_2,k_3,\dots,k_p-1} = {n\choose k_1, k_2, k_3, \dots, k_p}$$

where ${n\choose k_1, k_2, k_3, \dots , k_p}$ is the coefficient of the $x_1^{k_1}x_2^{k_2}\cdots x_p^{k_p}$ term in the expansion of $(x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_p)^{n}$.

The algebraic derivation for this general case is as follows.[^o7]p. 144 Let *p* be an integer such that $p \ge 2$, $k_1, k_2, k_3, \dots, k_p \in \mathbb{N}^{+}\!,$ and $n = k_1 + k_2 + k_3 + \cdots + k_p \ge 1$. Then

$$\begin{aligned} & {} \quad {n-1 \choose k_1-1,k_2,k_3, \dots, k_p}+{n-1 \choose k_1,k_2-1,k_3,\dots, k_p} + \cdots + {n-1 \choose k_1,k_2,k_3,\dots,k_p-1} \\ & = \frac{(n-1)!}{(k_1-1)!k_2!k_3! \cdots k_p!} + \frac{(n-1)!}{k_1!(k_2-1)!k_3!\cdots k_p!} + \cdots + \frac{(n-1)!}{k_1!k_2!k_3! \cdots (k_p-1)!} \\ & = \frac{k_1(n-1)!}{k_1!k_2!k_3! \cdots k_p!} + \frac{k_2(n-1)!}{k_1!k_2!k_3! \cdots k_p!} + \cdots + \frac{k_p(n-1)!}{k_1!k_2!k_3! \cdots k_p!} = \frac{(k_1+k_2+\cdots+k_p) (n-1)!}{k_1!k_2!k_3!\cdots k_p!} \\ & = \frac{n(n-1)!}{k_1!k_2!k_3! \cdots k_p!} = \frac{n!}{k_1!k_2!k_3! \cdots k_p!} = {n \choose k_1, k_2, k_3, \dots, k_p}. \end{aligned}$$
