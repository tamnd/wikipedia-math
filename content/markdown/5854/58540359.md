# List of unsolved problems in fair division

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of unsolved problems in fair division"}

This page lists notable open problems related to [fair division](wikipedia://en/669899) - a field in the intersection of mathematics, computer science, political science and economics.

:::

## Open problems in [fair cake-cutting](wikipedia://en/43212478) {#Open_problems_in_fair_cake-cutting}

### Query complexity of [envy-free cake-cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_cake-cutting) {#Query_complexity_of_envy-free_cake-cutting}

In the problem of [envy-free cake-cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_cake-cutting), there is a cake modeled as an interval, and *$n$* agents with different value measures over the cake. The value measures are accessible only via queries of the form "evaluate a given piece of cake" or "mark a piece of cake with a given value". With *$n=2$* agents, an envy-free division can be found using two queries, via [divide and choose](wikipedia://en/2260854). With *$n>2$* agents, there are several open problems regarding the number of required queries.

\1. First, assume that the entire cake must be allocated (i.e., there is *no disposal*), and pieces may be disconnected. *How many queries are required?*

- Lower bound: $\Omega(n^2)$;[^o4]
- Upper bound: $O\left(n^{n^{n^{n^{n^{n}}}}}\right)$.[^o5]

\2. Next, assume that some cake may be left unallocated (i.e., there is *[free disposal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_disposal)*), but the allocation must be [proportional](wikipedia://en/47899740) (in addition to envy-free): each agent must get at least *$1/n$* of the total cake value. Pieces may still be disconnected. *How many queries are required?*

- Lower bound: not known (theoretically it may be polynomially solvable).
- Upper bound: $(n!)^2 n^2$.[^o5]

\3. Next, assume there is free disposal, the allocation must still be proportional, but the pieces must be *connected*. *How many queries are required?*

- For $n=3$, there is an algorithm with 54 queries.[^o6]
- For $n\geq 4$, no finite algorithm is currently known.

\4. Next, assume there is free disposal, the pieces must be connected, but the allocation may be only approximately proportional (i.e., some agents may get less than *$1/n$* of the total cake value). *What value can be guaranteed to each agent using a finite envy-free protocol?*

- For $n=3$, there is an algorithm that attains 1/3, which is optimal.
- For $n=4$ (the smallest open case), there is an algorithm that attains 1/7.[^o6]
- For any $n$, there is an algorithm that attains $1/(3n)$.[^o5]

\5. Finally, assume the entire cake must be allocated, and pieces may be disconnected, but the number of cuts (or number of pieces per agent) should be as small as possible. *How many cuts do we need in order to find an envy-free allocation in a finite number of queries*?

::: {#o7 .entry cue=list-entry name="a finite algorithm does not exist for n-1 cuts"}

- For $n\geq 3$, [a finite algorithm does not exist for $n-1$ cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_cake-cutting#Hardness_result) (1 piece per agent).[^o8]

:::

::: {#o9 .entry cue=list-entry name="Selfridge–Conway procedure"}

- For $n=3$, [Selfridge–Conway procedure](wikipedia://en/25120023) solves the problem in finite time with 5 cuts (and at most 2 pieces per agent).

:::

- For $n\geq 4$, the Aziz-Mackenzie procedure solves the problem in finite time, but with many cuts (and many pieces per agent).
- Smallest open case: three agents and 3 or 4 cuts; four agents and 2 pieces per agent.

### Number of cuts for [cake-cutting with different entitlements](wikipedia://en/50676688) {#Number_of_cuts_for_cake-cutting_with_different_entitlements}

When all agents have equal entitlements, a [proportional cake-cutting](wikipedia://en/47899740) can be implemented using $n-1$ cuts, which is optimal.

*How many cuts are required for implementing a proportional cake-cutting among $n$ agents with different entitlements?*

- Lower bound: $2n-2$;[^o11]
- Upper bound: $3n-4$.[^o12]
- Smallest open case: $n=3$ agents with all different entitlements: $1/7$, $2/7$ and $4/7$.[^o11]

*How many cuts are required for implementing an [envy-free cake-cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_cake-cutting) among $n$ agents with different entitlements?*

- Lower bound: $2n-2$, since envy-free implies proportional.
- Upper bound: not known.

### Fair division of a partly burnt cake {#Fair_division_of_a_partly_burnt_cake}

A *partly burnt cake* is a metaphor to a cake in which agents may have both positive and negative valuations.[^o14]

A [proportional division](wikipedia://en/2260894) of such a cake always exists.

*What is the runtime complexity of calculating a connected-**proportional** allocation of partly burnt cake?*

Known cases:

::: {#o15 .entry cue=list-entry name="Even-Paz protocol"}

- When all valuations are positive, or all valuations are negative, the [Even-Paz protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even-Paz_protocol) finds a connected proportional division using $O(n\log n)$ queries, and this is optimal.

:::

- When valuations may be mixed, a moving-knife protocol can be used to find a connected proportional division using $O(n^2)$ queries.[^o16] *Can this be improved to $O(n\log n)$ ?*

An envy-free division of a partly burnt cake is guaranteed to exist if-and-only-if the number of agents is the power of a prime [integer](wikipedia://en/14563).[^o17] However, it cannot be found by a finite protocol - it can only be approximated. Given a small positive number *D*, an allocation is called *D*-envy-free if, for each agent, the allocation will become envy-free if we move the cuts by at most *D* (length units).

*What is the runtime complexity (as a function of D) of calculating a connected **D-envy-free** allocation of a partly burnt cake?[^o14]*

### [Truthful cake-cutting](wikipedia://en/62141894) {#Truthful_cake-cutting}

*Truthful cake-cutting* is the design of [truthful mechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truthful_mechanism) for fair cake-cutting. The currently known algorithms and impossibility results are shown [here](wikipedia://en/62141894#summary). The main cases in which it is unknown whether a deterministic truthful fair mechanism exists are:**[^o19]**

::: {#o20 .entry cue=list-entry name="Piecewise-uniform valuation"}

- There are 3 or more agents with *[piecewise-uniform valuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise-uniform_valuation)*, without free disposal.

:::

::: {#o21 .entry cue=list-entry name="Piecewise-constant valuation"}

- There are 2 or more agents with *[piecewise-constant valuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise-constant_valuation)*, with or without free disposal (and without additional requirements such as connectivity or non-wastefulness).

:::

## Open problems in [fair allocation of indivisible items](wikipedia://en/46974282) {#Open_problems_in_fair_allocation_of_indivisible_items}

### Approximate [maximin-share](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximin-share) fairness {#Approximate_maximin-share_fairness}

The *1-of-$n$ maximin share (MMS)* of an agent is the largest utility the agent can secure by partitioning the items into $n$ bundles and receiving the worst bundle. For two agents, [divide and choose](wikipedia://en/2260854) gives each agent at least his/her 1-of-2 MMS. For $n>2$ agents, it is almost always, but not always, possible to give each agent his/her 1-of-$n$ MMS. This raises several kinds of questions.

#### \1. Computational complexity {#1._Computational_complexity}

*What is the runtime complexity of deciding whether a given instance admits a 1-of-$n$ MMS allocation?*[^o25][^o26]

::: {#o27 .entry cue=list-entry name="Polynomial hierarchy"}

- Upper bound: ${NP}^{NP}$ (which is $\Sigma_2^P$ - level 2 in the [polynomial hierarchy](wikipedia://en/658651))

:::

- Lower bound: none (so it may be level 2 or 1 or even 0 of the hierarchy).

NOTE: the following related problems are known to be computationally hard:

::: {#o28 .entry cue=list-entry name="NP-hard"}

- *Calculating* the 1-of-$n$ MMS of a given agent is [NP-hard](wikipedia://en/54681) even if all agents have additive preferences (reduction from [partition problem](wikipedia://en/3269567)).

:::

::: {#o29 .entry cue=list-entry name="co-NP complete"}

- Deciding whether a *given* allocation is *1-of-$n$ MMS* is [co-NP complete](wikipedia://en/54680) for agents with additive preferences.

:::

#### \2. Cardinal approximation {#2._Cardinal_approximation}

*What is the largest fraction r such that there always exists an allocation giving each agent a utility of at least r times his 1-of-$n$ maximin share?*

Known cases:

- For two agents: $r=1$ by divide-and-choose.
- For three agents, even with additive valuations: $r<1$. By a carefully crafted example.[^o31]
- For any number of agents with additive valuations: $r\ge 3/4$.[^o32]
- For any number of agents with additive *negative* valuations (i.e., for chores): $r\ge 9/11$.[^o33]

#### \3. Ordinal approximation {#3._Ordinal_approximation}

*What is the smallest integer $N$ (as a function of $n$) such that there always exists an allocation among $n$ agents giving each agent at least his 1-of-$N$ MMS?*

Known cases:

::: {#o35 .entry cue=list-entry name="divide-and-choose"}

- For two agents: $N=2$. By [divide-and-choose](wikipedia://en/2260854).

:::

- For any number of agents with binary valuations: $N=n$. By round-robin. It gives EF1, which implies 1-of-$n$-MMS.
- For $n>2$ agents: $N>n$. By a carefully crafted example.[^o31]
- For any number of agents with additive valuations: $N\le 2n-1$, by round-robin. It gives EF1, which implies 1-of-$(2n-1)$-MMS.

::: {#o36 .entry cue=list-entry name="Envy-free matching"}

- For any number of agents with additive valuations: $N\le 2n-2$, using [envy-free matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_matching).[^o37]

:::

So the answer can be anything between $n+1$ and $2n-2$, inclusive. Smallest open case:

*For $n=4$ agents with additive valuations, does there always exist a 1-of-5 maximin-share allocation?*

*Note:* there always exists an [Approximate Competitive Equilibrium from Equal Incomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximate_Competitive_Equilibrium_from_Equal_Incomes) that guarantees the *1-of-($n+1$*) maximin-share.[^o38] However, this allocation may have excess supply, and - more importantly - excess demand: the sum of the bundles allocated to all agents might be slightly larger than the set of all items. Such an error is reasonable when allocating course seats among students, since a small excess supply can be corrected by adding a small number of seats. But the classic fair division problem assumes that items may not be added.

### Envy-free up to one item {#Envy-free_up_to_one_item}

An allocation is called **EF1** (envy-free up to one item) if, for any two agents $i$ and $j$, and for any subset of size at most one contained in the bundle of $j$, if we remove that subset from $j$'s bundle then $i$ does not envy. An EF1 allocation always exists and can be found by the [envy cycles algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-cycles_procedure). Combining it with other properties raises some open questions.

#### Pareto-optimal EF1 allocation (goods and bads) {#Pareto-optimal_EF1_allocation_(goods_and_bads)}

When all items are good and all valuations are additive, a PO+EF1 always exists: the allocation maximizing the product of utilities is PO+EF1.[^o41] Finding this maximizing allocation is NP-hard,[^o42] but in theory, it may be possible to find other PO+EF1 allocations (not maximizing the product).

*What is the run-time complexity of finding a PO+EF1 allocation of **goods**?*

A PO+EF1 allocation of *bads (chores)* is not known to exist, even when all valuations are additive.

*Does a PO+EF1 allocation of **bads** always exist?*

*What is the run-time complexity of finding* *such allocation, if it exists?*

#### Contiguous EF1 allocation {#Contiguous_EF1_allocation}

Often the goods are ordered on a line, for example, houses in a street. Each agent wants to get a contiguous block.[^o44]

*For three or more agents with additive valuations, does an EF1 allocation always exist?*

Known cases:

::: {#o45 .entry cue=list-entry name="Envy-free cake-cutting"}

- For two agents with additive valuations, the answer is yes: we can round a connected [envy-free cake-cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_cake-cutting) (e.g., found by [divide and choose](wikipedia://en/2260854)).

:::

::: {#o46 .entry cue=list-entry name="Sperner's lemma"}

- For $n$ agents with additive valuations, we can find an "EF minus 2" allocation by rounding a connected envy-free cake-cutting, and there also exists an **EF2** allocation (proof using a variant of [Sperner's lemma](wikipedia://en/465067)).[^o47]

:::

- For $n$ agents with additive *binary* valuations (every item value is either 0 or 1), an "EF minus 2" allocation is also EF1, so the answer is *yes*.

Even when a contiguous EF1 allocation exists, the runtime complexity of finding it is unclear:

*For three or more agents with binary additive valuations, what is the complexity of finding a contiguous EF1 allocation?*

- A connected envy-free cake-cutting might take infinitely many queries to find. An EF1 allocation can always be found in finite time by checking all possible allocations, but this algorithm requires exponential run-time.

#### Price of fairness {#Price_of_fairness}

The [price of fairness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_of_fairness) is the ratio between the maximum social welfare (sum of utilities) in any allocation, and the maximum social welfare in a fair allocation. *What is the price of EF1 fairness?*

::: {#o49 .entry cue=list-entry name="Round-robin"}

- The upper bound is *$O(n)$ -* by either [Round-robin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round-robin_scheduling) or maximum Nash welfare.

:::

- The lower bound is *$O(\sqrt n)$.[^o50]*

### Existence of EFX allocation {#Existence_of_EFX_allocation}

An allocation is called **EFX** ([envy-free up to any item](wikipedia://en/83172906)) if, for any two agents $i$ and $j$, and for any good in the bundle of $j$, if we remove that good from $j$'s bundle then $i$ does not envy.[^o52]

*For four agents with additive valuations, does there always exist an EFX allocation?*

For details of the known cases, see [Envy-freeness up to any item](wikipedia://en/83172906).

### Existence of Pareto-efficient PROPx allocation of bads {#Existence_of_Pareto-efficient_PROPx_allocation_of_bads}

An allocation of bads is called **PROPx** (aka FSx)[^o54] if, for any agent *$i$*, and for any bad owned by *$i$*, if we remove that bad from $i$'s bundle, then $i$'s disutility is less than $1/n$ the total disutility.

*Does there always exist an allocation of bads that is both PROPx and Pareto-efficient?*

Related known cases:

- A PROPx allocation of *goods* (even without Pareto-efficiency) may not exist.
- A PROPx allocation of *bads* (without Pareto-efficiency) always exists.
- A *PROP1* and Pareto-efficient allocation of either goods or bads always exists.

### Competitive equilibrium for almost all incomes {#Competitive_equilibrium_for_almost_all_incomes}

[Competitive equilibrium](wikipedia://en/9243099) (CE) is a very strong fairness notion - it implies Pareto-optimality and envy-freeness. When the incomes are equal, CE might not exist even when there are two agents and one good. But, in all other income-space, CE exists when there are two agents and one good. In other words, there is a competitive equilibrium for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) income-vectors.

*For two agents with additive valuations over any number of goods, does there exist a competitive equilibrium for almost incomes?*[^o56]

Known cases:

- With three or fewer goods: always *yes*.
- With four goods: *yes* for 2 agents with general valuations, *no* for 3 agents with general valuations, *no* for 4 agents, even with additive valuations.[^o57]
- With five or more goods: *no* for two agents with general valuations.

Open conjectures:

- When there are two agents with *additive* valuations, CE for almost all incomes exists for any number of goods.
- When there are three agents, even with additive valuations, CE for almost all incomes might not exist.

## Fair division of partly divisible items {#Fair_division_of_partly_divisible_items}

### Runtime complexity of fair allocation with bounded sharing {#Runtime_complexity_of_fair_allocation_with_bounded_sharing}

*Given $n\geq 2$ agents, $m$ items and an integer $k$, suppose at most $k$ items can be shared among two or more agents. What is the runtime complexity of deciding whether a proportional / envy-free allocation exists?*

Known cases:

::: {#o60 .entry cue=list-entry name="Partition problem"}

- With *$k=0$* and identical valuations, for any *$n\geq 2$,* the problem is equivalent to the [partition problem](wikipedia://en/3269567), and therefore it is NP-complete.

:::

- With *$k\geq n-1$*, the answer is always "yes", and an allocation can be found in polynomial time.[^o61]
- With *$n=3$* and *$k=1$* and identical valuations, an allocation can be found in polynomial time if it exists.[^o62]

Smallest open cases:

- *$n=3$* and *$k=1$* and different valuations.
- *$n=4$* and *$k\in\{1,2\}$* and identical valuations.
