# Bouquet graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bouquet graph"}

*Image: Topological Rose.png.* $B_4$, a bouquet with one vertex and four self-loop edges

In mathematics, a **bouquet graph** $B_m$, for an integer parameter $m$, is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) with one [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) and $m$ edges, all of which are [self-loops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-loop). It is the [graph-theoretic](wikipedia://en/12401) analogue of the [topological rose](wikipedia://en/3526954), a space of $m$ circles joined at a point. When the context of graph theory is clear, it can be called more simply a **bouquet**.

:::

*Image: Self-trial ribbon graph.svg.* [Ribbon graph](wikipedia://en/56039802) representation of an embedding of $B_3$ onto the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350).

Although bouquets have a very simple structure as graphs, they are of some importance in [topological graph theory](wikipedia://en/1631772) because their [graph embeddings](wikipedia://en/8149170) can still be non-trivial. In particular, every cellularly embedded graph can be reduced to an embedded bouquet by a [partial duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilson_operation) applied to the edges of any [spanning tree](wikipedia://en/455770) of the graph, or alternatively by [contracting the edges](wikipedia://en/3758115) of any spanning tree.

In graph-theoretic approaches to [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), every [Cayley–Serre graph](wikipedia://en/358277) (a variant of Cayley graphs with doubled edges) can be represented as the [covering graph](wikipedia://en/21123339) of a bouquet.
