# Globular set

*Image: Globular set.svg.* A globular set with 0-cells (vertices), 1-cells (gray edges), 2-cells (red edges), and 3-cells (blue edges). The source and target of each $k$-cell must be single ($k$-1)-cells. For example, the red edge *A* connects single 1-cells *a* and *b*, while *B* connects *c* and *d*, and *C* forms a self-connection on *c*.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Globular set"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a branch of mathematics, a **globular set** is a higher-dimensional generalization of a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986). Precisely, it is a sequence of sets $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ equipped with pairs of functions $s_n, t_n: X_n \to X_{n-1}$ such that

:::

- $$s_n \circ s_{n+1} = s_n \circ t_{n+1},$$
- $$t_n \circ s_{n+1} = t_n \circ t_{n+1}.$$

(Equivalently, it is a [presheaf](wikipedia://en/4840944) on the category of “globes”.) The letters "*s*", "*t*" stand for "source" and "target" and one imagines $X_n$ consists of directed edges at level *n*.

In the context of a graph, each dimension is represented as a set of **$k$-cells**. Vertices would make up the 0-cells, edges connecting vertices would be 1-cells, and then each dimension higher connects groups of the dimension beneath it.[^o2][^o3]

It can be viewed as a specific instance of the [polygraph](wikipedia://en/35054352). In a polygraph, a source or target of a $k$-cell may consist of an entire path of elements of ($k$-1)-cells, but a globular set restricts this to singular elements of ($k$-1)-cells.

A variant of the notion was used by [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck) to introduce the notion of an [∞-groupoid](wikipedia://en/40965675). Extending Grothendieck's work,[^o4] gave a definition of a [weak ∞-category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_∞-category) in terms of globular sets.
