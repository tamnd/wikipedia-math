# Rigid transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rigid transformation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **rigid transformation** (also called **Euclidean transformation** or **Euclidean isometry**) is a [geometric transformation](wikipedia://en/43194879) of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) that preserves the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between every pair of points.[^o2][^o3][^o4]

:::

The rigid transformations include [rotations](wikipedia://en/410009), [translations](wikipedia://en/204682), [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829), or any sequence of these. Reflections are sometimes excluded from the definition of a rigid transformation by requiring that the transformation also preserve the [handedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) of objects in the Euclidean space. (A reflection would not preserve handedness; for instance, it would transform a left hand into a right hand.) To avoid ambiguity, a transformation that preserves handedness is known as a **rigid motion**, a **Euclidean motion**, or a **proper rigid transformation**.

In dimension two, a rigid motion is either a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) or a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009). In dimension three, every rigid motion can be decomposed as the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of a rotation and a translation, and is thus sometimes called a **rototranslation**. In dimension three, all rigid motions are also [screw motions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw_motion) (this is [Chasles' theorem](wikipedia://en/42392462)).

In dimension at most three, any **improper rigid transformation** can be decomposed into an [improper rotation](wikipedia://en/244324) followed by a translation, or into a sequence of [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829).

Any object will keep the same [shape](wikipedia://en/169191#In_geometry) and size after a proper rigid transformation.

All rigid transformations are examples of [affine transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformations). The set of all (proper and improper) rigid transformations is a [mathematical group](wikipedia://en/19447) called the *[Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164)*, denoted $E(n)$ for $n$-dimensional Euclidean spaces. The set of rigid motions is called the special Euclidean group, and denoted $\operatorname{SE}(n)$.

In [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914), rigid motions in a 3-dimensional Euclidean space are used to represent displacements of [rigid bodies](wikipedia://en/455769). According to [Chasles' theorem](wikipedia://en/42392462), every rigid transformation can be expressed as a [screw motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw_motion).

## Distance formula {#Distance_formula}

A measure of distance between points, or [metric](wikipedia://en/20018), is needed in order to confirm that a transformation is rigid. The [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) formula for $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ is the generalization of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034). The formula gives the distance squared between two points $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{Y}$ as the sum of the squares of the distances along the coordinate axes, that is

$$d\left(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}\right)^2 = \left(X_1 - Y_1\right)^2 + \left(X_2 - Y_2\right)^2 + \dots + \left(X_n - Y_n\right)^2 = \left(\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{Y}\right) \cdot \left(\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{Y}\right).$$

where $\mathbf{X} = (X_{1}, X_{2}, ..., X_{n})$ and $\mathbf{Y} = (Y_{1}, Y_{2}, ..., Y_{n})$, and the dot denotes the [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product).

Using this distance formula, a rigid transformation $g : \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ has the property,

$$d(g(\mathbf{X}), g(\mathbf{Y}))^2 = d(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y})^2.$$

## Translations and linear transformations {#Translations_and_linear_transformations}

A [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) of a vector space adds a vector $\mathbf{d}$ to every vector in the space, which means it is the transformation

It is easy to show that this is a rigid transformation by showing that the distance between translated vectors equal the distance between the original vectors:

$$d(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{d},\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{d})^2 = (\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{w}-\mathbf{d})\cdot(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{w} -\mathbf{d})=(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w})\cdot(\mathbf{v}- \mathbf{w}) = d(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})^2.$$

A *linear transformation* of a vector space, $L : \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$, preserves [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632),

$$L(\mathbf{V}) = L(a\mathbf{v}+b\mathbf{w}) = aL(\mathbf{v})+bL(\mathbf{w}).$$

A linear transformation is a rigid transformation if it satisfies the condition,

$$d([L]\mathbf{v}, [L]\mathbf{w})^2 = d(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})^2,$$

that is

$$d([L]\mathbf{v}, [L]\mathbf{w})^2=([L]\mathbf{v}-[L]\mathbf{w})\cdot([L]\mathbf{v}-[L]\mathbf{w}) =([L](\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w}))\cdot([L](\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w})).$$

Now use the fact that the scalar product of two vectors **v**.**w** can be written as the matrix operation $\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{w}$, where the T denotes the matrix transpose, we have

$$d([L]\mathbf{v}, [L]\mathbf{w})^2 = (\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w})^\mathsf{T} [L]^\mathsf{T} [L](\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}).$$

Thus, the linear transformation *L* is rigid if its matrix satisfies the condition

$$[L]^\mathsf{T} [L]=[I],$$

where $[I]$ is the identity matrix. Matrices that satisfy this condition are called *orthogonal matrices.* This condition actually requires the columns of these matrices to be orthogonal unit vectors.

Matrices that satisfy this condition form a mathematical [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under the operation of matrix multiplication called the *orthogonal group of n×n matrices* and denoted $O(n)$.

Compute the determinant of the condition for an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620) to obtain

$$\det\left([L]^\mathsf{T} [L]\right) = \det[L]^2 = \det[I] = 1,$$

which shows that the matrix $[L]$ can have a determinant of either +1 or −1. Orthogonal matrices with determinant −1 are reflections, and those with determinant +1 are rotations. Notice that the set of orthogonal matrices can be viewed as consisting of two manifolds in $\mathbf{R}^{n\times n}$ separated by the set of singular matrices.

The set of rotation matrices is called the *special orthogonal group,* and denoted $\operatorname{SO}(n)$. It is an example of a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) because it has the structure of a manifold.
