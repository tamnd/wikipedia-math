# Floor effect

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Floor effect"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a **floor effect** (also known as a **basement effect**) arises when a data-gathering instrument has a lower limit to the data values it can reliably specify.[^o2] This lower limit is known as the "floor".[^o3] The "floor effect" is one type of scale attenuation effect;[^o4] the other scale attenuation effect is the "[ceiling effect](wikipedia://en/2010793)". Floor effects are occasionally encountered in [psychological testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_testing), when a test designed to estimate some psychological trait has a minimum standard score that may not distinguish some test-takers who differ in their responses on the test item content.[^o5] Giving preschool children an [IQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IQ) test designed for adults would likely show many of the test-takers with scores near the lowest standard score for adult test-takers (IQ 40 on most tests that were currently normed as of 2010). To indicate differences in current intellectual functioning among young children, IQ tests[^o6] specifically for young children are developed, on which many test-takers can score well above the floor score. An IQ test designed to help assess [intellectually disabled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_disability) persons might intentionally be designed with easier item content and a lower floor score to better distinguish among individuals taking the test as part of an assessment process.[^o7]

:::
