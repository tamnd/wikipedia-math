# Polynomial identity ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polynomial identity ring"}

In [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* is a **polynomial identity ring** if there is, for some *N* > 0, an element *P* ≠ 0 of the [free algebra](wikipedia://en/494669), **Z***X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*N*</sub>, over the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties) in *N* variables *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*N*</sub> such that

:::

$$P(r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_N) = 0$$

for all *N*-[tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) *r*<sub>1</sub>, *r*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *r*<sub>*N*</sub> taken from *R*.

Strictly the *X*<sub>*i*</sub> here are "non-commuting indeterminates", and so "polynomial identity" is a slight [abuse of language](wikipedia://en/1698066), since "polynomial" here stands for what is usually called a "non-commutative polynomial". The abbreviation **PI-ring** is common. More generally, the free algebra over any ring *S* may be used, and gives the concept of **PI-algebra**.

If the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of the polynomial *P* is defined in the usual way, the polynomial *P* is called **monic** if at least one of its terms of highest degree has coefficient equal to 1.

Every [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) is a PI-ring, satisfying the polynomial identity *XY* − *YX* = 0. Therefore, PI-rings are usually taken as *close generalizations of commutative rings*. If the ring has [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) *p* different from zero then it satisfies the polynomial identity *pX* = 0. To exclude such examples, sometimes it is defined that PI-rings must satisfy a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) identity.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- For example, if *R* is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) it is a PI-ring: this is true with

$$P(X_1,X_2) = X_1X_2-X_2X_1 = 0~$$

- The ring of 2 × 2 [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) over a commutative ring satisfies the **Hall identity**

$$(xy-yx)^2z=z(xy-yx)^2$$

This identity was used by (txt), but was found earlier by (txt).

- A major role is played in the theory by the **standard identity** *s*<sub>*N*</sub>, of length *N*, which generalises the example given for commutative rings (*N* = 2). It derives from the [Leibniz formula for determinants](wikipedia://en/5361510)

$$\det(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_N} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i = 1}^N a_{i,\sigma(i)}$$

by replacing each product in the summand by the product of the *X*<sub>*i*</sub> in the order given by the [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) σ. In other words each of the *N*! orders is summed, and the coefficient is 1 or −1 according to the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_of_a_permutation).

$$s_N(X_1,\ldots,X_N) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_N} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) X_{\sigma(1)}\dotsm X_{\sigma(N)}=0~$$

The *m* × *m* [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over any commutative ring satisfies a standard identity: the [Amitsur–Levitzki theorem](wikipedia://en/30373123) states that it satisfies *s*<sub>2*m*</sub>. The degree of this identity is optimal since the matrix ring can not satisfy any monic polynomial of degree less than 2*m*.

- Given a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k* of characteristic zero, take *R* to be the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) over a [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite)-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) *e*<sub>1</sub>, *e*<sub>2</sub>, *e*<sub>3</sub>, ... Then *R* is generated by the elements of this basis and

*e*<sub>*i*</sub> *e*<sub>*j*</sub> = −*e*<sub>*j*</sub> *e*<sub>*i*</sub>.

This ring does not satisfy *s*<sub>*N*</sub> for any *N* and therefore can not be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in any matrix ring. In fact *s*<sub>*N*</sub>(*e*<sub>1</sub>,*e*<sub>2</sub>,...,*e*<sub>*N*</sub>) = *N*!*e*<sub>1</sub>*e*<sub>2</sub>...*e*<sub>*N*</sub> ≠ 0. On the other hand it is a PI-ring since it satisfies \[\[*x*, *y*\], *z*\] := *xyz* − *yxz* − *zxy* + *zyx* = 0. It is enough to check this for monomials in the *e*<sub>*i*</sub>'s. Now, a monomial of [even](wikipedia://en/143135) degree commutes with every element. Therefore if either *x* or *y* is a monomial of even degree \[*x*, *y*\] := *xy* − *yx* = 0. If both are of [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) degree then \[*x*, *y*\] = *xy* − *yx* = 2*xy* has even degree and therefore commutes with *z*, i.e. \[\[*x*, *y*\], *z*\] = 0.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- Any [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) or [homomorphic](wikipedia://en/26411) [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a PI-ring is a PI-ring.
- A finite [direct product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_ring) of PI-rings is a PI-ring.
- A direct product of PI-rings, satisfying the same identity, is a PI-ring.
- It can always be assumed that the identity that the PI-ring satisfies is [multilinear](wikipedia://en/832212).
- If a ring is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) by *n* elements as a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over its [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) then it satisfies every alternating [multilinear polynomial](wikipedia://en/21890097) of degree larger than *n*. In particular it satisfies *s*<sub>*N*</sub> for *N* > *n* and therefore it is a PI-ring.
- If *R* and *S* are PI-rings then their [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) over the integers, $R\otimes_\mathbb{Z}S$, is also a PI-ring.
- If *R* is a PI-ring, then so is the ring of *n* × *n* matrices with coefficients in *R*.

:::

## PI-rings as generalizations of commutative rings {#PI-rings_as_generalizations_of_commutative_rings}

Among non-commutative rings, PI-rings satisfy the [Köthe conjecture](wikipedia://en/3100105). [Affine](wikipedia://en/11985661) PI-algebras over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) satisfy the [Kurosh conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurosh_conjecture), the [Nullstellensatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullstellensatz) and the [catenary property](wikipedia://en/10034460) for [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928).

If *R* is a PI-ring and *K* is a subring of its center such that *R* is [integral over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_extension) *K* then the [going up and going down properties](wikipedia://en/2083445) for prime ideals of *R* and *K* are satisfied. Also the *lying over* property (If *p* is a prime ideal of *K* then there is a prime ideal *P* of *R* such that $p$ is minimal over $P\cap K$) and the *incomparability* property (If *P* and *Q* are prime ideals of *R* and $P\subset Q$ then $P\cap K\subset Q\cap K$) are satisfied.

## The set of identities a PI-ring satisfies {#The_set_of_identities_a_PI-ring_satisfies}

If *F* := **Z***X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*N*</sub> is the free algebra in *N* variables and *R* is a PI-ring satisfying the polynomial *P* in *N* variables, then *P* is in the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of any homomorphism

$\tau$: *F* $\rightarrow$ *R*.

An [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *I* of *F* is called **T-ideal** if $f(I)\subset I$ for every [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) *f* of *F*.

Given a PI-ring, *R*, the set of all polynomial identities it satisfies is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) but even more it is a T-ideal. Conversely, if *I* is a T-ideal of *F* then *F*/*I* is a PI-ring satisfying all identities in *I*. It is assumed that *I* contains monic polynomials when PI-rings are required to satisfy monic polynomial identities.
