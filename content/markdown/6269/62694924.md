::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Inverse Pythagorean theorem"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **inverse Pythagorean theorem** (also known as the **reciprocal Pythagorean theorem**[^o3] or the **upside down Pythagorean theorem**[^o4]) is as follows:[^o5]

:::

Let $A$, $B$ be the endpoints of the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) of a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) $\triangle ABC$. Let $D$ be the foot of [a perpendicular](wikipedia://en/161241) dropped from $C$, the vertex of the [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956), to the hypotenuse. Then

$$\frac 1 {CD^2} = \frac 1 {AC^2} + \frac 1 {BC^2}.$$

This theorem should not be confused with proposition 48 in book 1 of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, the [converse](wikipedia://en/26513034#Converse) of the Pythagorean theorem, which states that if the square on one side of a triangle is equal to the sum of the squares on the other two sides then the other two sides contain a right angle.

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The area of triangle $\triangle ABC$ can be expressed in terms of either $AC$ and $BC$, or $AB$ and $CD$:

$$\begin{aligned} \tfrac{1}{2} AC \cdot BC &= \tfrac{1}{2} AB \cdot CD \\[4pt] (AC \cdot BC)^2 &= (AB \cdot CD)^2 \\[4pt] \frac{1}{CD^2} &= \frac{AB^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} \end{aligned}$$

given $CD > 0$, $AC > 0$ and $BC > 0$.

Using the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034),

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{CD^2} &= \frac{BC^2 + AC^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} \\[4pt] &= \frac{BC^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} + \frac{AC^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} \\[4pt] \quad \therefore \;\; \frac{1}{CD^2} &= \frac{ 1 }{AC^2} + \frac{1}{BC^2} \end{aligned}$$

*Image: Inverted pythagorean theorem.png.* Visual proof

as above.

Note in particular:

$$\begin{aligned} \tfrac{1}{2} AC \cdot BC &= \tfrac{1}{2} AB \cdot CD \\[4pt] CD &= \tfrac{AC \cdot BC}{AB} \\[4pt] \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Special case of the cruciform curve {#Special_case_of_the_cruciform_curve}

The [cruciform curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruciform_curve) or cross curve is a [quartic plane curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_plane_curve) given by the equation

$$x^2 y^2 - b^2 x^2 - a^2 y^2 = 0$$

where the two [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) determining the shape of the curve, $a$ and $b$ are each $CD$.

Substituting $x$ with $AC$ and $y$ with $BC$ gives

$$\begin{aligned} AC^2 BC^2 - CD^2 AC^2 - CD^2 BC^2 &= 0 \\[4pt] AC^2 BC^2 &= CD^2 BC^2 + CD^2 AC^2 \\[4pt] \frac{1}{CD^2} &= \frac{BC^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} + \frac{AC^2}{AC^2 \cdot BC^2} \\[4pt] \therefore \;\; \frac{1}{CD^2} &= \frac{1}{AC^2} + \frac{1}{BC^2} \end{aligned}$$

Inverse-Pythagorean triples can be generated using [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) parameters $t$ and $u$ as follows.[^o9]

$$\begin{aligned} AC &= (t^2 + u^2)(t^2 - u^2) \\ BC &= 2tu(t^2 + u^2) \\ CD &= 2tu(t^2 - u^2) \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o11 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

## Application {#Application}

If two identical lamps are placed at $A$ and $B$, the theorem and the [inverse-square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law) imply that the light intensity at $C$ is the same as when a single lamp is placed at $D$.
