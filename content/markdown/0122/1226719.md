# Cohomology operation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cohomology operation"}

In mathematics, the **cohomology operation** concept became central to [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), particularly [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), from the 1950s onwards, in the shape of the simple definition that if *F* is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) defining a [cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_theory), then a cohomology operation should be a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) from *F* to itself. Throughout there have been two basic points:

:::

1. the operations can be studied by combinatorial means; and
1. the effect of the operations is to yield an interesting [bicommutant](wikipedia://en/455964) theory.

The origin of these studies was the work of Pontryagin, Postnikov, and [Norman Steenrod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Steenrod), who first defined the [Pontryagin square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_square), [Postnikov square](wikipedia://en/31438901), and [Steenrod square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steenrod_square) operations for [singular cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology), in the case of mod 2 coefficients. The combinatorial aspect there arises as a formulation of the failure of a natural diagonal map, at [cochain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain) level. The general theory of the [Steenrod algebra](wikipedia://en/2146220) of operations has been brought into close relation with that of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901).

In the [Adams spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/4720689) the *bicommutant* aspect is implicit in the use of [Ext functors](wikipedia://en/840758), the [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) of Hom-functors; if there is a bicommutant aspect, taken over the Steenrod algebra acting, it is only at a *derived* level. The convergence is to groups in [stable homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/2946655), about which information is hard to come by. This connection established the deep interest of the cohomology operations for [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), and has been a research topic ever since. An [extraordinary cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraordinary_cohomology_theory) has its own cohomology operations, and these may exhibit a richer set on constraints.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A **cohomology operation** $\theta$ of type

$$(n,q,\pi,G)\,$$

is a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) of functors

$$\theta:H^{n}(-,\pi)\to H^{q}(-,G)\,$$

defined on [CW complexes](wikipedia://en/353022).

:::

## Relation to Eilenberg–MacLane spaces {#Relation_to_Eilenberg–MacLane_spaces}

Cohomology of CW complexes is [representable](wikipedia://en/844461) by an [Eilenberg–MacLane space](wikipedia://en/1073230), so by the [Yoneda lemma](wikipedia://en/34359) a cohomology operation of type $(n,q,\pi,G)$ is given by a [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) class of maps $K(\pi,n) \to K(G,q)$. Using [representability](wikipedia://en/844461) once again, the cohomology operation is given by an element of $H^{q}(K(\pi,n),G)$.

Symbolically, letting $[A,B]$ denote the set of homotopy classes of maps from $A$ to $B$,

$$\begin{aligned}\displaystyle\mathrm{Nat}(H^n(-,\pi),H^q(-,G)) &= \mathrm{Nat}([-,K(\pi,n)],[-,K(G,q)])\\ &= [K(\pi,n),K(G,q)]\\ &= H^q(K(\pi,n);G).\end{aligned}$$
