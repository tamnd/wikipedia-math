# Hyperbolic motion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperbolic motion"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **hyperbolic motions** are [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of a [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742). Under composition of mappings, the hyperbolic motions form a [continuous group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_group). This group is said to characterize the hyperbolic space. Such an approach to geometry was cultivated by [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) in his [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382). The idea of reducing geometry to its characteristic group was developed particularly by [Mario Pieri](wikipedia://en/2452874) in his reduction of the [primitive notions](wikipedia://en/23706) of geometry to merely [point](wikipedia://en/593693) and *motion*.

:::

Hyperbolic motions are often taken from [inversive geometry](wikipedia://en/295844): these are mappings composed of reflections in a line or a circle (or in a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) or a [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) for hyperbolic spaces of more than two dimensions). To distinguish the hyperbolic motions, a particular line or circle is taken as the [absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley_absolute). The proviso is that the absolute must be an [invariant set](wikipedia://en/1126638#Invariant_set) of all hyperbolic motions. The absolute divides the plane into two [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29), and hyperbolic motions must *not* permute these components.

One of the most prevalent contexts for inversive geometry and hyperbolic motions is in the study of mappings of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) by [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493). Textbooks on [complex functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_function) often mention two common models of hyperbolic geometry: the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) where the absolute is the real line on the complex plane, and the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) where the absolute is the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the complex plane. Hyperbolic motions can also be described on the [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189) of hyperbolic geometry.[^o2]

This article exhibits these examples of the use of hyperbolic motions: the extension of the metric $d(a,b) = |{\log(b/a)}|$ to the half-plane and the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738).

## Motions on the hyperbolic plane {#Motions_on_the_hyperbolic_plane}

Every [motion](wikipedia://en/36132944) ([transformation](wikipedia://en/43194879) or [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777)) of the hyperbolic plane to itself can be realized as the composition of at most three [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829). In *n*-dimensional hyperbolic space, up to *n*+1 reflections might be required. (These are also true for Euclidean and spherical geometries, but the classification below is different.)

All the isometries of the hyperbolic plane can be classified into these classes:

- Orientation preserving
  - the [identity isometry](wikipedia://en/15069) — nothing moves; zero reflections; zero [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622).
  - [inversion through a point (half turn)](wikipedia://en/12487536) — two reflections through mutually perpendicular lines passing through the given point, i.e. a rotation of 180 degrees around the point; two [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622).
  - [rotation](wikipedia://en/295829) around a normal point — two reflections through lines passing through the given point (includes inversion as a special case); points move on circles around the center; three degrees of freedom.
  - "rotation" around an [ideal point](wikipedia://en/4995922) (horolation) — two reflections through lines leading to the ideal point; points move along horocycles centered on the ideal point; two degrees of freedom.
  - translation along a straight line — two reflections through lines perpendicular to the given line; points off the given line move along hypercycles; three degrees of freedom.
- Orientation reversing
  - reflection through a line — one reflection; two degrees of freedom.
  - combined reflection through a line and translation along the same line — the reflection and translation commute; three reflections required; three degrees of freedom.

## Introduction of metric in the Poincaré half-plane model {#Introduction_of_metric_in_the_Poincaré_half-plane_model}

*Image: Ultraparallel theorem.svg.* For some hyperbolic motions in the half-plane see the [Ultraparallel theorem](wikipedia://en/1403889).

The points of the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) HP are given in [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) as {(*x*,*y*): *y* > 0} or in [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) as {(*r* cos *a*, *r* sin *a*): 0 \< *a* \< π, *r* > 0 }. The hyperbolic motions will be taken to be a [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of three fundamental hyperbolic motions. Let *p* = (*x,y*) or *p* = (*r* cos *a*, *r* sin *a*), *p* ∈ HP.

The fundamental motions are:

*p* → *q* = (*x* + *c*, *y* ), *c* ∈ **R** (left or right shift)

*p* → *q* = (*sx*, *sy* ), *s* > 0 ([dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homothetic_transformation))

*p* → *q* = ( *r*<sup>−1</sup> cos *a*, *r*<sup>−1</sup> sin *a* ) ([inversion in unit semicircle](wikipedia://en/295844#Circle_inversion)).

Note: the shift and dilation are mappings from inversive geometry composed of a pair of reflections in vertical lines or concentric circles respectively.

### Use of semi-circle Z {#Use_of_semi-circle_Z}

Consider the triangle {(0,0),(1,0),(1,tan *a*)}. Since 1 + tan<sup>2</sup>*a* = sec<sup>2</sup>*a*, the length of the triangle [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) is sec *a*, where sec denotes the [secant](wikipedia://en/30367#Reciprocal_functions) function. Set *r* = sec *a* and apply the third fundamental hyperbolic motion to obtain *q* = (*r* cos *a*, *r* sin *a*) where *r* = sec<sup>−1</sup>*a* = cos *a*. Now

\|*q* – (½, 0)|<sup>2</sup> = (cos<sup>2</sup>*a* – ½)<sup>2</sup> +cos<sup>2</sup>*a* sin<sup>2</sup>*a* = ¼

so that *q* lies on the semicircle *Z* of radius ½ and center (½, 0). Thus the tangent ray at (1, 0) gets mapped to *Z* by the third fundamental hyperbolic motion. Any semicircle can be re-sized by a dilation to radius ½ and shifted to *Z*, then the inversion carries it to the tangent ray. So the collection of hyperbolic motions permutes the semicircles with diameters on *y* = 0 sometimes with vertical rays, and vice versa. Suppose one agrees to measure length on vertical rays by using [logarithmic measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_measure):

*d*((*x*,*y*),(*x*,*z*)) = |log(*z*/*y*)|.

Then by means of hyperbolic motions one can measure distances between points on semicircles too: first move the points to *Z* with appropriate shift and dilation, then place them by inversion on the tangent ray where the logarithmic distance is known.

For *m* and *n* in HP, let *b* be the [perpendicular bisector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpendicular_bisector) of the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) connecting *m* and *n*. If *b* is parallel to the [abscissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscissa), then *m* and *n* are connected by a vertical ray, otherwise *b* intersects the abscissa so there is a semicircle centered at this intersection that passes through *m* and *n*. The set HP becomes a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) when equipped with the distance *d*(*m*,*n*) for *m*,*n* ∈ HP as found on the vertical ray or semicircle. One calls the vertical rays and semicircles the *hyperbolic lines* in HP. The geometry of points and hyperbolic lines in HP is an example of a [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610); nevertheless, the construction of the line and distance concepts for HP relies heavily on the original geometry of Euclid.

## Disk model motions {#Disk_model_motions}

Consider the disk D = {*z* ∈ **C** : *z z*\* \< 1 } in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) **C**. The geometric plane of [Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/648527) can be displayed in D with circular arcs perpendicular to the boundary of D signifying *hyperbolic lines*. Using the arithmetic and geometry of complex numbers, and [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493), there is the [Poincaré disc model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_disc_model) of the hyperbolic plane:

Suppose *a* and *b* are complex numbers with *a a*\* − *b b*\* = 1. Note that

\|*bz* + *a*\*|<sup>2</sup> − |*az* + *b*\*|<sup>2</sup> = (*aa*\* − *bb*\*)(1 − |*z*|<sup>2</sup>),

so that |*z*| \< 1 implies |(*a*z + *b*\*)/(*bz* + *a*\*)| \< 1 . Hence the disk D is an [invariant set](wikipedia://en/1126638#Invariant_set) of the Möbius transformation

f(*z*) = (*az* + *b*\*)/(*bz* + *a*\*).

Since it also permutes the hyperbolic lines, we see that these transformations are **motions** of the D model of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291). A complex matrix

$$q = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b^* & a^* \end{pmatrix}$$

with *aa*\* − *bb*\* = 1, which is an element of the special unitary group [SU(1,1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%281,1%29).
