# Isotropic measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isotropic measure"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), an **isotropic measure** is any mathematical [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) that is invariant under [linear isometries](wikipedia://en/254777#Linear_isometry). It is a standard simplification and assumption used in probability theory. Generally, it is used in the context of [measure theory](wikipedia://en/19873) on $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), for which it can be intuitive to study measures that are unchanged by rotations and translations. An obvious example of such a measure is the standard way of assigning a measure to [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of *n*-dimensional Euclidean space: [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An **isotropic measure** on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a ([Borel](wikipedia://en/4322)) measure that is [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670#Absolute_continuity_of_measures) on $\mathbb {R}^d \smallsetminus \{ 0 \}$ and that is invariant under [linear isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$.[^o4] Alternatively, an isotropic measure, $\mu(dz)$, is a measure for which there exists a real [density function](wikipedia://en/43487) $\mu_{0}(r)$ on $(0,\infty)$ such that $\mu(dz) = \mu_{0}\left( |z| \right)dz$ for $z \neq 0$.[^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

- The [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is invariant under [linear isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) and is hence an isotropic measure. In this case, $\mu(dz) = dz$.
- For $d=1$, the linear isometries of $\mathbb{R}^{1}$ are of the form $f(x) = x + c$ or $f(x) = -x + c$, for some constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Hence an isotropic measure on $\mathbb{R}^{1}$ must satisfy $\mu(A) = \mu(-A + b)$, for any $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{1}$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}$. The measure $\mu(dz) = |z|^{-2} dz$, for $z \neq 0$, is one such isotropic measure.

:::

## Unimodal measure {#Unimodal_measure}

In probability theory it is common that another assumption is added to measures in addition to the measure being isotropic. A **unimodal measure** (or **isotropic unimodal measure**) is any isotropic measure $\mu(dz) = \mu_{0}\left( |z| \right)dz$ such that $\mu_{0}(r)$ is nonincreasing on $(0,\infty)$. It is possible that $\mu\left( \left\{ 0 \right\} \right)>0$.[^o5]

## Isotropic and unimodal stochastic processes {#Isotropic_and_unimodal_stochastic_processes}

In studying [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895), in particular [Lévy processes](wikipedia://en/1025748),[^o10] a reasonable assumption to make is that, for each element of the index set, the probability distributions of the [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) are isotropic or even unimodal measures.

More specifically, an **isotropic Lévy process** is a [Lévy process](wikipedia://en/1025748), $X = \left( X_t, t \geq 0 \right)$, such that all its [distributions](wikipedia://en/23543), $p_{t} (dx)$, are isotropic measures.[^o4] A **unimodal Lévy process** (or **isotropic unimodal Lévy process**) is a [Lévy process](wikipedia://en/1025748), $X = \left( X_t, t \geq 0 \right)$, such that all its distributions, $p_{t} (dx)$, are unimodal measures.[^o4]
