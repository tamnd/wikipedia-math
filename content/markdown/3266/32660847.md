# Thomas Baxter (mathematician)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thomas Baxter"}

**Thomas Baxter** ( 1732–1740), was a schoolmaster and mathematician who published an erroneous method of [squaring the circle](wikipedia://en/201359). He was derided as a "pseudo-mathematician" by [F. Y. Edgeworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Y._Edgeworth), writing for the *[Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography)*.[^o2]

:::

When he was master of a private school at [Crathorne, North Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crathorne,_North_Yorkshire),[^o3] Baxter composed a book entitled *The Circle squared* (London: 1732), published in [octavo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavo).[^o7] The mathematical book begins with the untrue assertion that "if the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) be unity or one, the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) of that circle will be 3.0625", where the value should correctly be [pi](wikipedia://en/23601).[^o2] From this incorrect assumption, Baxter proves fourteen geometric theorems on circles, alongside some others on [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28geometry%29) and [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277), which Edgeworth refers to as of "equal absurdity" to Baxter's other assertions.[^o2] [Thomas Gent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Gent), who published the work, wrote in his reminisces, in *The Life of Mr. Thomas Gent*, that "as it never proved of any effect, it was converted to waste paper, to the great mortification of the author".[^o4][^o5]

This book has received harsh reviews from modern mathematicians and scholars. Antiquary [Edward Peacock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Peacock_%28antiquary%29) referred to it as "no doubt, great rubbish".[^o6] Mathematician [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289) included Baxter's proof among his *Budget of Paradoxes* (1872), dismissing it as an absurd work.[^o7]

Baxter published another work, *Matho, or the Principles of Astronomy and Natural Philosophy accommodated to the Use of Younger Persons* (London: 1740). Unlike Baxter's other work, this volume enjoyed considerable popularity in its time.[^o8][^o9]
