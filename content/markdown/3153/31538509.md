# Margrabe's formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Margrabe's formula"}

In [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), **Margrabe's formula**[^o2] is an [option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_%28finance%29) pricing formula applicable to an option to exchange one risky asset for another risky asset at maturity. It was derived by [William Margrabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Margrabe)[^o3] in 1978. Margrabe's paper has been cited by over 2000 subsequent articles.[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

## Formula {#Formula}

Suppose *S<sub>1</sub>(t)* and *S<sub>2</sub>(t)* are the prices of two risky assets at time *t*, and that each has a constant continuous dividend yield *q<sub>i</sub>*. The option, *C*, that we wish to price gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to exchange the second asset for the first at the time of maturity *T*. In other words, its payoff, *C(T)*, is max(0, *S<sub>1</sub>(T) - S<sub>2</sub>(T))*.

If the volatilities of *S<sub>i</sub>* are *σ<sub>i</sub>*, then $\textstyle\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2 \sigma_1\sigma_2\rho}$, where *ρ* is the Pearson's correlation coefficient of the Brownian motions of the *S<sub>i</sub>* 's.

Margrabe's formula states that the fair price for the option at time 0 is:

$$e^{-q_1 T}S_1(0) N(d_1) - e^{-q_2 T}S_2(0) N(d_2)$$

where:

$q_1,q_2$ are the expected dividend rates of the prices $S_1,S_2$ under the appropriate risk-neutral measure,

$N$ denotes the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) for a [standard normal](wikipedia://en/21462),

$$d_1 = (\ln (S_1(0)/S_2(0)) + (q_2 - q_1 + \sigma^2/2)T)/ \sigma\sqrt{T},$$

$$d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}.$$

:::

## Derivation {#Derivation}

Margrabe's model of the market assumes only the existence of the two risky assets, whose prices, as usual, are assumed to follow a [geometric Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/203523). The volatilities of these Brownian motions do not need to be constant, but it is important that the volatility of *S<sub>1</sub>/S<sub>2</sub>*, *σ*, is constant. In particular, the model does not assume the existence of a riskless asset (such as a [zero-coupon bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-coupon_bond)) or any kind of [interest rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate). The model does not require an equivalent risk-neutral probability measure, but an equivalent measure under S<sub>2</sub>.

The formula is quickly [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) by reducing the situation to one where we can apply the [Black-Scholes formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-Scholes_formula).

- First, consider both assets as priced in units of *S<sub>2</sub>* (this is called 'using *S<sub>2</sub>* as [numeraire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeraire)'); this means that a unit of the first asset now is worth *S<sub>1</sub>/S<sub>2</sub>* units of the second asset, and a unit of the second asset is worth 1.
- Under this change of numeraire pricing, the second asset is now a riskless asset and its dividend rate *q<sub>2</sub>* is the interest rate. The payoff of the option, repriced under this change of numeraire, is max(0, *S<sub>1</sub>(T)/S<sub>2</sub>(T) - 1)*.
- So the original option has become a [call option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_option) on the first asset (with its numeraire pricing) with a strike of 1 unit of the riskless asset. Note the dividend rate *q<sub>1</sub>* of the first asset remains the same even with change of pricing.
- Applying the [Black-Scholes formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-Scholes_formula) with these values as the appropriate inputs, e.g. initial asset value *S<sub>1</sub>(0)/S<sub>2</sub>(0)*, interest rate *q<sub>2</sub>*, volatility *σ*, etc., gives us the price of the option under numeraire pricing.
- Since the resulting option price is in units of *S<sub>2</sub>*, multiplying through by *S<sub>2</sub>(0)* will undo our change of numeraire, and give us the price in our original currency, which is the formula above. Alternatively, one can show it by the [Girsanov theorem](wikipedia://en/336940).
