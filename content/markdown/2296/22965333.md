# Supersingular prime (moonshine theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Supersingular prime"}

In [moonshine theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonshine_theory), a **supersingular prime** is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) that [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of the [Monster group](wikipedia://en/47422) $M$, which is the largest [sporadic simple group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_simple_group). There are precisely fifteen supersingular prime numbers: the first eleven primes [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2_%28number%29), [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_%28number%29), [5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_%28number%29), [7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_%28number%29), [11](wikipedia://en/208156), [13](wikipedia://en/37231), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [19](wikipedia://en/208292), [23](wikipedia://en/362203), [29](wikipedia://en/362212), and [31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/31_%28number%29); as well as [41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/41_%28number%29), [47](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/47_%28number%29), [59](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/59_%28number%29), and [71](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/71_%28number%29) .

:::

The non-supersingular primes are [37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37_%28number%29), [43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/43_%28number%29), [53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/53_%28number%29), [61](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/61_%28number%29), [67](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/67_%28number%29), and all primes greater than or equal to [73](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/73_%28number%29).

This usage of "supersingular prime" should not be confused with the related but distinct notion from [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705). In that context, a prime is called [supersingular *for* a given elliptic curve $E$](wikipedia://en/22965231) if reducing $E$ modulo $p$ yields a [supersingular elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/3028216)—a property that depends on the choice of curve, and every elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ has infinitely many such primes. By contrast, the fifteen supersingular primes defined here are not relative to any particular curve: they are characterized by a condition on *all* supersingular elliptic curves in characteristic $p$ at once (see below), and happen to coincide with the prime divisors of the order of the Monster group.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

[Andrew Ogg](wikipedia://en/31047928) (1975) proved that a prime number $p$ satisfies the following equivalent conditions:[^n1]

1. The [modular curve](wikipedia://en/669552) $X_0^+(p) = X_0(p)/w_p$, the quotient of $X_0(p)$ by the [Fricke involution](wikipedia://en/37607968) $w_p$, has [genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) zero.
1. Every [supersingular elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/3028216) in characteristic $p$ can be defined over the [prime subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_subfield) $\mathbb{F}_p$.
1. $p$ is one of the fifteen primes listed above.

The connection to elliptic curves arises through condition (2). For any prime $p$, there are only finitely many [$j$-invariants](wikipedia://en/451988) of supersingular elliptic curves in characteristic $p$; these $j$-invariants are the roots of the **supersingular polynomial** $ss_p(X) \in \mathbb{F}_p[X]$, and there are approximately $p/12$ of them (see [supersingular elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/3028216)).[^n2] In general, these $j$-invariants may lie in extensions of $\mathbb{F}_p$ (specifically in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$). Condition (2) states that for the fifteen supersingular primes—and only for these primes—every supersingular $j$-invariant already lies in $\mathbb{F}_p$ itself. For larger primes, some supersingular $j$-invariants necessarily require the quadratic extension $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$.

The equivalence of conditions (1) and (2) is a result in the arithmetic geometry of modular curves: the supersingular points on $X_0(p)$ in characteristic $p$ correspond to supersingular elliptic curves with a cyclic $p$-isogeny, and the genus of the quotient curve $X_0^+(p)$ controls whether all such points can be rational.[^n1]

:::

## Role in monstrous moonshine {#Role_in_monstrous_moonshine}

Shortly after establishing the equivalence of conditions (1) and (2), Ogg attended a talk by [Jacques Tits](wikipedia://en/219570) in which the [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) existence of a sporadic simple group—later constructed as the [Monster group](wikipedia://en/47422) by [Robert Griess](wikipedia://en/5034215) in 1982—was described, along with its predicted order. Ogg noticed that the prime factors of this predicted order were exactly the fifteen primes on his list, and offered a bottle of [Jack Daniel's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Daniel's) whiskey for an explanation of the coincidence.[^n1] This observation, connecting the arithmetic of modular curves to the representation theory of sporadic groups, was one of the motivating problems for the theory of [monstrous moonshine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonshine_theory), later developed by [Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and [Norton](wikipedia://en/1641166) (1979) and proved by [Richard Borcherds](wikipedia://en/292169) (1992).

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

All fifteen supersingular primes are [Chen primes](wikipedia://en/2331143), but 37, 53, and 67 are also Chen primes without being supersingular, and there are infinitely many Chen primes greater than 73.
