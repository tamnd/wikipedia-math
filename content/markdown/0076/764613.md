# Axiom schema

*Image: Axiom schema of replacement.svg.* Axiom schema of replacement: the image $F[A]$ of the domain set $A$ under the definable class function $F$ is itself a set, $B$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Axiom schema"}

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), an **axiom schema** (plural: **axiom schemata** or **axiom schemas**) is a rule or template that specifies a family of [axioms](wikipedia://en/928). A schema contains placeholders together with side conditions saying how those placeholders may be replaced; each permitted replacement is an *instance* of the schema.[^n1][^n2] Axiom schemata are commonly used to give finite descriptions of [theories](wikipedia://en/3512103) whose axioms include infinitely many formulas.[^n3]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let $L$ be the object language of a formal theory. An axiom schema is a [metalinguistic](wikipedia://en/402929) expression, or schema-template, containing one or more placeholders for expressions of $L$, together with side conditions specifying the expressions that may replace the placeholders.[^n1][^n2] The resulting object-language formulas are the instances of the schema and are taken as axioms of the theory.

The placeholders in an axiom schema may stand for terms, formulas, predicates, or relations, depending on the formal system. Side conditions often require that a term be [free for a variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_for_a_variable) in a formula, that a variable occur [free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable) in a formula, or that a variable not occur free in a specified formula.[^n3] Such conditions are part of the schema, not additional axioms of the object language.

This distinguishes a schematic letter from an ordinary object-language variable. For example, a schematic letter may be a placeholder for any formula of a language, whereas an object-language variable ranges over objects in the domain of interpretation.[^n1]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### First-order logic {#First-order_logic}

Many Hilbert-style presentations of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) use axiom schemata. For example, the quantifier axiom schema

$$\forall x\,\Phi(x)\rightarrow \Phi(t)$$

has as instances the formulas obtained by replacing $\Phi$ with an object-language formula and $t$ with a term, subject to the condition that $t$ is free for $x$ in $\Phi$.[^n3] Rules or axiom schemata of this kind avoid listing separately every formula obtained by the permitted substitutions.

### Arithmetic {#Arithmetic}

A standard first-order presentation of [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic) includes the induction schema. For every formula $\varphi(x,\vec y)$ in the language of arithmetic, with possible parameters $\vec y$, the schema has an instance of the form

$$\bigl(\varphi(0,\vec y)\land \forall x(\varphi(x,\vec y)\rightarrow \varphi(Sx,\vec y))\bigr)\rightarrow \forall x\,\varphi(x,\vec y).$$

Thus the induction principle is not a single first-order axiom but a family of axioms, one for each admissible formula $\varphi$.[^n3][^n4]

[Czesław Ryll-Nardzewski](wikipedia://en/1926283) proved that first-order Peano arithmetic is not finitely axiomatizable in its usual language.[^n5] Consequently, the induction schema cannot be replaced, in that language, by a finite list of axioms with the same deductive consequences.

### Set theory {#Set_theory}

The standard first-order axiomatization [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC) contains axiom schemata, including the [schema of separation](wikipedia://en/52386) and the [schema of replacement](wikipedia://en/52387). In the replacement schema, each formula that defines a functional relation gives an axiom asserting that the image of any set under that definable function is also a set.[^n6][^n7]

[Richard Montague](wikipedia://en/610182) proved a non-finite axiomatizability result for ZF-style set theories; in particular, assuming consistency, ZFC is not finitely axiomatizable in its ordinary first-order language.[^n8] Hence the replacement and separation schemata cannot simply be eliminated from ZFC by replacing them with finitely many axioms in the same language.

:::

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Finite axiomatisation"}

## Finite axiomatization {#Finite_axiomatization}

A theory is **finitely axiomatizable** if there is a finite set of sentences whose [deductive closure](wikipedia://en/7750539) is exactly the theory.[^n9] An axiom schema may be finitely describable while still specifying infinitely many axioms, since the schema is a metatheoretic recipe for generating instances rather than a finite set of object-language sentences.[^n10]

Whether a theory is finitely axiomatizable depends on the language in which the theory is formulated. A theory that is not finitely axiomatizable in one language may have a finitely axiomatized [conservative extension](wikipedia://en/1840214) in a richer language.

### Finitely axiomatized related theories {#Finitely_axiomatized_related_theories}

[Von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory](wikipedia://en/528491) (NBG) extends the language of set theory by adding classes. NBG is a conservative extension of ZFC for statements about sets, but unlike ZFC it can be finitely axiomatized: its class-existence theorem is obtained from finitely many class-existence axioms rather than from a schema.[^n7][^n11]

[New Foundations](wikipedia://en/945957) (NF), introduced by [W. V. O. Quine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._O._Quine), is usually presented with extensionality and a stratified comprehension schema.[^n12] [Theodore Hailperin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Hailperin) showed in 1944 that this comprehension principle is equivalent to a finite conjunction of instances, giving a finite axiomatization of NF.[^n13] Later presentations of NF and related systems may take finite axioms as basic and prove stratified comprehension as a theorem.[^n14]

:::

## In higher-order logic {#In_higher-order_logic}

Some axiom schemata in first-order theories can be replaced by single axioms in a higher-order language. For example, the first-order induction schema for arithmetic has a second-order analogue in which a variable quantifies over properties or classes directly:

$$\forall F\bigl((F(0)\land \forall x(F(x)\rightarrow F(Sx)))\rightarrow \forall x\,F(x)\bigr).$$

In this second-order sentence, $F$ is a genuine variable ranging over properties or classes, not a metalinguistic placeholder.[^n3] The second-order induction axiom and the first-order induction schema are therefore formally different, and the second-order axiom is stronger under the standard semantics for second-order logic.[^n3]

Analogously, some first-order set-theoretic schemata can be represented by quantifying over classes or higher-order objects in an expanded language. This is one reason class theories such as NBG can give finite axiomatizations while remaining conservative over ZFC for set-theoretic sentences.[^n7][^n11]
