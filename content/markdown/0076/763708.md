# Herman Goldstine

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Herman Goldstine"}

**Herman Heine Goldstine** (September 13, 1913 – June 16, 2004) was a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist), who worked as the director of the [IAS machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IAS_machine) at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) and helped to develop [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC), the first of the modern electronic digital computers. He subsequently worked for many years at [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) as an [IBM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Fellow), the company's most prestigious technical position.

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Goldstine was born in [Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago) in 1913 to Jewish parents. He attended the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago), where he joined the [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa) fraternity, and graduated with a degree in Mathematics in 1933, a master's degree in 1934, and a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in 1936. For three years he was a research assistant under [Gilbert Ames Bliss](wikipedia://en/3011234), an authority on the mathematical theory of [external ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_ballistics).

## Career {#Career}

### Early career {#Early_career}

In 1939 Goldstine began a teaching career at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan), until the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States)' entry into [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), when he joined the [U.S. Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army).

### BRL and the Moore School {#BRL_and_the_Moore_School}

As a result of the United States' entering [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), Goldstine left the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) where he was a professor in July 1942 to enlist in the Army. He was commissioned a [lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant) and worked as an ordnance mathematician calculating [firing tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firing_tables) at the [Ballistic Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistic_Research_Laboratory) (BRL) at [Aberdeen Proving Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen_Proving_Ground), [Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland). The firing tables were used in battle to find the appropriate [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation_%28ballistics%29) and [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) for aiming artillery, which had a range of several miles.

The firing table calculations were accomplished by about [one hundred women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_%28occupation%29) operating [mechanical calculators](wikipedia://en/432897#1900s_to_1970s). Each combination of gun, round and geographical region required a unique set of firing tables. It took about 750 calculations to compute a single trajectory and each table had about 3,000 trajectories. It took a [human computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_computer) at least 7 hours to calculate one trajectory.[^o8] To increase production, BRL enlisted the computing facilities of the [Moore School of Electrical Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore_School_of_Electrical_Engineering) at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) and Goldstine was the liaison between BRL and the university.

#### The ENIAC {#The_ENIAC}

While making some adjustments to the Moore School's mechanical [differential analyzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_analyzer), engineer [Joseph Chapline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Chapline) suggested Goldstine visit [John Mauchly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mauchly), a physics instructor at the Moore School, who had distributed a memorandum proposing that the calculations could be done thousands of times faster with an electronic computer using [vacuum tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tubes). Mauchly wrote a proposal and in June 1943 he and Goldstine secured funding from the Army for the project. The ENIAC was built in 30 months with 200,000 man hours. The ENIAC was huge, occupying a 30- by 60-foot room and weighing 30 tons with 18,000 [vacuum tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tubes). The device could only store 20 numbers and took days to program. It was completed in late 1945 as [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) was coming to an end.

#### The EDVAC {#The_EDVAC}

In spite of disappointment that ENIAC had not contributed to the war effort, interest remained strong in the Army to develop an electronic computer. Prior even to the ENIAC's completion, the Army procured a second contract from the Moore School to build a successor machine known as the [EDVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EDVAC). Goldstine, Mauchly, [J. Presper Eckert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Presper_Eckert) and [Arthur Burks](wikipedia://en/6479641) began to study the development of the new machine in the hopes of correcting the deficiencies of the ENIAC.

#### Meeting John von Neumann {#Meeting_John_von_Neumann}

In the summer of 1944 Goldstine had a chance encounter with the prominent mathematician [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) on a railway platform in [Aberdeen, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen,_Maryland), and Goldstine described his project at the University of Pennsylvania. Unbeknownst to Goldstine, von Neumann was then working on the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project), which was aiming to build the first [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb). The calculations needed for this project were also daunting.

#### The *First Draft* {#The_First_Draft}

As a result of his conversations with Goldstine, von Neumann joined the study group and wrote a memo called *[First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Draft_of_a_Report_on_the_EDVAC)*. Von Neumann intended this to be a memo to the study group, but Goldstine typed it up into a 101-page document that named von Neumann as the sole author. On June 25, 1946, Goldstine forwarded 24 copies of the document to those intimately involved in the EDVAC project; dozens or perhaps hundreds of mimeographs of the report were forwarded to von Neumann's colleagues at universities in the United States and in [Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Britain) in the weeks that followed. While incomplete, the paper was very well received and became a blueprint for building electronic digital computers. Due to von Neumann's prominence as a major American mathematician, the EDVAC architecture became known as the [von Neumann architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_architecture).

*Image: Flow chart of Planning and coding of problems for an electronic computing instrument, 1947.jpg.* [Flow chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_chart) from "Planning and coding of problems for an electronic computing instrument," 1947

One of the key ideas in the "first draft" was that the programmer could store a program in the computer's electronic memory, rather than program the computer using mechanical switches and patch cables. This and other ideas in the paper had been discussed in the EDVAC study group before von Neumann joined the group. The fact that Eckert and Mauchly, the actual inventors and designers of the ENIAC, were not named as co-authors created resentment that led to the group's dissolution at the end of the war.

Eckert and Mauchly went on to form the [Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eckert-Mauchly_Computer_Corporation), a company that in part survives today as the [Unisys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unisys) Corporation, while von Neumann, Goldstine and Burks moved on into academic life at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179). In Summer 1946, all of them were reunited to give presentations at the first computer course, which has come to be known as the [Moore School Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore_School_Lectures); Goldstine's presentations, given without notes, covered deeply and rigorously numerical mathematical methods useful in programs for digital computers.

### Institute for Advanced Study {#Institute_for_Advanced_Study}

After [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) Goldstine joined von Neumann and Burks at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) at [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey), where they built a computer referred to as the [IAS machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IAS_machine). Goldstine was appointed as assistant director of the project and was later its director, after 1954.

The IAS machine influenced the design of [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM)'s early computers through von Neumann, who was a consultant to IBM. When von Neumann died in 1957, the IAS computer project was terminated. Goldstine went on to become the founding director of the Mathematical Sciences Department at IBM's Watson Research Center in Yorktown Heights, New York.

### IBM {#IBM}

At IBM one of Goldstine's most significant roles was in fostering relations between IBM researchers and the academic community. In 1969 he was appointed an [IBM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Fellow), the company's most prestigious technical honor, and a consultant to the director of research. As a fellow Goldstine developed an interest in the history of computing and mathematical sciences. He wrote three books on the topic; *The Computer from Pascal to von Neumann*, *History of Numerical Analysis from the 16th Through the 19th Century* and *History of the Calculus of Variations from the Seventeenth Through the Nineteenth Century*. As the title implies, in *The Computer from Pascal to von Neumann*, Goldstine leaves little doubt that in his opinion von Neumann played a critical role in developing modern theories of computing.

### Later career {#Later_career}

In retirement Goldstine became executive director of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society) in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia) between 1985 and 1997, in which capacity he was able to attract many prestigious visitors and speakers.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

In 1941 he married [Adele Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adele_Goldstine), who was an [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC) programmer and who wrote the technical description for ENIAC. He had a daughter and a son with Adele, who died in 1964. Two years later, he married his second wife, Ellen Watson.

## Death {#Death}

Goldstine died on June 16, 2004, at his home in [Bryn Mawr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr,_Pennsylvania), [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania), after a long struggle with [Parkinson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease). His death was announced by the [Thomas J. Watson Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Watson_Research_Center) in [Yorktown Heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorktown_Heights), [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_%28state%29), where a [post-doctoral fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-doctoral_fellowship) was renamed in his honor.
