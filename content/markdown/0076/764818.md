# Jean-Victor Poncelet

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Jean-Victor Poncelet"}

**Jean-Victor Poncelet** (; 1 July 1788 – 22 December 1867) was a French [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who served most notably as the Commanding General of the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique). He is considered a reviver of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), and his work *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures* is considered the first definitive text on the subject since [Gérard Desargues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gérard_Desargues)' work on it in the 17th century. He later wrote an introduction to it: *Applications d'analyse et de géométrie*.[^o5]

:::

As a mathematician, his most notable work was in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), although an early collaboration with [Charles Julien Brianchon](wikipedia://en/1402609) provided a significant contribution to [Feuerbach's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feuerbach's_theorem). He also made discoveries about [projective harmonic conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_harmonic_conjugates); relating these to the poles and polar lines associated with [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673). He developed the concept of parallel lines meeting at a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) and defined the [circular points at infinity](wikipedia://en/8088700) that are on every circle of the plane. These discoveries led to the [principle of duality](wikipedia://en/539925), and the [principle of continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_continuity) and also aided in the development of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).[^o5]

As a military engineer, he served in [Napoleon](wikipedia://en/69880)'s campaign against the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire) in 1812, in which he was captured and held prisoner until 1814. Later, he served as a [professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor) of [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) at the École d'application in his home town of [Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metz), during which time he published *Introduction à la mécanique industrielle*, a work he is famous for, and improved the design of [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine) and [water wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel). While a professor of applied mechanics, he also, independently from [Coriolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspard-Gustave_de_Coriolis), pioneered the use of [work](wikipedia://en/149861) in mechanics and the [work-energy theorem](wikipedia://en/149861#Work–energy_principle), including coining the term "mechanical work".[^o6] In 1837, a tenured 'Chaire de mécanique physique et expérimentale' was specially created for him at the Sorbonne (the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris)).[^o7] In 1848, he became the commanding general of his *alma mater*, the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique).[^o5] He is honoured by having his name listed among notable French engineers and scientists displayed around the first stage of the [Eiffel tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_72_names_on_the_Eiffel_Tower).

## Biography {#Biography}

### Birth, education, and capture (1788–1814) {#Birth,_education,_and_capture_(1788–1814)}

*Image: Cloître sainte Constance (lycée Fabert de Metz).JPG.* The [Lycée Fabert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Fabert) in [Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metz), where Poncelet was fellow student.

Poncelet was born in [Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metz), France, on 1 July 1788, the [illegitimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_%28family_law%29) then legitimated [^o10] son of Claude Poncelet, a [lawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer) of the Parliament of Metz and wealthy landowner.[^o11] His mother, Anne-Marie Perrein, had a more modest background. [^o12] At a young age, he was sent to live with the Olier family at [Saint-Avold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Avold).[^o13] He returned to Metz for his secondary education, at [Lycée Fabert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Fabert).[^o11] After this, he attended the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique), a prestigious school in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989), from 1808 to 1810, though he fell behind in his studies in his third year due to poor health.[^o11] After graduation, he joined the Corps of [Military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military) [Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer). He attended the École d'application in his hometown during this time, and achieved the rank of [lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant) in the [French Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Army) the same year he graduated.[^o14]

Poncelet took part in [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_I_of_France)'s [invasion of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia) in 1812. His biographer Didion writes that he was part of the group which was cut from Marshal [Michel Ney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Ney)'s army at the [Battle of Krasnoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Krasnoi) and was forced to capitulate to the Russians,[^o15] though other sources say that he was left for dead.[^o11] Upon capture, he was interrogated by [General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General) [Mikhail Andreyevich Miloradovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Andreyevich_Miloradovich), but he did not disclose any information.[^o16] The Russians held him as a [prisoner of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner_of_war) and confined him at [Saratov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saratov).[^o17][^o18] During his imprisonment, in the years 1813–1814, he wrote his most notable work, *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures*, which outlined the foundations of projective geometry, as well as some new results. Poncelet, however, could not publish it until after his release in 1814.[^o18][^o5]

### Release and later employment (1822–1848) {#Release_and_later_employment_(1822–1848)}

In 1815, the year after his release, Poncelet was employed a military engineer at his hometown of Metz. In 1822, while at this position, he published *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures*. This was the first major work to discuss [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) since Desargues', though [Gaspard Monge](wikipedia://en/77840) had written a few minor works about it previously. It is considered the founding work of modern projective geometry.[^o14] [Joseph Diaz Gergonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Diaz_Gergonne) also wrote about this branch of geometry at approximately the same time, beginning in 1810. Poncelet published several papers about the subject in [Annales de Gergonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annales_de_Gergonne) (officially known as *Annales de mathématiques pures et appliquées*).[^o5] However, Poncelet and Gergonne ultimately engaged in a bitter priority dispute over the Principle of Duality.[^o14]

In 1825, he became the professor of [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) at the École d'Application in Metz, a position he held until 1835. During his tenure at this school, he improved the design of [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine) and [water wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel), deriving his work from the mechanics of the [Provençal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provence) mill from southern France.[^o20] Although the turbine of his design was not constructed until 1838, he envisioned such a design twelve years previous to that.[^o5] In 1835, he left École d'Application, and in December 1837 became a tenured professor at Sorbonne (the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris)), where a 'Chaire de mécanique physique et expérimentale' was specially created for him with the support of [Louis Jacques Thénard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Jacques_Thénard).[^o21]

### Commanding General at École Polytechnique (1848–1867) {#Commanding_General_at_École_Polytechnique_(1848–1867)}

In 1848, Poncelet became the Commanding General of his *alma mater*, the École Polytechnique.[^o23] He held the position until 1850, when he retired.

During this time, he wrote *Applications d'analyse et de géométrie*, which served as an introduction to his earlier work *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures*. It was published in two volumes in 1862 and 1864.[^o24] He was elected a Foreign Honorary Member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1865.[^o25]

### Poncelet–Steiner theorem {#Poncelet–Steiner_theorem}

Poncelet discovered the following theorem in 1822: [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9331) [compass and straightedge constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions) can be carried out using only a [straightedge](wikipedia://en/147854) if a single [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and its center is given. Swiss mathematician [Jakob Steiner](wikipedia://en/567445) proved this theorem in 1833, leading to the name of the theorem. The constructions that this theorem states are possible are known as Steiner constructions.

:::

### Poncelet's porism {#Poncelet's_porism}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Poncelet's porism** (sometimes referred to as **Poncelet's closure theorem**) states that whenever a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) is [inscribed](wikipedia://en/4470203) in one [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) and [circumscribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribe) another one, the polygon must be part of an infinite family of polygons that are all inscribed in and circumscribe the same two conics.[^o31][^o32]
