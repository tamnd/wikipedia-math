# Setoid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Setoid"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **setoid** (*X*, ~) is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) (or [type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_%28mathematics%29)) *X* equipped with an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) ~. A setoid may also be called **E-set**, **[Bishop](wikipedia://en/321995) set**, or **extensional set**.[^o2]

:::

Setoids are studied especially in [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478) and in [type-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-theoretic) [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358). Often in mathematics, when one defines an equivalence relation on a set, one immediately forms the [quotient set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set) (turning equivalence into [equality](wikipedia://en/90446)). In contrast, setoids may be used when a difference between identity and equivalence must be maintained, often with an interpretation of [intensional](wikipedia://en/14570) equality (the equality on the original set) and [extensional](wikipedia://en/39095) equality (the equivalence relation, or the equality on the quotient set).

::: {#o4 .proof cue=heading name="Proof theory"}

## Proof theory {#Proof_theory}

In proof theory, particularly the proof theory of [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics) based on the [Curry–Howard correspondence](wikipedia://en/254299), one often identifies a mathematical [proposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition_%28mathematics%29) with its set of [proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_%28mathematics%29) (if any). A given proposition may have many proofs, of course; according to the principle of proof irrelevance, normally only the truth of the proposition matters, not which proof was used. However, the Curry–Howard correspondence can turn proofs into [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775), and differences between algorithms are often important. So proof theorists may prefer to identify a proposition with a *setoid* of proofs, considering proofs equivalent if they can be converted into one another through [beta conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_conversion) or the like.

:::

## Type theory {#Type_theory}

In type-theoretic foundations of mathematics, setoids may be used in a type theory that lacks [quotient types](wikipedia://en/42011185) to model general mathematical sets. For example, in [Per Martin-Löf](wikipedia://en/1925764)'s [intuitionistic type theory](wikipedia://en/345023), there is no type of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), only a type of [regular Cauchy sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_Cauchy_sequence) of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). To do [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478) in Martin-Löf's framework, therefore, one must work with a *setoid* of real numbers, the type of regular Cauchy sequences equipped with the usual notion of equivalence. Predicates and functions of real numbers need to be defined for regular Cauchy sequences and proven to be compatible with the equivalence relation. Typically (although it depends on the type theory used), the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) will hold for functions between types (intensional functions), but not for functions between setoids (extensional functions). The term "set" is variously used either as a synonym of "type" or as a synonym of "setoid".[^o6]

## Constructive mathematics {#Constructive_mathematics}

In [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivism_%28mathematics%29), one often takes a setoid with an [apartness relation](wikipedia://en/7844336) instead of an equivalence relation, called a **constructive** setoid. One sometimes also considers a **partial** setoid using a [partial equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/3450320) or partial apartness (see e.g. Barthe *et al.*, section 1).
