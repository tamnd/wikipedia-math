# Autoregressive moving-average model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Autoregressive moving-average model"}

In the [statistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical) analysis of [time series](wikipedia://en/406624), an **autoregressive–moving-average** (**ARMA**) model is used to represent a [(weakly) stationary stochastic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_stochastic_process) by combining two components: [autoregression](wikipedia://en/1434444) (AR) and [moving average](wikipedia://en/20238265) (MA). These models are widely used for analyzing the structure of a series and for forecasting future values.

:::

The AR component specifies that the current value of the series depends linearly on its own past values (lags), while the MA component specifies that the current value depends on a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of past [error terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics). An ARMA model is typically denoted as ARMA(*p*, *q*), where *p* is the order of the autoregressive part and *q* is the order of the moving-average part.

The general ARMA model was described in the 1951 thesis of [Peter Whittle](wikipedia://en/2600214), *Hypothesis testing in time series analysis*, and it was popularized in the 1970 book by [George E. P. Box](wikipedia://en/2741338) and [Gwilym Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwilym_Jenkins).

ARMA models can be estimated by using the [Box–Jenkins method](wikipedia://en/2399066).

## Mathematical formulation {#Mathematical_formulation}

### Autoregressive model {#Autoregressive_model}

The notation AR(*p*) refers to the autoregressive model of order *p*. The AR(*p*) model is written as

$$X_t = \sum_{i=1}^p \varphi_i X_{t-i}+ \varepsilon_t$$

where $\varphi_1, \ldots, \varphi_p$ are [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) and the random variable $\varepsilon_t$ is [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182), usually [independent and identically distributed](wikipedia://en/30876902) (i.i.d.) [normal random variables](wikipedia://en/21462).[^o4][^o5]

In order for the model to remain [stationary](wikipedia://en/329898), the roots of its [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/1434444#Characteristic_polynomial) must lie outside the unit circle. For example, processes in the AR(1) model with $|\varphi_1| \ge 1$ are not stationary because the root of $1 - \varphi_1B = 0$ lies within the unit circle.[^o6]

The [augmented Dickey–Fuller test](wikipedia://en/3767048) can assesses the stability of an [intrinsic mode function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrinsic_mode_function) and trend components. For stationary time series, the ARMA models can be used, while for non-stationary series, [Long short-term memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_short-term_memory) models can be used to derive abstract features. The final value is obtained by reconstructing the predicted outcomes of each time series.

### Moving average model {#Moving_average_model}

The notation MA(*q*) refers to the moving average model of order *q*:

$$X_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{i=1}^q \theta_i \varepsilon_{t-i}\,$$

where the $\theta_1,...,\theta_q$ are the parameters of the model, $\mu$ is the expectation of $X_t$ (often assumed to equal 0), and $\varepsilon_1$, ..., $\varepsilon_t$ are i.i.d. white noise error terms that are commonly normal random variables.[^o8]

### ARMA model {#ARMA_model}

The notation ARMA(*p*, *q*) refers to the model with *p* autoregressive terms and *q* moving-average terms. This model contains the AR(*p*) and MA(*q*) models,[^o10]

$$X_t = \varepsilon_t + \sum_{i=1}^p \varphi_i X_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^q \theta_i \varepsilon_{t-i}.\,$$

### In terms of lag operator {#In_terms_of_lag_operator}

In some texts, the models is specified using the [lag operator](wikipedia://en/1481533) *L*. In these terms, the AR(*p*) model is given by

$$\varepsilon_t = \left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \varphi_i L^i\right) X_t = \varphi (L) X_t\,$$

where $\varphi$ represents the polynomial

$$\varphi (L) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \varphi_i L^i.\,$$

The MA(*q*) model is given by

$$X_t - \mu = \left(1 + \sum_{i=1}^q \theta_i L^i\right) \varepsilon_t = \theta (L) \varepsilon_t , \,$$

where $\theta$ represents the polynomial

$$\theta(L)= 1 + \sum_{i=1}^q \theta_i L^i .\,$$

Finally, the combined ARMA(*p*, *q*) model is given by

$$\left(1 - \sum_{i=1}^p \varphi_i L^i\right) X_t = \left(1 + \sum_{i=1}^q \theta_i L^i\right) \varepsilon_t \, ,$$

or more concisely,

$$\varphi(L) X_t = \theta(L) \varepsilon_t \,$$

or

$$\frac{\varphi(L)}{\theta(L)}X_t = \varepsilon_t \, .$$

This is the form used in [Box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Box), [Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwilym_M._Jenkins) & Reinsel.[^o12]

Moreover, starting summations from $i=0$ and setting $\phi_0 = -1$ and $\theta_0 = 1$, then we get an even more elegant formulation: $-\sum_{i=0}^p \phi_i L^i \; X_t = \sum_{i=0}^q \theta_i L^i \; \varepsilon_t \, .$

## Spectrum {#Spectrum}

The [spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672) of an ARMA process is

$$S(f) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\pi} \left\vert \frac{\theta(e^{-if})}{\phi(e^{-if})} \right\vert^2$$

where $\sigma^2$ is the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of the white noise, $\theta$ is the characteristic polynomial of the moving average part of the ARMA model, and $\phi$ is the characteristic polynomial of the autoregressive part of the ARMA model.[^o14][^o15]

## Fitting models {#Fitting_models}

### Choosing *p* and *q* {#Choosing_p_and_q}

An appropriate value of *p* in the ARMA(*p*, *q*) model can be found by plotting the [partial autocorrelation functions](wikipedia://en/16862495). Similarly, *q* can be estimated by using the [autocorrelation functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocorrelation_function). Both *p* and *q* can be determined simultaneously using extended autocorrelation functions (EACF).[^o18] Further information can be gleaned by considering the same functions for the residuals of a model fitted with an initial selection of *p* and *q*.

Brockwell & Davis recommend using [Akaike information criterion](wikipedia://en/690512) (AIC) for finding *p* and *q*.[^o19] Another option is the [Bayesian information criterion](wikipedia://en/2473272) (BIC).

### Estimating coefficients {#Estimating_coefficients}

After choosing *p* and *q,* ARMA models can be fitted by [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) regression to find the values of the parameters which minimize the error term. It is good practice to find the smallest values of *p* and *q* which provide an acceptable fit to the data. For a pure AR model, the [Yule-Walker equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AR_model#Calculation_of_the_AR_parameters) may be used to provide a fit.

ARMA outputs are used primarily to forecast (predict), and not to infer causation as in other areas of econometrics and regression methods such as OLS and 2SLS.

## History and interpretations {#History_and_interpretations}

The general ARMA model was described in the 1951 thesis of [Peter Whittle](wikipedia://en/2600214), who used mathematical analysis ([Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213) and [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659)) and statistical inference.[^o24][^o25] ARMA models were popularized by a 1970 book by [George E. P. Box](wikipedia://en/2741338) and Jenkins, who expounded an iterative ([Box–Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box–Jenkins)) method for choosing and estimating them. This method was useful for low-order polynomials (of degree three or less).[^o26]

ARMA is essentially an [infinite impulse response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_impulse_response) filter applied to white noise, with some additional interpretation placed on it.

In [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing), ARMA is represented as a digital filter with white noise at the input and the ARMA process at the output.

::: {#o28 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

ARMA is appropriate when a system is a function of a series of unobserved shocks (the MA or moving average part) as well as its own behavior. For example, stock prices may be shocked by fundamental information as well as exhibiting technical trending and [mean-reversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_reversion_%28finance%29) effects due to market participants.

:::

::: {#o30 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

There are various generalizations of ARMA. **Nonlinear** AR (NAR), nonlinear MA (NMA) and nonlinear ARMA (NARMA) model nonlinear dependence on past values and error terms. [Vector AR](wikipedia://en/2399016) (VAR) and vector ARMA (VARMA) model **multivariate** time series. [Autoregressive integrated moving average](wikipedia://en/1510134) (ARIMA) models non-stationary time series (that is, whose mean changes over time). [Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity](wikipedia://en/610752) (ARCH) models time series where the variance changes. Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA or periodic ARMA) models **periodic** variation. [Autoregressive fractionally integrated moving average](wikipedia://en/12531229) (ARFIMA, or Fractional ARIMA, FARIMA) model time-series that exhibits **long memory**. Multiscale AR (MAR) is indexed by the nodes of a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) instead of integers.

### Autoregressive–moving-average model with exogenous inputs (ARMAX) {#Autoregressive–moving-average_model_with_exogenous_inputs_(ARMAX)}

The notation ARMAX(*p*, *q*, *b*) refers to a model with *p* autoregressive terms, *q* moving average terms and *b* exogenous inputs terms. The last term is a linear combination of the last *b* terms of a known and external time series $d_t$. It is given by:

$$X_t = \varepsilon_t + \sum_{i=1}^p \varphi_i X_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^q \theta_i \varepsilon_{t-i} + \sum_{i=1}^b \eta_i d_{t-i}.\,$$

where $\eta_1, \ldots, \eta_b$ are the ***parameters*** of the exogenous input $d_t$.

Some nonlinear variants of models with exogenous variables have been defined: see for example [Nonlinear autoregressive exogenous model](wikipedia://en/957911).

Statistical packages implement the ARMAX model through the use of "exogenous" (that is, independent) variables. Care must be taken when interpreting the output of those packages, because the estimated parameters usually (for example, in [R](wikipedia://en/376707)[^o32] and [gretl](wikipedia://en/1166251)) refer to the regression:

$$X_t - m_t = \varepsilon_t + \sum_{i=1}^p \varphi_i (X_{t-i} - m_{t-i}) + \sum_{i=1}^q \theta_i \varepsilon_{t-i}.\,$$

where $m_t$ incorporates all exogenous (or independent) variables:

$$m_t = c + \sum_{i=0}^b \eta_i d_{t-i}.\,$$
