# Historical thinking

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Historical thinking"}

**Historical thinking** is a set of critical [literacy skills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literacy) for evaluating and analyzing [primary source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source) documents to construct a meaningful account of the past. Sometimes called historical reasoning skills, historical thinking skills are frequently described in contrast to historical content knowledge such as names, dates, and places. This [dichotomous](wikipedia://en/491865) presentation is often misinterpreted as a claim for the superiority of one form of knowing over the other. The distinction is generally made to underscore the importance of developing thinking skills that can be applied when individuals encounter any historical content. History educators have varying perspectives about the extent they should emphasize facts about the past, moral lessons, connections to current events, or historical thinking skills[^o2] and different belief about what historical thinking involves.

:::

## U.S. Academic Standards and Disciplinary Frameworks {#U.S._Academic_Standards_and_Disciplinary_Frameworks}

In the United States, the [National Center for History in the Schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Center_for_History_in_the_Schools) at the [University of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California), Los Angeles has developed history standards that include [benchmarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specification_%28technical_standard%29) for both content in U.S. and world history and historical thinking skills in [Kindergarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kindergarten) thru grade 4 and grades 5-12. In both of these age ranges, the Center defines historical thinking in five parts:

1. [Chronological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronological) Thinking
1. Historical [Comprehension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understanding)
1. Historical Analysis and [Interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671)
1. Historical [Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research) Capabilities
1. Historical Issues-Analysis and [Decision-Making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-Making)

As part of the national assessment effort called “The Nation's Report Card, ” the [United States Department of Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Department_of_Education) also developed benchmarks for student achievement in [U.S. history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._history). Their rubric divides history learning into three basic dimensions: major historical themes, chronological periods, and ways of knowing and thinking about history. The third dimension is further divided into two parts: historical knowledge and perspective, and historical analysis and interpretation.

## History Textbooks {#History_Textbooks}

History textbooks draw much attention from history educators and educational researchers. The use of [textbooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textbook) is nearly universal in [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History), [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government), and other [social studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_studies) courses at the [primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_education), and [secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education) levels in the U.S.; however, the role of textbooks in these courses remains controversial. Arguments against reliance on textbooks have ranged from ideological to pragmatic. While textbooks are often presented as the objective truth, they include constructed versions of a selected period in the past.

The construction and adoption of textbooks can be political, with groups fighting over the version of history they think should be presented to schoolchildren.[^o5] For example, Texas history textbooks did not include slavery as a central cause of the Civil War until 2018, even though slavery has long been understood to be a core issue causing the Civil War.

Historical thinking has been suggested as a way to avoid presenting only one narrative as the truth. In response to the controversy over Texas textbooks, [University of Northern Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Northern_Colorado) History Department Chair, Fritz Fischer said that "many of these problems could be solved if the school board prioritized making primary documents available to students, rather than deciding on which version of events ought to be taught."[^o5]

Still, other critics believe that using textbooks undermines the process of learning history by sacrificing thinking skills for content—that textbooks allow teachers to cover vast amounts of names, dates, and places while encouraging students simply to memorize instead of question or analyze historical events. For example, [Sam Wineburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Wineburg) argues, "Traditional history instruction constitutes a form of information, not a form of knowledge. Students might master an agreed-upon narrative, but they lacked any way of evaluating it, of deciding whether it or any other narrative, was compelling or true” (41).[^o6]

Despite their drawbacks, most textbook critics concede that textbooks are necessary tools in history education. Proponents of textbook-based [curricula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curricula) point out that history teachers require resources to support the broad scope of topics covered in the typical history classroom. Well-designed textbooks can provide a foundation on which enterprising educators can build other classroom activities that develop historical thinking.

## Teaching Models {#Teaching_Models}

Models for historical thinking have been developed to better prepare educators in facilitating historical thinking literacies in students.

### Benchmarks for Historical Thinking {#Benchmarks_for_Historical_Thinking}

Peter Seixas, a professor at the [University of British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_British_Columbia) and creator of *The Historical Thinking Project*, outlines six distinct but closely interrelated historical thinking concepts that constitute historical thinking literacy in students. The concepts focus on developing the skills necessary for students to create an account of the past using primary source documents and narratives, or what Seixas terms "traces" and "accounts." Although these benchmarks provide a model to develop historical literacies, Seixas states that these concepts can only be applied with substantial learning about the past[^o9]

- **Establishing Historical Significance** is the ability to identify which events, issues, and trends are historically significant and how they connect to one another.[^o10] [Historical significance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_significance) varies over time and between groups thus the criteria for determining what to study varies (e.g. Canadians will study Canadian history due to national connections).[^o11][^o12]
- **Using [Primary Sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source) as Evidence** involves locating, choosing, understanding, and providing context for the past using primary sources of evidence.[^o11] When teaching with primary sources, it is important to understand the differences between First, Second, and Third order sources, which are ordered by the originality of material and their proximity to the source of origin. *First Order Sources* or primary sources are contemporaneous records of events as they were [first described](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species_description) or as they happened, without interpretive commentary (e.g., reports, speeches, letters). Meanwhile, *second order or secondary sources* are those that corroborate, restate, explain, or interpret first order documents (e.g., commentaries, reviews, analyses). Finally *third order or tertiary sources* list or catalog other sources (e.g., encyclopedias). While second and third order sources are read for information, first order sources require students to draw inferences and make comparisons about the purposes, values, and world views of the authors to construct a narrative argument about history or assess what questions cannot be answered.
- **Identifying Continuity** is the ability to understand how issues change or stay the same over time and identify the change as progress or decline.[^o10] Placing historical events in chronological order is a way of identifying continuity and the ability to group events into identifiable periods helps to better understand their interconnection.[^o11]
- **Analyze Case and Consequences** requires students to recognize how humans can cause change that impacts present day social, political and natural (e.g., geographic) issues. Seixas and Peck note that this benchmark requires understanding causes and/or circumstances, that include "...long-term ideologies, institutions, and conditions, and short-term motivations, actions and events" that lead to particular consequences in history and affect the present.[^o13]
- **Taking a Historical Perspective** is understanding different social, cultural, intellectual, and emotional perspectives that formed the experiences and actions of people from the past.
- **Understanding the Moral Dimension of History** involves learning about moral issues today by examining the past. This is an important step in historical literacy because it requires reserving present day moral judgments to understand actions from the past without approving of those actions.[^o10]

### SCIM-C Strategy {#SCIM-C_Strategy}

Created by David Hicks, Peter E. Doolittle & E. Thomas Ewing, the SCIM-C strategy of historical thinking focuses on developing self-regulating practices when engaging with [primary sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source).[^o15] The SCIM-C strategy focuses on the development of historical questions to be answered when analyzing [primary sources.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source) This strategy provides a [scaffold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaffolding_Theory) for students as they build more complex investigation and analysis practices identified in the "capstone stage". The capstone stage in the SCIM-C model relies on students having analyzed a number of historical documents and having built some historical knowledge about the time, event, or issue being studied.[^o16]

- **Summarizing** is the process of finding information using a [primary source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source). This information can include the type of source (e.g. text, photograph), creator, subject, date it was created, and the opinion or perspective of the author.[^o15][^o16]
- **Contextualizing** is the skill of identifying when and in what context the [primary source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source) was created. By placing the primary source in context the source can more easily be treated a historical document separate from contemporary morals, ethics, and values.[^o15][^o16]
- **Inferring** is the ability to use the information gathered during the summarizing and contextualizing of a source to develop a greater understanding of the sub-text of a [primary source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_source). This stage relies on the ability to ask questions requiring inference on what is not stated directly in the source.[^o15][^o16]
- **Monitoring (Capstone Stage)** involves identifying initial assumptions that may have been a part of the historical question asked. This stage requires an analysis of the original question and whether the historical information found has answered that question or whether more questions need to be considered.[^o15][^o16]
- **Corroborating** is the final stage that can only occurs once several historical documents have been analyzed. This stage involves comparing evidence from a number of sources. This comparison includes looking for similarities and differences in perspectives, gaps in the information, and contradictions.[^o15][^o16]
