# Stipulative definition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stipulative definition"}

A **stipulative definition** is a type of [definition](wikipedia://en/7964) in which a term is given a new specific meaning for the purposes of [argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_argument) or discussion in a given context. When the term already exists, this definition may, but does not necessarily, contradict the [dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary) ([lexical](wikipedia://en/768500)) definition of the term. Because of this, a stipulative definition cannot be "correct" or "incorrect" within its stipulated context; it can only differ from other definitions, but it can be useful for its intended purpose.[^o2][^o3]

:::

For example, in the riddle of [induction](wikipedia://en/393736) by [Nelson Goodman](wikipedia://en/1179596), "[grue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grue_and_Bleen)" was *stipulated* to be "a property of an object that makes it appear green if observed before some future time *t*, and blue if observed afterward". "Grue" has no meaning in standard English; therefore, Goodman created the new term and gave it a *stipulative definition*.

## On stipulative definitions {#On_stipulative_definitions}

Stipulative definitions of existing terms are useful in making theoretical arguments, or stating specific cases. For example:

- Suppose we say that to love someone is to be willing to die for that person.
- Take "human" to mean any member of the species *Homo sapiens*.
- For the purposes of argument, we will define a "student" to be "a person under 18 enrolled in a local school".

Some of these are also [precising definitions](wikipedia://en/316745), a subtype of stipulative definition that may not contradict but only extend the [lexical definition](wikipedia://en/768500) of a term. [Theoretical definitions](wikipedia://en/814513), used extensively in [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and philosophy, are similar in some ways to stipulative definitions (although theoretical definitions are somewhat normative, more like [persuasive definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persuasive_definitions)).[^o3]

Many holders of controversial and highly charged opinions use stipulative definitions referred to as [persuasive definitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persuasive_definitions) to attach the emotional or other [connotations](wikipedia://en/55671) of a word to the meaning they would like to give it; for example, defining "murder" as "the killing of any living thing for any reason". The other side of such an argument is likely to use a different stipulative definition for the same term: "the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought" or "the premeditated killing of a human being". The lexical definition in such a case is likely to fall somewhere in between.

When a stipulative definition is confused with a lexical definition within an argument there is a risk of [equivocation](wikipedia://en/17690).
