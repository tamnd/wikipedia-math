# Complex projective space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complex projective space"}

*Image: Stereographic projection in 3D.svg.* The [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), the one-dimensional complex projective space, i.e. the [complex projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_projective_line).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **complex projective space** is the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) with respect to the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). By analogy, whereas the points of a [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) label the lines through the origin of a real [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the points of a complex projective space label the *[complex](wikipedia://en/217628)* lines through the origin of a complex Euclidean space (see below for an intuitive account). Formally, a complex projective space is the space of complex lines through the origin of an (*n*+1)-dimensional complex [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). The space is denoted variously as **P**(**C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>), **P**<sub>*n*</sub>(**C**) or **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>. When *n* = 1, the complex projective space **CP**<sup>1</sup> is the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), and when *n* = 2, **CP**<sup>2</sup> is the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155) (see there for a more elementary discussion).

:::

Complex projective space was first introduced by (von Staudt 1860) as an instance of what was then known as the "geometry of position", a notion originally due to [Lazare Carnot](wikipedia://en/26532764), a kind of [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484) that included other projective geometries as well. Subsequently, near the turn of the 20th century it became clear to the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424) that the complex projective spaces were the most natural domains in which to consider the solutions of [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) equations – [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) (Grattan-Guinness 2005, 445–446). In modern times, both the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and geometry of complex projective space are well understood and closely related to that of the [sphere](wikipedia://en/39782). Indeed, in a certain sense the (2*n*+1)-sphere can be regarded as a family of circles parametrized by **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>: this is the [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384). Complex projective space carries a ([Kähler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_metric)) [metric](wikipedia://en/195795), called the [Fubini&ndash;Study metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fubini&ndash;Study_metric), in terms of which it is a [Hermitian symmetric space](wikipedia://en/4127357) of rank 1.

Complex projective space has many applications in both mathematics and [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics). In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), complex projective space is the home of [projective varieties](wikipedia://en/320469), a well-behaved class of [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808). In topology, the complex projective space plays an important role as a [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) for complex [line bundles](wikipedia://en/361366): families of complex lines parametrized by another space. In this context, the infinite union of projective spaces ([direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566)), denoted **CP**<sup>∞</sup>, is the classifying space [K(Z,2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%28Z,2%29). In quantum physics, the [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) associated to a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state) of a quantum mechanical system is a [probability amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude), meaning that it has unit norm, and has an inessential overall phase: that is, the wave function of a pure state is naturally a point in the [projective Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/796760) of the state space. Complex projective manifold is 2n dimensional space or it is n dimensional complex space.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

*Image: Railroad-Tracks-Perspective.jpg.* Parallel lines in the plane intersect at the [vanishing point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_point) in the line at infinity.

The notion of a projective plane arises out of the idea of perspection in geometry and art: that it is sometimes useful to include in the Euclidean plane an additional "imaginary" line that represents the horizon that an artist, painting the plane, might see. Following each direction from the origin, there is a different point on the horizon, so the horizon can be thought of as the set of all directions from the origin. The Euclidean plane, together with its horizon, is called the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325), and the horizon is sometimes called a [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142). By the same construction, projective spaces can be considered in higher dimensions. For instance, the real projective 3-space is a Euclidean space together with a [plane at infinity](wikipedia://en/403143) that represents the horizon that an artist (who must, necessarily, live in four dimensions) would see.

These [real projective spaces](wikipedia://en/1014694) can be constructed in a slightly more rigorous way as follows. Here, let **R**<sup>*n*+1</sup> denote the [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) of *n*+1 dimensions, and regard the landscape to be painted as a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) in this space. Suppose that the eye of the artist is the origin in **R**<sup>*n*+1</sup>. Then along each line through his eye, there is a point of the landscape or a point on its horizon. Thus the real projective space is the space of lines through the origin in **R**<sup>*n*+1</sup>. Without reference to coordinates, this is the space of lines through the origin in an (*n*+1)-dimensional real [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).

To describe the complex projective space in an analogous manner requires a generalization of the idea of vector, line, and direction. Imagine that instead of standing in a real Euclidean space, the artist is standing in a complex Euclidean space **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> (which has real dimension 2*n*+2) and the landscape is a *complex* hyperplane (of real dimension 2*n*). Unlike the case of real Euclidean space, in the complex case there are directions in which the artist can look which do not see the landscape (because it does not have high enough dimension). However, in a complex space, there is an additional "phase" associated with the directions through a point, and by adjusting this phase the artist can guarantee that they typically see the landscape. The "horizon" is then the space of directions, but such that two directions are regarded as "the same" if they differ only by a phase. The complex projective space is then the landscape (**C**<sup>*n*</sup>) with the horizon attached "at infinity". Just like the real case, the complex projective space is the space of directions through the origin of **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>, where two directions are regarded as the same if they differ by a phase.

## Construction {#Construction}

Complex projective space is a [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) that may be described by *n* + 1 complex coordinates as

$$Z=(Z_1,Z_2,\ldots,Z_{n+1}) \in \mathbb{C}^{n+1}, \qquad (Z_1,Z_2,\ldots,Z_{n+1})\neq (0,0,\ldots,0)$$

where the tuples differing by an overall rescaling are identified:

$$(Z_1,Z_2,\ldots,Z_{n+1}) \equiv (\lambda Z_1,\lambda Z_2, \ldots,\lambda Z_{n+1}); \quad \lambda\in \mathbb{C},\qquad \lambda \neq 0.$$

That is, these are [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) in the traditional sense of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). The point set **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is covered by the patches $U_i=\{ Z \mid Z_i\ne0\}$. In *U*<sub>*i*</sub>, one can define a coordinate system by

$$z_1 = Z_1/Z_i, \quad z_2=Z_2/Z_i, \quad \dots, \quad z_{i-1}=Z_{i-1}/Z_i, \quad z_i = Z_{i+1}/Z_i, \quad \dots, \quad z_n=Z_{n+1}/Z_i.$$

The coordinate transitions between two different such charts *U*<sub>*i*</sub> and *U*<sub>*j*</sub> are [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) (in fact they are [fractional linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_linear_transformation)). Thus **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> carries the structure of a [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) of complex dimension *n*, and *[a fortiori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_fortiori)* the structure of a real [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) of real dimension 2*n*.

One may also regard **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> as a [quotient](wikipedia://en/237213) of the unit 2*n* + 1 [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) in **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> under the action of [U(1)](wikipedia://en/173993):

**CP**<sup>*n*</sup> = *S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup>/U(1).

This is because every line in **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> intersects the unit sphere in a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220). By first projecting to the unit sphere and then identifying under the natural action of U(1) one obtains **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>. For *n* = 1 this construction yields the classical [Hopf bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopf_bundle) $S^3\to S^2$. From this perspective, the differentiable structure on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is induced from that of *S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup>, being the quotient of the latter by a [compact group](wikipedia://en/750326) that acts properly.

:::

## Topology {#Topology}

The topology of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is determined inductively by the following [cell decomposition](wikipedia://en/353022). Let *H* be a fixed hyperplane through the origin in **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>. Under the projection map **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>\\{0} → **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>, *H* goes into a subspace that is homeomorphic to **CP**<sup>*n*−1</sup>. The complement of the image of *H* in **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is homeomorphic to **C**<sup>*n*</sup>. Thus **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> arises by attaching a 2*n*-cell to **CP**<sup>*n*−1</sup>:

$$\mathbf{CP}^n = \mathbf{CP}^{n-1}\cup \mathbf{C}^n.$$

Alternatively, if the 2*n*-cell is regarded instead as the open unit ball in **C**<sup>*n*</sup>, then the attaching map is the Hopf fibration of the boundary. An analogous inductive cell decomposition is true for all of the projective spaces; see (Besse 1978).

### CW-decomposition {#CW-decomposition}

One useful way to construct the complex projective spaces $\mathbf{CP}^n$ is through a recursive construction using [CW-complexes](wikipedia://en/353022). Recall that there is a homeomorphism $\mathbf{CP}^1 \cong S^2$ to the 2-sphere, giving the first space. We can then induct on the cells to get a [pushout map](wikipedia://en/582024)

$$\begin{matrix} S^3 & \hookrightarrow & D^4 \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \mathbf{CP}^1 & \to & \mathbf{CP}^2 \end{matrix}$$

where $D^4$ is the four ball, and $S^3 \to \mathbf{CP}^1$ represents the generator in $\pi_3(S^2)$ (hence it is homotopy equivalent to the [Hopf map](wikipedia://en/580384)). We can then inductively construct the spaces as pushout diagrams

$$\begin{matrix} S^{2n-1} & \hookrightarrow & D^{2n} \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ \mathbf{CP}^{n-1} & \to & \mathbf{CP}^n \end{matrix}$$

where $S^{2n-1} \to \mathbf{CP}^{n-1}$ represents an element in

$$\begin{aligned} \pi_{2n-1}(\mathbf{CP}^{n-1}) &\cong \pi_{2n-1}(S^{2n-2}) \\ &\cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \end{aligned}$$

The isomorphism of homotopy groups is described below, and the isomorphism of homotopy groups is a standard calculation in [stable homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/2946655) (which can be done with the [Serre spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/3085825), [Freudenthal suspension theorem](wikipedia://en/1880440), and the [Postnikov tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postnikov_tower)). The map comes from the [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526)

$$S^1 \hookrightarrow S^{2n-1} \twoheadrightarrow \mathbf{CP}^{n-1}$$

giving a non-contractible map, hence it represents the generator in $\mathbb{Z}/2$. Otherwise, there would be a homotopy equivalence $\mathbf{CP}^n \simeq \mathbf{CP}^{n-1}\times D^n$, but then it would be homotopy equivalent to $S^2$, a contradiction which can be seen by looking at the homotopy groups of the space.

### Point-set topology {#Point-set_topology}

Complex projective space is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), being a quotient of a compact, connected space.

### Homotopy groups {#Homotopy_groups}

From the fiber bundle

$$S^1 \hookrightarrow S^{2n+1} \twoheadrightarrow \mathbf{CP}^n$$

or more suggestively

$$U(1) \hookrightarrow S^{2n+1} \twoheadrightarrow \mathbf{CP}^n$$

**CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected). Moreover, by the [long exact homotopy sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_homotopy_sequence), the second homotopy group is π<sub>2</sub>(**CP**<sup>*n*</sup>) ≅ **Z**, and all the higher homotopy groups agree with those of *S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup>: π<sub>*k*</sub>(**CP**<sup>*n*</sup>) ≅ π<sub>*k*</sub>(*S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup>) for all *k* > 2.

### Homology {#Homology}

In general, the [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is based on the rank of the [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) being zero in odd dimensions; also *H*<sub>2*i*</sub>(**CP**<sup>*n*</sup>, **Z**) is [infinite cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic) for *i* = 0 to *n*. Therefore, the [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262) run

1, 0, 1, 0, ..., 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, ...

That is, 0 in odd dimensions, 1 in even dimensions 0 through 2n. The [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is therefore *n* + 1. By [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732) the same is true for the ranks of the [cohomology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group). In the case of cohomology, one can go further, and identify the [graded ring](wikipedia://en/103094) structure, for [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684); the generator of *H*<sup>2</sup>(**CP**<sup>n</sup>, **Z**) is the class associated to a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862), and this is a ring generator, so that the ring is isomorphic with

**Z**\[*T*\]/(*T*<sup>*n*+1</sup>),

with *T* a degree two generator. This implies also that the [Hodge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_number) *h*<sup>*i*,*i*</sup> = 1, and all the others are zero. See (Besse 1978).

### *K*-theory {#K-theory}

It follows from induction and [Bott periodicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bott_periodicity) that

$$K_\mathbf{C}^*(\mathbf{CP}^n) = K_\mathbf{C}^0(\mathbf{CP}^n) = \mathbf{Z}[H]/(H-1)^{n+1}.$$

The [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) satisfies

$$T\mathbf{CP}^n \oplus \vartheta^1 = H^{\oplus n+1},$$

where $\vartheta^1$ denotes the trivial line bundle, from the [Euler sequence](wikipedia://en/21166036). From this, the [Chern classes](wikipedia://en/294349) and [characteristic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_number) can be calculated explicitly.

### Classifying space {#Classifying_space}

There is a space $\mathbf{CP}^\infty$ which, in a sense, is the [inductive limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_limit) of $\mathbf{CP}^n$ as $n \to \infty$. It is [BU(1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BU%281%29), the [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) of [U(1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%281%29), the circle group, in the sense of [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), and so classifies complex [line bundles](wikipedia://en/361366). Equivalently it accounts for the first [Chern class](wikipedia://en/294349). This can be seen heuristically by looking at the fiber bundle maps

$$S^1 \hookrightarrow S^{2n+1} \twoheadrightarrow \mathbf{CP}^n$$

and $n \to \infty$. This gives a fiber bundle (called the **universal circle bundle**)

$$S^1 \hookrightarrow S^\infty \twoheadrightarrow \mathbf{CP}^\infty$$

constructing this space. Note using the long [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) of homotopy groups, we have $\pi_2(\mathbf{CP}^\infty) = \pi_1(S^1)$ hence $\mathbf{CP}^\infty$ is an [Eilenberg–MacLane space](wikipedia://en/1073230), a $K(\mathbb{Z},2)$. Because of this fact, and [Brown's representability theorem](wikipedia://en/1073746), we have the following isomorphism

$$H^2(X;\mathbb{Z}) \cong [X,\mathbf{CP}^\infty]$$

for any nice CW-complex $X$. Moreover, from the theory of [Chern classes](wikipedia://en/294349), every complex line bundle $L \to X$ can be represented as a pullback of the universal line bundle on $\mathbf{CP}^\infty$, meaning there is a pullback square

$$\begin{matrix} L & \to & \mathcal{L} \\ \downarrow & &\downarrow \\ X & \to & \mathbf{CP}^\infty \end{matrix}$$

where $\mathcal{L} \to \mathbf{CP}^\infty$ is the associated vector bundle of the principal $U(1)$-bundle $S^\infty \to \mathbf{CP}^\infty$. See, for instance, (Bott Tu 1982) and (Milnor Stasheff 1974).

## Differential geometry {#Differential_geometry}

The natural metric on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is the [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486), and its holomorphic isometry group is the [projective unitary group](wikipedia://en/4402359) PU(*n*+1), where the stabilizer of a point is

$$\mathrm{P}(1\times \mathrm{U}(n)) \cong \mathrm{PU}(n).$$

It is a [Hermitian symmetric space](wikipedia://en/4127357) (Kobayashi Nomizu 1996), represented as a coset space

$$U(n+1)/(U(1) \times U(n)) \cong SU(n+1)/S(U(1) \times U(n)).$$

The geodesic symmetry at a point *p* is the unitary transformation that fixes *p* and is the negative identity on the orthogonal complement of the line represented by *p*.

### Geodesics {#Geodesics}

Through any two points *p*, *q* in complex projective space, there passes a unique *complex* line (a **CP**<sup>1</sup>). A [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) of this complex line that contains *p* and *q* is a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) for the Fubini–Study metric. In particular, all of the geodesics are closed (they are circles), and all have equal length. (This is always true of Riemannian globally symmetric spaces of rank 1.)

The [cut locus](wikipedia://en/13664406) of any point *p* is equal to a hyperplane **CP**<sup>*n*−1</sup>. This is also the set of fixed points of the geodesic symmetry at *p* (less *p* itself). See (Besse 1978).

### Sectional curvature pinching {#Sectional_curvature_pinching}

It has [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) ranging from 1/4 to 1, and is the roundest manifold that is not a sphere (or covered by a sphere): by the [1/4-pinched sphere theorem](wikipedia://en/195243#Pinched_sectional_curvature), any complete, simply connected [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) with curvature strictly between 1/4 and 1 is diffeomorphic to the sphere. Complex projective space shows that 1/4 is sharp. Conversely, if a complete simply connected Riemannian manifold has sectional curvatures in the closed interval \[1/4,1\], then it is either diffeomorphic to the sphere, or isometric to the complex projective space, the [quaternionic projective space](wikipedia://en/2808931), or else the [Cayley plane](wikipedia://en/9410951) F<sub>4</sub>/Spin(9); see (Brendle Schoen 2008).

### Spin structure {#Spin_structure}

The odd-dimensional projective spaces can be given a [spin structure](wikipedia://en/2844303), the even-dimensional ones cannot.

## Algebraic geometry {#Algebraic_geometry}

Complex projective space is a special case of a [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810), and is a [homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363325) for various [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945). It is a [Kähler manifold](wikipedia://en/390538) carrying the [Fubini–Study metric](wikipedia://en/2574486), which is essentially determined by symmetry properties. It also plays a central role in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997); by [Chow's theorem](wikipedia://en/630505#Chow.27s_theorem), any compact complex [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095) of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is the zero locus of a finite number of polynomials, and is thus a projective [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808). See (Griffiths Harris 1994)

### Zariski topology {#Zariski_topology}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), complex projective space can be equipped with another topology known as the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) (Hartshorne 1977, §II.2). Let *S* = **C**\[*Z*<sub>0</sub>,...,*Z*<sub>*n*</sub>\] denote the [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) of polynomials in the (*n*+1) variables *Z*<sub>0</sub>,...,*Z*<sub>*n*</sub>. This ring is [graded](wikipedia://en/103094) by the total degree of each polynomial:

$$S = \bigoplus_{n=0}^\infty S_n.$$

Define a subset of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> to be *closed* if it is the simultaneous solution set of a collection of homogeneous polynomials. Declaring the complements of the closed sets to be open, this defines a topology (the Zariski topology) on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>.

### Structure as a scheme {#Structure_as_a_scheme}

Another construction of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> (and its Zariski topology) is possible. Let *S*<sub>\+</sub> ⊂ *S* be the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) spanned by the homogeneous polynomials of positive degree:

$$\bigoplus_{n>0}S_n.$$

Define [Proj *S*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proj) to be the set of all [homogeneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_ideal) [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) in *S* that do not contain *S*<sub>\+</sub>. Call a subset of Proj *S* closed if it has the form

$$V(I) = \{ p\in \operatorname{Proj} S\mid p\supseteq I\}$$

for some ideal *I* in *S*. The complements of these closed sets define a topology on Proj *S*. The ring *S*, by [localization at a prime ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring), determines a [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466) of [local rings](wikipedia://en/153391) on Proj *S*. The space Proj *S*, together with its topology and sheaf of local rings, is a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754). The subset of closed points of Proj *S* is homeomorphic to **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> with its Zariski topology. Local sections of the sheaf are identified with the [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) of total degree zero on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>.

### Line bundles {#Line_bundles}

All line bundles on complex projective space can be obtained by the following construction. A function *f* : **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>\\{0} → **C** is called [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/622844) of degree *k* if

$$f(\lambda z) = \lambda^k f(z)$$

for all λ ∈ **C**\\{0} and *z* ∈ **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>\\{0}. More generally, this definition makes sense in [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_%28linear_algebra%29) in **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>\\{0}. A set *V* ⊂ **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>\\{0} is called a cone if, whenever *v* ∈ *V*, then *λv* ∈ *V* for all λ ∈ **C**\\{0}; that is, a subset is a cone if it contains the complex line through each of its points. If *U* ⊂ **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is an open set (in either the analytic topology or the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744)), let *V* ⊂ **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>\\{0} be the cone over *U*: the preimage of *U* under the projection **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>\\{0} → **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>. Finally, for each integer *k*, let *O*(*k*)(*U*) be the set of functions that are homogeneous of degree *k* in *V*. This defines a [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466) of sections of a certain line bundle, denoted by *O*(*k*).

In the special case *k* = −1, the bundle *O*(−1) is called the [tautological line bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautological_line_bundle). It is equivalently defined as the subbundle of the product

$$\mathbf{C}^{n+1}\times\mathbf{CP}^n\to \mathbf{CP}^n$$

whose fiber over *L* ∈ **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is the set

$$\{(x,L)\mid x\in L\}.$$

These line bundles can also be described in the language of [divisors](wikipedia://en/1693055). Let *H* = **CP**<sup>*n*−1</sup> be a given complex hyperplane in **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>. The space of [meromorphic functions](wikipedia://en/80857) on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> with at most a simple pole along *H* (and nowhere else) is a one-dimensional space, denoted by *O*(*H*), and called the [hyperplane bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperplane_bundle). The dual bundle is denoted *O*(−*H*), and the *k*<sup>th</sup> tensor power of *O*(*H*) is denoted by *O*(*kH*). This is the sheaf generated by holomorphic multiples of a meromorphic function with a pole of order *k* along *H*. It turns out that

$$O(kH) \cong O(k).$$

Indeed, if *L*(*z*) = 0 is a linear defining function for *H*, then *L*<sup>−*k*</sup> is a meromorphic section of *O*(*k*), and locally the other sections of *O*(*k*) are multiples of this section.

Since *H*<sup>1</sup>(**CP**<sup>*n*</sup>,**Z**) = 0, the line bundles on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> are classified up to isomorphism by their [Chern classes](wikipedia://en/294349), which are integers: they lie in *H*<sup>2</sup>(**CP**<sup>*n*</sup>,**Z**) = **Z**. In fact, the first Chern classes of complex projective space are generated under [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732) by the homology class associated to a hyperplane *H*. The line bundle *O*(*kH*) has Chern class *k*. Hence every holomorphic line bundle on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a tensor power of *O*(*H*) or *O*(−*H*). In other words, the [Picard group](wikipedia://en/1850134) of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is generated as an abelian group by the hyperplane class \[*H*\] (Hartshorne 1977).
