# Erdős–Moser equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Erdős–Moser equation"}

::: {#o2 .problem cue=unsolved}

Does the Erdős–Moser equation have solutions other than $1^{1}+2^{1}=3^{1}$?

:::

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **Erdős–Moser equation** is

:::

$$1^k+2^k+\cdots+(m-1)^k=m^k,$$

where $m$ and $k$ are restricted to the positive [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers)—that is, it is considered as a [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109). The only known solution is $1^{1} + 2^{1} = 3^{1}$, and [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) conjectured that no further solutions exist. Any further solutions must have $m > 10^{10^{9}}$.[^o29]

Throughout this article, $p$ refers exclusively to [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666).

## Constraints on solutions {#Constraints_on_solutions}

In 1953, [Leo Moser](wikipedia://en/745180) proved that, in any further solutions,[^o34]

1. $k$ is even,
1. $p | (m - 1)$ implies $(p - 1) | k$,
1. $p | (m + 1)$ implies $(p - 1) | k$,
1. $p | (2m + 1)$ implies $(p - 1) | k$,
1. $m - 1$ is [squarefree](wikipedia://en/29525),
1. $m + 1$ is either squarefree or 4 times an odd squarefree number,
1. $2m - 1$ is squarefree,
1. $2m + 1$ is squarefree,
1. $$\sum_{p|(m-1)} \frac{m-1}{p} \equiv -1 \pmod{m-1},$$
1. $$\sum_{p|(m+1)} \frac{m+1}{p} \equiv -2 \pmod{m+1} \quad \text{(if }m+1\text{ is even)},$$
1. $$\sum_{p|(2m-1)} \frac{2m-1}{p} \equiv -2 \pmod{2m-1},$$
1. $\sum_{p|(2m+1)} \frac{2m+1}{p} \equiv -4 \pmod{2m+1},$ and
1. $$m > 10^{10^{6}}.$$

In 1966, it was additionally shown that[^o31]

1. $6 \le k + 2 < m < 3 (k + 1) / 2$, and
1. $m - 1$ cannot be prime.

In 1994, it was shown that[^o32]

1. [$\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,200)$](wikipedia://en/17961) divides $k$,
1. $m \equiv 3 (\bmod 2^{\operatorname{ord}_{2}(k) + 3})$, where $\operatorname{ord}_{2}(n)$ is the [2-adic valuation](wikipedia://en/16047618) of $n$; equivalently, $\operatorname{ord}_{2}(m - 3) = \operatorname{ord}_{2}(k) + 3$,
1. for any odd prime $p$ dividing $m$, we have $k \not\equiv 0, 2\pmod{p - 1}$,
1. any prime factor of $m$ must be [irregular](wikipedia://en/323689) and > 10000.

In 1999, Moser's method was refined to show that $m > 1.485 \times 10^{9,321,155}$.[^o28]

In 2002, it was shown that $k$ must be a multiple of $2^{3} \cdot 3\# \cdot 5\# \cdot 7\# \cdot 19\# \cdot 1000\#$, where the symbol $\#$ indicates the [primorial](wikipedia://en/422786); that is, $n\#$ is the product of all prime numbers $\le n$. This number exceeds $5.7462 \times 10^{427}$.

In 2009, it was shown that $2k / (2m - 3)$ must be a [convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_%28continued_fraction%29) of [$\ln(2)$](wikipedia://en/27153398); in what the authors of that paper call "one of very few instances where a large scale computation of a numerical constant has an application", it was then determined that $m > 2.7139 \times 10^{1,667,658,416}$.[^o29]

In 2010, it was shown that[^o33]

1. $m \equiv 3\pmod{8}$ and $m \equiv \pm1\pmod{3}$, and
1. $(m^{2} - 1) (4m^{2} - 1) / 12$ has at least 4,990,906 prime factors, none of which are repeated.

The number 4,990,906 arises from the fact that $150\%^{4990905}_{n=1} \tfrac{1}{p_{n}} < \tfrac{19}{6} < 150\%^{4990906}_{n=1} \tfrac{1}{p_{n}},$ where $p_{n}$ is the $n$<sup>th</sup> prime number.

## Moser's method {#Moser's_method}

First, let $p$ be a prime factor of $m - 1$. [Leo Moser](wikipedia://en/745180) showed[^o34] that this implies that $p - 1$ divides $k$ and that

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$1 + \frac{m - 1}{p} \equiv 0 \pmod{p}, \tag{1}$$

:::

which upon multiplying by $p$ yields

$$p + m - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{p^2}.$$

This in turn implies that $m - 1$ must be [squarefree](wikipedia://en/29525). Furthermore, since [nontrivial](wikipedia://en/193053) solutions have $m - 1 > 2$ and since all squarefree numbers in this range must have at least one odd prime factor, the assumption that $p - 1$ divides $k$ implies that $k$ must be even.

One congruence of the form ((1)) exists for each prime factor $p$ of $m - 1$. Multiplying all of them together yields

$$\prod_{p|(m-1)} \left( 1 + \frac{m-1}{p} \right) \equiv 0 \pmod{m-1}.$$

Expanding out the product yields

$$1 + \sum_{p|(m-1)} \frac{m-1}{p} + (\text{higher-order terms}) \equiv 0 \pmod{m-1},$$

where the higher-order terms are products of multiple factors of the form $(m - 1) / p$, with different values of $p$ in each factor. These terms are all divisible by $m - 1$, so they all drop out of the congruence, yielding

$$1 + \sum_{p|(m-1)} \frac{m-1}{p} \equiv 0 \pmod{m-1}.$$

Dividing out the modulus yields

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\frac{1}{m-1} + \sum_{p|(m-1)} \frac{1}{p} \equiv 0 \pmod{1}. \tag{2}$$

:::

Similar reasoning yields the congruences

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\frac{2}{m+1} + \sum_{p|(m+1)} \frac{1}{p} \equiv 0 \pmod{1} \quad \text{(if }m+1\text{ is even)}, \tag{3}$$

:::

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\frac{2}{2m-1} + \sum_{p|(2m-1)} \frac{1}{p} \equiv 0 \pmod{1}, \quad \text{and} \tag{4}$$

:::

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\frac{4}{2m+1} + \sum_{p|(2m+1)} \frac{1}{p} \equiv 0 \pmod{1}. \tag{5}$$

:::

The congruences ((2)), ((3)), ((4)), and ((5)) are quite restrictive; for example, the only values of $m < 1000$ which satisfy ((2)) are 3, 7, and 43, and these are ruled out by ((4)).

We now split into two cases: either $m + 1$ is even, or it is odd.

In the case that $m + 1$ is even, adding the left-hand sides of the congruences ((2)), ((3)), ((4)), and ((5)) must yield an integer, and this integer must be at least 4. Furthermore, the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) shows that no prime $p > 3$ can divide more than one of the numbers in the set $\{m - 1, m + 1, 2m - 1, 2m + 1\}$, and that 2 and 3 can divide at most two of these numbers. Letting $M = (m - 1) (m + 1) (2m - 1) (2m + 1)$, we then have

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\frac{1}{m-1} + \frac{2}{m+1} + \frac{2}{2m-1} + \frac{4}{2m+1} + \sum_{p|M} \frac{1}{p} \geq 4 - \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3} = 3.1666.... \tag{6}$$

:::

Since there are no nontrivial solutions with $m < 1000$, the part of the LHS of ((6)) outside the sigma cannot exceed $0.006$; we therefore have

$$\sum_{p|M} \frac{1}{p} > 3.16.$$

Therefore, if $\sum_{p \leq x} \frac{1}{p} < 3.16$, then $M > \prod_{p \leq x} p$. In Moser's original paper,[^o34] bounds on the [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826) are used to observe that

$$\sum_{p \leq 10^7} \frac{1}{p} < 3.16.$$

Therefore, $M$ must exceed the product of the first 10,000,000 primes. This in turn implies that $m > 10^{10^{6}}$ in this case.

In the case that $m + 1$ is odd, we cannot use ((3)), so instead of ((6)) we obtain

$$\frac{1}{m-1} + \frac{2}{2m-1} + \frac{4}{2m+1} + \sum_{p|N} \frac{1}{p} \geq 3 - \frac{1}{3} = 2.666...,$$

where $N = (m - 1) (2m - 1) (2m + 1)$. On the surface, this appears to be a weaker condition than ((6)), but since $m + 1$ is odd, the prime 2 cannot appear on the greater side of this inequality, and it turns out to be a stronger restriction on $m$ than the other case.

Therefore, any nontrivial solutions have $m > 10^{10^{6}}$.

In 1999, this method was refined by using computers to replace the prime-counting estimates with exact computations; this yielded the bound $m > 1.485 \times 10^{9,321,155}$.

## Bounding the ratio $m / (k + 1)$ {#Bounding_the_ratio}

Let $S_{k}(m) = 1^{k} + 2^{k} + \cdots + (m - 1)^{k}$. Then the Erdős–Moser equation becomes $S_{k}(m) = m^{k}$.

### Method 1: Integral comparisons {#Method_1:_Integral_comparisons}

By comparing the sum $S_{k}(m)$ to definite integrals of the function $x^{k}$, one can obtain the bounds $1 < m / (k + 1) < 3$.

The sum $S_{k}(m) = 1^{k} + 2^{k} + \cdots + (m - 1)^{k}$ is the [upper Riemann sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Riemann_sum) corresponding to the integral $\int_0^{m-1} x^k \, \mathrm{d}x$ in which the interval has been partitioned on the integer values of $x$, so we have

$$S_k(m) > \int_0^{m-1} x^k \; \mathrm{d}x.$$

By hypothesis, $S_{k}(m) = m^{k}$, so

$$m^k > \frac{(m-1)^{k+1}}{k+1},$$

which leads to

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\frac{m}{k+1} < \left( 1 + \frac{1}{m-1} \right)^{k+1}. \tag{7}$$

:::

Similarly, $S_{k}(m)$ is the lower Riemann sum corresponding to the integral $\int_1^m x^k \, \mathrm{d}x$ in which the interval has been partitioned on the integer values of $x$, so we have

$$S_k(m) \leq \int_1^m x^k \; \mathrm{d}x.$$

By hypothesis, $S_{k}(m) = m^{k}$, so

$$m^k \leq \frac{m^{k+1} - 1}{k+1} < \frac{m^{k+1}}{k+1},$$

and so

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$k+1 < m. \tag{8}$$

:::

Applying this to ((7)) yields

$$\frac{m}{k+1} < \left(1 + \frac{1}{m-1}\right)^m = \left(1 + \frac{1}{m-1}\right)^{m-1} \cdot \left(\frac{m}{m-1}\right) < e \cdot \frac{m}{m-1}.$$

Computation shows that there are no nontrivial solutions with $m \le 10$, so we have

$$\frac{m}{k+1} < e \cdot \frac{11}{11-1} < 3.$$

Combining this with ((8)) yields $1 < m / (k + 1) < 3$, as desired.

### Method 2: Algebraic manipulations {#Method_2:_Algebraic_manipulations}

The upper bound $m / (k + 1) < 3$ can be reduced to $m / (k + 1) < 3/2$ using an algebraic method:

Let $r$ be a positive integer. Then the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) yields

$$(\ell + 1)^{r+1} = \sum_{i=0}^{r+1} \binom{r+1}{i} \ell^{r+1-i}.$$

Summing over $\ell$ yields

$$\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1} (\ell + 1)^{r+1} = \sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1} \left( \sum_{i=0}^{r+1} \binom{r+1}{i} \ell^{r+1-i} \right).$$

Reindexing on the left and rearranging on the right yields

$$\sum_{\ell=2}^{m} \ell^{r+1} = \sum_{i=0}^{r+1} \binom{r+1}{i} \sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1} \ell^{r+1-i}$$

$$\sum_{\ell=1}^{m} \ell^{r+1} = 1 + \sum_{i=0}^{r+1} \binom{r+1}{i} S_{r+1-i}(m)$$

$$S_{r+1}(m+1) - S_{r+1}(m) = 1 + (r+1) S_{r}(m) + \sum_{i=2}^{r+1} \binom{r+1}{i} S_{r+1-i}(m)$$

::: {#o16 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$m^{r+1} = 1 + (r+1) S_{r}(m) + \sum_{i=2}^{r+1} \binom{r+1}{i} S_{r+1-i}(m). \tag{9}$$

:::

Taking $r = k$ yields

$$m^{k+1} = 1 + (k+1) S_{k}(m) + \sum_{i=2}^{k+1} \binom{k+1}{i} S_{k+1-i}(m).$$

By hypothesis, $S_{k} = m^{k}$, so

$$m^{k+1} = 1 + (k+1) m^k + \sum_{i=2}^{k+1} \binom{k+1}{i} S_{k+1-i}(m)$$

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$m^k ( m - (k+1) ) = 1 + \sum_{i=2}^{k+1} \binom{k+1}{i} S_{k+1-i}(m). \tag{10}$$

:::

Since the RHS is positive, we must therefore have

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$k + 1 < m. \tag{11}$$

:::

Returning to ((9)) and taking $r = k - 1$ yields

$$m^k = 1 + k \cdot S_{k-1}(m) + \sum_{i=2}^k \binom{k}{i} S_{k-i}(m)$$

$$m^k = 1 + \sum_{s=1}^k \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(m).$$

Substituting this into ((10)) to eliminate $m^{k}$ yields

$$\left( 1 + \sum_{s=1}^k \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(m) \right) ( m - (k+1) ) = 1 + \sum_{i=2}^{k+1} \binom{k+1}{i} S_{k+1-i}(m).$$

Reindexing the sum on the right with the substitution $i = s + 1$ yields

$$\left( 1 + \sum_{s=1}^k \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(m) \right) ( m - (k+1) ) = 1 + \sum_{s=1}^k \binom{k+1}{s+1} S_{k-s}(m)$$

$$m - (k+1) + ( m - (k+1) ) \sum_{s=1}^k \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(m) = 1 + \sum_{s=1}^k \frac{k+1}{s+1} \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(m)$$

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$m - k - 2 = \sum_{s=1}^k \left( \frac{k+1}{s+1} - m + (k+1) \right) \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(m). \tag{12}$$

:::

We already know from ((11)) that $k + 1 < m$. This leaves open the possibility that $m = k + 2$; however, substituting this into ((12)) yields

$$0 = \sum_{s=1}^k \left( \frac{k+1}{s+1} - 1 \right) \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(k+2)$$

$$0 = \sum_{s=1}^k \frac{k-s}{s+1} \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(k+2)$$

$$0 = \frac{k-k}{k+1} \binom{k}{k} S_{k-k}(k+2) + \sum_{s=1}^{k-1} \frac{k-s}{s+1} \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(k+2)$$

$$0 = 0 + \sum_{s=1}^{k-1} \frac{k-s}{s+1} \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(k+2),$$

which is impossible for $k > 1$, since the sum contains only positive terms. Therefore, any nontrivial solutions must have $m \ne k + 2$; combining this with ((11)) yields

$$k + 2 < m.$$

We therefore observe that the left-hand side of ((12)) is positive, so

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$0 < \sum_{s=1}^k \left( \frac{k+1}{s+1} - m + (k+1) \right) \binom{k}{s} S_{k-s}(m). \tag{13}$$

:::

Since $k > 1$, the sequence $\left\{ (k+1)/(s+1) - m + (k+1) \right\}_{s=1}^\infty$ is decreasing. This and ((13)) together imply that its first term (the term with $s = 1$) must be positive: if it were not, then every term in the sum would be nonpositive, and therefore so would the sum itself. Thus,

$$0 < \frac{k+1}{1+1} - m + (k+1),$$

which yields

$$m < \frac{3}{2} \cdot (k+1)$$

and therefore

$$\frac{m}{k+1} < \frac{3}{2},$$

as desired.

## Continued fractions {#Continued_fractions}

Any potential solutions to the equation must arise from the [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) of the [natural logarithm of 2](wikipedia://en/27153398): specifically, $2k / (2m - 3)$ must be a [convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_%28continued_fraction%29) of that number.[^o29]

By expanding the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of $(1 - y)^{k} e^{ky}$ about $y = 0$, one finds

$$(1 - y)^k = e^{-ky} \left( 1 - \frac{k}{2} y^2 - \frac{k}{3} y^3 + \frac{k(k-2)}{8} y^4 + \frac{k(5k-6)}{30} y^5 + O(y^6) \right) \quad \text{as } y \rightarrow 0.$$

More elaborate analysis sharpens this to

::: {#o22 .equation cue=numblk name="14"}

$$\begin{aligned} e^{-ky} \left( 1 - \frac{k}{2} y^2 - \frac{k}{3} y^3 + \frac{k(k-2)}{8} y^4 + \frac{k(5k-6)}{30} y^5 - \frac{k^3}{6} y^6 \right) \\ < (1 - y)^k < e^{-ky} \left( 1 - \frac{k}{2} y^2 - \frac{k}{3} y^3 + \frac{k(k-2)}{8} y^4 + \frac{k^2}{2} y^5 \right) \end{aligned} \tag{14}$$

:::

for $k > 8$ and $0 < y < 1$.

The Erdős–Moser equation is equivalent to

$$1 = \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \left( 1 - \frac{j}{m} \right)^k.$$

Applying ((14)) to each term in this sum yields

$$\begin{aligned} T_0 - \frac{k}{2m^2} T_2 - \frac{k}{3m^3} T_3 + \frac{k(k-2)}{8m^4} T_4 + \frac{k(5k-6)}{30m^5} T_5 - \frac{k^3}{6m^6} T_6 \qquad \qquad \\ < \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \left(1 - \frac{j}{m} \right)^k < T_0 - \frac{k}{2m^2} T_2 - \frac{k}{3m^3} T_3 + \frac{k(k-2)}{8m^4} T_4 + \frac{k^2}{2m^5} T_5, \end{aligned}$$

where $T_n = \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} j^n z^j$ and $z = e^{-k/m}$. Further manipulation eventually yields

::: {#o23 .equation cue=numblk name="15"}

$$\left\vert 1 - \frac{z}{1-z} + \frac{k}{2m^2}\frac{z^2+z}{(1-z)^3} + \frac{k}{3m^3}\frac{z^3+4z^2+z}{(1-z)^4} - \frac{k(k-2)}{8m^4}\frac{z^4+11z^3+11z^2+z}{(1-z)^5} \right\vert < \frac{110000}{m^3}. \tag{15}$$

:::

We already know that $k/m$ is bounded as $m \to \infty$; making the [ansatz](wikipedia://en/1359360) $k/m = c + O(1/m)$, and therefore $z = e^{-c} + O(1/m)$, and substituting it into ((15)) yields

$$1 - \frac{1}{e^c - 1} = O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right) \quad \text{as } m \rightarrow \infty;$$

therefore $c = \ln(2)$. We therefore have

::: {#o24 .equation cue=numblk name="16"}

$$\frac{k}{m} = \ln(2) + \frac{a}{m} + \frac{b}{m^2} + O\left(\frac{1}{m^3}\right) \quad \text{as } m \rightarrow \infty, \tag{16}$$

:::

and so

$$\frac{1}{z} = e^{k/m} = 2 + \frac{2a}{m} + \frac{a^2+2b}{m^2} + O\left(\frac{1}{m^3}\right) \quad \text{as } m \rightarrow \infty.$$

Substituting these formulas into ((15)) yields $a = -3 \ln(2) / 2$ and $b = (3 \ln(2) - 25/12) \ln(2)$. Putting these into ((16)) yields

$$\frac{k}{m} = \ln(2) \left(1 - \frac{3}{2m} - \frac{\frac{25}{12} - 3\ln(2)}{m^2} + O\left(\frac{1}{m^3}\right) \right) \quad \text{as } m \rightarrow \infty.$$

The term $O(1/m^{3})$ must be bounded [effectively](wikipedia://en/250237). To that end, we define the function

$$F(x,\lambda) = \left.\left( 1 - \frac{1}{t-1} + \frac{x\lambda}{2} \frac{t+t^2}{(t-1)^3} + \frac{x^2\lambda}{3} \frac{t+4t^2+t^3}{(t-1)^4} - \frac{x^2\lambda(\lambda-2x)}{8} \frac{t+11t^2+11t^3+t^4}{(t-1)^5} \right) \right\vert_{t=e^\lambda}.$$

The inequality ((15)) then takes the form

::: {#o25 .equation cue=numblk name="17"}

$$\left\vert F \left( \frac{1}{m} , \frac{k}{m} \right) \right\vert < \frac{110000}{m^3}, \tag{17}$$

:::

and we further have

$$\begin{aligned} F(x, \ln(2) (1 - \tfrac{3}{2}x \, \phantom{- \; 0.004x^2})) &> \phantom{-}0.005\phantom{15}x^2 - 100x^3 \quad \text{and} \\ F(x, \ln(2) (1 - \tfrac{3}{2}x - 0.004x^2)) &< -0.00015x^2 + 100x^3 \end{aligned}$$

for $x \le 0.01$. Therefore

$$\begin{aligned} F \left( \frac{1}{m} , \ln(2) \left( 1 - \frac{3}{2m} \, \phantom{ \; - \frac{0.004}{m^2} } \right) \right) &> \phantom{-}\frac{110000}{m^3} \quad \text{for } m > 2202 \cdot 10^4 \quad \text{and} \\ F \left( \frac{1}{m} , \ln(2) \left( 1 - \frac{3}{2m} - \frac{0.004}{m^2} \right) \right) &< - \frac{110000}{m^3} \quad \text{for } m > \phantom{0}734 \cdot 10^6. \end{aligned}$$

Comparing these with ((17)) then shows that, for $m > 10^{9}$, we have

$$\ln(2) \left( 1 - \frac{3}{2m} - \frac{0.004}{m^2} \right) < \frac{k}{m} < \ln(2) \left( 1 - \frac{3}{2m} \right),$$

and therefore

$$0 < \ln(2) - \frac{2k}{2m-3} < \frac{0.0111}{(2m-3)^2}.$$

Recalling that Moser showed[^o34] that indeed $m > 10^{9}$, and then invoking [Legendre's theorem on continued fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre's_theorem_on_continued_fractions), finally proves that $2k / (2m - 3)$ must be a [convergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_%28continued_fraction%29) to $\ln(2)$. Leveraging this result, 31 billion decimal digits of $\ln(2)$ can be used to exclude any nontrivial solutions below $10^{10^{9}}$.[^o29]
