# Area of a triangle

*Image: Triangle.GeometryArea - 2.svg.* A graphic derivation of the formula $T = b \times \tfrac{1}{2}h$ that avoids the usual procedure of doubling the area of the triangle and then halving it.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Area of a triangle"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), calculating the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is an elementary and widely encountered problem. The best known and simplest formula for the area of an arbitrary triangle is

$$\text{area} = \tfrac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height},$$

where "base" means the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of any side of the triangle, and "height" means the shortest distance between the line of the base and the corresponding *apex* (opposite corner of the triangle).

:::

Although simple, this formula is only useful if the height can be readily found, which is not always the case. For example, the [land surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_surveyor) of a triangular field might find it relatively easy to measure the length of each side, but relatively difficult to construct a 'height'. Various methods may be used in practice, depending on what is known about the triangle. Other frequently used formulas for the area of a triangle use [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), side lengths ([Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123)), vectors, [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates), [line integrals](wikipedia://en/21245414), [Pick's theorem](wikipedia://en/328252), or other properties.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Basic formula"}

## Basic formula {#Basic_formula}

*Image: Triangle.GeometryArea.svg.* The area of a triangle can be demonstrated, for example by means of the [congruence of triangles](wikipedia://en/39330#Congruence_of_triangles), as half of the area of a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) that has the same base length and height.

The *[area](wikipedia://en/1209)* of a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) [figure](wikipedia://en/169191) (two-dimensional shape) is a quantity which describes its size or extent. Conventionally, area is measured in units based on a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) whose side is one unit long (for instance, a [square meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_meter)). When using such units, the area of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) can be found by multiplying together the lengths of two adjacent sides, sometimes called the *base*, $b$, and *height*, $h$. For example, a rectangle which is 2 meters long and 5 meters tall has area 10 square meters.

A [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420), one with two sides (called the *legs*) that are [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) with a [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956) between them, is half of a rectangle with the two perpendicular sides as its base and height, [bisected](wikipedia://en/152547) along a diagonal. Thus the area $T$ of a right triangle can be found by [halving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halving) the area of the corresponding rectangle; that is, by multiplying the lengths of the two perpendicular sides and dividing the result by two: $T = b \times \tfrac12 h.$ Taking either leg of the right triangle to be the *base* of the triangle, the corresponding [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241) of the triangle is the other leg.

Any other triangle, choosing an arbitrary side to be its base, is likewise half of a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496), again bisected along a diagonal, which can then be [cut into pieces and rearranged](wikipedia://en/2915839) into a rectangle with the same base length and the same height; the triangle's area $T$ can therefore be calculated using the same formula, $T = \tfrac12 bh.$ For a general triangle, the *altitude* does not coincide with any of the sides, but is a new [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) perpendicular to the base with one of its endpoints at the *apex* (opposite corner) and the other endpoint on the [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line) containing the base. The length of this line segment is called the *height* or *altitude* of the triangle, and is the shortest distance between the apex and the line containing the base.

*Image: Lexell's theorem in the plane.png.* Orange triangles $\triangle ABC$ share a base $AB$ and area. The [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of their apex $C$ is a line (dashed green) parallel to the base.

One consequence of the triangle area formula is the analog in the plane of [Lexell's theorem](wikipedia://en/74122214) about spherical triangles: all of the triangles with a fixed base side, a fixed area, and the apex on the same side of the base line have their apex on a specific line [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to the base.[^o5]

:::

## Calculations from angles {#Calculations_from_angles}

*Image: Triangle.TrigArea.svg.* Applying trigonometry to find the altitude $h$.

The area $T$ of a triangle can be found through the application of [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261).

### Knowing SAS (side-angle-side) {#Knowing_SAS_(side-angle-side)}

Using the labels in the image on the right, the *height* or *[altitude](wikipedia://en/161241)* is $h = a \sin \gamma.$ Substituting this in the area formula $T = \tfrac12 bh$ derived above, the area of the triangle can be expressed as:

$$T = \tfrac12 ab\sin \gamma = \tfrac12 bc\sin \alpha = \tfrac12 ca\sin \beta$$

Where $a$ is the line $\overline{BC}$, $b$ is the line $\overline{AC}$, $c$ is the line $\overline{AB}$, $\alpha$ is the interior angle at $A$, $\beta$ is the interior angle at $B$, $\gamma$ is the interior angle at $C$.

Furthermore, since $\sin \alpha = \sin (\pi - \alpha) = \sin(\beta + \gamma),$ and similarly for the other two angles:

$$T = \tfrac12 ab\sin (\alpha+\beta) = \tfrac12 bc\sin (\beta+\gamma) = \tfrac12 ca\sin (\gamma+\alpha)$$

### Knowing AAS (angle-angle-side) {#Knowing_AAS_(angle-angle-side)}

Since $\cot \gamma = -\cot(\pi - \gamma) = -\cot(\alpha+\beta),$ and similarly for the other two angles:

$$T =\frac{b^{2}}{2(\cot\alpha-\cot(\alpha+\beta))}= \frac {b^{2}\sin \alpha\sin (\alpha + \beta)}{2\sin \beta},$$

and analogously if the known side is $a$ or $c$.

### Knowing ASA (angle-side-angle) {#Knowing_ASA_(angle-side-angle)}

$$T = \frac{a^{2}}{2(\cot \beta + \cot \gamma)} = \frac{a^{2}\sin \beta\sin \gamma}{2\sin(\beta + \gamma)},$$

and analogously if the known side is $b$ or $c$.

::: {#o12 .statement cue=named-heading name="Using side lengths (Heron's formula)"}

## Using side lengths (Heron's formula) {#Using_side_lengths_(Heron's_formula)}

A triangle's shape is uniquely determined by the lengths of the sides, so its metrical properties, including area, can be described in terms of those lengths. By [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123),

$$T = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$$

where $s= \tfrac12(a+b+c)$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617), or half of the triangle's perimeter.

Three other equivalent ways of writing Heron's formula are

$$\begin{aligned} T &= \tfrac14 \sqrt{(a^2+b^2+c^2)^2-2(a^4+b^4+c^4)} \\[5mu] &= \tfrac14 \sqrt{2(a^2b^2+a^2c^2+b^2c^2)-(a^4+b^4+c^4)} \\[5mu] &= \tfrac14 \sqrt{(a+b+c)(-a+b+c)(a-b+c)(a+b-c)}. \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o14 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formulas resembling Heron's formula"}

### Formulas resembling Heron's formula {#Formulas_resembling_Heron's_formula}

Three formulas have the same structure as Heron's formula but are expressed in terms of different variables. First, denoting the medians from sides $a$, $b$, and $c$ respectively as $m_{a}$, $m_{b}$, and $m_{c}$ and their semi-sum $\sigma = \tfrac12 (m_a + m_b + m_c),$ we have[^o15]

$$T = \tfrac43 \sqrt{\sigma (\sigma - m_a)(\sigma - m_b)(\sigma - m_c)}.$$

Next, denoting the altitudes from sides $a$, $b$, and $c$ respectively as $h_{a}$, $h_{b}$, and $h_{c}$, and denoting the semi-sum of the reciprocals of the altitudes as $H = \tfrac12 (h_a^{-1} + h_b^{-1} + h_c^{-1}),$ we have[^o16]

$$T^{-1} = 4 \sqrt{H(H-h_a^{-1})(H-h_b^{-1})(H-h_c^{-1})}.$$

And denoting the semi-sum of the angles' sines as $S = \tfrac12 (\sin \alpha + \sin \beta + \sin \gamma),$ we have[^o17]

$$T = D^{2} \sqrt{S(S-\sin \alpha)(S-\sin \beta)(S-\sin \gamma)}$$

where $D$ is the diameter of the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432): $D=\tfrac{a}{\sin \alpha} = \tfrac{b}{\sin \beta} = \tfrac{c}{\sin \gamma}.$

:::

:::

## Using vectors {#Using_vectors}

The area of triangle $\triangle ABC$ is half of the area of a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496):

$$T = \tfrac12\bigl\|(\mathbf b - \mathbf a) \wedge (\mathbf c - \mathbf a)\bigr\| = \tfrac12\bigl\|\mathbf a \wedge \mathbf b + \mathbf b \wedge \mathbf c + \mathbf c \wedge \mathbf a \bigr\|,$$

where $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{c}$ are [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) to the triangle's vertices from any arbitrary origin point, so that $\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{a}$ are the [translation vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_vector) from vertex $A$ to each of the others, and $\land$ is the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product). If vertex $A$ is taken to be the origin, this simplifies to $\tfrac12\| \mathbf b \wedge \mathbf c \|$.

The oriented relative [area of a parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496#Area) in any [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), a type of [bivector](wikipedia://en/884040), is defined as $\mathbf{u} \land \mathbf{v}$ where $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are translation vectors from one vertex of the parallelogram to each of the two adjacent vertices. In [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the magnitude of this bivector is a well-defined scalar number representing the area of the parallelogram. (For vectors in three-dimensional space, the bivector-valued wedge product has the same magnitude as the vector-valued [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092), but unlike the cross product, which is only defined in three-dimensional Euclidean space, the wedge product is well-defined in an affine space of any dimension.)

The area of triangle $\triangle ABC$ can also be expressed in terms of [dot products](wikipedia://en/157093). Taking vertex $A$ to be the origin and calling translation vectors to the other vertices $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$,

$$T = \tfrac12 \sqrt{\mathbf{b}^2 \mathbf{c}^2 - (\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{c})^2},$$

where for any Euclidean vector $\mathbf v^2 = \|\mathbf v\|^2 = \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v$.[^o19] This area formula can be derived from the previous one using the elementary vector identity $\mathbf u^2 \mathbf v^2 = (\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v)^2 + \|\mathbf u \wedge \mathbf v \|^2$.

In two-dimensional Euclidean space, for a vector $\mathbf{b}$ with coordinates $(x_{B}, y_{B})$ and vector $\mathbf{c}$ with coordinates $(x_{C}, y_{C})$, the magnitude of the wedge product is

$$\| \mathbf b \wedge \mathbf c \| = |x_B y_C - x_C y_B|.$$

(See the following section.)

## Using coordinates {#Using_coordinates}

If vertex $A$ is located at the origin $(0, 0)$ of a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) and the coordinates of the other two vertices are given by $B = (x_{B}, y_{B})$ and $C = (x_{C}, y_{C})$, then the area can be computed as $\tfrac{1}{2}$ times the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468)

$$T = \tfrac12\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}x_B & x_C \\ y_B & y_C \end{pmatrix}\right| = \tfrac12 |x_B y_C - x_C y_B|.$$

For three general vertices, the equation is:

$$T = \tfrac12 \left| \det\begin{pmatrix}x_A & x_B & x_C \\ y_A & y_B & y_C \\ 1 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} \right| = \tfrac12 \big| x_A y_B - x_A y_C + x_B y_C - x_B y_A + x_C y_A - x_C y_B \big|,$$

which can be written as

$$T = \tfrac12 \big| (x_A - x_C) (y_B - y_A) - (x_A - x_B) (y_C - y_A) \big|.$$

If the points are labeled sequentially in the counterclockwise direction, the above determinant expressions are positive and the absolute value signs can be omitted.[^o21] The above formula is known as the [shoelace formula](wikipedia://en/17863612) or the surveyor's formula.

If we locate the vertices in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) and denote them in counterclockwise sequence as $a = x_{A} + y_{A}i$, $b = x_{B} + y_{B}i$, and $c = x_{C} + y_{C}i$, and denote their complex conjugates as $\overline{a}$, $\overline{b}$, and $\overline{c}$, then the formula

$$T=\frac{i}{4}\begin{vmatrix}a & \bar a & 1 \\ b & \bar b & 1 \\ c & \bar c & 1 \end{vmatrix}$$

is equivalent to the shoelace formula.

In three dimensions, the area of a general triangle $A = (x_{A}, y_{A}, z_{A})$, $B = (x_{B}, y_{B}, z_{B})$ and $C = (x_{C}, y_{C}, z_{C}$) is the [Pythagorean sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_sum) of the areas of the respective projections on the three principal planes (i.e. $x = 0$, $y = 0$ and $z = 0$):

$$T = \tfrac12 \sqrt{\begin{vmatrix} x_A & x_B & x_C \\ y_A & y_B & y_C \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix}^2 + \begin{vmatrix} y_A & y_B & y_C \\ z_A & z_B & z_C \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix}^2 + \begin{vmatrix} z_A & z_B & z_C \\ x_A & x_B & x_C \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix}^2 }.$$

## Using line integrals {#Using_line_integrals}

The area within any closed curve, such as a triangle, is given by the [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) around the curve of the algebraic or signed distance of a point on the curve from an arbitrary oriented straight line $L$. Points to the right of $L$ as oriented are taken to be at negative distance from $L$, while the weight for the integral is taken to be the component of [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) parallel to $L$ rather than arc length itself.

This method is well suited to computation of the area of an arbitrary [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621). Taking $L$ to be the $x$-axis, the line integral between consecutive vertices $(x_{i}, y_{i})$ and $(x_{i+1}, y_{i+1})$ is given by the base times the mean height, namely

$$\tfrac12 (x_{i+1} - x_i)(y_i + y_{i+1}).$$

The sign of the area is an overall indicator of the direction of traversal, with negative area indicating counterclockwise traversal. The area of a triangle then falls out as the case of a polygon with three sides.

While the line integral method has in common with other coordinate-based methods the arbitrary choice of a coordinate system, unlike the others it makes no arbitrary choice of vertex of the triangle as origin or of side as base. Furthermore, the choice of coordinate system defined by $L$ commits to only two degrees of freedom rather than the usual three, since the weight is a local distance (e.g. $x_{i+1} - x_{i}$ in the above) whence the method does not require choosing an axis normal to $L$.

When working in [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) it is not necessary to convert to [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) to use line integration, since the line integral between consecutive vertices $(r_{i}, \theta_{i})$ and $(r_{i+1}, \theta_{i+1})$ of a polygon is given directly by

$$\tfrac12 \, r_i r_{i+1} \sin(\theta_{i+1} - \theta_i).$$

This is valid for all values of $\theta$, with some decrease in numerical accuracy when $\lvert \theta\rvert$ is many orders of magnitude greater than $\pi$. With this formulation negative area indicates clockwise traversal, which should be kept in mind when mixing polar and cartesian coordinates. Just as the choice of $y$-axis ($x = 0$) is immaterial for line integration in cartesian coordinates, so is the choice of zero heading ($\theta = 0$) immaterial here.

## Using Pick's theorem {#Using_Pick's_theorem}

See [Pick's theorem](wikipedia://en/328252) for a technique for finding the area of any arbitrary [lattice polygon](wikipedia://en/19635706) (one drawn on a grid with vertically and horizontally adjacent lattice points at equal distances, and with vertices on lattice points).

The theorem states:

$$T = I + \tfrac12 B - 1$$

where $I$ is the number of internal lattice points and $B$ is the number of lattice points lying on the border of the polygon.

:::

## Other area formulas {#Other_area_formulas}

*Image: visual\_proof\_triangle\_area\_is\_inradius\_times\_semiperimeter.svg.* [Proof without words](wikipedia://en/18045517) that the area of a triangle equals the product of its inradius and its semiperimeter

Numerous other area formulas exist, such as

$$T = rs,$$

where $r$ is the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius), and $s$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) (in fact, this formula holds for *all* [tangential polygons](wikipedia://en/35520654)), and[^o27]

$$T=r_a(s-a)=r_b(s-b)=r_c(s-c)$$

where $r_{a}$, $r_{b}$, $r_{c}$ are the radii of the [excircles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircles) tangent to sides $a$, $b$, $c$ respectively.

We also have

$$T = \tfrac12 D^{2}(\sin \alpha)(\sin \beta)(\sin \gamma)$$

and[^o28]

$$T = \frac{abc}{2D} = \frac{abc}{4R}$$

for circumdiameter $D$; and[^o29]

$$T = \tfrac14(\tan \alpha)(b^{2}+c^{2}-a^{2})$$

for angle $\alpha \ne 90^{\circ}$.

The area can also be expressed as[^o30]

$$T = \sqrt{rr_ar_br_c}.$$

In 1885, Baker[^o31] gave a collection of over a hundred distinct area formulas for the triangle. These include:

$$\begin{aligned} T &= \tfrac12\sqrt[3]{abch_ah_bh_c}, \\[2pt] &= \tfrac12 \sqrt{abh_ah_b}, \\[2pt] &= \frac{a+b}{2(h_a^{-1} + h_b^{-1})}, \\[2pt] &= \frac{Rh_bh_c}{a} \end{aligned}$$

for circumradius (radius of the circumcircle) $R$, and

$$T = \frac{h_ah_b}{2 \sin \gamma}.$$

:::

## Upper bound on the area {#Upper_bound_on_the_area}

The area $T$ of any triangle with perimeter $p$ satisfies

$$T\le \tfrac{p^2}{12\sqrt{3}},$$

with equality holding if and only if the triangle is equilateral.[^o33][^o34]

Other upper bounds on the area $T$ are given by[^o35]

$$4\sqrt{3}T \leq a^2+b^2+c^2$$

and

$$4\sqrt{3}T \leq \frac{9abc}{a+b+c},$$

both with equality holding if and only if the triangle is equilateral.

## History {#History}

In 300 BCE Greek mathematician [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) proved that the area of a triangle is half that of a parallelogram with the same base and height in his book *Elements of Geometry*.[^o37] [Heron of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron_of_Alexandria) found what is known as [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123) for the area of a triangle in terms of its sides, and a proof can be found in his book, *Metrica*, written around 60 CE. It has been suggested that [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) knew the formula over two centuries earlier,[^o38] and since *Metrica* is a collection of the mathematical knowledge available in the ancient world, it is possible that the formula predates the reference given in that work.

In 499 CE [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204), a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902)-[astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) from the classical age of [Indian mathematics](wikipedia://en/1848052) and [Indian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy), expressed the area of a triangle as one-half the base times the height in the *[Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya)*.[^o40]

A formula equivalent to Heron's was discovered by the Chinese independently of the Greeks. It was published in 1247 in *Shushu Jiuzhang* ("[Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Treatise_in_Nine_Sections)"), written by [Qin Jiushao](wikipedia://en/612837).[^o41]

## Bisecting the area {#Bisecting_the_area}

There are infinitely many [lines that bisect the area of a triangle](wikipedia://en/152547#Area_bisectors_and_perimeter_bisectors).[^o43] Three of them are the medians, which are the only area bisectors that go through the centroid. Three other area bisectors are parallel to the triangle's sides.

Any line through a triangle that splits both the triangle's area and its perimeter in half goes through the triangle's incenter. There can be one, two, or three of these for any given triangle.
