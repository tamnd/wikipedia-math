# PPAD (complexity)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="PPAD"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), **PPAD** ("Polynomial Parity Arguments on Directed graphs") is a [complexity class](wikipedia://en/502426) introduced by [Christos Papadimitriou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christos_Papadimitriou) in 1994. PPAD is a subclass of [TFNP](wikipedia://en/4668618) based on functions that can be shown to be total by a parity argument.[^o2][^o3] The class attracted significant attention in the field of [algorithmic game theory](wikipedia://en/16334749) because it contains the problem of computing a [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337): this problem was shown to be [complete](wikipedia://en/1176530) for PPAD by Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou with at least 3 players and later extended by Chen and Deng to 2 players.[^o4][^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

PPAD is a subset of the class [TFNP](wikipedia://en/4668618), the class of [function problems](wikipedia://en/663345) in [FNP](wikipedia://en/663351) that are guaranteed to be [total](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_function). The [TFNP](wikipedia://en/4668618) formal definition is given as follows:

A binary relation P(*x*,*y*) is in TFNP if and only if there is a deterministic polynomial time algorithm that can determine whether P(*x*,*y*) holds given both *x* and *y*, and for every *x*, there exists a *y* such that P(*x*,*y*) holds.

Subclasses of TFNP are defined based on the type of mathematical proof used to prove that a solution always exists. Informally, PPAD is the subclass of TFNP where the guarantee that there exists a *y* such that P(*x*,*y*) holds is based on a parity argument on a directed graph. The class is formally defined by specifying one of its complete problems, known as *End-Of-The-Line*:

G is a (possibly exponentially large) directed graph with every [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) having at most one predecessor and at most one successor. G is specified by giving a polynomial-time computable function f(*v*) (polynomial in the size of *v*) that returns the predecessor and successor (if they exist) of the vertex *v*. Given a vertex *s* in G with no predecessor, find a vertex *t≠s* with no predecessor or no successor. (The input to the problem is the source vertex *s* and the function f(*v*)). In other words, we want any source or sink of the directed graph other than *s*.

Such a *t* must exist if an *s* does, because the structure of G means that vertices with only one neighbour come in pairs. In particular, given *s*, we can find such a *t* at the other end of the string starting at *s*. (Note that this may take exponential time if we just evaluate f repeatedly.)

:::

## Proving membership in PPAD {#Proving_membership_in_PPAD}

In many cases, when a problem is said to "be in PPAD", it often means that finding an *approximate* solution to the problem is in PPAD. This is often necessary, as in many cases, the solutions might involve [irrational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_numbers), and thus cannot be output in finite time.[^o9]

However, there are cases in which solutions with rational numbers are guaranteed to exist. For such cases, Filos-Ratsikas, Hansen, Høgh and Hollender[^o9] present a general method for proving that computing an *exact* solution belongs to PPAD.

## Relations to other complexity classes {#Relations_to_other_complexity_classes}

PPAD is contained in (but not known to be equal to) [PPA](wikipedia://en/25494843) (the corresponding class of parity arguments for *undirected* graphs) which is contained in TFNP. PPAD is also contained in (but not known to be equal to) [PPP](wikipedia://en/25395828), another subclass of TFNP. It contains [CLS](http://people.csail.mit.edu/costis/CLS.pdf).[^o11]

PPAD is a class of problems that are believed to be hard, but obtaining PPAD-completeness is a weaker evidence of intractability than that of obtaining [NP-completeness](wikipedia://en/23385892). PPAD problems cannot be NP-complete, for the technical reason that NP is a class of decision problems, but the answer of PPAD problems is always yes, as a solution is known to exist, even though it might be hard to find that solution.[^o12] However, PPAD and NP are closely related. While the question whether a Nash equilibrium exists for a given game cannot be NP-hard because the answer is always yes, the question whether a *second* equilibrium exists is NP complete.[^o13] Examples of PPAD-complete problems include finding [Nash equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibria), [computing fixed points](wikipedia://en/73536266) in [Brouwer](wikipedia://en/4101) functions, and finding [Arrow-Debreu equilibria](wikipedia://en/1239556) in markets.[^o20]

Fearnley, Goldberg, Hollender and Savani[^o14] proved that a complexity class called [CLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CLS_%28complexity%29) is equal to the intersection of PPAD and [PLS](wikipedia://en/4669257).

Etessami and Yannakakis (who invented the related class [FIXP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIXP))[^o15] write that "The [piecewise-linear](wikipedia://en/444224) fragment of FIXP equals PPAD". In other words,[^o16] the problems in PPAD are the problems in FIXP in which the input function is piecewise-linear.

## Other notable complete problems {#Other_notable_complete_problems}

- Finding the [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337) on a 2-player game[^o4] or the [Epsilon-equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsilon-equilibrium) on a game with any number of players.[^o20]
- Finding a three-colored point in [Sperner's Lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperner's_Lemma).[^o21]
- Finding an [envy-free cake-cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_cake-cutting) when the utility functions are given by polynomial-time algorithms.[^o22]
