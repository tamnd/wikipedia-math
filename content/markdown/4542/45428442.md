# Bernstein–Kushnirenko theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bernstein–Kushnirenko theorem"}

The **Bernstein–Kushnirenko theorem**, also called **Bernstein–Khovanskii–Kushnirenko theorem** (**BKK theorem**),[^o2] states that the number of nonzero [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) solutions of a system of [Laurent polynomial](wikipedia://en/61351) equations $f_1= \cdots = f_n=0$ is equal to the [mixed volume](wikipedia://en/32953045) of the [Newton polytopes](wikipedia://en/31041066) of such polynomials, assuming that all nonzero coefficients of $f_n$ are generic.

:::

*Image: David N. Bernstein.jpg.* David N. Bernstein, circa 1975

It was [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) by [David Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bernstein_%28mathematician%29)[^o3] and Anatoliy Kushnirenko[^o4] in 1975. [Askold Khovanskii](wikipedia://en/43849047) has found about 15 different proofs of this theorem.[^o5]

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $A$ be a finite subset of $\mathbb{Z}^n.$ Consider the [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) $L_A$ of the Laurent polynomial [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) $\mathbb{C} \!\left[ x_1^{\pm 1}, \ldots, x_n^{\pm 1} \right]$ consisting of Laurent polynomials whose exponents are in $A$. That is:

$$L_A = \biggl\{ f \mathrel{\bigg|} f(x) = \sum_{\alpha \in A} c_\alpha x^\alpha, c_\alpha \in \mathbb{C} \biggr\},$$

where for each $\alpha = (a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ we have used the shorthand notation $x^\alpha$ to denote the [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) $x_1^{a_1} \cdots x_n^{a_n}.$

Now take $n$ finite subsets $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, with the corresponding subspaces of Laurent polynomials, $L_{A_1}, \ldots, L_{A_n}.$ Consider a generic system of equations from these subspaces, that is:

$$f_1(x) = \cdots = f_n(x) = 0,$$

where each $f_i$ is a generic element in the ([finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) vector space) $L_{A_i}.$

The Bernstein–Kushnirenko theorem states that the number of solutions $x \in (\mathbb{C} \setminus 0)^n$ of such a system is equal to

$$n! V(\Delta_1, \ldots, \Delta_n),$$

where $V$ denotes the [Minkowski](wikipedia://en/504105) [mixed volume](wikipedia://en/32953045) and for each $i, \Delta_i$ is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of the finite set of points $A_i$. Clearly, $\Delta_i$ is a [convex lattice polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_lattice_polytope); it can be interpreted as the [Newton polytope](wikipedia://en/31041066) of a generic element of the subspace $L_{A_i}$.

In particular, if all the sets $A_i$ are the same, $A = A_1 = \cdots = A_n,$ then the number of solutions of a generic system of Laurent polynomials from $L_A$ is equal to

$$n! \operatorname{vol} \Delta,$$

where $\Delta$ is the convex hull of $A$ and $\operatorname{vol}$ is the usual $n$-dimensional Euclidean [volume](wikipedia://en/32498). Note that even though the volume of a lattice polytope is not necessarily an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), it becomes an integer after multiplying by $n!$.
