# Automorphism group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Automorphism group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **automorphism group** of an object *X* is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) consisting of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of *X* under [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891). For example, if *X* is a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), then the automorphism group of *X* is the group of invertible [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformations) from *X* to itself (the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) of *X*). If instead *X* is a group, then its automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(X)$ is the group consisting of all [group automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_automorphism) of *X*.

:::

Especially in geometric contexts, an automorphism group is also called a [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186). A subgroup of an automorphism group is sometimes called a **transformation group**.

Automorphism groups are studied in a general way in the field of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

If *X* is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) with no additional structure, then any bijection from *X* to itself is an automorphism, and hence the automorphism group of *X* in this case is precisely the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of *X*. If the set *X* has additional structure, then it may be the case that not all bijections on the set preserve this structure, in which case the automorphism group will be a subgroup of the symmetric group on *X*. Some examples of this include the following:

- The automorphism group of a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $L/K$ is the group consisting of field automorphisms of *L* that [fix](wikipedia://en/47933712) *K*. If the field extension is [Galois](wikipedia://en/457064), the automorphism group is called the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of the field extension.
- The automorphism group of the [projective *n*-space](wikipedia://en/242135) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k* is the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) $\operatorname{PGL}_n(k).$[^o4]
- The automorphism group $G$ of a finite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) *n* is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, the [multiplicative group of integers modulo *n*](wikipedia://en/1195234), with the isomorphism given by $\overline{a} \mapsto \sigma_a \in G, \, \sigma_a(x) = x^a$.[^o5] In particular, $G$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974).
- The automorphism group of a finite-dimensional real [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{g}$ has the structure of a (real) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) (in fact, it is even a [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885): see below). If *G* is a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, then the automorphism group of *G* has a structure of a Lie group induced from that on the automorphism group of $\mathfrak{g}$.[^o6][^n1]

If *G* is a group [acting](wikipedia://en/12781) on a set *X*, the action amounts to a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from *G* to the automorphism group of *X* and conversely. Indeed, each left *G*-action on a set *X* determines $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(X), \, g \mapsto \sigma_g, \, \sigma_g(x) = g \cdot x$, and, conversely, each homomorphism $\varphi: G \to \operatorname{Aut}(X)$ defines an action by $g \cdot x = \varphi(g)x$. This extends to the case when the set *X* has more structure than just a set. For example, if *X* is a vector space, then a group action of *G* on *X* is a *[group representation](wikipedia://en/12695)* of the group *G*, representing *G* as a group of linear transformations (automorphisms) of *X*; these representations are the main object of study in the field of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200).

Here are some other facts about automorphism groups:

- Let $A, B$ be two finite sets of the same [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) and $\operatorname{Iso}(A, B)$ the set of all [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942) $A \mathrel{\overset{\sim}\to} B$. Then $\operatorname{Aut}(B)$, which is a symmetric group (see above), acts on $\operatorname{Iso}(A, B)$ from the left [freely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) and [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Remarkable_properties_of_actions); that is to say, $\operatorname{Iso}(A, B)$ is a [torsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsor) for $\operatorname{Aut}(B)$ (cf. #In category theory).
- Let *P* be a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) [projective module](wikipedia://en/364488) over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R*. Then there is an [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) $\operatorname{Aut}(P) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{GL}_n(R)$, unique up to [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839).[^o8]

:::

## In category theory {#In_category_theory}

Automorphism groups appear very naturally in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869).

If *X* is an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) in a category, then the automorphism group of *X* is the group consisting of all the invertible [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) from *X* to itself. It is the [unit group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) of the [endomorphism monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endomorphism_monoid) of *X*. (For some examples, see [PROP](wikipedia://en/5178835).)

If $A, B$ are objects in some category, then the set $\operatorname{Iso}(A, B)$ of all $A \mathrel{\overset{\sim}\to} B$ is a left $\operatorname{Aut}(B)$-[torsor](wikipedia://en/363441). In practical terms, this says that a different choice of a base point of $\operatorname{Iso}(A, B)$ differs unambiguously by an element of $\operatorname{Aut}(B)$, or that each choice of a base point is precisely a choice of a trivialization of the torsor.

If $X_1$ and $X_2$ are objects in categories $C_1$ and $C_2$, and if $F: C_1 \to C_2$ is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) mapping $X_1$ to $X_2$, then $F$ induces a group homomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(X_1) \to \operatorname{Aut}(X_2)$, as it maps invertible morphisms to invertible morphisms.

In particular, if *G* is a group viewed as a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) with a single object \* or, more generally, if *G* is a [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543), then each functor $F: G \to C$, *C* a category, is called an action or a representation of *G* on the object $F(*)$, or the objects $F(\operatorname{Obj}(G))$. Those objects are then said to be $G$-objects (as they are acted by $G$); cf. [$\mathbb{S}$-object](wikipedia://en/59530924). If $C$ is a module category like the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces, then $G$-objects are also called $G$-modules.

## Automorphism group functor {#Automorphism_group_functor}

Let $M$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field *k* that is equipped with some algebraic structure (that is, *M* is a finite-dimensional [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) over *k*). It can be, for example, an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) or a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944).

Now, consider *k*-[linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102) $M \to M$ that preserve the algebraic structure: they form a [vector subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace) $\operatorname{End}_{\text{alg}}(M)$ of $\operatorname{End}(M)$. The unit group of $\operatorname{End}_{\text{alg}}(M)$ is the automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(M)$. When a basis on *M* is chosen, $\operatorname{End}(M)$ is the space of [square matrices](wikipedia://en/166022) and $\operatorname{End}_{\text{alg}}(M)$ is the zero set of some [polynomial equations](wikipedia://en/23000), and the invertibility is again described by polynomials. Hence, $\operatorname{Aut}(M)$ is a [linear algebraic group](wikipedia://en/395885) over *k*.

Now base extensions applied to the above discussion determines a functor:[^o12] namely, for each [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* over *k*, consider the *R*-linear maps $M \otimes R \to M \otimes R$ preserving the algebraic structure: denote it by $\operatorname{End}_{\text{alg}}(M \otimes R)$. Then the unit group of the matrix ring $\operatorname{End}_{\text{alg}}(M \otimes R)$ over *R* is the automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(M \otimes R)$ and $R \mapsto \operatorname{Aut}(M \otimes R)$ is a [group functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_functor): a functor from the [category of commutative rings](wikipedia://en/15300181#Category_of_commutative_rings) over *k* to the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092). Even better, it is represented by a scheme (since the automorphism groups are defined by polynomials): this scheme is called the **automorphism group scheme** and is denoted by $\operatorname{Aut}(M)$.

In general, however, an automorphism group functor may not be represented by a scheme.
