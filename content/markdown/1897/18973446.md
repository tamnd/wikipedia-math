# Geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geometry"}

**Geometry**[^o2] is a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) concerned with properties of space such as the distance, shape, size, and relative position of figures.[^o3] Geometry is, along with [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), one of the oldest branches of mathematics. A mathematician who works in the field of geometry is called a *[geometer](wikipedia://en/161339)*. Until the 19th century, geometry was almost exclusively devoted to [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), which includes the notions of [point](wikipedia://en/593693), [line](wikipedia://en/946975), [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), [distance](wikipedia://en/39378), [angle](wikipedia://en/1196), [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970), and [curve](wikipedia://en/89246), as fundamental concepts.[^o4]

:::

Originally developed to model the physical world, geometry has applications in almost all sciences, and also in art, [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), and other activities that are related to graphics.[^o5] Geometry also has applications in areas of mathematics that are apparently unrelated. For example, methods of algebraic geometry are fundamental in [Wiles's proof](wikipedia://en/21950759) of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953), a problem that was stated in terms of [elementary arithmetic](wikipedia://en/1567386), and remained unsolved for several centuries.

During the 19th century, several discoveries enlarged dramatically the scope of geometry. One of the oldest such discoveries is [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125)'s [Theorema Egregium](wikipedia://en/259906) ("remarkable theorem") that asserts roughly that the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) of a surface is independent from any specific [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). This implies that surfaces can be studied *intrinsically*, that is, as stand-alone spaces, and has been expanded into the theory of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) and [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243). Later in the 19th century, it appeared that geometries without the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) ([non-Euclidean geometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometries)) can be developed without introducing any contradiction. The geometry that underlies [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) is a famous application of non-Euclidean geometry.

Since the late 19th century, the scope of geometry has been greatly expanded, and the field has been split in many subfields that depend on the underlying methods—[differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468) (also known as *combinatorial geometry*), etc.—or on the properties of Euclidean spaces that are disregarded—[projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) that consider only alignment of points but not distance and parallelism, [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890) that omits the concept of angle and distance, [finite geometry](wikipedia://en/267061) that omits [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28mathematics%29), and others. This enlargement of the scope of geometry led to a change of meaning of the word "space", which originally referred to the three-dimensional [space](wikipedia://en/27667) of the physical world and its [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model) provided by Euclidean geometry; presently a **geometric space**, or simply a *space* is a [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181) on which some geometry is defined.

## History {#History}

*Image: Westerner and Arab practicing geometry 15th century manuscript.jpg.* A [European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_in_Europe) and an [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) practicing geometry in the 15th century

The earliest recorded beginnings of geometry can be traced to ancient [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia) and [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) in the 2nd millennium BC.[^o7][^o8] Early geometry was a collection of empirically discovered principles concerning lengths, angles, areas, and volumes, which were developed to meet some practical need in [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), [construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), and various crafts. The earliest known texts on geometry are the [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_mathematics) [Rhind Papyrus](wikipedia://en/9210114) (2000–1800 BC) and [Moscow Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Mathematical_Papyrus) (c. 1890 BC), and the [Babylonian clay tablets](wikipedia://en/3861353), such as [Plimpton 322](wikipedia://en/2983547) (1900 BC). For example, the Moscow Papyrus gives a formula for calculating the volume of a truncated pyramid, or [frustum](wikipedia://en/89405).[^o9] Later clay tablets (350–50 BC) demonstrate that Babylonian astronomers implemented [trapezoid](wikipedia://en/201826) procedures for computing Jupiter's position and [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_%28vector%29) within time-velocity space. These geometric procedures anticipated the [Oxford Calculators](wikipedia://en/4555383), including the [mean speed theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_speed_theorem), by 14 centuries.[^o10] South of Egypt the [ancient Nubians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nubia) established a system of geometry including early versions of sun clocks.[^o11][^o12]

In the 7th century BC, the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) mathematician [Thales of Miletus](wikipedia://en/30072) used geometry to solve problems such as calculating the height of pyramids and the distance of ships from the shore. He is credited with the first use of deductive reasoning applied to geometry, by deriving four corollaries to [Thales's theorem](wikipedia://en/267471).[^o13] [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) established the [Pythagorean School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans), which is credited with the first proof of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034),[^o14] though the statement of the theorem has a long history.[^o15][^o16] [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388) (408–c. 355 BC) developed the [method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284), which allowed the calculation of areas and volumes of curvilinear figures,[^o17] as well as a theory of ratios that avoided the problem of [incommensurable magnitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incommensurable_magnitudes), which enabled subsequent geometers to make significant advances. Around 300 BC, geometry was revolutionized by Euclid, whose *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, widely considered the most successful and influential textbook of all time,[^o18] introduced [mathematical rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor) through the [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method) and is the earliest example of the format still used in mathematics today, that of definition, axiom, theorem, and proof. Although most of the contents of the *Elements* were already known, Euclid arranged them into a single, coherent logical framework.[^o19] The *Elements* was known to all educated people in the West until the middle of the 20th century and its contents are still taught in geometry classes today.[^o20] [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) (c. 287–212 BC) of [Syracuse, Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse,_Italy) used the method of exhaustion to calculate the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) under the arc of a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) with the [summation of an infinite series](wikipedia://en/15287), and gave remarkably accurate approximations of [pi](wikipedia://en/23601).[^o21] He also studied the [spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes_spiral) bearing his name and obtained formulas for the [volumes](wikipedia://en/32498) of [surfaces of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549).

*Image: Woman teaching geometry.jpg.* *Woman teaching geometry*. Illustration at the beginning of a medieval translation of [Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107), c. 1310.

[Indian](wikipedia://en/1848052) mathematicians also made many important contributions in geometry. The *[Shatapatha Brahmana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shatapatha_Brahmana)* (3rd century BC) contains rules for ritual geometric constructions that are similar to the *[Sulba Sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulba_Sutras)*.[^o22] According to (Hayashi 2005, 363), the *Śulba Sūtras* contain "the earliest extant verbal expression of the Pythagorean Theorem in the world, although it had already been known to the Old Babylonians. They contain lists of [Pythagorean triples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triples), which are particular cases of [Diophantine equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_equations).[^o24] In the [Bakhshali manuscript](wikipedia://en/4305817), there are a handful of geometric problems (including problems about volumes of irregular solids). The Bakhshali manuscript also "employs a decimal place value system with a dot for zero."[^o25] [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204)'s *[Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya)* (499) includes the computation of areas and volumes. [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) wrote his astronomical work *[Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasphutasiddhanta)* in 628. Chapter 12, containing 66 [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) verses, was divided into two sections: "basic operations" (including cube roots, fractions, ratio and proportion, and barter) and "practical mathematics" (including mixture, mathematical series, plane figures, stacking bricks, sawing of timber, and piling of grain).[^o26] In the latter section, he stated his famous theorem on the diagonals of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131). Chapter 12 also included a formula for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral (a generalization of [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123)), as well as a complete description of [rational triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_triangle) (*i.e.* triangles with rational sides and rational areas).[^o26]

In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), [mathematics in medieval Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam) contributed to the development of geometry, especially [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997).[^o27][^o28] [Al-Mahani](wikipedia://en/1782729) (b. 853) conceived the idea of reducing geometrical problems such as duplicating the cube to problems in algebra. [Thābit ibn Qurra](wikipedia://en/207547) (known as Thebit in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin)) (836–901) dealt with [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) operations applied to [ratios](wikipedia://en/87837) of geometrical quantities, and contributed to the development of [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202). [Omar Khayyam](wikipedia://en/92550) (1048–1131) found geometric solutions to [cubic equations](wikipedia://en/180787). The theorems of [Ibn al-Haytham](wikipedia://en/1645) (Alhazen), Omar Khayyam and [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](wikipedia://en/33642424) on [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278), including the [Lambert quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3515519) and [Saccheri quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3818958), were part of a line of research on the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) continued by later European geometers, including [Vitello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitello) (c. 1230), [Gersonides](wikipedia://en/61626) (1288–1344), Alfonso, [John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290), and [Giovanni Girolamo Saccheri](wikipedia://en/30875270), that by the 19th century led to the discovery of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).[^o32]

In the early 17th century, there were two important developments in geometry. The first was the creation of analytic geometry, or geometry with [coordinates](wikipedia://en/81931) and [equations](wikipedia://en/9284), by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) (1596–1650) and [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) (1601–1665).[^o33] This was a necessary precursor to the development of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and a precise quantitative science of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939).[^o34] The second geometric development of this period was the systematic study of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) by [Girard Desargues](wikipedia://en/358484) (1591–1661).[^o35] Projective geometry studies properties of shapes which are unchanged under [projections](wikipedia://en/519182) and [sections](wikipedia://en/367621), especially as they relate to [artistic perspective](wikipedia://en/380405).[^o36]

Two developments in geometry in the 19th century changed the way it had been studied previously.[^o37] These were the discovery of [non-Euclidean geometries](wikipedia://en/58610) by Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky, János Bolyai and Carl Friedrich Gauss and of the formulation of [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) as the central consideration in the [Erlangen programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen_programme) of [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) (which generalized the Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries). Two of the master geometers of the time were [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) (1826–1866), working primarily with tools from [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), and introducing the [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181), and [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), the founder of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and the geometric theory of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087). As a consequence of these major changes in the conception of geometry, the concept of "[space](wikipedia://en/5308894)" became something rich and varied, and the natural background for theories as different as [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) and [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586).[^o38]

## Main concepts {#Main_concepts}

The following are some of the most important concepts in geometry.[^o4][^o40]

::: {#o42 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Axioms"}

### Axioms {#Axioms}

*Image: Parallel postulate en.svg.* An illustration of Euclid's [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493)

[Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) took an abstract approach to geometry in his [Elements](wikipedia://en/244107),[^o43] one of the most influential books ever written.[^o44] Euclid introduced certain [axioms](wikipedia://en/928), or [postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postulate), expressing primary or self-evident properties of points, lines, and planes.[^o45] He proceeded to rigorously deduce other properties by mathematical reasoning. The characteristic feature of Euclid's approach to geometry was its rigor, and it has come to be known as *axiomatic* or *[synthetic](wikipedia://en/267484)* geometry.[^o46] At the start of the 19th century, the discovery of [non-Euclidean geometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometries) by [Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Ivanovich_Lobachevsky) (1792–1856), [János Bolyai](wikipedia://en/98912) (1802–1860), [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) (1777–1855) and others[^o47] led to a revival of interest in this discipline, and in the 20th century, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) (1862–1943) employed axiomatic reasoning in an attempt to provide a modern foundation of geometry.[^o48]

:::

### Spaces and subspaces {#Spaces_and_subspaces}

#### Points {#Points}

Points are generally considered fundamental objects for building geometry. They may be defined by the properties that they must have, as in Euclid's definition as "that which has no part",[^o52] or in [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484). In modern mathematics, they are generally defined as [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28set_theory%29) of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) called [space](wikipedia://en/5308894), which is itself [axiomatically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatically) defined.

With these modern definitions, every geometric shape is defined as a set of points; this is not the case in synthetic geometry, where a line is another fundamental object that is not viewed as the set of the points through which it passes.

However, there are modern geometries in which points are not primitive objects, or even without points.[^o53][^o54] One of the oldest such geometries is [Whitehead's point-free geometry](wikipedia://en/6290771), formulated by [Alfred North Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) in 1919–1920.

#### Lines {#Lines}

[Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) described a line as "breadthless length" which "lies equally with respect to the points on itself".[^o52] In modern mathematics, given the multitude of geometries, the concept of a line is closely tied to the way the geometry is described. For instance, in [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), a line in the plane is often defined as the set of points whose coordinates satisfy a given [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570),[^o56] but in a more abstract setting, such as [incidence geometry](wikipedia://en/1576323), a line may be an independent object, distinct from the set of points which lie on it.[^o57] In differential geometry, a [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) is a generalization of the notion of a line to [curved spaces](wikipedia://en/2073470).[^o58]

#### Planes {#Planes}

In Euclidean geometry a plane is a flat, two-dimensional surface that extends infinitely;[^o52] the definitions for other types of geometries are generalizations of that. Planes are used in many areas of geometry. For instance, planes can be studied as a [topological surface](wikipedia://en/27865) without reference to distances or angles;[^o60] it can be studied as an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), where collinearity and ratios can be studied but not distances;[^o61] it can be studied as the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) using techniques of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759);[^o62] and so on.

#### Curves {#Curves}

A [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve_%28geometry%29) is a 1-dimensional object that may be straight (like a line) or not; curves in 2-dimensional space are called [plane curves](wikipedia://en/971691) and those in 3-dimensional space are called [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve).[^o64]

In topology, a curve is defined by a function from an interval of the real numbers to another space.[^o60] In differential geometry, the same definition is used, but the defining function is required to be differentiable.[^o67] Algebraic geometry studies [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260), which are defined as [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties) of [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251) one.[^o68]

#### Surfaces {#Surfaces}

*Image: Sphere wireframe.svg.* A sphere is a surface that can be defined parametrically (by *x* = *r* sin *θ* cos *φ*, *y* = *r* sin *θ* sin *φ*, *z* = *r* cos *θ*) or implicitly (by *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> + *z*<sup>2</sup> − *r*<sup>2</sup> = 0).

A [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) is a two-dimensional object, such as a sphere or paraboloid.[^o66] In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625)[^o67] and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954),[^o60] surfaces are described by two-dimensional 'patches' (or [neighborhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29)) that are assembled by [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) or [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660), respectively. In algebraic geometry, surfaces are described by [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation).[^o68]

#### Solids {#Solids}

*Image: Blue ball.png.* In [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), a ball is the volume bounded by a sphere.

A [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_%28mathematics%29) is a three-dimensional object bounded by a closed surface; for example, a [ball](wikipedia://en/160556) is the volume bounded by a sphere.

#### Manifolds {#Manifolds}

A [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) is a generalization of the concepts of curve and surface. In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a manifold is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) where every point has a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29) that is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to Euclidean space.[^o60] In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) is a space where each neighborhood is [diffeomorphic](wikipedia://en/8564) to Euclidean space.[^o67]

Manifolds are used extensively in physics, including in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) and [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305).[^o71]

:::

### Angles {#Angles}

*Image: Angle obtuse acute straight.svg.* Acute (a), obtuse (b), and straight (c) angles. The acute and obtuse angles are also known as oblique angles.

[Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) defines a plane angle as the inclination to each other, in a plane, of two lines which meet each other, and do not lie straight with respect to each other.[^o52] In modern terms, an angle is the figure formed by two [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29), called the *sides* of the angle, sharing a common endpoint, called the *[vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417)* of the angle. The size of an angle is formalized as an [angular measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_measure).

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), angles are used to study [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) and [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), as well as forming an object of study in their own right.[^o52] The study of the angles of a triangle or of angles in a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) forms the basis of [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261).[^o74]

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the angles between [plane curves](wikipedia://en/971691) or [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve) or [surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_%28geometry%29) can be calculated using the [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28calculus%29).[^o75][^o76]

### Measures: length, area, and volume {#Measures:_length,_area,_and_volume}

[Length](wikipedia://en/18542), [area](wikipedia://en/1209), and [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) describe the [size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Size_%28mathematics%29) or extent of an object in one dimension, two dimension, and three dimensions respectively.

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) and [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), the length of a line segment can often be calculated by the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).[^o78]

Area and volume can be defined as fundamental quantities separate from length, or they can be described and calculated in terms of lengths in a plane or 3-dimensional space. Mathematicians have found many explicit [formulas for area](wikipedia://en/1209#List_of_formulas) and [formulas for volume](wikipedia://en/32498#Formulas) of various geometric objects. In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), area and volume can be defined in terms of [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532), such as the [Riemann integral](wikipedia://en/26390)[^o79] or the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288).[^o80]

Other geometrical measures include the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) and [compactness](wikipedia://en/4662632).

#### Metrics and measures {#Metrics_and_measures}

*Image: Chinese pythagoras.jpg.* Visual checking of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) for the (3, 4, 5) [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) as in the [Zhoubi Suanjing](wikipedia://en/6131715) 500–200 BC. The Pythagorean theorem is a consequence of the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric).

The concept of length or distance can be generalized, leading to the idea of [metrics](wikipedia://en/20018).[^o82] For instance, the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric) measures the distance between points in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), while the [hyperbolic metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_metric) measures the distance in the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane). Other important examples of metrics include the [Lorentz metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_metric) of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) and the semi-[Riemannian metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).[^o83]

In a different direction, the concepts of length, area and volume are extended by [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), which studies methods of assigning a size or *measure* to [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), where the measures follow rules similar to those of classical area and volume.[^o84]

### Congruence and similarity {#Congruence_and_similarity}

[Congruence](wikipedia://en/39330) and [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377) are concepts that describe when two shapes have similar characteristics.[^o86] In Euclidean geometry, similarity is used to describe objects that have the same shape, while congruence is used to describe objects that are the same in both size and shape.[^o87] [Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert), in his work on creating a more rigorous foundation for geometry, treated congruence as an undefined term whose properties are defined by [axioms](wikipedia://en/928).

Congruence and similarity are generalized in [transformation geometry](wikipedia://en/473774), which studies the properties of geometric objects that are preserved by different kinds of transformations.[^o88]

### Compass and straightedge constructions {#Compass_and_straightedge_constructions}

Classical geometers paid special attention to constructing geometric objects that had been described in some other way. Classically, the only instruments used in most geometric constructions are the [compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_%28drafting%29) and [straightedge](wikipedia://en/65593). Also, every construction had to be complete in a finite number of steps. However, some problems turned out to be difficult or impossible to solve by these means alone, and ingenious constructions using [neusis](wikipedia://en/3752339), parabolas and other curves, or mechanical devices, were found.

### Rotation and orientation {#Rotation_and_orientation}

The geometrical concepts of rotation and orientation define part of the placement of objects embedded in the plane or in space.

### Dimension {#Dimension}

Dimension (mathematics)

*Image: Von Koch curve.gif.* The [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959), with [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907)=log4/log3 and [topological dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_dimension)=1

Traditional geometry allowed dimensions 1 (a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) or curve), 2 (a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) or surface), and 3 (our ambient world conceived of as [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853)). Furthermore, mathematicians and physicists have used [higher dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_dimension) for nearly two centuries.[^o92] One example of a mathematical use for higher dimensions is the [configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596) of a physical system, which has a dimension equal to the system's [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622). For instance, the configuration of a screw can be described by five coordinates.[^o93]

In [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649), the concept of dimension has been extended from [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), to infinite dimension ([Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), for example) and positive [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) (in [fractal geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_geometry)).[^o94] In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), the [dimension of an algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/253251) has received a number of apparently different definitions, which are all equivalent in the most common cases.[^o95]

### Symmetry {#Symmetry}

*Image: Order-3 heptakis heptagonal tiling.png.* A [tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-3_bisected_heptagonal_tiling) of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291)

The theme of [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) in geometry is nearly as old as the science of geometry itself.[^o98] Symmetric shapes such as the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) and [platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) held deep significance for many ancient philosophers[^o99] and were investigated in detail before the time of Euclid.[^o45] Symmetric patterns occur in nature and were artistically rendered in a multitude of forms, including the graphics of [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127), and others.[^o100] In the second half of the 19th century, the relationship between symmetry and geometry came under intense scrutiny. [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887)'s [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382) proclaimed that, in a very precise sense, symmetry, expressed via the notion of a transformation [group](wikipedia://en/19447), determines what geometry *is*.[^o101] Symmetry in classical [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) is represented by [congruences](wikipedia://en/39330) and rigid motions, whereas in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) an analogous role is played by [collineations](wikipedia://en/4072976), [geometric transformations](wikipedia://en/43194879) that take straight lines into straight lines.[^o102] However it was in the new geometries of Bolyai and Lobachevsky, Riemann, [Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820) and Klein, and [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010) that Klein's idea to 'define a geometry via its [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186)' found its inspiration.[^o103] Both discrete and continuous symmetries play prominent roles in geometry, the former in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164),[^o104][^o105] the latter in [Lie theory](wikipedia://en/1595681) and [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243).[^o106][^o107]

A different type of symmetry is the principle of [duality](wikipedia://en/539925) in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849), among other fields. This meta-phenomenon can roughly be described as follows: in any [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977), exchange *point* with *plane*, *join* with *meet*, *lies in* with *contains*, and the result is an equally true theorem.[^o108] A similar and closely related form of duality exists between a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) and its [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988).[^o109]

## Contemporary geometry {#Contemporary_geometry}

### Euclidean geometry {#Euclidean_geometry}

[Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) is geometry in its classical sense.[^o112] As it models the space of the physical world, it is used in many scientific areas, such as [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography),[^o113] and many technical fields, such as [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering),[^o114] [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture),[^o115] [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608),[^o116] [aerodynamics](wikipedia://en/2819),[^o117] and [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation).[^o118] The mandatory educational curriculum of the majority of nations includes the study of Euclidean concepts such as [points](wikipedia://en/593693), [lines](wikipedia://en/946975), [planes](wikipedia://en/19469852), [angles](wikipedia://en/1196), [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), [congruence](wikipedia://en/39330), [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377), [solid figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_figure), [circles](wikipedia://en/6220), and [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202).[^o119]

#### Euclidean vectors {#Euclidean_vectors}

Euclidean vectors are used for a myriad of applications in physics and engineering, such as [position](wikipedia://en/1345771), [displacement](wikipedia://en/859275), [deformation](wikipedia://en/19194778), [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903), [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443), [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force), etc.

### Differential geometry {#Differential_geometry}

*Image: Hyperbolic triangle.svg.* [Differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) uses tools from [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) to study problems involving curvature.

[Differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) uses techniques of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) to study problems in geometry.[^o122] It has applications in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939),[^o123] [econometrics](wikipedia://en/10390),[^o124] and [bioinformatics](wikipedia://en/4214),[^o125] among others.

In particular, differential geometry is of importance to [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) due to [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) postulation that the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) is [curved](wikipedia://en/60770).[^o126] Differential geometry can either be *intrinsic* (meaning that the spaces it considers are [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) whose geometric structure is governed by a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric), which determines how distances are measured near each point) or *extrinsic* (where the object under study is a part of some ambient flat Euclidean space).[^o127]

#### Non-Euclidean geometry {#Non-Euclidean_geometry}

[Non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) consists of two geometries based on [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) closely related to those that specify [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417). As Euclidean geometry lies at the intersection of [metric geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_geometry) and [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890), non-Euclidean geometry arises by either replacing the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) with an alternative, or consideration of [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) other than the [definite quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/20437320) associated with [metric geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_geometry). In the former case, one obtains [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) and [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020), the traditional non-Euclidean geometries. When [isotropic quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/10917170) are admitted, then there are affine planes associated with the [planar algebras](wikipedia://en/58610#Planar_algebras), which give rise to [kinematic geometries](wikipedia://en/58610#Kinematic_geometries) that have also been called non-Euclidean geometry.

### Topology {#Topology}

*Image: Trefoil knot arb.png.* A thickening of the [trefoil knot](wikipedia://en/404773)

Topology is the field concerned with the properties of [continuous mappings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_mapping),[^o130] and can be considered a generalization of Euclidean geometry.[^o131] In practice, topology often means dealing with large-scale properties of spaces, such as [connectedness](wikipedia://en/87141) and [compactness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_%28topology%29).[^o60]

The field of topology, which saw massive development in the 20th century, is in a technical sense a type of [transformation geometry](wikipedia://en/473774), in which transformations are [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660).[^o132] This has often been expressed in the form of the saying 'topology is rubber-sheet geometry'. Subfields of topology include [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732), [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562), [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) and [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649).[^o133]

### Algebraic geometry {#Algebraic_geometry}

*Image: Calabi yau.jpg.* Quintic [Calabi–Yau threefold](wikipedia://en/188932)

Algebraic geometry is fundamentally the study by means of [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) methods of some geometrical shapes, called [algebraic sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_set), and defined as common [zeros](wikipedia://en/264210) of [multivariate polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_polynomial).[^o135] Algebraic geometry became an autonomous subfield of geometry c. 1900, with a theorem called [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215) that establishes a strong correspondence between algebraic sets and [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) of [polynomial rings](wikipedia://en/373065). This led to a parallel development of algebraic geometry, and its algebraic counterpart, called [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990).[^o136] From the late 1950s through the mid-1970s algebraic geometry had undergone major foundational development, with the introduction by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) of [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory), which allows using [topological methods](wikipedia://en/38801), including [cohomology theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_theory) in a purely algebraic context.[^o136] Scheme theory allowed to solve many difficult problems not only in geometry, but also in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). [Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiles'_proof_of_Fermat's_Last_Theorem) is a famous example of a long-standing problem of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) whose solution uses scheme theory and its extensions such as [stack theory](wikipedia://en/13185596). One of seven [Millennium Prize problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Prize_problems), the [Hodge conjecture](wikipedia://en/244518), is a question in algebraic geometry.[^o137]

Algebraic geometry has applications in many areas, including [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432)[^o138] and [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305).[^o139]

### Complex geometry {#Complex_geometry}

[Complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101) studies the nature of geometric structures modelled on, or arising out of, the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628).[^o141][^o142][^o143] Complex geometry lies at the intersection of differential geometry, algebraic geometry, and analysis of [several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables), and has found applications to [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305) and [mirror symmetry](wikipedia://en/644671).[^o144]

Complex geometry first appeared as a distinct area of study in the work of [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) in his study of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181).[^o145][^o146][^o147] Work in the spirit of Riemann was carried out by the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424) in the early 1900s. Contemporary treatment of complex geometry began with the work of [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483), who introduced the concept of [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) to the subject, and illuminated the relations between complex geometry and algebraic geometry.[^o148][^o149] The primary objects of study in complex geometry are [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742), [complex algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_algebraic_varieties), and [complex analytic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analytic_varieties), and [holomorphic vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_vector_bundles) and [coherent sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_sheaves) over these spaces. Special examples of spaces studied in complex geometry include Riemann surfaces, and [Calabi–Yau manifolds](wikipedia://en/188932), and these spaces find uses in string theory. In particular, [worldsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldsheet) of strings are modelled by Riemann surfaces, and [superstring theory](wikipedia://en/19595676) predicts that the extra 6 dimensions of 10 dimensional [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) may be modelled by Calabi–Yau manifolds.

### Discrete geometry {#Discrete_geometry}

*Image: Closepacking.svg.* Discrete geometry includes the study of various [sphere packings](wikipedia://en/368621).

[Discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468) is a subject that has close connections with [convex geometry](wikipedia://en/4165882).[^o151][^o152][^o153] It is concerned mainly with questions of relative position of simple geometric objects, such as points, lines and circles. Examples include the study of [sphere packings](wikipedia://en/368621), [triangulations](wikipedia://en/1440970), the Kneser-Poulsen conjecture, etc.[^o154][^o155] It shares many methods and principles with [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170).

### Computational geometry {#Computational_geometry}

[Computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927) deals with [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) and their [implementations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_%28computer_science%29) for manipulating geometrical objects. Important problems historically have included the [travelling salesman problem](wikipedia://en/31248), [minimum spanning trees](wikipedia://en/41795), [hidden-line removal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden-line_removal), and [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730).[^o157]

Although being a young area of geometry, it has many applications in [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision), [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing), [computer-aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer-aided_design), [medical imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_imaging), etc.[^o158]

### Geometric group theory {#Geometric_group_theory}

*Image: Cayley graph of F2.svg.* The Cayley graph of the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) on two generators *a* and *b*

Groups have been understood as geometric objects since [Klein's Erlangen programme](wikipedia://en/243382). [Geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) studies [group actions](wikipedia://en/12781) on objects that are regarded as geometric (significantly, isometric actions on [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018)) to study [finitely generated groups](wikipedia://en/963042), often involving large-scale geometric techniques[^o160] and borrowing from topology, geometry, dynamics and analysis.[^o161] It had a significant impact on [low-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/621774), a celebrated result being Agol's proof of the [virtually Haken conjecture](wikipedia://en/4692183) that combines [Perelman geometrization](wikipedia://en/220642) with [cubulation](wikipedia://en/54829672) techniques.[^o162]

Group actions on their [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) are foundational examples of isometric group actions. Other major topics include [quasi-isometries](wikipedia://en/12747259), [Gromov-hyperbolic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gromov-hyperbolic_group) and their generalizations ([relatively](wikipedia://en/21993809) and [acylindrically hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/55866076)), [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735) and [their automorphisms](wikipedia://en/12747184), [groups acting on trees](wikipedia://en/18673708), various notions of nonpositive curvature for groups ([CAT(0) groups](wikipedia://en/12942017), [Dehn functions](wikipedia://en/20017026), [automaticity](wikipedia://en/4224977)...), [right angled Artin groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_angled_Artin_group), and topics close to [combinatorial group theory](wikipedia://en/979834) such as [small cancellation theory](wikipedia://en/19882883) and algorithmic problems (e.g. the [word](wikipedia://en/33563), [conjugacy](wikipedia://en/4942384), and [isomorphism problems](wikipedia://en/18054026)). Other group-theoretic topics like [mapping class groups](wikipedia://en/31060586), [property (T)](wikipedia://en/1362724), [solvability](wikipedia://en/105012), [amenability](wikipedia://en/657430) and [lattices in Lie groups](wikipedia://en/15782871) are sometimes regarded as strongly geometric as well.[^o160][^o163][^o164][^o165]

### Convex geometry {#Convex_geometry}

[Convex geometry](wikipedia://en/4165882) investigates [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) shapes in the Euclidean space and its more abstract analogues, often using techniques of [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478) and [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492).[^o167] It has close connections to [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201), [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization) and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and important applications in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).

Convex geometry dates back to antiquity.[^o167] [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) gave the first known precise definition of convexity. The [isoperimetric problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isoperimetric_problem), a recurring concept in convex geometry, was studied by the Greeks as well, including [Zenodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenodorus_%28mathematician%29). Archimedes, [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954), [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331), and later [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler) and [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter) all studied [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) and their properties. From the 19th century on, mathematicians have studied other areas of convex mathematics, including higher-dimensional polytopes, volume and surface area of convex bodies, [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623), [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms), [tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiling_%28geometry%29) and [lattices](wikipedia://en/469246).

## Applications {#Applications}

Geometry has found applications in many fields, some of which are described below.

### Art {#Art}

*Image: Fes Medersa Bou Inania Mosaique2.jpg.* Bou Inania Madrasa, Fes, Morocco, zellige mosaic tiles forming elaborate geometric tessellations

Mathematics and art are related in a variety of ways. For instance, the theory of [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405) showed that there is more to geometry than just the metric properties of figures: perspective is the origin of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849).[^o171]

Artists have long used concepts of [proportion](wikipedia://en/81863) in design. [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius) developed a complicated theory of *ideal proportions* for the human figure.[^o172] These concepts have been used and adapted by artists from [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo) to modern comic book artists.[^o173]

The [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) is a particular proportion that has had a controversial role in art. Often claimed to be the most aesthetically pleasing ratio of lengths, it is frequently stated to be incorporated into famous works of art, though the most reliable and unambiguous examples were made deliberately by artists aware of this legend.[^o174]

[Tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiling_%28geometry%29), or tessellations, have been used in art throughout history. [Islamic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art) makes frequent use of tessellations, as did the art of [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127).[^o175] Escher's work also made use of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).

[Cézanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cézanne) advanced the theory that all images can be built up from the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), the [cone](wikipedia://en/782427), and the [cylinder](wikipedia://en/859283). This is still used in art theory today, although the exact list of shapes varies from author to author.[^o176][^o177]

### Architecture {#Architecture}

Geometry has many applications in architecture. In fact, it has been said that geometry lies at the core of architectural design.[^o179][^o180] Applications of geometry to architecture include the use of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) to create [forced perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_perspective),[^o181] the use of [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673) in constructing domes and similar objects,[^o115] the use of [tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellations),[^o115] and the use of symmetry.[^o115]

### Physics {#Physics}

The field of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), especially as it relates to mapping the positions of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) and [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) and describing the relationship between movements of celestial bodies, have served as an important source of geometric problems throughout history.[^o183]

[Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243) and [pseudo-Riemannian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian) geometry are used in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024).[^o184] [String theory](wikipedia://en/28305) makes use of several variants of geometry,[^o185] as does [quantum information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_information_theory).[^o186]

### Biology and medicine {#Biology_and_medicine}

Geometry plays a fundamental role in biology and medicine by providing quantitative methods for describing biological form, spatial organization, and mechanical interactions across multiple levels of organization, from individual cells to whole organisms. Geometric concepts are widely applied in morphometrics, developmental biology, biomechanics, medical imaging, and the analysis of complex biological systems.[^o188][^o189]

Geometric morphometrics has become an important tool for the quantitative analysis of biological shape and anatomical variation. By combining landmark-based geometry with multivariate statistical methods, it allows objective comparisons of morphological structures in developmental biology, evolutionary biology, anthropology, neuroanatomy, and clinical medicine. Such approaches are widely used to study craniofacial growth, skeletal morphology, and prenatal development.[^o190][^o191]

Geometry also contributes to the understanding of biological development. Modern developmental biology recognizes that tissue geometry, mechanical forces, and spatial organization interact with genetic and biochemical signaling to regulate morphogenesis, organ formation, and embryonic patterning. Geometric constraints influence processes such as neural tube closure, cortical folding, branching morphogenesis, and craniofacial development, while abnormalities in tissue geometry may contribute to congenital disorders such as craniosynostosis.[^o191][^o192][^o193]

Fractal geometry provides mathematical models for describing self-similar biological structures that cannot be adequately characterized using classical Euclidean geometry. Fractal analysis has been applied to vascular networks, the bronchial tree, neuronal dendrites, cardiac physiology, medical imaging, and the structural organization of tumors.[^o194][^o195]

Geometric principles are also fundamental to biomechanics and medical imaging. Mechanical analyses of bones, joints, muscles, and soft tissues rely on geometric models to describe shape, stress distribution, and movement, while modern imaging techniques—including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and three-dimensional reconstruction—use geometric algorithms for image registration, segmentation, quantitative morphometry, surgical planning, and computer-assisted diagnosis.[^o189]

### Other fields of mathematics {#Other_fields_of_mathematics}

*Image: Square root of 2 triangle.svg.* The Pythagoreans discovered that the sides of a triangle could have [incommensurable](wikipedia://en/753944) lengths.

[Calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) was strongly influenced by geometry.[^o33] For instance, the introduction of [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates) by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) and the concurrent developments of [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) marked a new stage for geometry, since geometric figures such as [plane curves](wikipedia://en/971691) could now be represented [analytically](wikipedia://en/2202) in the form of functions and equations. This played a key role in the emergence of [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus) in the 17th century. Analytic geometry continues to be a mainstay of pre-calculus and calculus curriculum.[^o197][^o198]

Another important area of application is [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).[^o199] In [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) the [Pythagoreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans) considered the role of numbers in geometry. However, the discovery of incommensurable lengths contradicted their philosophical views.[^o200] Since the 19th century, geometry has been used for solving problems in number theory, for example through the [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153) or, more recently, [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory), which is used in [Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/21950759).[^o201]
