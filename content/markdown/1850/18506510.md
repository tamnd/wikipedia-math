# Trimmed estimator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trimmed estimator"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a **trimmed estimator** is an [estimator](wikipedia://en/10043) derived from another estimator by excluding some of the [extreme values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_value), a process called [truncation](wikipedia://en/18374571). This is generally done to obtain a more [robust statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robust_statistic), and the extreme values are considered [outliers](wikipedia://en/160951).[^o2] Trimmed estimators also often have higher [efficiency](wikipedia://en/27250866) for [mixture distributions](wikipedia://en/708242), and [heavy-tailed distributions](wikipedia://en/8092200) than the corresponding untrimmed estimator, at the cost of lower efficiency for other distributions, such as the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462).

:::

Given an estimator, the x% trimmed version is obtained by discarding the x% lowest or highest observations or on both end: it is a statistic on the *middle* of the data. For instance, the 5% [trimmed mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trimmed_mean) is obtained by taking the mean of the 5% to 95% range. In some cases a trimmed estimator discards a fixed number of points (such as maximum and minimum) instead of a percentage.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The [median](wikipedia://en/18837) is the most trimmed statistic (nominally 50%), as it discards all but the most central data, and equals the fully trimmed mean – or indeed fully trimmed mid-range, or (for odd-size data sets) the fully trimmed maximum or minimum. Likewise, no degree of trimming has any effect on the median – a trimmed median is the median – because trimming always excludes an equal number of the lowest and highest values.

[Quantiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantiles) can be thought of as trimmed maxima or minima: for instance, the 5th [percentile](wikipedia://en/354907) is the 5% trimmed minimum.

Trimmed estimators used to estimate a [location parameter](wikipedia://en/17516) include:

- [Trimmed mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trimmed_mean)
- [Modified mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_mean), discarding the minimum and maximum values
- [Interquartile mean](wikipedia://en/44145), the 25% [trimmed mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trimmed_mean)
- [Midhinge](wikipedia://en/12559595), the 25% trimmed [mid-range](wikipedia://en/2814021)

Trimmed estimators used to estimate a [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503) include:

- [Interquartile range](wikipedia://en/14812), the 25% trimmed [range](wikipedia://en/27588)
- [Interdecile range](wikipedia://en/22109078), the 10% trimmed range

Trimmed estimators involving only linear combinations of points are examples of [L-estimators](wikipedia://en/17398955).

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Estimation {#Estimation}

Most often, trimmed estimators are used for [parameter estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_estimation) of the same parameter as the untrimmed estimator. In some cases the estimator can be used directly, while in other cases it must be adjusted to yield an [unbiased](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiased_estimator) [consistent estimator](wikipedia://en/1497569).

For example, when estimating a [location parameter](wikipedia://en/17516) for a symmetric distribution, a trimmed estimator will be unbiased (assuming the original estimator was unbiased), as it removes the same amount above and below. However, if the distribution has [skew](wikipedia://en/28212), trimmed estimators will generally be biased and require adjustment. For example, in a skewed distribution, the [nonparametric skew](wikipedia://en/34502950) (and [Pearson's skewness coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson's_skewness_coefficients)) measure the bias of the median as an estimator of the mean.

When estimating a [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503), using a trimmed estimator as a [robust measures of scale](wikipedia://en/22824029), such as to estimate the [population variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_variance) or population [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), one generally must multiply by a [scale factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_factor) to make it an unbiased consistent estimator; see [scale parameter: estimation](wikipedia://en/769503#Estimation).

For example, dividing the IQR by $2\sqrt{2} \operatorname{erf}^{-1}(1/2) \approx 1.349$ (using the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111)) makes it an unbiased, consistent estimator for the population standard deviation if the data follow a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462).

### Other uses {#Other_uses}

Trimmed estimators can also be used as statistics in their own right – for example, the median is a measure of location, and the IQR is a measure of dispersion. In these cases, the sample statistics can act as estimators of their own [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653). For example, the [MAD](wikipedia://en/10050999) of a sample from a standard [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003) is an estimator of the population MAD, which in this case is 1, whereas the population variance does not exist.
