# Erdős distinct distances problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Erdős distinct distances problem"}

In [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468), the **Erdős distinct distances problem** states that every set of points in the plane has a nearly linear number of distinct distances. It was posed by [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) in 1946.[^o2][^o3] The current best result was achieved by [Larry Guth](wikipedia://en/31875947) and [Nets Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nets_Katz) in 2015.[^o4][^o5][^o6]

:::

Erdős considered this problem as his "most striking contribution to geometry".[^o7]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=heading name="The conjecture"}

## The conjecture {#The_conjecture}

Place *n* distinct points in a plane. There are $\tfrac 12 (n^2-n)$ distinct pairs between them. Of these pairs, some have the same length, and some have different lengths. The maximum number of different lengths achievable is $\tfrac 12 (n^2-n)$, using the following points: $(0, 0), (0, 1), (0, 3), (0, 7), \dots, (0, 2^{n-1}-1)$.

The minimum number of different lengths achievable is more difficult to find. Let this number be $g(n)$. Equivalently, it is the smallest possible [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of their [distance set](wikipedia://en/58786399).

In his 1946 paper, Erdős proved the estimates

$$\sqrt{n-3/4}-1/2\leq g(n)\leq c n/\sqrt{\log n}$$

for some constant $c$. In big-O notation, $g \leq O(n / \sqrt {\log n})$.

The lower bound was given by an easy argument. The upper bound is given by a $\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}$ square grid. For such a grid, there are $O( n/\sqrt{\log n})$ numbers below *n* which are sums of two squares, expressed in [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578); see [Landau–Ramanujan constant](wikipedia://en/620712).

Erdős conjectured that the upper bound $O( n/\sqrt{\log n})$ is very close to being tight: $g(n) = \Omega(n^c)$ holds for every $c < 1$, using [big Omega notation](wikipedia://en/44578). More succinctly, $g(n) \geq \Omega_*(n)$.

He further conjectured that the upper bound is *exactly* tight: $g(n)= \Theta(n/\sqrt{\log n})$, and offered a [prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdős_problem) of \$500 for either proving or disproving the conjecture.[^o10]

:::

## Partial results {#Partial_results}

Paul Erdős' 1946 lower bound of $g(n) = \Omega(n^{1/2})$ was successively improved to:

- $g(n) = \Omega(n^{2/3})$ by [Leo Moser](wikipedia://en/745180) in 1952,[^o12]
- $g(n) = \Omega(n^{5/7})$ by [Fan Chung](wikipedia://en/246562) in 1984,[^o13]
- $g(n) = \Omega(n^{4/5}/\log n)$ by Fan Chung, [Endre Szemerédi](wikipedia://en/663489), and [William T. Trotter](wikipedia://en/41414002) in 1992,[^o14]
- $g(n) = \Omega(n^{4/5})$ by László A. Székely in 1993,[^o15]
- $g(n) = \Omega(n^{6/7})$ by [József Solymosi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/József_Solymosi) and Csaba D. Tóth in 2001,[^o16]
- $g(n) = \Omega(n^{(4e/(5e - 1)) - \text{ɛ}})$ by [Gábor Tardos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gábor_Tardos) in 2003,[^o17]
- $g(n) = \Omega(n^{((48 - 14e)/(55 - 16e)) - \text{ɛ}})$ by Nets Katz and Gábor Tardos in 2004,[^o18]
- $g(n) = \Omega(n/\log n)$ by Larry Guth and Nets Katz in 2015.[^o4]

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variants"}

## Variants {#Variants}

### Restricted subsets {#Restricted_subsets}

Instead of allowing the *n* distinct points to be placed anywhere in a plane, we can additionally require the points to satisfy constraints. In general, the more stringent the constraints are, the larger $g$ gets.

If we require the points to fall on a single line, then $g_{\text{line}}(n)= n-1$ by setting the points equally spaced. If we require the points to fall on a single circle, then $g_{\text{circle}}(n)= \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ by setting the points equally spaced around the circle. More generally, if we require the points to form a convex polygon, then $g_{\text{convex}}(n)= \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$. The same, if we require the points to form a strictly convex polygon.[^o22][^o23]

If we require the points to be in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736), meaning no three points are collinear and no four points are cocircular, then it is an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335). Currently the best result is $g_{\text{gen}}(n) \geq \Omega(n)$ and $g_{\text{gen}}\leq n2^{O(\sqrt{\log n})}$.[^o24]

If we require the points to be in general position and no four points form a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496), then it is an open problem. Currently the best result is $g_{\text{para}}(n) \geq \Omega(n)$ and $g_{\text{para}}\leq O(n^2/ \sqrt{\log n})$.[^o25][^o24]

See Section 3 of [^o26] for more problems and results of the kind.

### Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

Erdős also considered the higher-dimensional variant of the problem: for $d\ge 3$ let $g_d(n)$ denote the minimal possible number of distinct distances among $n$ points in $d$-dimensional Euclidean space. He proved that $g_d(n)=\Omega(n^{1/d})$ and $g_d(n)=O(n^{2/d})$ and conjectured that the upper bound is in fact sharp, i.e., $g_d(n)=\Theta(n^{2/d})$. József Solymosi and [Van H. Vu](wikipedia://en/21419416) obtained the lower bound $g_d(n)=\Omega(n^{2/d - 2/d(d+2)})$ in 2008.[^o28]

In the other direction, it's known currently that $g_3(n) \geq \Omega_*(n^{3/5})$, by applying the recursion relation of [^o29] to the result $g_2(n) \geq \Omega_*(n)$ of Guth.[^o26]

### Generic norms {#Generic_norms}

The same question can be asked for any [normed space](wikipedia://en/21538). Given a norm $\|\cdot \|$, define $g_{\|\cdot \|}(n)$ accordingly. The problem is solved in the *[generic](wikipedia://en/12886758)* case. Specifically, given any integer $d\geq 2$, for *almost all* norms $\|\cdot \|$,

$$g_{\|\cdot\|}(n) = (1-o(1)) n$$

That is, the set of norms that violate this condition is [meagre](wikipedia://en/226975) in the set of all norms of $\mathbb{R}^d$, regarded as a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), metrized by the [Hausdorff distance](wikipedia://en/429296) between the norm-unit balls.[^o26][^o31]
