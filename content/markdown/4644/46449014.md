# Circumcenter of mass

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Circumcenter of mass"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **circumcenter of mass** is a center associated with a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) which shares many of the properties of the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961). More generally, the circumcenter of mass may be defined for [simplicial polytopes](wikipedia://en/8853373) and also in the [spherical](wikipedia://en/174026) and [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291) geometries.

:::

In the special case when the polytope is a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) or [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733), the circumcenter of mass has been called the "quasicircumcenter" and has been used to define an [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) of a quadrilateral.[^o2][^o3] The circumcenter of mass allows us to define an Euler line for simplicial polytopes.

## Definition in the plane {#Definition_in_the_plane}

Let $P$ be an oriented polygon (with vertices counted countercyclically) in the plane with vertices $V_1,V_2,\ldots,V_n$ and let $O$ be an arbitrary point not lying on the sides (or their [extensions](wikipedia://en/43958459)). Consider the triangulation of $P$ by the oriented triangles $O V_i V_{i+1}$ (the index $i$ is viewed modulo $n$). Associate with each of these triangles its circumcenter $C_i$ with weight equal to its oriented area (positive if its sequence of vertices is countercyclical; negative otherwise). The circumcenter of mass of $P$ is the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) of these weighted circumcenters. The result is independent of the choice of point $O$.[^o5]

## Properties {#Properties}

In the special case when the polygon is [cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon), the circumcenter of mass coincides with the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter).

The circumcenter of mass satisfies an analog of Archimedes' Lemma, which states that if a polygon is decomposed into two smaller polygons, then the circumcenter of mass of that polygon is a weighted sum of the circumcenters of mass of the two smaller polygons. As a consequence, any triangulation with nondegenerate triangles may be used to define the circumcenter of mass.

For an [equilateral polygon](wikipedia://en/214547), the circumcenter of mass and center of mass coincide. More generally, the circumcenter of mass and center of mass coincide for a simplicial polytope for which each face has the sum of squares of its edges a constant.[^o8]

The circumcenter of mass is invariant under the operation of "recutting" of polygons[^o9] and the discrete bicycle (Darboux) transformation; in other words, the image of a polygon under these operations has the same circumcenter of mass as the original polygon. The [generalized Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622#Simplicial_polytope) makes other appearances in the theory of integrable systems.[^o10]

Let $V_i=(x_i,y_i)$ be the vertices of $P$ and let $A$ denote its area. The circumcenter of mass $CCM(P)$ of the polygon $P$ is given by the formula

$$CCM(P)=\frac{1}{4 A}(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} -y_i y_{i+1}^2+y_i^2 y_{i+1} +x_i^2 y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}^2 y_i, \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} -x_{i+1} y_i^2+x_i y_{i+1}^2+x_i x_{i+1}^2-x_i^2 x_{i+1}).$$

The circumcenter of mass can be extended to smooth curves via a limiting procedure. This continuous limit coincides with the center of mass of the homogeneous [lamina](wikipedia://en/3121604) bounded by the curve.

Under natural assumptions, the centers of polygons which satisfy Archimedes' Lemma are precisely the points of its Euler line. In other words, the only "well-behaved" centers which satisfy Archimedes' Lemma are the affine combinations of the circumcenter of mass and center of mass.

:::

## Generalized Euler line {#Generalized_Euler_line}

The circumcenter of mass allows an [Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622) to be defined for any polygon (and more generally, for a simplicial polytope). This [generalized Euler line](wikipedia://en/203622#Simplicial_polytope) is defined as the affine span of the center of mass and circumcenter of mass of the polytope.
