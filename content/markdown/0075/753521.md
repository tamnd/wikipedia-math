# Complemented lattice

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complemented lattice"}

*Image: Fano plane Hasse diagram.svg.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of a complemented lattice. A point $p$ and a line $l$ of the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404) are complements if and only if $p$ does not lie on $l$.

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) discipline of [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), a **complemented lattice** is a bounded [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) (with [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element) 0 and [greatest element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_element) 1), in which every element *a* has a **complement**, i.e. an element *b* satisfying *a* ∨ *b* = 1 and *a* ∧ *b* = 0. Complements need not be unique.

:::

A **relatively complemented lattice** is a lattice such that every [interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_%28partial_order%29) \[*c*, *d*\], viewed as a bounded lattice in its own right, is a complemented lattice.

An **orthocomplementation** on a complemented lattice is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) that is [order-reversing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-reversing) and maps each element to a complement. An orthocomplemented lattice satisfying a weak form of the [modular law](wikipedia://en/1089311) is called an **orthomodular lattice**.

In bounded [distributive lattices](wikipedia://en/310883), complements are unique. Every complemented distributive lattice has a unique orthocomplementation and is in fact a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959).

## Definition and basic properties {#Definition_and_basic_properties}

A **complemented lattice** is a bounded lattice (with [least element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_element) 0 and [greatest element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_element) 1), in which every element *a* has a **complement**, i.e. an element *b* such that

*a* ∨ *b* = 1 and *a* ∧ *b* = 0.

In general an element may have more than one complement. However, in a (bounded) [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883) every element will have at most one complement.[^o3] A lattice in which every element has exactly one complement is called a **uniquely complemented lattice**[^o4]

A lattice with the property that every interval (viewed as a sublattice) is complemented is called a **relatively complemented lattice**. In other words, a relatively complemented lattice is characterized by the property that for every element *a* in an interval \[*c*, *d*\] there is an element *b* such that

*a* ∨ *b* = *d* and *a* ∧ *b* = *c*.

Such an element *b* is called a complement of *a* relative to the interval.

A distributive lattice is complemented if and only if it is bounded and relatively complemented.[^o5][^o6] The lattice of [subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) provide an example of a complemented lattice that is not, in general, distributive.

## Orthocomplementation {#Orthocomplementation}

An **orthocomplementation** on a bounded lattice is a function that maps each element *a* to an "orthocomplement" *a*<sup>⊥</sup> in such a way that the following axioms are satisfied:[^o8]

**Complement law**

*a*<sup>⊥</sup> ∨ *a* = 1 and *a*<sup>⊥</sup> ∧ *a* = 0.

**Involution law**

*a*<sup>⊥⊥</sup> = *a*.

**Order-reversing**

if *a* ≤ *b* then *b*<sup>⊥</sup> ≤ *a*<sup>⊥</sup>.

An **orthocomplemented lattice** or **ortholattice** is a bounded lattice equipped with an orthocomplementation. The lattice of subspaces of an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856), and the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) operation, provides an example of an orthocomplemented lattice that is not, in general, distributive.[^o9]

[Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) are a special case of orthocomplemented lattices, which in turn are a special case of complemented lattices (with extra structure). The ortholattices are most often used in [quantum logic](wikipedia://en/663426), where the [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) of a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) represent quantum propositions and behave as an orthocomplemented lattice.

Orthocomplemented lattices, like Boolean algebras, satisfy [de Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669):

- (*a* ∨ *b*)<sup>⊥</sup> = *a*<sup>⊥</sup> ∧ *b*<sup>⊥</sup>
- (*a* ∧ *b*)<sup>⊥</sup> = *a*<sup>⊥</sup> ∨ *b*<sup>⊥</sup>.

## Orthomodular lattices {#Orthomodular_lattices}

A lattice is called [modular](wikipedia://en/1089311) if for all elements *a*, *b* and *c* the implication

if *a* ≤ *c*, then *a* ∨ (*b* ∧ *c*) = (*a* ∨ *b*) ∧ *c*

holds. This is weaker than [distributivity](wikipedia://en/310883); e.g. the above-shown lattice *M*<sub>3</sub> is modular, but not distributive.

A natural further weakening of this condition for orthocomplemented lattices, necessary for applications in quantum logic, is to require it only in the special case *b* = *a*<sup>⊥</sup>. An **orthomodular lattice** is therefore defined as an orthocomplemented lattice such that for any two elements the implication

if *a* ≤ *c*, then *a* ∨ (*a*<sup>⊥</sup> ∧ *c*) = *c*

holds.

Lattices of this form are of crucial importance for the study of [quantum logic](wikipedia://en/663426), since they are part of the axiomisation of the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) [formulation](wikipedia://en/20728) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916) and [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) observed that the [propositional](wikipedia://en/18154) [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_calculus) in quantum logic is "formally indistinguishable from the calculus of linear subspaces \[of a Hilbert space\] with respect to [set products](wikipedia://en/41780044), [linear sums](wikipedia://en/56357#Sum) and orthogonal complements" corresponding to the roles of *and*, *or* and *not* in Boolean lattices. This remark has spurred interest in the closed subspaces of a Hilbert space, which form an orthomodular lattice.[^o12]
