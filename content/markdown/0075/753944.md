# Commensurability (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commensurability"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), two non-[zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) *a* and *b* are said to be ***commensurable*** if their ratio *$\tfrac{a}{b}$* is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024); otherwise *a* and *b* are called ***incommensurable***. (Recall that a rational number is one that is equivalent to the ratio of two [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers).) There is a more general notion of [commensurability in group theory](wikipedia://en/29343178).

:::

For example, the numbers 3 and 2 are commensurable because their ratio, $\tfrac{3}{2}$, is a rational number. The numbers $\sqrt{3}$ and $2\sqrt{3}$ are also commensurable because their ratio, $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\sqrt{3}}=\frac{1}{2}$, is a rational number. However, the numbers $\sqrt{3}$ and 2 are incommensurable because their ratio, $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, is an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689).

More generally, it is immediate from the definition that if *a* and *b* are any two non-zero rational numbers, then *a* and *b* are commensurable; it is also immediate that if *a* is any irrational number and *b* is any non-zero rational number, then *a* and *b* are incommensurable. On the other hand, if both *a* and *b* are irrational numbers, then *a* and *b* may or may not be commensurable.

## History of the concept {#History_of_the_concept}

The [Pythagoreans](wikipedia://en/291170) are credited with the proof of the existence of [irrational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_numbers).[^o3][^o4] When the ratio of the *lengths* of two line segments is irrational, the line segments *themselves* (not just their lengths) are also described as being incommensurable.

A separate, more general and circuitous ancient Greek doctrine of proportionality for geometric [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301) was developed in Book V of Euclid's *Elements* in order to allow proofs involving incommensurable lengths, thus avoiding arguments which applied only to a historically restricted definition of [number](wikipedia://en/21690#History).

[Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s notion of commensurability is anticipated in passing in the discussion between [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) and the slave boy in Plato's dialogue entitled [Meno](wikipedia://en/594486), in which Socrates uses the boy's own inherent capabilities to solve a complex geometric problem through the Socratic Method. He develops a proof which is, for all intents and purposes, very Euclidean in nature and speaks to the concept of incommensurability.[^o5]

The usage primarily comes from translations of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s [*Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107), in which two line segments *a* and *b* are called commensurable precisely if there is some third segment *c* that can be laid end-to-end a whole number of times to produce a segment congruent to *a*, and also, with a different whole number, a segment congruent to *b*. Euclid did not use any concept of real number, but he used a notion of congruence of line segments, and of one such segment being longer or shorter than another.

That *$\tfrac{a}{b}$* is rational is a [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) for the existence of some real number *c*, and [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *m* and *n*, such that

*a* = *mc* and *b* = *nc*.

Assuming for simplicity that *a* and *b* are [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number), one can say that a [ruler](wikipedia://en/65593), marked off in units of length *c*, could be used to measure out both a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) of length *a*, and one of length *b*. That is, there is a common unit of [length](wikipedia://en/18542) in terms of which *a* and *b* can both be measured; this is the origin of the term. Otherwise the pair *a* and *b* are **incommensurable**.

## In group theory {#In_group_theory}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), two [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) Γ<sub>1</sub> and Γ<sub>2</sub> of a group *G* are said to be **commensurable** if the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) Γ<sub>1</sub> ∩ Γ<sub>2</sub> is of [finite index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_index) in both Γ<sub>1</sub> and Γ<sub>2</sub>.

Example: Let *a* and *b* be nonzero real numbers. Then the subgroup of the real numbers **R** [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by *a* is commensurable with the subgroup generated by *b* if and only if the real numbers *a* and *b* are commensurable, in the sense that *a*/*b* is rational. Thus the group-theoretic notion of commensurability generalizes the concept for real numbers.

There is a similar notion for two groups which are not given as subgroups of the same group. Two groups *G*<sub>1</sub> and *G*<sub>2</sub> are (**abstractly**) **commensurable** if there are subgroups *H*<sub>1</sub> ⊂ *G*<sub>1</sub> and *H*<sub>2</sub> ⊂ *G*<sub>2</sub> of finite index such that *H*<sub>1</sub> is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to *H*<sub>2</sub>.

## In topology {#In_topology}

Two [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) are sometimes said to be *commensurable* if they have [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) finite-sheeted [covering spaces](wikipedia://en/250323). Depending on the type of space under consideration, one might want to use [homotopy equivalences](wikipedia://en/212250) or [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) instead of homeomorphisms in the definition. If two spaces are commensurable, then their [fundamental groups](wikipedia://en/11004) are commensurable.

Example: any two [closed surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_surface) of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) at least 2 are commensurable with each other.
