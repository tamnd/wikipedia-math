# One-parameter group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="One-parameter group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **one-parameter group** or **one-parameter subgroup** usually means a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29) [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396)

:::

$$\varphi : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow G$$

from the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ (as an [additive group](wikipedia://en/2974)) to some other [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) $G$. If $\varphi$ is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) then $\varphi(\mathbb{R})$, the image, will be a subgroup of $G$ that is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$ as an additive group. Despite its name, "a one-parameter group" is not actually a group, but a homomorphism *between* groups.

One-parameter groups were introduced by [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010) in 1893 to define [infinitesimal transformations](wikipedia://en/658068). According to Lie, an *infinitesimal transformation* is an infinitely small transformation of the one-parameter group that it generates.[^o2] It is these infinitesimal transformations that generate a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) that is used to describe a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of any dimension.

The [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_%28group_theory%29) of a one-parameter group on a set is known as a [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735). A smooth vector field on a manifold, at a point, induces a *local flow* - a one parameter group of local diffeomorphisms, sending points along [integral curves](wikipedia://en/1322061#Generalization_to_differentiable_manifolds) of the vector field. The local flow of a vector field is used to define the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437) of tensor fields along the vector field.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A curve $\phi:\mathbb{R} \rightarrow G$ is called one-parameter subgroup of $G$ if it satisfies the condition[^o5]

$$\phi(t)\phi(s) = \phi(s+t).$$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Hyperbolic\_rotation.gif.* The group of [squeeze mappings](wikipedia://en/1140043) has one parameter.

In [Lie theory](wikipedia://en/1595681), one-parameter groups correspond to one-dimensional subspaces of the associated [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944). The [Lie group–Lie algebra correspondence](wikipedia://en/43302095) is the basis of a science begun by [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010) in the 1890s.

Another important case is seen in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), with $G$ being the group of [unitary operators](wikipedia://en/225999) on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). See [Stone's theorem on one-parameter unitary groups](wikipedia://en/893698).

In his monograph *Lie Groups*, [P. M. Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._M._Cohn) gave the following theorem:

Any connected 1-dimensional Lie group is analytically isomorphic either to the additive group of real numbers $\mathfrak{R}$, or to $\mathfrak{T}$, the additive group of real numbers $\mod 1$. In particular, every 1-dimensional Lie group is locally isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$.[^o8]

:::

## Physics {#Physics}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), one-parameter groups describe [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems).[^o10] Furthermore, whenever a system of physical laws admits a one-parameter group of [differentiable](wikipedia://en/7921) [symmetries](wikipedia://en/28186), then there is a [conserved quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law_%28physics%29), by [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159).

In the study of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) the use of the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) to calibrate spatio-temporal measurements has become common since [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) discussed it in 1908. The [principle of relativity](wikipedia://en/74327) was reduced to arbitrariness of which diameter of the unit hyperbola was used to determine a [world-line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World-line). Using the parametrization of the hyperbola with [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981), the theory of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) provided a calculus of relative motion with the one-parameter group indexed by [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673). The *rapidity* replaces the *velocity* in kinematics and dynamics of relativity theory. Since rapidity is unbounded, the one-parameter group it stands upon is non-compact. The rapidity concept was introduced by [E.T. Whittaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.T._Whittaker) in 1910, and named by [Alfred Robb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Robb) the next year. The rapidity parameter amounts to the length of a [hyperbolic versor](wikipedia://en/2644621#Hyperbolic_versor), a concept of the nineteenth century. Mathematical physicists [James Cockle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cockle_%28lawyer%29), [William Kingdon Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820), and [Alexander Macfarlane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Macfarlane) had all employed in their writings an equivalent mapping of the Cartesian plane by operator $(\cosh{a} + r\sinh{a})$, where $a$ is the hyperbolic angle and $r^2 = +1$.

## In GL(n,C) {#In_GL(n,C)}

An important example in the theory of Lie groups arises when $G$ is taken to be $\mathrm{GL}(n;\mathbb C)$, the group of invertible $n\times n$ matrices with complex entries. In that case, a basic result is the following:[^o12]

**Theorem**: Suppose $\varphi : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow\mathrm{GL}(n;\mathbb C)$ is a one-parameter group. Then there exists a unique $n\times n$ matrix $X$ such that

$$\varphi(t)=e^{tX}$$

for all $t\in\mathbb R$.

It follows from this result that $\varphi$ is differentiable, even though this was not an assumption of the theorem. The matrix $X$ can then be recovered from $\varphi$ as

$$\left.\frac{d\varphi(t)}{dt}\right|_{t=0} = \left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}e^{tX}=\left.(Xe^{tX})\right|_{t=0} = Xe^0=X.$$

This result can be used, for example, to show that any continuous homomorphism between matrix Lie groups is smooth.[^o13]

## Topology {#Topology}

A technical complication is that $\varphi(\mathbb{R})$ as a [subspace](wikipedia://en/910926) of $G$ may carry a topology that is [coarser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finer_topology) than that on $\mathbb{R}$; this may happen in cases where $\varphi$ is injective. Think for example of the case where $G$ is a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) $T$, and $\varphi$ is constructed by [winding a straight line round $T$ at an irrational slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_winding_of_a_torus).

In that case the induced topology may not be the standard one of the real line.
