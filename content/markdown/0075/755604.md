# Complexification

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complexification"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **complexification** of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ over the field of real numbers (a "real vector space") yields a vector space $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ over the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) [field](wikipedia://en/10603), obtained by formally extending the scaling of vectors by real numbers to include their scaling ("multiplication") by complex numbers. Any [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for $V$ (a space over the real numbers) may also serve as a basis for $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ over the complex numbers.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let $V$ be a real vector space. The **complexification** of $V$ is defined by taking the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of $V$ with the complex numbers (thought of as a 2-dimensional vector space over the reals):

$$V^{\mathbb{C}} = V\otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} .$$

The subscript, $\mathbb{R}$, on the tensor product indicates that the tensor product is taken over the real numbers (since $V$ is a real vector space this is the only sensible option anyway, so the subscript can safely be omitted). As it stands, $V^{\mathbb{C}}$ is only a real vector space. However, we can make $V^{\mathbb{C}}$ into a complex vector space by defining complex multiplication as follows:

$$\alpha(v \otimes \beta) = v\otimes(\alpha\beta)\qquad\mbox{ for all } v\in V \mbox{ and }\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{C}.$$

More generally, complexification is an example of [extension of scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_of_scalars) – here extending scalars from the real numbers to the complex numbers – which can be done for any [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634), or indeed for any morphism of rings.

If $V$ has finite dimension $n$ over $\mathbb{R}$, then the $V^{\mathbb{C}}$ has dimension $2n$ as a real vector space and dimension $n$ as a complex vector space.

Formally, complexification is a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) $\operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbf{R}} \to \operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbf{C}}$, from the category of real vector spaces to the category of complex vector spaces. This is the [adjoint functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_functor) – specifically the [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) – to the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) $\operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbf{C}} \to \operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbf{R}}$ forgetting the complex structure.

This forgetting of the complex structure of a complex vector space $V$ is called **decomplexification** (or sometimes "**realification**"). The decomplexification of a complex vector space $V$ with basis $e_{\mu}$ removes the possibility of complex multiplication of scalars, thus yielding a real vector space $W_{\mathbb{R}}$ of twice the dimension with a basis $\{e_{\mu}, ie_{\mu}\}.$[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

By the nature of the tensor product, every vector $v$ in $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ can be written uniquely in the form

$$v = v_1\otimes 1 + v_2\otimes i$$

where $v_{1}$ and $v_{2}$ are vectors in $V$. It is a common practice to drop the tensor product symbol and just write

$$v = v_1 + iv_2.\,$$

Multiplication by the complex number $a + i b$ is then given by the usual rule

$$(a+ib)(v_1 + iv_2) = (av_1 - bv_2) + i(bv_1 + av_2).\,$$

We can then regard $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ as the [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_vector_spaces) of two copies of $V$:

$$V^{\mathbb{C}} \cong V \oplus i V$$

with the above rule for multiplication by complex numbers.

There is a natural embedding of $V$ into $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ given by

$$v\mapsto v\otimes 1.$$

The vector space $V$ may then be regarded as a *real* [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of $V^{\mathbf{C}}$. If $V$ has a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $\{e_{i}\}$ (over the field $\mathbf{R}$) then a corresponding basis for $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ is given by $\{ e_{i} \otimes 1 \}$ over the field $\mathbf{C}$. The complex [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) of $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ is therefore equal to the real dimension of $V$:

$$\dim_{\mathbb{C}} V^{\mathbb{C}} = \dim_{\mathbb{R}} V.$$

Alternatively, rather than using tensor products, one can use this direct sum as the *definition* of the complexification:

$$V^{\mathbb{C}} := V \oplus V,$$

where $V^{\mathbb{C}}$ is given a [linear complex structure](wikipedia://en/1549805) by the operator $J$ defined as $J(v,w) := (-w,v),$ where $J$ encodes the operation of “multiplication by $i$”. In matrix form, $J$ is given by:

$$J = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -I_V \\ I_V & 0\end{bmatrix}.$$

This yields the identical space – a real vector space with linear complex structure is identical data to a complex vector space – though it constructs the space differently. Accordingly, $V^{\mathbb{C}}$ can be written as $V \oplus JV$ or $V \oplus i V,$ identifying $V$ with the first direct summand. This approach is more concrete, and has the advantage of avoiding the use of the technically involved tensor product, but is ad hoc.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The complexification of [real coordinate space](wikipedia://en/1482083) $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ is the [complex coordinate space](wikipedia://en/39125683) $\mathbf{C}^{n}$.
- Likewise, if $V$ consists of the $m\times n$ [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with real entries, $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ would consist of $m\times n$ matrices with complex entries.

:::

## Dickson doubling {#Dickson_doubling}

The process of complexification by moving from $\mathbf{R}$ to $\mathbf{C}$ was abstracted by twentieth-century mathematicians including [Leonard Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Dickson). One starts with using the [identity mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_mapping) $x* = x$ as a trivial [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) on $\mathbf{R}$. Next two copies of **R** are used to form $z = (a , b)$ with the [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) introduced as the involution $z* = (a, -b)$. Two elements $w$ and $z$ in the doubled set multiply by

$$w z = (a,b) \times (c,d) = (ac\ - \ d^*b,\ da \ + \ b c^*).$$

Finally, the doubled set is given a **norm** $N(z) = z* z$. When starting from $\mathbf{R}$ with the identity involution, the doubled set is $\mathbf{C}$ with the norm $a^{2} + b^{2}$. If one doubles $\mathbf{C}$, and uses conjugation (*a*, *b*)\* = (*a*\*, –*b*), the construction yields [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440). Doubling again produces [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436), also called Cayley numbers. It was at this point that Dickson in 1919 contributed to uncovering [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364).

The process can also be initiated with $\mathbf{C}$ and the trivial involution $z* = z$. The norm produced is simply $z^{2}$, unlike the generation of $\mathbf{C}$ by doubling $\mathbf{R}$. When this $\mathbf{C}$ is doubled it produces [bicomplex numbers](wikipedia://en/4802961), and doubling that produces [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070), and doubling again results in [bioctonions](wikipedia://en/51005149). When the base algebra is associative, the algebra produced by this [Cayley–Dickson construction](wikipedia://en/175609) is called a [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635) since it can be shown that it has the property

$$N(p\,q) = N(p)\,N(q)\,.$$

## Complex conjugation {#Complex_conjugation}

The complexified vector space $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ has more structure than an ordinary complex vector space. It comes with a [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) map:

$$\chi : V^{\mathbb{C}} \to \overline{V^{\mathbb{C}}}$$

defined by

$$\chi(v\otimes z) = v\otimes \bar z.$$

The map $\chi$ may either be regarded as a [conjugate-linear map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate-linear_map) from $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ to itself or as a complex linear [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) from $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ to its [complex conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugate_vector_space) $\overline {V^{\mathbb{C}}}$.

Conversely, given a complex vector space $W$ with a complex conjugation $\chi$, $W$ is isomorphic as a complex vector space to the complexification $V^{\mathbf{C}}$ of the real subspace

$$V = \{ w \in W : \chi(w) = w \}.$$

In other words, all complex vector spaces with complex conjugation are the complexification of a real vector space.

For example, when $W = \mathbf{C}^{n}$ with the standard complex conjugation

$$\chi(z_1,\ldots,z_n) = (\bar z_1,\ldots,\bar z_n)$$

the [invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826) $V$ is just the real subspace $\mathbf{R}^{n}$.

## Linear transformations {#Linear_transformations}

Given a real [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) $f : V \to W$ between two real vector spaces there is a natural complex linear transformation

$$f^{\mathbb{C}} : V^{\mathbb{C}} \to W^{\mathbb{C}}$$

given by

$$f^{\mathbb{C}}(v\otimes z) = f(v)\otimes z.$$

The map $f^{\mathbb{C}}$ is called the **complexification** of *f*. The complexification of linear transformations satisfies the following properties

- $$(\mathrm{id}_V)^{\mathbb{C}} = \mathrm{id}_{V^{\mathbb{C}}}$$
- $$(f \circ g)^{\mathbb{C}} = f^{\mathbb{C}} \circ g^{\mathbb{C}}$$
- $$(f+g)^{\mathbb{C}} = f^{\mathbb{C}} + g^{\mathbb{C}}$$
- $$(a f)^{\mathbb{C}} = a f^{\mathbb{C}} \quad \forall a \in \mathbb{R}$$

In the language of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) one says that complexification defines an ([additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_functor)) [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the [category of real vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_spaces) to the category of complex vector spaces.

The map $f^{\mathbf{C}}$ commutes with conjugation and so maps the real subspace of *V*${}^{\mathbf{C}}$ to the real subspace of $W^{\mathbf{C}}$ (via the map $f$). Moreover, a complex linear map $g : V^{\mathbf{C}} \to W^{\mathbf{C}}$ is the complexification of a real linear map [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it commutes with conjugation.

As an example consider a linear transformation from $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ to $\mathbf{R}^{m}$ thought of as an $m\times n$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859). The complexification of that transformation is exactly the same matrix, but now thought of as a linear map from $\mathbf{C}^{n}$ to $\mathbf{C}^{m}$.

## Dual spaces and tensor products {#Dual_spaces_and_tensor_products}

The [dual](wikipedia://en/7988) of a real vector space $V$ is the space $V*$ of all real linear maps from $V$ to $\mathbf{R}$. The complexification of $V*$ can naturally be thought of as the space of all real linear maps from $V$ to $\mathbf{C}$ (denoted $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{R}}(V,\mathbf{C})$). That is,

$$(V^*)^{\mathbb{C}} = V^*\otimes \mathbb{C} \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(V,\mathbb{C}).$$

The isomorphism is given by

$$(\varphi_1\otimes 1 + \varphi_2\otimes i) \leftrightarrow \varphi_1 + i \varphi_2$$

where $\varphi_{1}$ and $\varphi_{2}$ are elements of $V*$. Complex conjugation is then given by the usual operation

$$\overline{\varphi_1 + i\varphi_2} = \varphi_1 - i \varphi_2.$$

Given a real linear map $\varphi : V \to \mathbf{C}$ we may [extend by linearity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extend_by_linearity) to obtain a complex linear map $\varphi : V^{\mathbf{C}} \to \mathbf{C}$. That is,

$$\varphi(v\otimes z) = z\varphi(v).$$

This extension gives an isomorphism from $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{R}}(V,\mathbf{C})$ to $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{C}}(V^{\mathbf{C}},\mathbf{C})$. The latter is just the *complex* dual space to $V^{\mathbf{C}}$, so we have a [natural isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism):

$$(V^*)^{\mathbb{C}} \cong (V^{\mathbb{C}})^*.$$

More generally, given real vector spaces $V$ and $W$ there is a natural isomorphism

$$\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(V,W)^{\mathbb{C}} \cong \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(V^{\mathbb{C}},W^{\mathbb{C}}).$$

Complexification also commutes with the operations of taking [tensor products](wikipedia://en/30844), [exterior powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power) and [symmetric powers](wikipedia://en/57047243). For example, if $V$ and $W$ are real vector spaces there is a natural isomorphism

$$(V \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} W)^{\mathbb{C}} \cong V^{\mathbb{C}} \otimes_{\mathbb{C}} W^{\mathbb{C}}\,.$$

Note the left-hand tensor product is taken over the reals while the right-hand one is taken over the complexes. The same pattern is true in general. For instance, one has

$$(\Lambda_{\mathbb{R}}^k V)^{\mathbb{C}} \cong \Lambda_{\mathbb{C}}^k (V^{\mathbb{C}}).$$

In all cases, the isomorphisms are the “obvious” ones.
