# Trend-stationary process

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trend-stationary process"}

In the [statistical analysis](wikipedia://en/26685) of [time series](wikipedia://en/406624), a **trend-stationary process** is a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) from which an underlying trend (function solely of time) can be [removed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trend_estimation), leaving a [stationary process](wikipedia://en/329898). The trend does not have to be linear.

:::

Conversely, if the process requires differencing to be made stationary, then it is called [difference stationary](wikipedia://en/2398969) and possesses one or more [unit roots](wikipedia://en/2398969).[^o2][^o3] Those two concepts may sometimes be confused, but while they share many properties, they are different in many aspects. It is possible for a time series to be non-stationary, yet have no unit root and be trend-stationary. In both unit root and trend-stationary processes, the mean can be growing or decreasing over time; however, in the presence of a shock, trend-stationary processes are mean-reverting (i.e. transitory, the time series will converge again towards the growing mean, which was not affected by the shock) while unit-root processes have a permanent impact on the mean (i.e. no convergence over time).[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A process {*Y*} is said to be trend-stationary if[^o8]

$$Y_t = f(t) + e_t,$$

where *t* is time, *f* is any function mapping from the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438) to the reals, and {*e*} is a stationary process. The value $f(t)$ is said to be the trend value of the process at time *t*.

:::

## Simplest example: stationarity around a linear trend {#Simplest_example:_stationarity_around_a_linear_trend}

Suppose the variable *Y* evolves according to

$$Y_t = a \cdot t + b + e_t$$

where *t* is time and *e*<sub>*t*</sub> is the error term, which is hypothesized to be [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182) or more generally to have been generated by any stationary process. Then one can use[^o8][^o9][^o10] [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) to obtain an estimate $\hat{a}$ of the true underlying trend slope $a$ and an estimate $\hat{b}$ of the underlying intercept term *b*; if the estimate $\hat{a}$ is significantly different from zero, this is sufficient to show with high confidence that the variable *Y* is non-stationary. The [residuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics) from this regression are given by

$$\hat{e}_t = Y_t - \hat{a} \cdot t - \hat{b}.$$

If these estimated residuals can be statistically shown to be stationary (more precisely, if one can reject the hypothesis that the true underlying errors are non-stationary), then the residuals are referred to as the **detrended** data,[^o11] and the original series {*Y*<sub>*t*</sub>} is said to be trend-stationary even though it is not stationary.

## Stationarity around other types of trend {#Stationarity_around_other_types_of_trend}

### Exponential growth trend {#Exponential_growth_trend}

Many economic time series are characterized by [exponential growth](wikipedia://en/191933). For example, suppose that one hypothesizes that [gross domestic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) is characterized by stationary deviations from a trend involving a constant growth rate. Then it could be modeled as

$$\text{GDP}_t = Be^{at}U_t$$

with U<sub>t</sub> being hypothesized to be a stationary error process. To estimate the parameters $a$ and *B*, one first takes[^o11] the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) (ln) of both sides of this equation:

$$\ln (\text{GDP}_t) = \ln B + at + \ln (U_t).$$

This [log-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-linear_modeling) equation is in the same form as the previous linear trend equation and can be detrended in the same way, giving the estimated $(\ln U)_t$ as the detrended value of $(\ln \text{GDP})_t$, and hence the implied $U_t$ as the detrended value of $\text{GDP}_t$, assuming one can reject the hypothesis that $(\ln U)_t$ is non-stationary.

### Quadratic trend {#Quadratic_trend}

Trends do not have to be linear or log-linear. For example, a variable could have a quadratic trend:

$$Y_t = a \cdot t + c \cdot t^2 + b + e_t.$$

This can be regressed linearly in the coefficients using *t* and *t*<sup>2</sup> as regressors; again, if the residuals are shown to be stationary then they are the detrended values of $Y_t$.
