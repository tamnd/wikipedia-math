# Bayesian search theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bayesian search theory"}

**Bayesian search theory** is the application of [Bayesian statistics](wikipedia://en/404412) to the search for lost objects. It has been used several times to find sunken ships, for example [USS *Scorpion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Scorpion_%28SSN-589%29), and played a key role in the recovery of the flight recorders in the [Air France Flight 447](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_France_Flight_447) disaster of 2009. It has also been used in the attempts to locate the remains of [Malaysia Airlines Flight 370](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_370).[^o2][^o3][^o4]

:::

## Procedure {#Procedure}

The usual procedure is as follows:

1. Formulate as many reasonable hypotheses as possible about what may have happened to the object.
1. For each hypothesis, construct a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) for the location of the object.
1. Construct a function giving the probability of actually finding an object in location X when searching there if it really is in location X. In an ocean search, this is usually a function of water depth — in shallow water chances of finding an object are good if the search is in the right place. In deep water chances are reduced.
1. Combine the above information coherently to produce an overall probability density map. (Usually this simply means multiplying the two functions together.) This gives the probability of finding the object by looking in location X, for all possible locations X. (This can be visualized as a [contour map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_map) of probability.)
1. Construct a search path which starts at the point of highest probability and 'scans' over high probability areas, then intermediate probabilities, and finally low probability areas.
1. Revise all the probabilities continuously during the search. For example, if the hypotheses for location X imply the likely disintegration of the object and the search at location X has yielded no fragments, then the probability that the object is somewhere around there is greatly reduced (though not usually to zero) while the probabilities of its being at other locations is correspondingly increased. The revision process is done by applying [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569).

In other words, first search where it most probably will be found, then search where finding it is less probable, then search where the probability is even less (but still possible due to limitations on fuel, range, water currents, etc.), until insufficient hope of locating the object at acceptable cost remains.

The advantages of the Bayesian method are that all information available is used coherently (i.e., in a "leak-proof" manner) and the method automatically produces estimates of the cost for a given success probability. That is, even before the start of searching, one can say, hypothetically, "there is a 65% chance of finding it in a 5-day search. That probability will rise to 90% after a 10-day search and 97% after 15 days" or a similar statement. Thus the economic viability of the search can be estimated before committing resources to a search.

Apart from the [USS *Scorpion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Scorpion_%28SSN-589%29), other vessels located by Bayesian search theory include the [MV *Derbyshire*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MV_Derbyshire), the largest British vessel ever lost at sea, and the [SS *Central America*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Central_America). It also proved successful in the search for a lost [hydrogen bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bomb) following the [1966 Palomares B-52 crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_Palomares_B-52_crash) in Spain,[^o7] and the recovery in the Atlantic Ocean of the crashed [Air France Flight 447](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_France_Flight_447).

Bayesian search theory is incorporated into the CASP (Computer Assisted Search Program) mission planning software used by the [United States Coast Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Coast_Guard) for [search and rescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_and_rescue). This program was later adapted for inland search by adding terrain and ground cover factors for use by the [United States Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force) and [Civil Air Patrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Air_Patrol).

## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

Suppose a grid square has a probability *p* of containing the wreck and that the probability of successfully detecting the wreck if it is there is *q*. If the square is searched and no wreck is found, then, by Bayes' theorem, the revised probability of the wreck being in the square is given by

$$p' = \frac{p(1-q)}{(1-p)+p(1-q)} = p \frac{1-q}{1-pq} < p.$$

For every other grid square, if its prior probability is *r*, its posterior probability is given by

$$r' = r \frac{1}{1- pq} > r.$$

### USS *Scorpion* {#USS_Scorpion}

In May 1968, the [U.S. Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Navy)'s [nuclear submarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_submarine) [USS *Scorpion* (SSN-589)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Scorpion_%28SSN-589%29) failed to arrive as expected at her home port of [Norfolk, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norfolk,_Virginia). The command officers of the U.S. Navy were nearly certain that the vessel had been lost off the [Eastern Seaboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Coast_of_the_United_States), but an extensive search there failed to discover the remains of *Scorpion*.

Then, a Navy deep-water expert, [John P. Craven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Craven_USN), suggested that *Scorpion* had sunk elsewhere. Craven organised a search southwest of the [Azores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azores) based on a controversial approximate triangulation by [hydrophones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophone). He was allocated only a single ship, [*Mizar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USNS_Mizar_%28AGOR-11%29), and he took advice from Metron Inc., a firm of consultant mathematicians in order to maximise his resources. A Bayesian search methodology was adopted. Experienced submarine commanders were interviewed to construct hypotheses about what could have caused the loss of *Scorpion*.

The sea area was divided up into grid squares and a probability assigned to each square, under each of the hypotheses, to give a number of probability grids, one for each hypothesis. These were then added together to produce an overall probability grid. The probability attached to each square was then the probability that the wreck was in that square. A second grid was constructed with probabilities that represented the probability of successfully finding the wreck if that square were to be searched and the wreck were to be actually there. This was a known function of water depth. The result of combining this grid with the previous grid is a grid which gives the probability of finding the wreck in each grid square of the sea if it were to be searched.

At the end of October 1968, the Navy's oceanographic research ship, Mizar, located sections of the hull of *Scorpion* on the seabed, about 740 km southwest of the [Azores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azores),[^o10] under more than 3000 m of water. This was after the Navy had released sound tapes from its underwater "[SOSUS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SOSUS)" listening system, which contained the sounds of the destruction of *Scorpion*. The court of inquiry was subsequently reconvened and other vessels, including the [bathyscaphe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathyscaphe) [*Trieste II*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathyscaphe_Trieste_II), were dispatched to the scene, collecting many pictures and other data.

Although Craven received much credit for locating the wreckage of *Scorpion*, Gordon Hamilton, an acoustics expert who pioneered the use of [hydroacoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydroacoustics) to pinpoint Polaris missile splashdown locations, was instrumental in defining a compact "search box" wherein the wreck was ultimately found. Hamilton had established a listening station in the Canary Islands that obtained a clear signal of what some scientists believe was the noise of the vessel's pressure hull imploding as she passed [crush depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crush_depth). A [Naval Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Research_Laboratory) scientist named Chester "Buck" Buchanan, using a towed camera sled of his own design aboard *Mizar*, finally located *Scorpion*.[^o10] The towed camera sled, which was fabricated by J. L. "Jac" Hamm of Naval Research Laboratory's Engineering Services Division, is housed in the [National Museum of the United States Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_the_United_States_Navy). Buchanan had located the wrecked hull of [*Thresher*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Thresher_%28SSN-593%29) in 1964 using this technique.

## Searching in boxes {#Searching_in_boxes}

Assume that a stationary object is hidden in one of n boxes (locations). For each location $i$ there are three known parameters: the cost $c_i$ of a single search, the probability $a_i$ of finding the object by a single search if the object is there, and the probability $p_i$ that the object is there. A searcher looks for the object. They know the a priori probabilities at the beginning and update them by Bayes' law after each (unsuccessful) attempt.

The problem of finding the object in minimal expected cost is a classical problem solved by [David Blackwell](wikipedia://en/2202900); later, [David Assaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Assaf) expanded Blackwell's result to more than one object.[^o14] The optimal policy is: at each stage, look into the location which maximizes $\frac{p_i a_i}{c_i}$. This is a special case of the [Gittins index](wikipedia://en/9267447).
