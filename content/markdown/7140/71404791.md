# Thomas Bloom

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Thomas Bloom"}

**Thomas F. Bloom** is a mathematician, who is a [Royal Society University Research Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_University_Research_Fellowship) at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester).[^o3] He works in [arithmetic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/18243381) and [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513).

:::

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Thomas did his undergraduate degree in Mathematics and Philosophy at [Merton College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College,_Oxford). He then went on to do his PhD in mathematics at the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol) under the supervision of [Trevor Wooley](wikipedia://en/4651458). After finishing his PhD, he was a Heilbronn Research Fellow at the University of Bristol. In 2018, he became a postdoctoral research fellow at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) with [Timothy Gowers](wikipedia://en/365715). In 2021, he joined the University of Oxford as a Research Fellow.[^o5] Then, in 2024, he moved to the University of Manchester, where he also took on a Research Fellow position.

## Research {#Research}

In July 2020, Bloom and Sisask[^o7] proved that any $A \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ such that $\sum_{n \in A} \frac{1}{n}$ diverges must contain arithmetic progressions of length 3. This is the first non-trivial case of a [conjecture](wikipedia://en/4312009) of [Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) postulating that any such set must in fact contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.[^o8][^o9]

In November 2020, in joint work with [James Maynard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Maynard),[^o10] he improved the best-known bound for [square-difference-free sets](wikipedia://en/52290066), showing that a set $A \subset \{1,...,N\}$ with no square difference has size at most $\frac{N}{(\log N)^{c\log \log\log N}}$ for some $c>0$.

In December 2021, he proved [^o11] that any set $A \subset \mathbb{N}$ of positive upper density contains a finite $S \subset A$ such that $\sum_{n \in S} \frac{1}{n}=1$.[^o12] This answered a question of Erdős and Graham.[^o13]
