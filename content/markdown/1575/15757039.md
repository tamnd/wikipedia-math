# Z-group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Z-group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially in the area of [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) known as [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the term **Z-group** refers to a number of distinct types of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447):

:::

- in the study of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707), a **Z-group** is a finite group whose [Sylow subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_subgroup) are all [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327).
- in the study of [infinite groups](wikipedia://en/4587030), a **Z-group** is a group which possesses a very general form of [central series](wikipedia://en/15202655).
- in the study of [ordered groups](wikipedia://en/349014), a **Z-group** or **$\mathbb Z$-group** is a discretely ordered abelian group whose quotient over its minimal [convex subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_subgroup) is divisible. Such groups are [elementarily equivalent](wikipedia://en/972601) to the integers $(\mathbb Z,+,<)$. Z-groups are an alternative presentation of [Presburger arithmetic](wikipedia://en/23756).
- occasionally, **(Z)-group** is used to mean a [Zassenhaus group](wikipedia://en/1724820), a special type of [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634).

## Groups whose Sylow subgroups are cyclic {#Groups_whose_Sylow_subgroups_are_cyclic}

*Usage: (Suzuki 1955), (Bender Glauberman 1994, 2), , (Wonenburger 1976), (Çelik 1976)*

In the study of [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707), a **Z-group** is a finite group whose [Sylow subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_subgroup) are all [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327). The Z originates both from the German *Zyklische* and from their classification in (Zassenhaus 1935). In many standard textbooks these groups have no special name, other than **metacyclic groups**, but that term is often used more generally today. See [metacyclic group](wikipedia://en/7515561) for more on the general, modern definition which includes non-cyclic [*p*-groups](wikipedia://en/24664); see (Hall 1959, Th. 9.4.3) for the stricter, classical definition more closely related to Z-groups.

Every group whose Sylow subgroups are cyclic is itself [metacyclic](wikipedia://en/7515561), so [supersolvable](wikipedia://en/4966866). In fact, such a group has a cyclic [derived subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_subgroup) with cyclic maximal abelian quotient. Such a group has the presentation (Hall 1959, Th. 9.4.3):

$G(m,n,r) = \langle a,b | a^m = b^n = 1, bab^{-1} = a^r \rangle$, where *mn* is the order of *G*(*m*,*n*,*r*), the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354), gcd((*r*-1)*n*, *m*) = 1, and *r*<sup>*n*</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *m*).

The [character theory](wikipedia://en/691741) of Z-groups is well understood (Çelik 1976), as they are [monomial groups](wikipedia://en/16388251).

The derived length of a Z-group is at most 2, so Z-groups may be insufficient for some uses. A generalization due to Hall are the [A-groups](wikipedia://en/4938898), those groups with [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) Sylow subgroups. These groups behave similarly to Z-groups, but can have arbitrarily large derived length (Hall 1940). Another generalization due to (Suzuki 1955) allows the Sylow 2-subgroup more flexibility, including [dihedral](wikipedia://en/100267) and [generalized quaternion groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_quaternion_group).

## Group with a generalized central series {#Group_with_a_generalized_central_series}

*Usage: (Robinson 1996), (Kurosh 1960)*

The definition of [central series](wikipedia://en/15202655) used for **Z-group** is somewhat technical. A **series** of *G* is a collection *S* of subgroups of *G*, linearly ordered by inclusion, such that for every *g* in *G*, the subgroups *A*<sub>*g*</sub> = ∩ { *N* in *S* : *g* in *N* } and *B*<sub>*g*</sub> = ∪ { *N* in *S* : *g* not in *N* } are both in *S*. A (generalized) **central series** of *G* is a series such that every *N* in *S* is normal in *G* and such that for every *g* in *G*, the quotient *A*<sub>*g*</sub>/*B*<sub>*g*</sub> is contained in the center of *G*/*B*<sub>*g*</sub>. A **Z**-group is a group with such a (generalized) central series. Examples include the [hypercentral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercentral_group) whose transfinite [upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series) form such a central series, as well as the [hypocentral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypocentral_group) whose transfinite lower central series form such a central series (Robinson 1996).

## Special 2-transitive groups {#Special_2-transitive_groups}

*Usage: (Suzuki 1961)*

A **(Z)-group** is a group faithfully represented as a [doubly transitive permutation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_transitive_permutation_group) in which no non-identity element fixes more than two points. A **(ZT)-group** is a (Z)-group that is of odd degree and not a [Frobenius group](wikipedia://en/1716123), that is a [Zassenhaus group](wikipedia://en/1724820) of odd degree, also known as one of the groups [PSL(2,2<sup>*k*+1</sup>)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) or [Sz(2<sup>2*k*+1</sup>)](wikipedia://en/1691490#Suzuki–Ree_groups), for *k* any positive integer (Suzuki 1961).
