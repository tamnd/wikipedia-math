# Differential topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differential topology"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **differential topology** is the field dealing with the [topological properties](wikipedia://en/29954) and [smooth properties](wikipedia://en/1542238) of [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold). In this sense differential topology is distinct from the closely related field of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), which concerns the *geometric* properties of smooth manifolds, including notions of size, distance, and rigid shape. By comparison differential topology is concerned with coarser properties, such as the number of holes in a manifold, its [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type), or the structure of its [diffeomorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism_group). Because many of these coarser properties may be captured [algebraically](wikipedia://en/19616384), differential topology has strong links to [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).[^o2]

*Image: 3D-Cylinder with handle and torus with hole.png.* The [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) of the height function on a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) can describe its [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type).

The central goal of the field of differential topology is the [classification](wikipedia://en/4576710) of all smooth manifolds up to [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564). Since dimension is an invariant of smooth manifolds up to diffeomorphism type, this classification is often studied by classifying the ([connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_%28topology%29)) manifolds in each dimension separately:

:::

- In dimension 1, the only smooth manifolds up to diffeomorphism are the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), the [real number line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number_line), and allowing a [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824), the half-closed [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[0,1)$ and fully closed interval $[0,1]$.[^o3]
- In dimension 2, every [closed surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_surface) is classified up to diffeomorphism by its [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_%28topology%29), the number of holes (or equivalently its [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420)), and whether or not it is [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable). This is the famous [classification of closed surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865#Classification_of_closed_surfaces).[^o4][^o5] Already in dimension two the classification of non-[compact](wikipedia://en/6042) surfaces becomes difficult, due to the existence of exotic spaces such as [Jacob's ladder](wikipedia://en/33413362).
- In dimension 3, [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190)'s [geometrization conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642), proven by [Grigori Perelman](wikipedia://en/225266), gives a partial classification of compact three-manifolds. Included in this theorem is the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798), which states that any closed, [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) three-manifold is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) (and in fact diffeomorphic) to the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792).

*Image: Cobordism.svg.* A [cobordism](wikipedia://en/391832) (*W*; *M*, *N*), which generalises the notion of a diffeomorphism.

Beginning in dimension 4, the classification becomes much more difficult for two reasons.[^o6][^o7] Firstly, every [finitely presented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group) group appears as the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of some [4-manifold](wikipedia://en/1362795), and since the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) is a diffeomorphism invariant, this makes the classification of 4-manifolds at least as difficult as the classification of finitely presented groups. By the [word problem for groups](wikipedia://en/33563), which is equivalent to the [halting problem](wikipedia://en/21391870), it is impossible to classify such groups, so a full topological classification is impossible. Secondly, beginning in dimension four it is possible to have smooth manifolds that are homeomorphic, but with distinct, non-diffeomorphic [smooth structures](wikipedia://en/1542238). This is true even for the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^4$, which admits many [exotic $\mathbb{R}^4$](wikipedia://en/2180637) structures. This means that the study of differential topology in dimensions 4 and higher must use tools genuinely outside the realm of the regular continuous topology of [topological manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119193). One of the central open problems in differential topology is the [four-dimensional smooth Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/8536216), which asks if every smooth 4-manifold that is homeomorphic to the [4-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-sphere), is also diffeomorphic to it. That is, does the 4-sphere admit only one [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1114931)? This conjecture is true in dimensions 1, 2, and 3, by the above classification results, but is known to be false in dimension 7 due to the [Milnor spheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_sphere).

Important tools in studying the differential topology of smooth manifolds include the construction of smooth [topological invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant) of such manifolds, such as [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) or the [intersection form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_form_%284-manifold%29), as well as smoothable topological constructions, such as smooth [surgery theory](wikipedia://en/5961115) or the construction of [cobordisms](wikipedia://en/391832). [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) is an important tool which studies smooth manifolds by considering the [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206) on the manifold, demonstrating how the smooth structure of the manifold enters into the set of tools available.[^o8] Oftentimes more geometric or analytical techniques may be used, by equipping a smooth manifold with a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) or by studying a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) on it. Care must be taken to ensure that the resulting information is insensitive to this choice of extra structure, and so genuinely reflects only the topological properties of the underlying smooth manifold. For example, the [Hodge theorem](wikipedia://en/501590) provides a geometric and analytical interpretation of the de Rham cohomology, and [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/64324611) was used by [Simon Donaldson](wikipedia://en/976305) to prove facts about the intersection form of simply connected 4-manifolds.[^o9] In some cases techniques from contemporary [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) may appear, such as [topological quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/443757), which can be used to compute topological invariants of smooth spaces.

Famous theorems in differential topology include the [Whitney embedding theorem](wikipedia://en/477578), the [hairy ball theorem](wikipedia://en/485168), the [Hopf theorem](wikipedia://en/32796318), the [Poincaré–Hopf theorem](wikipedia://en/1267288), [Donaldson's theorem](wikipedia://en/2180754), and the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798).

## Description {#Description}

Differential topology considers the properties and structures that require only a [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238) on a manifold to be defined. Smooth manifolds are 'softer' than manifolds with extra geometric structures, which can act as obstructions to certain types of equivalences and [deformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) that exist in differential topology. For instance, volume and [Riemannian curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_curvature) are [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638) that can distinguish different geometric structures on the same smooth manifold—that is, one can smoothly "flatten out" certain manifolds, but it might require distorting the space and affecting the curvature or volume.

On the other hand, smooth manifolds are more rigid than the [topological manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119193). [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) discovered that some spheres have more than one smooth structure—see [Exotic sphere](wikipedia://en/1114931) and [Donaldson's theorem](wikipedia://en/2180754). [Michel Kervaire](wikipedia://en/13644049) exhibited topological manifolds with no smooth structure at all.[^o11] Some constructions of smooth manifold theory, such as the existence of [tangent bundles](wikipedia://en/211794),[^o12] can be done in the topological setting with much more work, and others cannot.

One of the main topics in differential topology is the study of special kinds of smooth mappings between manifolds, namely [immersions](wikipedia://en/6993953) and [submersions](wikipedia://en/356413), and the intersections of submanifolds via [transversality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29). More generally one is interested in properties and invariants of smooth manifolds that are carried over by [diffeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphisms), another special kind of smooth mapping. [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) is another branch of differential topology, in which topological information about a manifold is deduced from changes in the [rank](wikipedia://en/7880215) of the [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) of a function.

For a list of differential topology topics, see the following reference: [List of differential geometry topics](wikipedia://en/345396).

## Differential topology versus differential geometry {#Differential_topology_versus_differential_geometry}

Differential topology and differential geometry are first characterized by their *similarity*. They both study primarily the properties of differentiable manifolds, sometimes with a variety of structures imposed on them.

*Image: Mug\_and\_Torus\_morph.gif.* Animation of a coffee cup transforming into a donut shape

One major difference lies in the nature of the problems that each subject tries to address. In one view,[^o5] differential topology distinguishes itself from differential geometry by studying primarily those problems that are *inherently global*. Consider the example of a coffee cup and a donut. From the point of view of differential topology, the donut and the coffee cup are *the same* (in a sense). This is an inherently global view, though, because there is no way for the differential topologist to tell whether the two objects are the same (in this sense) by looking at just a tiny (*local*) piece of either of them. They must have access to each entire (*global*) object.

From the point of view of differential geometry, the coffee cup and the donut are *different* because it is impossible to rotate the coffee cup in such a way that its configuration matches that of the donut. This is also a global way of thinking about the problem. But an important distinction is that the geometer does not need the entire object to decide this. By looking, for instance, at just a tiny piece of the handle, they can decide that the coffee cup is different from the donut because the handle is thinner (or more curved) than any piece of the donut.

To put it succinctly, differential topology studies structures on manifolds that, in a sense, have no interesting local structure. Differential geometry studies structures on manifolds that do have an interesting local (or sometimes even infinitesimal) structure.

More mathematically, for example, the problem of constructing a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) between two manifolds of the same dimension is inherently global since *locally* two such manifolds are always diffeomorphic. Likewise, the problem of computing a quantity on a manifold that is invariant under differentiable mappings is inherently global, since any local invariant will be *trivial* in the sense that it is already exhibited in the topology of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Moreover, differential topology does not restrict itself necessarily to the study of diffeomorphism. For example, [symplectic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_topology)—a subbranch of differential topology—studies global properties of [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356). Differential geometry concerns itself with problems—which may be local *or* global—that always have some non-trivial local properties. Thus differential geometry may study differentiable manifolds equipped with a *[connection](wikipedia://en/308815)*, a *[metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29)* (which may be [Riemannian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric), [pseudo-Riemannian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian_metric), or [Finsler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finsler_metric)), a special sort of *[distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_integration_theorem)* (such as a [CR structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CR_structure)), and so on.

This distinction between differential geometry and differential topology is blurred, however, in questions specifically pertaining to local diffeomorphism invariants such as the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at a point. Differential topology also deals with questions like these, which specifically pertain to the properties of differentiable mappings on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (for example the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794), [jet bundles](wikipedia://en/1521924), the [Whitney extension theorem](wikipedia://en/5456164), and so forth).

The distinction is concise in abstract terms:

- Differential topology is the study of the (infinitesimal, local, and global) properties of structures on manifolds that have *only trivial* [local moduli](wikipedia://en/361609).
- Differential geometry is such a study of structures on manifolds that have one or more *non-trivial* local moduli.
