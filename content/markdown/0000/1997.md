# Algebraic geometry

*Image: Togliatti surface.png.* This [Togliatti surface](wikipedia://en/20705037) is an [algebraic surface](wikipedia://en/648311) of degree five. The picture represents a portion of its real [locus](wikipedia://en/542465).

branches

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic geometry"}

**Algebraic geometry** is a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) which uses [abstract algebraic](wikipedia://en/19616384) techniques, mainly from [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), to solve [geometrical problems](wikipedia://en/18973446). Classically, it studies [zeros](wikipedia://en/264210) of [multivariate polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_polynomial); the modern approach generalizes this in a few different aspects.

:::

The fundamental objects of study in algebraic geometry are [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808), which are geometric manifestations of [solutions](wikipedia://en/567840) of [systems of polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_of_polynomial_equations). Examples of the most studied classes of algebraic varieties are [lines](wikipedia://en/946975), [circles](wikipedia://en/6220), [parabolas](wikipedia://en/23231), [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277), [hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052), [cubic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_curve) like [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225), and quartic curves like [lemniscates](wikipedia://en/250908) and [Cassini ovals](wikipedia://en/2040906). These are [plane algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_algebraic_curve). A point of the plane lies on an algebraic curve if its coordinates satisfy a given [polynomial equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation). Basic questions involve the study of points of special interest like [singular points](wikipedia://en/4230456), [inflection points](wikipedia://en/379845) and [points at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139). More advanced questions involve the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of the curve and the relationship between curves defined by different equations.

Algebraic geometry occupies a central place in modern mathematics and has multiple conceptual connections with such diverse fields as [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), topology and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). As a study of systems of polynomial equations in several variables, the subject of algebraic geometry begins with finding specific solutions via [equation solving](wikipedia://en/537048), and then proceeds to understand the intrinsic properties of the totality of solutions of a system of equations. This understanding requires both conceptual theory and computational technique.

In the 20th century, algebraic geometry split into several subareas.

- The mainstream of algebraic geometry is devoted to the study of the complex points of the algebraic varieties and more generally to the points with coordinates in an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018).
- [Real algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/7252198) is the study of the real algebraic varieties.
- [Diophantine geometry](wikipedia://en/502205) and, more generally, [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177) is the study of algebraic varieties over [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) that are not algebraically closed and, specifically, over fields of interest in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), such as the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field), [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615), [function fields](wikipedia://en/447645), and [*p*-adic fields](wikipedia://en/51423).
- A large part of [singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763#Singularities_in_algebraic_geometry) is devoted to the singularities of algebraic varieties.
- Computational algebraic geometry is an area that has emerged at the intersection of algebraic geometry and [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805), with the rise of computers. It consists mainly of [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) design and [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) development for the study of properties of explicitly given algebraic varieties.

Much of the development of mainstream algebraic geometry in the 20th century occurred within an abstract algebraic framework, with increasing emphasis being placed on "intrinsic" properties of algebraic varieties not dependent on any particular way of embedding the variety in an ambient coordinate space; this parallels developments in topology, [differential](wikipedia://en/8625) and [complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101). One key achievement of this abstract algebraic geometry is [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck)'s [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory) which allows one to use [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory) to study algebraic varieties in a way which is very similar to its use in the study of [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_manifold) and [analytic manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742). This is obtained by extending the notion of point: In classical algebraic geometry, a point of an affine variety may be identified, through [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215), with a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring), while the points of the corresponding affine scheme are all prime ideals of this ring. This means that a point of such a scheme may be either a usual point or a sub-variety. This approach also enables a unification of the language and the tools of classical algebraic geometry, mainly concerned with complex points, and of algebraic number theory. [Wiles' proof](wikipedia://en/21950759) of the longstanding conjecture called [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) is an example of the power of this approach.

## Basic notions {#Basic_notions}

### Zeros of simultaneous polynomials {#Zeros_of_simultaneous_polynomials}

*Image: Slanted circle.png.* Sphere and slanted circle

In classical algebraic geometry, the main objects of interest are the vanishing sets of collections of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), meaning the set of all points that simultaneously satisfy one or more [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_of_polynomial_equations). For instance, the [two-dimensional](wikipedia://en/39782) [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) of radius 1 in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>3</sup> could be defined as the set of all points $(x, y, z)$ with

$$x^2+y^2+z^2-1=0.\,$$

A "slanted" circle in **R**<sup>3</sup> can be defined as the set of all points $(x, y, z)$ which satisfy the two polynomial equations

$$x^2+y^2+z^2-1=0,\,$$

$$x+y+z=0.\,$$

### Affine varieties {#Affine_varieties}

First we start with a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k*. In classical algebraic geometry, this field was always the complex numbers **C**, but many of the same results are true if we assume only that *k* is [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018). We consider the [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) of dimension *n* over *k*, denoted **A**<sup>n</sup>(*k*) (or more simply **A**<sup>*n*</sup>, when *k* is clear from the context). When one fixes a coordinate system, one may identify **A**<sup>n</sup>(*k*) with *k*<sup>*n*</sup>. The purpose of not working with *k*<sup>*n*</sup> is to emphasize that one "forgets" the vector space structure that *k*<sup>n</sup> carries.

A function *f* : **A**<sup>*n*</sup> → **A**<sup>1</sup> is said to be *polynomial* (or *regular*) if it can be written as a polynomial, that is, if there is a polynomial *p* in *k*\[*x*<sub>1</sub>,...,*x*<sub>*n*</sub>\] such that *f*(*M*) = *p*(*t*<sub>1</sub>,...,*t*<sub>*n*</sub>) for every point *M* with coordinates (*t*<sub>1</sub>,...,*t*<sub>*n*</sub>) in **A**<sup>*n*</sup>. The property of a function to be polynomial (or regular) does not depend on the choice of a coordinate system in **A**<sup>*n*</sup>.

When a coordinate system is chosen, the regular functions on the affine *n*-space may be identified with the ring of [polynomial functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function) in *n* variables over *k*. Therefore, the set of the regular functions on **A**<sup>*n*</sup> is a ring, which is denoted *k*\[**A**<sup>*n*</sup>\].

We say that a polynomial *vanishes* at a point if evaluating it at that point gives zero. Let *S* be a set of polynomials in *k*\[**A**<sup>n</sup>\]. The *vanishing set of S* (or *vanishing locus* or *zero set*) is the set *V*(*S*) of all points in **A**<sup>*n*</sup> where every polynomial in *S* vanishes. Symbolically,

$$V(S) = \{(t_1,\dots,t_n) \mid p(t_1,\dots,t_n) = 0 \text{ for all } p \in S\}.\,$$

A subset of **A**<sup>*n*</sup> which is *V*(*S*), for some *S*, is called an *algebraic set*. The *V* stands for *variety* (a specific type of algebraic set to be defined below).

Given a subset *U* of **A**<sup>*n*</sup>, can one recover the set of polynomials which generate it? If *U* is *any* subset of **A**<sup>*n*</sup>, define *I*(*U*) to be the set of all polynomials whose vanishing set contains *U*. The *I* stands for [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977): if two polynomials *f* and *g* both vanish on *U*, then *f*+*g* vanishes on *U*, and if *h* is any polynomial, then *hf* vanishes on *U*, so *I*(*U*) is always an ideal of the polynomial ring *k*\[**A**<sup>*n*</sup>\].

Two natural questions to ask are:

- Given a subset *U* of **A**<sup>*n*</sup>, when is *U* = *V*(*I*(*U*))?
- Given a set *S* of polynomials, when is *S* = *I*(*V*(*S*))?

The answer to the first question is provided by introducing the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744), a topology on **A**<sup>*n*</sup> whose closed sets are the algebraic sets, and which directly reflects the algebraic structure of *k*\[**A**<sup>*n*</sup>\]. Then *U* = *V*(*I*(*U*)) if and only if *U* is an algebraic set or equivalently a Zariski-closed set. The answer to the second question is given by [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215). In one of its forms, it says that *I*(*V*(*S*)) is the [radical](wikipedia://en/252009) of the ideal generated by *S*. In more abstract language, there is a [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411), giving rise to two [closure operators](wikipedia://en/483120); they can be identified, and naturally play a basic role in the theory; the [example](wikipedia://en/156411#Examples) is elaborated at Galois connection.

For various reasons we may not always want to work with the entire ideal corresponding to an algebraic set *U*. [Hilbert's basis theorem](wikipedia://en/13733) implies that ideals in *k*\[**A**<sup>*n*</sup>\] are always finitely generated.

An algebraic set is called *[irreducible](wikipedia://en/2652476)* if it cannot be written as the union of two smaller algebraic sets. Any algebraic set is a finite union of irreducible algebraic sets and this decomposition is unique. Thus its elements are called the *irreducible components* of the algebraic set. An irreducible algebraic set is also called a *[variety](wikipedia://en/248808)*. It turns out that an algebraic set is a variety if and only if it may be defined as the vanishing set of a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) of the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065).

Some authors do not make a clear distinction between algebraic sets and varieties and use *irreducible variety* to make the distinction when needed.

### Regular functions {#Regular_functions}

Just as [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) are the natural maps on [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) and [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) are the natural maps on [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219), there is a natural class of functions on an algebraic set, called *regular functions* or *polynomial functions*. A regular function on an algebraic set *V* contained in **A**<sup>*n*</sup> is the restriction to *V* of a regular function on **A**<sup>*n*</sup>. For an algebraic set defined on the field of the complex numbers, the regular functions are smooth and even [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478).

It may seem unnaturally restrictive to require that a regular function always extend to the ambient space, but it is very similar to the situation in a [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), where the [Tietze extension theorem](wikipedia://en/31404) guarantees that a continuous function on a closed subset always extends to the ambient topological space.

Just as with the regular functions on affine space, the regular functions on *V* form a ring, which we denote by *k*\[*V*\]. This ring is called the *[coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of V*.

Since regular functions on V come from regular functions on **A**<sup>*n*</sup>, there is a relationship between the coordinate rings. Specifically, if a regular function on *V* is the restriction of two functions *f* and *g* in *k*\[**A**<sup>*n*</sup>\], then *f* − *g* is a polynomial function which is null on *V* and thus belongs to *I*(*V*). Thus *k*\[*V*\] may be identified with *k*\[**A**<sup>*n*</sup>\]/*I*(*V*).

### Morphism of affine varieties {#Morphism_of_affine_varieties}

Using regular functions from an affine variety to **A**<sup>1</sup>, we can define [regular maps](wikipedia://en/24457573) from one affine variety to another. First we will define a regular map from a variety into affine space: Let *V* be a variety contained in **A**<sup>*n*</sup>. Choose *m* regular functions on *V*, and call them *f*<sub>1</sub>, . . ., *f*<sub>*m*</sub>. We define a *regular map* *f* from *V* to **A**<sup>*m*</sup> by letting *f* = (*f*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*m*</sub>). In other words, each *f*<sub>*i*</sub> determines one coordinate of the [range](wikipedia://en/579311) of *f*.

If *V*′ is a variety contained in **A**<sup>*m*</sup>, we say that *f* is a *regular map* from *V* to *V*′ if the range of *f* is contained in *V*′.

The definition of the regular maps apply also to algebraic sets. The regular maps are also called *morphisms*, as they make the collection of all affine algebraic sets into a [category](wikipedia://en/5869), where the objects are the affine algebraic sets and the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) are the regular maps. The affine varieties is a subcategory of the category of the algebraic sets.

Given a regular map *g* from *V* to *V*′ and a regular function *f* of *k*\[*V*′\], then *f* ∘ *g* ∈ *k*\[*V*\]. The map *f* → *f* ∘ *g* is a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from *k*\[*V*′\] to *k*\[*V*\]. Conversely, every ring homomorphism from *k*\[*V*′\] to *k*\[*V*\] defines a regular map from *V* to *V*′. This defines an [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051) between the category of algebraic sets and the [opposite category](wikipedia://en/310885) of the finitely generated [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749) *k*-algebras. This equivalence is one of the starting points of [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory).

### Rational function and birational equivalence {#Rational_function_and_birational_equivalence}

In contrast to the preceding sections, this section concerns only varieties and not algebraic sets. On the other hand, the definitions extend naturally to projective varieties (next section), as an affine variety and its projective completion have the same field of functions.

If *V* is an affine variety, its coordinate ring is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) and has thus a [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) which is denoted *k*(*V*) and called the *field of the rational functions* on *V* or, shortly, the *[function field](wikipedia://en/2783843)* of *V*. Its elements are the restrictions to *V* of the [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) over the affine space containing *V*. The [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of a rational function *f* is not *V* but the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of the sub-variety (a hypersurface) where the denominator of *f* vanishes.

As with regular maps, one may define a *rational map* from a variety *V* to a variety *V*'. As with the regular maps, the rational maps from *V* to *V*' may be identified to the [field homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411) from *k*(*V*') to *k*(*V*).

Two affine varieties are *birationally equivalent* if there are two rational functions between them which are [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_inverse) one to the other in the regions where both are defined. Equivalently, they are birationally equivalent if their function fields are isomorphic.

An affine variety is a *[rational variety](wikipedia://en/865939)* if it is birationally equivalent to an affine space. This means that the variety admits a *rational parameterization*, that is a [parametrization](wikipedia://en/453198) with [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210). For example, the circle of equation $x^2+y^2-1=0$ is a rational curve, as it has the [parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108)

$$x=\frac{2\,t}{1+t^2}$$

$$y=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\,,$$

which may also be viewed as a rational map from the line to the circle.

The problem of [resolution of singularities](wikipedia://en/9914115) is to know if every algebraic variety is birationally equivalent to a variety whose projective completion is non-singular (see also [smooth completion](wikipedia://en/34289679)). It was solved in the affirmative in [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 0 by [Heisuke Hironaka](wikipedia://en/13822) in 1964 and is yet unsolved in finite characteristic.

### Projective variety {#Projective_variety}

*Image: Parabola & cubic curve in projective space.png.* Parabola (*y* = *x*<sup>2</sup>, red) and cubic (*y* = *x*<sup>3</sup>, blue) in projective space

Just as the formulas for the roots of second, third, and fourth degree polynomials suggest extending real numbers to the more algebraically complete setting of the complex numbers, many properties of algebraic varieties suggest extending affine space to a more geometrically complete projective space. Whereas the complex numbers are obtained by adding the number *i*, a root of the polynomial *x*<sup>2</sup> + 1, projective space is obtained by adding in appropriate points "at infinity", points where parallel lines may meet.

To see how this might come about, consider the variety *V*(*y* − *x*<sup>2</sup>). If we draw it, we get a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231). As *x* goes to positive infinity, the slope of the line from the origin to the point (*x*, *x*<sup>2</sup>) also goes to positive infinity. As *x* goes to negative infinity, the slope of the same line goes to negative infinity.

Compare this to the variety *V*(*y* − *x*<sup>3</sup>). This is a [cubic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_curve). As *x* goes to positive infinity, the slope of the line from the origin to the point (*x*, *x*<sup>3</sup>) goes to positive infinity just as before. But unlike before, as *x* goes to negative infinity, the slope of the same line goes to positive infinity as well; the exact opposite of the parabola. So the behaviour "at infinity" of *V*(*y* − *x*<sup>3</sup>) is different from the behaviour "at infinity" of *V*(*y* − *x*<sup>2</sup>).

The consideration of the *projective completion* of the two curves, which is their prolongation "at infinity" in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350), allows us to quantify this difference: the point at infinity of the parabola is a [regular point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_point_of_an_algebraic_variety), whose tangent is the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142), while the point at infinity of the cubic curve is a [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758). Also, both curves are rational, as they are parameterized by *x*, and the [Riemann-Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-Roch_theorem_for_algebraic_curves) implies that the cubic curve must have a singularity, which must be at infinity, as all its points in the affine space are regular.

Thus many of the properties of algebraic varieties, including birational equivalence and all the topological properties, depend on the behavior "at infinity" and so it is natural to study the varieties in projective space. Furthermore, the introduction of projective techniques made many theorems in algebraic geometry simpler and sharper: For example, [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217) on the number of intersection points between two varieties can be stated in its sharpest form only in projective space. For these reasons, projective space plays a fundamental role in algebraic geometry.

Nowadays, the *[projective space](wikipedia://en/242135)* **P**<sup>*n*</sup> of dimension *n* is usually defined as the set of the lines passing through a point, considered as the origin, in the affine space of dimension *n* + 1, or equivalently to the set of the vector lines in a vector space of dimension *n* + 1. When a coordinate system has been chosen in the space of dimension *n* + 1, all the points of a line have the same set of coordinates, up to the multiplication by an element of *k*. This defines the [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) of a point of **P**<sup>*n*</sup> as a sequence of *n* + 1 elements of the base field *k*, defined up to the multiplication by a nonzero element of *k* (the same for the whole sequence).

A polynomial in *n* + 1 variables vanishes at all points of a line passing through the origin if and only if it is [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/1698977). In this case, one says that the polynomial *vanishes* at the corresponding point of **P**<sup>*n*</sup>. This allows us to define a *projective algebraic set* in **P**<sup>*n*</sup> as the set *V*(*f*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*k*</sub>), where a finite set of homogeneous polynomials {*f*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *f*<sub>*k*</sub>} vanishes. Like for affine algebraic sets, there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the projective algebraic sets and the reduced [homogeneous ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_ideal) which define them. The *projective varieties* are the projective algebraic sets whose defining ideal is prime. In other words, a projective variety is a projective algebraic set, whose [homogeneous coordinate ring](wikipedia://en/23833909) is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), the *projective coordinates ring* being defined as the quotient of the graded ring or the polynomials in *n* + 1 variables by the homogeneous (reduced) ideal defining the variety. Every projective algebraic set may be uniquely decomposed into a finite union of projective varieties.

The only regular functions which may be defined properly on a projective variety are the constant functions. Thus this notion is not used in projective situations. On the other hand, the *field of the rational functions* or *function field* is a useful notion, which, similarly to the affine case, is defined as the set of the quotients of two homogeneous elements of the same degree in the homogeneous coordinate ring.

## Real algebraic geometry {#Real_algebraic_geometry}

Real algebraic geometry is the study of real algebraic varieties.

The fact that the field of the real numbers is an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430) cannot be ignored in such a study. For example, the curve of equation $x^2+y^2-a=0$ is a circle if $a>0$, but has no real points if $a<0$. Real algebraic geometry also investigates, more broadly, *[semi-algebraic sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-algebraic_set)*, which are the solutions of systems of polynomial inequalities. For example, neither branch of the [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052) of equation $x y-1 = 0$ is a real algebraic variety. However, the branch in the first quadrant is a semi-algebraic set defined by $x y-1=0$ and $x>0$.

One open problem in real algebraic geometry is the following part of [Hilbert's sixteenth problem](wikipedia://en/381750): Decide which respective positions are possible for the ovals of a non-singular [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) of degree 8.

## Computational algebraic geometry {#Computational_algebraic_geometry}

One may date the origin of computational algebraic geometry to meeting EUROSAM'79 (International Symposium on Symbolic and Algebraic Manipulation) held at [Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marseille), France, in June 1979. At this meeting,

- Dennis S. Arnon showed that [George E. Collins](wikipedia://en/41768958)'s [Cylindrical algebraic decomposition](wikipedia://en/12218154) (CAD) allows the computation of the topology of semi-algebraic sets,
- [Bruno Buchberger](wikipedia://en/1924233) presented [Gröbner bases](wikipedia://en/357328) and his algorithm to compute them, and
- [Daniel Lazard](wikipedia://en/34227839) presented a new algorithm for solving systems of homogeneous polynomial equations with a [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/6511) which is essentially polynomial in the expected number of solutions and thus singly exponential in the number of the unknowns. This algorithm is strongly related to [Macaulay](wikipedia://en/3592252)'s [multivariate resultant](wikipedia://en/2195020).

Since then, most results in this area are related to one or several of these items either by using or improving one of these algorithms, or by finding algorithms whose complexity is singly exponential in the number of the variables.

A body of mathematical theory complementary to symbolic methods called [numerical algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/52802478) has been developed over the last several decades. The main computational method is [homotopy continuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_continuation). This supports, for example, a model of [floating-point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_point) computation for solving problems of algebraic geometry.

### Gröbner basis {#Gröbner_basis}

A [Gröbner basis](wikipedia://en/357328) is a system of [generators](wikipedia://en/25977#Definitions_and_motivation) of a polynomial [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) whose computation allows the deduction of many properties of the affine algebraic variety defined by the ideal.

Given an ideal *I* defining an algebraic set *V*:

- *V* is empty (over an algebraically closed extension of the basis field) if and only if the Gröbner basis for any [monomial ordering](wikipedia://en/696408) is reduced to {1}.
- By means of the [Hilbert series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_series), one may compute the [dimension](wikipedia://en/253251) and the [degree](wikipedia://en/2776558) of *V* from any Gröbner basis of *I* for a monomial ordering refining the total degree.
- If the dimension of *V* is 0, then one may compute the points (finite in number) of *V* from any Gröbner basis of *I* (see [Systems of polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_of_polynomial_equations)).
- A Gröbner basis computation allows one to remove from *V* all irreducible components which are contained in a given hypersurface.
- A Gröbner basis computation allows one to compute the Zariski closure of the image of *V* by the projection on the *k* first coordinates, and the subset of the image where the projection is not [proper](wikipedia://en/603782).
- More generally, Gröbner basis computations allow one to compute the Zariski closure of the image and the [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of a rational function of *V* into another affine variety.

Gröbner basis computations do not allow one to compute directly the [primary decomposition](wikipedia://en/1208391) of *I* nor the prime ideals defining the irreducible components of *V*, but most algorithms for this involve Gröbner basis computation. The algorithms which are not based on Gröbner bases use [regular chains](wikipedia://en/18814716) but may need Gröbner bases in some exceptional situations.

Gröbner bases are deemed to be difficult to compute. In fact they may contain, in the worst case, polynomials whose degree is doubly exponential in the number of variables and a number of polynomials which is also doubly exponential. However, this is only a worst-case complexity, and the complexity bound of Lazard's algorithm of 1979 may frequently apply. [Faugère F5 algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faugère_F5_algorithm) realizes this complexity, as it may be viewed as an improvement of Lazard's 1979 algorithm. It follows that the best implementations allow one to compute almost routinely with algebraic sets of degree more than 100. This means that, presently, the difficulty of computing a Gröbner basis is strongly related to the intrinsic difficulty of the problem.

### Cylindrical algebraic decomposition (CAD) {#Cylindrical_algebraic_decomposition_(CAD)}

CAD is an algorithm which was introduced in 1973 by G. Collins to implement with an acceptable complexity the [Tarski–Seidenberg theorem](wikipedia://en/19389633) on [quantifier elimination](wikipedia://en/2150441) over the real numbers.

This theorem concerns the formulas of the [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) whose [atomic formulas](wikipedia://en/4472066) are polynomial equalities or inequalities between polynomials with real coefficients. These formulas are thus the formulas which may be constructed from the atomic formulas by the logical operators *and* (∧), *or* (∨), *not* (¬), *for all* (∀), and *there exists* (∃). Tarski's theorem asserts that, from such a formula, one may compute an equivalent formula without quantifiers (∀, ∃).

The complexity of CAD is doubly exponential in the number of variables. This means that CAD allows one, in theory, to solve every problem of real algebraic geometry which may be expressed by such a formula—this is almost every problem concerning explicitly given varieties and semi-algebraic sets.

While Gröbner basis computation has doubly exponential complexity only in rare cases, CAD has almost always this high complexity. This implies that, unless most polynomials appearing in the input are linear, it may not solve problems with more than four variables.

Since 1973, most of the research on this subject is devoted either to improving CAD or finding alternative algorithms in special cases of general interest.

As an example of the state of art, there are efficient algorithms to find at least one point in every connected component of a semi-algebraic set, and thus to test whether a semi-algebraic set is empty. On the other hand, CAD is yet, in practice, the best algorithm to count the number of connected components.

### Asymptotic complexity vs. practical efficiency {#Asymptotic_complexity_vs._practical_efficiency}

The basic general algorithms of computational geometry have a doubly exponential worst-case [complexity](wikipedia://en/7543). More precisely, if *d* is the maximal degree of the input polynomials and *n* the number of variables, then their complexity is at most $d^{2^{cn}}$ for some constant *c*, and, for some inputs, the complexity is at least $d^{2^{c'n}}$ for another constant *c*′.

During the last 20 years of the 20th century, various algorithms were introduced to solve specific subproblems with a better complexity. Most of these algorithms have a complexity $d^{O(n^2)}$.[^o15]

Among those algorithms which solve a subproblem of the problems solved by Gröbner bases, one may cite *testing whether an affine variety is empty* and *solving nonhomogeneous polynomial systems which have a finite number of solutions.* Such algorithms are rarely implemented because, on most entries, [Faugère's F4 and F5 algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faugère's_F4_and_F5_algorithms) have a better practical efficiency and probably a similar or better complexity (*probably* because the evaluation of the complexity of Gröbner basis algorithms on a particular class of entries is a difficult task which has been done only in a few special cases).

The main algorithms of real algebraic geometry which solve a problem solved by CAD are related to the topology of semi-algebraic sets. One may cite *counting the number of connected components*, *testing whether two points are in the same components*, and *computing a [Whitney stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_stratification) of a real algebraic set*. They have a complexity of $d^{O(n^2)}$, but the constant involved by *O* notation is so high that using them to solve any nontrivial problem effectively solved by CAD, is impossible even if one could use all the existing computing power in the world. Therefore, these algorithms have never been implemented and it is an active research area to search for algorithms with have together a good asymptotic complexity and a good practical efficiency.

## Abstract modern viewpoint {#Abstract_modern_viewpoint}

The modern approaches to algebraic geometry redefine and effectively extend the range of basic objects in various levels of generality to schemes, [formal schemes](wikipedia://en/18829483), [ind-schemes](wikipedia://en/44417413), [algebraic spaces](wikipedia://en/2394027), [algebraic stacks](wikipedia://en/717377), and so on. The need for this arises already from the useful ideas within the theory of varieties; for example, the formal functions of Zariski can be accommodated by introducing [nilpotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element) in structure rings; considering spaces of loops and arcs, constructing quotients by group actions, and developing formal grounds for natural [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602) and [deformation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) lead to some of the further extensions.

Most remarkably, in the early 1960s, [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) were subsumed into [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042)'s concept of a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754). Their local objects are affine schemes or prime spectra which are [locally ringed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_ringed_space) which form a category which is antiequivalent to the category of commutative unital rings, extending the duality between the category of affine algebraic varieties over a field *k*, and the category of finitely generated reduced *k*-algebras. The gluing is along the Zariski topology; one can glue within the category of locally ringed spaces, but also, using the [Yoneda embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoneda_embedding), within the more abstract category of [presheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presheaves) of sets over the category of affine schemes. The Zariski topology in the set-theoretic sense is then replaced by a [Grothendieck topology](wikipedia://en/12910). Grothendieck introduced Grothendieck topologies having in mind more exotic but geometrically finer and more sensitive examples than the crude Zariski topology, namely the [étale topology](wikipedia://en/5707590), and the two flat Grothendieck topologies: fppf and fpqc; nowadays some other examples have become prominent, including the [Nisnevich topology](wikipedia://en/14627005). Sheaves can be furthermore generalized to [stacks](wikipedia://en/13185596) in the sense of Grothendieck, usually with some additional representability conditions leading to [Artin stacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin_stack) and, even finer, [Deligne–Mumford stacks](wikipedia://en/40639179), both often called [algebraic stacks](wikipedia://en/717377).

Sometimes, other algebraic sites replace the category of affine schemes. For example, [Nikolai Durov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Durov) has introduced commutative algebraic [monads](wikipedia://en/355140) as a generalization of local objects in a generalized algebraic geometry. Versions of a [tropical geometry](wikipedia://en/867041), of an [absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294) over a [field of one element](wikipedia://en/13798040), and an algebraic analogue of [Arakelov's geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arakelov's_geometry) were realized in this setup.

Another formal generalization is possible to [universal algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/17736871) in which every [variety of algebras](wikipedia://en/1021753) has its own algebraic geometry. The term *variety of algebras* should not be confused with *algebraic variety*.

The language of schemes, stacks, and generalizations has proved to be a valuable way of dealing with geometric concepts and become cornerstones of modern algebraic geometry.

Algebraic stacks can be further generalized, and for many practical questions like [deformation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) and [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602), this is often the most natural approach. One can extend the [Grothendieck site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_site) of affine schemes to a [higher-categorical](wikipedia://en/5274013) site of [derived affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_affine_scheme), by replacing the commutative rings with an [infinity category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity_category) of [differential graded commutative algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_graded_commutative_algebra), or of simplicial commutative rings or a similar category with an appropriate variant of a Grothendieck topology. One can also replace presheaves of sets with presheaves of [simplicial sets](wikipedia://en/1027784) (or of [infinity groupoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity-groupoid)). Then, in presence of an appropriate homotopic machinery, one can develop a notion of derived stack as such a presheaf on the infinity category of derived affine schemes, which satisfies certain infinite-categorical versions of sheaf axioms (and to be algebraic, inductively a sequence of representability conditions). [Quillen model categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quillen_model_category), Segal categories, and [quasicategories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicategory) are some of the most-used tools to formalize this, yielding the *[derived algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/40778281)*, introduced by the school of [Carlos Simpson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Simpson), including Andre Hirschowitz, [Bertrand Toën](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Toën), Gabrielle Vezzosi, Michel Vaquié, and others; and developed further by [Jacob Lurie](wikipedia://en/26663863), [Bertrand Toën](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Toën), and [Gabriele Vezzosi](wikipedia://en/56323308). Another (noncommutative) version of derived algebraic geometry, using A-infinity categories, has been developed from the early 1990s by [Maxim Kontsevich](wikipedia://en/104509) and followers.

## History {#History}

### Before the 16th century {#Before_the_16th_century}

Some of the roots of algebraic geometry date back to the work of the [Hellenistic Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Greece) from the 5th century BC. The [Delian problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delian_problem), for instance, was to construct a length *x* so that the cube of side *x* contained the same volume as the rectangular box *a*<sup>2</sup>*b* for given sides *a* and *b*. [Menaechmus](wikipedia://en/1944319) (c. 350 BC) considered the problem geometrically by intersecting the pair of plane conics *ay* = *x*<sup>2</sup> and *xy* = *ab*. In the 3rd century BC, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) and [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/257242) systematically studied additional problems on [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections) using coordinates.[^o20][^n1] [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/257242) in the Conics further developed a method that is so similar to analytic geometry that his work is sometimes thought to have anticipated the work of [Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) by some 1800 years.[^o22] His application of reference lines, a [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007), and a [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) is essentially no different from our modern use of a coordinate frame, where the distances measured along the diameter from the point of tangency are the abscissas, and the segments parallel to the tangent and intercepted between the axis and the curve are the ordinates. He further developed relations between the abscissas and the corresponding coordinates using geometric methods like using parabolas and curves.[^o23][^o24][^o25] Medieval mathematicians, including [Omar Khayyam](wikipedia://en/92550), [Leonardo of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_of_Pisa), [Gersonides](wikipedia://en/61626) and [Nicole Oresme](wikipedia://en/593659) in the [Medieval Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages),[^n2] solved certain cubic and quadratic equations by purely algebraic means and then interpreted the results geometrically. The [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people) mathematician [Omar Khayyám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayyám) (born 1048 AD) believed that there was a relationship between [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), and [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446).[^n3][^o28][^o29] This was criticized by Jeffrey Oaks, who claims that the study of curves by means of equations originated with Descartes in the seventeenth century.[^o30]

### Renaissance {#Renaissance}

Such techniques of applying geometrical constructions to algebraic problems were also adopted by a number of [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) mathematicians such as [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145) and [Niccolò Fontana "Tartaglia"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Fontana_Tartaglia) on their studies of the cubic equation. The geometrical approach to construction problems, rather than the algebraic one, was favored by most 16th- and 17th-century mathematicians, notably [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068) who argued against the use of algebraic and analytical methods in geometry.[^n4] The French mathematicians [Franciscus Vieta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscus_Vieta) and later [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) and [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) revolutionized the conventional way of thinking about construction problems through the introduction of [coordinate geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_geometry). They were interested primarily in the properties of *algebraic curves*, such as those defined by [Diophantine equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_equations) (in the case of Fermat), and the algebraic reformulation of the classical Greek works on conics and cubics (in the case of Descartes).

During the same period, Blaise Pascal and [Gérard Desargues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gérard_Desargues) approached geometry from a different perspective, developing the [synthetic](wikipedia://en/267484) notions of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). Pascal and Desargues also studied curves, but from the purely geometrical point of view: the analog of the Greek *ruler-and-compass construction*. Ultimately, the [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) of Descartes and Fermat won out, for it supplied the 18th-century mathematicians with concrete quantitative tools needed to study physical problems using the new calculus of [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281). However, by the end of the 18th century, most of the algebraic character of coordinate geometry was subsumed by the *calculus of infinitesimals* of [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) and [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902).

### 19th and early 20th century {#19th_and_early_20th_century}

It took the simultaneous 19th-century developments of [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) and [Abelian integrals](wikipedia://en/1138853) in order to bring the old algebraic ideas back into the geometrical fold. The first of these new developments was seized up by [Edmond Laguerre](wikipedia://en/1011080) and [Arthur Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034), who attempted to ascertain the generalized metric properties of projective space. Cayley introduced the idea of *homogeneous polynomial forms*, and more specifically [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478), on projective space. Subsequently, [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) studied projective geometry (along with other types of geometry) from the viewpoint that the geometry on a space is encoded in a certain class of [transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_group) on the space. By the end of the 19th century, projective geometers were studying more general kinds of transformations on figures in projective space. Rather than the [projective linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_linear_transformation) which were normally regarded as giving the fundamental [Kleinian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleinian_geometry) on projective space, they concerned themselves also with the higher-degree [birational transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_transformation). This weaker notion of congruence would later lead members of the 20th century [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424) to classify [algebraic surfaces](wikipedia://en/648311) up to [birational isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_isomorphism).

The second early-19th-century development, that of Abelian integrals, would lead [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) to the development of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181).

In the same period began the algebraization of the algebraic geometry through [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990). The prominent results in this direction are [Hilbert's basis theorem](wikipedia://en/13733) and [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215), which are the basis of the connection between algebraic geometry and commutative algebra, and [Macaulay](wikipedia://en/3592252)'s [multivariate resultant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_resultant), which is the basis of [elimination theory](wikipedia://en/245978). Probably because of the size of the computation which is implied by multivariate resultants, elimination theory was forgotten during the middle of the 20th century until it was renewed by [singularity theory](wikipedia://en/581763) and computational algebraic geometry.

### 20th century {#20th_century}

[B. L. van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._L._van_der_Waerden), [Oscar Zariski](wikipedia://en/359352), and [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) developed a foundation for algebraic geometry based on contemporary [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), including [valuation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valuation_theory) and the theory of [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977). One of the goals was to give a rigorous framework for proving the results of the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/383424). In particular, this school used systematically the notion of [generic point](wikipedia://en/3043978) without any precise definition, which was first given by these authors during the 1930s.

In the 1950s and 1960s, [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) and [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) recast the foundations making use of [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory). Later, from about 1960, and largely led by Grothendieck, the idea of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) was worked out, in conjunction with a very refined apparatus of [homological techniques](wikipedia://en/139410). After a decade of rapid development the field stabilized in the 1970s, and new applications were made, both to [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and to more classical geometric questions on algebraic varieties, [singularities](wikipedia://en/581763), [moduli](wikipedia://en/361609), and [formal moduli](wikipedia://en/3018981).

An important class of varieties, not easily understood directly from their defining equations, are the [abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/247152), which are the projective varieties whose points form an abelian [group](wikipedia://en/19447). The prototypical examples are the [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225), which have a rich theory. They were instrumental in the [proof](wikipedia://en/21950759) of [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953) and are also used in [elliptic-curve cryptography](wikipedia://en/9966).

In parallel with the abstract trend of the algebraic geometry, which is concerned with general statements about varieties, methods for effective computation with concretely-given varieties have also been developed, which lead to the new area of computational algebraic geometry. One of the founding methods of this area is the theory of [Gröbner bases](wikipedia://en/357328), introduced by [Bruno Buchberger](wikipedia://en/1924233) in 1965. Another founding method, more specially devoted to real algebraic geometry, is the [cylindrical algebraic decomposition](wikipedia://en/12218154), introduced by [George E. Collins](wikipedia://en/41768958) in 1973.

See also: [derived algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/40778281).

:::

## Analytic geometry {#Analytic_geometry}

An [analytic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_variety) over the field of real or complex numbers is defined locally as the set of common solutions of several equations involving [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478). It is analogous to the concept of [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) in that it carries a structure sheaf of analytic functions instead of regular functions. Any [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) is a complex analytic variety. Since analytic varieties may have [singular points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity), not all complex analytic varieties are manifolds. Over a non-archimedean field analytic geometry is studied via [rigid analytic spaces](wikipedia://en/5828941).

Modern analytic geometry over the field of complex numbers is closely related to complex algebraic geometry, as has been shown by [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483) in his paper *[GAGA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GAGA)*,[^o36] the name of which is French for *Algebraic geometry and analytic geometry*. The GAGA results over the field of complex numbers may be extended to rigid analytic spaces over non-archimedean fields.[^o37]

## Applications {#Applications}

Algebraic geometry now finds applications in [statistics](wikipedia://en/17314682),[^o40] [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039),[^o41][^o42] [robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotics),[^o43] [error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometric_code),[^o44] [phylogenetics](wikipedia://en/3986130)[^o45] and [geometric modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_modelling).[^o46] There are also connections to [string theory](wikipedia://en/2164886),[^o47] [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924),[^o48] [graph matchings](wikipedia://en/581797),[^o49] [solitons](wikipedia://en/51654)[^o50] and [integer programming](wikipedia://en/411215).[^o51]
