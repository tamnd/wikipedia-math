# Biconditional introduction

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Biconditional introduction"}

**Biconditional introduction.**

If $P \to Q$ is true, and if $Q \to P$ is true, then one may infer that $P \leftrightarrow Q$ is true.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Biconditional introduction"}

In [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus), **biconditional introduction**[^o3][^o4][^o5] is a [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [rule of inference](wikipedia://en/252311). It allows for one to [infer](wikipedia://en/317465) a [biconditional](wikipedia://en/228783) from two [conditional statements](wikipedia://en/658808). The rule makes it possible to introduce a biconditional statement into a [logical proof](wikipedia://en/1197184). If $P \to Q$ is true, and if $Q \to P$ is true, then one may infer that $P \leftrightarrow Q$ is true. For example, from the statements "if I'm breathing, then I'm alive" and "if I'm alive, then I'm breathing", it can be inferred that "I'm breathing [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) I'm alive". Biconditional introduction is the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) of [biconditional elimination](wikipedia://en/4287). The rule can be stated formally as:

:::

$$\frac{P \to Q, Q \to P}{\therefore P \leftrightarrow Q}$$

where the rule is that wherever instances of "$P \to Q$" and "$Q \to P$" appear on lines of a proof, "$P \leftrightarrow Q$" can validly be placed on a subsequent line.

## Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

The *biconditional introduction* rule may be written in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation:

$$(P \to Q), (Q \to P) \vdash (P \leftrightarrow Q)$$

where $\vdash$ is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) symbol meaning that $P \leftrightarrow Q$ is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) when $P \to Q$ and $Q \to P$ are both in a proof;

or as the statement of a truth-functional [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) or [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of propositional logic:

$$((P \to Q) \land (Q \to P)) \to (P \leftrightarrow Q)$$

where $P$, and $Q$ are propositions expressed in some [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102).
