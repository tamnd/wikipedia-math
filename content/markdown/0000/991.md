# Absolute value

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Absolute value"}

*Image: Absolute value.svg.* The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the absolute value function for real numbers

*Image: AbsoluteValueDiagram.svg.* The absolute value of a number may be thought of as its distance from zero.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **absolute value** or **modulus** of a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x$, denoted $|x|$, is the ([non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative)) [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301#Numbers) of $x$ [measured](wikipedia://en/19022) without regard to its [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270). Namely, $|x|=x$ if $x$ is a [positive number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number), and $|x|=-x$ if $x$ is [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) (in which case $-x$ is positive), and $|0|=0$. For example, the absolute value of 3 is 3, and the absolute value of −3 is also 3. The absolute value of a number may be thought of as its [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from zero.

:::

Generalisations of the absolute value for real numbers occur in a wide variety of mathematical settings. For example, an absolute value is also defined for the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), [ordered rings](wikipedia://en/525697), [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) and [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370). The absolute value is closely related to the notions of [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301), [distance](wikipedia://en/39378), and [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) in various mathematical and physical contexts.

## Terminology and notation {#Terminology_and_notation}

In 1806, [Jean-Robert Argand](wikipedia://en/543727) introduced the term *module*, meaning *unit of measure* in French, specifically for the *complex* absolute value,[^o3][^o4] and it was borrowed into English in 1866 as the Latin equivalent *modulus*.[^o3] The term *absolute value* has been used in this sense from at least 1806 in French[^o5] and 1857 in English. The notation $\lvert x\rvert$, with a [vertical bar](wikipedia://en/641073) on each side, was introduced by [Karl Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459) in 1841.[^o6] Other names for *absolute value* include *numerical value*[^o3] and *magnitude*.[^o3] The absolute value of $x$ has also been denoted $\operatorname{abs} x$ in some mathematical publications,[^n1] and in [spreadsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet), programming languages, and computational software packages, the absolute value of $x$ is generally represented by `abs(''x'')`, or a similar expression,[^n2] as it has been since the earliest days of [high-level programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_programming_language).[^n3]

The vertical bar notation also appears in a number of other mathematical contexts: for example, when applied to a set, it denotes its [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174); when applied to a [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_%28math%29), it denotes its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468).[^n4] Vertical bars denote the absolute value only for algebraic objects for which the notion of an absolute value is defined, notably an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) of a [normed division algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_division_algebra), for example, a real number, a complex number, or a quaternion. A closely related but distinct notation is the use of vertical bars for either the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm)[^n5] or [sup norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sup_norm)[^n6] of a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$, although double vertical bars with subscripts ($\|\cdot\|_2$ and $\|\cdot\|_\infty$, respectively) are a more common and less ambiguous notation.

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

::: {#o16 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Absolute value of a real number"}

### Real numbers {#Real_numbers}

For any [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x$, the absolute value or modulus of $x$ is denoted by $|x|$, with a [vertical bar](wikipedia://en/641073) on each side of the quantity, and is defined as[^n7]

$$|x| = \begin{cases} x, & \text{if } x \geq 0 \\ -x, & \text{if } x < 0. \end{cases}$$

The absolute value of $x$ is thus always either a [positive number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) or [zero](wikipedia://en/34513), but never [negative](wikipedia://en/154616). When $x$ itself is negative ($x < 0$), then its absolute value is necessarily positive ($|x|=-x>0$).[^n8]

From an [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) point of view, the absolute value of a real number is that number's [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from zero along the [real number line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number_line), and more generally, the absolute value of the difference of two real numbers (their [absolute difference](wikipedia://en/28021681)) is the distance between them.[^n8] The notion of an abstract [distance function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_function) in mathematics can be seen to be a generalisation of the absolute value of the difference.[^n9] See below.

Since the [square root symbol](wikipedia://en/24971503) represents the unique *positive* [square root](wikipedia://en/29208), when applied to a positive number, it follows that[^n10]

$$|x| = \sqrt{x^2}.$$

This is equivalent to the definition above, and may be used as an alternative definition of the absolute value of real numbers.[^n11]

The absolute value has the following four fundamental properties ($a$, $b$ are real numbers), that are used for generalization of this notion to other domains:[^n12]

| $\|a\| \ge 0$ | Non-negativity[^n12] |
|---|---|
| $\|a\| = 0 \iff a = 0$ | Positive-definiteness[^n12] |
| $\|ab\| = \left\|a\right\| \left\|b\right\|$ | [Multiplicativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicativeness)[^n12] |
| $\|a+b\| \le \|a\| + \|b\|$ | [Subadditivity](wikipedia://en/745789), specifically the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941)[^n12] |

Non-negativity, positive definiteness, and multiplicativity are readily apparent from the definition. To see that subadditivity holds, first note that $|a+b|=s(a+b)$ where $s=\pm 1$, with its sign chosen to make the result positive. Now, since $-1 \cdot x \le |x|$ and $+1 \cdot x \le |x|$, it follows that, whichever of $\pm1$ is the value of $s$, one has $s \cdot x\leq |x|$ for all real $x$. Consequently, $|a+b|=s \cdot (a+b) = s \cdot a + s \cdot b \leq |a| + |b|$, as desired.

Some additional useful properties are given below. These are either immediate consequences of the definition or implied by the four fundamental properties above.

| $\bigl\| \left\|a\right\| \bigr\| = \|a\|$ | [Idempotence](wikipedia://en/14972) (the absolute value of the absolute value is the absolute value) |
|---|---|
| $\left\|-a\right\| = \|a\|$ | [Evenness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_function) ([reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873) of the graph)[^n13] |
| $\|a - b\| = 0 \iff a = b$ | [Identity of indiscernibles](wikipedia://en/630398) (equivalent to positive-definiteness) |
| $\|a - b\| \le \|a - c\| + \|c - b\|$ | [Triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941#Example_norms) (equivalent to subadditivity) |
| $\left\|\frac{a}{b}\right\| = \frac{\|a\|}{\|b\|}\$ (if $b \ne 0$) | Preservation of division – equivalent to multiplicativity[^n14] |
| $\|a-b\| \geq \bigl\| \left\|a\right\| - \left\|b\right\| \bigr\|$ | [Reverse triangle inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_triangle_inequality) – equivalent to subadditivity[^n14] |

Two other useful properties concerning inequalities are:[^n14]

| $\|a\| \le b \iff -b \le a \le b$ |
|---|
| $\|a\| \ge b \iff a \le -b\$ or $a \ge b$ |

These relations may be used to solve inequalities involving absolute values. For example:

| $\|x-3\| \le 9$ | $\iff -9 \le x-3 \le 9$ |
|---|---|
|  | $\iff -6 \le x \le 12$ |

The absolute value, as "distance from zero", is used to define the absolute difference between arbitrary real numbers, the standard [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) on the real numbers.

### Complex numbers {#Complex_numbers}

*Image: Complex conjugate picture.svg.* The absolute value of a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $z$ is the distance $r$ of $z$ from the origin. It is also seen in the picture that $z$ and its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) $\bar z$ have the same absolute value.

Since the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) are not [ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_set), the definition given at the top for the real absolute value cannot be directly applied to complex numbers. However, the geometric interpretation of the absolute value of a real number as its distance from 0 can be generalised. The absolute value of a complex number is defined by the Euclidean distance of its corresponding point in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) from the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432). This can be computed using the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034): for any complex number

$$z = x + iy,$$

where $x$ and $y$ are real numbers, the absolute value or modulus of $z$ is denoted $|z|$ and is defined by[^n15]

$$|z| = \sqrt{\operatorname{Re}(z)^2 + \operatorname{Im}(z)^2}=\sqrt{x^2 + y^2},$$

the [Pythagorean addition](wikipedia://en/14597289) of $x$ and $y$, where $\operatorname{Re}(z)=x$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z)=y$ denote the real and imaginary parts of $z$, respectively. When the imaginary part $y$ is zero, this coincides with the definition of the absolute value of the real number $x$.[^n15]

When a complex number $z$ is expressed in its [polar form](wikipedia://en/5826#Polar_form) as $z = r e^{i \theta},$ its absolute value is $|z| = r.$

Since the product of any complex number $z$ and its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) $\bar z = x - iy$, with the same absolute value, is always the non-negative real number $\left(x^2 + y^2\right)$, the absolute value of a complex number $z$ is the square root of $z \cdot \overline{z},$ which is therefore called the [absolute square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_square) or *squared modulus* of $z$:[^n15]

$$|z| = \sqrt{z \cdot \overline{z}}.$$

This generalizes the alternative definition for reals: $|x| = \sqrt{x\cdot x}$.

The complex absolute value shares the four fundamental properties given above for the real absolute value. The identity $|z|^n = |z^n|$ is a special case of multiplicativity that is often useful by itself.[^n15]

## Absolute value function {#Absolute_value_function}

*Image: Absolute value.svg.* The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the absolute value function for real numbers

*Image: Absolute value composition.svg.* [Composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_of_functions) of absolute value with a [cubic function](wikipedia://en/243709) in different orders

The real absolute value function is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) everywhere. It is [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable) everywhere except for $x = 0$. It is [monotonically decreasing](wikipedia://en/48260) on the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) −∞, 0 and monotonically increasing on the interval 0, +∞.[^n16] Since a real number and its [opposite](wikipedia://en/228312) have the same absolute value, it is an [even function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_function), and is hence not [invertible](wikipedia://en/14907).[^n17] The real absolute value function is a [piecewise linear](wikipedia://en/444224), [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568).[^n13]

For both real and complex numbers, the absolute value function is [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) (meaning that the absolute value of any absolute value is itself).

### Relationship to the sign function {#Relationship_to_the_sign_function}

The absolute value function of a real number returns its value irrespective of its sign, whereas the [sign (or signum) function](wikipedia://en/505449) returns a number's sign irrespective of its value. The following equations show the relationship between these two functions:

$$|x| = x \operatorname{sgn}(x),$$

or

$$|x| \operatorname{sgn}(x) = x,$$

and for $x \ne 0$,

$$\operatorname{sgn}(x) = \frac{|x|}{x} = \frac{x}{|x|}.$$

### Relationship to the max and min functions {#Relationship_to_the_max_and_min_functions}

Let $s,t\in\mathbb{R}$, then the following relationship to the [minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum) and [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum) functions hold:

$$|t-s|= -2 \min(s,t)+s+t$$

and

$$|t-s|=2 \max(s,t)-s-t.$$

The formulas can be derived by considering each case $s>t$ and $t>s$ separately.

From the last formula one can derive also $|t|= \max(t,-t)$.

### Derivative {#Derivative}

The real absolute value function has a [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) for every $x \ne 0$, given by a [step function](wikipedia://en/170939) equal to the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449) except at $x = 0$ where the absolute value function is not [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable):[^o46][^n18]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\left|x\right|}{dx} &= \frac{x}{|x|} = \begin{cases} -1 & x<0 \\ 1 & x>0 \end{cases} \\[7mu] &= \operatorname{sgn} x\quad \text{for } x \ne 0. \end{aligned}$$

The real absolute value function is an example of a continuous function that achieves a [global minimum](wikipedia://en/298420) where the derivative does not exist.

The [subdifferential](wikipedia://en/3462862) of $\lvert x\rvert$ at $x = 0$ is the interval −1, 1.[^n19]

The [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) absolute value function is continuous everywhere but [complex differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_differentiable) *nowhere* because it violates the [Cauchy–Riemann equations](wikipedia://en/7583).[^o46]

The second derivative of $\lvert x\rvert$ with respect to $x$ is zero everywhere except zero, where it does not exist. As a [generalised function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalised_function), the second derivative may be taken as two times the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021).

### Antiderivative {#Antiderivative}

The [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) (indefinite [integral](wikipedia://en/15532)) of the real absolute value function is

$$\int \left|x\right| dx = \frac{x\left|x\right|}{2} + C,$$

where $C$ is an arbitrary [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939). This is not a [complex antiderivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_antiderivative) because complex antiderivatives can only exist for complex-differentiable ([holomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic)) functions, which the complex absolute value function is not.

### Derivatives and antiderivatives of compositions {#Derivatives_and_antiderivatives_of_compositions}

The following three formulae are special cases of the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113):

$${\text{d}^n \over \text{d}x^n} f(|x|)= (\operatorname{sgn} x)^n f^{(n)}(|x|)\quad \text{for } x \ne 0\,,$$

if the absolute value is inside a function, and

$${\text{d}^n \over \text{d}x^n} |f(x)|=\operatorname{sgn}(f(x)) f^{(n)}(x)\quad \text{for } f(x) \ne 0\,,$$

if another function is inside the absolute value. Combining both, the result is:

$${\text{d}^n \over \text{d}x^n} |f(|x|)|=(\operatorname{sgn} x)^n \operatorname{sgn}(f(|x|)) f^{(n)}(|x|)\quad \text{for } x \ne 0, f(|x|) \ne 0\,.$$

From these formulae and using [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252), antiderivatives can also be obtained:

$$\int {f(|x|)\text{d}x}= \operatorname{sgn} (x) F(|x|)\quad \text{for } x \ne 0\,,$$

$$\int {|f(x)| \text{d}x} = \int {{|f(x)| \over f(x)} f(x) \text{d}x} = \operatorname{sgn}(f(x)) F(x)\quad \text{for } f(x) \ne 0\,,$$

$$\int {|f(|x|)| \text{d}x} = \int {{|f(|x|)| \over f(|x|)} f(|x|) \text{d}x} = \operatorname{sgn} (x) \operatorname{sgn}(f(|x|)) F(|x|)\quad \text{for } x \ne 0, f(|x|) \ne 0\,,$$

supposing the derivative of the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449#Differentiation) is 0.

### Power rule for expressions with absolute values {#Power_rule_for_expressions_with_absolute_values}

Using [chain](wikipedia://en/6113) and [product](wikipedia://en/248988) rules, the [power rule](wikipedia://en/147912) for expressions of the type $x^n |x|^m$ can be written as:

$${\text{d} \over \text{d}x} x^n |x|^m = (n+m)x^{n-1}|x|^m\,.$$

This holds true even for $n=0$:

$${\text{d} \over \text{d}x} |x|^m = mx^{-1}|x|^m\,.$$

:::

## Distance {#Distance}

The absolute value is closely related to the idea of [distance](wikipedia://en/39378). As noted above, the absolute value of a real or complex number is the distance from that number to the origin, along the real number line, for real numbers, or in the complex plane, for complex numbers, and more generally, the absolute value of the difference of two real or complex numbers is the distance between them.

The standard [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) between two points

$$a = (a_1, a_2, \dots , a_n)$$

and

$$b = (b_1, b_2, \dots , b_n)$$

in [Euclidean $n$-space](wikipedia://en/9697) is defined as:[^n9]

$$\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i-b_i)^2}.$$

This can be seen as a generalisation, since for $a_1$ and $b_1$ real, i.e. in a 1-space, according to the alternative definition of the absolute value,

$$|a_1 - b_1| = \sqrt{(a_1 - b_1)^2} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^1(a_i-b_i)^2},$$

and for $a = a_1 + i a_2$ and $b = b_1 + i b_2$ complex numbers, i.e. in a 2-space,

{|

\|- |$|a - b|$ |$= |(a_1 + i a_2) - (b_1 + i b_2)|$ |- | |$= |(a_1 - b_1) + i(a_2 - b_2)|$ |- | |$= \sqrt{(a_1 - b_1)^2 + (a_2 - b_2)^2} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^2(a_i-b_i)^2}.$ |}

The above shows that the "absolute value"-distance, for real and complex numbers, agrees with the standard Euclidean distance, which they inherit as a result of considering them as one and two-dimensional Euclidean spaces, respectively.

The properties of the absolute value of the difference of two real or complex numbers: non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, symmetry and the triangle inequality given above, can be seen to motivate the more general notion of a [distance function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_function) as follows:

A real valued function $d$ on a set $X\,\times\,X$ is called a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) (or a *distance function*) on $X$, if it satisfies the following four axioms:

{|

\|- |style="width:250px" | $d(a, b) \ge 0$ |Non-negativity |- |$d(a, b) = 0 \iff a = b$ |Identity of indiscernibles |- |$d(a, b) = d(b, a)$ |Symmetry |- |$d(a, b) \le d(a, c) + d(c, b)$ |Triangle inequality |}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Ordered rings {#Ordered_rings}

The definition of absolute value given for real numbers above can be extended to any [ordered ring](wikipedia://en/525697). That is, if $a$ is an element of an ordered ring *R*, then the **absolute value** of $a$, denoted by $\lvert a\rvert$, is defined to be:

$$|a| = \left\{ \begin{array}{rl} a, & \text{if } a \geq 0 \\ -a, & \text{if } a < 0. \end{array}\right.$$

where $-a$ is the [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312) of $a$, 0 is the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225), and \< and ≥ have the usual meaning with respect to the ordering in the ring.[^n20]

### Fields {#Fields}

The four fundamental properties of the absolute value for real numbers can be used to generalise the notion of absolute value to an arbitrary field, as follows.

A real-valued function $v$ on a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$ is called an *absolute value* (also a *modulus*, *magnitude*, *value*, or *valuation*)[^n21] if it satisfies the following four axioms:

{| cellpadding=10

\|- |$v(a) \ge 0$ |Non-negativity |- |$v(a) = 0 \iff a = \mathbf{0}$ |Positive-definiteness |- |$v(ab) = v(a) v(b)$ |Multiplicativity |- |$v(a+b) \le v(a) + v(b)$ |Subadditivity or the triangle inequality |}

Where **0** denotes the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225) of $F$. It follows from positive-definiteness and multiplicativity that $v(\mathbf{1}) = 1$, where **1** denotes the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) of $F$. The real and complex absolute values defined above are examples of absolute values for an arbitrary field.

If $v$ is an absolute value on $F$, then the function $d$ on $F\,\times\,F$, defined by $d(a,\,b) = v(a - b)$, is a metric and the following are equivalent:

- $d$ satisfies the [ultrametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrametric) inequality $d(x, y) \leq \max(d(x,z),d(y,z))$ for all $x$, $y$, $z$ in $F$.
- $\left\{ v\left( \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbf{1}\right) : n \in \mathbb{N} \right\}$ is [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258) in **R**.
- $v\left({\textstyle \sum_{k=1}^n } \mathbf{1}\right) \le 1\$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
- $v(a) \le 1 \Rightarrow v(1+a) \le 1\$ for all $a \in F$.
- $v(a + b) \le \max \{v(a), v(b)\}\$ for all $a, b \in F$.

An absolute value which satisfies any (hence all) of the above conditions is said to be **non-Archimedean**, otherwise it is said to be [Archimedean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_field).[^n22]

### Vector spaces {#Vector_spaces}

Again the fundamental properties of the absolute value for real numbers can be used, with a slight modification, to generalise the notion to an arbitrary vector space.

A real-valued function on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ over a field $F$, represented as $\lVert \rVert$, is called an **absolute value**, but more usually a [**norm**](wikipedia://en/990534), if it satisfies the following axioms:

For all $a$ in $F$, and $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{u}$ in $V$,

| $\\|\mathbf{v}\\| \ge 0$ | Non-negativity |
|---|---|
| $\\|\mathbf{v}\\| = 0 \iff \mathbf{v} = 0$ | Positive-definiteness |
| $\\|a \mathbf{v}\\| = \left\|a\right\| \left\\|\mathbf{v}\right\\|$ | Absolute homogeneity or positive scalability |
| $\\|\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}\\| \le \\|\mathbf{v}\\| + \\|\mathbf{u}\\|$ | Subadditivity or the triangle inequality |

The norm of a vector is also called its *length* or *magnitude*.

In the case of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$, the function defined by

$$\|(x_1, x_2, \dots , x_n) \| = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2}$$

is a norm called the Euclidean norm. When the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ are considered as the one-dimensional vector space $\mathbb{R}^1$, the absolute value is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), and is the $p$-norm (see [L<sup>p</sup> space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L^p_space#Definition)) for any $p$. In fact the absolute value is the "only" norm on $\mathbb{R}^1$, in the sense that, for every norm $\lVert \rVert$ on $\mathbb{R}^1$, $\lVert x\rVert = \lVert 1\rVert\,\cdot\,\lvert x\rvert$.

The complex absolute value is a special case of the norm in an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856), which is identical to the Euclidean norm when the complex plane is identified as the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) $\mathbb{R}^2$.

### Composition algebras {#Composition_algebras}

Every composition algebra *A* has an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) *x* → *x*\* called its **conjugation**. The product in *A* of an element *x* and its conjugate *x*\* is written *N*(*x*) = *x x*\* and called the **norm of x**.

The real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$, and quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ are all composition algebras with norms given by [definite quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/20437320). The absolute value in these [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) is given by the square root of the composition algebra norm.

In general, the norm of a composition algebra may be a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) that is not definite and has [null vectors](wikipedia://en/1453977). However, as in the case of division algebras, when an element *x* has a non-zero norm, then *x* has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) given by *x*\*/*N*(*x*).
