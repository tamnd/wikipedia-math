# Characteristic subgroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Characteristic subgroup"}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a **characteristic subgroup** is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) that is mapped to itself by every [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of the parent [group](wikipedia://en/19447).[^n1][^n2] Because every [conjugation map](wikipedia://en/49176#Properties) is an [inner automorphism](wikipedia://en/61839), every characteristic subgroup is [normal](wikipedia://en/21918); though the converse is not true. Examples of characteristic subgroups include the [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) and the [center of a group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is called a **characteristic subgroup** if for every automorphism $\varphi$ of $G$, one has $\varphi(H)\leq H$; then write **$H \operatorname{char} G$**.

This is equivalent to the stronger condition $\varphi(H) = H$ for every automorphism $\varphi$ of $G$, because $\varphi^{-1}(H)\leq H$ implies the reverse inclusion $H\leq \varphi(H)$.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

Given $H\,\mathrm{char}\,G$, every automorphism of $G$ induces an automorphism of the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $G/H$, which yields a homomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(G)\to\operatorname{Aut}(G/H)$.

If $G$ has a unique subgroup $H$ of a given [index](wikipedia://en/230581), then $H$ is characteristic in $G$.

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

### Normal subgroup {#Normal_subgroup}

A subgroup $H$ of $G$ that is invariant under all inner automorphisms is called [normal](wikipedia://en/21918); sometimes called an invariant subgroup.

Since $\operatorname{Inn}(G)\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ and a characteristic subgroup is invariant under all automorphisms, every characteristic subgroup is normal. However, not every normal subgroup is characteristic. Here are several examples:

- Let $H$ be a nontrivial group, and let $G=H\times H$ be the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015). Then the subgroups $\{1\}\times H$ and $H\times \{1\}$ are both normal, but neither is characteristic. In particular, neither of these subgroups is invariant under the automorphism $(x,y)\mapsto (y,x)$, that switches the two factors.
- For a concrete example of this, let $V\cong\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ be the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928). Since this group is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974), every subgroup is normal; however, every permutation of the $3$ non-identity elements is an automorphism of $V$, so the $3$ subgroups of order $2$ are not characteristic. Formally, $V=\{e,a,b,ab\}$. If $H=\{e,a\}$ for example and $T$ is the automorphism switching $a$ and $b$, then $T(H)$ is not contained in $H$.
- In the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) of order 8, each of the cyclic subgroups of order 4 is normal, but none of these are characteristic. However, the subgroup, $\{1,-1\}$, is characteristic, since it is the only subgroup of order 2.
- If $n>2$ is even, the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of order $2n$ has $3$ subgroups of [index](wikipedia://en/230581) $2$, all of which are normal. One of these is the cyclic subgroup, which is characteristic. The other two subgroups are dihedral; these are permuted by an [outer automorphism](wikipedia://en/628466) of the parent group, and are therefore not characteristic.

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Strictly characteristic subgroup"}

### Strictly characteristic subgroup {#Strictly_characteristic_subgroup}

A *strictly characteristic subgroup*, or a *distinguished subgroup*, is one which is invariant under [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569). For [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707), surjectivity of an endomorphism implies injectivity, so a surjective endomorphism is an automorphism; thus, strictly characteristic is equivalent to characteristic. This is not the case for infinite groups.

:::

::: {#o13 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Fully characteristic subgroup"}

### Fully characteristic subgroup {#Fully_characteristic_subgroup}

For an even stronger constraint, a *fully characteristic subgroup* (also, *fully invariant subgroup*) of a group $G$, is a subgroup *H* ≤ *G* that is invariant under every [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396#Types) of $G$ (and not just every automorphism):

$$\forall\varphi \in \operatorname{End}(G)\text{：} \varphi(H) \le H.$$

Every group has itself (the improper subgroup) and the trivial subgroup as two of its fully characteristic subgroups. The [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) of a group is always a fully characteristic subgroup.[^n3][^n4]

Every endomorphism of $G$ induces an endomorphism of $G/H$, which yields a map $\operatorname{End}(G) \to \operatorname{End}(G/H)$.

:::

### Verbal subgroup {#Verbal_subgroup}

An even stronger constraint is [verbal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21674070), which is the image of a fully invariant subgroup of a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) under a homomorphism. More generally, any [verbal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21674070) is always fully characteristic. For any [reduced free group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_free_group), and, in particular, for any [free group](wikipedia://en/59735), the converse also holds: every fully characteristic subgroup is verbal.

## Transitivity {#Transitivity}

The property of being characteristic or fully characteristic is [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463); if $H$ is a (fully) characteristic subgroup of $K$, and $K$ is a (fully) characteristic subgroup of $G$, then $H$ is a (fully) characteristic subgroup of $G$.

$$H \operatorname{char} K \operatorname{char} G \Rightarrow H \operatorname{char} G.$$

Moreover, while normality is not transitive, it is true that every characteristic subgroup of a normal subgroup is normal.

$$H \operatorname{char} K \lhd G \Rightarrow H \lhd G$$

Similarly, while being strictly characteristic (distinguished) is not transitive, it is true that every fully characteristic subgroup of a strictly characteristic subgroup is strictly characteristic.

However, unlike normality, if $H \operatorname{char} G$ and $K$ is a subgroup of $G$ containing $H$, then in general $H$ is not necessarily characteristic in $K$.

$$H \operatorname{char} G, H < K < G \text{⇏} H \operatorname{char} K$$

## Containments {#Containments}

Every subgroup that is fully characteristic is certainly strictly characteristic and characteristic; but a characteristic or even strictly characteristic subgroup need not be fully characteristic.

The [center of a group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) is always a strictly characteristic subgroup, but it is not always fully characteristic. For example, the finite group of order $12$, $\operatorname{Sym}(3) \times \mathbb{Z}/2 \mathbb{Z}$, has a homomorphism taking $(\pi,y)$ to $((1,2)^y,0)$, which takes the center, $1 \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, into a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(3) \times 1$, which meets the center only in the identity.

The relationship amongst these subgroup properties can be expressed as:

[Normal](wikipedia://en/21918) ⇐ **Characteristic** ⇐ Strictly characteristic ⇐ [Fully characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fully_characteristic_subgroup) ⇐ [Verbal](wikipedia://en/21674070)

## Examples {#Examples}

### Finite example {#Finite_example}

Consider the group $G = S_3 \times \mathbb{Z}_2$ (the group of order 12 that is the direct product of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of order 6 and a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 2). The center of $G$ is isomorphic to its second factor $\mathbb{Z}_2$. Note that the first factor, $S_3$, contains subgroups isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$, for instance ${e, (12)}$; let $f: \mathbb{Z}_2 \rightarrow \text{S}_3$ be the morphism mapping $\mathbb{Z}_2$ onto the indicated subgroup. Then the composition of the projection of $G$ onto its second factor $\mathbb{Z}_2$, followed by $f$, followed by the inclusion of $S_3$ into $G$ as its first factor, provides an endomorphism of $G$ under which the image of the center, $\mathbb{Z}_2$, is not contained in the center, so here the center is not a fully characteristic subgroup of $G$.

### Cyclic groups {#Cyclic_groups}

Every subgroup of a cyclic group is characteristic.

### Subgroup functors {#Subgroup_functors}

The [derived subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_subgroup) (or commutator subgroup) of a group is a verbal subgroup. The [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) is a fully invariant subgroup.

### Topological groups {#Topological_groups}

The [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787) of a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) is always a characteristic subgroup.

:::
