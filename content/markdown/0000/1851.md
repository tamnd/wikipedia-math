# Antiprism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Antiprism"}

*Image: Octagonal antiprism.png.* Octagonal antiprism

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **$n$-gonal antiprism** or **$n$-antiprism** is a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) composed of two [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) [direct](wikipedia://en/652164) copies (not mirror images) of an $n$-sided [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621), connected by an alternating band of $2n$ [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654). They are represented by the [Conway notation](wikipedia://en/5021705) $An$.

:::

Antiprisms are a subclass of [prismatoids](wikipedia://en/23917), and are a (degenerate) type of [snub polyhedron](wikipedia://en/3752558).

Antiprisms are similar to [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007), except that the bases are twisted relatively to each other, and that the side faces (connecting the bases) are $2n$ triangles, rather than $n$ [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278).

The [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) of an $n$-gonal antiprism is an $n$-gonal [trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/719845).

## History {#History}

In his 1619 book *[Harmonices Mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonices_Mundi)*, [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) observed the existence of the infinite family of antiprisms.[^o3] This has conventionally been thought of as the first discovery of these shapes, but they may have been known earlier: an unsigned printing block for the [net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_%28geometry%29) of a [hexagonal antiprism](wikipedia://en/2860125) has been attributed to [Hieronymus Andreae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieronymus_Andreae), who died in 1556.[^o4]

The German form of the word "antiprism" was used for these shapes in the 19th century; Karl Heinze credits its introduction to Theodor Wittstein.[^o5] Although the English "anti-prism" had been used earlier for an [optical prism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_%28optics%29) used to cancel the effects of a primary optical element,[^o6] the first use of "antiprism" in English in its geometric sense appears to be in the early 20th century in the works of [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328).[^o7]

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

### Right antiprism {#Right_antiprism}

For an antiprism with [regular $n$-gon](wikipedia://en/333306) bases, one usually considers the case where these two copies are twisted by an angle of $\tfrac{180}{n}$ degrees. The axis of a regular polygon is the line [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the polygon plane and lying in the polygon centre.

For an antiprism with [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) regular $n$-gon bases, twisted by an angle of $\tfrac{180}{n}$ degrees, more regularity is obtained if the bases have the same axis: are *[coaxial](wikipedia://en/542587)*; i.e. (for non-[coplanar](wikipedia://en/1508434) bases): if the line connecting the base centers is perpendicular to the base planes. Then the antiprism is called a **right antiprism**, and its $2n$ side faces are [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428).[^o11]

The [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/2694525) of a right $n$-antiprism is $D_{nd}$ of order $4n$ known as an [antiprismatic symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiprismatic_symmetry), because it could be obtained by rotation of the bottom half of a prism by $\pi/n$ in relation to the top half. A concave polyhedron created in this way would have this symmetry group, hence prefix "anti" before "prismatic".[^o12] There are two exceptions having groups different than $D_{nd}$:

- $n = 2$: the regular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), which has the larger symmetry group $T_{d}$ of order [$24$](wikipedia://en/2871265#Polyhedral_symmetry), which has three versions of $D_{2d}$ as subgroups;
- $n = 3$: the regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), which has the larger symmetry group $O_{h}$ of order $48$, which has four versions of $D_{3d}$ as subgroups.[^o13]

If a right 2- or 3-antiprism is not uniform, then its symmetry group is $D_{2d}$ or $D_{3d}$ as usual. The symmetry group contains [inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_in_a_point) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $n$ is odd.

The [rotation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29) is $D_{n}$ of order $2n$, except in the cases of:

- $n = 2$: the regular tetrahedron, which has the larger rotation group $T$ of order $12$, which has only one subgroup $D_{2}$;
- $n = 3$: the regular octahedron, which has the larger rotation group $O$ of order $24$, which has four versions of $D_{3}$ as subgroups.

If a right 2- or 3-antiprism is not uniform, then its rotation group is $D_{2}$ or $D_{3}$ as usual. The right $n$-antiprisms have congruent regular $n$-gon bases and congruent isosceles triangle side faces, thus have the same (dihedral) symmetry group as the uniform $n$-antiprism, for $n \ge 4$.

[Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) for the vertices of a right $n$-antiprism (i.e. with regular $n$-gon bases and $2n$ isosceles triangle side faces, circumradius of the bases equal to 1) are:

$$\left( \cos\frac{k\pi}{n}, \sin\frac{k\pi}{n}, (-1)^k h \right)$$

where $0 \le k \le 2n - 1$;

if the $n$-antiprism is uniform (i.e. if the triangles are equilateral), then:

$$2h^2 = \cos\frac{\pi}{n} - \cos\frac{2\pi}{n}.$$

### Uniform antiprism {#Uniform_antiprism}

A **[uniform](wikipedia://en/11064788) $n$-antiprism** has two [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [*regular*](wikipedia://en/333306) $n$-gons as base faces, and $2n$ [*equilateral*](wikipedia://en/173285) triangles as side faces. As do uniform prisms, the uniform antiprisms form an infinite class of vertex-transitive polyhedra. For $n = 2$, one has the digonal antiprism (degenerate antiprism), which is visually identical to the regular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606); for $n = 3$, the regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) is a *triangular antiprism* (non-degenerate antiprism).[^o11]

## Properties {#Properties}

### Volume and surface area {#Volume_and_surface_area}

Let $a$ be the edge-length of a [uniform](wikipedia://en/2537223) $n$-gonal antiprism; then the volume is:

$$V = \frac{n\sqrt{4\cos^2\frac{\pi}{2n}-1}\sin \frac{3\pi}{2n} }{12\sin^2\frac{\pi}{n}}~a^3,$$

and the surface area is:

$$A = \frac{n}{2} \left( \cot\frac{\pi}{n} + \sqrt{3} \right) a^2.$$

Furthermore, the volume of a regular right $n$-gonal antiprism with side length of its bases $l$ and height $h$ is given by:[^n1]

$$V = \frac{nhl^2}{12} \left( \csc\frac{\pi}{n} + 2\cot\frac{\pi}{n}\right).$$

#### Derivation {#Derivation}

The circumradius of the horizontal circumcircle of the regular $n$-gon at the base is

$$R(0) = \frac{l}{2\sin\frac{\pi}{n}}.$$

The vertices at the base are at

$$\left(\begin{array}{c}R(0)\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n} \\ R(0)\sin\frac{2\pi m}{n} \\ 0\end{array}\right),\quad m=0..n-1;$$

the vertices at the top are at

$$\left(\begin{array}{c}R(0)\cos\frac{2\pi (m+1/2)}{n}\\R(0)\sin\frac{2\pi (m+1/2)}{n}\\h\end{array}\right), \quad m=0..n-1.$$

Via linear interpolation, points on the outer triangular edges of the antiprism that connect vertices at the bottom with vertices at the top are at

$$\left(\begin{array}{c} \frac{R(0)}{h}[(h-z)\cos\frac{2\pi m}{n}+z\cos\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{n}]\\ \frac{R(0)}{h}[(h-z)\sin\frac{2\pi m}{n}+z\sin\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{n}]\\ \\z\end{array}\right), \quad 0\le z\le h, m=0..n-1$$

and at

$$\left(\begin{array}{c} \frac{R(0)}{h}[(h-z)\cos\frac{2\pi (m+1)}{n}+z\cos\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{n}]\\ \frac{R(0)}{h}[(h-z)\sin\frac{2\pi (m+1)}{n}+z\sin\frac{\pi(2m+1)}{n}]\\ \\z\end{array}\right), \quad 0\le z\le h, m=0..n-1.$$

By building the sums of the squares of the $x$ and $y$ coordinates in one of the previous two vectors, the squared circumradius of this section at altitude $z$ is

$$R(z)^2 = \frac{R(0)^2}{h^2}[h^2-2hz+2z^2+2z(h-z)\cos\frac{\pi}{n}].$$

The horizontal section at altitude $0\le z\le h$ above the base is a $2n$-gon (truncated $n$-gon) with $n$ sides of length $l_1(z)=l(1-z/h)$ alternating with $n$ sides of length $l_2(z)=lz/h$. (These are derived from the length of the difference of the previous two vectors.) It can be dissected into $n$ isoceless triangles of edges $R(z),R(z)$ and $l_1$ (semiperimeter $R(z)+l_1(z)/2$) plus $n$ isoceless triangles of edges $R(z),R(z)$ and $l_2(z)$ (semiperimeter $R(z)+l_2(z)/2$). According to Heron's formula the areas of these triangles are

$$Q_1(z) = \frac{R(0)^2}{h^2} (h-z)\left[(h-z)\cos\frac{\pi}{n}+z\right] \sin\frac{\pi}{n}$$

and

$$Q_2(z) = \frac{R(0)^2}{h^2} z\left[z\cos\frac{\pi}{n}+h-z\right] \sin\frac{\pi}{n} .$$

The area of the section is $n[Q_1(z)+Q_2(z)]$, and the volume is

$$V = n\int_0^h [Q_1(z)+Q_2(z)] dz = \frac{nh}{3}R(0)^2\sin\frac{\pi}{n}(1+2\cos\frac{\pi}{n}) = \frac{nh}{12}l^2\frac{1+2\cos\frac{\pi}{n}}{\sin\frac{\pi}{n}} .$$

The volume of a right $n$-gonal [prism](wikipedia://en/283007) with the same $l$ and $h$ is:

$$V_{\mathrm{prism}}=\frac{nhl^2}{4} \cot\frac{\pi}{n}$$

which is smaller than that of an antiprism.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### In higher dimensions {#In_higher_dimensions}

Four-dimensional antiprisms can be defined as having two [dual polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polyhedra) as parallel opposite faces, so that each [three-dimensional face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29) between them comes from two dual parts of the polyhedra: a vertex and a dual polygon, or two dual edges. Every three-dimensional convex polyhedron is combinatorially equivalent to one of the two opposite faces of a four-dimensional antiprism, constructed from its [canonical polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_polyhedron) and its polar dual.[^o24] However, there exist four-dimensional polychora that cannot be combined with their duals to form five-dimensional antiprisms.[^o25]

A **right star $n$-antiprism** has two [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) coaxial [regular](wikipedia://en/333306) [***convex***](wikipedia://en/1817885) or [***star***](wikipedia://en/313303) polygon base faces, and $2n$ [isosceles triangle](wikipedia://en/315428) side faces.

Any star antiprism with *regular* convex or star polygon bases can be made a *right* star antiprism (by translating and/or twisting one of its bases, if necessary).

*Image: Antiprisms.pdf.* All the non-star and star uniform antiprisms up to 15 sides, together with those of a 29-gon (or [icosaenneagon](wikipedia://en/39728316#List_of_n-gons_by_Greek_numerical_prefixes)). For example, the icosaenneagrammic crossed antiprism ($29/q$) with the greatest $q$, such that it can be uniform, has $q = 19$ and is depicted at the bottom right corner of the image. For $q \ge 20$ up to $28$ the crossed antiprism cannot be uniform.Note: Octagrammic crossed antiprism (8/5) is missing.

In the retrograde forms, but not in the prograde forms, the triangles joining the convex or star bases intersect the axis of rotational symmetry. Thus:

- Retrograde star antiprisms with regular convex polygon bases cannot have all equal edge lengths, and so cannot be uniform. "Exception": a retrograde star antiprism with equilateral triangle bases (vertex configuration: 3.3/2.3.3) can be uniform; but then, it has the appearance of an equilateral triangle: it is a degenerate star polyhedron.
- Similarly, some retrograde star antiprisms with regular star polygon bases cannot have all equal edge lengths, and so cannot be uniform. Example: a retrograde star antiprism with regular star $\{7/5\}$-gon bases (vertex configuration: 3.3.3.7/5) cannot be uniform.

Also, star antiprism compounds with regular star $\{p/q\}$-gon bases can be constructed if $p$ and $q$ have common factors. Example: a star (10/4)-antiprism is the compound of two star (5/2)-antiprisms.

The dual of a ($p/q$) antiprism with $q < p/2$ is a [$p/q$-trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/719845#Star_trapezohedron), and the dual of a ($p/q$) antiprism with $q > p/2$, i.e. crossed antiprism, is a ($p/q$) concave trapezohedron, where "concave" refers to 2-dimensional faces of the 3D solid.[^o29]

#### Number of uniform crossed antiprisms {#Number_of_uniform_crossed_antiprisms}

If the notation $(p/q)$ is used for an antiprism, then for $q > p/2$ the antiprism is crossed (by definition) and for $q < p/2$ is not. In this section all antiprisms are assumed to be non-degenerate, i.e. $p \ge 3$, $q \ne p/2$. Also, the condition $(p,q) = 1$ ($p$ and $q$ are relatively prime) holds, as compounds are excluded from counting. The number of uniform crossed antiprisms for fixed $p$ can be determined using simple inequalities. The condition on possible $q$ is

$\tfrac{p}{2} < q < \tfrac{2}{3} p$ and $(p,q) = 1.$

Examples:

- $p$ = 3: 2 ≤ $q$ ≤ 1 – a uniform triangular crossed antiprism does not exist.
- $p$ = 5: 3 ≤ $q$ ≤ 3 – one antiprism of the type (5/3) can be uniform.
- $p$ = 29: 15 ≤ $q$ ≤ 19 – there are five possibilities (15 thru 19) shown in the rightmost column, below the (29/1) convex antiprism, on the image above.
- $p$ = 15: 8 ≤ $q$ ≤ 9 – antiprism with $q$ = 8 is a solution, but $q$ = 9 must be rejected, as (15,9) = 3 and $\tfrac{15}{9}$ = $\tfrac{5}{3}$. The antiprism (15/9) is a compound of three antiprisms (5/3). Since 9 satisfies the inequalities, the compound can be uniform, and if it is, then its parts must be. Indeed, the antiprism (5/3) can be uniform by example 2.
