::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Center"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **center** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ is the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of elements that [commute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) with every element of $G$. It is denoted $Z(G)$, from German *Zentrum,* meaning *center*. In [set-builder notation](wikipedia://en/220089),

:::

$$Z(G) = \{z \in G\mid \forall g \in G, zg = gz\}.$$

The center is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918), $Z(G)\triangleleft G$, and also a [characteristic](wikipedia://en/7198) subgroup, but is not necessarily [fully characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fully_characteristic_subgroup). The [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526), $G / Z(G)$, is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the [inner automorphism](wikipedia://en/61839) group, $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$.

A group $G$ is abelian if and only if $Z(G) = G$. At the other extreme, a group is said to be **centerless** if $Z(G)$ is [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059); i.e., consists only of the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962).

The elements of the center are **central elements**.

## As a subgroup {#As_a_subgroup}

The center of *G* is always a [subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgroup_%28mathematics%29) of $G$. In particular:

1. $Z(G)$ contains the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of $G$, because it commutes with every element of $g$, by definition: $eg = g = ge$, where $e$ is the identity;
1. If $x$ and $y$ are in $Z(G)$, then so is $xy$, by associativity: $(xy)g = x(yg) = x(gy) = (xg)y = (gx)y = g(xy)$ for each $g \in G$; i.e., $Z(G)$ is closed;
1. If $x$ is in $Z(G)$, then so is $x^{-1}$ as, for all $g$ in $G$, $x^{-1}$ commutes with $g$: $(gx = xg) \Rightarrow (x^{-1}gxx^{-1} = x^{-1}xgx^{-1}) \Rightarrow (x^{-1}g = gx^{-1})$.

Furthermore, the center of $G$ is always an [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) and [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $G$. Since all elements of $Z(G)$ commute, it is closed under [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_closure).

A group homomorphism $f : G \to H$ might not restrict to a homomorphism between their centers. The image elements $f (g)$ commute with the image $f ( G )$, but they need not commute with all of $H$ unless $f$ is surjective. Thus the center mapping $G\to Z(G)$ is not a functor between categories Grp and Ab, since it does not induce a map of arrows.

## Conjugacy classes and centralizers {#Conjugacy_classes_and_centralizers}

By definition, an element is central whenever its [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176) contains only the element itself; i.e. $\operatorname{Cl}(g) = \{g\}$.

The center is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all the [centralizers](wikipedia://en/145661) of elements of $G$:

> $$Z(G) = \bigcap_{g\in G} Z_G(g).$$

As centralizers are subgroups, this again shows that the center is a subgroup.

## Conjugation {#Conjugation}

Consider the map $f : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(G)$, from $G$ to the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of $G$ defined by $f(g) = \phi_{g}$, where $\phi_{g}$ is the automorphism of $G$ defined by

$$f(g)(h) = \phi_{g}(h) = ghg^{-1}.$$

The function, $f$ is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396), and its [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is precisely the center of $G$, and its image is called the [inner automorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_automorphism_group) of $G$, denoted $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$. By the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem) we get,

$$G/Z(G) \simeq \operatorname{Inn}(G).$$

The [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) of this map is the group $\operatorname{Out}(G)$ of [outer automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_automorphism), and these form the [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215)

$$1 \longrightarrow Z(G) \longrightarrow G \longrightarrow \operatorname{Aut}(G) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Out}(G) \longrightarrow 1.$$

## Examples {#Examples}

- The center of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), $G$, is all of $G$.
- The center of the [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518), $H$, is the set of matrices of the form: $$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & z\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
- The center of a [nonabelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonabelian_group) [simple group](wikipedia://en/47600) is trivial.
- The center of the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267), $D_{n}$, is trivial for odd $n \ge 3$. For even $n \ge 4$, the center consists of the identity element together with the 180° rotation of the [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621).
- The center of the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130), $Q_{8} = \{1, -1, i, -i, j, -j, k, -k\}$, is $\{1, -1\}$.
- The center of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901), $S_{n}$, is trivial for $n \ge 3$.
- The center of the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726), $A_{n}$, is trivial for $n \ge 4$.
- The center of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$, $\operatorname{GL}_{n}(F)$, is the collection of [scalar matrices](wikipedia://en/174080), $\{\operatorname{sI}_{n} \mid s \in F \backslash \{0\}\}$.
- The center of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954), $O_{n}(F)$ is $\{I_{n}, -I_{n}\}$.
- The center of the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group), $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ is the whole group when $n = 2$, and otherwise $\{I_{n}, -I_{n}\}$ when *n* is even, and trivial when *n* is odd.
- The center of the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993), $U(n)$ is $\left\{ e^{i\theta} \cdot I_n \mid \theta \in [0, 2\pi) \right\}$.
- The center of the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997), $\operatorname{SU}(n)$ is $\left\lbrace e^{i\theta} \cdot I_n \mid \theta = \frac{2k\pi}{n}, k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1 \right\rbrace$.
- The center of the multiplicative group of non-zero [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) is the multiplicative group of non-zero [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).
- Using the [class equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_equation), one can prove that the center of any non-trivial [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [p-group](wikipedia://en/24664) is non-trivial.
- If the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $G/Z(G)$ is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327), $G$ is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) (and hence $G = Z(G)$, so $G/Z(G)$ is trivial).
- The center of the [Rubik's Cube group](wikipedia://en/2467690) consists of two elements – the identity (i.e. the solved state) and the [superflip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superflip). The center of the [Pocket Cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pocket_Cube) group is trivial.
- The center of the [Megaminx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaminx) group has order 2, and the center of the [Kilominx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilominx) group is trivial.

:::

## Higher centers {#Higher_centers}

Quotienting out by the center of a group yields a sequence of groups called the **[upper central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_central_series)**:

$$(G_{0} = G) \longrightarrow (G_{1} = G_{0}/Z(G_{0})) \longrightarrow (G_{2} = G_{1}/Z(G_{1})) \longrightarrow \cdots$$

The kernel of the map $G \to G_{i}$ is the **$i$th center**[^o9] of $G$ (**second center**, **third center**, etc.), denoted $Z^{i}(G)$.[^o10] Concretely, the ($i+1$)-st center comprises the elements that commute with all elements up to an element of the $i$th center. Following this definition, one can define the 0th center of a group to be the identity subgroup. This can be continued to [transfinite ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfinite_ordinals) by [transfinite induction](wikipedia://en/30461); the union of all the higher centers is called the **[hypercenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercenter)**.[^o11]

The [ascending chain](wikipedia://en/30330#Chains) of subgroups

$$1 \le Z(G) \le Z^{2}(G) \le \cdots$$

stabilizes at *i* (equivalently, $Z^{i}(G) = Z^{i+1}(G)$) if and only if $G_{i}$ is centerless.

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- For a centerless group, all higher centers are zero, which is the case $Z^{0}(G) = Z^{1}(G)$ of stabilization.
- By [Grün's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grün's_lemma), the quotient of a [perfect group](wikipedia://en/585271) by its center is centerless, hence all higher centers equal the center. This is a case of stabilization at $Z^{1}(G) = Z^{2}(G)$.

:::
