# Automorphism

*Image: Klein-automorphism.svg.* An automorphism of the Klein four-group shown as a mapping between two Cayley graphs, a permutation in cycle notation, and a mapping between two Cayley tables.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Automorphism"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **automorphism** is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) from a [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961) to itself. It is, in some sense, a [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) of the object, and a way of [mapping](wikipedia://en/516931) the object to itself while preserving all of its structure. The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all automorphisms of an object forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), called the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772). It is, loosely speaking, the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of the object.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) such as a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), or [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), an *automorphism* is simply a [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) of an object into itself. (The definition of a homomorphism depends on the type of algebraic structure; see, for example, [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396), [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411), and [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator).)

More generally, for an object in some [category](wikipedia://en/53759), an automorphism is a morphism of the object to itself that has an inverse morphism; that is, a morphism $f: X\to X$ is an automorphism if there is a morphism $g: X\to X$ such that $g\circ f= f\circ g = \operatorname {id}_X,$ where $\operatorname {id}_X$ is the [identity morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_morphism) of $X$. For algebraic structures, the two definitions are equivalent; in this case, the identity morphism is simply the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069), and is often called the *trivial automorphism*.

:::

## Automorphism group {#Automorphism_group}

The automorphisms of an object $X$ form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891), which is called the *[automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772)* of $X$. This results straightforwardly from the definition of a category.

The automorphism group of an object $X$ in a category $C$ is often denoted $\operatorname{Aut}_{C}(X)$, or simply Aut(*X*) if the category is clear from context.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), an arbitrary [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of the elements of a set *X* is an automorphism. The automorphism group of *X* is also called the symmetric group on *X*.
- In [elementary arithmetic](wikipedia://en/1567386), the set of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), $\mathbb{Z}$, considered as a group under addition, has a unique nontrivial automorphism: negation. Considered as a ring, however, it has only the trivial automorphism. Generally speaking, negation is an automorphism of any [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), but not of a ring or field.
- A group automorphism is a [group isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) from a group to itself. Informally, it is a permutation of the group elements such that the structure remains unchanged. For every group *G* there is a natural group homomorphism *G* → Aut(*G*) whose [image](wikipedia://en/579311) is the group Inn(*G*) of [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839) and whose [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is the [center](wikipedia://en/7125) of *G*. Thus, if *G* has [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) center it can be embedded into its own automorphism group.[^o7]
- In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) *V* → *V*. An automorphism is an invertible linear operator on *V*. When the vector space is finite-dimensional, the automorphism group of *V* is the same as the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), GL(*V*). (The algebraic structure of [all endomorphisms of *V*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endomorphism_algebra) is itself an algebra over the same base field as *V*, whose [invertible elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) precisely consist of GL(*V*).)
- A field automorphism is a [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) to itself.
  - The field $\mathbb{Q}$ of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) has no other automorphism than the identity, since an automorphism must fix the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225) $0$ and the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) $1$; the sum of a finite number of $1$ must be fixed, as well as the additive inverses of these sums (that is, the automorphism fixes all [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers)); finally, since every rational number is the quotient of two integers, all rational numbers must be fixed by any automorphism.
  - The field $\mathbb{R}$ of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) has no automorphisms other than the identity. Indeed, the rational numbers must be fixed by every automorphism, per above; an automorphism must preserve inequalities since $x<y$ is equivalent to $\exists z\mid y-x=z^2,$ and the latter property is preserved by every automorphism; finally every real number must be fixed since it is the [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound) of a sequence of rational numbers.
  - The field $\mathbb{C}$ of the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) has a unique nontrivial automorphism that fixes the real numbers. It is the [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation), which maps $i$ to $-i.$ The [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) implies the existence of [uncountably many](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) automorphisms that do not fix the real numbers.[^o8][^o9]
  - The study of automorphisms of [algebraic field extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_field_extension) is the starting point and the main object of [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316).
- The automorphism group of the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) ($\mathbb H$) as a ring are the inner automorphisms, by the [Skolem–Noether theorem](wikipedia://en/693453): maps of the form *a* ↦ *bab*<sup>−1</sup>. This group is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/186057) to [SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29), the group of rotations in 3-dimensional space.
- The automorphism group of the [octonions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octonions) ($\mathbb O$) is the [exceptional](wikipedia://en/6573043) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) [G<sub>2</sub>](wikipedia://en/292864).
- In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) an [automorphism of a graph](wikipedia://en/15094186) is a permutation of the nodes that preserves edges and non-edges. In particular, if two nodes are joined by an edge, so are their images under the permutation.
- In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an automorphism may be called a [motion](wikipedia://en/36132944) of the space. Specialized terminology is also used:
  - In [metric geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_geometry) an automorphism is a self-[isometry](wikipedia://en/254777). The automorphism group is also called the [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694).
  - In the category of [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), an automorphism is a [biholomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biholomorphy) map (also called a [conformal map](wikipedia://en/50627)), from a surface to itself. For example, the automorphisms of the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) are [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493).
  - An automorphism of a differentiable [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) *M* is a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) from *M* to itself. The automorphism group is sometimes denoted Diff(*M*).
  - In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), morphisms between topological spaces are called [continuous maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29), and an automorphism of a topological space is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) of the space to itself, or self-homeomorphism (see [homeomorphism group](wikipedia://en/1531409)). In this example it is *not sufficient* for a morphism to be bijective to be an isomorphism.

:::

## History {#History}

One of the earliest group automorphisms (automorphism of a group, not simply a group of automorphisms of points) was given by the Irish mathematician [William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1856, in his [icosian calculus](wikipedia://en/316081), where he discovered an order two automorphism,[^o11] writing:

> so that $\mu$ is a new fifth root of unity, connected with the former fifth root $\lambda$ by relations of perfect reciprocity.

## Inner and outer automorphisms {#Inner_and_outer_automorphisms}

In some categories—notably [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), and [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944)—it is possible to separate automorphisms into two types, called "inner" and "outer" automorphisms.

In the case of groups, the [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839) are the conjugations by the elements of the group itself. For each element *a* of a group *G*, conjugation by *a* is the operation *φ*<sub>*a*</sub> : *G* → *G* given by *φ*<sub>*a*</sub>(*g*) = *aga*<sup>−1</sup> (or *a*<sup>−1</sup>*ga*; usage varies). One can easily check that conjugation by *a* is a group automorphism. The inner automorphisms form a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of Aut(*G*), denoted by Inn(*G*); this is called [Goursat's lemma](wikipedia://en/5114212).

The other automorphisms are called [outer automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_automorphism). The [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) Aut(*G*) / Inn(*G*) is usually denoted by Out(*G*); the non-trivial elements are the [cosets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosets) that contain the outer automorphisms.

The same definition holds in any [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) or [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) where *a* is any [invertible element](wikipedia://en/289666). For [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944) the definition is slightly different.
