# Charles Sanders Peirce

::: {#o8 .subject cue=lead name="Charles Sanders Peirce"}

**Charles Sanders Peirce** ([^o9] ; September 10, 1839 – April 19, 1914) was an American scientist, mathematician, [logician](wikipedia://en/46426065), and philosopher who is sometimes known as "the father of [pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170)".[^o10][^o11] According to philosopher [Paul Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Weiss_%28philosopher%29), writing in 1934, Peirce was "the most original and versatile of America's philosophers and America's greatest logician".[^o10] [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) wrote in 1959, "he was one of the most original minds of the later nineteenth century and certainly the greatest American thinker ever".[^o84]

:::

Educated as a chemist and employed as a scientist for thirty years, Peirce made major contributions to logic, such as theories of [relations](wikipedia://en/7870034) and [quantification](wikipedia://en/43507260). [C. I. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Irving_Lewis) wrote, "The contributions of C. S. Peirce to symbolic logic are more numerous and varied than those of any other writer—at least in the nineteenth century." For Peirce, logic also encompassed much of what is now called [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) and the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science). He saw logic as the formal branch of [semiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics) or study of [signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_%28semiotics%29), of which he is a founder, which foreshadowed the debate among [logical positivists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivists) and proponents of [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language) that dominated 20th-century Western philosophy. Peirce's study of signs also included a [tripartite theory of predication](wikipedia://en/13529329).

Additionally, he defined the concept of [abductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/60459), as well as rigorously formulating [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881) and [deductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/61093). He was one of the [founders of statistics](wikipedia://en/19095208). As early as 1886, he saw that [logical operations could be carried out by electrical switching circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gate). The same idea was used decades later to produce digital computers.[^o12]

In [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), Peirce was an "[objective idealist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_idealism)" in the tradition of German philosopher [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) as well as a [scholastic realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotistic_realism) about universals. He also held a commitment to the ideas of continuity and chance as real features of the universe, views he labeled [synechism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synechism) and [tychism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tychism) respectively. Peirce believed an epistemic [fallibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallibilism) and anti-[skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skepticism) went along with these views.

## Biography {#Biography}

### Early life {#Early_life}

*Image: Charles Sanders Peirce's birthplace building.jpg.* Peirce's birthplace. Now part of [Lesley University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesley_University)'s Graduate School of Arts and Social Sciences.

Peirce was born at 3 Phillips Place in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts). He was the son of Sarah Hunt Mills and [Benjamin Peirce](wikipedia://en/364270), himself a professor of mathematics and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University). At age 12, Charles read his older brother's copy of [Richard Whately](wikipedia://en/207580)'s *Elements of Logic*, then the leading English-language text on the subject. So began his lifelong fascination with logic and reasoning.[^o15]

He suffered from his late teens onward from a nervous condition then known as "facial neuralgia", which would today be diagnosed as [trigeminal neuralgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigeminal_neuralgia). His biographer, Joseph Brent, says that when in the throes of its pain "he was, at first, almost stupefied, and then aloof, cold, depressed, extremely suspicious, impatient of the slightest crossing, and subject to violent outbursts of temper".[^o16] Its consequences may have led to the social isolation of his later life.

### Education {#Education}

Peirce went on to earn a Bachelor of Arts degree and a Master of Arts degree (1862) from Harvard. In 1863 the [Lawrence Scientific School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Scientific_School) awarded him a [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) degree, Harvard's first *summa cum laude* [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) degree.[^o18] His academic record was otherwise undistinguished.[^o19] At Harvard, he began lifelong friendships with [Francis Ellingwood Abbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Ellingwood_Abbot), [Chauncey Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauncey_Wright), and [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James).[^o20] One of his Harvard instructors, [Charles William Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_William_Eliot), formed an unfavorable opinion of Peirce. This proved fateful, because Eliot, while President of Harvard (1869–1909—a period encompassing nearly all of Peirce's working life), repeatedly vetoed Peirce's employment at the university.[^o21]

### United States Coast Survey {#United_States_Coast_Survey}

*Image: Charles Sanders Peirce in 1859.jpg.* Peirce in 1859

Between 1859 and 1891, Peirce was intermittently employed in various scientific capacities by the United States Coast Survey, which in 1878 was renamed the [United States Coast and Geodetic Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Coast_and_Geodetic_Survey),[^o23] where he enjoyed his highly influential father's protection until the latter's death in 1880.[^o24] At the Survey, he worked mainly in [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) and [gravimetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravimetry), refining the use of [pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) to determine small local variations in the Earth's [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity).[^o23]

#### American Civil War {#American_Civil_War}

This employment exempted Peirce from having to take part in the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War); it would have been very awkward for him to do so, as the [Boston Brahmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Brahmin) Peirces sympathized with the [Confederacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confederate_States_of_America).[^o26] No members of the Peirce family volunteered or enlisted. Peirce grew up in a home where [white supremacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacy) was taken for granted, and slavery was considered natural.[^o27] Peirce's father had described himself as a [secessionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secession_in_the_United_States) until the outbreak of the war, after which he became a [Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_%28American_Civil_War%29) partisan, providing donations to the [Sanitary Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Sanitary_Commission), the leading Northern war charity.

Peirce liked to use the following [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046) to illustrate the unreliability of [traditional](wikipedia://en/321652) forms of logic (for the first premise arguably [assumes the conclusion](wikipedia://en/43582)):[^o28] All Men are equal in their political rights. Negroes are Men. Therefore, negroes are equal in political rights to whites.

#### Travels to Europe {#Travels_to_Europe}

He was elected a resident fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in January 1867.[^o30] The Survey sent him to Europe five times,[^o31] first in 1870, returning in 1871, as part of a group sent to observe a [solar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse) that was to take place on December 22, 1870.[^o32] There, he sought out [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289), [William Stanley Jevons](wikipedia://en/207021), and [William Kingdon Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820),[^o33] British mathematicians and logicians whose turn of mind resembled his own. In 1875, Peirce conducted experiments at the [Geneva Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva_Observatory) with his [reversible pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kater's_pendulum), and commissioned the Société genevoise d'instruments physiques in [Plainpalais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plainpalais) a [vacuum chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_chamber) for his pendulum.[^o34][^o35] This work led in 1901 to the [Earth ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/16112368) proposed by [Friedrich Robert Helmert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Robert_Helmert), whose parameter values were remarkably close to reality.[^o36]

#### Harvard observatory {#Harvard_observatory}

From 1869 to 1872, he was employed as an assistant in Harvard's astronomical observatory, doing important work on determining the brightness of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) and the shape of the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way).[^o38] In 1872 he founded the [Metaphysical Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Metaphysical_Club), a conversational philosophical club that Peirce, the future [Supreme Court Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_Justice) [Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Wendell_Holmes_Jr.), the philosopher and psychologist [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James), amongst others, formed in January 1872 in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts), and dissolved in December 1872. Other members of the club included [Chauncey Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauncey_Wright), [John Fiske](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fiske_%28philosopher%29), [Francis Ellingwood Abbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Ellingwood_Abbot), [Nicholas St. John Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_St._John_Green), and [Joseph Bangs Warner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bangs_Warner).[^o39] The discussions eventually birthed Peirce's notion of pragmatism.

#### National Academy of Sciences {#National_Academy_of_Sciences}

*Image: Peirce Quincuncial Projection 1879.jpg.* "The World on a [Quincuncial Projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peirce_quincuncial_projection)", 1879.[^o41] Peirce's projection of a sphere onto a square [keeps angles true](wikipedia://en/50627) except at four isolated points on the equator, and has less scale variation than the [Mercator projection](wikipedia://en/20771). It can be [tessellated](wikipedia://en/321671); that is, multiple copies can be joined continuously edge-to-edge.

On April 20, 1877, he was elected a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences).[^o42] Also in 1877, he proposed measuring the meter as so many [wavelengths](wikipedia://en/33125) of light of a certain [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779),[^o43] the kind of definition employed [from 1960 to 1983 and since 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre#Wavelength_definition).

In 1879 Peirce developed [Peirce quincuncial projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peirce_quincuncial_projection), having been inspired by [H. A. Schwarz](wikipedia://en/3165834)'s 1869 [conformal transformation of a circle onto a polygon of *n* sides](wikipedia://en/1990796) (known as the Schwarz–Christoffel mapping).[^o44]

#### 1880 to 1891 {#1880_to_1891}

During the 1880s, Peirce's indifference to bureaucratic detail waxed while his Survey work's quality and timeliness waned. Peirce took years to write reports that he should have completed in months. Meanwhile, he wrote entries, ultimately thousands, during 1883–1909 on philosophy, logic, science, and other subjects for the encyclopedic *[Century Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Century_Dictionary)*.[^o46] In 1885, an investigation by the [Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_B._Allison) Commission exonerated Peirce, but led to the dismissal of Superintendent [Julius Hilgard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Hilgard) and several other Coast Survey employees for misuse of public funds.[^o47] In 1891, Peirce resigned from the Coast Survey at Superintendent [Thomas Corwin Mendenhall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Corwin_Mendenhall)'s request.[^o48]

### Johns Hopkins University {#Johns_Hopkins_University}

In 1879, Peirce was appointed lecturer in logic at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University), which had strong departments in areas that interested him, such as philosophy ([Royce](wikipedia://en/494060) and [Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey) completed their PhDs at Hopkins), psychology (taught by [G. Stanley Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._Stanley_Hall) and studied by [Joseph Jastrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Jastrow), who coauthored a landmark empirical study with Peirce), and mathematics (taught by [J.&nbsp;J. Sylvester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.&nbsp;J._Sylvester), who came to admire Peirce's work on mathematics and logic). His *[Studies in Logic by Members of the Johns Hopkins University](wikipedia://en/3967710#SIL)* (1883) contained works by himself and [Allan Marquand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_Marquand), [Christine Ladd](wikipedia://en/9397829), [Benjamin Ives Gilman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Ives_Gilman), and Oscar Howard Mitchell,[^o50] several of whom were his graduate students.[^o51] Peirce's nontenured position at Hopkins was the only academic appointment he ever held.

Brent documents something Peirce never suspected, namely that his efforts to obtain academic employment, grants, and scientific respectability were repeatedly frustrated by the covert opposition of a major Canadian-American scientist of the day, [Simon Newcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Newcomb).[^o52] Newcomb had been a favourite student of Peirce's father; although "no doubt quite bright", "like [Salieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Salieri) in [Peter Shaffer's Amadeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amadeus_%28play%29) he also had just enough talent to recognize he was not a genius and just enough pettiness to resent someone who was". Additionally "an intensely devout and literal-minded Christian of rigid moral standards", he was appalled by what he considered Peirce's personal shortcomings.[^o53] Peirce's efforts may also have been hampered by what Brent characterizes as "his difficult personality".[^o54] In contrast, [Keith Devlin](wikipedia://en/574895) believes that Peirce's work was too far ahead of his time to be appreciated by the academic establishment of the day and that this played a large role in his inability to obtain a tenured position.[^o55]

### Personal life {#Personal_life}

*Image: JulietteAndCharles.JPG.* Juliette and Charles by a well at their home Arisbe in 1907

Peirce's personal life undoubtedly worked against his professional success. After his first wife, [Harriet Melusina Fay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melusina_Fay_Peirce) ("Zina"), left him in 1875,[^o57] Peirce, while still legally married, became involved with [Juliette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juliette_Peirce), whose last name, given variously as Froissy and Pourtalai,[^o58] and nationality (she spoke French)[^o59] remain uncertain.[^o60] When his divorce from Zina became final in 1883, he married Juliette.[^o61] That year, Newcomb pointed out to a Johns Hopkins trustee that Peirce, while a Hopkins employee, had lived and traveled with a woman to whom he was not married; the ensuing scandal led to his dismissal in January 1884.[^o62] Over the years Peirce sought academic employment at various universities without success.[^o63] He had no children by either marriage.[^o64]

### Later life and poverty {#Later_life_and_poverty}

*Image: Charles S. Peirce house PA1.jpg.* Arisbe in 2011

*Image: Gravestone Charles Sanders Peirce and Juliette Peirce.jpg.* Charles and Juliette Peirce's grave

In 1887, Peirce spent part of his inheritance from his parents to buy 2000 acre of rural land near [Milford, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milford,_Pennsylvania), which never yielded an economic return.[^o66] There he had an 1854 farmhouse remodeled to his design.[^o67] The Peirces named the property "[Arisbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juliette_Peirce#Arisbe)". There they lived with few interruptions for the rest of their lives,[^o68] Charles writing prolifically, with much of his work remaining unpublished to this day (see Works). Living beyond their means soon led to grave financial and legal difficulties.[^o69] Charles spent much of his last two decades unable to afford heat in winter and subsisting on old bread donated by the local baker. Unable to afford new stationery, he wrote on the [verso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verso) side of old manuscripts. An outstanding warrant for assault and unpaid debts led to his being a fugitive in New York City for a while.[^o70] Several people, including his brother [James Mills Peirce](wikipedia://en/55559637)[^o71] and his neighbors, relatives of [Gifford Pinchot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Pinchot), settled his debts and paid his property taxes and mortgage.[^o72]

Peirce did some scientific and engineering consulting and wrote much for meager pay, mainly encyclopedic dictionary entries, and reviews for *[The Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nation_%28U.S._periodical%29)* (with whose editor, [Wendell Phillips Garrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendell_Phillips_Garrison), he became friendly). He did translations for the [Smithsonian Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_Institution), at its director [Samuel Langley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Langley)'s instigation. Peirce also did substantial mathematical calculations for Langley's research on powered flight. Hoping to make money, Peirce tried inventing.[^o73] He began but did not complete several books.[^o74] In 1888, President [Grover Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grover_Cleveland) appointed him to the [Assay Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assay_Commission).[^o75]

From 1890 on, he had a friend and admirer in Judge Francis C. Russell of Chicago,[^o76] who introduced Peirce to editor [Paul Carus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Carus) and owner [Edward C. Hegeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_C._Hegeler) of the pioneering American philosophy journal *[The Monist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Monist)*, which eventually published at least 14 articles by Peirce.[^o77] He wrote many texts in [James Mark Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mark_Baldwin)'s *[Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology](wikipedia://en/3967710#Peirce's_definitions_in_the_Baldwin)* (1901–1905); half of those credited to him appear to have been written actually by [Christine Ladd-Franklin](wikipedia://en/9397829) under his supervision.[^o78] He applied in 1902 to the newly formed [Carnegie Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Institution) for a grant to write a systematic book describing his life's work. The application was doomed; his nemesis, Newcomb, served on the Carnegie Institution executive committee, and its president had been president of Johns Hopkins at the time of Peirce's dismissal.[^o79]

The one who did the most to help Peirce in these desperate times was his old friend [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James), dedicating his *Will to Believe* (1897) to Peirce, and arranging for Peirce to be paid to give two series of lectures at or near Harvard (1898 and 1903).[^o80] Most important, each year from 1907 until James's death in 1910, James wrote to his friends in the Boston intelligentsia to request financial aid for Peirce; the fund continued even after James died. Peirce reciprocated by designating James's eldest son as his heir should Juliette predecease him.[^o81] It has been believed that this was also why Peirce used "Santiago" ("St. James" in English) as a middle name, but he appeared in print as early as 1890 as Charles Santiago Peirce. (See [Charles Santiago Sanders Peirce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Santiago_Sanders_Peirce) for discussion and references).

### Death {#Death}

Peirce died destitute in [Milford, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milford,_Pennsylvania), twenty years before his widow. Juliette Peirce kept the urn with Peirce's ashes at Arisbe. In 1934, Pennsylvania Governor [Gifford Pinchot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gifford_Pinchot) arranged for Juliette's burial in Milford Cemetery. The urn with Peirce's ashes was interred with Juliette.

## Legacy {#Legacy}

[Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) (1959) wrote "Beyond doubt \[...\] he was one of the most original minds of the later nineteenth century and certainly the greatest American thinker ever".[^o84] Russell and [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395)'s *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*, published from 1910 to 1913, does not mention Peirce (Peirce's work was not widely known until later).[^o85] [A. N. Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._N._Whitehead), while reading some of Peirce's unpublished manuscripts soon after arriving at Harvard in 1924, was struck by how Peirce had anticipated his own "process" thinking. (On Peirce and [process metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_metaphysics), see Lowe 1964.[^o38]) [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) viewed Peirce as "one of the greatest philosophers of all times".[^o86][^o87]

Yet, Peirce's achievements were not immediately recognized. [Umberto Eco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umberto_Eco) described Peirce as "undoubtedly the greatest unpublished writer of our generation".[^o88] The [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Encyclopedia_of_Philosophy) says of Peirce that although "long considered an eccentric figure whose contribution to pragmatism was to provide its name and whose importance was as an influence upon James and Dewey, Peirce's significance in his own right is now largely accepted."[^o89] His imposing contemporaries [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James) and [Josiah Royce](wikipedia://en/494060)[^o90] admired him and [Cassius Jackson Keyser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassius_Jackson_Keyser), at Columbia and [C. K. Ogden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._K._Ogden), wrote about Peirce with respect but to no immediate effect.

The first scholar to give Peirce his considered professional attention was Royce's student [Morris Raphael Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Raphael_Cohen), the editor of an anthology of Peirce's writings entitled *[Chance, Love, and Logic](wikipedia://en/3967710#CLL)* (1923), and the author of the first bibliography of Peirce's scattered writings.[^o91] [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey) studied under Peirce at Johns Hopkins.[^o51] From 1916 onward, Dewey's writings repeatedly mention Peirce with deference. His 1938 *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* is much influenced by Peirce.[^o92] The publication of the first six volumes of *Collected Papers* (1931–1935) was the most important event to date in Peirce studies and one that Cohen made possible by raising the needed funds;[^o93] however it did not prompt an outpouring of secondary studies. The editors of those volumes, [Charles Hartshorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Hartshorne) and [Paul Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Weiss_%28philosopher%29), did not become Peirce specialists. Early landmarks of the secondary literature include the monographs by Buchler (1939), [Feibleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Feibleman) (1946), and [Goudge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._A._Goudge) (1950), the 1941 PhD thesis by [Arthur W. Burks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_W._Burks) (who went on to edit volumes 7 and 8), and the studies edited by Wiener and Young (1952). The [Charles S. Peirce Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_S._Peirce_Society) was founded in 1946. Its *Transactions*, an academic quarterly specializing in Peirce's pragmatism and American philosophy has appeared since 1965.[^o94] (See Phillips 2014, 62 for discussion of Peirce and Dewey relative to [transactionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactionalism).)

By 1943 such was Peirce's reputation, in the US at least, that *Webster's Biographical Dictionary* said that Peirce was "now regarded as the most original thinker and greatest logician of his time".[^o95]

In 1949, while doing unrelated archival work, the historian of mathematics [Carolyn Eisele](wikipedia://en/43466157) (1902–2000) chanced on an autograph letter by Peirce. So began her forty years of research on Peirce, “the mathematician and scientist,” culminating in several works (Eisele 1976, 1979, 1985). In 1952, the [Scottish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland) philosopher [W. B. Gallie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._B._Gallie) had his book *Peirce and Pragmatism*[^o96] published, which introduced the work of Peirce to an international readership. [A.J. Ayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.J._Ayer), the English philosopher, provided the Editorial Foreword to Gallie's book. In it he credited Peirce's philosophy as being 'not only of great historical significance, as one of the original sources of American pragmatism, but also extremely important in itself.' Ayer concluded: 'it is clear from Professor Gallie's exposition of his doctrines that he is a philosopher from whom we still have much to learn.'[^o96]

Beginning around 1960, Max Fisch (1900-1995),[^o97] the philosopher and [historian of ideas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_ideas), emerged as an authority on Peirce (Fisch, 1986).[^o98] He included many of his relevant articles in a survey (Fisch 1986: 422–448) of the impact of Peirce's thought through 1983.

Peirce has gained an international following, marked by university research centers devoted to Peirce studies and [pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170) in Brazil (CeneP/CIEP and Centro de Estudos de Pragmatismo), Finland (HPRC and Commens), Germany (Wirth's group, Hoffman's and Otte's group, and Deuser's and Härle's group[^o99]), France (L'I.R.S.C.E.), Spain (GEP), and Italy (CSP). His writings have been translated into several languages, including German, French, Finnish, Spanish, and Swedish. Since 1950, there have been French, Italian, Spanish, British, and Brazilian Peirce scholars of note. For many years, the North American philosophy department most devoted to Peirce was the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto), thanks in part to the leadership of [Thomas Goudge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._A._Goudge) and David Savan. In recent years, U.S. Peirce scholars have clustered at [Indiana University – Purdue University Indianapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUPUI), home of the Peirce Edition Project (PEP) –, and [Pennsylvania State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_State_University).

Currently, considerable interest is being taken in Peirce's ideas by researchers wholly outside the arena of academic philosophy. The interest comes from industry, business, technology, intelligence organizations, and the military; and it has resulted in the existence of a substantial number of agencies, institutes, businesses, and laboratories in which ongoing research into and development of Peircean concepts are being vigorously undertaken.

In recent years, Peirce's trichotomy of signs is exploited by a growing number of practitioners for marketing and design tasks.

[John Deely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Deely) writes that Peirce was the last of the "moderns" and "first of the postmoderns". He lauds Peirce's doctrine of signs as a contribution to the dawn of the [Postmodern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postmodern) epoch. Deely additionally comments that "Peirce stands...in a position analogous to the position occupied by [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo) as last of the Western [Fathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_Fathers) and first of the medievals".[^o100]

## Works {#Works}

Peirce's reputation rests largely on academic papers published in American scientific and scholarly journals such as *Proceedings of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences)*, the *Journal of Speculative Philosophy*, *[The Monist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Monist)*, *[Popular Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_Science) Monthly*, the *[American Journal of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Journal_of_Mathematics)*, *Memoirs of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences)*, *[The Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nation_%28U.S._periodical%29)*, and others. See [Articles by Peirce, published in his lifetime](wikipedia://en/3967710#Articles_by_Peirce,_published_in_his_lifetime) for an extensive list with links to them online. The only full-length book (neither extract nor pamphlet) that Peirce authored and saw published in his lifetime[^o102] was *[Photometric Researches](wikipedia://en/3967710#PR)* (1878), a 181-page monograph on a photometric approach, measuring stellar brightness, to a more precise determination of the shape of the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way). While at Johns Hopkins, he edited *Studies in Logic* (1883), containing chapters by himself and his graduate students. Besides lectures during his years (1879–1884) as lecturer in Logic at Johns Hopkins, he gave at least nine series of lectures, many now published; see [Lectures by Peirce](wikipedia://en/3967710#Lectures_by_Peirce).

After Peirce's death, [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) obtained from Peirce's widow the papers found in his study, but did not microfilm them until 1964. Only after Richard Robin (1967)[^o103] catalogued this *[Nachlass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nachlass)* did it become clear that Peirce had left approximately 1,650 unpublished manuscripts, totaling over 100,000 pages,[^o104] mostly still unpublished except [on microfilm](wikipedia://en/3967710#mf). On the vicissitudes of Peirce's papers, see Houser (1989).[^o105] Reportedly the papers remain in unsatisfactory condition.[^o106]

The first published anthology of Peirce's articles was the one-volume *[Chance, Love and Logic: Philosophical Essays](wikipedia://en/3967710#CLL)*, edited by [Morris Raphael Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Raphael_Cohen), 1923, still in print. [Other one-volume anthologies](wikipedia://en/3967710#Other_collections) were published in 1940, 1957, 1958, 1972, 1994, and 2009, most still in print. ## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

Peirce's most important work in pure mathematics was in logical and foundational areas. He also worked on [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), various geometries, [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and [Listing numbers](wikipedia://en/28345268), [Bell numbers](wikipedia://en/201022), [graphs](wikipedia://en/12401), the [four-color problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-color_problem), and the nature of continuity.

He worked on applied mathematics in economics, engineering, and map projections, and was especially active in [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and statistics.[^o111]

**Discoveries**

↓ The [Peirce arrow](wikipedia://en/209154), symbol for "(neither) ... **nor** ...", also called the *Quine dagger* Peirce made a number of striking discoveries in formal logic and foundational mathematics, nearly all of which came to be appreciated only long after he died:

In 1860,[^o112] he suggested a cardinal arithmetic for infinite numbers, years before any work by [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216) (who completed [his dissertation in 1867](wikipedia://en/12216#Teacher_and_researcher)) and without access to [Bernard Bolzano](wikipedia://en/302185)'s 1851 (posthumous) *Paradoxien des Unendlichen*.

In 1880–1881,[^o113] he showed how [Boolean algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebra_%28logic%29) could be done via a [repeated sufficient single binary operation](wikipedia://en/5279259) ([logical NOR](wikipedia://en/209154)), anticipating [Henry M. Sheffer](wikipedia://en/2993325) by 33 years. (See also [De Morgan's Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Morgan's_Laws).)

In 1881,[^o114] he set out the [axiomatization of natural number arithmetic](wikipedia://en/25005), a few years before [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) and [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043). In the same paper Peirce gave, years before Dedekind, the first purely cardinal definition of a finite set in the sense now known as "[Dedekind-finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind-finite)", and implied by the same stroke an important formal definition of an [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679) (Dedekind-infinite), as a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) that can be put into a [one-to-one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_correspondence) with one of its proper [subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsets).

In 1885,[^o115] he distinguished between first-order and second-order quantification.[^o116] In the same paper he set out what can be read as the first (primitive) [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory), anticipating [Zermelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo) by about two decades (Brady 2000,[^o117] pp. 132–133).

*Image: PeirceAlphaGraphs.svg.* [Existential graphs](wikipedia://en/2235037): Alpha graphs

In 1886, he saw that Boolean calculations could be carried out via electrical switches,[^o12] anticipating [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693) by more than 50 years. By the later 1890s[^o118] he was devising [existential graphs](wikipedia://en/2235037), a diagrammatic notation for the [predicate calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_calculus). Based on them are [John F. Sowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Sowa)'s [conceptual graphs](wikipedia://en/346755) and Sun-Joo Shin's [diagrammatic reasoning](wikipedia://en/9939257).

**The New Elements of Mathematics**

Peirce wrote drafts for an introductory textbook, with the working title *The New Elements of Mathematics*, that presented mathematics from an original standpoint. Those drafts and many other of his previously unpublished mathematical manuscripts finally appeared[^o111] in *The New Elements of Mathematics by Charles S. Peirce* (1976), edited by mathematician [Carolyn Eisele](wikipedia://en/43466157).

**Nature of mathematics**

Peirce agreed with [Auguste Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte) in regarding mathematics as more basic than philosophy and the special sciences (of nature and mind). Peirce [classified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_the_sciences_%28Peirce%29) mathematics into three subareas: (1) mathematics of logic, (2) discrete series, and (3) pseudo-continua (as he called them, including the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers)) and continua. Influenced by his father [Benjamin](wikipedia://en/364270), Peirce argued that mathematics studies purely hypothetical objects and is not just the science of quantity but is more broadly the science which draws necessary conclusions; that mathematics aids logic, not vice versa; and that logic itself is part of philosophy and is the science *about* drawing conclusions necessary and otherwise.[^o119]

### Probability and statistics {#Probability_and_statistics}

Peirce held that science achieves statistical probabilities, not certainties, and that spontaneity ("absolute chance") is real (see [Tychism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tychism) on his view). Most of his statistical writings promote the [frequency interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_probability) of probability (objective ratios of cases), and many of his writings express skepticism about (and criticize the use of) [probability](wikipedia://en/27576) when such models are not based on objective [randomization](wikipedia://en/65462). Though Peirce was largely a frequentist, his [possible world semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_world_semantics) introduced the ["propensity" theory of probability](wikipedia://en/11351089) before [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper).[^o123][^o124] Peirce (sometimes with [Joseph Jastrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Jastrow)) investigated the [probability judgments](wikipedia://en/4890) of experimental subjects, "perhaps the very first" elicitation and estimation of [subjective probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjective_probability) in [experimental psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_psychology) and (what came to be called) [Bayesian statistics](wikipedia://en/404412).[^o3]

Peirce formulated modern statistics in "[Illustrations of the Logic of Science](wikipedia://en/3967710#illus)" (1877–1878) and "[A Theory of Probable Inference](wikipedia://en/3967710#SIL)" (1883). With a [repeated measures design](wikipedia://en/15943873), Charles Sanders Peirce and Joseph Jastrow introduced [blinded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blind_experiment), [controlled randomized experiments](wikipedia://en/163180) in 1884[^o125] (Hacking 1990:205)[^o2] (before [Ronald A. Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_A._Fisher)).[^o3] He invented [optimal design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_design) for experiments on gravity, in which he "[corrected the means](wikipedia://en/634)". He used [correlation](wikipedia://en/157057) and [smoothing](wikipedia://en/4155662). Peirce extended the work on [outliers](wikipedia://en/4015679) by [Benjamin Peirce](wikipedia://en/364270), his father.[^o3] He introduced the terms "[confidence](wikipedia://en/280911)" and "[likelihood](wikipedia://en/44968)" (before [Jerzy Neyman](wikipedia://en/2386106) and [Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_A._Fisher)). (See [Stephen Stigler](wikipedia://en/1276686)'s historical books and [Ian Hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Hacking) 1990.[^o2])

### Philosophy of mathematics {#Philosophy_of_mathematics}

Peirce saw mathematics as the science that analyses the [substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_theory) of fundamental hypotheses (which are defined by Peirce as propositions that are true of [potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentiality_and_actuality) objects) drawn from the [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind), drawing from criticisms of his father's earlier notion of the subject as "the science which draws necessary conclusions" via arguing that, since the "necessary" conclusions in this instance are unable to be drawn from unless we use perfect [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge), that this definition must be changed to that of *hypotheses*.[^o128] The [substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_theory) of mathematical hypotheses are never truly [experienced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience), but it is taught that any type of interaction with them must be strictly cognitive. To him, what makes mathematics special as a science compared to, say, [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) or [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) is that the two examine what can be *perceived* to an extent (even philosophy deals with universal truths/aspects of [perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception) or [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge), for example), but mathematical concepts/statements are completely [abstract](wikipedia://en/2972) and self-evident; as such, mathematicians are the most foreign amongst scientists regarding abstraction.[^o129] Expanding on his view that mathematics revolves around these hypotheses, he later takes that all branches of mathematics must take a single "general hypothesis" which is supposed to comprehensively describe all objects in that branch in a simple and abstract manner,[^o130] akin to our modern types of [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358). It is seen that a [mathematical abstraction](wikipedia://en/454446) is "a substance whose being consists in the truth of some proposition concerning a more primary substance".[^o131] Still, Peirce takes the view of abstractions being more of a mental state, with some abstractions (such as aggregates of objects; sets) being more "real" compared to others (such as algebraic manipulations).[^o131]

As most of his works related to the philosophy of mathematics were written during the [foundational crisis of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_of_mathematics), he often goes on digressions about the essence of mathematical reasoning and justification. For example, [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) was perceived by him to be "in-between" [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) and [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) regarding empirical basis; geometry is regarded as a branch of mathematics which is heavily intertwined with the physical world, whereas arithmetic is considered to be about [a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori) statements, and logic is (or was) a middle ground. Even the notion of [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285) as inherently [self-evident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-evident) (akin to [syllogisms](wikipedia://en/48046)) is cast into doubt by giving the example of a [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist) who mixes together several [compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compounds), creating a [reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction) which results in the formation of an entirely new compound. In this case, the parts of the reaction or new compounds formed by this mixing are not expected by the chemist, and are not in any way "contained" nor inherently predicted by the content of the initial compounds.[^o132] Because of this perceived inadequacy with reducing all mathematical reasoning to syllogisms, he pivots to seeing proof in terms of the observation and relational manipulation of [diagrams](wikipedia://en/2235037). These diagrams may vary by mathematical sub-field; in geometry, for example, these diagrams directly refer to visual manipulations of objects, whereas in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the relationships and rules are merely ascribed to different parts of the diagrams.

He defines continuity (in the [mathematical sense](wikipedia://en/169589) and in other ways) in terms of Aristotle's notion of a continuous function or segment, which is "something whose parts have a common limit".[^o133]

## As a philosopher {#As_a_philosopher}

Peirce was a working scientist for 30 years, and arguably was a professional philosopher only during the five years he lectured at Johns Hopkins. He learned philosophy mainly by reading, each day, a few pages of [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)'s *[Critique of Pure Reason](wikipedia://en/347543)*, in the original German, while a Harvard undergraduate. His writings bear on a wide array of disciplines, including mathematics, [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), philosophy, statistics, [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy),[^o38] [metrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrology),[^o4] [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608), [experimental psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_psychology),[^o5] economics,[^o6][^o135] [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics),[^o7] and the [history and philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_and_philosophy_of_science). This work has enjoyed renewed interest and approval, a revival inspired not only by his anticipations of recent scientific developments but also by his demonstration of how philosophy can be applied effectively to human problems.

Peirce's philosophy includes a pervasive three-category system: belief that truth is immutable and is both independent from actual opinion ([fallibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallibilism)) and discoverable (no radical skepticism), logic as formal semiotic on signs, on arguments, and on inquiry's ways—including philosophical [pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170) (which he founded), critical common-sensism, and [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method)—and, in metaphysics: [Scholastic realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotistic_realism), e.g. [John Duns Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Duns_Scotus), belief in God, freedom, and at least an attenuated immortality, [objective idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_idealism), and belief in the reality of continuity and of absolute chance, mechanical necessity, and creative love.[^o222] In his work, fallibilism and pragmatism may seem to work somewhat like [skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skepticism) and [positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positivism), respectively, in others' work. However, for Peirce, fallibilism is balanced by an [anti-skepticism](wikipedia://en/95170#antiskep) and is a basis for belief in the reality of absolute chance and of continuity,[^o136] and pragmatism commits one to anti-[nominalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominalist) belief in the reality of the general (CP 5.453–457).

For Peirce, First Philosophy, which he also called cenoscopy, is less basic than mathematics and more basic than the special sciences (of nature and mind). It studies positive phenomena in general, phenomena available to any person at any waking moment, and does not settle questions by resorting to special experiences.[^o137] He [divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_the_sciences_%28Peirce%29) such philosophy into (1) phenomenology (which he also called phaneroscopy or categorics), (2) normative sciences (esthetics, ethics, and logic), and (3) metaphysics; his views on them are discussed in order below.

Peirce did not write extensively in aesthetics and ethics,[^o138] but came by 1902 to hold that aesthetics, ethics, and logic, in that order, comprise the normative sciences.[^o139] He characterized aesthetics as the study of the good (grasped as the admirable), and thus of the ends governing all conduct and thought.[^o140]

Pragmatism (1907 MS in *The Essential Peirce*, 2) Peirce's recipe for pragmatic thinking, which he called *[pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170)* and, later, *[pragmaticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmaticism)*, is recapitulated in several versions of the so-called *[pragmatic maxim](wikipedia://en/14500570)*. Here is one of his more [emphatic reiterations](wikipedia://en/14500570#2) of it:

Consider what effects that might *conceivably* have practical bearings you *conceive* the objects of your *conception* to have. Then, your *conception* of those effects is the whole of your *conception* of the object.

As a movement, pragmatism began in the early 1870s in discussions among Peirce, [William James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James), and others in [the Metaphysical Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Metaphysical_Club). James among others regarded some articles by Peirce such as "The Fixation of Belief" (1877) and especially "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878) as foundational to [pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170).[^o142] Peirce (CP 5.11–12), like James (*Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking*, 1907), saw pragmatism as embodying familiar attitudes, in philosophy and elsewhere, elaborated into a new deliberate method for fruitful thinking about problems. Peirce differed from James and the early [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey), in some of their tangential enthusiasms, in being decidedly more rationalistic and realistic, in several senses of those terms, throughout the preponderance of his own philosophical moods.

In 1905 Peirce coined the new name [pragmaticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmaticism) "for the precise purpose of expressing the original definition", saying that "all went happily" with James's and [F.C.S. Schiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F.C.S._Schiller)'s variant uses of the old name "pragmatism" and that he coined the new name because of the old name's growing use in "literary journals, where it gets abused". Yet he cited as causes, in a 1906 manuscript, his differences with James and Schiller and, in a 1908 publication, his differences with James as well as literary author [Giovanni Papini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Papini)'s declaration of pragmatism's indefinability. Peirce in any case regarded his views that truth is immutable and infinity is real, as being opposed by the other pragmatists, but he remained allied with them on other issues.[^o143]

Pragmatism begins with the idea that belief is that on which one is prepared to act. Peirce's pragmatism is a method of clarification of conceptions of objects. It equates any conception of an object to a conception of that object's effects to a general extent of the effects' conceivable implications for informed practice. It is a method of sorting out conceptual confusions occasioned, for example, by distinctions that make (sometimes needed) formal yet not practical differences. He formulated both pragmatism and statistical principles as aspects of scientific logic, in his "Illustrations of the Logic of Science" series of articles. In the second one, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear", Peirce discussed three grades of clearness of conception:

1. Clearness of a conception familiar and readily used, even if unanalyzed and undeveloped.
1. Clearness of a conception in virtue of clearness of its parts, in virtue of which logicians called an idea "distinct", that is, clarified by analysis of just what makes it applicable. Elsewhere, echoing Kant, Peirce called a likewise distinct definition "nominal" (CP 5.553).
1. Clearness in virtue of clearness of conceivable practical implications of the object's conceived effects, such that fosters fruitful reasoning, especially on difficult problems. Here he introduced that which he later called the [pragmatic maxim](wikipedia://en/14500570).

By way of example of how to clarify conceptions, he addressed conceptions about truth and the real as questions of the presuppositions of reasoning in general. In clearness's second grade (the "nominal" grade), he defined truth as a sign's correspondence to its object, and the real as the object of such correspondence, such that truth and the real are independent of that which you or I or any actual, definite [community of inquirers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_of_inquiry) think. After that needful but confined step, next in clearness's third grade (the pragmatic, practice-oriented grade) he defined truth as that opinion which *would* be reached, sooner or later but still inevitably, by research taken far enough, such that the real does depend on that ideal final opinion—a dependence to which he appeals in theoretical arguments elsewhere, for instance for the long-run validity of the rule of induction.[^o144] Peirce argued that even to argue against the independence and discoverability of truth and the real is to presuppose that there is, about that very question under argument, a truth with just such independence and discoverability.

Peirce said that a conception's meaning consists in "[all general modes of rational conduct](wikipedia://en/14500570#2)" implied by "acceptance" of the conception—that is, if one were to accept, first of all, the conception as true, then what could one conceive to be consequent general modes of rational conduct by all who accept the conception as true?—the whole of such consequent general modes is the whole meaning. His pragmatism does not equate a conception's meaning, its intellectual purport, with the conceived benefit or cost of the conception itself, like a meme (or, say, propaganda), outside the perspective of its being true, nor, since a conception is general, is its meaning equated with any definite set of actual consequences or upshots corroborating or undermining the conception or its worth. His pragmatism also bears no resemblance to "vulgar" pragmatism, which misleadingly connotes a ruthless and [Machiavellian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machiavelli) search for mercenary or political advantage. Instead the pragmatic maxim is the heart of his pragmatism as a method of experimentational mental [reflection](wikipedia://en/14500570#6)[^o145] arriving at conceptions in terms of conceivable confirmatory and disconfirmatory circumstances—a method hospitable to the formation of explanatory hypotheses, and conducive to the use and improvement of verification.[^o146]

Peirce's pragmatism, as method and theory of definitions and conceptual clearness, is part of his theory of inquiry,[^o147] which he variously called speculative, general, formal or [universal rhetoric](wikipedia://en/34319899) or simply methodeutic.[^o148] He applied his pragmatism as a method throughout his work.

### Theory of inquiry {#Theory_of_inquiry}

In "The Fixation of Belief" (1877), Peirce gives his take on the psychological origin and aim of inquiry. On his view, individuals are motivated to inquiry by desire to escape the feelings of anxiety and unease which Peirce takes to be characteristic of the state of doubt. Doubt is described by Peirce as an "uneasy and dissatisfied state from which we struggle to free ourselves and pass into the state of belief." Peirce uses words like "irritation" to describe the experience of being in doubt and to explain why he thinks we find such experiences to be motivating. The irritating feeling of doubt is appeased, Peirce says, through our efforts to achieve a settled state of satisfaction with what we land on as our answer to the question which led to that doubt in the first place. This settled state, namely, belief, is described by Peirce as "a calm and satisfactory state which we do not wish to avoid." Our efforts to achieve the satisfaction of belief, by whichever methods we may pursue, are what Peirce calls "inquiry". Four methods which Peirce describes as having been actually pursued throughout the history of thought are summarized below in the section after next.

#### Critical common-sensism {#Critical_common-sensism}

Critical common-sensism,[^o151] treated by Peirce as a consequence of his pragmatism, is his combination of [Thomas Reid's common-sense philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_School_of_Common_Sense) with a [fallibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallibilism) that recognizes that propositions of our more or less vague common sense now indubitable may later come into question, for example because of transformations of our world through science. It includes efforts to raise genuine doubts in tests for a core group of common indubitables that change slowly, if at all.

#### Rival methods of inquiry {#Rival_methods_of_inquiry}

In "The Fixation of Belief" (1877), Peirce described inquiry in general not as the pursuit of truth *per se* but as the struggle to move from irritating, inhibitory doubt born of surprise, disagreement, and the like, and to reach a secure belief, belief being that on which one is prepared to act. That let Peirce frame scientific inquiry as part of a broader spectrum and as spurred, like inquiry generally, by actual doubt, not mere verbal, quarrelsome, or [hyperbolic doubt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_doubt), which he held to be fruitless. Peirce sketched four methods of settling opinion, ordered from least to most successful:

1. The method of tenacity (policy of sticking to initial belief) – which brings comforts and decisiveness but leads to trying to ignore contrary information and others' views as if truth were intrinsically private, not public. The method goes against the social impulse and easily falters since one may well notice when another's opinion seems as good as one's own initial opinion. Its successes can be brilliant but tend to be transitory.
1. The method of authority – which overcomes disagreements but sometimes brutally. Its successes can be majestic and long-lasting, but it cannot regulate people thoroughly enough to withstand doubts indefinitely, especially when people learn about other societies present and past.
1. The method of the a priori – which promotes conformity less brutally but fosters opinions as something like tastes, arising in conversation and comparisons of perspectives in terms of "what is agreeable to reason". Thereby it depends on fashion in [paradigms](wikipedia://en/175308) and goes in circles over time. It is more intellectual and respectable but, like the first two methods, sustains accidental and capricious beliefs, destining some minds to doubt it.
1. The method of science – wherein inquiry supposes that the real is discoverable but independent of particular opinion, such that, unlike in the other methods, inquiry can, by its own account, go wrong ([fallibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallibilism)), not only right, and thus purposely tests itself and criticizes, corrects, and improves itself.

Peirce held that, in practical affairs, slow and stumbling ratiocination is often dangerously inferior to instinct and traditional sentiment, and that the scientific method is best suited to theoretical research,[^o153] which in turn should not be trammeled by the other methods and practical ends; reason's "first rule"[^o175] is that, in order to learn, one must desire to learn and, as a corollary, must not block the way of inquiry. [Scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) excels over the others finally by being deliberately designed to arrive—eventually—at the most secure beliefs, upon which the most successful practices can be based. Starting from the idea that people seek not truth *per se* but instead to subdue irritating, inhibitory doubt, Peirce showed how, through the struggle, some can come to submit to truth for the sake of belief's integrity, seek as truth the guidance of potential conduct correctly to its given goal, and wed themselves to the scientific method.

#### Scientific method {#Scientific_method}

Insofar as clarification by pragmatic reflection suits explanatory hypotheses and fosters predictions and testing, pragmatism points beyond the usual duo of foundational alternatives: [deduction](wikipedia://en/61093) from self-evident truths, or *[rationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism)*; and [induction](wikipedia://en/393736) from experiential phenomena, or *[empiricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empiricism)*.

Based on his critique of three modes of argument and different from either [foundationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundationalism) or [coherentism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherentism), Peirce's approach seeks to justify claims by a three-phase dynamic of inquiry:

1. Active, [abductive](wikipedia://en/60459) genesis of theory, with no prior assurance of truth;
1. Deductive application of the contingent theory so as to clarify its practical implications;
1. Inductive testing and evaluation of the utility of the provisional theory in anticipation of future experience, in both senses: *[prediction](wikipedia://en/246066)* and *control*.

Thereby, Peirce devised an approach to inquiry far more solid than the flatter image of inductive generalization *simpliciter*, which is a mere re-labeling of phenomenological patterns. Peirce's pragmatism was the first time the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) was proposed as an [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) for philosophical questions.

A theory that succeeds better than its rivals in predicting and controlling our world is said to be nearer the truth. This is an operational notion of truth used by scientists.

Peirce extracted the pragmatic [model](wikipedia://en/994704) or [theory](wikipedia://en/30746) of inquiry from its raw materials in classical logic and refined it in parallel with the early development of symbolic logic to address problems about the nature of scientific reasoning.

Abduction, deduction, and induction make incomplete sense in isolation from one another but comprise a cycle understandable as a whole insofar as they collaborate toward the common end of inquiry. In the pragmatic way of thinking about conceivable practical implications, every thing has a purpose, and, as possible, its purpose should first be denoted. Abduction hypothesizes an explanation for deduction to clarify into implications to be tested so that induction can evaluate the hypothesis, in the struggle to move from troublesome uncertainty to more secure belief. No matter how traditional and needful it is to study the modes of inference in abstraction from one another, the integrity of inquiry strongly limits the effective [modularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modularity_%28programming%29) of its principal components.

Peirce's outline of the scientific method in §III–IV of "A Neglected Argument"[^o155] is summarized below (except as otherwise noted). There he also reviewed plausibility and inductive precision (issues of critique of arguments).

1. *Abductive* (or retroductive) phase. Guessing, inference to explanatory hypotheses for selection of those best worth trying. From abduction, Peirce distinguishes induction as inferring, on the basis of tests, the proportion of truth in the hypothesis. Every inquiry, whether into ideas, brute facts, or norms and laws, arises from surprising observations in one or more of those realms (and for example at any stage of an inquiry already underway). All explanatory content of theories comes from abduction, which guesses a new or outside idea so as to account in a simple, economical way for a surprising or complicated phenomenon. The modicum of success in our guesses far exceeds that of random luck, and seems born of attunement to nature by developed or inherent instincts, especially insofar as best guesses are optimally plausible and simple in the sense of the "facile and natural", as by [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo)'s natural light of reason and as distinct from "logical simplicity".[^o156] Abduction is the most fertile but least secure mode of inference. Its general rationale is inductive: it succeeds often enough and it has no substitute in expediting us toward new truths.[^o157] In 1903, Peirce called pragmatism "the logic of abduction".[^o158] Coordinative method leads from abducting a plausible hypothesis to judging it for its testability[^o159] and for how its trial would economize inquiry itself.[^o159] The hypothesis, being insecure, needs to have practical implications leading at least to mental tests and, in science, lending themselves to scientific tests. A simple but unlikely guess, if not costly to test for falsity, may belong first in line for testing. A guess is intrinsically worth testing if it has plausibility or reasoned objective probability, while [subjective likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjective_probability), though reasoned, can be misleadingly seductive. Guesses can be selected for trial strategically, for their caution (for which Peirce gave as example the game of [Twenty Questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty_Questions)), breadth, or incomplexity.[^o160] One can discover only that which would be revealed through their sufficient experience anyway, and so the point is to expedite it; economy of research demands the leap, so to speak, of abduction and governs its art.[^o159]
1. *Deductive* phase. Two stages:

i. Explication. Not clearly premised, but a deductive analysis of the hypothesis so as to render its parts as clear as possible.

ii. Demonstration: Deductive Argumentation, [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9331) in procedure. Explicit deduction of consequences of the hypothesis as predictions about evidence to be found. [Corollarial](wikipedia://en/168865) or, if needed, Theorematic.

1. *Inductive* phase. Evaluation of the hypothesis, inferring from observational or experimental tests of its deduced consequences. The long-run validity of the rule of induction is deducible from the principle (presuppositional to reasoning in general) that the real "is only the object of the final opinion to which sufficient investigation would lead";[^o144] in other words, anything excluding such a process would never be real. Induction involving the ongoing accumulation of evidence follows "a method which, sufficiently persisted in", will "diminish the error below any predesignate degree". Three stages:

i. Classification. Not clearly premised, but an inductive classing of objects of experience under general ideas.

ii. Probation: direct Inductive Argumentation. Crude or Gradual in procedure. Crude Induction, founded on experience in one mass (CP 2.759), presumes that future experience on a question will not differ utterly from all past experience (CP 2.756). Gradual Induction makes a new estimate of the proportion of truth in the hypothesis after each test, and is Qualitative or Quantitative. Qualitative Gradual Induction depends on estimating the relative evident weights of the various qualities of the subject class under investigation (CP 2.759; see also *Collected Papers of Charles Sanders Peirce*, 7.114–120). Quantitative Gradual Induction depends on how often, in a fair sample of instances of *S*, *S* is found actually accompanied by *P* that was predicted for *S* (CP 2.758). It depends on measurements, or statistics, or counting.

iii. Sentential Induction. "...which, by Inductive reasonings, appraises the different Probations singly, then their combinations, then makes self-appraisal of these very appraisals themselves, and passes final judgment on the whole result".

#### Against Cartesianism {#Against_Cartesianism}

Peirce drew on the methodological implications of the four incapacities—no genuine introspection, no intuition in the sense of non-inferential cognition, no thought but in signs, and no conception of the absolutely incognizable—to attack philosophical [Cartesianism](wikipedia://en/25525), of which he said that:[^o183]

1. "It teaches that philosophy must begin in universal doubt" – when, instead, we start with preconceptions, "prejudices \[...\] which it does not occur to us *can* be questioned", though we may find reason to question them later. "Let us not pretend to doubt in philosophy what we do not doubt in our hearts."
1. "It teaches that the ultimate test of certainty is...in the individual consciousness" – when, instead, in science a theory stays on probation till agreement is reached, then it has no actual doubters left. No lone individual can reasonably hope to fulfill philosophy's multi-generational dream. When "candid and disciplined minds" continue to disagree on a theoretical issue, even the theory's author should feel doubts about it.
1. It trusts to "a single thread of inference depending often upon inconspicuous premisses" – when, instead, philosophy should, "like the successful sciences", proceed only from tangible, scrutinizable premisses and trust not to any one argument but instead to "the multitude and variety of its arguments" as forming, not a chain at least as weak as its weakest link, but "a cable whose fibers", soever "slender, are sufficiently numerous and intimately connected".
1. It renders many facts "absolutely inexplicable, unless to say that 'God makes them so' is to be regarded as an explanation" – when, instead, philosophy should avoid being "unidealistic", misbelieving that something real can defy or evade all possible ideas, and supposing, inevitably, "some absolutely inexplicable, unanalyzable ultimate", which explanatory surmise explains nothing and so is inadmissible.

## Logic, or semiotic {#Logic,_or_semiotic}

In 1918, the logician [C. I. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Irving_Lewis) wrote, "The contributions of C.S. Peirce to symbolic logic are more numerous and varied than those of any other writer—at least in the nineteenth century."[^o166]

### Relational logic {#Relational_logic}

Beginning with his first paper on the ["Logic of Relatives" (1870)](wikipedia://en/3967710#LOR1870), Peirce extended the [theory of relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relations) pioneered by [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289). Beginning in 1940, [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) and his students rediscovered aspects of Peirce's larger vision of relational logic, developing the perspective of [relation algebra](wikipedia://en/4363670).

Relational logic gained applications. In mathematics, it influenced the abstract analysis of [E. H. Moore](wikipedia://en/1532860) and the [lattice theory](wikipedia://en/469304) of [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916). In computer science, the [relational model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_model) for [databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database) was developed with Peircean ideas in work of [Edgar F. Codd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_F._Codd), who was a doctoral student[^o168] of [Arthur W. Burks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_W._Burks), a Peirce scholar. In economics, relational logic was used by [Frank P. Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285), [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), and [Paul Samuelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Samuelson) to study preferences and utility and by [Kenneth J. Arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_J._Arrow) in *[Social Choice and Individual Values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Choice_and_Individual_Values)*, following Arrow's association with Tarski at [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York).

### Quantifiers {#Quantifiers}

On Peirce and his contemporaries [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015) and [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233) (1982)[^o116] documented that Frege's work on the logic of quantifiers had little influence on his contemporaries, although it was published four years before the work of Peirce and his student Oscar Howard Mitchell. Putnam found that mathematicians and logicians learned about the logic of quantifiers through the independent work of Peirce and Mitchell, particularly through Peirce's "On the Algebra of Logic: A Contribution to the Philosophy of Notation"[^o115] (1885), published in the premier American mathematical journal of the day, and cited by [Peano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano) and Schröder, among others, who ignored Frege. They also adopted and modified Peirce's notations, typographical variants of those now used. Peirce apparently was ignorant of Frege's work, despite their overlapping achievements in logic, [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_language), and the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358).

Peirce's work on formal logic had admirers besides [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015):

- Philosophical algebraist [William Kingdon Clifford](wikipedia://en/250820)[^o170] and logician [William Ernest Johnson](wikipedia://en/2635773), both British;
- The Polish school of logic and foundational mathematics, including [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920);
- [Arthur Prior](wikipedia://en/308764), who praised and studied Peirce's logical work in a 1964 paper[^o38] and in *Formal Logic* (saying on page 4 that Peirce "perhaps had a keener eye for essentials than any other logician before or since").

### Philosophy of logic {#Philosophy_of_logic}

A philosophy of logic, grounded in his categories and semiotic, can be extracted from Peirce's writings and, along with Peirce's logical work more generally, is exposited and defended in Hilary Putnam (1982);[^o116] the Introduction in Nathan Houser *et al.* (1997);[^o172] and [Randall Dipert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randall_Dipert)'s chapter in [Cheryl Misak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheryl_Misak) (2004).[^o173]

#### Logic as philosophical {#Logic_as_philosophical}

Peirce regarded logic *per se* as a division of philosophy, as a normative science based on esthetics and ethics, as more basic than metaphysics,[^o175] and as "the art of devising methods of research".[^o176] More generally, as inference, "logic is rooted in the social principle", since inference depends on a standpoint that, in a sense, is unlimited.[^o177] Peirce called (with no sense of deprecation) "mathematics of logic" much of the kind of thing which, in current research and applications, is called simply "logic". He was productive in both (philosophical) logic and logic's mathematics, which were connected deeply in his work and thought.

Peirce argued that logic is formal semiotic: the formal study of signs in the broadest sense, not only signs that are artificial, linguistic, or symbolic, but also signs that are semblances or are indexical such as reactions. Peirce held that "all this universe is perfused with signs, if it is not composed exclusively of signs",[^o178] along with their representational and inferential relations. He argued that, since all thought takes time, all thought is in signs[^o179] and sign processes ("semiosis") such as the inquiry process. He [divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_the_sciences_%28Peirce%29) logic into: (1) speculative grammar, or stechiology, on how signs can be meaningful and, in relation to that, what kinds of signs there are, how they combine, and how some embody or incorporate others; (2) logical critic, or logic proper, on the modes of inference; and (3) speculative or [universal rhetoric](wikipedia://en/34319899), or methodeutic,[^o148] the philosophical theory of inquiry, including pragmatism.

#### Presuppositions of logic {#Presuppositions_of_logic}

In his "F.R.L." \[First Rule of Logic\] (1899), Peirce states that the first, and "in one sense, the sole", rule of reason is that, *to learn, one needs to desire to learn* and desire it without resting satisfied with that which one is inclined to think.[^o175] So, the first rule is, *to wonder*. Peirce proceeds to a critical theme in research practices and the shaping of theories:

> ...there follows one [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) which itself deserves to be inscribed upon every wall of the city of philosophy:
>
> Do not block the way of inquiry.

Peirce adds, that method and economy are best in research but no outright sin inheres in trying any theory in the sense that the investigation via its trial adoption can proceed unimpeded and undiscouraged, and that "the one unpardonable offence" is a philosophical barricade against truth's advance, an offense to which "metaphysicians in all ages have shown themselves the most addicted". Peirce in many writings holds that [logic precedes metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_the_sciences_%28Peirce%29) (ontological, religious, and physical).

Peirce goes on to list four common barriers to inquiry: (1) Assertion of absolute certainty; (2) maintaining that something is absolutely unknowable; (3) maintaining that something is absolutely inexplicable because absolutely basic or ultimate; (4) holding that perfect exactitude is possible, especially such as to quite preclude unusual and anomalous phenomena. To refuse absolute theoretical certainty is the heart of *fallibilism*, which Peirce unfolds into refusals to set up any of the listed barriers. Peirce elsewhere argues (1897) that logic's presupposition of fallibilism leads at length to the view that chance and continuity are very real ([tychism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tychism) and [synechism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synechism)).[^o136]

The First Rule of Logic pertains to the mind's presuppositions in undertaking reason and logic; presuppositions, for instance, that truth and the real do not depend on yours or my opinion of them but do depend on representational relation and consist in the destined end in investigation taken far enough (see below). He describes such ideas as, collectively, hopes which, in particular cases, one is unable seriously to doubt.[^o181]

#### Four incapacities {#Four_incapacities}

The *Journal of Speculative Philosophy* series (1868–1869), including

Questions concerning certain Faculties claimed for Man (1868)

Some Consequences of Four Incapacities (1868)

Grounds of Validity of the Laws of Logic: Further Consequences of Four Incapacities (1869) In three articles in 1868–1869,[^o179][^o183][^o184] Peirce rejected mere verbal or [hyperbolic doubt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_doubt) and first or ultimate principles, and argued that we have (as he numbered them[^o183]):

1. No power of Introspection. All knowledge of the internal world comes by hypothetical reasoning from known external facts.
1. No power of Intuition (cognition without logical determination by previous cognitions). No cognitive stage is absolutely first in a process. All mental action has the form of inference.
1. No power of thinking without signs. A cognition must be interpreted in a subsequent cognition in order to be a cognition at all.
1. No conception of the absolutely incognizable.

(The above sense of the term "intuition" is almost Kant's, said Peirce. It differs from the current looser sense that encompasses instinctive or anyway half-conscious inference.)

Peirce argued that those incapacities imply the reality of the general and of the continuous, the validity of the modes of reasoning,[^o184] and the falsity of philosophical [Cartesianism](wikipedia://en/25525) (see below).

Peirce rejected the conception (usually ascribed to Kant) of the unknowable thing-in-itself[^o183] and later said that to "dismiss make-believes" is a prerequisite for pragmatism.[^o185]

#### Logic as formal semiotic {#Logic_as_formal_semiotic}

Peirce sought, through his wide-ranging studies through the decades, formal philosophical ways to articulate thought's processes, and also to explain the workings of science. These inextricably entangled questions of a dynamics of inquiry rooted in nature and nurture led him to develop his semiotic with very broadened conceptions of signs and inference, and, as its culmination, a theory of inquiry for the task of saying 'how science works' and devising research methods. This would be logic by the medieval definition taught for centuries: art of arts, science of sciences, having the way to the principles of all methods.[^o176] Influences radiate from points on parallel lines of inquiry in [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s work, in such *loci* as: the basic terminology of [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) in *[On the Soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Soul)*; the founding description of [sign relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_relation) in *[On Interpretation](wikipedia://en/910746)*; and the differentiation of [inference](wikipedia://en/317465) into three modes that are commonly translated into English as *[abduction](wikipedia://en/60459)*, *[deduction](wikipedia://en/61093)*, and *[induction](wikipedia://en/393736)*, in the *[Prior Analytics](wikipedia://en/491442)*, as well as inference by [analogy](wikipedia://en/103533) (called *paradeigma* by Aristotle), which Peirce regarded as involving the other three modes.

Peirce began writing on semiotic in the 1860s, around the time when he devised his system of three categories. He called it both *[semiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotic)* and *semeiotic*. Both are current in singular and plural. He based it on the conception of a triadic [sign relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_relation), and defined *[semiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiosis)* as "action, or influence, which is, or involves, a cooperation of *three* subjects, such as a sign, its object, and its interpretant, this tri-relative influence not being in any way resolvable into actions between pairs".[^o187] As to signs in thought, Peirce emphasized the reverse: "To say, therefore, that thought cannot happen in an instant, but requires a time, is but another way of saying that every thought must be interpreted in another, or that all thought is in signs."[^o179]

Peirce held that all thought is in signs, issuing in and from interpretation, where *sign* is the word for the broadest variety of conceivable semblances, diagrams, metaphors, symptoms, signals, designations, symbols, texts, even mental concepts and ideas, all as determinations of a mind or *quasi-mind*, that which at least functions like a mind, as in the work of crystals or bees[^o188]—the focus is on sign action in general rather than on psychology, linguistics, or social studies (fields which he also pursued).

Inquiry is a kind of inference process, a manner of thinking and semiosis. Global divisions of ways for phenomena to stand as signs, and the subsumption of inquiry and thinking within inference as a sign process, enable the study of inquiry on semiotics' three levels:

1. Conditions for meaningfulness. Study of significatory elements and combinations, their grammar.
1. Validity, conditions for true representation. Critique of arguments in their various separate modes.
1. Conditions for determining interpretations. Methodology of inquiry in its mutually interacting modes.

Peirce uses examples often from common experience, but defines and discusses such things as assertion and interpretation in terms of philosophical logic. In a formal vein, Peirce said:

*On the Definition of Logic*. Logic is *formal semiotic*. A sign is something, *A*, which brings something, *B*, its *interpretant* sign, determined or created by it, into the same sort of correspondence (or a lower implied sort) with something, *C*, its *object*, as that in which itself stands to *C*. This definition no more involves any reference to human thought than does the definition of a line as the place within which a particle lies during a lapse of time. It is from this definition that I deduce the principles of logic by mathematical reasoning, and by mathematical reasoning that, I aver, will support criticism of [Weierstrassian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass) severity, and that is perfectly evident. The word "formal" in the definition is also defined.[^o189]

## Signs {#Signs}

### Sign relation {#Sign_relation}

Peirce's theory of signs is known to be one of the most complex semiotic theories due to its generalistic claim. Anything is a sign—not absolutely as itself, but instead in some relation or other. The *[sign relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sign_relation)* is the key. It defines three roles encompassing (1) the sign, (2) the sign's subject matter, called its *object*, and (3) the sign's meaning or ramification as formed into a kind of effect called its *interpretant* (a further sign, for example a translation). It is an irreducible *[triadic relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triadic_relation)*, according to Peirce. The roles are distinct even when the things that fill those roles are not. The roles are but three; a sign of an object leads to one or more interpretants, and, as signs, they lead to further interpretants.

*Extension × intension = information.* Two traditional approaches to sign relation, necessary though insufficient, are the way of *[extension](wikipedia://en/39095)* (a sign's objects, also called breadth, denotation, or application) and the way of *[intension](wikipedia://en/14570)* (the objects' characteristics, qualities, attributes referenced by the sign, also called depth, [comprehension](wikipedia://en/3885297), significance, or connotation). Peirce adds a third, the way of *[information](wikipedia://en/4095359)*, including change of information, to integrate the other two approaches into a unified whole.[^o192] For example, because of the equation above, if a term's total amount of information stays the same, then the more that the term 'intends' or signifies about objects, the fewer are the objects to which the term 'extends' or applies.

*Determination.* A sign depends on its object in such a way as to represent its object—the object enables and, in a sense, determines the sign. A physically causal sense of this stands out when a sign consists in an indicative reaction. The interpretant depends likewise on both the sign and the object—an object determines a sign to determine an interpretant. But this determination is not a succession of dyadic events, like a row of toppling dominoes; sign determination is triadic. For example, an interpretant does not merely represent something which represented an object; instead an interpretant represents something *as* a sign representing the object. The object (be it a quality or fact or law or even fictional) determines the sign to an interpretant through one's collateral experience[^o193] with the object, in which the object is found or from which it is recalled, as when a sign consists in a chance semblance of an absent object. Peirce used the word "determine" not in a strictly deterministic sense, but in a sense of "specializes", *bestimmt*,[^o194] involving variable amount, like an influence.[^o195] Peirce came to define representation and interpretation in terms of (triadic) determination.[^o196] The object determines the sign to determine another sign—the interpretant—to be related to the object *as the sign is related to the object*, hence the interpretant, fulfilling its function as sign of the object, determines a further interpretant sign. The process is logically structured to perpetuate itself, and is definitive of sign, object, and interpretant in general.[^o195]

### Semiotic elements {#Semiotic_elements}

Peirce held there are exactly three basic elements in semiosis (sign action):

1. A *sign* (or *representamen*) represents, in the broadest possible sense of "represents". It is something interpretable as saying something about something. It is not necessarily symbolic, linguistic, or artificial—a cloud might be a sign of rain for instance, or ruins the sign of ancient civilization.[^o198] As Peirce sometimes put it (he defined *sign* at least 76 times[^o195]), the sign stands *for* the object *to* the interpretant. A sign represents its object in some respect, which respect is the sign's *ground*.[^n1]
1. An *object* (or *semiotic object*) is a subject matter of a sign and an interpretant. It can be anything thinkable, a quality, an occurrence, a rule, etc., even fictional, such as [Prince Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hamlet).[^o199] All of those are special or partial objects. The object most accurately is the [universe of discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe_of_discourse) to which the partial or special object belongs.[^o199] For instance, a perturbation of Pluto's orbit is a sign about Pluto but ultimately not only about Pluto. An object either (i) is *immediate* to a sign and is the object as represented in the sign or (ii) is a *dynamic* object, the object as it really is, on which the immediate object is founded "as on bedrock".[^o163]
1. An *[interpretant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretant)* (or *interpretant sign*) is a sign's meaning or ramification as formed into a kind of idea or effect, an interpretation, human or otherwise. An interpretant is a sign (a) of the object and (b) of the interpretant's "predecessor" (the interpreted sign) as a sign of the same object. An interpretant either (i) is *immediate* to a sign and is a kind of quality or possibility such as a word's usual meaning, or (ii) is a *dynamic* interpretant, such as a state of agitation, or (iii) is a *final* or *normal* interpretant, a sum of the lessons which a sufficiently considered sign *would* have as effects on practice, and with which an actual interpretant may at most coincide.

Some of the understanding needed by the mind depends on familiarity with the object. To know what a given sign denotes, the mind needs some experience of that sign's object, experience outside of, and collateral to, that sign or sign system. In that context Peirce speaks of collateral experience, collateral observation, collateral acquaintance, all in much the same terms.[^o193]

### Classes of signs {#Classes_of_signs}

Among Peirce's many sign typologies, three stand out, interlocked. The first typology depends on the sign itself, the second on how the sign stands for its denoted object, and the third on how the sign stands for its object to its interpretant. Also, each of the three typologies is a three-way division, a [trichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichotomy_%28philosophy%29), via Peirce's three phenomenological categories: (1) quality of feeling, (2) reaction, resistance, and (3) representation, mediation.[^n2]

I. *Qualisign, sinsign, legisign* (also called*tone, token, type,* and also called *potisign, actisign, famisign*):[^o163] This typology classifies every sign according to the sign's own phenomenological category—the qualisign is a quality, a possibility, a "First"; the sinsign is a reaction or resistance, a singular object, an actual event or fact, a "Second"; and the legisign is a habit, a rule, a representational relation, a "Third".

II. *Icon, index, symbol*: This typology, the best known one, classifies every sign according to the category of the sign's way of denoting its object—the icon (also called semblance or likeness) by a quality of its own, the index by factual connection to its object, and the symbol by a habit or rule for its interpretant.

III. *Rheme, dicisign, argument* (also called *sumisign, dicisign, suadisign,* also *seme, pheme, delome,*[^o163] and regarded as very broadened versions of the traditional *term, proposition, argument*): This typology classifies every sign according to the category which the interpretant attributes to the sign's way of denoting its object—the rheme, for example a term, is a sign interpreted to represent its object in respect of quality; the dicisign, for example a proposition, is a sign interpreted to represent its object in respect of fact; and the argument is a sign interpreted to represent its object in respect of habit or law. This is the culminating typology of the three, where the sign is understood as a structural element of inference.

Every sign belongs to one class or another within (I) *and* within (II) *and* within (III). Thus each of the three typologies is a three-valued parameter for every sign. The three parameters are not independent of each other; many co-classifications are absent, for reasons pertaining to the lack of either habit-taking or singular reaction in a quality, and the lack of habit-taking in a singular reaction. The result is not 27 but instead ten classes of signs fully specified at this level of analysis.

## Modes of inference {#Modes_of_inference}

Borrowing a brace of concepts from [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), Peirce examined three basic modes of [inference](wikipedia://en/317465)—*[abduction](wikipedia://en/60459)*, *[deduction](wikipedia://en/61093)*, and *[induction](wikipedia://en/393736)*—in his "critique of arguments" or "logic proper". Peirce also called abduction "retroduction", "presumption", and, earliest of all, "hypothesis". He characterized it as guessing and as inference to an explanatory hypothesis. He sometimes expounded the modes of inference by transformations of the categorical [syllogism Barbara (AAA)](wikipedia://en/48046#Barbara_%28AAA-1%29), for example in "Deduction, Induction, and Hypothesis" (1878).[^o202] He does this by rearranging the *rule* (Barbara's major premise), the *case* (Barbara's minor premise), and the *result* (Barbara's conclusion):

::: {#o203 .table cue=table}

| Deduction | Induction | Hypothesis (Abduction) |
|---|---|---|
| *Rule:* All the beans from this bag are white. *Case:* These beans are beans from this bag. $\therefore$ *Result:* These beans are white. | *Case:* These beans are \[randomly selected\] from this bag. *Result:* These beans are white. $\therefore$ *Rule:* All the beans from this bag are white. | *Rule:* All the beans from this bag are white. *Result:* These beans \[oddly\] are white. $\therefore$ *Case:* These beans are from this bag. |

:::

In 1883, in "A Theory of Probable Inference" (*Studies in Logic*), Peirce equated hypothetical inference with the induction of characters of objects (as he had done in effect before[^o183]). Eventually dissatisfied, by 1900 he distinguished them once and for all and also wrote that he now took the syllogistic forms and the doctrine of logical extension and comprehension as being less basic than he had thought. In 1903 he presented the following logical form for abductive inference:[^o204]

The surprising fact, C, is observed;

But if A were true, C would be a matter of course,

Hence, there is reason to suspect that A is true.

The logical form does not also cover induction, since induction neither depends on surprise nor proposes a new idea for its conclusion. Induction seeks facts to test a hypothesis; abduction seeks a hypothesis to account for facts. "Deduction proves that something *must* be; Induction shows that something *actually is* operative; Abduction merely suggests that something *may be*."[^o205] Peirce did not remain quite convinced that one logical form covers all abduction.[^o206] In his [methodeutic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodeutic) or theory of inquiry (see below), he portrayed abduction as an economic initiative to further inference and study, and portrayed all three modes as clarified by their coordination in essential roles in inquiry: hypothetical explanation, deductive prediction, inductive testing.

## Metaphysics {#Metaphysics}

Peirce [divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_the_sciences_%28Peirce%29) metaphysics into (1) ontology or general metaphysics, (2) psychical or religious metaphysics, and (3) physical metaphysics.

### Ontology {#Ontology}

On the issue of universals, Peirce was a [scholastic realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotistic_realism), declaring the reality of [generals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem_of_universals) as early as 1868.[^o209] According to Peirce, his category he called "thirdness", the more general facts about the world, are extra-mental realities. Regarding [modalities](wikipedia://en/333365) (possibility, necessity, etc.), he came in later years to regard himself as having wavered earlier as to just how positively real the modalities are. In his 1897 "The Logic of Relatives" he wrote:

I formerly defined the possible as that which in a given state of information (real or feigned) we do not know not to be true. But this definition today seems to me only a twisted phrase which, by means of two negatives, conceals an anacoluthon. We know in advance of experience that certain things are not true, because we see they are impossible.

Peirce retained, as useful for some purposes, the definitions in terms of information states, but insisted that the pragmaticist is committed to a strong [modal realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_realism) by conceiving of objects in terms of predictive general conditional propositions about how they *would* behave under certain circumstances.[^o210]

#### Continua {#Continua}

Continuity and [synechism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synechism) are central in Peirce's philosophy: "I did not at first suppose that it was, as I gradually came to find it, the master-Key of philosophy".[^o212]

From a mathematical point of view, he embraced [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990) and worked long on the mathematics of continua. He long held that the real numbers constitute a pseudo-continuum;[^o213] that a true continuum is the real subject matter of *analysis situs* ([topology](wikipedia://en/29954)); and that a true continuum of instants exceeds—and within any lapse of time has room for—any [Aleph number](wikipedia://en/293961) (any infinite *multitude* as he called it) of instants.[^o214]

In 1908 Peirce wrote that he found that a true continuum might have or lack such room. Jérôme Havenel (2008): "It is on 26 May 1908, that Peirce finally gave up his idea that in every continuum there is room for whatever collection of any multitude. From now on, there are different kinds of continua, which have different properties."[^o215]

### Psychical or religious metaphysics {#Psychical_or_religious_metaphysics}

Peirce believed in God, and characterized such belief as founded in an instinct explorable in musing over the worlds of ideas, brute facts, and evolving habits—and it is a belief in God not as an *actual* or *existent* being (in Peirce's sense of those words), but all the same as a *real* being.[^o217] In "A Neglected Argument for the Reality of God" (1908),[^o155] Peirce sketches, for God's reality, an argument to a hypothesis of God as the Necessary Being, a hypothesis which he describes in terms of how it would tend to develop and become compelling in musement and inquiry by a normal person who is led, by the hypothesis, to consider as being purposed the features of the worlds of ideas, brute facts, and evolving habits (for example scientific progress), such that the thought of such purposefulness will "stand or fall with the hypothesis"; meanwhile, according to Peirce, the hypothesis, in supposing an "infinitely incomprehensible" being, starts off at odds with its own nature as a purportively true conception, and so, no matter how much the hypothesis grows, it both (A) inevitably regards itself as partly true, partly vague, and as continuing to define itself without limit, and (B) inevitably has God appearing likewise vague but growing, though God as the Necessary Being is not vague or growing; but the hypothesis will hold it to be *more* false to say the opposite, that God is purposeless. Peirce also argued that the will is free[^o218] and (see [Synechism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synechism)) that there is at least an attenuated kind of immortality.

### Physical metaphysics {#Physical_metaphysics}

Peirce held the view, which he called [objective idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_idealism), that "matter is effete mind, inveterate habits becoming physical laws".[^o220] Peirce observed that "[Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berkeley)'s metaphysical theories have at first sight an air of paradox and levity very unbecoming to a bishop".[^o221]

Peirce asserted the reality of (1) "absolute chance" or randomness (his [tychist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tychism) view), (2) "mechanical necessity" or physical laws ([anancist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananke) view), and (3) what he called the "law of love" ([agapist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agapism) view), echoing his categories Firstness, Secondness, and Thirdness, respectively.[^o222] He held that fortuitous variation (which he also called "sporting"), mechanical necessity, and creative love are the three modes of evolution (modes called "tychasm", "anancasm", and "agapasm")[^o163] of the cosmos and its parts. He found his conception of agapasm embodied in [Lamarckian evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamarckism); the overall idea in any case is that of evolution tending toward an end or goal, and it could also be the evolution of a mind or a society; it is the kind of evolution which manifests workings of mind in some general sense. He said that overall he was a synechist, holding with reality of continuity,[^o222] especially of space, time, and law.[^o223]

## Philosophy of science {#Philosophy_of_science}

Peirce outlined two fields, "Cenoscopy" and "Science of Review", both of which he called philosophy. Both included philosophy about science. In 1903 he arranged them, from more to less theoretically basic, thus:[^o137]

1. Science of Discovery.
   1. Mathematics.
   1. Cenoscopy (philosophy as discussed earlier in this article – categorial, normative, metaphysical), as First Philosophy, concerns positive phenomena in general, does not rely on findings from special sciences, and includes the *general* study of inquiry and scientific method.
   1. Idioscopy, or the Special Sciences (of nature and mind).
1. Science of Review, as Ultimate Philosophy, arranges "... the results of discovery, beginning with digests, and going on to endeavor to form a philosophy of science". His examples included [Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt)'s *[Cosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Von_Humboldt#Cosmos)*, [Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte)'s *[Philosophie positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Course_of_Positive_Philosophy)*, and [Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Spencer)'s *Synthetic Philosophy*.
1. Practical Science, or the Arts.

Peirce placed, within Science of Review, the work and theory of [classifying the sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_the_sciences_%28Peirce%29) (including mathematics and philosophy). His classifications, on which he worked for many years, draw on argument and wide knowledge, and are of interest both as a map for navigating his philosophy and as an accomplished polymath's survey of research in his time.
