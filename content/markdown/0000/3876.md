# Binomial distribution

*Image: Pascal's triangle; binomial distribution.svg.* Binomial distribution for $p = 0.5$with $n$ and $k$ as in [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497)The probability that a ball in a [Galton box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean_machine) with 8 layers ($n = 8$) ends up in the central bin ($k = 4$) is $70/256$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binomial distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **binomial distribution** with parameters $n$ and $p$ is the [discrete probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_probability_distribution) of the number of successes in a sequence of $n$ [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) [experiments](wikipedia://en/19621951), each asking a [yes–no question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yes–no_question), and each with its own [Boolean](wikipedia://en/2757237)-valued [outcome](wikipedia://en/39778851): *success* (with probability $p$) or *failure* (with probability $q = 1 - p$). A single success/failure experiment is also called a [Bernoulli trial](wikipedia://en/102653) or Bernoulli experiment, and a sequence of outcomes is called a [Bernoulli process](wikipedia://en/102651). For a single trial, that is, when $n = 1$, the binomial distribution is a [Bernoulli distribution](wikipedia://en/199189). The binomial distribution is the basis for the [binomial test](wikipedia://en/935655) of [statistical significance](wikipedia://en/160995).[^o2]

:::

The binomial distribution is frequently used to model the number of successes in a sample of size $n$ drawn [with replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/With_replacement) from a [population](wikipedia://en/27585) of size $N$. If the sampling is carried out without replacement, the draws are not independent and so the resulting distribution is a [hypergeometric distribution](wikipedia://en/180841), not a binomial one. However, for $N$ much larger than $n$, the binomial distribution remains a good approximation, and is widely used.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

### Probability mass function {#Probability_mass_function}

If the [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ follows the binomial distribution with parameters $n \in \mathbb{N}$ (a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474)) and $p \in [0, 1]$, we write $X \sim B(n, p)$. The probability of getting exactly $k$ successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials (with the same rate $p$) is given by the [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725):

$$f(k,n,p) = \Pr(X = k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$$

for $k = 0, 1, 2, ..., n$, where

$$\binom{n}{k} =\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$$

is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668). The formula can be understood as follows: $p^{k} q^{n-k}$ is the probability of obtaining the sequence of $n$ independent Bernoulli trials in which $k$ trials are "successes" and the remaining $n - k$ trials are "failures". Since the trials are independent with probabilities remaining constant between them, any sequence of $n$ trials with $k$ successes (and $n - k$ failures) has the same probability of being achieved (regardless of positions of successes within the sequence). There are $\binom{n}{k}$ such sequences, since the binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{k}$ counts the number of ways to choose the positions of the $k$ successes among the $n$ trials. The binomial distribution is concerned with the probability of obtaining any of these sequences, meaning the probability of obtaining one of them ($p^{k} q^{n-k}$) must be added $\binom{n}{k}$ times, hence $\textstyle \Pr(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$.

In creating reference tables for binomial distribution probability, usually, the table is filled in up to $\textstyle \tfrac{n}{2}$ values. This is because for $\textstyle k > \tfrac{n}{2}$, the probability can be calculated by its complement as

$$f(k,n,p)=f(n-k,n,1-p).$$

Looking at the expression $f(k, n, p)$ as a function of $k$, there is a $k$ value that maximizes it. This $k$ value can be found by calculating

$$\frac{f(k+1,n,p)}{f(k,n,p)}=\frac{(n-k)p}{(k+1)(1-p)}$$

and comparing it to 1. There is always an integer $M$ that satisfies[^o6]

$$(n+1)p-1 \leq M < (n+1)p.$$

$f(k, n, p)$ is monotone increasing for $k < M$ and monotone decreasing for $k > M$, with the exception of the case where $(n + 1)p$ is an integer. In this case, there are two values for which $f$ is maximal: $(n + 1)p$ and $(n + 1)p - 1$. $M$ is the most probable outcome (that is, the most likely, although this can still be unlikely overall) of the Bernoulli trials and is called the [mode](wikipedia://en/1432127).

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Suppose a [biased coin](wikipedia://en/9597100) comes up heads with probability 0.3 when tossed. The probability of seeing exactly 4 heads in 6 tosses is

$$f(4,6,0.3) = \binom{6}{4} 0.3^4 (1-0.3)^{6-4}= 0.059535.$$

:::

### Cumulative distribution function {#Cumulative_distribution_function}

The [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) can be expressed as:

$$F(k;n,p) = \Pr(X \le k) = \sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k \rfloor} {n\choose i}p^i(1-p)^{n-i},$$

where $\lfloor k\rfloor$ is the "floor" under $k$; that is, the [greatest integer](wikipedia://en/54267) less than or equal to $k$.

It can also be represented in terms of the [regularized incomplete beta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularized_incomplete_beta_function), as follows:[^o10]

$$\begin{aligned} F(k;n,p) & = \Pr(X \le k) \\ &= I_{1-p}(n-k, k+1) \\ & = (n-k) {n \choose k} \int_0^{1-p} t^{n-k-1} (1-t)^k \, dt , \end{aligned}$$

which is equivalent to the [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) of the [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) and of the [$F$-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116):[^o11]

$$F(k;n,p) = F_{\text{beta-distribution}}\left(x=1-p;\alpha=n-k,\beta=k+1\right)$$

$$F(k;n,p) = F_{F\text{-distribution}}\left(x=\frac{1-p}{p}\frac{k+1}{n-k};d_1=2(n-k),d_2=2(k+1)\right).$$

Some closed-form bounds for the cumulative distribution function are given below.

## Properties {#Properties}

### Expected value and variance {#Expected_value_and_variance}

If $X \sim B(n, p)$, that is, $X$ is a binomially distributed random variable, $n$ being the total number of experiments and $p$ the probability of each experiment yielding a successful result, then the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of $X$ is:[^o15]

$$\operatorname{E}[X] = np.$$

This follows from the linearity of the expected value along with the fact that $X$ is the sum of $n$ identical Bernoulli random variables, each with expected value $p$. In other words, if $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are identical (and independent) Bernoulli random variables with parameter $p$, then $X = X_{1} + ... + X_{n}$ and

$$\operatorname{E}[X] = \operatorname{E}[X_1 + \cdots + X_n] = \operatorname{E}[X_1] + \cdots + \operatorname{E}[X_n] = p + \cdots + p = np.$$

The [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) is:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = npq = np(1 - p).$$

This similarly follows from the fact that the variance of a sum of independent random variables is the sum of the variances.

### Higher moments {#Higher_moments}

The first 6 [central moments](wikipedia://en/19983), defined as $\mu _{c}=\operatorname {E} \left[(X-\operatorname {E} [X])^{c}\right]$, are given by

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_1 &= 0, \\ \mu_2 &= np \left(1-p\right), \\ \mu_3 &= np \left(1-p\right) \left(1-2p\right), \\ \mu_4 &= np \left(1-p\right) \left[1 + \left(3n-6\right) p \left(1-p\right)\right],\\ \mu_5 &= np \left(1-p\right) \left(1-2p\right) \left[1 + \left(10n-12\right) p \left(1-p\right)\right],\\ \mu_6 &= np \left(1-p\right) \left[1 - 30p\left(1-p\right)\left[1-4p(1-p)\right] + 5np \left(1-p\right)\left[5 - 26p\left(1-p\right)\right] + 15n^2 p^2 \left(1-p\right)^2\right]. \end{aligned}$$

The non-central moments satisfy

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname {E}[X] &= np, \\ \operatorname {E}[X^2] &= np(1-p)+n^2p^2, \end{aligned}$$

and in general[^o17][^o18]

$$\operatorname {E}[X^c] = \sum_{k=0}^c \left\{ {c \atop k} \right\} n^{\underline{k}} p^k,$$

where $\left\{{c\atop k}\right\}$ are the [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292), and $n^{\underline{k}} = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$ is the $k$-th [falling power](wikipedia://en/230193) of $n$. A simple bound [^o19] follows by bounding the Binomial moments via the [higher Poisson moments](wikipedia://en/23009144#Higher_moments):

$$\operatorname {E}[X^c] \le \left[\frac{c}{\ln\left(1+\frac{c}{np}\right)}\right]^c \le (np)^c \exp\left(\frac{c^2}{2np}\right).$$

This shows that if $c=O(\sqrt{np})$, then $\operatorname {E}[X^c]$ is at most a constant factor away from $\operatorname {E}[X]^c$.

The [moment-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment-generating_function) is $M_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}] = (1-p+p e^t)^n$.

### Mode {#Mode}

Usually the [mode](wikipedia://en/1432127) of a binomial $B(n, p)$ distribution is equal to $\lfloor (n+1)p\rfloor$, where $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ is the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function). However, when $(n + 1)p$ is an integer and $p$ is neither 0 nor 1, then the distribution has two modes: $(n + 1)p$ and $(n + 1)p - 1$. When $p$ is equal to 0 or 1, the mode will be 0 and $n$ correspondingly. These cases can be summarized as follows:

$$\text{mode} = \begin{cases} \lfloor (n+1)\,p\rfloor & \text{if }(n+1)p\text{ is 0 or a noninteger}, \\ (n+1)\,p\ \text{ and }\ (n+1)\,p - 1 &\text{if }(n+1)p\in\{1,\dots,n\}, \\ n & \text{if }(n+1)p = n + 1. \end{cases}$$

::: {#o21 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** Let

$$f(k)=\binom nk p^k q^{n-k}.$$

For $p=0$ only $f(0)$ has a nonzero value with $f(0)=1$. For $p=1$ we find $f(n)=1$ and $f(k)=0$ for $k\neq n$. This proves that the mode is 0 for $p=0$ and $n$ for $p=1$.

Let $0 < p < 1$. We find

$$\frac{f(k+1)}{f(k)} = \frac{(n-k)p}{(k+1)(1-p)}.$$

From this follows

$$\begin{aligned} k > (n+1)p-1 \Rightarrow f(k+1) < f(k) \\ k = (n+1)p-1 \Rightarrow f(k+1) = f(k) \\ k < (n+1)p-1 \Rightarrow f(k+1) > f(k) \end{aligned}$$

So when $(n+1)p-1$ is an integer, then $(n+1)p-1$ and $(n+1)p$ is a mode. In the case that $(n+1)p-1\notin \mathbb{Z}$, then only $\lfloor (n+1)p-1\rfloor+1=\lfloor (n+1)p\rfloor$ is a mode.[^o22]

:::

### Median {#Median}

In general, there is no single formula to find the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) for a binomial distribution, and it may even be non-unique. However, several special results have been established:

- If $np$ is an integer, then the mean, median, and mode coincide and equal $np$.[^o24][^o25]
- Any median $m$ must lie within the interval $\lfloor np \rfloor\leq m \leq \lceil np \rceil$.[^o26]
- A median $m$ cannot lie too far away from the mean:$|m-np|\leq \min\{{\ln2}, \max\{p,1-p\}\}$.[^o27]
- The median is unique and equal to $m = \operatorname{round}(np)$ when $\lvert m - np\rvert \le \min\{p, 1 - p\}$ (except for the case when $p = 1/2$ and $n$ is odd).[^o26]
- When $p$ is a rational number (with the exception of $p = 1/2$ and $n$ odd), the median is unique.[^o28]
- When $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $n$ is odd, any number $m$ in the interval $\frac{1}{2} \left(n-1\right) \leq m \leq \frac{1}{2} \left(n+1\right)$ is a median of the binomial distribution. If $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $n$ is even, then $m = \tfrac{n}{2}$ is the unique median.

### Tail bounds {#Tail_bounds}

For $k \le np$, upper bounds can be derived for the lower tail of the cumulative distribution function $F(k;n,p) = \Pr(X \le k)$, the probability that there are at most $k$ successes. Since $\Pr(X \ge k) = F(n-k;n,1-p)$, these bounds can also be seen as bounds for the upper tail of the cumulative distribution function for $k \ge np$.

[Hoeffding's inequality](wikipedia://en/1136348) yields the simple bound

$$F(k;n,p) \leq \exp\left(-2 n\left(p-\frac{k}{n}\right)^2\right), \!$$

which is however not very tight. In particular, for $p = 1$, we have that $F(k; n, p) = 0$ (for fixed $k$, $n$ with $k < n$), but Hoeffding's bound evaluates to a positive constant.

A sharper bound can be obtained from the [Chernoff bound](wikipedia://en/741759):[^o30]

$$F(k;n,p) \leq \exp\left(-n D{\left(\frac{k}{n}\parallel p\right)}\right)$$

where $D(a \parallel p)$ is the [relative entropy (or Kullback-Leibler divergence)](wikipedia://en/1115052) between an $a$-coin and a $p$-coin (that is, between the $\operatorname{Bernoulli}(a)$ and $\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)$ distribution):

$$D(a\parallel p)=(a)\ln\frac{a}{p}+(1-a)\ln\frac{1-a}{1-p}. \!$$

Asymptotically, this bound is reasonably tight; see [^o30] for details.

One can also obtain *lower* bounds on the tail $F(k; n, p)$, known as anti-concentration bounds. By approximating the binomial coefficient with [Stirling's formula](wikipedia://en/151783) it can be shown that[^o31]

$$F(k;n,p) \geq \frac{1}{\sqrt{8n\tfrac{k}{n}(1-\tfrac{k}{n})}} \exp\left(-n D{\left(\frac{k}{n}\parallel p\right)}\right),$$

which implies the simpler but looser bound

$$F(k;n,p) \geq \frac1{\sqrt{2n}} \exp\left(-nD\left(\frac{k}{n}\parallel p\right)\right).$$

For $p = 1/2$ and $k \ge 3n/8$ for even $n$, it is possible to make the denominator constant:[^o32]

$$F(k;n,\tfrac{1}{2}) \geq \frac{1}{15} \exp\left(- 16n \left(\frac{1}{2} -\frac{k}{n}\right)^2\right). \!$$

:::

## Statistical inference {#Statistical_inference}

### Estimation of parameters {#Estimation_of_parameters}

When $n$ is known, the parameter $p$ can be estimated using the proportion of successes:

$$\widehat{p} = \frac{x}{n}.$$

This estimator is found using [maximum likelihood estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood_estimator) and also the [method of moments](wikipedia://en/2175504). This estimator is [unbiased](wikipedia://en/8450479) and uniformly with [minimum variance](wikipedia://en/1583470), proven using [Lehmann–Scheffé theorem](wikipedia://en/342602), since it is based on a [minimal sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_sufficient) and [complete](wikipedia://en/194618) statistic (that is, $x$). It is also [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569) both in probability and in [MSE](wikipedia://en/201816). This statistic is [asymptotically](wikipedia://en/3001287) [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) thanks to the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406), because it is the same as taking the [mean](wikipedia://en/612) over Bernoulli samples. It has a variance of $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}$, a property which is used in various ways, such as in [Wald's confidence intervals](wikipedia://en/3698838#Wald_interval).

A closed form [Bayes estimator](wikipedia://en/6978815) for $p$ also exists when using the [Beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) as a [conjugate](wikipedia://en/846412) [prior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_distribution). When using a general $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ as a prior, the [posterior mean](wikipedia://en/6978815#Posterior_mean) estimator is:

$$\widehat{p}_b = \frac{x+\alpha}{n+\alpha+\beta}.$$

The Bayes estimator is [asymptotically efficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_efficiency_%28Bayes%29) and as the sample size approaches infinity ($n \to \infty$), it approaches the [MLE](wikipedia://en/140806) solution.[^o35] The Bayes estimator is [biased](wikipedia://en/8450479) (how much depends on the priors), [admissible](wikipedia://en/6978815#Admissibility) and [consistent](wikipedia://en/1497569) in probability. Using the Bayesian estimator with the Beta distribution can be used with [Thompson sampling](wikipedia://en/34130293).

For the special case of using the [standard uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_uniform_distribution) as a [non-informative prior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-informative_prior), $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha{=}1,\, \beta{=}1) = U(0,1)$, the posterior mean estimator becomes:

$$\widehat{p}_b = \frac{x+1}{n+2}.$$

(A [posterior mode](wikipedia://en/6978815#Posterior_mode) should just lead to the standard estimator.) This method is called the [rule of succession](wikipedia://en/798571), which was introduced in the 18th century by [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783).

When relying on [Jeffreys prior](wikipedia://en/1901158), the prior is $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha{=}\tfrac{1}{2}, \, \beta{=}\tfrac{1}{2})$,[^o36] which leads to the estimator:

$$\widehat{p}_{\mathrm{Jeffreys}} = \frac{x+\frac{1}{2}}{n+1}.$$

When estimating $p$ with very rare events and a small $n$ (for example, if $x = 0$), then using the standard estimator leads to $\widehat{p} = 0,$ which sometimes is unrealistic and undesirable. In such cases there are various alternative estimators.[^o37] One way is to use the Bayes estimator $\widehat{p}_b$, leading to:

$$\widehat{p}_b = \frac{1}{n+2}.$$

Another method is to use the upper bound of the [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911) obtained using the [rule of three](wikipedia://en/16941667):

$$\widehat{p}_{\text{rule of 3}} = \frac{3}{n}.$$

### Confidence intervals for the parameter p {#Confidence_intervals_for_the_parameter_p}

Even for quite large values of $n$, the actual distribution of the mean is significantly nonnormal.[^o39] Because of this problem several methods to estimate confidence intervals have been proposed.

In the equations for confidence intervals below, the variables have the following meaning:

- $n_{1}$ is the number of successes out of $n$, the total number of trials
- $\widehat{p\,} = \frac{n_1}{n}$ is the proportion of successes
- $z$ is the $1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha$ [quantile](wikipedia://en/25184) of a [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution) (that is, [probit](wikipedia://en/1689101)) corresponding to the target error rate $\alpha$. For example, for a 95% [confidence level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_level) the error $\alpha=0.05$, so $1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha=0.975$ and $z=1.96$.

#### Wald method {#Wald_method}

$$\widehat{p\,} \pm z \sqrt{ \frac{ \widehat{p\,} ( 1 -\widehat{p\,} )}{ n } } .$$

A [continuity correction](wikipedia://en/572382) of $0.5 / n$ may be added.

#### Agresti–Coull method {#Agresti–Coull_method}

[^o42]

$$\tilde{p} \pm z \sqrt{ \frac{ \tilde{p} ( 1 - \tilde{p} )}{ n + z^2 } }$$

Here the estimate of $p$ is modified to

$$\tilde{p}= \frac{ n_1 + \frac{1}{2} z^2}{ n + z^2 }$$

This method works well for $n > 10$ and $n_{1} \ne 0, n$.[^o43] See here for $n\leq 10$.[^o44] For $n_{1} = 0, n$ use the Wilson (score) method below.

#### Arcsine method {#Arcsine_method}

[^o46]

$$\sin^2 \left(\arcsin \left(\sqrt{\hat{p}}\right) \pm \frac{z}{2\sqrt{n}} \right).$$

#### Wilson (score) method {#Wilson_(score)_method}

The notation in the formula below differs from the previous formulas in two respects:[^o48]

- Firstly, $z_{x}$ has a slightly different interpretation in the formula below: it has its ordinary meaning of 'the $x$th quantile of the standard normal distribution', rather than being a shorthand for 'the $(1 - x)$th quantile'.
- Secondly, this formula does not use a plus-minus to define the two bounds. Instead, one may use $z = z_{\alpha / 2}$ to get the lower bound, or use $z = z_{1 - \alpha/2}$ to get the upper bound. For example: for a 95% confidence level the error $\alpha=0.05$, so one gets the lower bound by using $z = z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.025} = - 1.96$, and one gets the upper bound by using $z = z_{1 - \alpha/2} = z_{0.975} = 1.96$.

$$\frac{ \hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{2n} + z \sqrt{ \frac{\hat{p} \left(1 - \hat{p}\right)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4 n^2} } }{ 1 + \frac{z^2}{n} }$$

[^o49]

#### Comparison {#Comparison}

The so-called "exact" ([Clopper–Pearson](wikipedia://en/3698838#Clopper–Pearson_interval)) method is the most conservative.[^o39] (*Exact* does not mean perfectly accurate; rather, it indicates that the estimates will not be less conservative than the true value.)

The Wald method, although commonly recommended in textbooks, is the most biased.

## Related distributions {#Related_distributions}

### Sums of binomials {#Sums_of_binomials}

If $X \sim B(n, p)$ and $Y \sim B(m, p)$ are independent binomial variables with the same probability $p$, then $X + Y$ is again a binomial variable; its distribution is $Z = X + Y \sim B(n + m, p)$:[^o53]

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname P(Z=k) &= \sum_{i=0}^k\left[\binom{n}i p^i (1-p)^{n-i}\right]\left[\binom{m}{k-i} p^{k-i} (1-p)^{m-k+i}\right]\\ &= \binom{n+m}k p^k (1-p)^{n+m-k} \end{aligned}$$

A Binomial distributed random variable $X \sim B(n, p)$ can be considered as the sum of $n$ Bernoulli distributed random variables. So the sum of two Binomial distributed random variables $X \sim B(n, p)$ and $Y \sim B(m, p)$ is equivalent to the sum of $n + m$ Bernoulli distributed random variables, which means $Z = X + Y \sim B(n + m, p)$. This can also be proven directly using the addition rule.

However, if $X$ and $Y$ do not have the same probability $p$, then the variance of the sum will be [smaller than the variance of a binomial variable](wikipedia://en/44198675) distributed as $B(n + m, \overline{p})$.

### Poisson binomial distribution {#Poisson_binomial_distribution}

The binomial distribution is a special case of the [Poisson binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/28647736), which is the distribution of a sum of $n$ independent non-identical [Bernoulli trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_trials) $B(p_{i})$.[^o55]

### Ratio of two binomial distributions {#Ratio_of_two_binomial_distributions}

This result was first derived by Katz and coauthors in 1978.[^o57]

Let $X \sim B(n, p_{1})$ and $Y \sim B(m, p_{2})$ be independent. Let $T = (X/n) / (Y/m)$.

Then $\log(T)$ is approximately normally distributed with mean $\log(p_{1}/p_{2})$ and variance $((1/p_{1}) - 1)/n + ((1/p_{2}) - 1)/m$.

### Conditional binomials {#Conditional_binomials}

If $X \sim B(n, p)$ and $Y | X \sim B(X, q)$ (the conditional distribution of $Y$, given $X$), then $Y$ is a simple binomial random variable with distribution $Y \sim B(n, pq)$.

For example, imagine throwing $n$ balls to a basket $U_{X}$ and taking the balls that hit and throwing them to another basket $U_{Y}$. If $p$ is the probability to hit $U_{X}$ then $X \sim B(n, p)$ is the number of balls that hit $U_{X}$. If $q$ is the probability to hit $U_{Y}$ then the number of balls that hit $U_{Y}$ is $Y \sim B(X, q)$ and therefore $Y \sim B(n, pq)$.

::: {#o59 .proof cue=collapse name="[Proof]"}

Since $X \sim \mathrm{B}(n, p)$ and $Y \sim \mathrm{B}(X, q)$, by the [law of total probability](wikipedia://en/312383),

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[Y = m] &= \sum_{k = m}^{n} \Pr[Y = m \mid X = k] \Pr[X = k] \\[2pt] &= \sum_{k=m}^n \binom{n}{k} \binom{k}{m} p^k q^m (1-p)^{n-k} (1-q)^{k-m} \end{aligned}$$

Since $\tbinom{n}{k} \tbinom{k}{m} = \tbinom{n}{m} \tbinom{n-m}{k-m},$ the equation above can be expressed as

$$\Pr[Y = m] = \sum_{k=m}^{n} \binom{n}{m} \binom{n-m}{k-m} p^k q^m (1-p)^{n-k} (1-q)^{k-m}$$

Factoring $p^k = p^m p^{k-m}$ and pulling all the terms that don't depend on $k$ out of the sum now yields

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[Y = m] &= \binom{n}{m} p^m q^m \left( \sum_{k=m}^n \binom{n-m}{k-m} p^{k-m} (1-p)^{n-k} (1-q)^{k-m} \right) \\[2pt] &= \binom{n}{m} (pq)^m \left( \sum_{k=m}^n \binom{n-m}{k-m} \left(p(1-q)\right)^{k-m} (1-p)^{n-k} \right) \end{aligned}$$

After substituting $i = k - m$ in the expression above, we get

$$\Pr[Y = m] = \binom{n}{m} (pq)^m \left( \sum_{i=0}^{n-m} \binom{n-m}{i} (p - pq)^i (1-p)^{n-m - i} \right)$$

Notice that the sum (in the parentheses) above equals $(p - pq + 1 - p)^{n-m}$ by the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677). Substituting this in finally yields

$$\begin{aligned} \Pr[Y=m] &= \binom{n}{m} (pq)^m (p - pq + 1 - p)^{n-m}\\[4pt] &= \binom{n}{m} (pq)^m (1-pq)^{n-m} \end{aligned}$$

and thus $Y \sim \mathrm{B}(n, pq)$ as desired.

:::

### Bernoulli distribution {#Bernoulli_distribution}

The [Bernoulli distribution](wikipedia://en/199189) is a special case of the binomial distribution, where $n = 1$. Symbolically, $X \sim B(1, p)$ has the same meaning as $X \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)$. Conversely, any binomial distribution, $B(n, p)$, is the distribution of the sum of $n$ independent [Bernoulli trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_trials), $\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)$, each with the same probability $p$.[^o61]

### Normal approximation {#Normal_approximation}

*Image: Binomial Distribution.svg.* Binomial [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) and normal [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) approximation for $n = 6$ and $p = 0.5$

If $n$ is large enough, then the skew of the distribution is not too great. In this case a reasonable approximation to $B(n, p)$ is given by the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462)

$$\mathcal{N}(np,\,np(1-p)),$$

and this basic approximation can be improved in a simple way by using a suitable [continuity correction](wikipedia://en/572382). The basic approximation generally improves as $n$ increases (at least 20) and is better when $p$ is not near to 0 or 1.[^o63] Various [rules of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thumb) may be used to decide whether $n$ is large enough, and $p$ is far enough from the extremes of zero or one:

- One rule[^o63] is that for $n > 5$ the normal approximation is adequate if the absolute value of the skewness is strictly less than 0.3; that is, if $$\frac{|1-2p|}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}=\frac1{\sqrt{n}}\left|\sqrt{\frac{1-p}p}-\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}}\,\right|<0.3.$$

This can be made precise using the [Berry–Esseen theorem](wikipedia://en/508012).

- A stronger rule states that the normal approximation is appropriate only if everything within 3 standard deviations of its mean is within the range of possible values; that is, only if $$\mu \pm 3\sigma = n p \pm 3 \sqrt{np(1-p)}\in(0,n).$$

This 3-standard-deviation rule is equivalent to the following conditions, which also imply the first rule above.

$$n>9 \left(\frac{1-p}{p} \right)\quad\text{and}\quad n>9\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).$$

::: {#o64 .proof cue=collapse name="[Proof]"}

The rule $np\pm3\sqrt{np(1-p)}\in(0,n)$ is totally equivalent to request that

$$np-3\sqrt{np(1-p)}>0\quad\text{and}\quad np+3\sqrt{np(1-p)}<n.$$

Moving terms around yields:

$$np>3\sqrt{np(1-p)}\quad\text{and}\quad n(1-p)>3\sqrt{np(1-p)}.$$

Since $0<p<1$, we can apply the square power and divide by the respective factors $np^2$ and $n(1-p)^2$, to obtain the desired conditions:

$$n>9 \left(\frac{1-p}p\right) \quad\text{and}\quad n>9 \left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).$$

Notice that these conditions automatically imply that $n>9$. On the other hand, apply again the square root and divide by 3,

$$\frac{\sqrt{n}}3>\sqrt{\frac{1-p}p}>0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\sqrt{n}}3 > \sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}}>0.$$

Subtracting the second set of inequalities from the first one yields:

$$\frac{\sqrt{n}}3>\sqrt{\frac{1-p}p}-\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}}>-\frac{\sqrt{n}}3;$$

and so, the desired first rule is satisfied,

$$\left|\sqrt{\frac{1-p}p}-\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}}\,\right|<\frac{\sqrt{n}}3.$$

:::

- Another commonly used rule is that both values $np$ and $n(1 - p)$ must be greater than[^o65][^o66] or equal to 5. However, the specific number varies from source to source, and depends on how good an approximation one wants. In particular, if one uses 9 instead of 5, the rule implies the results stated in the previous paragraphs.

::: {#o67 .proof cue=collapse name="[Proof]"}

Assume that both values $np$ and $n(1-p)$ are greater than 9. Since $0< p<1$, we easily have that

$$np\geq9>9(1-p)\quad\text{and}\quad n(1-p)\geq9>9p.$$

We only have to divide now by the respective factors $p$ and $1-p$, to deduce the alternative form of the 3-standard-deviation rule:

$$n>9 \left(\frac{1-p}p\right) \quad\text{and}\quad n>9 \left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).$$

:::

The following is an example of applying a [continuity correction](wikipedia://en/572382). Suppose one wishes to calculate $\Pr(X \le 8)$ for a binomial random variable $X$. If $Y$ has a distribution given by the normal approximation, then $\Pr(X \le 8)$ is approximated by $\Pr(Y \le 8.5)$. The addition of 0.5 is the continuity correction; the uncorrected normal approximation gives considerably less accurate results.

This approximation, known as [de Moivre–Laplace theorem](wikipedia://en/19451264), is a huge time-saver when undertaking calculations by hand (exact calculations with large $n$ are very onerous); historically, it was the first use of the normal distribution, introduced in [Abraham de Moivre](wikipedia://en/57327)'s book *[The Doctrine of Chances](wikipedia://en/161715)* in 1738. Nowadays, it can be seen as a consequence of the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406) since $B(n, p)$ is a sum of $n$ independent, identically distributed [Bernoulli variables](wikipedia://en/199189) with parameter $p$. This fact is the basis of a [hypothesis test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_test), a "proportion z-test", for the value of $p$ using $x / n$, the sample proportion and estimator of $p$, in a [common test statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_test_statistics).[^o68]

For example, suppose one randomly samples $n$ people out of a large population and ask them whether they agree with a certain statement. The proportion of people who agree will of course depend on the sample. If groups of $n$ people were sampled repeatedly and truly randomly, the proportions would follow an approximate normal distribution with mean equal to the true proportion $p$ of agreement in the population and with standard deviation

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$$

::: {#o70 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Poisson approximation"}

### Poisson approximation {#Poisson_approximation}

The binomial distribution converges towards the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) as the number of trials goes to infinity while the product $np$ converges to a finite limit. Therefore, the Poisson distribution with parameter $\lambda = np$ can be used as an approximation to $B(n, p)$ of the binomial distribution if $n$ is sufficiently large and $p$ is sufficiently small. According to rules of thumb, this approximation is good if $n \ge 20$ and $p \le 0.05$[^o71] such that $np \le 1$, or if $n > 50$ and $p < 0.1$ such that $np < 5$,[^o72] or if $n \ge 100$ and $np \le 10$.[^o73][^o74]

Concerning the accuracy of Poisson approximation, see Novak,[^o75] ch. 4, and references therein.

:::

### Limiting distributions {#Limiting_distributions}

- *[Poisson limit theorem](wikipedia://en/19465941)*: As $n$ approaches $\infty$ and $p$ approaches 0 with the product $np$ held fixed, the $\operatorname{Binomial}(n, p)$ distribution approaches the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) $\lambda = np$.[^o73]
- *[de Moivre–Laplace theorem](wikipedia://en/19451264)*: As $n$ approaches $\infty$ while $p$ remains fixed, the distribution of $$\frac{X-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}$$ approaches the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) with expected value 0 and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) 1. This result is sometimes loosely stated by saying that the distribution of $X$ is [asymptotically normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_normality) with expected value 0 and [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) 1. This result is a specific case of the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406).

### Beta distribution {#Beta_distribution}

The binomial distribution and beta distribution are different views of the same model of repeated Bernoulli trials. The binomial distribution is the [PMF](wikipedia://en/154725) of $k$ successes given $n$ independent events each with a probability $p$ of success. Mathematically, when $\alpha = k + 1$ and $\beta = n - k + 1$, the beta distribution and the binomial distribution are related by a factor of $n + 1$:

$$\operatorname{Beta}(p;\alpha;\beta) = (n+1)\mathrm{B}(k;n;p)$$

[Beta distributions](wikipedia://en/207074) also provide a family of [prior probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_distribution) for binomial distributions in [Bayesian inference](wikipedia://en/49571):[^o78]

$$P(p;\alpha,\beta) = \frac{p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}}{\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)}.$$

Given a uniform prior, the posterior distribution for the probability of success $p$ given $n$ independent events with $k$ observed successes is a beta distribution.[^o79]

## Computational methods {#Computational_methods}

### Random number generation {#Random_number_generation}

Methods for [random number generation](wikipedia://en/2736939) where the [marginal distribution](wikipedia://en/506077) is a binomial distribution are well-established.[^o82][^o83] One way to generate [random variates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variate) samples from a binomial distribution is to use an inversion algorithm. To do so, one must calculate the probability that $\Pr(X = k)$ for all values $k$ from $0$ through $n$. (These probabilities should sum to a value close to one, in order to encompass the entire sample space.) Then by using a [pseudorandom number generator](wikipedia://en/45524) to generate samples uniformly between 0 and 1, one can transform the calculated samples into discrete numbers by using the probabilities calculated in the first step.

## History {#History}

This distribution was derived by [Jacob Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/64740). He considered the case where $p = r/(r + s)$ where $p$ is the probability of success and $r$ and $s$ are positive integers. [Blaise Pascal](wikipedia://en/4068) had earlier considered the case where $p = 1/2$, tabulating the corresponding binomial coefficients in what is now recognized as [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497).[^o85]
