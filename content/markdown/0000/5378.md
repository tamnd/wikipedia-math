# Physical cosmology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Physical cosmology"}

*Image: CMB Timeline300 no WMAP.jpg.* Artist conception of the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116) cosmological model, the most widely accepted out of all in physical cosmology (neither time nor size to scale)

**Physical cosmology** is a branch of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) concerned with modeling the universe based on the [laws of physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_physics). A **cosmological model** provides a [mathematical description](wikipedia://en/20590) of the largest-scale structures and dynamics of the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) and allows study of fundamental questions about its [origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogony), structure, [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_the_universe), and [ultimate fate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_fate).[^o2] Cosmology as a [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) originated with the [Copernican principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_principle), which implies that [celestial bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object) obey identical [physical laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law) to those on Earth, and [Newtonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_mechanics), which first allowed those physical laws to be understood.

:::

Physical cosmology, as it is now understood, began in 1915 with the development of [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s [general theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), followed by major observational discoveries in the 1920s: first, [Edwin Hubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble) discovered that the universe contains a huge number of external [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) beyond the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way); then, work by [Vesto Slipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesto_Slipher) and others showed that the universe is [expanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expanding_universe). These advances made it possible to speculate about the origin of the universe, and allowed the establishment of the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116) theory, by [Georges Lemaître](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Lemaître), as the leading cosmological model. A few researchers still advocate a handful of [alternative cosmologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_cosmology);[^o3] however, most cosmologists agree that the Big Bang theory best explains the observations.[^o4]

Dramatic advances in observational cosmology since the 1990s, including the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376), distant [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernovae) and galaxy [redshift surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift_survey), have led to the development of a [standard model of cosmology](wikipedia://en/985963). Although this model requires the universe to contain large amounts of [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter) and [dark energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy) whose nature is currently not well understood, it gives detailed predictions that are in excellent agreement with many diverse observations.[^o4]

Cosmology draws heavily on the work of many disparate areas of research in [theoretical](wikipedia://en/19594028) and [applied physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_physics). Areas relevant to cosmology include [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics) experiments and [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics_phenomenology), theoretical and observational [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), general relativity, [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), and [plasma physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_physics).

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="History of physical cosmology"}

## Subject history {#Subject_history}

Modern cosmology developed along tandem tracks of theory and observation. In 1916, Albert Einstein published his theory of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), which provided a unified description of gravity as a geometric property of space and time.[^o7] At the time, Einstein believed in a [static universe](wikipedia://en/3050160), but found that his original formulation of the theory did not permit it.[^o8] This is because masses distributed throughout the universe gravitationally attract, and move toward each other over time.[^o9] However, he realized that his equations permitted the introduction of a constant term which could counteract the attractive force of gravity on the cosmic scale. Einstein published his first paper on relativistic cosmology in 1917, in which he added this *[cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992)* to his field equations in order to force them to model a static universe.[^o10] The Einstein model describes a static universe; space is finite and unbounded (analogous to the surface of a sphere, which has a finite area but no edges). However, this so-called Einstein model is unstable to small perturbations—it will eventually start to [expand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_expansion_of_space) or contract.[^o8] It was later realized that Einstein's model was just one of a larger set of possibilities, all of which were consistent with general relativity and the [cosmological principle](wikipedia://en/173937). The cosmological solutions of general relativity were found by [Alexander Friedmann](wikipedia://en/728892) in the early 1920s.[^o11] His equations describe the [Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker) universe, which may expand or contract, and whose geometry may be open, flat, or closed.

*Image: History of the Universe.svg.* History of the [Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) – [gravitational waves](wikipedia://en/8111079) are hypothesized to arise from [cosmic inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_%28cosmology%29), a rapidly accelerated expansion just after the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116)[^o55][^o56][^o57]

In the 1910s, [Vesto Slipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesto_Melvin_Slipher) (and later [Carl Wilhelm Wirtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Wirtz)) interpreted the [red shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_shift) of [spiral nebulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula) as a [Doppler shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_shift) that indicated they were receding from Earth.[^o12][^o13] However, it is difficult to determine the distance to astronomical objects. One way is to compare the physical size of an object to its [angular size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_size), but a physical size must be assumed in order to do this. Another method is to measure the [brightness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brightness) of an object and assume an intrinsic [luminosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity), from which the distance may be determined using the [inverse-square law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse-square_law). Due to the difficulty of using these methods, they did not realize that the nebulae were actually galaxies outside our own [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way), nor did they speculate about the cosmological implications. In 1927, the Belgian Roman Catholic [priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priest) [Georges Lemaître](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Lemaître) independently derived the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker equations and proposed, on the basis of the recession of spiral nebulae, that the universe began with the "explosion" of a "primeval [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom)"[^o14]—which was later called the Big Bang. In 1929, [Edwin Hubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Hubble) provided an observational basis for Lemaître's theory. Hubble showed that the spiral nebulae were galaxies by determining their distances using measurements of the brightness of [Cepheid variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cepheid_variable) stars. He discovered a relationship between the redshift of a galaxy and its distance. He interpreted this as evidence that the galaxies are receding from Earth in every direction at speeds proportional to their distance from Earth.[^o15] This fact is now known as [Hubble's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble's_law), though the numerical factor Hubble found relating recessional velocity and distance was off by a factor of ten, due to not knowing about the types of Cepheid variables.

Given the cosmological principle, Hubble's law suggested that the universe was expanding. Two primary explanations were proposed for the expansion. One was Lemaître's Big Bang theory, advocated and developed by [George Gamow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gamow). The other explanation was [Fred Hoyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hoyle)'s [steady state model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady_state_model) in which new matter is created as the galaxies move away from each other. In this model, the universe is roughly the same at any point in time.[^o16][^o17]

For a number of years, support for these theories was evenly divided. However, the observational evidence began to support the idea that the universe evolved from a hot dense state. The discovery of the cosmic microwave background in 1965 lent strong support to the Big Bang model,[^o17] and since the precise measurements of the cosmic microwave background by the [Cosmic Background Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Explorer) in the early 1990s, few cosmologists have seriously proposed other theories of the origin and evolution of the cosmos.

:::

## Energy of the cosmos {#Energy_of_the_cosmos}

The lightest [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_elements), primarily [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) and [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium), were created during the Big Bang through the process of [nucleosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleosynthesis).[^o40] In a sequence of [stellar nucleosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_nucleosynthesis) reactions, smaller atomic nuclei are then combined into larger atomic nuclei, ultimately forming stable [iron group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_group) elements such as [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) and [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel), which have the highest nuclear [binding energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_energies).[^o19] The net process results in a *later energy release*, meaning subsequent to the Big Bang.[^o20] Such reactions of nuclear particles can lead to *sudden energy releases* from [cataclysmic variable stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataclysmic_variable_star) such as [novae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nova). Gravitational collapse of matter into [black holes](wikipedia://en/4650) also powers the most energetic processes, generally seen in the nuclear regions of galaxies, forming *[quasars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar)* and *[active galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_galaxies)*.

Cosmologists cannot explain all cosmic phenomena exactly, such as those related to the [accelerating expansion of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerating_universe), using conventional [forms of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_forms). Instead, cosmologists propose a new form of energy called [dark energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy) that permeates all space.[^o21] One hypothesis is that dark energy is just the [vacuum energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_energy), a component of empty space that is associated with the [virtual particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_particle) that exist due to the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883).[^o22]

There is no clear way to define the total energy in the universe using the most widely accepted theory of gravity, general relativity. Therefore, it remains controversial whether the total energy is conserved in an expanding universe. For instance, each [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) that travels through intergalactic space loses energy due to the [redshift](wikipedia://en/26262) effect. This energy is not transferred to any other system, so seems to be permanently lost. On the other hand, some cosmologists insist that energy is conserved in some sense; this follows the law of [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088).[^o23]

Different forms of energy may dominate the cosmos—[relativistic particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_particle) which are referred to as [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation), or non-relativistic particles referred to as matter. Relativistic particles are particles whose [rest mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_mass) is zero or negligible compared to their [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327), and so move at the speed of light or very close to it; non-relativistic particles have much higher rest mass than their energy and so move much slower than the speed of light.

As the universe expands, both matter and radiation become diluted. However, the [energy densities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density) of radiation and matter dilute at different rates. As a particular volume expands, mass-energy density is changed only by the increase in volume, but the energy density of radiation is changed both by the increase in volume and by the increase in the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) of the photons that make it up. Thus the energy of radiation becomes a smaller part of the universe's total energy than that of matter as it expands. The very early universe is said to have been 'radiation dominated' and radiation controlled the deceleration of expansion. Later, as the average energy per photon becomes roughly 10 [eV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronvolt) and lower, matter dictates the rate of deceleration and the universe is said to be 'matter dominated'. The intermediate case is not treated well [analytically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_solution). As the expansion of the universe continues, matter dilutes even further and the cosmological constant becomes dominant, leading to an acceleration in the universe's expansion.

## History of the universe {#History_of_the_universe}

The history of the universe is a central issue in cosmology. The history of the universe is divided into different periods called epochs, according to the dominant forces and processes in each period. The standard cosmological model is known as the [Lambda-CDM model](wikipedia://en/985963).

::: {#o26 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cosmological Equations"}

### Equations of motion {#Equations_of_motion}

Within the [standard cosmological model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_cosmological_model), the [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) governing the universe as a whole are derived from general relativity with a small, positive cosmological constant.[^o27] The solution is an expanding universe; due to this expansion, the radiation and matter in the universe cool and become diluted. At first, the expansion is slowed down by [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation) attracting the radiation and matter in the universe. However, as these become diluted, the cosmological constant becomes more dominant and the expansion of the universe starts to accelerate rather than decelerate. In our universe this happened billions of years ago.[^o28]

:::

### Particle physics in cosmology {#Particle_physics_in_cosmology}

During the earliest moments of the universe, the average energy density was very high, making knowledge of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics) critical to understanding this environment. Hence, [scattering](wikipedia://en/164483) processes and [decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_decay) of unstable [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particles) are important for cosmological models of this period.

As a rule of thumb, a scattering or a decay process is cosmologically important in a certain epoch if the time scale describing that process is smaller than, or comparable to, the time scale of the expansion of the universe. The time scale that describes the expansion of the universe is $1/H$ with $H$ being the [Hubble parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_parameter), which varies with time. The expansion timescale $1/H$ is roughly equal to the age of the universe at each point in time.

### Timeline of the Big Bang {#Timeline_of_the_Big_Bang}

Observations suggest that the universe began around 13.8 billion years ago.[^o31] Since then, the evolution of the universe has passed through three phases. The very early universe, which is still poorly understood, was the split second in which the universe was so hot that [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subatomic_particle) had energies higher than those currently accessible in [particle accelerators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator) on Earth. Therefore, while the basic features of this epoch have been worked out in the Big Bang theory, the details are largely based on educated guesses.

Following this, in the early universe, the evolution of the universe proceeded according to known [high energy physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_energy_physics). This is when the first protons, electrons and neutrons formed, then nuclei and finally atoms. With the formation of neutral hydrogen, the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) was emitted. Finally, the epoch of structure formation began, when matter started to aggregate into the first [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) and [quasars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar), and ultimately galaxies, [clusters of galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clusters_of_galaxies) and [superclusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercluster) formed. The future of the universe is not yet firmly known, but according to the [ΛCDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ΛCDM) model it will continue expanding forever.

## Areas of study {#Areas_of_study}

Below, some of the most active areas of inquiry in cosmology are described, in roughly chronological order. This does not include all of the Big Bang cosmology, which is presented in *[Timeline of the Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_Big_Bang).*

### Very early universe {#Very_early_universe}

*Image: Big bang inflation vs standard genericchart.png.* The inflationary theory as an augmentation to the Big Bang theory was first proposed by Alan Guth of MIT. Inflation solves the 'horizon problem' by making the early universe much more compact than was assumed in the standard model. Given such smaller size, causal contact (i.e., thermal communication) would have been possible among all regions of the early universe. The image was an adaptation from various generic charts depicting the growth of the size of the observable universe, for both the standard model and inflationary model respectively, of the Big Bang theory.

The early, hot universe appears to be well explained by the Big Bang from roughly 10<sup>−33</sup> seconds onwards, but there are several [problems](wikipedia://en/4116#Problems). One is that there is no compelling reason, using current particle physics, for the universe to be [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_of_the_universe), homogeneous, and [isotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic) *(see the [cosmological principle](wikipedia://en/173937))*. Moreover, [grand unified theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_unified_theory) of particle physics suggest that there should be [magnetic monopoles](wikipedia://en/175470) in the universe, which have not been found. These problems are resolved by a brief period of [cosmic inflation](wikipedia://en/5382), which drives the universe to [flatness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatness_%28cosmology%29), smooths out [anisotropies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropy) and inhomogeneities to the observed level, and exponentially dilutes the monopoles.[^o34] The physical model behind cosmic inflation is extremely simple, but it has not yet been confirmed by particle physics, and there are difficult problems reconciling inflation and [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267). Some cosmologists think that [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305) and [brane cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brane_cosmology) will provide an alternative to inflation.[^o35]

Another major problem in cosmology is what caused the universe to contain far more matter than [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter). Cosmologists can observationally deduce that the universe is not split into regions of matter and antimatter. If it were, there would be [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray) and [gamma rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray) produced as a result of [annihilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annihilation), but this is not observed. Therefore, some process in the early universe must have created a small excess of matter over antimatter, and this (currently not understood) process is called *[baryogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryogenesis)*. Three required conditions for baryogenesis were derived by [Andrei Sakharov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Sakharov) in 1967, and requires a violation of the particle physics [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741#In_physics), called [CP-symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CP-symmetry), between matter and antimatter.[^o36] However, particle accelerators measure too small a violation of CP-symmetry to account for the baryon asymmetry. Cosmologists and particle physicists look for additional violations of the CP-symmetry in the early universe that might account for the [baryon asymmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon_asymmetry).[^o37]

Both the problems of baryogenesis and cosmic inflation are very closely related to particle physics, and their resolution might come from high energy theory and [experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator), rather than through observations of the universe.

### Big Bang Theory {#Big_Bang_Theory}

Big Bang nucleosynthesis is the theory of the formation of the elements in the early universe. It finished when the universe was about three minutes old and its [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) dropped below that at which [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion) could occur. Big Bang nucleosynthesis had a brief period during which it could operate, so only the very lightest elements were produced. Starting from hydrogen [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) ([protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton)), it principally produced [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium), [helium-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium), and [lithium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium). Other elements were produced in only trace abundances. The basic theory of nucleosynthesis was developed in 1948 by George Gamow, [Ralph Asher Alpher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Asher_Alpher), and [Robert Herman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Herman).[^o39] It was used for many years as a probe of physics at the time of the Big Bang, as the theory of Big Bang nucleosynthesis connects the abundances of primordial light elements with the features of the early universe.[^o40] Specifically, it can be used to test the [equivalence principle](wikipedia://en/857235),[^o41] to probe [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter), and test [neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino) physics.[^o42] Some cosmologists have proposed that Big Bang nucleosynthesis suggests there is a fourth "sterile" species of neutrino.[^o43]

#### Standard model of Big Bang cosmology {#Standard_model_of_Big_Bang_cosmology}

The **ΛCDM** (**Lambda cold dark matter**) or **[Lambda-CDM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda-CDM)** model is a [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) of the Big Bang cosmological model in which the universe contains a cosmological constant, denoted by [Lambda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda) ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) **Λ**), associated with dark energy, and [cold dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_dark_matter) (abbreviated **CDM**). It is frequently referred to as the **standard model** of Big Bang cosmology.[^o45][^o46]

### Cosmic microwave background {#Cosmic_microwave_background}

The cosmic microwave background is radiation left over from [decoupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decoupling_%28cosmology%29) after the epoch of [recombination](wikipedia://en/26244469) when neutral atoms first formed. At this point, radiation produced in the Big Bang stopped [Thomson scattering](wikipedia://en/624714) from charged ions. The radiation, first observed in 1965 by [Arno Penzias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arno_Penzias) and [Robert Woodrow Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Woodrow_Wilson), has a perfect thermal [black-body](wikipedia://en/44364) spectrum. It has a temperature of 2.7 [kelvins](wikipedia://en/19593121) today and is isotropic to one part in 10<sup>5</sup>. [Cosmological perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/3998149), which describes the evolution of slight inhomogeneities in the early universe, has allowed cosmologists to precisely calculate the angular [power spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_spectrum) of the radiation, and it has been measured by the recent satellite experiments ([COBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Explorer) and [WMAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WMAP))[^o48] and many ground and balloon-based experiments (such as [Degree Angular Scale Interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_Angular_Scale_Interferometer), [Cosmic Background Imager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Imager), and [Boomerang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOOMERanG_experiment)).[^o49] One of the goals of these efforts is to measure the basic parameters of the Lambda-CDM model with increasing accuracy, as well as to test the predictions of the Big Bang model and look for new physics. The results of measurements made by WMAP, for example, have placed limits on the neutrino masses.[^o50]

Newer experiments, such as [QUIET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QUIET) and the [Atacama Cosmology Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Cosmology_Telescope), are trying to measure the [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) of the cosmic microwave background.[^o51] These measurements are expected to provide further confirmation of the theory as well as information about cosmic inflation, and the so-called secondary anisotropies,[^o52] such as the [Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunyaev-Zel'dovich_effect) and [Sachs-Wolfe effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sachs-Wolfe_effect), which are caused by interaction between [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) and [clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_cluster) with the cosmic microwave background.[^o53][^o54]

On 17 March 2014, astronomers of the [BICEP2 Collaboration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BICEP_and_Keck_Array#BICEP2) announced the apparent detection of [*B*-mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-modes) polarization of the CMB, considered to be evidence of [primordial gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_gravitational_wave) that are predicted by the theory of [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_%28cosmology%29) to occur during the earliest phase of the Big Bang.[^o55][^o56][^o57][^o58] However, later that year the [Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_spacecraft) collaboration provided a more accurate measurement of [cosmic dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_dust), concluding that the B-mode signal from dust is the same strength as that reported from BICEP2.[^o59][^o60] On 30 January 2015, a joint analysis of BICEP2 and [Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_%28spacecraft%29) data was published and the [European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency) announced that the signal can be entirely attributed to interstellar dust in the Milky Way.[^o61]

### Formation and evolution of large-scale structure {#Formation_and_evolution_of_large-scale_structure}

Understanding the formation and evolution of the largest and earliest structures (i.e., quasars, galaxies, [clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_groups_and_clusters) and [superclusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercluster)) is one of the largest efforts in cosmology. Cosmologists study a model of **hierarchical structure formation** in which structures form from the bottom up, with smaller objects forming first, while the largest objects, such as superclusters, are still assembling.[^o63] One way to study structure in the universe is to survey the visible galaxies, in order to construct a three-dimensional picture of the galaxies in the universe and measure the matter [power spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_spectrum). This is the approach of the *[Sloan Digital Sky Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Digital_Sky_Survey)* and the [2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2dF_Galaxy_Redshift_Survey).[^o64][^o65]

Another tool for understanding structure formation is simulations, which cosmologists use to study the gravitational aggregation of matter in the universe, as it clusters into [filaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_filament), superclusters and [voids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Void_%28astronomy%29). Most simulations contain only non-baryonic [cold dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_dark_matter), which should suffice to understand the universe on the largest scales, as there is much more dark matter in the universe than visible, baryonic matter. More advanced simulations are starting to include baryons and study the formation of individual galaxies. Cosmologists study these simulations to see if they agree with the galaxy surveys, and to understand any discrepancy.[^o66]

*Image: Gravitational lens found in the DESI Legacy Surveys data (noirlab2104c).jpg.* An example of a gravitational lens found in the DESI Legacy Surveys data. There are four sets of lensed images in DESI-090.9854-35.9683, corresponding to four distinct background galaxies—from the outermost giant red arc to the innermost bright blue arc, arranged in four concentric circles. All of them are gravitationally warped—or lensed—by the orange galaxy at the very center. Dark matter is expected to produce gravitational lensing also.

Other, complementary observations to measure the distribution of matter in the distant universe and to probe [reionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reionization) include:

- The [Lyman-alpha forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyman-alpha_forest), which allows cosmologists to measure the distribution of neutral atomic hydrogen gas in the early universe, by measuring the absorption of light from distant quasars by the gas.[^o67]
- The [21-centimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_line) [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_%28electromagnetic_radiation%29) line of neutral atomic hydrogen also provides a sensitive test of cosmology.[^o68]
- [Weak lensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_lensing), the distortion of a distant image by [gravitational lensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_lensing) due to dark matter.[^o69]

These will help cosmologists settle the question of when and how structure formed in the universe.

### Dark matter {#Dark_matter}

Evidence from [Big Bang nucleosynthesis](wikipedia://en/44058), the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376), structure formation, and [galaxy rotation curves](wikipedia://en/183083) suggests that about 23% of the mass of the universe consists of non-baryonic dark matter, whereas only 4% consists of visible, [baryonic matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryonic_matter). The gravitational effects of dark matter are well understood, as it behaves like a cold, [non-radiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay) fluid that forms [haloes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactic_halo) around galaxies. Dark matter has never been detected in the laboratory, and the particle physics nature of dark matter remains completely unknown. Without observational constraints, there are a number of candidates, such as a stable [supersymmetric](wikipedia://en/224636) particle, a [weakly interacting massive particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_interacting_massive_particle), a gravitationally-interacting massive particle, an [axion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axion), and a [massive compact halo object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massive_compact_halo_object). Alternatives to the dark matter hypothesis include a modification of gravity at small accelerations ([MOND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOND)) or an effect from brane cosmology. [TeVeS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeVeS) is a version of MOND that can explain gravitational lensing.[^o71]

### Dark energy {#Dark_energy}

If the universe is [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatness_%28cosmology%29), there must be an additional component making up 73% (in addition to the 23% dark matter and 4% baryons) of the energy density of the universe. This is called dark energy. In order not to interfere with Big Bang nucleosynthesis and the cosmic microwave background, it must not cluster in haloes like baryons and dark matter. There is strong observational evidence for dark energy, as the total energy density of the universe is known through constraints on the flatness of the universe, but the amount of clustering matter is tightly measured, and is much less than this. The case for dark energy was strengthened in 1999, when measurements demonstrated that the expansion of the universe has begun to gradually accelerate.[^o73]

Apart from its density and its clustering properties, nothing is known about dark energy. *[Quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267)* predicts a cosmological constant (CC) much like dark energy, but 120 [orders of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude) larger than that observed.[^o74] [Steven Weinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Weinberg) and a number of string theorists *(see [string landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_landscape))* have invoked the 'weak [anthropic principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic_principle)': i.e. the reason that physicists observe a universe with such a small cosmological constant is that no physicists (or any life) could exist in a universe with a larger cosmological constant. Many cosmologists find this an unsatisfying explanation: perhaps because while the weak anthropic principle is self-evident (given that living observers exist, there must be at least one universe with a cosmological constant (CC) which allows for life to exist) it does not attempt to explain the context of that universe.[^o75] For example, the weak anthropic principle alone does not distinguish between:

- Only one universe will ever exist and there is some underlying principle that constrains the CC to the value we observe.
- Only one universe will ever exist and although there is no underlying principle fixing the CC, we got lucky.
- Lots of universes exist (simultaneously or serially) with a range of CC values, and of course ours is one of the life-supporting ones.

Other possible explanations for dark energy include [quintessence](wikipedia://en/39137)[^o76] or a modification of gravity on the largest scales.[^o77] The effect on cosmology of the dark energy that these models describe is given by the dark energy's [equation of state](wikipedia://en/1540704), which varies depending upon the theory. The nature of dark energy is one of the most challenging problems in cosmology.

A better understanding of dark energy is likely to solve the problem of the [ultimate fate of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_fate_of_the_universe). In the current cosmological epoch, the accelerated expansion due to dark energy is preventing structures larger than [superclusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercluster) from forming. It is not known whether the acceleration will continue indefinitely, perhaps even increasing until a [Big Rip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Rip), or whether it will eventually reverse, lead to a [Big Freeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_death_of_the_universe), or follow some other scenario.[^o78]

### Gravitational waves {#Gravitational_waves}

[Gravitational waves](wikipedia://en/8111079) are ripples in the [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) that propagate as [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) at the speed of light, generated in certain gravitational interactions that propagate outward from their source. [Gravitational-wave astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational-wave_astronomy) is an emerging branch of [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy) which aims to use gravitational waves to collect observational data about sources of detectable gravitational waves such as [binary star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star) systems composed of [white dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_dwarf), [neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star), and [black holes](wikipedia://en/4650); and events such as [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova), and the formation of the [early universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronology_of_the_universe) shortly after the Big Bang.[^o80]

In 2016, the [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO) Scientific Collaboration and [Virgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_interferometer) Collaboration teams announced that they had made the [first observation of gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves), originating from a [pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_black_hole) of [merging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_collision) black holes using the Advanced LIGO detectors.[^o81][^o82][^o83] On 15 June 2016, a [second detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GW151226) of gravitational waves from coalescing black holes was announced.[^o84] Besides LIGO, many other [gravitational-wave observatories (detectors)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational-wave_observatory) are under construction.[^o85]

### Other areas of inquiry {#Other_areas_of_inquiry}

Cosmologists also study:

- Whether [primordial black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_black_hole) were formed in our universe, and what happened to them.[^o87]
- Detection of cosmic rays with energies above the [GZK cutoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GZK_cutoff),[^o88] and whether it signals a failure of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) at high energies.
- The [equivalence principle](wikipedia://en/857235),[^o41] whether or not Einstein's general theory of relativity is the correct theory of [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation),[^o89] and if the fundamental [laws of physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_physics) are the same everywhere in the universe.[^o90]
- **Biophysical cosmology**: a type of physical cosmology that studies and understands [life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life) as part or an inherent part of physical cosmology. It stresses that [life is inherent to the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe#Habitability) and therefore [frequent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life#In_the_universe).[^o91]
