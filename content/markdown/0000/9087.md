# Dynamical system

*Image: Dynsys.png.* A set of dynamical systems. Top left: a [cellular automaton](wikipedia://en/54342). Top center: [Exterior billiards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_billiards). Top right a constrained [3-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-body_problem). Bottom left a [Poincare section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincare_section) of a [Standard map](wikipedia://en/9503407) ([chaos](wikipedia://en/6295) arise in the dotted regions). Middle bottom: a chaotic [Dynamical billiards](wikipedia://en/2160429) (a symptom of [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295) here are the trajectories filling the configuration space). Bottom right: A [geodesic flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_flow) such as light on a surface, trajectories are [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) i.e. [minimum paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maupertius_principle), in this case the phase space is a [torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torus_%28mathematics%29) (stable orbits arise when the periods are rational, if [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689) that is a path to [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295)).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dynamical system"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) and [systems theory](wikipedia://en/29238), a **dynamical system** is the description of how a system evolves in time. The study of dynamical systems is the focus of *[dynamical systems theory](wikipedia://en/990632)*.[^o2][^o3] It has applications to a wide variety of fields such as mathematics, physics, [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology),[^o4] [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering),[^o5] [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics),[^o6] [history](wikipedia://en/20155525), and [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine). Dynamical systems are a fundamental part of [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295), [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) dynamics, [bifurcation theory](wikipedia://en/2326042), the [self-assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-assembly) and [self-organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-organization) processes, and the [edge of chaos](wikipedia://en/428932) concept.

:::

For example, an [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) can experimentally record the positions of how the [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planets) move in the sky, and this can be considered a complete enough description of a dynamical system. In the case of planets there is also enough knowledge to codify this information as a set of [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations) with [initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_conditions), or as a [map](wikipedia://en/516931) from the present state to a future state in a predefined [state space](wikipedia://en/548156) with a [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) parameter t, or as an [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101).[^o7]

## Overview {#Overview}

*Image: Rudolphine\_tables.jpg.* Two pages of the [Rudolphine tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolphine_tables) showing eclipses of the Sun and Moon, data was collected from [Tycho Brahe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_brahe) and published by [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler)[^o7]

The concept of a dynamical system has its origins in [Newtonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_mechanics) and more precisely in [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182). There, as in other natural sciences and engineering disciplines, there is some need to predict the evolution of the system, but maybe also pose other questions such as stability, qualitative or long term behaviour, dependence on parameters, existence of periodic, stochastic or chaotic behaviour.[^o7] The relation from one state and another is either explicit such as a function in the parameter t predicting position and velocity of a particle or implicit such as a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309), [difference equation](wikipedia://en/146806) or other [time scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_scale_calculus). Some times it may not be possible to define such a description, there may not even be a differential equation predicting [stock price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_price), or it maybe impossible to build one but still talk stock prices can be considered a dynamical system based on experimental data changing over time.[^o9]

Important properties are existence and uniqueness of solutions, [integrability](wikipedia://en/2573213) (i.e. the existence of conserved quantities), the possibility to solve the system and be able to compute the state at any point in time. Other properties are whether the system is [discrete](wikipedia://en/8492), [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122), [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable), [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function), [deterministic](wikipedia://en/522958), [ergodic](wikipedia://en/258980), [stochastic](wikipedia://en/292222) or [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295).[^o10][^o11]

If the system can be solved, then, given an initial point, it is possible to determine all its future positions, a collection of points known as a *[trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory)* or *[orbit](wikipedia://en/252250)*.

Before the advent of [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computers), finding an orbit required sophisticated mathematical techniques and could be accomplished only for a small class of dynamical systems.[^o12] Numerical methods implemented on electronic computing machines have simplified the task of determining the orbits of a dynamical system.[^o13]

*Image: Stability\_Diagram.png.* **Stability diagram** classifying [Poincaré maps](wikipedia://en/876732#Poincaré_maps_and_stability_analysis) of linear [autonomous system](wikipedia://en/312398)

For simple dynamical systems, knowing the trajectory is often sufficient, but most dynamical systems are too complicated to be understood in terms of individual trajectories. The difficulties arise because:

- The systems studied may only be known approximately—the parameters of the system may not be known precisely or terms may be missing from the equations. The approximations used bring into question the validity or relevance of numerical solutions. To address these questions several notions of stability have been introduced in the study of dynamical systems, such as [Lyapunov stability](wikipedia://en/363360) or [structural stability](wikipedia://en/2515807). The [stability of the dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_stability) implies that there is a class of models or initial conditions for which the trajectories would be equivalent. The operation for comparing orbits to establish their [equivalence](wikipedia://en/9259) changes with the different notions of stability.[^o7]
- The type of trajectory may be more important than one particular trajectory. Some trajectories may be periodic, whereas others may wander through many different states of the system. Applications often require enumerating these classes or maintaining the system within one class. Classifying all possible trajectories has led to the qualitative study of dynamical systems, that is, properties that do not change under coordinate changes. [Linear dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/4786318) and [systems that have two numbers describing a state](wikipedia://en/2784317) are examples of dynamical systems where the possible classes of orbits are understood.[^o14]
- The behavior of trajectories as a function of a parameter may be what is needed for an application. As a parameter is varied, the dynamical systems may have [bifurcation points](wikipedia://en/2326042) where the qualitative behavior of the dynamical system changes. For example, it may go from having only periodic motions to apparently erratic behavior, as in the [transition to turbulence of a fluid](wikipedia://en/154664).[^o15]
- The trajectories of the system may appear erratic, as if random. In these cases it may be necessary to compute averages using one very long trajectory or many different trajectories. The averages are well defined for [ergodic systems](wikipedia://en/258986) and a more detailed understanding has been worked out for [hyperbolic systems](wikipedia://en/2320078). Understanding the probabilistic aspects of dynamical systems has helped establish the foundations of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) and of [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295).[^o16]

## History {#History}

*Image: Forced Duffing equation Poincaré section.png.* A two-dimensional Poincaré section of the forced [Duffing equation](wikipedia://en/2987943)

Many people regard French mathematician [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) as the founder of dynamical systems.[^o20] Poincaré published two now classical monographs, "New Methods of Celestial Mechanics" (1892–1899) and "Lectures on Celestial Mechanics" (1905–1910). In them, he successfully applied the results of their research to the problem of the motion of three bodies and studied in detail the behavior of solutions (frequency, stability, asymptotic, and so on). These papers included the [Poincaré recurrence theorem](wikipedia://en/2404544), which states that certain systems will, after a sufficiently long but finite time, return to a state very close to the initial state.[^o7]

[Aleksandr Lyapunov](wikipedia://en/65425) developed many important approximation methods. His methods, which he developed in 1899, make it possible to define the stability of sets of ordinary differential equations. He created the modern theory of the stability of a dynamical system.[^o7]

In 1913, [George David Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/30871906) proved Poincaré's "[Last Geometric Theorem](wikipedia://en/35737249)", a special case of the [three-body problem](wikipedia://en/1208420), a result that made him world-famous. In 1927, he published his *Dynamical Systems*.[^o21]

Birkhoff's most durable result has been his 1931 discovery of what is now called the [ergodic theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_theorem). Combining insights from [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) on the [ergodic hypothesis](wikipedia://en/258980) with [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), this theorem solved, at least in principle, a fundamental problem of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481). The ergodic theorem has also had repercussions for dynamics.[^o7]

*Image: Smale Horseshoe Map.svg.* The Smale [horseshoe map](wikipedia://en/449364) $f$ is the composition of three geometrical transformations.

[Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294) made significant advances as well. His first contribution was the [Smale horseshoe](wikipedia://en/449364) that jumpstarted significant research in dynamical systems. He also outlined a research program carried out by many others.

[Oleksandr Mykolaiovych Sharkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oleksandr_Mykolaiovych_Sharkovsky) developed [Sharkovsky's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharkovsky's_theorem) on the periods of discrete dynamical systems in 1964. One of the implications of the theorem is that if a discrete dynamical system on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) has a [periodic point](wikipedia://en/4941621) of period 3, then it must have periodic points of every other period.[^o7]

In the late 20th century, the dynamical system perspective to partial differential equations started gaining popularity. Palestinian mechanical engineer [Ali H. Nayfeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_H._Nayfeh) applied [nonlinear dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_dynamics) in [mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) systems.[^o22] His pioneering work in applied nonlinear dynamics has been influential in the construction and maintenance of [machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machines) and [structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structures) that are common in daily life, such as [ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ships), [cranes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_%28machine%29), [bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridges), [buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buildings), [skyscrapers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyscrapers), [jet engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engines), [rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engines), [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft) and [spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft).[^o23]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The most general definition unifies several concepts in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) such as [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) and [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) such as [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), [quantum state](wikipedia://en/30876419) and [thermodynamic state](wikipedia://en/2747182), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) such as [system theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_theory), [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) and even [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773).

### Mathematical intuition {#Mathematical_intuition}

From a [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) perspective in the most general case the state space X is treated as a generic [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). This space X has a [semi-group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-group) structure on it (i.e. where only [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) is required) and there is most often a natural choice for an [Identity](wikipedia://en/298428) element, which is typically attached to the origin of the chosen [reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_frame). This semi-group can be intuitively interpreted as the time coordinate t.[^o27] Time in fact has an addition operation and an origin, the identity, like a group. The action of the semi-group on X is a set of [maps](wikipedia://en/516931) from X to itself parametric in the time t, and this is intuitively the time evolution.[^o28]

### Generalizing the state space {#Generalizing_the_state_space}

It is possible to allow different choices of the [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_%28controls%29) space such as a function space (e.g. the pressure, temperature and velocity of a gas in a rocket are a function in the space of solutions of some fluid dynamics [PDEs](wikipedia://en/52564) and they may vary over time),[^o30][^o31] a [quantum state space](wikipedia://en/9718351) (e.g. the state of an atom can be described by a set of [functions](wikipedia://en/11180) in an [hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) and a set of [probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilities) for these),[^o31][^o32] or a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) (e.g. the state of a black hole can be described by a [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) on a [Riemann manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) and its position will be a vector in the same manifold).[^o33] Other choices can be a [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), a [Configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596) or even a [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) (e.g. the set of [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_numbers) or a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615)).[^o34]

### Time as a multidimensional manifold {#Time_as_a_multidimensional_manifold}

*Image: STS-3 Canadarm captures PDP.jpg.* Considering two [control](wikipedia://en/7039) variables of a [robotic arm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotic_arm) which are typically angles and assuming complete rotations of 360 degrees the space of configurations will then be a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800)

Time can be generalized too as a generic set of continuous parameters, for example the [control](wikipedia://en/7039) parameters of a [robot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holonomic_system) can be a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). There is no need that time has a direction, that is [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiability) or even that it has whatsoever meaning similar to the intuition of [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time), in fact it can be generalized to even more general algebraic objects.[^o36]

A general class of systems are defined over multiple independent variables and are therefore called [multidimensional systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multidimensional_systems). Such systems are useful for modeling, and for example in [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing).

Time typically is considered often an external parameter as in [classical](wikipedia://en/19555586) and [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), and this is typically called time domain representation, and it goes hand in hand with the [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) formulation. This is not necessarily always the case: [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) for example is [frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_frame) independent,[^o37] and gravity has an influence over time too, and in [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) the use of the [Lagrangian mechanics](wikipedia://en/23371726) formulation is more common[^o38] where time and space are on same footing. In both cases the literature still talks about dynamical systems.

### Discrete dynamical system {#Discrete_dynamical_system}

Time can also be a discrete parameter. When time is generalized to the multi-dimensional case, i.e. as a general set of control or external parameters, this space can be interpreted as a [Lattice](wikipedia://en/291472), i.e. as the discrete points of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) or the [tics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tick_size) of a [stock price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_price).[^o40] Discrete Time events therefore can be counted by integers, for example like the measurements of the position of the planets in the sky, but this can vastly differ from the intuition of time as a clock that has equispaced time events. One of the tasks is typically to extract some mathematical model from the data.[^o41]

### Not deterministic {#Not_deterministic}

*Image: Difference between deterministic and Nondeterministic.svg.* A [Nondeterministic Turing machine](wikipedia://en/21935) is an example of discrete dynamical system where computation cannot be represented sequentially but only on a tree, given each input state may have multiple output states

The *evolution rule* of the dynamical system is a function that describes what future states follow from the current state. Often the function is [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_system_%28mathematics%29), that is, for a given time interval only one future state follows from the current state.[^o43][^o44] However, some systems are not [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic) they may allow multiple future states (i.e. the maps are generalized into [multivalued functions](wikipedia://en/222367) and not uniquely defined everywhere) and the system can be subject to a [bifurcation](wikipedia://en/2326042).

### Stochastic {#Stochastic}

Some systems are also [stochastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_system), either in the input parameters such as an [oscillator with a random force](wikipedia://en/166890), or in the initial conditions, or in the predicted variables as in a [Stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454). In that random events also affect the evolution of the state variables, and this includes stochastic [jump processes](wikipedia://en/2180750) which are not continuous, a prototype example of a stochastic dynamical system are [stock prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_price).[^o46]

### Chaotic and Quantum systems {#Chaotic_and_Quantum_systems}

*Image: False color image of the far field of a submerged turbulent jet.jpg.* False color image of the far field of a submerged turbulent jet

Lastly, there are [chaotic systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaotic_systems) (i.e. typically deterministic but not predictable) such as:

- [complex dynamics](wikipedia://en/650518)
- [Hyperbolic dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_dynamics)
- multiplicative chaos[^o48][^o49][^o50][^o51]
- non-deterministic chaos[^o52][^o53]

And [quantum systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_systems) (i.e. deterministic until they are measured), or [quantum chaotic](wikipedia://en/313779) systems.[^o7]

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Assume that X is a non empty set with elements called states. Assume a general transformation:

$$T : X \to X$$

It is possible to interpret X as a state space and T as the evolution between states.[^o56] Adding different structures on T and on X allows to model different properties of the dynamical system.

It is possible to model time evolution: $\hat{T}$ can be a semigroup with one parameter $t$ called [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) that will also belong to a semi-group such as $N (t>0)$ in the discrete time case, $R^{+} (t>0)$ in the continuous time case.

A semigroup structure introduces associativity

$$\hat{T_1}(\hat{T_2}\hat{T_3})=(\hat{T_1}\hat{T_2})\hat{T_3}$$

which implies a composition law between different time evolutions:[^o57]

$$\hat{T}(t_1+t_2)=\hat{T}(t_1)\hat{T}(t_2)$$

this is also ultimately a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564).

It is possible to define an origin of time $t=0$ adding an identity to the semi-group

$$\hat{T}(0)=\mathbf{1}$$

and it is finally possible also to model reversible time evolution: T can be a group such as $\mathbf{Z}$ or $R$, and being a group this in fact has a definition of inverse transformations:[^o58]

$$\exists! \hat{T}^{-1}: \hat{T}^{-1} = \hat{T}(-t), \hat{T}(-t)\hat{T}(t) = \mathbf{1}$$

More commonly there are multiple classes of definitions for a dynamical system: a first one is motivated by [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) and is geometrical in flavor, there is an additional [differentiability](wikipedia://en/8564) structure; a second one is motivated by [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) and is [measure theoretical](wikipedia://en/19873#Measure_theory) in flavor, there is an additional [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_set_topology) structure and a last one which is motivated by [Category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) and is more [abstract](wikipedia://en/19616384) in flavour.

## Geometrical definition {#Geometrical_definition}

*Image: PenduleEspaceDesPhases.png.* A simple [pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) interpreted as a [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735), the circular orbits are the stable ones when the pendulum is oscillating back and forward, the red orbits are when the pendulum has enough speed to rotate indefinitely in one direction around the center

In the geometrical definition, a dynamical system is the tuple $\langle \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{M}, f\rangle$. $\mathcal{T}$ is the domain for time – there are many choices, usually the reals or the integers, possibly restricted to be non-negative. $\mathcal{M}$ is a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), and typically, but not always, *f* is an evolution rule *t* → *f*<sup>*t*</sup> (with $t\in\mathcal{T}$) such that *f<sup>t</sup>* is a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) of the manifold to itself.[^o60]

Typically there is some extra structure on the manifold:

1. To be locally a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), this allows to use standard techniques of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180).
1. A [symplectic](wikipedia://en/294298) structure which is the typical case for a [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101)
1. A Euclidean Space such as configuration space due to [holonomic constraints](wikipedia://en/9777067)
1. A discrete structure like a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806).
1. A set of [diffeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphisms) such as [coordinate transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_transformations)
1. A [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) like in a [Riemann manifold](wikipedia://en/144652)

### Real dynamical system {#Real_dynamical_system}

A *real dynamical system*, *real-time dynamical system*, *[continuous time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_time) dynamical system*, or *[flow](wikipedia://en/2619735)* is a tuple (*T*, *M*, Φ) with *T* an [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) in the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) **R**, *M* a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) typically but not necessarily locally [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), and Φ a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122).[^o63]

Being locally homeomorphic to a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) allows to use theorems of existence and uniqueness of solutions for differential equations,[^o64] and linear operators,[^o65] and this makes it analogous the classical definition based on a system of differential equations.

#### Differentiability {#Differentiability}

If Φ is [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable) the system is called a *differentiable dynamical system*. The function *f* is therefore a "smooth" mapping of the time-domain $\mathcal{T}$ into the space of diffeomorphisms of the manifold to itself. In other terms, *f*(*t*) is a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564), for every time *t* in the domain $\mathcal{T}$ The [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) *M* is then typically locally [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic) to a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989).[^o67]

#### Dimensionality {#Dimensionality}

If the manifold *M* is locally diffeomorphic to **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, the dynamical system is *finite-dimensional*; if not, the dynamical system is *infinite-dimensional*.[^o69]

*Image: Conformal\_power\_half.svg.* [Conformal maps](wikipedia://en/50627) are simple examples of [complex dynamics](wikipedia://en/650518) and incompressible [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320)

#### Flows {#Flows}

When *T* is taken to be the reals, the dynamical system is called *global* or a *[flow](wikipedia://en/2619735)*; and if *T* is restricted to the non-negative reals, then the dynamical system is a *semi-flow*.

#### Classical definition {#Classical_definition}

The modern geometrical definition assumes a [map](wikipedia://en/516931) that provides an explicit description of the dynamical system, this is motivated by [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986), by [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) and by mathematical techniques that go beyond differential equations. An explicit description is often not available, the classical geometrical definition is implicit, rooted in classical mechanics, and based on a standard set of [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations) and a finite set of [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622):

The totality of states of motion may be set into one-to-one correspondence with the points, P, of a closed n-dimensional manifold, M, in such wise that for suitable coordinates $x_1,...,x_n$ the differential equations of motion may be written:

$$\frac{dx_i}{dt} = u_i(x_1,...,x_n,t);(i=1,...,n)$$

There can be different regularity conditions to the functions $u_i$ such as being [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable) or [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478).[^o72]

This definition implies the [existence and uniqueness](wikipedia://en/498383) of solutions of such equations.

#### Lagrangian Dynamical system {#Lagrangian_Dynamical_system}

*Image: Thermohaline\_Circulation\_2.png.* An advanced application of lagrangian dynamics is typically in oceanography,[^o74] one can interpret the [Thermohaline circulation](wikipedia://en/387457) as a [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) or incompressible fluid regime

It is also possible to cast the geometrical definition in terms of a [variational principle](wikipedia://en/491097):

Let M be a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219), TM its [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794), and $L: TM\to \mathbb{R}$ a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206). A [map](wikipedia://en/516931) $\gamma: \mathbb{R}\to M$ is called a motion in the Lagrangian system, with configuration [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) M and [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) L, if $\gamma$ is an extremal of the functional:

$$\Phi(\gamma)=\int_{t_0}^{t_1} L(\gamma,\dot{\gamma}) dt$$

where $\dot{\gamma}\in TM_{\gamma(t)}$ is called velocity vector.[^o75]

## Measure theoretical definition {#Measure_theoretical_definition}

*Image: Streamlines\_around\_a\_NACA\_0012.svg.* [Potential flows](wikipedia://en/58320) are very straightforward examples of volume preserving dynamical systems

A dynamical system may be defined formally as the triplet (*T*, $\mathfrak{M}$, Φ), where Φ is a measure-preserving transformation of a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $\mathfrak{M}$, Φ is also the action of a semigroup T as the general case. Here the [Measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) $\mathfrak{M}$ is defined by the triplet (*X*, Σ, *μ*), where *X* is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691), Σ is a [sigma-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) on *X* and μ is a finite [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on the [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) (*X*, Σ). Often $\mathfrak{M}$ is a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325), i.e. a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) where the total measure of the full space is 1, in other words with a finite and normalized [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437). [^o84] [^o85]

A map Φ: *X* → *X* is said to be [Σ-measurable](wikipedia://en/44055) if and only if, for every σ in Σ, one has $\Phi^{-1}\sigma \in \Sigma$. A map Φ is said to **preserve the measure** if and only if, for every *σ* in Σ, one has $\mu(\Phi^{-1}\sigma ) = \mu(\sigma)$. Combining the above, a map Φ is said to be a **measure-preserving transformation of *X***, if it is a map from *X* to itself, it is Σ-measurable, and is measure-preserving.

The triplet (*T*, $\mathfrak{M}$, Φ), with $\mathfrak{M}=$(*X*, Σ, *μ*) for such a Φ, is then defined to be a **measure-preserving dynamical system**.[^o86][^o87]

::: {#o89 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Conventions"}

### Conventions {#Conventions}

There are two conventions for the definition of the maps:

1. Physics and continuous time convention: The map Φ embodies the time evolution of the dynamical system, it is possible to interpret it for discrete time evolution as Φ=$\Phi_n$parametrized by $n \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ or for a continuous time evolution as Φ=$\Phi_t$ where $t \in \mathbb{R}^+$, in the general case each map is different. Assuming an initial time $t_0$ and a state vector $\vec{\mathbb{x}}(t)\in X$, the map can be seen as a time translation map $\vec{\mathbb{x}}(t+t_0) = \hat{\Phi}(t)[ \vec{\mathbb{x}}(t_0) ]$.[^o90] In the discrete case this means that the total map $\hat{\Phi}_n = \Phi_n \circ \Phi_{n-1} \circ \dots \circ \Phi_1 \circ \Phi_0$ can be seen as composition of individual maps.[^o91] This convention is also related to the study of [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_points) and [Cauchy sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_sequences).
1. Mathematics and discrete convention: all maps are defined as the evolution of the state for one time step to the next one, i.e. from $t_n \to t_{n+1}$, or in the continuous case from $t \to t + \delta t$. The maps $\hat{\Phi}_n: x\in X, x_{n} \to x_{n+1}$ are the same map $\Phi$ for all time steps $t_n$, this can be interpreted also as an [iteration over the map](wikipedia://en/1448702) $\hat{\Phi}_n = \Phi^n = \Phi \circ \Phi \circ \dots \circ \Phi$. This convention is also related to the study of [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738), and [recursion](wikipedia://en/25407).

In the language of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200): [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) in the [state space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space) X is the [induced representation](wikipedia://en/357452) of [time translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_translation) in the [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) T, and the [map](wikipedia://en/516931) Φ is the [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) that induces the [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) on X,[^o92] i.e. there is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) beteeen the time group T and the state space X where $T(t_0+t) = T(t) \circ T(t_0)$. In the language of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) the [infinitesimal generator](wikipedia://en/17945) of time translation $t \to t+ \delta t$ is the derivative in time ${\partial}_t$, and also the time evolution operator.[^o93]

### Relation to geometric definition {#Relation_to_geometric_definition}

The measure theoretical definition assumes the existence of a measure-preserving transformation, in physical terminology the measure is a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) over the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101). The intuition comes from [Hamiltonian systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_systems) which are [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) preserving. The fact that the energy is an [integral of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_of_motion) induces volume conservation on the phase space, or incompressibility of the [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735),[^o97] extra integrals of motions induce further foliations on the phase space.. Another intuition comes from [permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutations) which are "incompressible". One can interpret the set of all possible [permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutations) of a set [^o98] as the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) induced by the conservation of number of elements of the set.[^o99][^o100]

Many different invariant measures can be associated to any one evolution rule, in the same manner as multiple probability densities can induce the same [macro-state](wikipedia://en/3066350). If the dynamical system is given by a system of differential equations the appropriate measure must be determined, the symmetries are not explicit and it may not even be possible to define a probability measure in certain patological cases. This makes it difficult to develop ergodic theory starting from differential equations, so it becomes convenient to have a dynamical systems-motivated definition within [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) that side-steps the choice of measure [^o101] and assumes the choice has been made.

Informally and [heuristically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristically) from an [ergodic theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_theorem) perspective [time averages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_averages) are equal to [space averages](wikipedia://en/9653)/[ensemble averages](wikipedia://en/59052#Ensemble_average). Ultimately [^o102][time averages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_averages) are [constant of motions](wikipedia://en/5391037), they corresponds to [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetries) due to the [noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether_theorem), and ultimately symmetries corresponds to space averages[^o103][^o104][^o105]

A simple construction (sometimes called the [Krylov–Bogolyubov theorem](wikipedia://en/7635266)) shows that for a large class of systems it is always possible to construct a measure so as to make the evolution rule of the dynamical system a measure-preserving transformation. In the construction a given measure of the state space is summed for all future points of a trajectory, assuring the invariance.[^o106]

Some systems have a natural measure, such as the [Liouville measure](wikipedia://en/312301) in [Hamiltonian systems](wikipedia://en/1197531), chosen over other invariant measures, such as the measures supported on periodic orbits of the Hamiltonian system. For chaotic [dissipative systems](wikipedia://en/237587) the choice of invariant measure is technically more challenging. The measure needs to be supported on the [attractor](wikipedia://en/191094), but attractors have zero [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) and the invariant measures must be [singular](wikipedia://en/4304500)[^o107] with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198). For [dissipative systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipative_systems) typically a small region of [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) shrinks under time evolution,[^o108] for [chaotic systems](wikipedia://en/6295) a small region of phase space instead typically grows and expands at least to a full finite region of the phase space

Heuristically [dissipation](wikipedia://en/164572) has an effect opposite to [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295) it tends to dampen and limit motion in general but also it dampens [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664) and [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295), expanding to a finite region of phase space means to be [sensitive to initial conditions](wikipedia://en/4024), [ergodic regimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_system) tend to grow and cover in full a finite region of [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), i.e to have [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [periodic orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit), and [chaotic regimes](wikipedia://en/6295) are also [topologically transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_transitive)[^o109]

For hyperbolic dynamical systems, the [Sinai–Ruelle–Bowen measures](wikipedia://en/65740749) appear to be the natural choice. They are constructed on the geometrical structure of [stable and unstable manifolds](wikipedia://en/5466649) of the dynamical system; they behave physically under small perturbations; and they explain many of the observed statistics of hyperbolic systems.

### Topological dynamical system {#Topological_dynamical_system}

A [topological dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_dynamical_system) is a dynamical system (**T**, *X*, Φ) on a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) and/or [Hausdorff topological space](wikipedia://en/13637) *X*.[^o111][^o112][^o113] Φ is a [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) and therefore a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660).[^o114]

Topological dynamics is typically done from a [global](wikipedia://en/42027498) perspective, i.e. considering all possible [initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_conditions) and outcomes (e.g. [equilibrium points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_points), [periodic orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbits), [attractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractors) or [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295)), the [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and the groupings of the trajectories, the long term behaviour in time of the system, the [homeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphisms) (i.e. continuous deformations) of the trajectories. Typically the [trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectories) are [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable), and the systems are also [flows](wikipedia://en/2619735).

These techniques are often [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) in nature and complementary to [ergodic dynamics](wikipedia://en/258986), to [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) and to [integral of motions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_of_motion) via the [Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether_theorem). For example one of the goals of topological dynamics is to classify [topological conjugacy](wikipedia://en/5506149) [classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugacy_classes), groupings of type of motions with respect to an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660), one of the goals of [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) instead is ergodic decomposition , one of the goals of [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) is to classify all the [group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_theorem_%28group_theory%29) [conjugacy classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugacy_classes) of the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) and an [integrable system](wikipedia://en/2573213) is when all [integral of motions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_of_motion) can be derived and are known.

#### Compactification {#Compactification}

It is often useful to study the continuous extension Φ\* of Φ to the [one-point compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-point_compactification) *X\** of *X*. Even after losing the differential structure of the original system, there are compactness arguments to analyze the new system (**R**, *X\**, Φ\*). This is similar in spirit to [Projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) where all limit points to infinity are the same point. Another more general technique is to use [Stone–Čech compactification](wikipedia://en/52897).[^o116][^o117][^o118] which is similar in spirit to [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890) where all limit points at infinity are considered different.

#### Relevance {#Relevance}

In compact dynamical systems the [limit set](wikipedia://en/5492198) of any orbit is [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty), [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected).

As an example in a topological dynamical system the limit orbit of an [attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_attractor) is contained within the manifold itself. This is a non trivial statement for multiple reasons: limit orbits may never be reached; limit orbits may have [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) zero; attaching a probability to a limit orbit would be non trivial; an attractor may also have multiple limit orbits and the distinction between different [compactifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactifications) may be relevant.

## Definition with Category theory {#Definition_with_Category_theory}

### Categories vs semi-groups {#Categories_vs_semi-groups}

### Definition with monoids {#Definition_with_monoids}

In the context of category theory, categories are always defined together with an Identity map, therefore these definitions are based on [monoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoids) instead of semi-groups.[^o124][^o114]

A **dynamical system**[^o125][^o126][^o114]

is a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) (*T*, *X*, Φ) where *T* is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652), written additively, *X* is a non-empty [set](wikipedia://en/26691) and Φ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427):

$$\Phi: U \subseteq (T \times X) \to X$$

with:

$$\mathrm{proj}_{2}(U) = X$$

(where $\mathrm{proj}_{2}$ is the 2nd [projection map](wikipedia://en/3913145)) and for any *x* in *X*:

$$\Phi(0,x) = x$$

$$\Phi(t_2,\Phi(t_1,x)) = \Phi(t_2 + t_1, x),$$

for $\, t_1,\, t_2 + t_1 \in I(x)$ and $\ t_2 \in I(\Phi(t_1, x))$, where we have defined the set $I(x) := \{ t \in T : (t,x) \in U \}$ for any *x* in *X*.

In particular, in the case that $U = T \times X$ we have for every *x* in *X* that $I(x) = T$ and thus that Φ defines a [monoid action](wikipedia://en/1058218) of *T* on *X*.

The function Φ(*t*,*x*) is called the **evolution function** of the dynamical system: it associates to every point *x* in the set *X* a unique image, depending on the variable *t*, called the **evolution parameter**. *X* is called **[phase space](wikipedia://en/191101)** or **state space**, while the variable *x* represents an **initial state** of the system.

We often write:

$$\Phi_x(t) \equiv \Phi(t,x)$$

$$\Phi^t(x) \equiv \Phi(t,x)$$

if we take one of the variables as constant. The function

$$\Phi_x:I(x) \to X$$

is called the **flow** through *x* and its [graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_%28function%29) is called the **[trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory)** through *x*. The set

$$\gamma_x \equiv\{\Phi(t,x) : t \in I(x)\}$$

is called the **[orbit](wikipedia://en/252250)** through *x*. The orbit through *x* is the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of the flow through *x*.

A subset *S* of the state space *X* is called Φ-**invariant** if for all *x* in *S* and all *t* in *T*

$$\Phi(t,x) \in S.$$

Thus, in particular, if *S* is Φ-**invariant**, $I(x) = T$ for all *x* in *S*. That is, the flow through *x* must be defined for all time for every element of *S*.

## Construction of dynamical systems {#Construction_of_dynamical_systems}

The concept of *evolution in time* is central to the theory of dynamical systems as seen in the previous sections: the basic reason for this fact is that the starting motivation of the theory was the study of time behavior of [classical mechanical systems](wikipedia://en/19555586). But a system of [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) must be solved before it becomes a dynamic system. For example, consider an [initial value problem](wikipedia://en/498383) such as the following:

$$\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})$$

$$\boldsymbol{x}|_{{t=0}}=\boldsymbol{x}_0$$

where

- $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}$ represents the [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) of the material point **x**
- *M* is a finite dimensional manifold
- **v**: *T* × *M* → *TM* is a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> or **C**<sup>*n*</sup> and represents the change of [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) induced by the known [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) acting on the given material point in the phase space *M*. The change is not a vector in the phase space *M*, but is instead in the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) *TM*.

There is no need for higher order derivatives in the equation, nor for the parameter *t* in *v*(*t*,*x*), because these can be eliminated by considering systems of higher dimensions.

Depending on the properties of this vector field, the mechanical system is called

- **autonomous**, when **v**(*t*, **x**) = **v**(**x**)
- **homogeneous** when **v**(*t*, **0**) = 0 for all *t*

The solution can be found using standard ODE techniques and is denoted as the evolution function already introduced above

$$\boldsymbol{{x}}(t)=\Phi(t,\boldsymbol{{x}}_0)$$

The dynamical system is then (*T*, *M*, Φ).

Some formal manipulation of the system of [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) shown above gives a more general form of equations a dynamical system must satisfy

$$\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{v}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=0 \qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad \mathfrak{{G}}\left(t,\Phi(t,\boldsymbol{{x}}_0)\right)=0$$

where $\mathfrak{G}:{{(T\times M)}^M}\to\mathbf{C}$ is a [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) from the set of evolution functions to the field of the complex numbers.

This equation is useful when modeling mechanical systems with complicated constraints.

Many of the concepts in dynamical systems can be extended to infinite-dimensional manifolds—those that are locally [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989)—in which case the differential equations are [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564).

## Linear dynamical systems {#Linear_dynamical_systems}

Linear dynamical systems are at the heart of any [system engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_engineering) and [system theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_theory) curriculum. Historically linear systems up to the 1970s reflected most of [system theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_theory) as a whole (i.e. before the widespread availability of computers). They include the basic features of any dynamical system, such as [attenuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuation) [saturation](wikipedia://en/9678) and [oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation), and at least locally they can approximate also any non linear systems.

Linear dynamical systems can be solved in terms of simple functions such as exponentials and simple trigonometric functions (i.e. complex exponentials), and the behavior of all orbits can be classified.

In a linear system the phase space is the *N*-dimensional Euclidean space, so any point in phase space can be represented by a vector with *N* numbers. N dimensional Linear dynamical systems are also not [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295)

The analysis of linear systems is also simplified and possible because they satisfy a [superposition principle](wikipedia://en/1201321): if *u*(*t*) and *w*(*t*) satisfy a linear differential equation that describe a system, then so will a linear combination $\alpha u(t) + \beta w(t)$. With [superposition](wikipedia://en/1201321) is possible to generate new solutions from known ones, therefore is just necessary to classify the fundamental solutions to know all of them.

### Flows {#Flows}

For a [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735), the vector field v(*x*) is an [affine](wikipedia://en/38449) function of the position in the phase space, that is,

$$\dot{x} = v(x) = A x + b,$$

with *A* a matrix, *b* a vector of numbers and *x* the position vector. The solution to this system can be found by using the superposition principle (linearity). The case *b* ≠ 0 with *A* = 0 is just a straight line in the direction of *b*:

$$\Phi^t(x_1) = x_1 + b t.$$

When *b* is zero and *A* ≠ 0 the origin is an equilibrium (or singular) point of the flow, that is, if *x*<sub>0</sub> = 0, then the orbit remains there. For other initial conditions, the equation of motion is given by the [exponential of a matrix](wikipedia://en/672731): for an initial point *x*<sub>0</sub>,

$$\Phi^t(x_0) = e^{t A} x_0.$$

When *b* = 0, the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of *A* determine the structure of the phase space. From the eigenvalues and the [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of *A* it is possible to determine if an initial point will converge or diverge to the equilibrium point at the origin.

The distance between two different initial conditions in the case *A* ≠ 0 will change exponentially in most cases, either converging exponentially fast towards a point, or diverging exponentially fast. Linear systems display sensitive dependence on initial conditions in the case of divergence. For nonlinear systems this is one of the (necessary but not sufficient) conditions for [chaotic behavior](wikipedia://en/6295).

### Maps {#Maps}

A [discrete-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time_dynamical_system), [affine](wikipedia://en/38449) dynamical system has the form of a [matrix difference equation](wikipedia://en/27567102):

$$x_{n+1} = A x_n + b,$$

with *A* a matrix and *b* a vector. As in the continuous case, the change of coordinates *x* → *x* + (1 − *A*)<sup>–1</sup>*b* removes the term *b* from the equation. In the new [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931), the origin is a fixed point of the map and the solutions are of the linear system *A*<sup>*n*</sup>*x*<sub>0</sub>. The solutions for the map are no longer curves, but points that hop in the phase space. The orbits are organized in curves, or fibers, which are collections of points that map into themselves under the action of the map.

As in the continuous case, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of *A* determine the structure of phase space. For example, if *u*<sub>1</sub> is an eigenvector of *A*, with a real eigenvalue smaller than one, then the straight lines given by the points along *α* *u*<sub>1</sub>, with *α* ∈ **R**, is an invariant curve of the map. Points in this straight line run into the fixed point.

There are also many other discrete dynamical systems such as [Chaotic maps](wikipedia://en/2067823).

### Local dynamics {#Local_dynamics}

The qualitative properties of dynamical systems do not change under a smooth change of coordinates (this is sometimes taken as a definition of qualitative): a *singular point* of the vector field (a point where *v*(*x*) = 0) will remain a singular point under smooth transformations; a *periodic orbit* is a loop in phase space and smooth deformations of the phase space cannot alter it being a loop. It is in the neighborhood of singular points and periodic orbits that the structure of a phase space of a dynamical system can be well understood. In the qualitative study of dynamical systems, the approach is to show that there is a change of coordinates (usually unspecified, but computable) that makes the dynamical system as simple as possible.

### Rectification {#Rectification}

A flow in most small patches of the phase space can be made very simple. If *y* is a point where the vector field *v*(*y*) ≠ 0, then there is a change of coordinates for a region around *y* where the vector field becomes a series of parallel vectors of the same magnitude. This is known as the rectification theorem.

The *rectification theorem* says that away from [singular points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) the dynamics of a point in a small patch is a straight line. The patch can sometimes be enlarged by stitching several patches together, and when this works out in the whole phase space *M* the dynamical system is *integrable*. In most cases the patch cannot be extended to the entire phase space. There may be singular points in the vector field (where *v*(*x*) = 0); or the patches may become smaller and smaller as some point is approached. The more subtle reason is a global constraint, where the trajectory starts out in a patch, and after visiting a series of other patches comes back to the original one. If the next time the orbit loops around phase space in a different way, then it is impossible to rectify the vector field in the whole series of patches.

### Near periodic orbits {#Near_periodic_orbits}

In general, in the neighborhood of a periodic orbit the rectification theorem cannot be used. Poincaré developed an approach that transforms the analysis near a periodic orbit to the analysis of a map. Pick a point *x*<sub>0</sub> in the orbit γ and consider the points in phase space in that neighborhood that are perpendicular to *v*(*x*<sub>0</sub>). These points are a [Poincaré section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_section) *S*(*γ*, *x*<sub>0</sub>), of the orbit. The flow now defines a map, the [Poincaré map](wikipedia://en/876732) *F* : *S* → *S*, for points starting in *S* and returning to *S*. Not all these points will take the same amount of time to come back, but the times will be close to the time it takes *x*<sub>0</sub>.

The intersection of the periodic orbit with the Poincaré section is a fixed point of the Poincaré map *F*. By a translation, the point can be assumed to be at *x* = 0. The Taylor series of the map is *F*(*x*) = *J* · *x* + O(*x*<sup>2</sup>), so a change of coordinates *h* can only be expected to simplify *F* to its linear part

$$h^{-1} \circ F \circ h(x) = J \cdot x.$$

This is known as the conjugation equation. Finding conditions for this equation to hold has been one of the major tasks of research in dynamical systems. Poincaré first approached it assuming all functions to be analytic and in the process discovered the non-resonant condition. If *λ*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *λ*<sub>*ν*</sub> are the eigenvalues of *J* they will be resonant if one eigenvalue is an integer linear combination of two or more of the others. As terms of the form *λ*<sub>*i*</sub> – Σ (multiples of other eigenvalues) occurs in the denominator of the terms for the function *h*, the non-resonant condition is also known as the small divisor problem.

### Conjugation results {#Conjugation_results}

The results on the existence of a solution to the conjugation equation depend on the eigenvalues of *J* and the degree of smoothness required from *h*. As *J* does not need to have any special symmetries, its eigenvalues will typically be complex numbers. When the eigenvalues of *J* are not in the unit circle, the dynamics near the fixed point *x*<sub>0</sub> of *F* is called *[hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_fixed_point)* and when the eigenvalues are on the unit circle and complex, the dynamics is called *elliptic*.

In the hyperbolic case, the [Hartman–Grobman theorem](wikipedia://en/9933752) gives the conditions for the existence of a continuous function that maps the neighborhood of the fixed point of the map to the linear map *J* · *x*. The hyperbolic case is also *structurally stable*. Small changes in the vector field will only produce small changes in the Poincaré map and these small changes will reflect in small changes in the position of the eigenvalues of *J* in the complex plane, implying that the map is still hyperbolic.

The [Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM)](wikipedia://en/16972) theorem gives the behavior near an elliptic point.

## Bifurcation theory {#Bifurcation_theory}

*Image: Saddlenode.gif.* bifurcation with saddle point and equilibrium point

When the evolution map Φ<sup>*t*</sup> (or the [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) it is derived from) depends on a parameter μ, the structure of the phase space will also depend on this parameter. Small changes may produce no qualitative changes in the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) until a special value *μ*<sub>0</sub> is reached. At this point the phase space changes qualitatively and the dynamical system is said to have gone through a bifurcation.

Bifurcation theory considers a structure in phase space (typically a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738), a periodic orbit, or an invariant [torus](wikipedia://en/74800)) and studies its behavior as a function of the parameter *μ*. At the bifurcation point the structure may change its stability, split into new structures, or merge with other structures. By using Taylor series approximations of the maps and an understanding of the differences that may be eliminated by a change of coordinates, it is possible to catalog the bifurcations of dynamical systems.

*Image: Chaosorderchaos.png.* Period halving bifurcations, followed by equilibrium, followed by [period-doubling bifurcations](wikipedia://en/4457659)

The bifurcations of a hyperbolic fixed point *x*<sub>0</sub> of a system family *F<sub>μ</sub>* can be characterized by the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of the first derivative of the system *DF*<sub>*μ*</sub>(*x*<sub>0</sub>) computed at the bifurcation point. For a map, the bifurcation will occur when there are eigenvalues of *DF<sub>μ</sub>* on the unit circle. For a flow, it will occur when there are eigenvalues on the imaginary axis. For more information, see the main article on [Bifurcation theory](wikipedia://en/2326042).

Some bifurcations can lead to very complicated structures in phase space. For example, the [Ruelle–Takens scenario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruelle–Takens_scenario) describes how a periodic orbit bifurcates into a torus and the torus into a [strange attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractor). In another example, [Feigenbaum period-doubling](wikipedia://en/79173) describes how a stable periodic orbit goes through a series of [period-doubling bifurcations](wikipedia://en/4457659).

## Ergodic systems {#Ergodic_systems}

In many dynamical systems, it is possible to choose the coordinates of the system so that the volume (really a ν-dimensional volume) in phase space is invariant. This happens for mechanical systems derived from Newton's laws as long as the coordinates are the position and the momentum and the volume is measured in units of (position) × (momentum). The flow takes points of a subset *A* into the points Φ<sup>*t*</sup>(*A*) and invariance of the phase space means that

$$\mathrm{vol} (A) = \mathrm{vol} ( \Phi^t(A) ).$$

In the [Hamiltonian formalism](wikipedia://en/198319), given a coordinate it is possible to derive the appropriate (generalized) momentum such that the associated volume is preserved by the flow. The volume is said to be computed by the [Liouville measure](wikipedia://en/312301).

In a Hamiltonian system, not all possible configurations of position and momentum can be reached from an initial condition. Because of energy conservation, only the states with the same energy as the initial condition are accessible. The states with the same energy form an energy shell Ω, a sub-manifold of the phase space. The volume of the energy shell, computed using the Liouville measure, is preserved under evolution.

For systems where the volume is preserved by the flow, Poincaré discovered the [recurrence theorem](wikipedia://en/2404544): Assume the phase space has a finite Liouville volume and let *F* be a phase space volume-preserving map and *A* a subset of the phase space. Then almost every point of *A* returns to *A* infinitely often. The Poincaré recurrence theorem was used by [Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655) to object to [Boltzmann](wikipedia://en/544255)'s derivation of the increase in entropy in a dynamical system of colliding atoms.

One of the questions raised by Boltzmann's work was the possible equality between time averages and space averages, what he called the [ergodic hypothesis](wikipedia://en/258980). The hypothesis states that the length of time a typical trajectory spends in a region *A* is vol(*A*)/vol(Ω).

The ergodic hypothesis turned out not to be the essential property needed for the development of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) and a series of other ergodic-like properties were introduced to capture the relevant aspects of physical systems. [Koopman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Koopman) approached the study of ergodic systems by the use of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). An observable *a* is a function that to each point of the phase space associates a number (say instantaneous pressure, or average height). The value of an observable can be computed at another time by using the evolution function φ<sup>t</sup>. This introduces an operator *U*<sup>*t*</sup>, the [transfer operator](wikipedia://en/1346096),

$$(U^t a)(x) = a(\Phi^{-t}(x)).$$

By studying the spectral properties of the linear operator *U* it becomes possible to classify the ergodic properties of Φ<sup>*t*</sup>. In using the Koopman approach of considering the action of the flow on an observable function, the finite-dimensional nonlinear problem involving Φ<sup>*t*</sup> gets mapped into an infinite-dimensional linear problem involving *U*.

The Liouville measure restricted to the energy surface Ω is the basis for the averages computed in [equilibrium statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481). An average in time along a trajectory is equivalent to an average in space computed with the [Boltzmann factor exp(−β*H*)](wikipedia://en/28481#Canonical_ensemble). This idea has been generalized by Sinai, Bowen, and Ruelle (SRB) to a larger class of dynamical systems that includes dissipative systems. [SRB measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRB_measure) replace the Boltzmann factor and they are defined on attractors of chaotic systems.

## Nonlinear dynamical systems and chaos {#Nonlinear_dynamical_systems_and_chaos}

### Chaotic systems {#Chaotic_systems}

Simple nonlinear dynamical systems, including [piecewise linear](wikipedia://en/444224) systems, can exhibit strongly unpredictable behavior, which might seem to be random, despite the fact that they are fundamentally deterministic. This unpredictable behavior has been called *[chaos](wikipedia://en/6295)*. [Hyperbolic systems](wikipedia://en/2320078) are precisely defined dynamical systems that exhibit the properties ascribed to chaotic systems. In hyperbolic systems the [tangent spaces](wikipedia://en/30807) perpendicular to an orbit can be decomposed into a combination of two parts: one with the points that converge towards the orbit (the *stable manifold*) and another of the points that diverge from the orbit (the *unstable manifold*).

This branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) deals with the long-term qualitative behavior of dynamical systems. Here, the focus is not on finding precise solutions to the equations defining the dynamical system (which is often hopeless), but rather to answer questions like "Will the system settle down to a [steady state](wikipedia://en/1326107) in the long term, and if so, what are the possible [attractors](wikipedia://en/191094)?" or "Does the long-term behavior of the system depend on its initial condition?"

The chaotic behavior of complex systems is not the issue. [Meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904) has been known for years to involve complex—even chaotic—behavior. Chaos theory has been so surprising because chaos can be found within almost trivial systems. The [Pomeau–Manneville scenario](wikipedia://en/33909868) of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) and the [Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem](wikipedia://en/2864857) arose with just second-degree polynomials; the [horseshoe map](wikipedia://en/449364) is piecewise linear.

### Solutions of finite duration {#Solutions_of_finite_duration}

For non-linear autonomous ODEs it is possible under some conditions to develop solutions of finite duration,[^o163] meaning here that in these solutions the system will reach the value zero at some time, called an ending time, and then stay there forever after. This can occur only when system trajectories are not uniquely determined forwards and backwards in time by the dynamics, thus solutions of finite duration imply a form of "backwards-in-time unpredictability" closely related to the forwards-in-time unpredictability of chaos. This behavior cannot happen for [Lipschitz continuous](wikipedia://en/51731) differential equations according to the proof of the [Picard-Lindelof theorem](wikipedia://en/666177). These solutions are non-Lipschitz functions at their ending times and cannot be analytical functions on the whole real line.

As example, the equation:

$$y'= -\text{sgn}(y)\sqrt{|y|},\,\,y(0)=1$$

Admits the finite duration solution:

$$y(t)=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\frac{t}{2}+\left|1-\frac{t}{2}\right|\right)^2$$

that is zero for $t \geq 2$ and is not Lipschitz continuous at its ending time $t = 2.$

## Algebraic dynamical system {#Algebraic_dynamical_system}

Algebraic dynamical systems can be considered as a special algebraic case of the classical geometric definition. This is based on an algebraic time evolution map as the [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) or implicitly as a system of algebraic equations instead of [ODEs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations). From a [measure theoretic perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), the [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) is a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270), a [variety](wikipedia://en/248808), an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924), and a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315): the focus is then on symmetries, and [ergodic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic) behavior. These systems are often studied with methods from [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316).

In the context of [Algebraic quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/346489) the [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) can be a [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184), namely [observables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observables) are not just algebras of functions[^o165] but an [operator algebra](wikipedia://en/455987), and this is treated together with a global gauge group (i.e. a group of constraints that leaves the observable invariant).[^o166]

In the context of [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) the [neural network](wikipedia://en/21523) itself (e.g. [Recursive neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive_neural_network), [diffusion models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_models)) can be treated as a set of algebraic equations (often these are finite approximations such as [gradient descent](wikipedia://en/201489), there is a concept of [time step](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_step), [evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) dynamics across the network, and eventually there is a separate set of algebraic constraints such as enforcing unwanted behaviors.[^o167][^o168]

In the context of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), typically one studies the iterations of a [rational map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_map) over specific [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_fields), this area is called Algebraic Dynamics,[^o169] it studies things such as the rational points of algebraic curves, and is deeply connected to [Arithmetic dynamics](wikipedia://en/17584701)

One such examples is the [Poncelet map](wikipedia://en/82880402), where a point is moving in successive steps between two given [conics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conics), and the equations are algebraic (i.e. tangents and intersections). Another example can be a [billiard](wikipedia://en/2160429) with a border that is an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260), such as an elliptic billiard, in this case the dynamics is defined by reflections.[^o170]

Note that [algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_curves) are [differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable) almost every where (a part from a finite number of points), therefore one can study them with analytic methods. In these two examples, the continuous dynamics is piecewise linear, the important events are typically discrete, albeit may be still an infinite or even measurable set (such as an ergodic trajectory in the billiard), the important events are when an algebraic equation applies (such as a reflection on a border). These systems can therefore be studied as discrete dynamical systems too.

## Effective Dynamics {#Effective_Dynamics}

Effective dynamics is the coarse-graining of equations of motion that describe a system at a certain scale, or a specific behaviour, typically getting rid of certain [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622). Instead of solving the [microstate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstate), i.e. the exact interactions for all atoms, molecules, or neural network nodes, effective dynamics models an "effective" force, energy or set of parameters and equations that describe the aggregate effect.

Typically effective dynamics arises from simplifying or specializing complex non linear phenomena, a major way to achieve this is with [ensemble averages](wikipedia://en/59052#Ensemble_average) and this links it deeply with [Ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) and the [Renormalization group](wikipedia://en/291462). Key aspect of effective dynamics is the concept of [emergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence), such as: [linear behaviour](wikipedia://en/91591) emergence from non linear equations as in [solitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solitons) or [linear](wikipedia://en/91591) emergence from [ensemble averages](wikipedia://en/59052#Ensemble_average), emergence of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_and_disorder) and [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622) in [phase transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transitions) such as in the [Landau theory](wikipedia://en/995169), emergence of [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) patterns in [percolation](wikipedia://en/299184) dynamics.

Examples of these approaches are Effective Field Theories,Reynolds Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS), disordered quantum systems,[^o172] thermodynamics and Phase transitions.

## Category theory for dynamical systems {#Category_theory_for_dynamical_systems}

In the period between 2000 and 2020, category theory has been applied to system theory (e.g. to open systems and subsystems)[^o174] and to dynamical systems.[^o175][^o176][^o177] The motivation is to study common properties across dynamical systems, topological dynamical systems (i.e. with compact state space), and measure preserving dynamical systems (e.g. hamiltonian systems).[^o178] It is also possible to draw an analogy between group representation theory (such as [irreducible representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_representations)) and [ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic) decomposition[^o179] i.e. that every invariant (i.e. [conservative](wikipedia://en/6956)) measure is a mixture of ergodic ones, in an analogous fashion to the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406). Ultimately this can be compared to the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556) and to prime number decomposition.[^o180]

- [Sci.Nonlinear FAQ 2.0 (Sept 2003)](http://amath.colorado.edu/faculty/jdm/faq-Contents.html) provides definitions, explanations and resources related to nonlinear science

**Online books or lecture notes**

- [Geometrical theory of dynamical systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/math.HO/0111177). Nils Berglund's lecture notes for a course at [ETH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH) at the advanced undergraduate level.
- [Dynamical systems](https://archive.org/details/dynamicalsystems00birk). George D. Birkhoff's 1927 book already takes a modern approach to dynamical systems.
- [Chaos: classical and quantum](http://chaosbook.org/). An introduction to dynamical systems from the periodic orbit point of view.
- [Learning Dynamical Systems](https://www.cs.brown.edu/research/ai/dynamics/tutorial/home.html). Tutorial on learning dynamical systems.
- [Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems](https://www.mat.univie.ac.at/~gerald/ftp/book-ode/). Lecture notes by [Gerald Teschl](wikipedia://en/28661853)
