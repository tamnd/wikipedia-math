# Butterfly effect

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Butterfly effect"}

*Image: Double pendulum simultaneous realisations.ogv.* Experimental demonstration of the butterfly effect with six recordings of the same [double pendulum](wikipedia://en/63967). In each recording, the pendulum starts with almost the same initial condition. Over time, the differences in the dynamics grow from almost unnoticeable to drastic.

In [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295), the **butterfly effect** is the sensitive dependence on [initial conditions](wikipedia://en/1186804) in which a small change in one state of a [deterministic](wikipedia://en/522958) [nonlinear system](wikipedia://en/146103) can result in large differences in a later state.

:::

The term is closely associated with the work of the mathematician and meteorologist [Edward Norton Lorenz](wikipedia://en/1706783). He said the butterfly effect is derived from the example of the details of a [tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado) (the exact time of formation, the exact path taken) being influenced by minor perturbations such as a distant [butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly) flapping its wings several weeks earlier. Lorenz originally used a seagull causing a storm but was persuaded to make it more poetic with the use of a butterfly and tornado by 1972.[^o2][^o3] He discovered the effect when he observed runs of his [weather model](wikipedia://en/1505381) with initial condition data that were rounded in a seemingly inconsequential manner. He said the weather model would fail to reproduce the results of runs with the unrounded initial condition data. A very small change in initial conditions had created a significantly different outcome.[^o4]

The idea that small causes may have large effects in weather was earlier acknowledged by the French mathematician and physicist [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740). The American mathematician and philosopher [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185) also contributed to this theory. Lorenz's work placed the concept of *instability* of the [Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) onto a quantitative base and linked the concept of instability to the properties of large classes of dynamic systems which are undergoing [nonlinear dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_dynamics) and [deterministic chaos](wikipedia://en/6295).[^o5]

The concept of the butterfly effect has since been used outside the context of weather science as a broad term for any situation where a small change is supposed to be the cause of larger consequences.

## History {#History}

In *[The Vocation of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Vocation_of_Man)* (1800), [Johann Gottlieb Fichte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Gottlieb_Fichte) says "you could not remove a single grain of sand from its place without thereby ... changing something throughout all parts of the immeasurable whole".

[Chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295) and the sensitive dependence on initial conditions were described in numerous forms of literature. This is evidenced by the case of the [three-body problem](wikipedia://en/1208420) by Poincaré in 1890.[^o7] He later proposed that such phenomena could be common, for example, in meteorology.[^o8]

In 1898, [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675) noted general divergence of trajectories in spaces of negative curvature. [Pierre Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Duhem) discussed the possible general significance of this in 1908.[^o7]

In 1950, [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) noted: "The displacement of a single electron by a billionth of a centimetre at one moment might make the difference between a man being killed by an avalanche a year later, or escaping."[^o9]

The idea that the death of one butterfly could eventually have a far-reaching [ripple effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripple_effect) on subsequent historical events made its earliest known appearance in "[A Sound of Thunder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Sound_of_Thunder)", a 1952 short story by [Ray Bradbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Bradbury) in which a time traveller alters the future by inadvertently treading on a butterfly in the past.[^o10]

More precisely, though, almost the exact idea and the exact phrasing —of a tiny insect's wing affecting the entire atmosphere's winds— was published in a children's book which became extremely successful and well-known globally in 1962, the year before Lorenz published: "...whatever we do affects everything and everyone else, if even in the tiniest way. Why, when a housefly flaps his wings, a breeze goes round the world."

\-- The Princess of Pure Reason

In 1961, Lorenz was running a numerical computer model to redo a weather prediction from the middle of the previous run as a shortcut. He entered the initial condition 0.506 from the printout instead of entering the full precision 0.506127 value. The result was a completely different weather scenario.[^o11]

Lorenz wrote:

At one point I decided to repeat some of the computations in order to examine what was happening in greater detail. I stopped the computer, typed in a line of numbers that it had printed out a while earlier, and set it running again. I went down the hall for a cup of coffee and returned after about an hour, during which time the computer had simulated about two months of weather. The numbers being printed were nothing like the old ones. I immediately suspected a weak [vacuum tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube) or some other computer trouble, which was not uncommon, but before calling for service I decided to see just where the mistake had occurred, knowing that this could speed up the servicing process. Instead of a sudden break, I found that the new values at first repeated the old ones, but soon afterward differed by one and then several units in the last \[decimal\] place, and then began to differ in the next to the last place and then in the place before that. In fact, the differences more or less steadily doubled in size every four days or so, until all resemblance with the original output disappeared somewhere in the second month. This was enough to tell me what had happened: the numbers that I had typed in were not the exact original numbers, but were the rounded-off values that had appeared in the original printout. The initial round-off errors were the culprits; they were steadily amplifying until they dominated the solution.

In 1963, Lorenz published a theoretical study of this effect in a highly cited, seminal paper called *Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow*[^o4][^o13] (the calculations were performed on a [Royal McBee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_McBee) [LGP-30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGP-30) computer).[^o14][^o15] Elsewhere he stated:

*Image: Polydamas swallowtail (Battus polydamas polydamas).JPG.* A *[Battus polydamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battus_polydamas)* butterfly in Brazil

Following proposals from colleagues, in later speeches and papers, Lorenz used the more poetic [butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly). According to Lorenz, when he failed to provide a title for a talk he was to present at the 139th meeting of the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science) in 1972, Philip Merilees concocted *Does the flap of a butterfly's wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?* as a title.[^o2] Although a butterfly flapping its wings has remained constant in the expression of this concept, the location of the butterfly, the consequences, and the location of the consequences have varied widely.[^o16]

The phrase refers to the effect of a butterfly's wings creating tiny changes in the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) that may ultimately alter the path of a [tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado) or delay, accelerate, or even prevent the occurrence of a tornado in another location. The butterfly does not power or directly create the tornado, but the term is intended to imply that the flap of the butterfly's wings can *cause* the tornado: in the sense that the flap of the wings is a part of the initial conditions of an interconnected complex web; one set of conditions leads to a tornado, while the other set of conditions doesn't. The flapping wing creates a small change in the initial condition of the system, which cascades to large-scale alterations of events (compare: [domino effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domino_effect)). Had the butterfly not flapped its wings, the [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) of the system might have been vastly different—but it's also equally possible that the set of conditions without the butterfly flapping its wings is the set that leads to a tornado.

The butterfly effect presents an obvious challenge to prediction, since initial conditions for a system such as the weather can never be known to complete accuracy. This problem motivated the development of [ensemble forecasting](wikipedia://en/2451045), in which a number of forecasts are made from perturbed initial conditions.[^o17]

Some scientists have since argued that the weather system is not as sensitive to initial conditions as previously believed.[^o18] [David Orrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Orrell) argues that the major contributor to weather forecast error is model error, with sensitivity to initial conditions playing a relatively small role.[^o19][^o20] [Stephen Wolfram](wikipedia://en/52469) also notes that the [Lorenz equations](wikipedia://en/5642583) are highly simplified and do not contain terms that represent viscous effects; he believes that these terms would tend to damp out small perturbations.[^o21] Recent studies using generalized [Lorenz models](wikipedia://en/5642583) that included additional dissipative terms and nonlinearity suggested that a larger heating parameter is required for the onset of chaos.[^o22]

While the "butterfly effect" is often explained as being synonymous with sensitive dependence on initial conditions of the kind described by Lorenz in his 1963 paper (and previously observed by Poincaré), the butterfly metaphor was originally applied[^o2] to work he published in 1969[^o23] which took the idea a step further. Lorenz proposed a mathematical model for how tiny motions in the atmosphere scale up to affect larger systems. He found that the systems in that model could only be predicted up to a specific point in the future, and beyond that, reducing the error in the initial conditions would not increase the predictability (as long as the error is not zero). This demonstrated that a deterministic system could be "observationally indistinguishable" from a non-deterministic one in terms of predictability. Recent re-examinations of this paper suggest that it offered a significant challenge to the idea that our universe is deterministic, comparable to the challenges offered by quantum physics.[^o24][^o25]

In the book entitled *The Essence of Chaos* published in 1993,[^o29] Lorenz defined butterfly effect as: "The phenomenon that a small alteration in the state of a dynamical system will cause subsequent states to differ greatly from the states that would have followed without the alteration." This feature is the same as sensitive dependence of solutions on initial conditions (SDIC).[^o4] In the same book, Lorenz applied the activity of skiing and developed an idealized skiing model for revealing the sensitivity of time-varying paths to initial positions. A predictability horizon is determined before the onset of SDIC.[^o26]

## Theory and mathematical definition {#Theory_and_mathematical_definition}

*Image: Lorenz attractor yb.svg.* A plot of Lorenz' [strange attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractor) for values ρ=28, σ = 10, β = 8/3. The butterfly effect or sensitive dependence on initial conditions is the property of a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) that, starting from any of various arbitrarily close alternative [initial conditions](wikipedia://en/1186804) on the attractor, the [iterated points](wikipedia://en/68833#Mathematics) will become arbitrarily spread out from each other.

[Recurrence](wikipedia://en/2404544), the approximate return of a system toward its initial conditions, together with sensitive dependence on initial conditions, are the two main ingredients for chaotic motion. They have the practical consequence of making [complex systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_system), such as the [weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather), difficult to predict past a certain time range (approximately a week in the case of weather) since it is impossible to measure the starting atmospheric conditions completely accurately.

A [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) displays sensitive dependence on initial conditions if points arbitrarily close together separate over time at an exponential rate. The definition is not topological, but essentially metrical. Lorenz[^o29] defined sensitive dependence as follows:

*The property characterizing an orbit (i.e., a solution) if most other orbits that pass close to it at some point do not remain close to it as time advances.*

If *M* is the [state space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space_%28dynamical_system%29) for the map $f^t$, then $f^t$ displays sensitive dependence to initial conditions if for any x in *M* and any δ > 0, there are y in *M*, with distance $d(. , .)$ such that $0 < d(x, y) < \delta$ and such that

$$d(f^\tau(x), f^\tau(y)) > \mathrm{e}^{a\tau} \, d(x,y)$$

for some positive parameter *a*. The definition does not require that all points from a neighborhood separate from the base point *x*, but it requires one positive [Lyapunov exponent](wikipedia://en/166441). In addition to a positive Lyapunov exponent, boundedness is another major feature within chaotic systems.[^o30]

The simplest mathematical framework exhibiting sensitive dependence on initial conditions is provided by a particular parametrization of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137):

$$x_{n+1} = 4 x_n (1-x_n) , \quad 0\leq x_0\leq 1,$$

which, unlike most chaotic maps, has a [closed-form solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-form_solution):

$$x_{n}=\sin^{2}(2^{n} \theta \pi)$$

where the [initial condition](wikipedia://en/1186804) parameter $\theta$ is given by $\theta = \tfrac{1}{\pi}\sin^{-1}(x_0^{1/2})$. For rational $\theta$, after a finite number of [iterations](wikipedia://en/1448702) $x_n$ maps into a [periodic sequence](wikipedia://en/4941621). But [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) $\theta$ are irrational, and, for irrational $\theta$, $x_n$ never repeats itself – it is non-periodic. This solution equation clearly demonstrates the two key features of chaos – stretching and folding: the factor 2<sup>*n*</sup> shows the exponential growth of stretching, which results in sensitive dependence on initial conditions (the butterfly effect), while the squared sine function keeps $x_n$ folded within the range \[0, 1\].

## In physical systems {#In_physical_systems}

### In weather {#In_weather}

#### Overview {#Overview}

The butterfly effect is most familiar in terms of weather; it can easily be demonstrated in standard weather prediction models, for example. The climate scientists James Annan and William Connolley explain that chaos is important in the development of weather prediction methods; models are sensitive to initial conditions. They add the caveat: "Of course the existence of an unknown butterfly flapping its wings has no direct bearing on weather forecasts, since it will take far too long for such a small perturbation to grow to a significant size, and we have many more immediate uncertainties to worry about. So the direct impact of this phenomenon on weather prediction is often somewhat wrong."[^o34]

#### Differentiating types of butterfly effects {#Differentiating_types_of_butterfly_effects}

The concept of the butterfly effect encompasses several phenomena. The two kinds of butterfly effects, including the sensitive dependence on initial conditions,[^o4] and the ability of a tiny perturbation to create an organized circulation at large distances,[^o2] are not exactly the same.[^o36] In Palmer et al.,[^o24] a new type of butterfly effect is introduced, highlighting the potential impact of small-scale processes on finite predictability within the Lorenz 1969 model. Additionally, the identification of ill-conditioned aspects of the Lorenz 1969 model points to a practical form of finite predictability.[^o26] These two distinct mechanisms suggesting finite predictability in the Lorenz 1969 model are collectively referred to as the third kind of butterfly effect.[^o38] The authors in have considered Palmer et al.'s suggestions and have aimed to present their perspective without raising specific contentions.[^o38]

The third kind of butterfly effect with finite predictability was primarily proposed based on a convergent geometric series, known as Lorenz's and Lilly's formulas.[^o24] Ongoing discussions are addressing the validity of these two formulas for estimating predictability limits.[^o37]

A comparison of the two kinds of butterfly effects[^o2][^o4] and the third kind of butterfly effect[^o23][^o24][^o25] has been documented.[^o38] In recent studies,[^o26][^o39] it was reported that both meteorological and non-meteorological linear models have shown that instability plays a role in producing a butterfly effect, which is characterized by brief but significant exponential growth resulting from a small disturbance.

#### Recent debates on butterfly effects {#Recent_debates_on_butterfly_effects}

The first kind of butterfly effect (BE1), known as SDIC (Sensitive Dependence on Initial Conditions), is widely recognized and demonstrated through idealized chaotic models. However, opinions differ regarding the second kind of butterfly effect, specifically the impact of a butterfly flapping its wings on tornado formation, as indicated in two 2024 articles.[^o41][^o42] In more recent discussions published by *Physics Today*,[^o43][^o44] it is acknowledged that the second kind of butterfly effect (BE2) has never been rigorously verified using a realistic weather model. While the studies suggest that BE2 is unlikely in the real atmosphere,[^o41][^o43] its invalidity in this context does not negate the applicability of BE1 in other areas, such as pandemics or historical events.[^o45]

For the third kind of butterfly effect, the limited predictability within the Lorenz 1969 model is explained by scale interactions in one article[^o24] and by system ill-conditioning in another more recent study.[^o26]

#### Finite predictability in chaotic systems {#Finite_predictability_in_chaotic_systems}

According to Lighthill (1986),[^o47] the presence of SDIC (commonly known as the butterfly effect) implies that chaotic systems have a finite predictability limit. In a literature review,[^o48] it was found that Lorenz's perspective on the predictability limit can be condensed into the following statement:

- (A). The Lorenz 1963 model qualitatively revealed the essence of a finite predictability within a chaotic system such as the atmosphere. However, it did not determine a precise limit for the predictability of the atmosphere.
- (B). In the 1960s, the two-week predictability limit was originally estimated based on a doubling time of five days in real-world models. Since then, this finding has been documented in Charney et al. (1966)[^o49][^o50] and has become a consensus.

Recently, a short video has been created to present Lorenz's perspective on predictability limit.[^o51]

A recent study refers to the two-week predictability limit, initially calculated in the 1960s with the Mintz-Arakawa model's five-day doubling time, as the "Predictability Limit Hypothesis."[^o52] Inspired by Moore's Law, this term acknowledges the collaborative contributions of Lorenz, Mintz, and Arakawa under Charney's leadership. The hypothesis supports the investigation into extended-range predictions using both partial differential equation (PDE)-based physics methods and Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques.

### In quantum mechanics {#In_quantum_mechanics}

The potential for sensitive dependence on initial conditions (the butterfly effect) has been studied in a number of cases in [semiclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiclassical_physics) and [quantum physics](wikipedia://en/25202), including atoms in strong fields and the anisotropic [Kepler problem](wikipedia://en/2410571).[^o54][^o55] Some authors have argued that extreme (exponential) dependence on initial conditions is not expected in pure quantum treatments;[^o56][^o57] however, the sensitive dependence on initial conditions demonstrated in classical motion is included in the semiclassical treatments developed by [Martin Gutzwiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Gutzwiller)[^o58] and John B. Delos and co-workers.[^o59] The random matrix theory and simulations with quantum computers prove that some versions of the butterfly effect in quantum mechanics do not exist.[^o60]

Other authors suggest that the butterfly effect can be observed in quantum systems. Zbyszek P. Karkuszewski et al. consider the time evolution of quantum systems which have slightly different [Hamiltonians](wikipedia://en/14381). They investigate the level of sensitivity of quantum systems to small changes in their given Hamiltonians.[^o61] David Poulin et al. presented a quantum algorithm to measure fidelity decay, which "measures the rate at which identical initial states diverge when subjected to slightly different dynamics". They consider fidelity decay to be "the closest quantum analog to the (purely classical) butterfly effect".[^o62] Whereas the classical butterfly effect considers the effect of a small change in the position and/or velocity of an object in a given [Hamiltonian system](wikipedia://en/1197531), the quantum butterfly effect considers the effect of a small change in the Hamiltonian system with a given initial position and velocity.[^o63][^o64] This quantum butterfly effect has been demonstrated experimentally.[^o65] Quantum and semiclassical treatments of system sensitivity to initial conditions are known as [quantum chaos](wikipedia://en/313779).[^o56][^o63]

The butterfly effect has appeared across media such as literature (for instance, *[A Sound of Thunder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Sound_of_Thunder)*), films and television (such as *[The Simpsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Simpsons)*), video games (such as *[Life Is Strange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_Is_Strange)*), webcomics (such as *[Homestuck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homestuck)*), musical references (such as "Butterfly Effect" by [Travis Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travis_Scott)), AI-driven expansive language models, and more.
