# Bayesian probability

Bayesian statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bayesian probability"}

**Bayesian probability** ( or ) is an [interpretation of the concept of probability](wikipedia://en/23538), in which, instead of [frequency](wikipedia://en/10869) or [propensity](wikipedia://en/11351089) of some phenomenon, probability is interpreted as reasonable expectation[^o2] representing a state of knowledge[^o3] or as quantification of a personal belief.[^o4]

:::

The Bayesian interpretation of probability can be seen as an extension of [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) that enables reasoning with [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis);[^o5][^o6] that is, with propositions whose [truth or falsity](wikipedia://en/161711) is unknown. In the Bayesian view, a probability is assigned to a hypothesis, whereas under [frequentist inference](wikipedia://en/15537745), a hypothesis is typically tested without being assigned a probability.

Bayesian probability belongs to the category of evidential probabilities; to evaluate the probability of a hypothesis, the Bayesian probabilist specifies a [prior probability](wikipedia://en/472877). This, in turn, is then updated to a [posterior probability](wikipedia://en/357672) in the light of new, relevant [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data) (evidence).[^o7] The Bayesian interpretation provides a standard set of procedures and formulae to perform this calculation.

The term *Bayesian* derives from the 18th-century English mathematician and theologian [Thomas Bayes](wikipedia://en/149117), who provided the first mathematical treatment of a non-trivial problem of statistical [data analysis](wikipedia://en/2720954) using what is now known as [Bayesian inference](wikipedia://en/49571).[^o8] Mathematician [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783) pioneered and popularized what is now called Bayesian probability.[^o8]

## Bayesian methodology {#Bayesian_methodology}

Bayesian methods are characterized by concepts and procedures as follows:

- The use of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), or more generally unknown quantities,[^o29] to model all sources of [uncertainty](wikipedia://en/63778) in statistical models including uncertainty resulting from lack of information (see also [aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty](wikipedia://en/5987648#Aleatoric_and_epistemic_uncertainty)).
- The need to determine the prior [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) taking into account the available (prior) information.
- The sequential use of [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569): as more data become available, calculate the posterior distribution using Bayes' theorem; subsequently, the posterior distribution becomes the next prior.
- While for the frequentist, a [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) is a [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094#Treatment_in_logic) (which must be [either true or false](wikipedia://en/24097)) so that the frequentist probability of a hypothesis being correct is either 0 or 1, in Bayesian statistics, the probability that can be assigned to a hypothesis can also be in a range from 0 to 1 if the truth value is uncertain.

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Objective Bayesian probability"}

## Objective and subjective Bayesian probabilities {#Objective_and_subjective_Bayesian_probabilities}

Broadly speaking, there are two interpretations of Bayesian probability. For objectivists, who interpret probability as an extension of [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), *probability* quantifies the reasonable expectation that everyone (even a "robot") who shares the same knowledge should share in accordance with the rules of Bayesian statistics, which can be justified by [Cox's theorem](wikipedia://en/49089).[^o3][^o12] For subjectivists, *probability* corresponds to a personal belief.[^o4] Rationality and coherence allow for substantial variation within the constraints they pose; the constraints are justified by the [Dutch book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_book) argument or by [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216) and [de Finetti's theorem](wikipedia://en/180835).[^o4] The objective and subjective variants of Bayesian probability differ mainly in their interpretation and construction of the prior probability.

:::

## History {#History}

The term *Bayesian* derives from [Thomas Bayes](wikipedia://en/149117) (1702–1761), who proved a special case of what is now called [Bayes' theorem](wikipedia://en/49569) in a paper titled "[An Essay Towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances](wikipedia://en/33200324)".[^o15] In that special case, the prior and posterior distributions were [beta distributions](wikipedia://en/207074) and the data came from [Bernoulli trials](wikipedia://en/102653). It was [Pierre-Simon Laplace](wikipedia://en/344783) (1749–1827) who introduced a general version of the theorem and used it to approach problems in [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182), medical statistics, [reliability](wikipedia://en/239138), and [jurisprudence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisprudence).[^o16] Early Bayesian inference, which used uniform priors following Laplace's [principle of insufficient reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_insufficient_reason), was called "[inverse probability](wikipedia://en/908703)" (because it [infers](wikipedia://en/393736) backwards from observations to parameters, or from effects to causes).[^o17] After the 1920s, "inverse probability" was largely supplanted by a collection of methods that came to be called [frequentist statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequentist_statistics).[^o17]

In the 20th century, the ideas of Laplace developed in two directions, giving rise to *objective* and *subjective* currents in Bayesian practice. [Harold Jeffreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jeffreys)' *Theory of Probability* (first published in 1939) played an important role in the revival of the Bayesian view of probability, followed by works by [Abraham Wald](wikipedia://en/1239430) (1950) and [Leonard J. Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_J._Savage) (1954). The adjective *Bayesian* itself dates to the 1950s; the derived *Bayesianism*, *neo-Bayesianism* is of 1960s coinage.[^o18][^o19][^o20] In the objectivist stream, the statistical analysis depends on only the model assumed and the data analysed.[^o21] No subjective decisions need to be involved. In contrast, "subjectivist" statisticians deny the possibility of fully objective analysis for the general case.

In the 1980s, there was a dramatic growth in research and applications of Bayesian methods, mostly attributed to the discovery of [Markov chain Monte Carlo](wikipedia://en/236801) methods and the consequent removal of many of the computational problems, and to an increasing interest in nonstandard, complex applications.[^o22] While frequentist statistics remains strong (as demonstrated by the fact that much of undergraduate teaching is based on it [^o23]), Bayesian methods are widely accepted and used, e.g., in the field of [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488).[^o24]

:::

## Justification {#Justification}

The use of Bayesian probabilities as the basis of [Bayesian inference](wikipedia://en/49571) has been supported by several arguments, such as [Cox axioms](wikipedia://en/49089), the [Dutch book argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_book), arguments based on [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216) and [de Finetti's theorem](wikipedia://en/180835).

### Axiomatic approach {#Axiomatic_approach}

[Richard T. Cox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Threlkeld_Cox) showed that Bayesian updating follows from several axioms, including two [functional equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_equations) and a hypothesis of differentiability.[^o12][^o27] The assumption of differentiability or even continuity is controversial; Halpern found a counterexample based on his observation that the Boolean algebra of statements may be finite.[^o28] Other axiomatizations have been suggested by various authors with the purpose of making the theory more rigorous.[^o29]

### Dutch book approach {#Dutch_book_approach}

[Bruno de Finetti](wikipedia://en/180825) proposed the Dutch book argument based on betting. A clever [bookmaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bookmaker) makes a [Dutch book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_book) by setting the [odds](wikipedia://en/172069) and bets to ensure that the bookmaker profits—at the expense of the gamblers—regardless of the outcome of the event (a horse race, for example) on which the gamblers bet. It is associated with [probabilities](wikipedia://en/22934) implied by the odds not being [coherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_%28philosophical_gambling_strategy%29).

However, [Ian Hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Hacking) noted that traditional Dutch book arguments did not specify Bayesian updating: they left open the possibility that non-Bayesian updating rules could avoid Dutch books. For example, [Hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Hacking) writes[^o31][^o32] "And neither the Dutch book argument, nor any other in the personalist arsenal of proofs of the probability axioms, entails the dynamic assumption. Not one entails Bayesianism. So the personalist requires the dynamic assumption to be Bayesian. It is true that in consistency a personalist could abandon the Bayesian model of learning from experience. Salt could lose its savour."

In fact, there are non-Bayesian updating rules that also avoid Dutch books (as discussed in the literature on "[probability kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_kinematics)"[^o33] following the publication of [Richard C. Jeffrey](wikipedia://en/4294354)'s rule, which is itself regarded as Bayesian[^o34]). The additional hypotheses sufficient to (uniquely) specify Bayesian updating are substantial[^o35] and not universally seen as satisfactory.[^o36]

### Decision theory approach {#Decision_theory_approach}

A [decision-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_decision_theory) justification of the use of Bayesian inference (and hence of Bayesian probabilities) was given by [Abraham Wald](wikipedia://en/1239430), who proved that every [admissible](wikipedia://en/1776726) statistical procedure is either a Bayesian procedure or a limit of Bayesian procedures.[^o38] Conversely, every Bayesian procedure is [admissible](wikipedia://en/1776726).[^o39]

## Personal probabilities and objective methods for constructing priors {#Personal_probabilities_and_objective_methods_for_constructing_priors}

Following the work on [expected utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility) [theory](wikipedia://en/16414027) of [Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) and [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), decision-theorists have accounted for [rational behavior](wikipedia://en/16414027) using a probability distribution for the [agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent-based_model). [Johann Pfanzagl](wikipedia://en/72137364) completed the *[Theory of Games and Economic Behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_Games_and_Economic_Behavior)* by providing an axiomatization of **subjective probability** and utility, a task left uncompleted by von Neumann and [Oskar Morgenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Morgenstern): their original theory supposed that all the agents had the same probability distribution, as a convenience.[^o42] Pfanzagl's axiomatization was endorsed by Oskar Morgenstern: "Von Neumann and I have anticipated ... \[the question whether probabilities\] might, perhaps more typically, be subjective and have stated specifically that in the latter case axioms could be found from which could derive the desired numerical utility together with a number for the probabilities (cf. p. 19 of The Theory of Games and Economic Behavior). We did not carry this out; it was demonstrated by Pfanzagl ... with all the necessary rigor".[^o43]

Ramsey and [Savage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Jimmie_Savage) noted that the individual agent's probability distribution could be objectively studied in experiments. Procedures for [testing hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing) about probabilities (using finite samples) are due to [Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285) (1931) and [de Finetti](wikipedia://en/180825) (1931, 1937, 1964, 1970). Both [Bruno de Finetti](wikipedia://en/180825)[^o44][^o45] and [Frank P. Ramsey](wikipedia://en/238285)[^o45][^o46] acknowledge their debts to [pragmatic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatic_philosophy), particularly (for Ramsey) to [Charles S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117).[^o45][^o46]

The "Ramsey test" for evaluating probability distributions is implementable in theory, and has kept experimental psychologists occupied for a half century.[^o47] This work demonstrates that Bayesian-probability propositions can be [falsified](wikipedia://en/11283), and so meet an empirical criterion of [Charles S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117), whose work inspired Ramsey. (This [falsifiability](wikipedia://en/11283)-criterion was popularized by [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper).[^o48][^o49])

Modern work on the experimental evaluation of personal probabilities uses the randomization, [blinding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_blind), and Boolean-decision procedures of the Peirce-Jastrow experiment.[^o50] Since individuals act according to different probability judgments, these agents' probabilities are "personal" (but amenable to objective study).

Personal probabilities are problematic for science and for some applications where decision-makers lack the knowledge or time to specify an informed probability-distribution (on which they are prepared to act). To meet the needs of science and of human limitations, Bayesian statisticians have developed "objective" methods for specifying prior probabilities.

Indeed, some Bayesians have argued the prior state of knowledge defines *the* (unique) prior probability-distribution for "regular" statistical problems; cf. [well-posed problems](wikipedia://en/176673). Finding the right method for constructing such "objective" priors (for appropriate classes of regular problems) has been the quest of statistical theorists from Laplace to [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes), [Harold Jeffreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jeffreys), and [Edwin Thompson Jaynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Thompson_Jaynes). These theorists and their successors have suggested several methods for constructing "objective" priors (Unfortunately, it is not always clear how to assess the relative "objectivity" of the priors proposed under these methods):

- [Maximum entropy](wikipedia://en/201718)
- [Transformation group analysis](wikipedia://en/40645)
- [Reference analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José-Miguel_Bernardo)

Each of these methods contributes useful priors for "regular" one-parameter problems, and each prior can handle some challenging [statistical models](wikipedia://en/27576) (with "irregularity" or several parameters). Each of these methods has been useful in Bayesian practice. Indeed, methods for constructing "objective" (alternatively, "default" or "ignorance") priors have been developed by avowed subjective (or "personal") Bayesians like [James Berger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Berger_%28statistician%29) ([Duke University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_University)) and [José-Miguel Bernardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José-Miguel_Bernardo) ([Universitat de València](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Valencia)), simply because such priors are needed for Bayesian practice, particularly in science.[^o51] The quest for "the universal method for constructing priors" continues to attract statistical theorists.[^o51]

Thus, the Bayesian statistician needs either to use informed priors (using relevant expertise or previous data) or to choose among the competing methods for constructing "objective" priors.
