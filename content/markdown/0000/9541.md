# Design of experiments

*Image: Response surface metodology.jpg.* Design of experiments with full [factorial design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorial_design) (left), [response surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Response_surface) with second-degree polynomial (right)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Design of experiments"}

The **design of experiments** (**DOE**),[^o2] also known as **experimental design**, refers to the construction of [procedures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experiments) that attempt to explain how changes in one aspect of a system will lead to changes in other aspects of a system. In general, the design of experiments involves decisions about which aspects of the system to change and which to [control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_variable) based on [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) about the sources of variance in the aspects of the system considered by the experimenter.

:::

DOE is generally associated with experiments where the design introduces conditions that directly affect the variation, but DOE may also refer to the design of [quasi-experiments](wikipedia://en/11864322), in which [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalistic_observation) conditions that influence the variation are selected for observation.

In its simplest form, an experiment aims at predicting the outcome by introducing a change of the preconditions, which is represented by one or more [independent variables](wikipedia://en/437701), also referred to as "input variables" or "predictor variables." The change in one or more independent variables is generally hypothesized to result in a change in one or more [dependent variables](wikipedia://en/437701), also referred to as "output variables" or "response variables." The experimental design may also identify control variables that must be held constant to prevent external factors from affecting the results. Experimental design involves not only the selection of suitable independent, dependent, and control variables, but planning the delivery of the experiment under statistically optimal conditions given the constraints of available resources. There are multiple approaches for determining the set of design points (unique combinations of the settings of the independent variables) to be used in the experiment.

Main concerns in experimental design include the establishment of [validity](wikipedia://en/239140), [reliability](wikipedia://en/239138), and [replicability](wikipedia://en/47651). For example, these concerns can be partially addressed by carefully choosing the independent variable, reducing the risk of measurement error, and ensuring that the documentation of the method is sufficiently detailed. Related concerns include achieving appropriate levels of [statistical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power) and [sensitivity](wikipedia://en/5599330).

Correctly designed experiments advance knowledge in the natural and social sciences and engineering, with design of experiments methodology recognised as a key tool in the successful implementation of a [Quality by Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_by_design) (QbD) framework.[^o3] Other applications include marketing and policy making. The study of the design of experiments is an important topic in [metascience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metascience).

## History {#History}

### Statistical experiments, following Charles S. Peirce {#Statistical_experiments,_following_Charles_S._Peirce}

A theory of [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577) was developed by [Charles S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) in "[Illustrations of the Logic of Science](wikipedia://en/3967710#illus)" (1877–1878)[^o6] and "[A Theory of Probable Inference](wikipedia://en/3967710#SIL)" (1883),[^o7] two publications that emphasized the importance of randomization-based inference in statistics.[^o8]

#### Randomized experiments {#Randomized_experiments}

Charles S. Peirce randomly assigned volunteers to a [blinded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blinding_%28medicine%29), [repeated-measures design](wikipedia://en/15943873) to evaluate their ability to discriminate weights.[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13] Peirce's experiment inspired other researchers in psychology and education, which developed a research tradition of randomized experiments in laboratories and specialized textbooks in the 1800s.[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13]

#### Optimal designs for regression models {#Optimal_designs_for_regression_models}

[Charles S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) also contributed the first English-language publication on an [optimal design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_design) for [regression](wikipedia://en/826997) [models](wikipedia://en/27576) in 1876.[^o15] A pioneering [optimal design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_design) for [polynomial regression](wikipedia://en/21893202) was suggested by [Gergonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Diaz_Gergonne) in 1815. In 1918, [Kirstine Smith](wikipedia://en/40337435) published optimal designs for polynomials of degree six (and less).[^o16][^o17]

### Sequences of experiments {#Sequences_of_experiments}

The use of a sequence of experiments, where the design of each may depend on the results of previous experiments, including the possible decision to stop experimenting, is within the scope of [sequential analysis](wikipedia://en/3509530), a field that was pioneered[^o19] by [Abraham Wald](wikipedia://en/1239430) in the context of sequential tests of statistical hypotheses.[^o20] [Herman Chernoff](wikipedia://en/1181131) wrote an overview of optimal sequential designs,[^o34] while [adaptive designs](wikipedia://en/65297106) have been surveyed by S. Zacks.[^o21] One specific type of sequential design is the "two-armed bandit", generalized to the [multi-armed bandit](wikipedia://en/2854828), on which early work was done by [Herbert Robbins](wikipedia://en/745193) in 1952.[^o22]

## Fisher's principles {#Fisher's_principles}

A methodology for designing experiments was proposed by [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824), in his innovative books: *The Arrangement of Field Experiments* (1926) and *[The Design of Experiments](wikipedia://en/17229561)* (1935). Much of his pioneering work dealt with agricultural applications of statistical methods. As a mundane example, he described how to test the [lady tasting tea](wikipedia://en/26297705) [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis), that a certain lady could distinguish by flavour alone whether the milk or the tea was first placed in the cup. These methods have been broadly adapted in biological, psychological, and agricultural research.[^o25]

**Comparison**

In some fields of study it is not possible to have independent measurements to a traceable [metrology standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_%28metrology%29). Comparisons between treatments are much more valuable and are usually preferable, and often compared against a [scientific control](wikipedia://en/1640288) or traditional treatment that acts as baseline.

**[Randomization](wikipedia://en/65462)**

Random assignment is the process of assigning individuals at random to groups or to different groups in an experiment, so that each individual of the population has the same chance of becoming a participant in the study. The random assignment of individuals to groups (or conditions within a group) distinguishes a rigorous, "true" experiment from an observational study or "quasi-experiment".[^o26] There is an extensive body of mathematical theory that explores the consequences of making the allocation of units to treatments by means of some random mechanism (such as tables of random numbers, or the use of randomization devices such as playing cards or dice). Assigning units to treatments at random tends to mitigate [confounding](wikipedia://en/3105999), which makes effects due to factors other than the treatment to appear to result from the treatment. Random assignment does not guarantee that treatment and control groups will be exactly identical. However, it tends to balance known and unknown confounding variables between groups on average. This strengthens causal inference.[^o27]

The risks associated with random allocation (such as having a serious imbalance in a key characteristic between a treatment group and a control group) are calculable and hence can be managed down to an acceptable level by using enough experimental units. However, if the population is divided into several subpopulations that somehow differ, and the research requires each subpopulation to be equal in size, stratified sampling can be used. In that way, the units in each subpopulation are randomized, but not the whole sample. The results of an experiment can be generalized reliably from the experimental units to a larger [statistical population](wikipedia://en/27585) of units only if the experimental units are a [random sample](wikipedia://en/160361) from the larger population; the probable error of such an extrapolation depends on the sample size, among other things.

**[Statistical replication](wikipedia://en/10306262)**

Measurements are usually subject to variation and [measurement uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_uncertainty); thus they are repeated and full experiments are replicated to help identify the sources of variation, to better estimate the true effects of treatments, to further strengthen the experiment's reliability and validity, and to add to the existing knowledge of the topic.[^o28] However, certain conditions must be met before the replication of the experiment is commenced: the original research question has been published in a [peer-reviewed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-review) journal or widely cited, the researcher is independent of the original experiment, the researcher must first try to replicate the original findings using the original data, and the write-up should state that the study conducted is a replication study that tried to follow the original study as strictly as possible.[^o29]

**[Blocking](wikipedia://en/1822339)**

*Image: No block vs block chart.jpg.* Blocking (right)

Blocking is the non-random arrangement of experimental units into groups (blocks) consisting of units that are similar to one another. Blocking reduces known but irrelevant sources of variation between units and thus allows greater precision in the estimation of the source of variation under study.

**[Orthogonality](wikipedia://en/102221#Statistics.2C_econometrics.2C_and_economics)**

*Image: Factorial Design.svg.* Example of orthogonal factorial design

Orthogonality concerns the forms of comparison (contrasts) that can be legitimately and efficiently carried out. Contrasts can be represented by vectors and sets of orthogonal contrasts are uncorrelated and independently distributed if the data are normal. Because of this independence, each orthogonal treatment provides different information to the others. If there are *T* treatments and *T* − 1 orthogonal contrasts, all the information that can be captured from the experiment is obtainable from the set of contrasts.

**Multifactorial experiments**

Use of multifactorial experiments instead of the [one-factor-at-a-time method](wikipedia://en/1759969). These are efficient at evaluating the effects and possible [interactions](wikipedia://en/443083) of several factors (independent variables). Analysis of [experiment](wikipedia://en/59861) design is built on the foundation of the [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634), a collection of models that partition the observed variance into components, according to what factors the experiment must estimate or test.

## Example {#Example}

*Image: Balance à tabac 1850.JPG.*

This example of design experiments is attributed to [Harold Hotelling](wikipedia://en/935621), building on examples from [Frank Yates](wikipedia://en/165733).[^o32][^o33][^o34] The experiments designed in this example involve [combinatorial designs](wikipedia://en/2761968).[^o35]

Weights of eight objects are measured using a [pan balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_balance) and set of standard weights. Each weighing measures the weight difference between objects in the left pan and any objects in the right pan by adding calibrated weights to the lighter pan until the balance is in equilibrium. Each measurement has a [random error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics) $\epsilon$. The average error is zero; the [standard deviations](wikipedia://en/27590) of the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the errors is the same number σ on different weighings; errors on different weighings are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence). Denote the true weights by

$$\mathbf{\theta} = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_8)\,.$$

We consider two different experiments with the same amount of measurements:

Weigh each of the eight objects individually.

$$\begin{array}{lcc} & \text{left pan} & \text{right pan} \\ \hline \text{1st weighing:} & 1\ & \text{(empty)} \\ \text{2st weighing:} & 2\ & \text{(empty)} \\ \text{3rd weighing:} & 3\ & \text{(empty)} \\ ... & ... & ... \end{array}$$

Do the eight weighings according to the following schedule:

$$\begin{array}{lcc} & \text{left pan} & \text{right pan} \\ \hline \text{1st weighing:} & 1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6\ 7\ 8 & \text{(empty)} \\ \text{2nd:} & 1\ 2\ 3\ 8\ & 4\ 5\ 6\ 7 \\ \text{3rd:} & 1\ 4\ 5\ 8\ & 2\ 3\ 6\ 7 \\ \text{4th:} & 1\ 6\ 7\ 8\ & 2\ 3\ 4\ 5 \\ \text{5th:} & 2\ 4\ 6\ 8\ & 1\ 3\ 5\ 7 \\ \text{6th:} & 2\ 5\ 7\ 8\ & 1\ 3\ 4\ 6 \\ \text{7th:} & 3\ 4\ 7\ 8\ & 1\ 2\ 5\ 6 \\ \text{8th:} & 3\ 5\ 6\ 8\ & 1\ 2\ 4\ 7 \end{array}$$

Let *y*<sub>*i*</sub> be the measured difference for *i* = 1, ..., 8. The relationship between the true weights and experimental measurements may be represented with a [general linear model](wikipedia://en/877698), with the [design matrix](wikipedia://en/949189) $W$ having entries from $\{-1, 0, 1\}$:

$$y = W\theta + \epsilon$$

The first design is represented by an [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) while the second design is represented by an 8x8 [Hadamard matrix](wikipedia://en/507553), $H$, both examples of [weighing matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighing_matrices).

The weights are typically estimated using the [method of least squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_least_squares). Using a weighing matrix, this is equivalent to inverting on the measurements:

$$\hat \theta _{A} = I^{-1}y = y$$

$$\hat \theta _{B}= H^{-1}y$$

The question of design of experiments is: *which experiment is better*?

Investigating estimate A vs B for the first weight:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat \theta _ {A,1}) = \operatorname{Var}(y_1) = \sigma^2$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat \theta _ {B,1}) = \operatorname{Var}(\frac{y_1 + y_2 - y_3 - y_4 + y_5 + y_6 - y_7 - y_8}{8}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{8}$$

A similar result follows for the remaining weight estimates. Thus, the second experiment gives us 8 times as much precision for the estimate of a single item, despite costing the same number of resources (number of weightings).

Many problems of the design of experiments involve [combinatorial designs](wikipedia://en/2761968), as in this example and others.[^o35]

:::

## Avoiding false positives {#Avoiding_false_positives}

[False positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_positive) conclusions, often resulting from the [pressure to publish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publish_or_perish) or the author's own [confirmation bias](wikipedia://en/59160), are an inherent hazard in many fields.[^o37]

Use of [double-blind designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-blind) can prevent [biases](wikipedia://en/40786) potentially leading to [false positives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_positives) in the [data collection](wikipedia://en/11501746) phase. When a double-blind design is used, participants are randomly assigned to experimental groups but the researcher is unaware of what participants belong to which group. Therefore, the researcher can not affect the participants' response to the intervention.[^o38]

Experimental designs with undisclosed [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622) are a problem,[^o39] in that they can lead to conscious or unconscious "[p-hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-hacking)": trying multiple things until you get the desired result. It typically involves the manipulation – perhaps unconsciously – of the process of [statistical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_analysis) and the [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622) until they return a figure below the [p\<.05 level](wikipedia://en/554994) of [statistical significance](wikipedia://en/160995).[^o40][^o41]

P-hacking can be prevented by [preregistering](wikipedia://en/59031980) researches, in which researchers have to send their data analysis plan to the journal they wish to publish their paper in before they even start their data collection, so no data manipulation is possible.[^o42][^o43]

Another way to prevent this is taking a double-blind design to the data-analysis phase, making the study triple-blind, where the data are sent to a data-analyst unrelated to the research who scrambles up the data so there is no way to know which participants belong to before they are potentially taken away as outliers.[^o38]

Clear and complete [documentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Documentation) of the experimental [methodology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodology) is also important in order to support [replication of results](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_of_results).[^o44]

## Discussion topics when setting up an experimental design {#Discussion_topics_when_setting_up_an_experimental_design}

An experimental design or randomized [clinical trial](wikipedia://en/241717) requires careful consideration of several factors before actually doing the experiment.[^o46] An experimental design is the laying out of a detailed experimental plan in advance of doing the experiment. Some of the following topics have already been discussed in the principles of experimental design section:

1. How many factors does the design have, and are the levels of these factors fixed or random?
1. Are control conditions needed, and what should they be?
1. Manipulation checks: did the manipulation really work?
1. What are the background variables?
1. What is the sample size? How many units must be collected for the experiment to be generalisable and have enough [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power)?
1. What is the relevance of interactions between factors?
1. What is the influence of delayed effects of substantive factors on outcomes?
1. How do response shifts affect self-report measures?
1. How feasible is repeated administration of the same measurement instruments to the same units at different occasions, with a post-test and follow-up tests?
1. What about using a proxy pretest?
1. Are there [confounding variables](wikipedia://en/3105999)?
1. Should the client/patient, researcher or even the analyst of the data be blind to conditions?
1. What is the feasibility of subsequent application of different conditions to the same units?
1. How many of each control and noise factors should be taken into account?

The independent variable of a study often has many levels or different groups. In a true experiment, researchers can have an experimental group, which is where their intervention testing the hypothesis is implemented, and a control group, which has all the same element as the experimental group, without the interventional element. Thus, when everything else except for one intervention is held constant, researchers can certify with some certainty that this one element is what caused the observed change. In some instances, having a control group is not ethical. This is sometimes solved using two different experimental groups. In some cases, independent variables cannot be manipulated, for example when testing the difference between two groups who have a different disease, or testing the difference between genders (obviously variables that would be hard or unethical to assign participants to). In these cases, a quasi-experimental design may be used.

## Causal attributions {#Causal_attributions}

In the pure experimental design, the independent (predictor) variable is manipulated by the researcher – that is – every participant of the research is chosen randomly from the population, and each participant chosen is assigned randomly to conditions of the independent variable. Only when this is done is it possible to certify with high probability that the reason for the differences in the outcome variables are caused by the different conditions. Therefore, researchers should choose the experimental design over other design types whenever possible. However, the nature of the independent variable does not always allow for manipulation. In those cases, researchers must be aware of not certifying about causal attribution when their design doesn't allow for it. For example, in observational designs, participants are not assigned randomly to conditions, and so if there are differences found in outcome variables between conditions, it is likely that there is something other than the differences between the conditions that causes the differences in outcomes, that is – a third variable. The same goes for studies with correlational design.

## Statistical control {#Statistical_control}

It is best that a process be in reasonable statistical control prior to conducting designed experiments. When this is not possible, proper blocking, replication, and randomization allow for the careful conduct of designed experiments.[^o49] To control for nuisance variables, researchers institute **control checks** as additional measures. Investigators should ensure that uncontrolled influences (e.g., source credibility perception) do not skew the findings of the study. A [manipulation check](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manipulation_checks) is one example of a control check. Manipulation checks allow investigators to isolate the chief variables to strengthen support that these variables are operating as planned.

One of the most important requirements of experimental research designs is the necessity of eliminating the effects of [spurious](wikipedia://en/484778), intervening, and [antecedent variables](wikipedia://en/497464). In the most basic model, cause (X) leads to effect (Y). But there could be a third variable (Z) that influences (Y), and X might not be the true cause at all. Z is said to be a spurious variable and must be controlled for. The same is true for [intervening variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intervening_variable) (a variable in between the supposed cause (X) and the effect (Y)), and anteceding variables (a variable prior to the supposed cause (X) that is the true cause). When a third variable is involved and has not been controlled for, the relation is said to be a [zero order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_order_%28statistics%29) relationship. In most practical applications of experimental research designs there are several causes (X1, X2, X3). In most designs, only one of these causes is manipulated at a time.

## Experimental designs after Fisher {#Experimental_designs_after_Fisher}

Some efficient designs for estimating several main effects were found independently and in near succession by [Raj Chandra Bose](wikipedia://en/1162266) and [K. Kishen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kishen) in 1940 at the [Indian Statistical Institute](wikipedia://en/2238418), but remained little known until the [Plackett–Burman designs](wikipedia://en/7300309) were published in *[Biometrika](wikipedia://en/1734178)* in 1946. About the same time, [C. R. Rao](wikipedia://en/1418009) introduced the concepts of [orthogonal arrays](wikipedia://en/17887653) as experimental designs. This concept played a central role in the development of [Taguchi methods](wikipedia://en/442079) by [Genichi Taguchi](wikipedia://en/440240), which took place during his visit to Indian Statistical Institute in early 1950s. His methods were successfully applied and adopted by Japanese and Indian industries and subsequently were also embraced by US industry albeit with some reservations.

In 1950, [Gertrude Mary Cox](wikipedia://en/2168672) and [William Gemmell Cochran](wikipedia://en/2223833) published the book *Experimental Designs,* which became the major reference work on the design of experiments for statisticians for years afterwards.

Developments of the theory of [linear models](wikipedia://en/17904) have encompassed and surpassed the cases that concerned early writers. Today, the theory rests on advanced topics in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), [algebra](wikipedia://en/17314682) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/2761968).

As with other branches of statistics, experimental design is pursued using both [frequentist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequentist_statistics) and [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/1706860) approaches: In evaluating statistical procedures like experimental designs, [frequentist statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequentist_statistics) studies the [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) while [Bayesian statistics](wikipedia://en/404412) updates a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/4890) on the parameter space.

## Human participant constraints {#Human_participant_constraints}

Laws and ethical considerations preclude some carefully designed experiments with human subjects. Legal constraints are dependent on [jurisdiction](wikipedia://en/447842). Constraints may involve [institutional review boards](wikipedia://en/1983847), [informed consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informed_consent) and [confidentiality](wikipedia://en/353859) affecting both clinical (medical) trials and behavioral and social science experiments.[^o60] In the field of toxicology, for example, experimentation is performed on laboratory *animals* with the goal of defining safe exposure limits for *humans*.[^o61] Balancing the constraints are views from the medical field.[^o62] Regarding the randomization of patients, "... if no one knows which therapy is better, there is no ethical imperative to use one therapy or another." (p 380) Regarding experimental design, "...it is clearly not ethical to place subjects at risk to collect data in a poorly designed study when this situation can be easily avoided...". (p 393)
