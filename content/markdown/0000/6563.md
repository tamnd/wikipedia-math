# Conjunction introduction

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Conjunction introduction"}

**Conjunction introduction.**

If the proposition $P$ is true, and the proposition $Q$ is true, then the logical conjunction of the two propositions $P$ and $Q$ is true.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Conjunction introduction"}

**Conjunction introduction** (often abbreviated simply as **conjunction** and also called **and introduction** or **adjunction**)[^o3][^o4][^o5] is a [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [rule of inference](wikipedia://en/252311) of [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus). The rule makes it possible to introduce a [conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) into a [logical proof](wikipedia://en/1197184). It is the [inference](wikipedia://en/317465) that if the [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) $P$ is true, and the proposition $Q$ is true, then the logical conjunction of the two propositions $P$ and $Q$ is true. For example, if it is true that "it is raining", and it is true that "the cat is inside", then it is true that "it is raining and the cat is inside". The rule can be stated:

:::

$$\frac{P,Q}{\therefore P \land Q}$$

where the rule is that wherever an instance of "$P$" and "$Q$" appear on lines of a proof, a "$P \land Q$" can be placed on a subsequent line.

## Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

The *conjunction introduction* rule may be written in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation:

$$P, Q \vdash P \land Q$$

where $P$ and $Q$ are propositions expressed in some [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102), and $\vdash$ is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) [symbol](wikipedia://en/24028642) meaning that $P \land Q$ is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) if $P$ and $Q$ are each on lines of a proof in some [logical system](wikipedia://en/396102).
