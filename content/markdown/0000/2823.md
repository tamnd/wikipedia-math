# Antiderivative

*Image: Slope Field.png.* The [slope field](wikipedia://en/1866685) of $F(x) = \frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^2}{2} - x + C$, showing three of the infinitely many solutions that can be produced by varying the [arbitrary constant](wikipedia://en/147939) $C$.

Integral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Antiderivative"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), an **antiderivative**, **inverse derivative**, **primitive function**, **primitive integral** or **indefinite integral** of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ is a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) $F$ whose [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is equal to the original function $f$. This can be stated symbolically as $F' = f$.[^o2][^o3] The process of solving for antiderivatives is called **antidifferentiation** (or **indefinite integration**), and its opposite operation is called *differentiation*, which is the process of finding a derivative. Antiderivatives are often denoted by capital [Roman letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_letters) such as $F$ and $G$.

:::

Antiderivatives are related to [definite integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) through the [second fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696): the definite integral of a function over a [closed interval](wikipedia://en/49172) where the function is [Riemann integrable](wikipedia://en/26390) is equal to the difference between the values of an antiderivative evaluated at the endpoints of the interval.

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), antiderivatives arise in the context of [rectilinear motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectilinear_motion) (e.g., in explaining the relationship between [position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_%28physics%29), [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_%28physics%29) and [acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration_%28physics%29)).[^o4] The [discrete](wikipedia://en/8492) equivalent of the notion of antiderivative is [antidifference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antidifference).

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The function $F(x) = \tfrac{x^3}{3}$ is an antiderivative of $f(x) = x^2$, since the derivative of $\tfrac{x^3}{3}$ is $x^2$. Since the derivative of a [constant](wikipedia://en/320861) is [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29), $x^2$ will have an [infinite](wikipedia://en/142679) number of antiderivatives, such as $\tfrac{x^3}{3}, \tfrac{x^3}{3}+1, \tfrac{x^3}{3}-2$, etc. Thus, all the antiderivatives of $x^2$ can be obtained by changing the value of $C$ in $F(x) = \tfrac{x^3}{3}+C$, where $C$ is an arbitrary constant known as the [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939). The [graphs](wikipedia://en/87352) of antiderivatives of a given function are [vertical translations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_translation) of each other, with each graph's vertical location depending upon the [value](wikipedia://en/4095147) $C$.

More generally, the [power function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_function) $f(x) = x^n$ has antiderivative $F(x) = \tfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$ if $n \ne -1$, and $F(x) = \ln |x| + C$ if $n = -1$.

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the integration of [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) yields [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903) plus a constant. The constant is the initial velocity term that would be lost upon taking the derivative of velocity, because the derivative of a constant term is zero. This same pattern applies to further integrations and derivatives of motion (position, velocity, acceleration, and so on).[^o4] Thus, integration produces the relations of acceleration, velocity and [displacement](wikipedia://en/859275):

$$\begin{aligned} \int a \, dt &= v + v_0 \\ \int v \, dt &= s + s_0 \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Uses and properties {#Uses_and_properties}

Antiderivatives can be used to [compute definite integrals](wikipedia://en/15532#Calculating_integrals), using the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696): if $F$ is an antiderivative of the [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) $f$ over the interval $[a,b]$, then:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).$$

Because of this, each of the infinitely many antiderivatives of a given function $f$ may be called the "indefinite integral" of *f* and written using the integral symbol with no bounds:

$$\int f(x)\,dx.$$

If $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$, and the function $f$ is defined on some interval, then every other antiderivative $G$ of $f$ differs from $F$ by a constant: there exists a number $c$ such that $G(x) = F(x)+c$ for all $x$. $c$ is called the [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939). If the domain of $F$ is a [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of two or more (open) intervals, then a different constant of integration may be chosen for each of the intervals. For instance

$$F(x) = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{1}{x} + c_1 & x<0 \\[1ex] -\dfrac{1}{x} + c_2 & x>0 \end{cases}$$

is the most general antiderivative of $f(x)=1/x^2$ on its natural domain $(-\infty,0) \cup (0,\infty).$

Every [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) $f$ has an antiderivative, and one antiderivative $F$ is given by the definite integral of $f$ with variable upper boundary:

$$F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt ~,$$

for any $a$ in the domain of $f$. Varying the lower boundary produces other antiderivatives, but not necessarily all possible antiderivatives. This is another formulation of the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696).

There are many [elementary functions](wikipedia://en/10412) whose antiderivatives, even though they exist, cannot be expressed in terms of elementary functions. Elementary functions are [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), [exponential functions](wikipedia://en/9678), [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860), [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367), [inverse trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/374220) and their combinations under composition and [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632). Examples of these [nonelementary integrals](wikipedia://en/1520379) are

- the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111) $$\int e^{-x^2}\,dx,$$
- the [Fresnel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresnel_function) $$\int \sin x^2\,dx,$$
- the [sine integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_integral) $$\int \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx,$$
- the [logarithmic integral function](wikipedia://en/47137) $$\int\frac{1}{\log x}\,dx,$$ and
- [sophomore's dream](wikipedia://en/9353592) $$\int x^{x}\,dx.$$

For a more detailed discussion, see also [Differential Galois theory](wikipedia://en/833506).

## Techniques of integration {#Techniques_of_integration}

Finding antiderivatives of elementary functions is often considerably harder than finding their derivatives (indeed, there is no pre-defined method for computing indefinite integrals).[^o9] For some elementary functions, it is impossible to find an antiderivative in terms of other elementary functions. To learn more, see [elementary functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_function_%28differential_algebra%29) and [nonelementary integral](wikipedia://en/1520379).

There exist many properties and techniques for finding antiderivatives. These include, among others:

- The [linearity of integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearity_of_integration) (which breaks complicated integrals into simpler ones)
- [Integration by substitution](wikipedia://en/193748), often combined with [trigonometric identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identities) or the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476)
- The [inverse chain rule method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_chain_rule_method) (a special case of integration by substitution)
- [Integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) (to integrate products of functions)
- [Inverse function integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_function_integration) (a formula that expresses the antiderivative of the inverse $f^{-1}$ of an invertible and continuous function $f$, in terms of $f^{-1}$ and the antiderivative of $f$).
- The method of [partial fractions in integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_fractions_in_integration) (which allows us to integrate all [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210)—fractions of two polynomials)
- The [Risch algorithm](wikipedia://en/289450)
- Additional techniques for multiple integrations (see for instance [double integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_integral), [polar coordinates](wikipedia://en/25120), the [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) and the [Stokes' theorem](wikipedia://en/25441497))
- [Numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) (a technique for approximating a definite integral when no elementary antiderivative exists, as in the case of $\exp(-x^{2})$)
- Algebraic manipulation of integrand (so that other integration techniques, such as integration by substitution, may be used)
- [Cauchy formula for repeated integration](wikipedia://en/3169300) (to calculate the $n$-times antiderivative of a function) $$\int_{x_0}^x \int_{x_0}^{x_1} \cdots \int_{x_0}^{x_{n-1}} f(x_n) \,dx_n \cdots \, dx_2\, dx_1 = \int_{x_0}^x f(t) \frac{(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\,dt.$$

[Computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) can be used to automate some or all of the work involved in the symbolic techniques above, which is particularly useful when the algebraic manipulations involved are very complex or lengthy. Integrals which have already been derived can be looked up in a [table of integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_integrals).

## Of non-continuous functions {#Of_non-continuous_functions}

Non-continuous functions can have antiderivatives. While there are still open questions in this area, it is known that:

- Some highly [pathological functions](wikipedia://en/372742) with large sets of discontinuities may nevertheless have antiderivatives.
- In some cases, the antiderivatives of such pathological functions may be found by [Riemann integration](wikipedia://en/26390), while in other cases these functions are not Riemann integrable.

Assuming that the domains of the functions are open intervals:

- A necessary, but not sufficient, condition for a function $f$ to have an antiderivative is that $f$ have the [intermediate value property](wikipedia://en/14884). That is, if $[a, b]$ is a subinterval of the domain of $f$ and $y$ is any real number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists a $c$ between $a$ and $b$ such that $f(c) = y$. This is a consequence of [Darboux's theorem](wikipedia://en/4130888).
- The set of discontinuities of $f$ must be a [meagre set](wikipedia://en/226975). This set must also be an [F-sigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-sigma) set (since the set of discontinuities of any function must be of this type). Moreover, for any meagre F-sigma set, one can construct some function $f$ having an antiderivative, which has the given set as its set of discontinuities.
- If $f$ has an antiderivative, is [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) on closed finite subintervals of the domain and has a set of discontinuities of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) 0, then an antiderivative may be found by integration in the sense of Lebesgue. In fact, using more powerful integrals like the [Henstock–Kurzweil integral](wikipedia://en/412708), every function for which an antiderivative exists is integrable, and its general integral coincides with its antiderivative.
- If $f$ has an antiderivative $F$ on a closed interval $[a,b]$, then for any choice of partition $a=x_0 <x_1 <x_2 <\dots <x_n=b,$ if one chooses sample points $x_i^*\in[x_{i-1},x_i]$ as specified by the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662), then the corresponding Riemann sum [telescopes](wikipedia://en/508070) to the value $F(b)-F(a)$. $$\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)(x_i-x_{i-1}) & = \sum_{i=1}^n [F(x_i)-F(x_{i-1})] \\ & = F(x_n)-F(x_0) = F(b)-F(a) \end{aligned}$$ However, if $f$ is unbounded, or if $f$ is bounded but the set of discontinuities of $f$ has positive Lebesgue measure, a different choice of sample points $x_i^*$ may give a significantly different value for the Riemann sum, no matter how fine the partition. See Example 4 below.

## Basic formulae {#Basic_formulae}

- If ${\frac{d}{dx}} f(x) = g(x)$, then $\int g(x) dx = f(x) + C$.
- $$\int 1dx = x + C$$
- $$\int a\ dx = ax + C$$
- $$\int x^n\ dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C;\ n \neq -1$$
- $$\int \sin{x}\ dx = -\cos{x} + C$$
- $$\int \cos{x}\ dx = \sin{x} + C$$
- $$\int \sec^2{x}\ dx = \tan{x} + C$$
- $$\int \csc^2{x}\ dx = -\cot{x} + C$$
- $$\int \sec{x}\tan{x}\ dx = \sec{x} + C$$
- $$\int \csc{x}\cot{x}\ dx = -\csc{x} + C$$
- $$\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + C$$
- $$\int e^{x}\ dx = e^{x} + C$$
- $$\int a^{x}\ dx = \frac{a^{x}}{\ln a} + C;\ a > 0,\ a \neq 1$$
- $$\int \frac{1}\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\ dx = \arcsin\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C$$
- $$\int \frac{1}{a^2 + x^2}\ dx = \frac{1}{a}\arctan\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C$$
