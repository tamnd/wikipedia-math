# Convolution

*Image: comparison convolution correlation.svg.* Visual comparison of convolution, [cross-correlation](wikipedia://en/714163), and [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724). For the operations involving function $f$, and assuming the height of $f$ is 1.0, the value of the result at 5 different points is indicated by the shaded area below each point. The symmetry of $f$ is the reason $f \star g$ and $g*f$ are identical in this example.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Convolution"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) (in particular, [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180)), **convolution** is a [mathematical operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) on two [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ and $g$ that produces a third function $f*g$, as the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the product of the two functions after one is reflected about the y-axis and shifted. The term *convolution* refers to both the resulting function and to the process of computing it. The integral is evaluated for all values of shift, producing the convolution function. The choice of which function is reflected and shifted before the integral does not change the integral result (see commutativity). Graphically, it expresses how the 'shape' of one function is modified by the other.

:::

Some features of convolution are similar to [cross-correlation](wikipedia://en/714163): for real-valued functions, of a continuous or discrete variable, convolution $f*g$ differs from cross-correlation $f \star g$ only in that either $f(x)$ or $g(x)$ is reflected about the y-axis in convolution; thus it is a cross-correlation of $g(-x)$ and $f(x)$, or $f(-x)$ and $g(x)$.Reasons for the reflection include:

It is necessary to implement the equivalent of the pointwise product of the Fourier transforms of $f$ and $g$.

When the convolution is viewed as a [moving weighted average](wikipedia://en/20238265), the weighting function, $g(-x)$, is often specified in terms of another function, $g(x)$, called the [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825) of a [linear time-invariant system](wikipedia://en/1383899#Impulse_response_and_convolution). For complex-valued functions, the cross-correlation operator is the [adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) of the convolution operator.

Convolution has applications that include [probability](wikipedia://en/22934), [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics), [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy), [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324) and [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing), [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision) and [human vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_perception), [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), and [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309).[^o2]

The convolution can be defined for functions on [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) and other [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) (as [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364)). For example, [periodic functions](wikipedia://en/76848), such as the [discrete-time Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/1216914), can be defined on a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and convolved by [periodic convolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_convolution). (See row 18 at DTFT.) A *discrete convolution* can be defined for functions on the set of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563).

Generalizations of convolution have applications in the field of [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [numerical linear algebra](wikipedia://en/7330660), and in the design and implementation of [finite impulse response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_impulse_response) filters in signal processing.

Computing the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of the convolution operation is known as [deconvolution](wikipedia://en/275626).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The convolution of $f$ and $g$ is written $f * g$, denoting the operator with the symbol $*$. The symbol 2217 is different than 2A, which is often used to denote complex conjugation. See Asterisk. It is defined as the integral of the product of the two functions after one is reflected about the y-axis and shifted. As such, it is a particular kind of [integral transform](wikipedia://en/430912):

$$(f * g)(t) := \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau) g(t - \tau) \, d\tau.$$

An equivalent definition is (see commutativity):

$$(f * g)(t) := \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t - \tau) g(\tau)\, d\tau.$$

While the symbol $t$ is used above, it need not represent the time domain. At each $t$, the convolution formula can be described as the area under the function $f(\tau)$ weighted by the function $g(-\tau)$ shifted by the amount $t$. As $t$ changes, the weighting function $g(t-\tau)$ emphasizes different parts of the input function $f(\tau)$; If $t$ is a positive value, then $g(t-\tau)$ is equal to $g(-\tau)$ that slides or is shifted along the $\tau$-axis toward the right (toward $+\infty$) by the amount of $t$, while if $t$ is a negative value, then $g(t-\tau)$ is equal to $g(-\tau)$ that slides or is shifted toward the left (toward $-\infty$) by the amount of $|t|$.

For functions $f$, $g$ [supported](wikipedia://en/381013) on only $[0,\infty)$ (i.e., zero for negative arguments), the integration limits can be truncated, resulting in:

$$(f * g)(t) = \int_{0}^{t} f(\tau) g(t - \tau)\, d\tau \quad \ \text{for } f, g : [0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}.$$

For the multi-dimensional formulation of convolution, see *domain of definition* (below).

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

### Notation {#Notation}

A common engineering notational convention is:[^o7]

$$f(t) * g(t) \mathrel{:=} \underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\tau) g(t - \tau)\, d\tau}_{(f * g )(t)},$$

which has to be interpreted carefully to avoid confusion. For instance, $f(t) * g(t-t_0)$ is equivalent to $(f*g)(t-t_0)$, but $f(t-t_0) * g(t-t_0)$ is in fact equivalent to $(f * g)(t-2t_0)$.[^o8]

:::

### Relations with other transforms {#Relations_with_other_transforms}

Given two functions $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ with [bilateral Laplace transforms](wikipedia://en/1502985) (two-sided Laplace transform)

$$F(s) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-su} \ f(u) \ \text{d}u$$

and

$$G(s) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-sv} \ g(v) \ \text{d}v$$

respectively, the convolution operation $(f * g)(t)$ can be defined as the [inverse Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/245688) of the product of $F(s)$ and $G(s)$.[^o10][^o11] More precisely,

$$\begin{aligned} F(s) \cdot G(s) &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-su} \ f(u) \ \text{d}u \cdot \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-sv} \ g(v) \ \text{d}v \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-s(u + v)} \ f(u) \ g(v) \ \text{d}u \ \text{d}v \end{aligned}$$

Let $t = u + v$, then

$$\begin{aligned} F(s) \cdot G(s) &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-st} \ f(u) \ g(t - u) \ \text{d}u \ \text{d}t \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-st} \underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(u) \ g(t - u) \ \text{d}u}_{(f * g)(t)} \ \text{d}t \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-st} (f * g)(t) \ \text{d}t. \end{aligned}$$

Note that $F(s) \cdot G(s)$ is the bilateral Laplace transform of $(f * g)(t)$. A similar derivation can be done using the [unilateral Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) (one-sided Laplace transform).

The convolution operation also describes the output (in terms of the input) of an important class of operations known as *linear time-invariant* (LTI). See [LTI system theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LTI_system_theory#Overview) for a derivation of convolution as the result of LTI constraints. In terms of the [Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/52247) of the input and output of an LTI operation, no new frequency components are created. The existing ones are only modified (amplitude and/or phase). In other words, the output transform is the pointwise product of the input transform with a third transform (known as a [transfer function](wikipedia://en/31146)). See [Convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/53268) for a derivation of that property of convolution. Conversely, convolution can be derived as the inverse Fourier transform of the pointwise product of two Fourier transforms.

:::

## Historical developments {#Historical_developments}

One of the earliest uses of the convolution integral appeared in [D'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d'Alembert)'s derivation of [Taylor's theorem](wikipedia://en/51714) in *Recherches sur différents points importants du système du monde,* published in 1754.[^o18]

Also, an expression of the type:

$$\int f(u)\cdot g(x - u) \, du$$

is used by [Sylvestre François Lacroix](wikipedia://en/2289727) on page 505 of his book entitled *Treatise on differences and series*, which is the last of 3 volumes of the encyclopedic series: Traité du calcul différentiel et du calcul intégral, Chez Courcier, Paris, 1797–1800.[^o19] Soon thereafter, convolution operations appear in the works of [Pierre Simon Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Simon_Laplace), [Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Joseph_Fourier), [Siméon Denis Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670), and others. The term itself did not come into wide use until the 1950s or 1960s. Prior to that it was sometimes known as *Faltung* (which means *folding* in [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language)), *composition product*, *superposition integral*, and *[Carson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Renshaw_Carson)'s integral*.[^o20] Yet it appears as early as 1903, though the definition is rather unfamiliar in older uses.[^o21][^o22]

The operation:

$$\int_0^t \varphi(s)\psi(t - s) \, ds,\quad 0 \le t < \infty,$$

is a particular case of composition products considered by the Italian mathematician [Vito Volterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vito_Volterra) in 1913.[^o23]

## Circular convolution {#Circular_convolution}

When a function $g_T$ is periodic, with period $T$, then for functions, $f$, such that $f * g_T$ exists, the convolution is also periodic and identical to:

$$(f * g_T)(t) \equiv \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} \left[\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty f(\tau + kT)\right] g_T(t - \tau)\, d\tau,$$

where $t_0$ is an arbitrary choice. The summation is called a [periodic summation](wikipedia://en/29262224) of the function $f$.

When $g_T$ is a periodic summation of another function, $g$, then $f*g_T$ is known as a *circular* or *cyclic* convolution of $f$ and $g$.

And if the periodic summation above is replaced by $f_T$, the operation is called a *periodic* convolution of $f_T$ and $g_T$.

## Discrete convolution {#Discrete_convolution}

*Image: 2D Convolution Animation.gif.* Discrete 2D convolution animation

For complex-valued functions $f$ and $g$ defined on the set $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers, the *discrete convolution* of $f$ and $g$ is given by:[^o26]

$$(f * g)[n] = \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty f[m] g[n - m],$$

or equivalently (see commutativity) by:

$$(f * g)[n] = \sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty f[n-m] g[m].$$

The convolution of two finite sequences is defined by extending the sequences to finitely supported functions on the set of integers. When the sequences are the coefficients of two [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), then the coefficients of the [ordinary product of the two polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_multiplication) are the convolution of the original two sequences. This is known as the [Cauchy product](wikipedia://en/863791) of the coefficients of the sequences.

Thus, when $g$ is non-zero over a finite interval $[-m,+m]$ (representing, for instance, a [finite impulse response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_impulse_response)), a finite summation may be used:[^o27]

$$(f * g)[n]=\sum_{m=-M}^M f[n-m]g[m].$$

### Circular discrete convolution {#Circular_discrete_convolution}

When a function $g_{_N}$ is periodic, with period $N,$ then for functions, $f,$ such that $f*g_{_N}$ exists, the convolution is also periodic and identical to**:**

$$(f * g_{_N})[n] \equiv \sum_{m=0}^{N-1} \left(\sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty {f}[m + kN]\right) g_{_N}[n - m].$$

The summation on $k$ is called a [periodic summation](wikipedia://en/29262224) of the function $f.$

If $g_{_N}$ is a periodic summation of another function, $g,$ then $f*g_{_N}$ is known as a [circular convolution](wikipedia://en/3367262) of $f$ and $g.$

Circular convolution arises most often in the context of fast convolution with a [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT) algorithm.

### Fast convolution algorithms {#Fast_convolution_algorithms}

In many situations, discrete convolutions can be converted to circular convolutions so that fast transforms with a convolution property can be used to implement the computation. For example, convolution of digit sequences is the kernel operation in [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) of multi-digit numbers, which can therefore be efficiently implemented with transform techniques ((Knuth 1997, §4.3.3.C); (von zur Gathen Gerhard 2003, §8.2)).

(Eq.1) requires $N$ arithmetic operations per output value and $N^{2}$ operations for $N$ outputs. That can be significantly reduced with any of several fast algorithms. [Digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing) and other applications typically use fast convolution algorithms to reduce the cost of the convolution to O($N$ log $N$) complexity.

The most common fast convolution algorithms use [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT) algorithms via the [circular convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/8811#Circular_convolution_theorem_and_cross-correlation_theorem). Specifically, the [circular convolution](wikipedia://en/3367262) of two finite-length sequences is found by taking an FFT of each sequence, multiplying pointwise, and then performing an inverse FFT. Convolutions of the type defined above are then efficiently implemented using that technique in conjunction with zero-extension and/or discarding portions of the output. Other fast convolution algorithms, such as the [Schönhage–Strassen algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schönhage–Strassen_algorithm) or the Mersenne transform,[^o34] use fast Fourier transforms in other [rings](wikipedia://en/48404). The Winograd method is used as an alternative to the FFT.[^o35] It significantly speeds up 1D,[^o36] 2D,[^o37] and 3D[^o38] convolution.

If one sequence is much longer than the other, zero-extension of the shorter sequence and fast circular convolution is not the most computationally efficient method available.[^o39] Instead, decomposing the longer sequence into blocks and convolving each block allows for faster algorithms such as the [overlap–save method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overlap–save_method) and [overlap–add method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overlap–add_method).[^o40] A hybrid convolution method that combines block and [FIR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_impulse_response) algorithms allows for a zero input-output latency that is useful for real-time convolution computations.[^o41]

:::

## Domain of definition {#Domain_of_definition}

The convolution of two complex-valued functions on $\mathbf{R}^{d}$ is itself a complex-valued function on $\mathbf{R}^{d}$, defined by:

$$(f * g )(x) = \int_{\mathbf{R}^d} f(y)g(x-y)\,dy = \int_{\mathbf{R}^d} f(x-y)g(y)\,dy,$$

and is well-defined only if $f$ and $g$ decay sufficiently rapidly at infinity in order for the integral to exist. Conditions for the existence of the convolution may be tricky, since a blow-up in $g$ at infinity can be easily offset by sufficiently rapid decay in $f$. The question of existence thus may involve different conditions on $f$ and $g$:

### Compactly supported functions {#Compactly_supported_functions}

If $f$ and $g$ are [compactly supported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122), then their convolution exists, and is also compactly supported and continuous (Hörmander 1983, Chapter 1). More generally, if either function (say $f$) is compactly supported and the other is [locally integrable](wikipedia://en/4258398), then the convolution $f\ast g$ is well-defined and continuous.

Convolution of $f$ and $g$ is also well defined when both functions are locally square integrable on $\mathbf{R}$ and supported on an interval of the form $[a, +\infty)$ (or both supported on $[-\infty, a]$).

### Integrable functions {#Integrable_functions}

The convolution of $f$ and $g$ exists if $f$ and $g$ are both [Lebesgue integrable functions](wikipedia://en/26064288) in [$L^{1}$($\mathbf{R}^{d}$)](wikipedia://en/45194), and in this case $f\ast g$ is also integrable (Stein Weiss 1971, Theorem 1.3). This is a consequence of [Tonelli's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245#Tonelli's_theorem). This is also true for functions in $L^{1}$, under the discrete convolution, or more generally for the convolution on any group.

Likewise, if $f \in L^{1}$($\mathbf{R}^{d}$) and $g \in L^{p}$($\mathbf{R}^{d}$) where $1 \le p \le \infty$, then $f*g \in L^{p}$($\mathbf{R}^{d}$), and

$$\|{f}* g\|_p\le \|f\|_1\|g\|_p.$$

In the particular case $p = 1$, this shows that $L^{1}$ is a [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665) under the convolution (and equality of the two sides holds if $f$ and $g$ are non-negative almost everywhere).

More generally, [Young's inequality](wikipedia://en/38542576) implies that the convolution is a continuous bilinear map between suitable $L^{p}$ spaces. Specifically, if $1 \le p, q, r \le \infty$ satisfy:

$$\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{r}+1,$$

then

$$\left\Vert f*g\right\Vert_r\le\left\Vert f\right\Vert_p\left\Vert g\right\Vert_q,\quad f\in L^p,\ g\in L^q,$$

so that the convolution is a continuous bilinear mapping from $L^{p}\times L^{q}$ to $L^{r}$. The Young inequality for convolution is also true in other contexts (circle group, convolution on $\mathbf{Z}$). The preceding inequality is not sharp on the real line: when $1 < p, q, r < \infty$, there exists a constant $B_{p,q} < 1$ such that:

$$\left\Vert f*g\right\Vert_r\le B_{p,q}\left\Vert f\right\Vert_p\left\Vert g\right\Vert_q,\quad f\in L^p,\ g\in L^q.$$

The optimal value of $B_{p,q}$ was discovered in 1975[^o47] and independently in 1976,[^o48] see [Brascamp–Lieb inequality](wikipedia://en/11365496).

A stronger estimate is true provided $1 < p, q, r < \infty$:

$$\|f * g\|_r\le C_{p,q}\|f\|_p\|g\|_{q,w}$$

where $\|g\|_{q,w}$ is the [weak $L^{q}$](wikipedia://en/45194#Weak_Lp) norm. Convolution also defines a bilinear continuous map $L^{p,w}\times L^{q,w}\to L^{r,w}$ for $1< p,q,r<\infty$, owing to the weak Young inequality:[^o49]

$$\|f * g\|_{r,w}\le C_{p,q}\|f\|_{p,w}\|g\|_{r,w}.$$

### Functions of rapid decay {#Functions_of_rapid_decay}

In addition to compactly supported functions and integrable functions, functions that have sufficiently rapid decay at infinity can also be convolved. An important feature of the convolution is that if *f* and *g* both decay rapidly, then *f*∗*g* also decays rapidly. In particular, if *f* and *g* are [rapidly decreasing functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapidly_decreasing_function), then so is the convolution *f*∗*g*. Combined with the fact that convolution commutes with differentiation (see #Properties), it follows that the class of [Schwartz functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_function) is closed under convolution (Stein Weiss 1971, Theorem 3.3).

### Distributions {#Distributions}

If *f* is a smooth function that is [compactly supported](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) and *g* is a distribution, then *f*∗*g* is a smooth function defined by

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} {f}(y)g(x-y)\,dy = (f*g)(x) \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^d) .$$

More generally, it is possible to extend the definition of the convolution in a unique way with $\varphi$ the same as *f* above, so that the associative law

$$f* (g* \varphi) = (f* g)* \varphi$$

remains valid in the case where *f* is a distribution, and *g* a compactly supported distribution (Hörmander 1983, §4.2).

### Measures {#Measures}

The convolution of any two [Borel measures](wikipedia://en/4322) *μ* and *ν* of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) is the measure $\mu*\nu$ defined by (Rudin 1962)

$$\int_{\mathbf{R}^d} f(x) \, d(\mu*\nu)(x) = \int_{\mathbf{R}^d}\int_{\mathbf{R}^d}f(x+y)\,d\mu(x)\,d\nu(y).$$

In particular,

$$(\mu*\nu)(A) = \int_{\mathbf{R}^d\times\mathbf R^d}1_A(x+y)\, d(\mu\times\nu)(x,y),$$

where $A\subset\mathbf R^d$ is a measurable set and $1_A$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $A$.

This agrees with the convolution defined above when μ and ν are regarded as distributions, as well as the convolution of L<sup>1</sup> functions when μ and ν are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

The convolution of measures also satisfies the following version of Young's inequality

$$\|\mu* \nu\|\le \|\mu\|\|\nu\|$$

where the norm is the [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561) of a measure. Because the space of measures of bounded variation is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), convolution of measures can be treated with standard methods of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) that may not apply for the convolution of distributions.

## Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o59 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Algebraic properties"}

### Algebraic properties {#Algebraic_properties}

The convolution defines a product on the [linear space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_space) of integrable functions. This product satisfies the following algebraic properties, which formally mean that the space of integrable functions with the product given by convolution is a commutative [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) without [identity](wikipedia://en/14962) (Strichartz 1994, §3.3). Other linear spaces of functions, such as the space of continuous functions of compact support, are [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under the convolution, and so also form commutative associative algebras.

**[Commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity)**

$$f * g = g * f$$

Proof: By definition:

$$(f * g)(t) = \int^\infty_{-\infty} f(\tau)g(t - \tau)\, d\tau$$

Changing the variable of integration to $u = t - \tau$ the result follows.

**[Associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity)**

$$f * (g * h) = (f * g) * h$$

Proof: This follows from using [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245) (i.e., double integrals can be evaluated as iterated integrals in either order).

**[Distributivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity)**

$$f * (g + h) = (f * g) + (f * h)$$

Proof: This follows from linearity of the integral.

**Associativity with scalar multiplication**

$$a (f * g) = (a f) * g$$

for any real (or complex) number $a$.

**[Multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity)**

No algebra of functions possesses an identity for the convolution. The lack of identity is typically not a major inconvenience, since most collections of functions on which the convolution is performed can be convolved with a [delta distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta) (a unitary impulse, centered at zero) or, at the very least (as is the case of *L*<sup>1</sup>) admit [approximations to the identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nascent_delta_function). The linear space of compactly supported distributions does, however, admit an identity under the convolution. Specifically,

$$f * \delta = f$$

where *δ* is the delta distribution.

**Inverse element**

Some distributions *S* have an [inverse element](wikipedia://en/45199) *S*<sup>−1</sup> for the convolution which then must satisfy

$$S^{-1} * S = \delta$$

from which an explicit formula for *S*<sup>−1</sup> may be obtained.The set of invertible distributions forms an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) under the convolution.

**Complex conjugation**

$$\overline{f * g} = \overline{f} * \overline{g}$$

**Time reversal**

If $q(t) = r(t)*s(t),$ then $q(-t) = r(-t)*s(-t).$

> Proof (using [convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/53268)):
>
> $$q(t) \ \stackrel{\mathcal{F}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\ \ Q(f) = R(f)S(f)$$
>
> $$q(-t) \ \stackrel{\mathcal{F}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\ \ Q(-f) = R(-f)S(-f)$$
>
> $$\begin{aligned} q(-t) &= \mathcal{F}^{-1}\bigg\{R(-f)S(-f)\bigg\}\\ &= \mathcal{F}^{-1}\bigg\{R (-f)\bigg\} * \mathcal{F}^{-1}\bigg\{S(-f)\bigg\}\\ &= r(-t) * s(-t) \end{aligned}$$

**Relationship with differentiation**

$$(f * g)' = f' * g = f * g'$$

Proof:

$$\begin{aligned} (f * g)' & = \frac{d}{dt} \int^\infty_{-\infty} f(\tau) g(t - \tau) \, d\tau \\ & =\int^\infty_{-\infty} f(\tau) \frac{\partial}{\partial t} g(t - \tau) \, d\tau \\ & =\int^\infty_{-\infty} f(\tau) g'(t - \tau) \, d\tau = f* g'. \end{aligned}$$

**Relationship with integration**

If $F(t) = \int^t_{-\infty} f(\tau) d\tau,$ and $G(t) = \int^t_{-\infty} g(\tau) \, d\tau,$ then

$$(F * g)(t) = (f * G)(t) = \int^t_{-\infty}(f * g)(\tau)\,d\tau.$$

:::

### Integration {#Integration}

If *f* and *g* are integrable functions, then the integral of their convolution on the whole space is simply obtained as the product of their integrals:[^o62]

$$\int_{\mathbf{R}^d}(f * g)(x) \, dx=\left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^d}f(x) \, dx\right) \left(\int_{\mathbf{R}^d}g(x) \, dx\right).$$

This follows from [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245). The same result holds if *f* and *g* are only assumed to be nonnegative measurable functions, by [Tonelli's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245#Tonelli's_theorem).

### Differentiation {#Differentiation}

In the one-variable case,

$$\frac{d}{dx}(f * g) = \frac{df}{dx} * g = f * \frac{dg}{dx}$$

where $\frac{d}{dx}$ is the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921). More generally, in the case of functions of several variables, an analogous formula holds with the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(f * g) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} * g = f * \frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i}.$$

A particular consequence of this is that the convolution can be viewed as a "smoothing" operation: the convolution of *f* and *g* is differentiable as many times as *f* and *g* are in total.

These identities hold for example under the condition that *f* and *g* are absolutely integrable and at least one of them has an absolutely integrable (L<sup>1</sup>) weak derivative, as a consequence of [Young's convolution inequality](wikipedia://en/38542576). For instance, when *f* is continuously differentiable with compact support, and *g* is an arbitrary locally integrable function,

$$\frac{d}{dx}(f* g) = \frac{df}{dx} * g.$$

These identities also hold much more broadly in the sense of tempered distributions if one of *f* or *g* is a [rapidly decreasing tempered distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Convolution_versus_multiplication), a compactly supported tempered distribution or a Schwartz function and the other is a tempered distribution. On the other hand, two positive integrable and infinitely differentiable functions may have a nowhere continuous convolution.

In the discrete case, the [difference operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_operator) *D* *f*(*n*) = *f*(*n* + 1) − *f*(*n*) satisfies an analogous relationship:

$$D(f * g) = (Df) * g = f * (Dg).$$

::: {#o65 .statement cue=heading name="Convolution theorem"}

### Convolution theorem {#Convolution_theorem}

The [convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/53268) states that[^o66]

$$\mathcal{F}\{f * g\} = \mathcal{F}\{f\}\cdot \mathcal{F}\{g\}$$

where $\mathcal{F}\{f\}$ denotes the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of $f$.

#### Convolution in other types of transformations {#Convolution_in_other_types_of_transformations}

Versions of this theorem also hold for the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610), [two-sided Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/1502985), [Z-transform](wikipedia://en/171589) and [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297).

#### Convolution on matrices {#Convolution_on_matrices}

If $\mathcal W$ is the [Fourier transform matrix](wikipedia://en/1090930), then

$$\mathcal W\left(C^{(1)}x \ast C^{(2)}y\right) = \left(\mathcal W C^{(1)} \bullet \mathcal W C^{(2)}\right)(x \otimes y) = \mathcal W C^{(1)}x \circ \mathcal W C^{(2)}y,$$

where $\bullet$ is [face-splitting product](wikipedia://en/64530953#Face-splitting_product),[^o69][^o70][^o71][^o72][^o73] $\otimes$ denotes [Kronecker product](wikipedia://en/712430), $\circ$ denotes [Hadamard product](wikipedia://en/34260496) (this result is an evolving of [count sketch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count_sketch) properties[^o74]).

This can be generalized for appropriate matrices $\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}$:

$$\mathcal W\left((\mathbf{A}x) \ast (\mathbf{B}y)\right) = \left((\mathcal W \mathbf{A}) \bullet (\mathcal W \mathbf{B})\right)(x \otimes y) = (\mathcal W \mathbf{A}x) \circ (\mathcal W \mathbf{B}y)$$

from the properties of the [face-splitting product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-splitting_product).

:::

### Translational equivariance {#Translational_equivariance}

The convolution commutes with translations, meaning that

$$\tau_x (f * g) = (\tau_x f) * g = f * (\tau_x g)$$

where τ<sub>*x*</sub>f is the translation of the function *f* by *x* defined by

$$(\tau_x f)(y) = f(y - x).$$

If *f* is a [Schwartz function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_function), then *τ<sub>x</sub>f* is the convolution with a translated Dirac delta function *τ*<sub>*x*</sub>*f* = *f* ∗ *τ*<sub>*x*</sub> *δ*. So translation invariance of the convolution of Schwartz functions is a consequence of the associativity of convolution.

Furthermore, under certain conditions, convolution is the most general translation invariant operation. Informally speaking, the following holds

Suppose that *S* is a bounded [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) acting on functions which commutes with translations: *S*(*τ<sub>x</sub>f*) = *τ<sub>x</sub>*(*Sf*) for all *x*. Then *S* is given as convolution with a function (or distribution) *g*<sub>*S*</sub>; that is *Sf* = *g*<sub>*S*</sub> ∗ *f*.

Thus some translation invariant operations can be represented as convolution. Convolutions play an important role in the study of [time-invariant systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-invariant_system), and especially [LTI system theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LTI_system_theory). The representing function *g*<sub>*S*</sub> is the [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825) of the transformation *S*.

A more precise version of the theorem quoted above requires specifying the class of functions on which the convolution is defined, and also requires assuming in addition that *S* must be a [continuous linear operator](wikipedia://en/1802169) with respect to the appropriate [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). It is known, for instance, that every continuous translation invariant continuous linear operator on *L*<sup>1</sup> is the convolution with a finite [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322). More generally, every continuous translation invariant continuous linear operator on *L*<sup>*p*</sup> for 1 ≤ *p* \< ∞ is the convolution with a [tempered distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Tempered_distributions_and_Fourier_transform) whose [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) is bounded. To wit, they are all given by bounded [Fourier multipliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_multiplier).

:::

## Convolutions on groups {#Convolutions_on_groups}

If *G* is a suitable [group](wikipedia://en/19447) endowed with a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) λ, and if *f* and *g* are real or complex valued [integrable](wikipedia://en/26064288) functions on *G*, then we can define their convolution by

$$(f * g)(x) = \int_G f(y) g\left(y^{-1}x\right)\,d\lambda(y).$$

It is not commutative in general. In typical cases of interest *G* is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and λ is a (left-) [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645). In that case, unless *G* is [unimodular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodular_group), the convolution defined in this way is not the same as $\int f\left(xy^{-1}\right)g(y) \, d\lambda(y)$. The preference of one over the other is made so that convolution with a fixed function *g* commutes with left translation in the group:

$$L_h(f* g) = (L_hf)* g.$$

Furthermore, the convention is also required for consistency with the definition of the convolution of measures given below. However, with a right instead of a left Haar measure, the latter integral is preferred over the former.

On [locally compact abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454325), a version of the [convolution theorem](wikipedia://en/53268) holds: the Fourier transform of a convolution is the pointwise product of the Fourier transforms. The [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) **T** with the Lebesgue measure is an immediate example. For a fixed *g* in *L*<sup>1</sup>(**T**), we have the following familiar operator acting on the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) *L*<sup>2</sup>(**T**):

$$T {f}(x) = \frac{1}{2 \pi} \int_{\mathbf{T}} {f}(y) g( x - y) \, dy.$$

The operator *T* is [compact](wikipedia://en/6690773). A direct calculation shows that its adjoint *T\** is convolution with

$$\bar{g}(-y).$$

By the commutativity property cited above, *T* is [normal](wikipedia://en/190167): *T*\* *T* = *TT*\* . Also, *T* commutes with the translation operators. Consider the family *S* of operators consisting of all such convolutions and the translation operators. Then *S* is a commuting family of normal operators. According to [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/6690773), there exists an orthonormal basis {*h<sub>k</sub>*} that simultaneously diagonalizes *S*. This characterizes convolutions on the circle. Specifically, we have

$$h_k (x) = e^{ikx}, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z},\;$$

which are precisely the [characters](wikipedia://en/293418) of **T**. Each convolution is a compact [multiplication operator](wikipedia://en/976642) in this basis. This can be viewed as a version of the convolution theorem discussed above.

A discrete example is a finite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order *n*. Convolution operators are here represented by [circulant matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulant_matrices), and can be diagonalized by the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811).

A similar result holds for compact groups (not necessarily abelian): the matrix coefficients of finite-dimensional [unitary representations](wikipedia://en/292788) form an orthonormal basis in *L*<sup>2</sup> by the [Peter–Weyl theorem](wikipedia://en/294474), and an analog of the convolution theorem continues to hold, along with many other aspects of [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) that depend on the Fourier transform.

## Convolution of measures {#Convolution_of_measures}

Let *G* be a (multiplicatively written) topological group. If μ and ν are [Radon measures](wikipedia://en/521933) on *G*, then their convolution *μ*∗*ν* is defined as the [pushforward measure](wikipedia://en/9412979) of the [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) and can be written as[^o78]

$$(\mu * \nu)(E) = \iint 1_E(xy) \,d\mu(x) \,d\nu(y)$$

for each measurable subset *E* of *G*. The convolution is also a Radon measure, whose [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561) satisfies

$$\|\mu * \nu\| \le \left\|\mu\right\| \left\|\nu\right\|.$$

In the case when *G* is [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) with (left-)[Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) λ, and μ and ν are [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670) with respect to a λ, [so that each has a density function](wikipedia://en/338746), then the convolution μ∗ν is also absolutely continuous, and its density function is just the convolution of the two separate density functions. In fact, if *either* measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the Haar measure, then so is their convolution.[^o79]

If μ and ν are [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) on the topological group (**R**,+), then the convolution *μ*∗*ν* is the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the sum *X* + *Y* of two [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* and *Y* whose respective distributions are μ and ν.

## Infimal convolution {#Infimal_convolution}

In [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201), the **infimal convolution** of proper (not identically $+\infty$) [convex functions](wikipedia://en/245568) $f_1,\dots,f_m$ on $\mathbb R^n$ is defined by:[^o81]

$$(f_1*\cdots*f_m)(x)=\inf_x \{ f_1(x_1)+\cdots+f_m(x_m) | x_1+\cdots+x_m = x\}.$$

It can be shown that the infimal convolution of convex functions is convex. Furthermore, it satisfies an identity analogous to that of the Fourier transform of a traditional convolution, with the role of the Fourier transform is played instead by the [Legendre transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_transform):

$$\varphi^*(x) = \sup_y ( x\cdot y - \varphi(y)).$$

We have:

$$(f_1*\cdots *f_m)^*(x) = f_1^*(x) + \cdots + f_m^*(x).$$

## Bialgebras {#Bialgebras}

Let (*X*, Δ, ∇, *ε*, *η*) be a [bialgebra](wikipedia://en/310887) with comultiplication Δ, multiplication ∇, unit η, and counit *ε*. The convolution is a product defined on the [endomorphism algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endomorphism_algebra) End(*X*) as follows. Let *φ*, *ψ* ∈ End(*X*), that is, *φ*, *ψ*: *X* → *X* are functions that respect all algebraic structure of *X*, then the convolution *φ*∗*ψ* is defined as the composition

$$X \mathrel{\xrightarrow{\Delta}} X \otimes X \mathrel{\xrightarrow{\phi\otimes\psi}} X \otimes X \mathrel{\xrightarrow{\nabla}} X.$$

The convolution appears notably in the definition of [Hopf algebras](wikipedia://en/325714) (Kassel 1995, §III.3). A bialgebra is a Hopf algebra if and only if it has an antipode: an endomorphism *S* such that

$$S * \operatorname{id}_X = \operatorname{id}_X * S = \eta\circ\varepsilon.$$

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Halftone, Gaussian Blur.jpg.* [Gaussian blur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_blur) can be used to obtain a smooth grayscale digital image of a [halftone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halftone) print.

Convolution and related operations are found in many applications in science, engineering and mathematics.

- [Convolutional neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolutional_neural_network) apply multiple cascaded *convolution* kernels with applications in [machine vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_vision) and [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence).[^o86][^o87] Though these are actually **cross-correlations** rather than convolutions.[^o88]
- In non-[neural-network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network)-based [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing)
  - In [digital image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_image_processing) convolutional filtering plays an important role in many important [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) in [edge detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_detection) and related processes (see [Kernel (image processing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28image_processing%29))
  - In [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), an out-of-focus photograph is a convolution of the sharp image with a lens function. The photographic term for this is [bokeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bokeh).
  - In image processing applications such as adding blurring.
- In digital data processing
  - In [analytical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemistry), [Savitzky–Golay smoothing filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savitzky–Golay_smoothing_filter) are used for the analysis of spectroscopic data. They can improve [signal-to-noise ratio](wikipedia://en/41706) with minimal distortion of the spectra
  - In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a weighted [moving average](wikipedia://en/990809) is a convolution.
- In [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics), [reverberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverberation) is the convolution of the original sound with [echoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Echo_%28phenomenon%29) from objects surrounding the sound source.
  - In digital signal processing, convolution is used to map the [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825) of a real room on a digital audio signal.
  - In [electronic music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_music) convolution is the imposition of a [spectral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum) or rhythmic structure on a sound. Often this envelope or structure is taken from another sound. The convolution of two signals is the filtering of one through the other.[^o89]
- In [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), the convolution of one function (the [input signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_%28electrical_engineering%29)) with a second function (the impulse response) gives the output of a [linear time-invariant system](wikipedia://en/1383899) (LTI). At any given moment, the output is an accumulated effect of all the prior values of the input function, with the most recent values typically having the most influence (expressed as a multiplicative factor). The impulse response function provides that factor as a function of the elapsed time since each input value occurred.
- In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), wherever there is a [linear system](wikipedia://en/722503) with a "[superposition principle](wikipedia://en/1201321)", a convolution operation makes an appearance. For instance, in [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy) line broadening due to the Doppler effect on its own gives a [Gaussian](wikipedia://en/21462) [spectral line shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line_shape) and collision broadening alone gives a [Lorentzian](wikipedia://en/7003) line shape. When both effects are operative, the line shape is a convolution of Gaussian and Lorentzian, a [Voigt function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voigt_function).
  - In [time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-resolved_spectroscopy#Time-resolved_fluorescence_spectroscopy), the excitation signal can be treated as a chain of delta pulses, and the measured fluorescence is a sum of exponential decays from each delta pulse.
  - In [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924), the [large eddy simulation](wikipedia://en/1178438) (LES) [turbulence model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence_model) uses the convolution operation to lower the range of length scales necessary in computation thereby reducing computational cost.
- In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the sum of two [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_%28probability%29) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) is the convolution of their individual distributions.
  - In [kernel density estimation](wikipedia://en/2090057), a distribution is estimated from sample points by convolution with a kernel, such as an isotropic Gaussian.[^n1]
- In radiotherapy treatment planning systems, most part of all modern codes of calculation applies a [convolution-superposition algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolution-superposition_algorithm).
- In structural reliability, the reliability index can be defined based on the convolution theorem.
  - The definition of reliability index for limit state functions with nonnormal distributions can be established corresponding to the [joint distribution function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution_function). In fact, the joint distribution function can be obtained using the convolution theory.[^n2]
- In [Smoothed-particle hydrodynamics](wikipedia://en/1266110), simulations of fluid dynamics are calculated using particles, each with surrounding kernels. For any given particle $i$, some physical quantity $A_i$ is calculated as a convolution of $A_j$ with a weighting function, where $j$ denotes the neighbors of particle $i$: those that are located within its kernel. The convolution is approximated as a summation over each neighbor.[^o92]
- In [Fractional calculus](wikipedia://en/229939) convolution is instrumental in various definitions of fractional integral and fractional derivative.
