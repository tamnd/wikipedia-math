# Arithmetic–geometric mean

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Arithmetic–geometric mean"}

*Image: Generalized means + agm.png.* Plot of the arithmetic–geometric mean $\operatorname{agm}(1,x)$ among several [generalized means](wikipedia://en/13143).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **arithmetic–geometric mean** (AGM or agM[^o2]) of two [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) $x$ and $y$ is the mutual limit of a sequence of [arithmetic means](wikipedia://en/612) and a sequence of [geometric means](wikipedia://en/13046). The arithmetic–geometric mean is used in fast [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) for [exponential](wikipedia://en/9678), [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367), and other [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110), as well as some [mathematical constants](wikipedia://en/48779551), in particular, [computing $\pi$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing_π).

:::

The AGM is defined as the limit of the interdependent [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) $a_i$ and $g_i$. Assuming $x \geq y \geq 0$, we write:

$$\begin{aligned} a_0 &= x,\\ g_0 &= y\\ a_{n+1} &= \tfrac12(a_n + g_n),\\ g_{n+1} &= \sqrt{a_n g_n}\, . \end{aligned}$$

These two sequences [converge](wikipedia://en/285773) to the same number, the arithmetic–geometric mean of $x$ and $y$; it is denoted by $M(x, y)$, or sometimes by $\operatorname{agm}(x, y)$ or $\operatorname{AGM}(x, y)$.

The arithmetic–geometric mean can be extended to [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) and, when the [branches](wikipedia://en/483173#Branch_cuts) of the square root are allowed to be taken inconsistently, it is a [multivalued function](wikipedia://en/222367).[^o2]

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

To find the arithmetic–geometric mean of $a_{0} = 24$ and $g_{0} = 6$, iterate as follows:

$$\begin{array}{rcccl} a_1 & = & \tfrac12(24 + 6) & = & 15\\ g_1 & = & \sqrt{24 \cdot 6} & = & 12\\ a_2 & = & \tfrac12(15 + 12) & = & 13.5\\ g_2 & = & \sqrt{15 \cdot 12} & = & 13.416\ 407\ 8649\dots\\ & & \vdots & & \end{array}$$

The first five iterations give the following values:

| $n$ | $a_{n}$ | $g_{n}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 24 | 6 |
| 1 | 15 | 12 |
| 2 | \13.5 | \13.416 407 864 998 738 178 455 042... |
| 3 | \13.458 203 932 499 369 089 227 521... | \13.458 139 030 990 984 877 207 090... |
| 4 | \13.458 171 481 745 176 983 217 305... | \13.458 171 481 706 053 858 316 334... |
| 5 | \13.458 171 481 725 615 420 766 820... | \13.458 171 481 725 615 420 766 806... |

:::

The number of digits in which $a_{n}$ and $g_{n}$ agree (underlined) approximately doubles with each iteration. The arithmetic–geometric mean of 24 and 6 is the common limit of these two sequences, which is approximately \13.4581714817256154207668131569743992430538388544.[^o6]

:::

## History {#History}

The first algorithm based on this sequence pair appeared in the works of [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793). Its properties were further analyzed by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125).[^o2]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Both the geometric mean and arithmetic mean of two positive numbers $x$ and $y$ are between the two numbers. (They are *strictly* between when $x \ne y$.) The geometric mean of two positive numbers is [never greater than the arithmetic mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means).[^o10] So the geometric means are an increasing sequence $g_{0} \le g_{1} \le g_{2} \le ...$; the arithmetic means are a decreasing sequence $a_{0} \ge a_{1} \ge a_{2} \ge ...$; and $g_{n} \le M(x, y) \le a_{n}$ for any $n$. These are strict inequalities if $x \ne y$.

$M(x, y)$ is thus a number between $x$ and $y$; it is also between the geometric and arithmetic mean of $x$ and $y$.

If $r \ge 0$ then $M(rx, ry) = r M(x, y)$.

There is an integral-form expression for $M(x, y)$:

$$\begin{aligned} M(x,y) &= \frac{\pi}{2} \left( \int_0^\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{x^2\cos^2\theta+y^2\sin^2\theta}} \right)^{-1}\\ &=\pi\left(\int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{\sqrt{t(t+x^2)(t+y^2)}}\right)^{-1}\\ &= \frac{\pi}{4} \cdot \frac{x + y}{K\left( \frac{x - y}{x + y} \right)} \end{aligned}$$

where $K(k)$ is the [complete elliptic integral of the first kind](wikipedia://en/9960):

$$K(k) = \int_0^\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\theta}}$$

Since the arithmetic–geometric process converges so quickly, it provides an efficient way to compute elliptic integrals, which are used, for example, in [elliptic filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_filter) design.[^o12]

The arithmetic–geometric mean is connected to the [Jacobi theta function](wikipedia://en/449745) $\theta_3$ by[^o13]

$$\begin{aligned} M(1,x) &= \theta_3^{-2}{\left(\exp \left(-\pi \frac{M(1,x)}{M\left(1,\sqrt{1-x^2}\right)}\right)\right)} \\ &= \left[\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp \left(-n^2 \pi \frac{M(1,x)}{M\left(1,\sqrt{1-x^2}\right)}\right)\right]^{-2}, \end{aligned}$$

which upon setting $x=1/\sqrt{2}$ gives

$$M(1,1/\sqrt{2})=\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-n^2\pi}\right)^{-2}.$$

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

The reciprocal of the arithmetic–geometric mean of 1 and the [square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682) is [Gauss's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_constant).

$$\frac{1}{M(1, \sqrt{2})} = G = 0.8346268\dots$$

In 1799, Gauss proved[^o15] that

$$M(1,\sqrt{2})=\frac{\pi}{\varpi}$$

where $\varpi$ is the [lemniscate constant](wikipedia://en/2079065).

In 1941, $M(1,\sqrt{2})$ (and hence $G$) was proved [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325) by [Theodor Schneider](wikipedia://en/10312621).[^o16][^o17][^o18] The set $\{\pi,M(1,1/\sqrt{2})\}$ is [algebraically independent](wikipedia://en/287808) over $\mathbb{Q}$,[^o19][^o20] but the set $\{\pi,M(1,1/\sqrt{2}),M'(1,1/\sqrt{2})\}$ (where the prime denotes the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) with respect to the second variable) is not algebraically independent over $\mathbb{Q}$. In fact,[^o21]

$$\pi=2\sqrt{2}\frac{M^3(1,1/\sqrt{2})}{M'(1,1/\sqrt{2})}.$$

The [geometric–harmonic mean](wikipedia://en/697531) GH can be calculated using analogous sequences of geometric and [harmonic](wikipedia://en/14463) means, and in fact $\operatorname{GH}(x, y) = 1/M(1/x, 1/y) = xy/M(x, y)$.[^o22] The arithmetic–harmonic mean [is equivalent to the geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046#Iterative_means).

The arithmetic–geometric mean can be used to compute – among others – [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860#Arithmetic–geometric_mean_approximation), [complete and incomplete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind](wikipedia://en/9960),[^o23] and [Jacobi elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/450004).[^o24]

::: {#o26 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of existence"}

## Proof of existence {#Proof_of_existence}

The [inequality of arithmetic and geometric means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means) implies that

$$g_n \leq a_n$$

and thus

$$g_{n + 1} = \sqrt{g_n \cdot a_n} \geq \sqrt{g_n \cdot g_n} = g_n$$

that is, the sequence $g_{n}$ is nondecreasing and bounded above by the larger of $x$ and $y$. By the [monotone convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/146263), the sequence is convergent, so there exists a $g$ such that:

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}g_n = g$$

However, we can also see that:

$$a_n = \frac{g_{n + 1}^2}{g_n}$$

and so:

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}a_n = \lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{g_{n + 1}^2}{g_{n}} = \frac{g^2}{g} = g$$

[Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)

:::

::: {#o28 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of the integral-form expression"}

## Proof of the integral-form expression {#Proof_of_the_integral-form_expression}

This proof is given by Gauss.[^o2] Let

$$I(x,y) = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{x^2\cos^2\theta+y^2\sin^2\theta}} ,$$

Changing the variable of integration to $\theta'$, where

$$\begin{aligned} \sin\theta &= \frac{2x\sin\theta'}{(x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta'} \\ \Rightarrow d(\sin\theta) &= d\left(\frac{2x\sin\theta'}{(x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta'}\right)\\ \Rightarrow \cos\theta\ d\theta &= 2x \frac{(x+y)-(x-y)\sin^2\theta'}{((x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta')^2}\ \cos\theta' d\theta' \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} \cos\theta &= \frac{\sqrt{(x+y)^2-2(x^2+y^2)\sin^2\theta'+(x-y)^2\sin^4\theta'}}{(x+y) + (x-y)\sin^2\theta'}\\[1ex] &= \frac{\cos\theta'\sqrt{(x-y)^2\cos^2\theta'+4xy}}{(x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta'}\\[1ex] &= \frac{\cos\theta'\sqrt{(x+y)^2\cos^2\theta'+4xy\sin^2\theta'}}{(x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta'}, \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} \Rightarrow \cos\theta\ d\theta &=\frac{\cos\theta'\sqrt{(x+y)^2\cos^2\theta'+4xy\sin^2\theta'}}{(x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta'}\ d\theta \\[1ex] &= 2x \frac{(x+y)-(x-y)\sin^2\theta'}{((x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta')^2}\ \cos\theta' d\theta' , \end{aligned}$$

$$\Rightarrow d\theta = \frac{x((x+y)-(x-y)\sin^2\theta')}{((x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta')} \frac{2 d\theta'}{\sqrt{(x+y)^2\cos^2\theta'+4xy\sin^2\theta'}} \ ,$$

$$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{x^2\cos^2\theta+y^2\sin^2\theta} &= \frac{\sqrt{{x^2 \left[(x+y)^2-2(x^2+y^2)\sin^2\theta'+(x-y)^2\sin^4\theta'\right] + 4x^2y^2\sin^2\theta'}}}{((x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta')}\\ &= \frac{x \left((x+y)-(x-y)\sin^2\theta'\right)}{\left((x+y)+(x-y)\sin^2\theta'\right)} \end{aligned}$$

This yields

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{x^2\cos^2\theta+y^2\sin^2\theta}} &= \frac{2 d\theta'}{\sqrt{(x+y)^2\cos^2\theta'+4xy\sin^2\theta'}} \\ &= \frac{d\theta'}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^2\cos^2\theta' + \left(\sqrt{xy}\right)^2\sin^2\theta'}}, \end{aligned}$$

gives

$$\begin{aligned} I(x,y) &= \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\theta'}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)^2\cos^2\theta' + \left(\sqrt{xy}\right)^2 \sin^2\theta'}} \\ &= I{\left(\tfrac{x+y}{2},\sqrt{xy}\right)} . \end{aligned}$$

Thus, we have

$$\begin{aligned} I(x,y) &= I(a_1, g_1) = I(a_2, g_2) = \cdots\\ &= I\bigl(M(x,y),M(x,y)\bigr) = \frac{\pi}{2M(x,y)} . \end{aligned}$$

The last equality comes from observing that $I(z,z) = \pi/(2z)$.

Finally, we obtain the desired result

$$M(x,y) = \frac{\pi}{2 I(x,y)}.$$

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### The number *π* {#The_number_π}

According to the [Gauss–Legendre algorithm](wikipedia://en/12916),[^o32]

$$\pi = \frac{4\,M(1,1/\sqrt{2})^2} {1 - \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^\infty 2^{j+1} c_j^2} ,$$

where

$$c_j = \frac{1}{2}\left(a_{j-1}-g_{j-1}\right) ,$$

with $a_0=1$ and $g_0=1/\sqrt{2}$, which can be computed without loss of precision using

$$c_j = \frac{c_{j-1}^2}{4a_j} .$$

### Complete elliptic integral *K*(sin*α*) {#Complete_elliptic_integral_K(sinα)}

Taking $a_0 = 1$ and $g_0 = \cos\alpha$ yields the AGM

$$M(1,\cos\alpha) = \frac{\pi}{2K(\sin \alpha)} ,$$

where $K(k)$ is a complete [elliptic integral of the first kind](wikipedia://en/9960):

$$K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1 - k^2 \sin^2\theta}}.$$

That is to say that this [quarter period](wikipedia://en/1471280) may be efficiently computed through the AGM,

$$K(k) = \frac{\pi}{2M(1,\sqrt{1-k^2})} .$$

### Other applications {#Other_applications}

Using this property of the AGM along with the ascending transformations of [John Landen](wikipedia://en/1400598),[^o35] [Richard P. Brent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_P._Brent)[^o36] suggested the first AGM algorithms for the fast evaluation of elementary [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021) ($e^{x}$, $\cos x$, $\sin x$). Subsequently, many authors went on to study the use of the AGM algorithms.[^o37]
