# Euler's sum of powers conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euler's sum of powers conjecture"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Euler's conjecture** is a [disproved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_disproved_mathematical_ideas) [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) related to [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953). It was presented by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in 1778 to the [Academy of Sciences of St. Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences). It states that for all [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) $n$ and $k$ greater than 1, if the sum of $n$ many $k$th powers of positive integers is itself a $k$th power, then $n$ is greater than or equal to $k$:

:::

$$a_1^k + a_2^k + \dots + a_n^k = b^k \implies n \ge k$$

The conjecture represents an attempt to generalize Fermat's Last Theorem, which is the special case $n = 2$: if $a_1^k + a_2^k = b^k,$ then $2 \ge k$.

Although the conjecture holds for the case $k = 3$ (which follows from Fermat's Last Theorem for the third powers), it was disproved for $k = 4$ and $k = 5$. It is unknown whether the conjecture fails or holds for any value $k \ge 6$.

## Background {#Background}

Euler was aware of the equality 59${}^{4}$ + 158${}^{4}$ = 133${}^{4}$ + 134${}^{4}$ involving sums of four fourth powers;[^o3][^o4] this, however, is not a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) because no term is isolated on one side of the equation. He also provided a complete solution to the four cubes problem as in [Plato's number](wikipedia://en/18602826) 3${}^{3}$ + 4${}^{3}$ + 5${}^{3}$ = 6${}^{3}$ or the [taxicab number](wikipedia://en/513117) 1729.[^o5][^o6] The general solution of the equation $x_1^3+x_2^3=x_3^3+x_4^3$ is

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &=\lambda( 1-(a-3b)(a^2+3b^2)) \\[2pt] x_2 &=\lambda( (a+3b)(a^2+3b^2)-1 )\\[2pt] x_3 &=\lambda( (a+3b)-(a^2+3b^2)^2 )\\[2pt] x_4 &= \lambda( (a^2+3b^2)^2-(a-3b)) \end{aligned}$$

where $a$, $b$ and ${\lambda}$ are any rational numbers.

The conjecture was presented in 1778 but only published after Euler's death.[^o3][^o4]

## Counterexamples {#Counterexamples}

Euler's conjecture was disproven by [L. J. Lander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_J._Lander) and [T. R. Parkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_R._Parkin) in 1966 when, through a direct computer search on a [CDC 6600](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDC_6600), they found a counterexample for $k = 5$.[^o9] This was published in a paper comprising just two sentences.[^o9] A total of four primitive (that is, in which the [summands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summands) do not all have a [common factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_factor)) counterexamples are known:

$$\begin{aligned} 144^5 &= 27^5 + 84^5 + 110^5 + 133^5 \\ 14132^5 &= (-220)^5 + 5027^5 + 6237^5 + 14068^5 \\ 85359^5 &= 55^5 + 3183^5 + 28969^5 + 85282^5 \\ 1956878^5 &= 719115^5 + 1331622^5 + (-1340632)^5 + 1956213^5 \end{aligned}$$

(Lander & Parkin, 1966); (Scher & Seidl, 1996); (Frye, 2004); (Braun, 2026)[^o10].

In 1988, [Noam Elkies](wikipedia://en/1136999) published a method to construct an infinite sequence of counterexamples for the $k = 4$ case.[^o11] His smallest counterexample was

$$20615673^4 = 2682440^4 + 15365639^4 + 18796760^4.$$

A particular case of Elkies' solutions can be reduced to the identity[^o12][^o13]

$$(85v^2 + 484v - 313)^4 + (68v^2 - 586v + 10)^4 + (2u)^4 = (357v^2 - 204v + 363)^4,$$

where

$$u^2 = 22030 + 28849v - 56158v^2 + 36941v^3 - 31790v^4.$$

This is an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) with a [rational point](wikipedia://en/2960200) at $v_{1} = -\tfrac{31}{467}$. From this initial rational point, one can compute an infinite collection of others. Substituting $v_{1}$ into the identity and removing common factors gives the numerical example cited above.

In 1988, [Roger Frye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Frye) found the smallest possible counterexample

$$95800^4 + 217519^4 + 414560^4 = 422481^4$$

for $k = 4$ by a direct computer search using techniques suggested by Elkies. This solution is the only one with values of the variables below 1,000,000.[^o14]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

*Image: Plato\_number.svg.* One interpretation of Plato's number, 3<sup>3</sup> + 4<sup>3</sup> + 5<sup>3</sup> = 6<sup>3</sup>

In 1967, L. J. Lander, T. R. Parkin, and [John Selfridge](wikipedia://en/995084) conjectured[^o18] that if

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^k = \sum_{j=1}^{m} b_j^k,$$

where $a_{i} \ne b_{j}$ are positive integers for all $1 \le i \le n$ and $1 \le j \le m$, then $m + n \ge k$. In the special case $m = 1$, the conjecture states that if

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^k = b^k$$

(under the conditions given above) then $n \ge k - 1$.

The special case may be described as the problem of giving a [partition](wikipedia://en/104790) of a perfect power into few like powers. For $k = 4, 5, 7, 8$ and $n = k$ or $k - 1$, there are many known solutions. Some of these are listed below.

See for more data.

### $k = 3$

From Fermat's Last Theorem, we know that there can't be a solution to $a^3 + b^3 = c^3$. (The minimum positive value of a sum of third powers is $9^3 - 8^3 - 6^3 = 1$, which provides a solution to the equation (*a* = (1, 6, 8), *b* = 9), where however the smallest member isn't larger than 1.)

The smallest solution with terms > 1 is

$$3^3 + 4^3 + 5^3 = 6^3$$

([Plato's number](wikipedia://en/18602826) 216) This is the case $a = 1$, $b = 0$ of [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717)'s formula[^o19]

$$(3a^2+5ab-5b^2)^3 + (4a^2-4ab+6b^2)^3 + (5a^2-5ab-3b^2)^3 = (6a^2-4ab+4b^2)^3$$

A cube as the sum of three cubes can also be parameterized in one of two ways:[^o19]

$$\begin{aligned} a^3(a^3+b^3)^3 &= b^3(a^3+b^3)^3+a^3(a^3-2b^3)^3+b^3(2a^3-b^3)^3 \\[6pt] a^3(a^3+2b^3)^3 &= a^3(a^3-b^3)^3+b^3(a^3-b^3)^3+b^3(2a^3+b^3)^3. \end{aligned}$$

The number 2,100,000<sup>3</sup> can be expressed as the sum of three positive cubes in nine different ways.[^o19]

### $k = 4$

$$\begin{aligned} 422481^4 &= 95800^4 + 217519^4 + 414560^4 \\[4pt] 353^4 &= 30^4 + 120^4 + 272^4 + 315^4 \end{aligned}$$

(R. Frye, 1988);[^o11] (R. Norrie, smallest, 1911).[^o18]

### $k = 5$

$$\begin{aligned} 144^5 &= 27^5 + 84^5 + 110^5 + 133^5 \\[2pt] 72^5 &= 19^5 + 43^5 + 46^5 + 47^5 + 67^5 \\[2pt] 94^5 &= 21^5 + 23^5 + 37^5 + 79^5 + 84^5 \\[2pt] 107^5 &= 7^5 + 43^5 + 57^5 + 80^5 + 100^5 \end{aligned}$$

(Lander & Parkin, 1966);[^o20][^o21][^o22] (Lander, Parkin, Selfridge, smallest, 1967);[^o18] (Lander, Parkin, Selfridge, second smallest, 1967);[^o18] (Sastry, 1934, third smallest).[^o18]

### $k = 6$

It has been known since 2002 that there are no solutions for $k = 6$ whose final term is ≤ 730000.[^o23]

### $k = 7$

$$568^7 = 127^7 + 258^7 + 266^7 + 413^7 + 430^7 + 439^7 + 525^7$$

(M. Dodrill, 1999).[^o24]

### $k = 8$

$$1409^8 = 90^8 + 223^8 + 478^8 + 524^8 + 748^8 + 1088^8 + 1190^8 + 1324^8$$

(S. Chase, 2000).[^o25]
