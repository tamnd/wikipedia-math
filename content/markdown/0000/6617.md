# Compactification (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Compactification"}

*Image: Warsaw\_Circle.png.* This is the compactification in the plane of the graph of sin(1/x) over the interval (0,2/π\], with a judiciously chosen arc from (0,-1) to (2/π,1).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649), **compactification** is the process or result of making a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) into a [compact space](wikipedia://en/6042).[^o2] A compact space is a space in which every [open cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) of the space contains a finite subcover. The methods of compactification are various, but each is a way of controlling points from "going off to infinity" by in some way adding "points at infinity" or preventing such an "escape".

:::

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="An example"}

## An example {#An_example}

Consider the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) with its ordinary topology. This space is not compact; in a sense, points can go off to infinity to the left or to the right. It is possible to turn the real line into a compact space by adding a single "point at infinity" which we will denote by ∞. The resulting compactification is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to a circle in the plane (which, as a closed and bounded subset of the Euclidean plane, is compact). Every sequence that ran off to infinity in the real line will then converge to ∞ in this compactification. The direction in which a number approaches infinity on the number line (either in the - direction or + direction) is still preserved on the circle; for if a number approaches towards infinity from the - direction on the number line, then the corresponding point on the circle can approach ∞ from the left for example. Then if a number approaches infinity from the + direction on the number line, then the corresponding point on the circle can approach ∞ from the right.

Intuitively, the process can be pictured as follows: first shrink the real line to the [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) (−[$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601), $\pi$) on the *x*-axis; then bend the ends of this interval upwards (in positive *y*-direction) and move them towards each other, until you get a circle with one point (the topmost one) missing. This point is our new point ∞ "at infinity"; adding it in completes the compact circle.

A bit more formally: we represent a point on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) by its [angle](wikipedia://en/1196), in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), going from −$\pi$ to $\pi$ for simplicity. Identify each such point *θ* on the circle with the corresponding point on the real line [tan](wikipedia://en/31482)(*θ*/2). This function is undefined at the point $\pi$, since tan($\pi$/2) is undefined; we will identify this point with our point ∞.

Since tangents and inverse tangents are both continuous, our identification function is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) between the real line and the unit circle without ∞. What we have constructed is called the *Alexandroff one-point compactification* of the real line, discussed in more generality below. It is also possible to compactify the real line by adding *two* points, +∞ and −∞; this results in the [extended real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_real_line).

## Definition {#Definition}

An [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) of a topological space *X* as a [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) subset of a compact space is called a **compactification** of *X*. It is often useful to embed [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) in [compact spaces](wikipedia://en/6042), because of the special properties compact spaces have.

Embeddings into compact [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) may be of particular interest. Since every compact Hausdorff space is a [Tychonoff space](wikipedia://en/30554), and every subspace of a Tychonoff space is Tychonoff, we conclude that any space possessing a Hausdorff compactification must be a Tychonoff space. In fact, the converse is also true; being a Tychonoff space is both necessary and sufficient for possessing a Hausdorff compactification.

The fact that large and interesting classes of non-compact spaces do in fact have compactifications of particular sorts makes compactification a common technique in topology.

### Alexandroff one-point compactification {#Alexandroff_one-point_compactification}

For any noncompact topological space *X* the (**Alexandroff**) **one-point compactification** α*X* of *X* is obtained by adding one extra point ∞ (often called a *point at infinity*) and defining the [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) of the new space to be the open sets of *X* together with the sets of the form *G* ∪ $\{\infty\}$, where *G* is an open subset of *X* such that $X \setminus G$ is closed and compact. The one-point compactification of *X* is Hausdorff if and only if *X* is Hausdorff and [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact).[^o8]

### Stone–Čech compactification {#Stone–Čech_compactification}

Of particular interest are Hausdorff compactifications, i.e., compactifications in which the compact space is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637). A topological space has a Hausdorff compactification if and only if it is [Tychonoff](wikipedia://en/30554). In this case, there is a unique ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660)) "most general" Hausdorff compactification, the [Stone–Čech compactification](wikipedia://en/52897) of *X*, denoted by *βX*; formally, this exhibits the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of Compact Hausdorff spaces and continuous maps as a [reflective subcategory](wikipedia://en/3967424) of the category of Tychonoff spaces and continuous maps.

"Most general" or formally "reflective" means that the space *βX* is characterized by the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) that any [continuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) from *X* to a compact Hausdorff space *K* can be extended to a continuous function from *βX* to *K* in a unique way. More explicitly, *βX* is a compact Hausdorff space containing *X* such that the [induced topology](wikipedia://en/910926) on *X* by *βX* is the same as the given topology on *X*, and for any continuous map *f* : *X* → *K*, where *K* is a compact Hausdorff space, there is a unique continuous map *g* : *βX* → *K* for which *g* restricted to *X* is identically *f*.

The Stone–Čech compactification can be constructed explicitly as follows: let *C* be the set of continuous functions from *X* to the closed interval \[0, 1\]. Then each point in *X* can be identified with an evaluation function on *C*. Thus *X* can be identified with a subset of \[0, 1\]<sup>*C*</sup>, the space of *all* functions from *C* to \[0, 1\]. Since the latter is compact by [Tychonoff's theorem](wikipedia://en/82739), the closure of *X* as a subset of that space will also be compact. This is the Stone–Čech compactification.[^o10][^o11]

### Spacetime compactification {#Spacetime_compactification}

[Walter Benz](wikipedia://en/18994043) and [Isaak Yaglom](wikipedia://en/11606627) have shown how [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) onto a single-sheet [hyperboloid](wikipedia://en/145844) can be used to provide a [compactification for split complex numbers](wikipedia://en/2580834#Compactification). In fact, the hyperboloid is part of a [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570) in real projective four-space. The method is similar to that used to provide a base manifold for [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) of the [conformal group of spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_group_of_spacetime).[^o14]

## Projective space {#Projective_space}

[Real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a compactification of Euclidean space **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. For each possible "direction" in which points in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> can "escape", one new point at infinity is added (but each direction is identified with its opposite). The Alexandroff one-point compactification of **R** we constructed in the example above is in fact homeomorphic to **RP**<sup>1</sup>. Note however that the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) **RP**<sup>2</sup> is *not* the one-point compactification of the plane **R**<sup>2</sup> since more than one point is added.

[Complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is also a compactification of **C**<sup>*n*</sup>; the Alexandroff one-point compactification of the plane **C** is (homeomorphic to) the complex projective line **CP**<sup>1</sup>, which in turn can be identified with a sphere, the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799).

Passing to projective space is a common tool in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) because the added points at infinity lead to simpler formulations of many theorems. For example, any two different lines in **RP**<sup>2</sup> intersect in precisely one point, a statement that is not true in **R**<sup>2</sup>. More generally, [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217), which is fundamental in [intersection theory](wikipedia://en/3148602), holds in projective space but not affine space. This distinct behavior of intersections in affine space and projective space is reflected in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) in the [cohomology rings](wikipedia://en/1069723) – the cohomology of affine space is trivial, while the cohomology of projective space is non-trivial and reflects the key features of intersection theory (dimension and degree of a subvariety, with intersection being [Poincaré dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_dual) to the [cup product](wikipedia://en/848684)).

Compactification of [moduli spaces](wikipedia://en/361609) generally require allowing certain degeneracies – for example, allowing certain singularities or reducible varieties. This is notably used in the Deligne–Mumford compactification of the [moduli space of algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/3010057).

## Compactification and discrete subgroups of Lie groups {#Compactification_and_discrete_subgroups_of_Lie_groups}

In the study of [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) subgroups of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) is often a candidate for more subtle **compactification** to preserve structure at a richer level than just topological.

For example, [modular curves](wikipedia://en/669552) are compactified by the addition of single points for each [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758), making them [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181) (and so, since they are compact, [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260)). Here the cusps are there for a good reason: the curves parametrize a space of [lattices](wikipedia://en/469246), and those lattices can degenerate ('go off to infinity'), often in a number of ways (taking into account some auxiliary structure of *level*). The cusps stand in for those different 'directions to infinity'.

That is all for lattices in the plane. In $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) the same questions can be posed, for example about $\text{SO}(n) \setminus \text{SL}_n(\textbf{R}) / \text{SL}_n(\textbf{Z}).$ This is harder to compactify. There are a variety of compactifications, such as the [Borel–Serre compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel–Serre_compactification), the [reductive Borel–Serre compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductive_Borel–Serre_compactification), and the [Satake compactifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satake_compactification), that can be formed.

## Other compactification theories {#Other_compactification_theories}

- The theories of [ends of a space](wikipedia://en/6862105) and [prime ends](wikipedia://en/23404066).
- Some 'boundary' theories such as the [collaring of an open manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collaring_of_an_open_manifold), [Martin boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_boundary), [Shilov boundary](wikipedia://en/13520887) and [Furstenberg boundary](wikipedia://en/45320987).
- The [Bohr compactification](wikipedia://en/634780) of a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) arises from the consideration of [almost periodic functions](wikipedia://en/405512).
- The [projective line over a ring](wikipedia://en/1271901) for a [topological ring](wikipedia://en/60097) may compactify it.
- The [Baily–Borel compactification](wikipedia://en/16083822) of a quotient of a [Hermitian symmetric space](wikipedia://en/4127357).
- The [wonderful compactification](wikipedia://en/37580247) of a quotient of algebraic groups.
- The compactifications that are simultaneously convex subsets in a locally convex space are called [convex compactifications](wikipedia://en/67432243), their additional linear structure allowing e.g. for developing a differential calculus and more advanced considerations e.g. in relaxation in variational calculus or optimization theory.[^o18]
