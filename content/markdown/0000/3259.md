# Amicable numbers

*Image: Amicable numbers rods 220 and 284.png.* Demonstration with [Cuisenaire rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisenaire_rods) of the amicability of the pair of numbers (220,284), the first of the series.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Amicable numbers"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **amicable numbers** are two different [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) related in such a way that the [sum](wikipedia://en/61338) of the [proper divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_divisor) of each is equal to the other number. That is, *s*(*a*)=*b* and *s*(*b*)=*a*, where *s*(*n*)=σ(*n*) − *n* is equal to the sum of positive divisors of *n* except *n* itself (see also [divisor function](wikipedia://en/407249)).

:::

The smallest pair of amicable numbers is ([220](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/220_%28number%29), [284](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/284_%28number%29)). They are amicable because the proper divisors of 220 are 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 11, 20, 22, 44, 55 and 110, of which the sum is 284; and the proper divisors of 284 are 1, 2, 4, 71 and 142, of which the sum is 220.

The first ten amicable pairs are: (220, 284), (1184, 1210), (2620, 2924), (5020, 5564), (6232, 6368), (10744, 10856), (12285, 14595), (17296, 18416), (63020, 76084), and (66928, 66992) . It is unknown if there are infinitely many pairs of amicable numbers.

A pair of amicable numbers constitutes an [aliquot sequence](wikipedia://en/486266) of [period](wikipedia://en/810250) 2. A related concept is that of a [perfect number](wikipedia://en/23670), which is a number that equals the sum of *its own* proper divisors, in other words a number which forms an aliquot sequence of period 1. Numbers that are members of an aliquot sequence with period greater than 2 are known as [sociable numbers](wikipedia://en/332307).

## History {#History}

::: {#o3 .problem cue=unsolved}

Are there infinitely many amicable numbers?

:::

Amicable numbers were known to the [Pythagoreans](wikipedia://en/291170), who credited them with many mystical properties. A general formula by which some of these numbers could be derived was invented circa 850 by the [Iraqi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraqi_people) mathematician [Thābit ibn Qurra](wikipedia://en/207547) (826–901). Other [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) mathematicians who studied amicable numbers are [al-Majriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maslamah_Ibn_Ahmad_al-Majriti) (died 1007), [al-Baghdadi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Tahir_al-Baghdadi) (980–1037), and [al-Fārisī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamāl_al-Dīn_al-Fārisī) (1260–1320). The [Iranian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran) mathematician [Muhammad Baqir Yazdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_Baqir_Yazdi) (16th century) discovered the pair (9363584, 9437056), though this has often been attributed to [Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525).[^o4] Much of the work of [Eastern mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam) in this area has been forgotten.

Thābit ibn Qurra's formula was rediscovered by [Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) (1601–1665) and [Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) (1596–1650), to whom it is sometimes ascribed, and extended by [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) (1707–1783). It was extended further by [Borho](wikipedia://en/37392549) in 1972. Fermat and Descartes also rediscovered pairs of amicable numbers known to Arab mathematicians. Euler also discovered dozens of new pairs.[^o15] The second smallest pair, (1184, 1210), was discovered in 1867 by 16-year-old B. Nicolò I. Paganini (not to be confused with the composer and violinist), having been overlooked by earlier mathematicians.[^o5][^o6]

## Rules for generation {#Rules_for_generation}

While these rules do generate some pairs of amicable numbers, many other pairs are known, so these rules are by no means comprehensive.

In particular, the two rules below produce only even amicable pairs, so they are of no interest for the open problem of finding amicable pairs coprime to 210 = 2·3·5·7, while over 1000 pairs coprime to 30 = 2·3·5 are known \[García, Pedersen & te Riele (2003), Sándor & Crstici (2004)\].

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Thābit ibn Qurrah theorem"}

### Thābit ibn Qurrah theorem {#Thābit_ibn_Qurrah_theorem}

The **Thābit ibn Qurrah theorem** is a method for discovering amicable numbers invented in the 9th century by the [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Thābit ibn Qurrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thābit_ibn_Qurrah).[^o12]

It states that if

$$\begin{aligned} p &= 3 \times 2^{n-1} - 1, \\ q &= 3 \times 2^{n} - 1, \\ r &= 9 \times 2^{2n - 1} - 1, \end{aligned}$$

where $n > 1$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) and $p, q, r$ are [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), then $2^{n} \times p \times q$ and $2^{n} \times r$ are a pair of amicable numbers. This formula gives the pairs $(220, 284)$ for $n = 2$, $(17296, 18416)$ for $n = 4$, and $(9363584, 9437056)$ for $n = 7$, but no other such pairs are known. Numbers of the form $3 \times 2^{n} - 1$ are known as [Thabit numbers](wikipedia://en/1817053). In order for Ibn Qurrah's formula to produce an amicable pair, two consecutive Thabit numbers must be prime; this severely restricts the possible values of $n$.

To establish the theorem, Thâbit ibn Qurra proved nine [lemmas](wikipedia://en/18634) divided into two groups. The first three lemmas deal with the determination of the aliquot parts of a [natural integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_integer). The second group of lemmas deals more specifically with the formation of perfect, abundant and deficient numbers.[^o12]

:::

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Euler rule"}

### Euler's rule {#Euler's_rule}

*Euler's rule* is a generalization of the Thâbit ibn Qurra theorem. It states that if

$$\begin{aligned} p &= (2^{n-m} + 1) \times 2^m - 1, \\ q &= (2^{n-m} + 1) \times 2^n - 1, \\ r &= (2^{n-m} + 1)^2 \times 2^{m+n} - 1, \end{aligned}$$

where $n > m > 0$ are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and $p, q, r$ are [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), then $2^{n} \times p \times q$ and $2^{n} \times r$ are a pair of amicable numbers.

Note that

$$\begin{aligned} p q &= r - (2^{n-m} + 1) (2^n + 2^m) + 2 \\ pq &= r - ((2^{n-m} + 1) (2^n + 2^m) - 2) \\ pq &= r - (p + q) \end{aligned}$$

thus $pq + (p+q) = r$

Thābit ibn Qurra's theorem corresponds to the case $m = n - 1$. Euler's rule creates additional amicable pairs for $(m,n) = (1,8), (29,40)$ with no others being known. Euler (1747 & 1750) overall found 58 new pairs increasing the number of pairs that were then known to 61.[^o15][^o16]

:::

## Regular pairs {#Regular_pairs}

Let ($m$, $n$) be a pair of amicable numbers with $m < n$, and write $m = gM$ and $n = gN$ where $g$ is the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) of $m$ and $n$. If $M$ and $N$ are both [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to $g$ and [square free](wikipedia://en/29525) then the pair ($m$, $n$) is said to be **regular** ; otherwise, it is called **irregular** or **exotic**. If ($m$, $n$) is regular and $M$ and $N$ have $i$ and $j$ prime factors respectively, then $(m, n)$ is said to be of **type** $(i, j)$.

For example, with $(m, n) = (220, 284)$, the greatest common divisor is $4$ and so $M = 55$ and $N = 71$. Therefore, $(220, 284)$ is regular of type $(2, 1)$.

## Twin amicable pairs {#Twin_amicable_pairs}

An amicable pair $(m, n)$ is twin if there are no integers between $m$ and $n$ belonging to any other amicable pair .

## Other results {#Other_results}

In every known case, the numbers of a pair are either both [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_numbers) or both odd. It is not known whether an even-odd pair of amicable numbers exists, but if it does, the even number must either be a square number or twice one, and the odd number must be a square number. However, amicable numbers where the two members have different smallest prime factors do exist: there are seven such pairs known.[^o20] Also, every known pair shares at least one common prime [factor](wikipedia://en/49492). It is not known whether a pair of [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) amicable numbers exists, though if any does, the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of the two must be greater than 10<sup>67</sup>.[^o21][^o22] Also, a pair of co-prime amicable numbers cannot be generated by Thabit's formula (above), nor by any similar formula.

In 1955 [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) showed that the density of amicable numbers, relative to the positive integers, was 0.[^o23]

In 1968 [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) noted that most even amicable pairs have sums divisible by 9,[^o24] and that a rule for characterizing the exceptions was obtained.[^o25]

According to the sum of amicable pairs conjecture, as the number of the [amicable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeis:A360054) numbers approaches infinity, the percentage of the sums of the amicable pairs divisible by ten approaches 100% .

Although all amicable pairs up to 10,000 are even pairs, the proportion of odd amicable pairs increases steadily towards higher numbers, and presumably there are more of them than of the even amicable pairs (sequence [A360054](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeis:A360054) in [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeis:)).

There are amicable pairs where the sum of one number from the first pair and one number from the second pair equals the sum of the remaining two numbers, e.g. 67212 = 220 + 66992 = 284 + 66928 where (220, 284) and (66928, 66992) are two amicable pairs (sequence [A359334](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeis:A359334) in [OEIS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oeis:)).

[Gaussian integer](wikipedia://en/48628) amicable pairs exist,[^o26][^o27] e.g. s(8008 + 3960*i*) = 4232 − 8280*i* and s(4232 − 8280*i*) = 8008 + 3960*i*.[^o28]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

::: {#o32 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Amicable quadruple"}

### Amicable tuples {#Amicable_tuples}

Amicable numbers $(m, n)$ satisfy $\sigma(m)-m=n$ and $\sigma(n)-n=m$ which can be written together as $\sigma(m)=\sigma(n)=m+n$. This can be generalized to larger tuples, say $(n_1,n_2,\ldots,n_k)$, where we require

$$\sigma(n_1)=\sigma(n_2)= \dots =\sigma(n_k) = n_1+n_2+ \dots +n_k$$

For example, (1980, 2016, 2556) is an [amicable triple](wikipedia://en/59747263) , and (3270960, 3361680, 3461040, 3834000) is an amicable quadruple .

Amicable [multisets](wikipedia://en/305303) are defined analogously and generalizes this a bit further .

:::

### Sociable numbers {#Sociable_numbers}

Sociable numbers are the numbers in cyclic lists of numbers (with a length greater than 2) where each number is the sum of the proper divisors of the preceding number. For example, $1264460 \mapsto 1547860 \mapsto 1727636 \mapsto 1305184 \mapsto 1264460 \mapsto\dots$ are sociable numbers of order 4.

#### Searching for sociable numbers {#Searching_for_sociable_numbers}

The [aliquot sequence](wikipedia://en/486266) can be represented as a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986), $G_{n,s}$, for a given integer $n$, where $s(k)$ denotes the sum of the proper divisors of $k$.[^o35] [Cycles](wikipedia://en/168609) in $G_{n,s}$ represent [sociable numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociable_numbers) within the interval $[1,n]$. Two special cases are loops that represent [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_numbers) and cycles of length two that represent [amicable pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amicable_pairs).

:::
