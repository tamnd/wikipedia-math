# Chinese numerals

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chinese numerals"}

**Chinese numerals** are words and characters used to denote [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) in [written Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Written_Chinese). Speakers of Chinese languages use three written [numeral systems](wikipedia://en/21170): the international system of [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786), and two indigenous systems ('small writing' 小写, and 'big writing' 大写).

:::

The more familiar indigenous system is based on [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters) that correspond to [numerals in the spoken language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_%28linguistics%29). These may be shared with other languages of the [Chinese cultural sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_cultural_sphere) such as [Korean](wikipedia://en/994834), [Japanese](wikipedia://en/146615), and [Vietnamese](wikipedia://en/40568016). Most people and institutions in China primarily use the Arabic or mixed Arabic-Chinese systems for convenience, with traditional Chinese numerals used in finance, mainly for writing amounts on cheques, banknotes, some ceremonial occasions, some boxes, and on commercials.

The other indigenous system consists of the [Suzhou numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzhou_numerals), or *huama*, a positional system, the only surviving form of the [rod numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_numerals). These were once used by Chinese mathematicians, and later by merchants in Chinese markets, such as those in [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong) until the 1990s, but were gradually supplanted by Arabic numerals.

## Basic counting in Chinese {#Basic_counting_in_Chinese}

Traditionally, the Chinese numeral system is a [sign-value notation](wikipedia://en/5984280) consisting of the same [Chinese characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_characters) used by the [Chinese written language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_written_language) to write spoken numbers. Similar to spelling-out numbers in English (e.g., "one thousand nine hundred forty-five"), it is not an independent system *per se*. Since it reflects spoken language, it generally does not use the [positional system](wikipedia://en/437052) as in [Arabic numerals](wikipedia://en/1786), in the same way that spelling out numbers in English does not. In modern times, Chinese numerals may adopt a positional system in some rare contexts such as sequence numbers.

### Ordinary numerals {#Ordinary_numerals}

There are characters representing the numbers zero through nine, and other characters representing [powers of ten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powers_of_ten) such as tens, hundreds, thousands, [ten thousands](wikipedia://en/20088) and hundred millions. There are two sets of characters for ordinary Chinese numerals: one for everyday writing, known as (), and another for use in commercial, accounting or financial contexts, known as ().

#### Financial numerals {#Financial_numerals}

The financial numerals were developed by [Wu Zetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Zetian) (690–705) and were further refined by the [Hongwu Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hongwu_Emperor) (1328–1398).[^o6] They were adopted because the characters used for writing everyday numerals are geometrically simple, so simply using those numerals cannot prevent forgeries in the same way spelling numbers out in English would.[^o7] A forger could easily change the characters (30) to (5000) by adding a few strokes. That would not be possible when writing using the financial characters (30) and (5000). They are also referred to as "banker's numerals" or "anti-fraud numerals". For the same reason, rod numerals were never used in commercial records. Outside China, the financial numerals were adopted [in Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_financial_numerals) and [in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_financial_numerals) for similar purposes.

### SI prefixes {#SI_prefixes}

In the People's Republic of China, the early translation for the [SI prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_prefix) in 1981 was different from those used today. The larger (, , , , ) and smaller Chinese numerals (, , , , ) were defined as translation for the SI prefixes as *mega*, *giga*, *tera*, *peta*, *exa*, *micro*, *nano*, *pico*, *femto*, *atto*, resulting in the creation of yet more values for each numeral.[^o24]

The Republic of China (Taiwan) defined as the translation for *mega* and as the translation for *tera*. This translation is widely used in official documents, academic communities, informational industries, etc. However, the civil broadcasting industries sometimes use to represent "[megahertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megahertz)".

### Usage {#Usage}

[Chinese grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_grammar) requires the use of [classifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classifier_%28linguistics%29) (measure words) when a numeral is used together with a noun to express a quantity. For example, "three people" is expressed as , "three ( particle) person", where / is a [classifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classifier_%28linguistics%29). There exist many [different classifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Chinese_classifiers), for use with different sets of nouns, although / is the most common, and may be used informally in place of other classifiers.

Chinese uses [cardinal numbers](wikipedia://en/6173) in certain situations in which English would use [ordinals](wikipedia://en/26547932). For example, (literally "three [story/storey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storey)") means "third floor" ("second floor" in British Storey). Likewise, (literally "twenty-one century") is used for "21st century".[^o51]

Numbers of years are commonly spoken as a sequence of digits, as in ("two zero zero one") for the year 2001.[^o52] Names of months and days (in the Western system) are also expressed using numbers: ("one month") for January, etc.; and ("week one") for Monday, etc. There is only one exception: Sunday is , or informally , both literally "week day". When meaning "week", "" and "" are interchangeable. "" or "" means "day of worship". [Chinese Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Catholics) call Sunday "" , "Lord's day".[^o53]

Full dates are usually written in the format 2001年1月20日 for January 20, 2001 (using "year", "month", and "day") – all the numbers are read as cardinals, not ordinals, with no leading zeroes, and the year is read as a sequence of digits. For brevity the , and may be dropped to give a date composed of just numbers. For example "6-4" in Chinese is "six-four", short for "month six, day four" i.e. June Fourth, a common Chinese shorthand for the [1989 Tiananmen Square protests and massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989_Tiananmen_Square_protests_and_massacre) (because of the violence that occurred on June 4). For another example 67, in Chinese is sixty seven, short for year nineteen sixty seven, a common Chinese shorthand for the [1967 Hong Kong riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_Hong_Kong_riots).

## Counting rod and Suzhou numerals {#Counting_rod_and_Suzhou_numerals}

*Image: Chounumerals.svg.* Counting rod numerals

In the same way that [Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657) were standard in ancient and medieval Europe for mathematics and commerce, the Chinese formerly used the [rod numerals](wikipedia://en/26661047), which is a positional system. The Suzhou numerals () system is a variation of the [Southern Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_dynasty) rod numerals. Nowadays, the *huāmǎ* system is only used for displaying prices in Chinese markets or on traditional handwritten invoices.

There is a common method of using of one hand to signify the numbers one to ten. While the five digits on one hand can easily express the numbers one to five, six to ten have special signs that can be used in commerce or day-to-day communication.

## Historical use of numerals in China {#Historical_use_of_numerals_in_China}

*Image: Shang numerals.jpg.* Shang oracle bone numerals of 14th century B.C.[^o57]

*Image: 西周金文.jpg.* West Zhou dynasty bronze script

*Image: Rod numeral positioning.JPG.* Counting rod numeral example from the [Yongle Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongle_Encyclopedia) showing the number 71,824

*Image: Counting board.jpg.* Japanese counting board with grids

Most Chinese numerals of later periods were descendants of the [Shang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shang_dynasty) oracle numerals of the 14th century BC. The [oracle bone script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle_bone_script) numerals were found on tortoise shell and animal bones. In early civilizations, the Shang were able to express any numbers, however large, with only nine symbols and a counting board though it was still not positional.[^o58]

Some of the bronze script numerals such as 1, 2, 3, 4, 10, 11, 12, and 13 became part of the system of [rod numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_numerals).

In this system, horizontal rod numbers are used for the tens, thousands, hundred thousands etc. It is written in *[Sunzi Suanjing](wikipedia://en/30291690)* that "one is vertical, ten is horizontal".[^o59]

| *Image: Counting rod v7.png.* | *Image: Counting rod h1.png.* | *Image: Counting rod v8.png.* | *Image: Counting rod h2.png.* | *Image: Counting rod v4.png.* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | 1 | 8 | 2 | 4 |

The counting rod numerals system has place value and decimal numerals for computation, and was used widely by Chinese merchants, mathematicians and astronomers from the [Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty) to the 16th century.

[Alexander Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Wylie_%28missionary%29), Christian missionary to China, in 1853 already refuted the notion that "the Chinese numbers were written in words at length", and stated that in ancient China, calculation was carried out by means of counting rods, and "the written character is evidently a rude presentation of these". After being introduced to the rod numerals, he said "Having thus obtained a simple but effective system of figures, we find the Chinese in actual use of a method of notation depending on the theory of local value \[i.e. place-value\], several centuries before such theory was understood in Europe, and while yet the science of numbers had scarcely dawned among the Arabs."[^o60]

During the [Ming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ming_dynasty) and [Qing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qing_dynasty) dynasties (after Arabic numerals were introduced into China), some Chinese mathematicians used Chinese numeral characters as positional system digits. After the Qing period, both the Chinese numeral characters and the Suzhou numerals were replaced by Arabic numerals in mathematical writings.

## Cultural influences {#Cultural_influences}

Traditional Chinese numeric characters are also used in [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) and [Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korea) and were used in [Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam) before the 20th century. In vertical text, using characters for numbers is the norm, while in horizontal text, Arabic numerals are most common. Chinese numeric characters are also used in much the same formal or decorative fashion that Roman numerals are in Western cultures. Chinese numerals may appear together with Arabic numbers on the same sign or document.
