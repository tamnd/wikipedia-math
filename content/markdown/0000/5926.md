# Computation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Computation"}

A **computation** is any type of [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) or non-arithmetic [calculation](wikipedia://en/239121) that is well-defined.[^o2][^o3] Common examples of computation are [mathematical equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_equation) solving and the [execution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Execution_%28computing%29) of computer [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms).

:::

Mechanical or electronic devices (or, [historically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_computing_hardware), people) that perform computations are known as *[computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer)*. [Computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) is an academic field that involves the study of computation.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

The notion that mathematical statements should be 'well-defined' had been argued by mathematicians since at least the [1600s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1600s_%28decade%29),[^o5] but agreement on a suitable definition proved elusive.[^o6] A candidate definition was proposed independently by several mathematicians in the 1930s.[^o7] The best-known variant was formalised by the mathematician [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208), who defined a well-defined statement or calculation as any statement that could be expressed in terms of the initialisation parameters of a [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403).[^o8] Other (mathematically equivalent) definitions include [Alonzo Church](wikipedia://en/49731)'s *[lambda-definability](wikipedia://en/18203)*, [Herbrand](wikipedia://en/1074105)-[Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel)-[Kleene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleene)'s *[general recursiveness](wikipedia://en/26469)* and [Emil Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Post)'s *1-definability*.[^o7]

Today, any formal statement or calculation that exhibits this quality of well-definedness is termed **computable**, while the statement or calculation itself is referred to as a **computation**.

Turing's definition apportioned "well-definedness" to a very large class of mathematical statements, including all well-formed [algebraic statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations), and all statements written in modern computer programming languages.[^o9]

Despite the widespread uptake of this definition, there are some mathematical concepts that have no well-defined characterisation under this definition. This includes [the halting problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_halting_problem) and [the busy beaver game](wikipedia://en/67911). It remains an open question as to whether there exists a more powerful definition of 'well-defined' that is able to capture both computable and 'non-computable' statements.[^o10][^o11]

Some examples of mathematical statements that are computable include:

- All statements characterised in modern programming languages, including [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++), [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29), and [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29)[^o9]
- All calculations carried by an electronic [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer), [calculator](wikipedia://en/7593) or [abacus](wikipedia://en/655)
- All calculations carried out on an [analytical engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_engine)
- All calculations carried out on a [Turing Machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Machine)
- The majority of mathematical statements and calculations given in maths textbooks

Some examples of mathematical statements that are *not* computable include:

- Calculations or statements which are ill-defined, such that they cannot be unambiguously encoded into a Turing machine: ("Paul loves me twice as much as Joe").
- Problem statements that appear to be well-defined, but for which it can be proved that no Turing machine exists to solve them (such as [the halting problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_halting_problem))

Computation can be seen as a purely physical process occurring inside a closed [physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) called a [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer). Turing's 1937 proof, *[On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Computable_Numbers,_with_an_Application_to_the_Entscheidungsproblem)*, demonstrated that there is a formal equivalence between computable statements and particular physical systems, commonly called [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computers). Examples of such physical systems are: [Turing machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machines), human mathematicians following strict rules, [digital computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_computer), [mechanical computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_computer), [analog computers](wikipedia://en/2428) and others.

## Alternative accounts of computation {#Alternative_accounts_of_computation}

### The mapping account {#The_mapping_account}

An alternative account of computation is found throughout the works of [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233) and others. [Peter Godfrey-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Godfrey-Smith) has dubbed this the "simple mapping account."[^o14] [Gualtiero Piccinini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gualtiero_Piccinini)'s summary of this account states that a physical system can be said to perform a specific computation when there is a mapping between the state of that system and the computation such that the "microphysical states \[of the system\] mirror the state transitions between the computational states."[^o15]

### The semantic account {#The_semantic_account}

Philosophers such as [Jerry Fodor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerry_Fodor)[^o17] have suggested various accounts of computation with the restriction that [semantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic) content be a necessary condition for computation (that is, what differentiates an arbitrary physical system from a computing system is that the operands of the computation represent something). This notion attempts to prevent the logical abstraction of the mapping account of [pancomputationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_physics#Pancomputationalism_or_the_computational_universe_theory), the idea that everything can be said to be computing everything.

### The mechanistic account {#The_mechanistic_account}

[Gualtiero Piccinini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gualtiero_Piccinini) proposes an account of computation based on [mechanical philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_philosophy). It states that physical computing systems are types of mechanisms that, by design, perform physical computation, or the manipulation (by a functional mechanism) of a "medium-independent" vehicle according to a rule. "Medium-independence" requires that the property can be instantiated by multiple realizers and multiple mechanisms, and that the inputs and outputs of the mechanism also be [multiply realizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_realizability). In short, medium-independence allows for the use of physical variables with properties other than voltage (as in typical digital computers); this is imperative in considering other types of computation, such as that which occurs in the [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain) or in a [quantum computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computer). A rule, in this sense, provides a mapping among inputs, outputs, and internal states of the physical computing system.[^o19]

## Mathematical models {#Mathematical_models}

In the [theory of computation](wikipedia://en/30402), a diversity of mathematical models of computation has been developed. Typical mathematical [models of computers](wikipedia://en/1773278) are the following:

- State models including [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403), [pushdown automaton](wikipedia://en/24510), [finite-state automaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-state_automaton), and [PRAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_random_access_machine)
- Functional models including [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203)
- Logical models including [logic programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_programming)
- Concurrent models including [actor model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actor_model) and [process calculi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_calculi)

Giunti calls the models studied by computation theory *computational systems,* and he argues that all of them are mathematical [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) with discrete time and discrete state space.[^o22] He maintains that a computational system is a complex object which consists of three parts. First, a mathematical dynamical system $DS$ with discrete time and discrete state space; second, a computational setup $H=\left(F, B_F\right)$, which is made up of a theoretical part $F$, and a real part $B_F$; third, an interpretation $I_{DS,H}$, which links the dynamical system $DS$ with the setup $H$.[^o23]
