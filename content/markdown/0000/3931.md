# Binary relation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binary relation"}

*Image: Illustration of a binary relationship R.svg.* An example of a binary relation R between two finite sets of [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers), A and B. Note that R is a subset of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095), A *×* B. In this example, R = {(a, b) ∈ A *×* B: a \< b}.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **binary relation** associates some elements of one [set](wikipedia://en/26691) called the *domain* with some elements of another set (possibly the same) called the *[codomain](wikipedia://en/50264)*.[^o2] Precisely, a binary relation over sets $X$ and $Y$ is a set of [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) $(x,y)$, where $x$ is an element of $X$ and $y$ is an element of $Y$.[^o3] It encodes the common concept of relation: an element $x$ is *related* to an element $y$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the pair $(x, y)$ belongs to the set of ordered pairs that defines the binary relation.

:::

An example of a binary relation is the "[divides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divides)" relation over the set of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) $\mathbb{P}$ and the set of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $\mathbb{Z}$, in which each prime $p$ is related to each integer $z$ that is a [multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisibility) of $p$, but not to an integer that is not a [multiple](wikipedia://en/3912709) of $p$. In this relation, for instance, the prime number $2$ is related to numbers such as $-4$, $0$, $6$, $10$, but not to $1$ or $9$, just as the prime number $3$ is related to $0$, $6$, and $9$, but not to $4$ or $13$.

A binary relation is called a [homogeneous relation](wikipedia://en/12987179) when $X = Y$. A binary relation is also called a *heterogeneous relation* when it is not necessary that $X = Y$.

Binary relations, and especially homogeneous relations, are used in many branches of mathematics to model a wide variety of concepts. These include, among others:

- the "[is greater than](wikipedia://en/89489)", "[is equal to](wikipedia://en/90446)", and "divides" relations in [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118);
- the "[is congruent to](wikipedia://en/39330)" relation in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446);
- the "is adjacent to" relation in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401);
- the "is [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) to" relation in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).

A [function](wikipedia://en/185427) may be defined as a binary relation that meets additional constraints.[^o4] Binary relations are also heavily used in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science).

A binary relation over sets $X$ and $Y$ can be identified with an element of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $X \times Y.$ Since a powerset is a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) for [set inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_inclusion) ($\subseteq$), relations can be manipulated using set operations ([union](wikipedia://en/47949), [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429), and [complementation](wikipedia://en/54347)) and [algebra of sets](wikipedia://en/961805).

In some systems of [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory), relations are extended to [classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_%28mathematics%29), which are generalizations of sets. This extension is needed for, among other things, modeling the concepts of "is an element of" or "is a subset of" in set theory, without running into logical inconsistencies such as [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095).

A binary relation is the most studied special case $n = 2$ of an [$n$-ary relation](wikipedia://en/19509) over sets $X_1, \dots, X_n$, which is a subset of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $X_1 \times \cdots \times X_n.$[^o3]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given sets $X$ and $Y$, the Cartesian product $X \times Y$ is defined as $\{ (x, y) \mid x \in X \text{ and } y \in Y \},$ and its elements are called [*ordered pairs*.](wikipedia://en/22362)

A binary relation $R$ over sets $X$ and $Y$ is a subset of $X \times Y.$[^o7][^o8] The set $X$ is called the domain[^o3] or set of departure of $R$, and the set $Y$ the codomain or set of destination of $R$. In order to specify the choices of the sets $X$ and $Y$, some authors define a binary relation or correspondence as an ordered triple $(X, Y, G)$, where $G$ is a subset of $X \times Y$ called the graph of the binary relation. The statement $(x, y) \in R$ reads "$x$ is $R$-related to $y$" and is denoted by $xRy$.[^o9][^o10][^o11] The domain of definition or active domain[^o3] of $R$ is the set of all $x$ such that $xRy$ for at least one $y$. The *[codomain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codomain_space) of definition*, active codomain,[^o3] image or range of $R$ is the set of all $y$ such that $xRy$ for at least one $x$. The field of $R$ is the union of its domain of definition and its codomain of definition.[^o12][^o13][^o14]

When $X = Y,$ a binary relation is called a [homogeneous relation](wikipedia://en/12987179) (or endorelation). To emphasize the fact that $X$ and $Y$ are allowed to be different, a binary relation is also called a **heterogeneous relation**.[^o15][^o16][^o17] The prefix *hetero* is from the Greek ἕτερος (*heteros*, "other, another, different").

A heterogeneous relation has been called a **rectangular relation**,[^o17] suggesting that it does not have the square-like symmetry of a homogeneous relation on a set where $A = B.$ Commenting on the development of binary relations beyond homogeneous relations, researchers wrote, "... a variant of the theory has evolved that treats relations from the very beginning as heterogeneous or rectangular, i.e. as relations where the normal case is that they are relations between different sets."[^o18]

The terms *correspondence*,[^o19] **dyadic relation** and **two-place relation** are synonyms for binary relation, though some authors use the term "binary relation" for any subset of a [Cartesian](wikipedia://en/24104095) product $X \times Y$ without reference to $X$ and $Y$, and reserve the term "correspondence" for a binary relation with reference to $X$ and $Y$.

In a binary relation, the order of the elements is important; if $x \neq y$ then $yRx$ can be true or false independently of $xRy$. For example, $3$ divides $9$, but $9$ does not divide $3$.

:::

## Operations {#Operations}

### Union {#Union}

If $R$ and $S$ are binary relations over sets $X$ and $Y$ then $R \cup S = \{ (x, y) \mid xRy \text{ or } xSy \}$ is the union relation of $R$ and $S$ over $X$ and $Y$.

The identity element is the empty relation, in which no $x$ is related to any $y$.

For example, $\leq$ is the union of $<$ and $=$, and $\geq$ is the union of $>$ and $=$.

### Intersection {#Intersection}

If $R$ and $S$ are binary relations over sets $X$ and $Y$ then $R \cap S = \{ (x, y) \mid xRy \text{ and } xSy \}$ is the intersection relation of $R$ and $S$ over $X$ and $Y$.

The identity element is the universal relation, in which every $x$ is related to every $y$.

For example, the relation "is divisible by 6" is the intersection of the relations "is divisible by 3" and "is divisible by 2".

### Composition {#Composition}

If $R$ is a binary relation over sets $X$ and $Y$, and $S$ is a binary relation over sets $Y$ and $Z$ then $S \circ R = \{ (x, z) \mid \text{ there exists } y \in Y \text{ such that } xRy \text{ and } ySz \}$ (also denoted by $R; S$) is the composition relation of $R$ and $S$ over $X$ and $Z$.

If $X=Y=Z$, the identity element w.r.t. composition is the identity relation on $X$, in which $x \in X$ is related only to itself.

The order of $R$ and $S$ in the notation $S \circ R$ used here agrees with the standard notational order for [composition of functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_of_functions). For example, the composition (is parent of)$\circ$(is mother of) yields (is grandmother of), while the composition (is mother of)$\circ$(is parent of) yields (is maternal grandparent of). For the latter case, if $x$ is the parent of $y$ and $y$ is the mother of $z$, then $x$ is the maternal grandparent of $z$.

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Converse"}

### Converse {#Converse}

If $R$ is a binary relation over sets $X$ and $Y$ then $R^\textsf{T} = \{ (y, x) \mid xRy \}$ is the converse relation,[^o26] also called inverse relation,[^o27] of $R$ over $Y$ and $X$.

For example, $=$ is the converse of itself, as is $\neq$, and $<$ and $>$ are each other's converse, as are $\leq$ and $\geq.$ A binary relation is equal to its converse if and only if it is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459).

:::

### Complement {#Complement}

If $R$ is a binary relation over sets $X$ and $Y$ then $\bar{R} = \{ (x, y) \mid \neg xRy \}$ (also denoted by $\neg R$) is the complementary relation of $R$ over $X$ and $Y$.

For example, $=$ and $\neq$ are each other's complement, as are $\subseteq$ and $\not \subseteq$, $\supseteq$ and $\not \supseteq$, $\in$ and $\not \in$, and for [total orders](wikipedia://en/30330) also $<$ and $\geq$, and $>$ and $\leq$.

The complement of the [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020) $R^\textsf{T}$ is the converse of the complement: $\overline{R^\mathsf{T}} = \bar{R}^\mathsf{T}.$

If $X = Y,$ the complement has the following properties:

- If a relation is symmetric, then so is the complement.
- The complement of a reflexive relation is irreflexive—and vice versa.
- The complement of a [strict weak order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_weak_order) is a total preorder—and vice versa.

### Restriction {#Restriction}

If $R$ is a binary [homogeneous relation](wikipedia://en/12987179) over a set $X$ and $S$ is a subset of $X$ then $R_{\vert S} = \{ (x, y) \mid xRy \text{ and } x \in S \text{ and } y \in S \}$ is the restriction relation of $R$ to $S$ over $X$.

If $R$ is a binary relation over sets $X$ and $Y$ and if $S$ is a subset of $X$ then $R_{\vert S} = \{ (x, y) \mid xRy \text{ and } x \in S \}$ is the left-restriction relation of $R$ to $S$ over $X$ and $Y$.

If a relation is [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458), irreflexive, [symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459), [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/1176), [asymmetric](wikipedia://en/1229368), [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463), [total](wikipedia://en/12982585), [trichotomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichotomy_%28mathematics%29), a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order), [total order](wikipedia://en/30330), [strict weak order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_weak_order), [total preorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_weak_order#Total_preorders) (weak order), or an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259), then so too are its restrictions.

However, the transitive closure of a restriction is a subset of the restriction of the transitive closure, i.e., in general not equal. For example, restricting the relation "$x$ is parent of $y$" to females yields the relation "$x$ is mother of the woman $y$"; its transitive closure does not relate a woman with her paternal grandmother. On the other hand, the transitive closure of "is parent of" is "is ancestor of"; its restriction to females does relate a woman with her paternal grandmother.

Also, the various concepts of [completeness](wikipedia://en/549445) (not to be confused with being "total") do not carry over to restrictions. For example, over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) a property of the relation $\leq$ is that every [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) subset $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with an [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) in $\mathbb{R}$ has a [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) (also called supremum) in $\mathbb{R}.$ However, for the rational numbers this supremum is not necessarily rational, so the same property does not hold on the restriction of the relation $\leq$ to the rational numbers.

A binary relation $R$ over sets $X$ and $Y$ is said to be contained in a relation $S$ over $X$ and $Y$, written $R \subseteq S,$ if $R$ is a subset of $S$, that is, for all $x \in X$ and $y \in Y,$ if $xRy$, then $xSy$. If $R$ is contained in $S$ and $S$ is contained in $R$, then $R$ and $S$ are called equal written $R = S$. If $R$ is contained in $S$ but $S$ is not contained in $R$, then $R$ is said to be smaller than $S$, written $R \subsetneq S.$ For example, on the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), the relation $>$ is smaller than $\geq$, and equal to the composition $> \circ >$.

### Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

Binary relations over sets $X$ and $Y$ can be represented algebraically by [logical matrices](wikipedia://en/4109196) indexed by $X$ and $Y$ with entries in the [Boolean semiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_semiring) (addition corresponds to OR and multiplication to AND) where [matrix addition](wikipedia://en/125276) corresponds to union of relations, [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) corresponds to composition of relations (of a relation over $X$ and $Y$ and a relation over $Y$ and $Z$),[^o31] the [Hadamard product](wikipedia://en/34260496) corresponds to intersection of relations, the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888) corresponds to the empty relation, and the [matrix of ones](wikipedia://en/690246) corresponds to the universal relation. Homogeneous relations (when $X = Y$) form a [matrix semiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_semiring) (indeed, a [matrix semialgebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_semialgebra) over the Boolean semiring) where the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) corresponds to the identity relation.[^o32]

## Types of binary relations {#Types_of_binary_relations}

*Image: The four types of binary relations.png.* Examples of four types of binary relations over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438): one-to-one (in green), one-to-many (in blue), many-to-one (in red), many-to-many (in black).

Some important types of binary relations $R$ over sets $X$ and $Y$ are listed below.

Uniqueness properties:

- **Injective**[^o41] (also called **left-unique**[^o42]): for all $x, y \in X$ and all $z \in Y,$ if $xRz$ and $yRz$ then $x = y$. In other words, every element of the codomain has *at most* one [preimage](wikipedia://en/579311) element. For such a relation, $Y$ is called *a [primary key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_key)* of $R$.[^o3] For example, the green and blue binary relations in the diagram are injective, but the red one is not (as it relates both $-1$ and $1$ to $1$), nor the black one (as it relates both $-1$ and $1$ to $0$).
- **[Functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_relationship)**[^o41][^o43][^o44] (also called **right-unique**[^o42] or **univalent**[^o45]): for all $x \in X$ and all $y, z \in Y,$ if $xRy$ and $xRz$ then $y = z$. In other words, every element of the domain has *at most* one [image](wikipedia://en/579311) element. Such a binary relation is called a [partial function](wikipedia://en/23577) or partial mapping.[^o46] For such a relation, $\{ X \}$ is called a primary key of $R$.[^o3] For example, the red and green binary relations in the diagram are functional, but the blue one is not (as it relates $1$ to both $1$ and $-1$), nor the black one (as it relates $0$ to both $-1$ and $1$).
- **One-to-one**: injective and functional. For example, the green binary relation in the diagram is one-to-one, but the red, blue and black ones are not.
- **One-to-many**: injective and not functional. For example, the blue binary relation in the diagram is one-to-many, but the red, green and black ones are not.
- **Many-to-one**: functional and not injective. For example, the red binary relation in the diagram is many-to-one, but the green, blue and black ones are not.
- **Many-to-many**: not injective nor functional. For example, the black binary relation in the diagram is many-to-many, but the red, green and blue ones are not.

Totality properties (only definable if the domain $X$ and codomain $Y$ are specified):

- **[Total](wikipedia://en/1482218)**[^o41] (also called **left-total**[^o42]): for all $x \in X$ there exists a $y \in Y$ such that $xRy$. In other words, every element of the domain has *at least* one image element. In other words, the domain of definition of $R$ is equal to $X$. This property, is different from the definition of [connected](wikipedia://en/57495962) (also called total by some authors) in [Properties](wikipedia://en/12987179#Properties). Such a binary relation is called a [multivalued function](wikipedia://en/222367). For example, the red and green binary relations in the diagram are total, but the blue one is not (as it does not relate $-1$ to any real number), nor the black one (as it does not relate $2$ to any real number). As another example, $>$ is a total relation over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). But it is not a total relation over the positive integers, because there is no $y$ in the positive integers such that $1 > y$.[^o47] However, $<$ is a total relation over the positive integers, the rational numbers and the real numbers. Every reflexive relation is total: for a given $x$, choose $y = x$.
- **Surjective**[^o41] (also called **right-total**[^o42]): for all $y \in Y$, there exists an $x \in X$ such that $xRy$. In other words, every element of the codomain has *at least* one preimage element. In other words, the codomain of definition of $R$ is equal to $Y$. For example, the green and blue binary relations in the diagram are surjective, but the red one is not (as it does not relate any real number to $-1$), nor the black one (as it does not relate any real number to $2$).

Uniqueness and totality properties (only definable if the domain $X$ and codomain $Y$ are specified):

- A **[function](wikipedia://en/185427)** (also called **mapping**[^o42]): a binary relation that is functional and total. In other words, every element of the domain has *exactly* one image element. For example, the red and green binary relations in the diagram are functions, but the blue and black ones are not.
  - An **[injection](wikipedia://en/45196)**: a function that is injective. For example, the green relation in the diagram is an injection, but the red one is not; the black and the blue relation is not even a function.
  - A **[surjection](wikipedia://en/27873)**: a function that is surjective. For example, the green relation in the diagram is a surjection, but the red one is not.
  - A **[bijection](wikipedia://en/1830142)**: a function that is injective and surjective. In other words, every element of the domain has *exactly* one image element and every element of the codomain has *exactly* one preimage element. For example, the green binary relation in the diagram is a bijection, but the red one is not.

If relations over proper classes are allowed:

- **Set-like** (also called **local**): for all $x \in X$, the [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of all $y \in Y$ such that $yRx$, i.e. $\{y \in Y, yRx\}$, is a set. For example, the relation $\in$ is set-like, and every relation on two sets is set-like.[^o48] The usual ordering \< over the class of [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932) is a set-like relation, while its inverse > is not.

## Sets versus classes {#Sets_versus_classes}

Certain mathematical "relations", such as "equal to", "subset of", and "member of", cannot be understood to be binary relations as defined above, because their domains and codomains cannot be taken to be sets in the usual systems of [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory). For example, to model the general concept of "equality" as a binary relation $=$, take the domain and codomain to be the "class of all sets", which is not a set in the usual set theory.

In most mathematical contexts, references to the relations of equality, membership and subset are harmless because they can be understood implicitly to be restricted to some set in the context. The usual work-around to this problem is to select a "large enough" set $A$, that contains all the objects of interest, and work with the restriction $=_A$ instead of $=$. Similarly, the "subset of" relation $\subseteq$ needs to be restricted to have domain and codomain $P(A)$ (the power set of a specific set $A$): the resulting set relation can be denoted by $\subseteq_A.$ Also, the "member of" relation needs to be restricted to have domain $A$ and codomain $P(A)$ to obtain a binary relation $\in_A$ that is a set. [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) has shown that assuming $\in$ to be defined over all sets leads to a contradiction in [naive set theory](wikipedia://en/4944), see *[Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095)*.

Another solution to this problem is to use a set theory with proper classes, such as [NBG](wikipedia://en/528491) or [Morse–Kelley set theory](wikipedia://en/2693655), and allow the domain and codomain (and so the graph) to be [proper classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class): in such a theory, equality, membership, and subset are binary relations without special comment. (A minor modification needs to be made to the concept of the ordered triple $(X, Y, G)$, as normally a proper class cannot be a member of an ordered tuple; or of course one can identify the binary relation with its graph in this context.)[^o50] With this definition one can for instance define a binary relation over every set and its power set.

## Homogeneous relation {#Homogeneous_relation}

A **homogeneous relation** over a set $X$ is a binary relation over $X$ and itself, i.e. it is a subset of the Cartesian product $X \times X.$[^o17][^o52][^o53] It is also simply called a (binary) relation over $X$.

A homogeneous relation $R$ over a set $X$ may be identified with a [directed simple graph permitting loops](wikipedia://en/12401#Directed_graph), where $X$ is the vertex set and $R$ is the edge set (there is an edge from a vertex $x$ to a vertex $y$ if and only if $xRy$). The set of all homogeneous relations $\mathcal{B}(X)$ over a set $X$ is the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) $2^{X \times X}$ which is a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) augmented with the [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) of mapping of a relation to its [converse relation](wikipedia://en/3941020). Considering [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170) as a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) on $\mathcal{B}(X)$, it forms a [semigroup with involution](wikipedia://en/22679231).

Some important properties that a homogeneous relation $R$ over a set $X$ may have are:

- [Reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458): for all $x \in X,$ $xRx$. For example, $\geq$ is a reflexive relation but > is not.
- [Irreflexive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreflexive_relation): for all $x \in X,$ not $xRx$. For example, $>$ is an irreflexive relation, but $\geq$ is not.
- [Symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459): for all $x, y \in X,$ if $xRy$ then $yRx$. For example, "is a blood relative of" is a symmetric relation.
- [Antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/1176): for all $x, y \in X,$ if $xRy$ and $yRx$ then $x = y.$ For example, $\geq$ is an antisymmetric relation.[^o54]
- [Asymmetric](wikipedia://en/1229368): for all $x, y \in X,$ if $xRy$ then not $yRx$. A relation is asymmetric if and only if it is both antisymmetric and irreflexive.[^o55] For example, > is an asymmetric relation, but $\geq$ is not.
- [Transitive](wikipedia://en/200463): for all $x, y, z \in X,$ if $xRy$ and $yRz$ then $xRz$. A transitive relation is irreflexive if and only if it is asymmetric.[^o56] For example, "is ancestor of" is a transitive relation, while "is parent of" is not.
- [Connected](wikipedia://en/57495962): for all $x, y \in X,$ if $x \neq y$ then $xRy$ or $yRx$.
- [Strongly connected](wikipedia://en/57495962): for all $x, y \in X,$ $xRy$ or $yRx$.
- [Dense](wikipedia://en/6320997#Generalizations): for all $x, y \in X,$ if $xRy ,$ then some $z \in X$ exists such that $xRz$ and $zRy$.

A [partial order](wikipedia://en/23572#Formal_definition) is a relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. A [strict partial order](wikipedia://en/23572#Correspondence_of_strict_and_non-strict_partial_order_relations) is a relation that is irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive. A [total order](wikipedia://en/30330) is a relation that is reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive and connected.[^o57] A [strict total order](wikipedia://en/30330#Strict_total_order) is a relation that is irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive and connected. An [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) is a relation that is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. For example, "$x$ divides $y$" is a partial, but not a total order on [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) $\mathbb{N},$ "$x < y$" is a strict total order on $\mathbb{N},$ and "$x$ is parallel to $y$" is an equivalence relation on the set of all lines in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410).

All operations defined in section also apply to homogeneous relations. Beyond that, a homogeneous relation over a set $X$ may be subjected to closure operations like:

**[Reflexive closure](wikipedia://en/22603618)**

the smallest reflexive relation over $X$ containing $R$,

**[Transitive closure](wikipedia://en/292239)**

the smallest transitive relation over $X$ containing $R$,

**[Equivalence closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_closure)**

the smallest [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) over $X$ containing $R$.

## Calculus of relations {#Calculus_of_relations}

Developments in [algebraic logic](wikipedia://en/7870034) have facilitated usage of binary relations. The [calculus of relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_relations) includes the [algebra of sets](wikipedia://en/961805), extended by [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170) and the use of [converse relations](wikipedia://en/3941020). The inclusion $R \subseteq S,$ meaning that $aRb$ implies $aSb$, sets the scene in a [lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_%28order_theory%29) of relations. But since $P \subseteq Q \equiv (P \cap \bar{Q} = \varnothing ) \equiv (P \cap Q = P),$ the inclusion symbol is superfluous. Nevertheless, composition of relations and manipulation of the operators according to [Schröder rules](wikipedia://en/7472170#Schröder_rules), provides a calculus to work in the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $A \times B.$

In contrast to homogeneous relations, the [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170) operation is only a [partial function](wikipedia://en/23577). The necessity of matching target to source of composed relations has led to the suggestion that the study of heterogeneous relations is a chapter of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) as in the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), except that the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) of this category are relations. The objects of the category [Rel](wikipedia://en/5930730) are sets, and the relation-morphisms compose as required in a [category](wikipedia://en/53759).[^o59]

## Induced concept lattice {#Induced_concept_lattice}

Binary relations have been described through their induced [concept lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept_lattice): A **concept** $C \subset R$ satisfies two properties:

- The [logical matrix](wikipedia://en/4109196) of $C$ is the [outer product](wikipedia://en/103109) of logical vectors $C_{i j} = u_i v_j , \quad u, v$ [logical vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_vector).
- $C$ is maximal, not contained in any other outer product. Thus $C$ is described as a *non-enlargeable rectangle*.

For a given relation $R \subseteq X \times Y,$ the set of concepts, enlarged by their joins and meets, forms an "induced lattice of concepts", with inclusion $\sqsubseteq$ forming a [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582).

The [MacNeille completion theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacNeille_completion_theorem) (1937) (that any partial order may be embedded in a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777)) is cited in a 2013 survey article "Decomposition of relations on concept lattices".[^o61] The decomposition is

$R = f E g^\textsf{T}$, where $f$ and $g$ are [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), called mappings or left-total, functional relations in this context. The "induced concept lattice is isomorphic to the cut completion of the partial order $E$ that belongs to the minimal decomposition $(f, g, E)$ of the relation $R$."

Particular cases are considered below: $E$ total order corresponds to Ferrers type, and $E$ identity corresponds to difunctional, a generalization of [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on a set.

Relations may be ranked by the **Schein rank** which counts the number of concepts necessary to cover a relation.[^o62] Structural analysis of relations with concepts provides an approach for [data mining](wikipedia://en/42253).[^o63]

## Particular relations {#Particular_relations}

- *Proposition*: If $R$ is a [surjective relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective_relation) and $R^\mathsf{T}$ is its transpose, then $I \subseteq R^\textsf{T} R$ where $I$ is the $m \times m$ identity relation.
- *Proposition*: If $R$ is a [serial relation](wikipedia://en/12982585), then $I \subseteq R R^\textsf{T}$ where $I$ is the $n \times n$ identity relation.

::: {#o66 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Difunctional"}

### Difunctional {#Difunctional}

The idea of a difunctional relation is to partition objects by distinguishing attributes, as a generalization of the concept of an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). One way this can be done is with an intervening set $Z = \{ x, y, z, \ldots \}$ of [indicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indicator_%28research%29). The partitioning relation $R = F G^\textsf{T}$ is a [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170) using functional relations $F \subseteq A \times Z \text{ and } G \subseteq B \times Z.$ [Jacques Riguet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Riguet) named these relations **difunctional** since the composition $F G^\mathsf{T}$ involves functional relations, commonly called *partial functions*.

In 1950 Riguet showed that such relations satisfy the inclusion:[^o67]

$$R R^\textsf{T} R \subseteq R$$

In [automata theory](wikipedia://en/103356), the term **rectangular relation** has also been used to denote a difunctional relation. This terminology recalls the fact that, when represented as a [logical matrix](wikipedia://en/4109196), the columns and rows of a difunctional relation can be arranged as a [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464) with rectangular blocks of ones on the (asymmetric) main diagonal.[^o68] More formally, a relation $R$ on $X \times Y$ is difunctional if and only if it can be written as the union of Cartesian products $A_i \times B_i$, where the $A_i$ are a partition of a subset of $X$ and the $B_i$ likewise a partition of a subset of $Y$.[^o69]

Using the notation $\{y \mid xRy\} = xR$, a difunctional relation can also be characterized as a relation $R$ such that wherever $x_1 R$ and $x_2 R$ have a non-empty intersection, then these two sets coincide; formally $x_1 \cap x_2 \neq \varnothing$ implies $x_1 R = x_2 R.$[^o70]

In 1997 researchers found "utility of binary decomposition based on difunctional dependencies in [database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Database) management."[^o71] Furthermore, difunctional relations are fundamental in the study of [bisimulations](wikipedia://en/397571).[^o72]

In the context of homogeneous relations, a [partial equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/3450320) is difunctional.

:::

### Ferrers type {#Ferrers_type}

A [strict order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_order) on a set is a homogeneous relation arising in [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924). In 1951 [Jacques Riguet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Riguet) adopted the ordering of an [integer partition](wikipedia://en/104790), called a [Ferrers diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrers_diagram), to extend ordering to binary relations in general.[^o74]

The corresponding logical matrix of a general binary relation has rows which finish with a sequence of ones. Thus the dots of a Ferrer's diagram are changed to ones and aligned on the right in the matrix.

An algebraic statement required for a Ferrers type relation R is

$$R \bar{R}^\textsf{T} R \subseteq R.$$

If any one of the relations $R, \bar{R}, R^\textsf{T}$ is of Ferrers type, then all of them are. [^o75]

::: {#o77 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Contact relation"}

### Contact {#Contact}

Suppose $B$ is the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $A$, the set of all [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of $A$. Then a relation $g$ is a **contact relation** if it satisfies three properties:

1. $$\text{for all } x \in A, Y = \{ x \} \text{ implies } xgY.$$
1. $$Y \subseteq Z \text{ and } xgY \text{ implies } xgZ.$$
1. $$\text{for all } y \in Y, ygZ \text{ and } xgY \text{ implies } xgZ.$$

The [set membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_membership) relation, $\epsilon =$ "is an element of", satisfies these properties so $\epsilon$ is a contact relation. The notion of a general contact relation was introduced by [Georg Aumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Aumann) in 1970.[^o78][^o79]

In terms of the calculus of relations, sufficient conditions for a contact relation include

$$C^\textsf{T} \bar{C} \subseteq \ni \bar{C} \equiv C \overline{\ni \bar{C}} \subseteq C,$$

where $\ni$ is the converse of set membership ($\in$).[^o84]

:::

## Preorder R\\R {#Preorder_R\R}

Every relation $R$ generates a [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) $R \backslash R$ which is the [left residual](wikipedia://en/7472170#Quotients).[^o81] In terms of converse and complements, $R \backslash R \equiv \overline{R^\textsf{T} \bar{R}}.$ Forming the diagonal of $R^\textsf{T} \bar{R}$, the corresponding row of $R^{\textsf{T}}$ and column of $\bar{R}$ will be of opposite logical values, so the diagonal is all zeros. Then

$R^\textsf{T} \bar{R} \subseteq \bar{I} \implies I \subseteq \overline{R^\textsf{T} \bar{R}} = R \backslash R$, so that $R \backslash R$ is a [reflexive relation](wikipedia://en/200458).

To show [transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463), one requires that $(R\backslash R)(R\backslash R) \subseteq R \backslash R.$ Recall that $X = R \backslash R$ is the largest relation such that $R X \subseteq R.$ Then

$$R(R\backslash R) \subseteq R$$

$R(R\backslash R) (R\backslash R )\subseteq R$ (repeat)

$\equiv R^\textsf{T} \bar{R} \subseteq \overline{(R \backslash R)(R \backslash R)}$ (Schröder's rule)

$\equiv (R \backslash R)(R \backslash R) \subseteq \overline{R^\textsf{T} \bar{R}}$ (complementation)

$\equiv (R \backslash R)(R \backslash R) \subseteq R \backslash R.$ (definition)

The [inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_%28set_theory%29) relation Ω on the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $U$ can be obtained in this way from the [membership relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) $\in$ on subsets of $U$:

$\Omega = \overline{\ni \bar{\in}} = \in \backslash \in .$[^o84]

::: {#o83 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Fringe of a relation"}

## Fringe of a relation {#Fringe_of_a_relation}

Given a relation $R$, its **fringe** is the sub-relation defined as

$$\operatorname{fringe}(R) = R \cap \overline{R \bar{R}^\textsf{T} R}.$$

When $R$ is a partial identity relation, difunctional, or a block diagonal relation, then $\operatorname{fringe}(R) = R$. Otherwise the $\operatorname{fringe}$ operator selects a boundary sub-relation described in terms of its logical matrix: $\operatorname{fringe}(R)$ is the side diagonal if $R$ is an upper right triangular [linear order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_order) or [strict order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_order). $\operatorname{fringe}(R)$ is the block fringe if $R$ is irreflexive ($R \subseteq \bar{I}$) or upper right block triangular. $\operatorname{fringe}(R)$ is a sequence of boundary rectangles when $R$ is of Ferrers type.

On the other hand, $\operatorname{fringe}(R) = \emptyset$ when $R$ is a [dense](wikipedia://en/6320997), linear, strict order.[^o84]

:::
