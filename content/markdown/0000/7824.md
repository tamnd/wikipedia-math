# Colin Maclaurin

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Colin Maclaurin"}

**Colin Maclaurin** (; Cailean MacLabhruinn; February 1698 – 14 June 1746)[^o3] was a Scottish [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who made important contributions to [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923). He is also known for being a [child prodigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_prodigy) and holding the record for being the youngest professor. The Maclaurin series, a special case of the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448), is named after him.

:::

Owing to changes in [orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthography) since that time (his name was originally rendered as **M'Laurine**[^o5]), his surname is alternatively written **MacLaurin.**[^o6]

## Early life {#Early_life}

Maclaurin was born in [Kilmodan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilmodan), [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland) and was the youngest of three sons. His father, John Maclaurin, a minister, died when Maclaurin was in infancy, and his mother died when he was nine years of age. He was then raised by his uncle,[^o8] Daniel MacLaurin.[^o9]

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

At eleven, Maclaurin, a [child prodigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_prodigy) at the time, entered the [University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Glasgow). He graduated [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) three years later by defending a thesis on *the Power of Gravity,* and remained at Glasgow to study [divinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity_%28academic_discipline%29) until he was 19, when he was elected professor of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) in a ten-day competition at [Marischal College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marischal_College) and University in Aberdeen. This record as the world's youngest professor endured until February 2008, when [Alia Sabur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alia_Sabur) was appointed as a professor in South Korea for one year.[^o11]

In the vacations of 1719 and 1721, Maclaurin went to London, where he became acquainted with [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), [Benjamin Hoadly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Hoadly_%28physician%29), [Samuel Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Clarke), [Martin Folkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Folkes), and other philosophers. He was admitted as a member of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society).

In 1722, having provided a temporary replacement teacher for his class at Aberdeen, he travelled on the Continent as tutor to George Hume, the son of [Alexander Hume, 2nd Earl of Marchmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hume,_2nd_Earl_of_Marchmont). During their time in [Lorraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Lorraine), he wrote his essay on the percussion of bodies (*Demonstration des loix du choc des corps*), which gained the prize of the [Royal Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1724. Upon the death of his pupil at [Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montpellier), Maclaurin returned to Aberdeen.

In 1725, Maclaurin was appointed deputy to the mathematical professor at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh), James Gregory (brother of [David Gregory](wikipedia://en/703683) and nephew of the esteemed [James Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gregory_%28astronomer_and_mathematician%29)), upon the recommendation of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627). On 3 November of that year Maclaurin succeeded Gregory, and went on to raise the character of that university as a school of science. Newton was so impressed with Maclaurin that he had offered to pay his salary himself.

## Contributions to mathematics {#Contributions_to_mathematics}

*Image: Acta Eruditorum - I monete geometria, 1747 – BEIC 13417751 (cropped).jpg.* Illustration of critique of *De fluxionibus libri duo* published in [Acta Eruditorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acta_Eruditorum), 1747

Maclaurin used [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) to characterize maxima, minima, and points of inflection for infinitely differentiable functions in his *Treatise of Fluxions*. Maclaurin attributed the series to [Brook Taylor](wikipedia://en/210429), though the series was known before to [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [Gregory](wikipedia://en/220425). Nevertheless, Maclaurin received credit for his use of the series, and the Taylor series expanded around 0 is sometimes known as the *Maclaurin series*.[^o13]

*Image: Colin Maclaurin color.jpg.* Colin Maclaurin (1698–1746)

Maclaurin also made significant contributions to the gravitation attraction of ellipsoids, a subject that furthermore attracted the attention of d'Alembert, A.-C. Clairaut, Euler, Laplace, Legendre, Poisson and Gauss. Maclaurin showed that an oblate spheroid was a possible equilibrium in Newton's theory of gravity. The subject continues to be of scientific interest, and Nobel Laureate [Subramanyan Chandrasekhar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subramanyan_Chandrasekhar) dedicated a chapter of his book *Ellipsoidal Figures of Equilibrium* to [Maclaurin spheroids](wikipedia://en/3826733).[^o13] Maclaurin corresponded extensively with [Clairaut](wikipedia://en/196273), [Maupertuis](wikipedia://en/196221), and [d'Ortous de Mairan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_d'Ortous_de_Mairan).[^o14][^o15]

Independently from [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler) and using the same methods, Maclaurin discovered the [Euler–Maclaurin formula](wikipedia://en/9637). He used it to sum powers of [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389), derive [Stirling's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling's_formula), and to derive the Newton–Cotes numerical integration formulas which includes [Simpson's rule](wikipedia://en/219962) as a special case.[^o13]

Maclaurin contributed to the study of [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960), reducing many intractable integrals to problems of finding arcs for hyperbolas. His work was continued by d'Alembert and Euler, who gave a more concise approach.[^o13]

In his *Treatise of Algebra* (Ch. XII, Sect 86), published in 1748 two years after his death, Maclaurin proved a rule for solving square linear systems in the cases of 2 and 3 unknowns, and discussed the case of 4 unknowns.[^o16][^o17] This publication preceded by two years [Cramer](wikipedia://en/499331)'s publication of a generalization of the rule to *n* unknowns, now commonly known as [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008).

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

*Image: The grave of Colin Maclaurin, Greyfriars Kirkyard.jpg.* The grave of Colin Maclaurin, Greyfriars Kirkyard

*Image: Colin MacLaurin Road, Edinburgh.JPG.* Colin Maclaurin Road, Edinburgh

In 1733, Maclaurin married Anne Stewart, the daughter of Walter Stewart, the [Solicitor General for Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solicitor_General_for_Scotland), by whom he had seven children. His eldest son [John Maclaurin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maclaurin,_Lord_Dreghorn) studied law, was a [Senator of the College of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senator_of_the_College_of_Justice), and became Lord Dreghorn; he was also joint founder of the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh).[^o19]

Maclaurin actively opposed the [Jacobite rising of 1745](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobite_rising_of_1745) and superintended the operations necessary for the defence of Edinburgh against the Highland army. Maclaurin compiled a diary of his exertions against the Jacobites, both within and without the city.[^o20] When the Highland army entered the city, however, he fled to [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York), where he was invited to stay by the [Archbishop of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_York).

*Image: Maclaurin grave.jpg.* Memorial, Greyfriars Kirkyard, Edinburgh

On his journey south, Maclaurin fell from his horse, and the fatigue, anxiety, and cold to which he was exposed on that occasion laid the foundations of [dropsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropsy). He returned to Edinburgh after the [Jacobite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobitism) army marched south, but died soon after his return.

He is buried at [Greyfriars Kirkyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greyfriars_Kirkyard), [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh). The simple table stone is inscribed simply "C. M. Nat MDCXCVIII Ob MDCCXLVI" and stands close to the south-west corner of the church but is supplemented by a more wordy memorial on the outer wall of the church.

The mathematician and former [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT) President [Richard Cockburn Maclaurin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Cockburn_Maclaurin) was from the same family.

The Maclaurin Society (MacSoc), the Mathematics and Statistics Society at Glasgow University, is named in his honour.

Colin Maclaurin Road within Edinburgh University's [King's Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Buildings) complex is named in his honour.

## Notable works {#Notable_works}

Some of his important works are:

- *Geometria Organica* – 1720
- *De Linearum Geometricarum Proprietatibus* – 1720
- *Treatise on Fluxions* – 1742 (763 pages in two volumes. The first systematic exposition of Newton's methods.)
- *Treatise of Algebra* – 1748 (two years after his death.)
- *Account of Newton's Discoveries* – Incomplete upon his death and published in 1748[^o22]
- (1749). "Account of Sir Isaac Newton's philosophical discoveries". Laurent Durand. <https://gutenberg.beic.it/webclient/DeliveryManager?pid=743185>

Colin Maclaurin was the name used for the new Mathematics and Actuarial Mathematics and Statistics Building at [Heriot-Watt University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heriot-Watt_University), Edinburgh.
