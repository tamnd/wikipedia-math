# Conservation law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conservation law"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a **conservation law** states that a particular measurable property of an isolated [physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) does not change as the system evolves over time. Exact conservation laws include [conservation of mass-energy](wikipedia://en/422481#Conservation_of_mass_and_energy), [conservation of linear momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_linear_momentum), [conservation of angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum), and [conservation of electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_electric_charge). There are also many approximate conservation laws, which apply to such quantities as [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass), [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29),[^o2] [lepton number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton_number), [baryon number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon_number), [strangeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strangeness), [hypercharge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercharge), etc. These quantities are conserved in certain classes of physical processes, but not in all.

:::

A local conservation law is usually expressed mathematically as a [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793), a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) which gives a relation between the amount of the quantity and the "transport" of that quantity. It states that the amount of the conserved quantity at a point or within a volume can only change by the amount of the quantity which flows in or out of the volume.

From [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159), every differentiable [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) leads to a local conservation law.[^o3][^o4][^o5] Other conserved quantities can exist as well.

::: {#o7 .statement cue=named-heading name="Conservation laws as fundamental laws of nature"}

## Conservation laws as fundamental laws of nature {#Conservation_laws_as_fundamental_laws_of_nature}

Conservation laws are fundamental to our understanding of the physical world, in that they describe which processes can or cannot occur in nature. For example, the conservation law of energy states that the total quantity of energy in an isolated system does not change, though it may change form. In general, the total quantity of the property governed by that law remains unchanged during physical processes. With respect to classical physics, conservation laws include conservation of energy, mass (or matter), linear momentum, angular momentum, and electric charge. With respect to [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics), particles cannot be created or destroyed except in pairs, where one is ordinary and the other is an antiparticle. With respect to symmetries and invariance principles, three special conservation laws have been described, associated with inversion or reversal of space, time, and charge.

Conservation laws are considered to be fundamental [laws](wikipedia://en/244629) of nature, with broad application in physics, as well as in other fields such as chemistry, biology, geology, and engineering.

Most conservation laws are exact, or absolute, in the sense that they apply to all possible processes. Some conservation laws are partial, in that they hold for some processes but not for others.

One particularly important result concerning *local* conservation laws is [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159), which states that there is a one-to-one correspondence between each one of them and a *differentiable* [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) of the [Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe). For example, the local conservation of energy follows from the [uniformity of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-translation_symmetry) and the local [conservation of angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum) arises from the [isotropy](wikipedia://en/14865) of [space](wikipedia://en/27667),[^o3][^o8][^o5] i.e. because there is no preferred direction of space. Notably, there is no conservation law associated with [time-reversal](wikipedia://en/1937658), although more complex conservation laws combining time-reversal with [other symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPT_invariance) are known.

Accordingly, the conserved quantity, [CPT parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPT_parity), can usually not be meaningfully calculated or determined.

## Approximate laws {#Approximate_laws}

There are also **approximate** conservation laws. These are approximately true in particular situations, such as low speeds, short time scales, or certain interactions.

- [Conservation of (macroscopic) mechanical energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy#Conservation_of_mechanical_energy) (approximately true for processes close to free of dissipative forces like friction)
- [Conservation of (rest) mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass) (approximately true for [nonrelativistic speeds](wikipedia://en/26962))
- Conservation of [baryon number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon_number) (See [chiral anomaly](wikipedia://en/312229) and [sphaleron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphaleron))
- Conservation of [lepton number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton_number) (In the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641))
- Conservation of [flavor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavour_%28particle_physics%29) (violated by the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction))
- Conservation of [strangeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strangeness) (violated by the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction))
- Conservation of [space-parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29) (violated by the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction))
- Conservation of [charge-parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_conjugation) (violated by the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction))
- Conservation of [time-parity](wikipedia://en/151013) (violated by the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction))
- Conservation of [CP parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CP_symmetry) (violated by the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction))
- Conservation of [CPT symmetry](wikipedia://en/151040) (In the [Standard Model](wikipedia://en/47641)).

## Global and local conservation laws {#Global_and_local_conservation_laws}

The total amount of some conserved quantity in the universe could remain unchanged if an equal amount were to appear at one point *A* and simultaneously disappear from another separate point *B*. For example, an amount of energy could appear on Earth without changing the total amount in the Universe if the same amount of energy were to disappear from some other region of the Universe. This weak form of "global" conservation is really not a conservation law because it is not [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariant), so phenomena like the above do not occur in nature.[^o23][^o24] Due to [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), if the appearance of the energy at *A* and disappearance of the energy at *B* are simultaneous in one [inertial reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_reference_frame), they [will not be simultaneous](wikipedia://en/3406142) in other inertial reference frames moving with respect to the first. In a [moving frame](wikipedia://en/439082) one will occur before the other; either the energy at *A* will appear *before* or *after* the energy at *B* disappears. In both cases, during the interval energy will not be conserved.

A stronger form of conservation law requires that, for the amount of a conserved quantity at a point to change, there must be a flow, or *flux* of the quantity into or out of the point. For example, the amount of [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) at a point is never found to change without an [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) into or out of the point that carries the difference in charge. Since it only involves continuous *[local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_locality)* changes, this stronger type of conservation law is [Lorentz invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariant); a quantity conserved in one reference frame is conserved in all moving reference frames.[^o23][^o24] This is called a *local conservation* law.[^o23][^o24] Local conservation also implies global conservation; that the total amount of the conserved quantity in the Universe remains constant. All of the conservation laws listed above are local conservation laws. A local conservation law is expressed mathematically by a *[continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793)*, which states that the change in the quantity in a volume is equal to the total net "flux" of the quantity through the surface of the volume. The following sections discuss continuity equations in general.

:::

## Differential forms {#Differential_forms}

In [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918), the most general form of an exact conservation law is given by a [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793). For example, conservation of electric charge $q$ is

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = - \nabla \cdot \mathbf{j} \,$$

where $\nabla\cdot$ is the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) operator, $\rho$ is the density of $q$ (amount per unit volume), $\mathbf{j}$ is the flux of $q$ (amount crossing a unit area in unit time), and $t$ is time.

If we assume that the motion $\mathbf{u}$ of the charge is a continuous function of position and time, then

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{j} &= \rho \mathbf{u} \\ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} &= - \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{u}) \,. \end{aligned}$$

In one space dimension this can be put into the form of a homogeneous first-order [quasilinear](wikipedia://en/16678376) [hyperbolic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_equation):[^o26]p. 43

$$y_t + A(y) y_x = 0$$

where the dependent variable $y$ is called the *density* of a *conserved quantity*, and $A(y)$ is called the *[current Jacobian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_Jacobian)*, and the [subscript notation for partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/10265555#Partial_derivatives) has been employed. The more general inhomogeneous case:

$$y_t + A(y) y_x = s$$

is not a conservation equation but the general kind of [balance equation](wikipedia://en/21990785) describing a [dissipative system](wikipedia://en/237587). The dependent variable $y$ is called a *nonconserved quantity*, and the inhomogeneous term $s(y,x,t)$ is the *[source](wikipedia://en/8328)*, or [dissipation](wikipedia://en/164572). For example, balance equations of this kind are the momentum and energy [Navier-Stokes equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navier-Stokes_equations), or the [entropy balance](wikipedia://en/9891#Entropy_balance_equation_for_open_systems) for a general [isolated system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_system).

In the **one-dimensional space** a conservation equation is a first-order [quasilinear](wikipedia://en/16678376) [hyperbolic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_equation) that can be put into the *advection* form:

$$y_t + a(y) y_x = 0$$

where the dependent variable $y(x,t)$ is called the density of the *conserved* (scalar) quantity, and $a(y)$ is called the **current coefficient**, usually corresponding to the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) in the conserved quantity of a [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density) of the conserved quantity $j(y)$:[^o26]p. 43

$$a(y) = j_y (y)$$

In this case since the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) applies:

$$j_x = j_y (y) y_x = a(y) y_x$$

The conservation equation can be put into the current density form:

$$y_t + j_x (y) = 0$$

In a **space with more than one dimension** the former definition can be extended to an equation that can be put into the form:

$$y_t + \mathbf a(y) \cdot \nabla y = 0$$

where the *conserved quantity* is $y(\mathbf{r},t)$; $\cdot$ denotes the [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product); $\nabla$ is the [nabla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabla_symbol) operator, here indicating a [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461); and $a(y)$ is a vector of current coefficients, analogously corresponding to the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of a vector current density associated to the conserved quantity $\mathbf{j}(y)$:

$$y_t + \nabla \cdot \mathbf j(y) = 0$$

This is the case for the [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793):

$$\rho_t + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf u) = 0$$

Here the conserved quantity is the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass), with [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) $\rho(\mathbf{r},t)$ and current density $\rho\mathbf{u}$, identical to the [momentum density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum_conservation), while $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}, t)$ is the [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity).

In the **general case** a conservation equation can be also a system of this kind of equations (a [vector equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_equation)) in the form:[^o26]p. 43

$$\mathbf y_t + \mathbf A(\mathbf y) \cdot \nabla \mathbf y = \mathbf 0$$

where $\mathbf{y}$ is called the *conserved* (**vector**) quantity, $\nabla y$ is its [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461), $\mathbf{0}$ is the [zero vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_vector), and $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{y})$ is called the [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) of the current density. In fact as in the former scalar case, also in the vector case **A**(**y**) usually corresponding to the Jacobian of a [current density matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density_matrix) $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{y})$:

$$\mathbf A( \mathbf y) = \mathbf J_{\mathbf y} (\mathbf y)$$

and the conservation equation can be put into the form:

$$\mathbf y_t + \nabla \cdot \mathbf J (\mathbf y)= \mathbf 0$$

For example, this the case for Euler equations (fluid dynamics). In the simple incompressible case they are:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot \mathbf u &= 0 \, , & \frac{\partial \mathbf u}{\partial t} + \mathbf u \cdot \nabla \mathbf u + \nabla s &= \mathbf{0}, \end{aligned}$$

where:

- $\boldsymbol{u}$ is the [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29), with components in a N-dimensional space $u_{1}, u_{2}, ..., u_{N}$,
- $s$ is the specific [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) (pressure per unit [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density)) giving the [source term](wikipedia://en/379868),

It can be shown that the conserved (vector) quantity and the current [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) for these equations are respectively:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{y} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \mathbf u \end{pmatrix}; & \mathbf{J} &= \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf u\\ \mathbf u \otimes \mathbf u + s \mathbf I\end{pmatrix}; \end{aligned}$$

where $\otimes$ denotes the [outer product](wikipedia://en/103109).

## Integral and weak forms {#Integral_and_weak_forms}

Conservation equations can usually also be expressed in integral form: the advantage of the latter is substantially that it requires less smoothness of the solution, which paves the way to [weak form](wikipedia://en/3368981), extending the class of admissible solutions to include discontinuous solutions.[^o26]p. 62–63 By integrating in any space-time domain the current density form in 1-D space:

$$y_t + j_x (y)= 0$$

and by using [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), the integral form is:

$$\int_{- \infty}^\infty y \, dx + \int_0^\infty j (y) \, dt = 0$$

In a similar fashion, for the scalar multidimensional space, the integral form is:

$$\oint \left[y \, d^N \mathbf{r} + j (y) \, dt\right] = 0$$

where the line integration is performed along the boundary of the domain, in an anticlockwise manner.[^o26]p. 62–63

Moreover, by defining a [test function](wikipedia://en/301505) *φ*(**r**,*t*) continuously differentiable both in time and space with compact support, the [weak form](wikipedia://en/3368981) can be obtained pivoting on the [initial condition](wikipedia://en/1186804). In 1-D space it is:

$$\int_0^\infty \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left[ \phi_t y + \phi_x j(y) \right] dx \, dt = - \int_{-\infty}^\infty \phi(x,0) y(x,0) \, dx$$

In the weak form all the partial derivatives of the density and current density have been passed on to the test function, which with the former hypothesis is sufficiently smooth to admit these derivatives.[^o26]p. 62–63
