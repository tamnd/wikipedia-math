# Earth

::: {#o7 .subject cue=lead name="Earth"}

**Earth** is the third [planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) from the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) and the only [astronomical object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object) known to [harbor life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_habitability). This is made possible by Earth being an [ocean world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_world), the only one in the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) sustaining liquid [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water). Almost all of Earth's water is contained in its [ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean), which covers [\70.8%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_distribution_on_Earth) of [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust). The remaining 29.2% of Earth's crust is [land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land), which is predominantly located within Earth's [land hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_hemisphere) in the form of [continental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent) [landmasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landmass). Most of Earth's land is at least somewhat [humid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid) and covered by [vegetation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetation), while large [ice sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheet) at Earth's [polar deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_desert) retain more water than Earth's [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater), lakes, rivers, and [atmospheric water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor#In_Earth's_atmosphere) combined. Earth's crust consists of slowly moving [tectonic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tectonic_plates), which interact to produce mountain ranges, [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano), and earthquakes. Earth has [a liquid outer core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_outer_core) that generates a [magnetosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetosphere) capable of deflecting most of the destructive [solar winds](wikipedia://en/28538) and [cosmic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_radiation).

:::

Earth has [a dynamic atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth), which sustains Earth's surface conditions and protects it from most [meteoroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteoroid) and [ultraviolet light at entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_layer). It is composed primarily of [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen) and [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen). [Water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) is widely present in the atmosphere, [forming clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud#Formation) that cover most of the planet. The water vapor acts as a [greenhouse gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas) and, together with other greenhouse gases in the atmosphere, particularly [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) (CO<sub>2</sub>), creates the conditions for both liquid surface water and water vapor to persist via the capturing of [energy from the Sun's light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance). This process maintains the current average surface temperature of \14.76 C, at which water is liquid under normal [atmospheric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure). Differences in the amount of captured energy between geographic regions (as with the [equatorial region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_region) receiving more sunlight than the polar regions) drive [atmospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_circulation) and [ocean currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current), producing a global [climate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_system) with different [climate regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_region), and a range of weather phenomena such as [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation), allowing components such as [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_cycle) and [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_cycle) to [cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogeochemical_cycle).

Earth is [rounded](wikipedia://en/51776) into [an ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/16112368) with [a circumference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_circumference) of about 40000 km. It is the [densest planet in the Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Solar_System_objects_by_size). Of the four [rocky planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_planets), it is the largest and most massive. Overall, Earth is the sixth largest object in the Solar System after the Sun and the [outer planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_Solar_System). Earth is about eight [light-minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-minute) (1 [astronomical unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit) \[AU\]) away from the Sun and [orbits it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit), taking a year (about 365.25 days) to complete one revolution. [Earth rotates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_rotation) around its own axis in slightly less than a day (in about 23 hours and 56 minutes). [Earth's axis of rotation](wikipedia://en/9228#Axial_tilt_and_seasons) is tilted with respect to the perpendicular to its orbital plane around the Sun, producing seasons. Earth is [orbited](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) by one permanent [natural satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite), the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), which orbits Earth at 384400 km—1.28 light seconds—and is roughly a quarter as wide as Earth. The Moon's gravity helps stabilize Earth's [axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt), causes [tides](wikipedia://en/30718), and [gradually slows Earth's rotation](wikipedia://en/30647). Likewise, Earth's gravitational pull has already made the Moon's rotation [tidally locked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidally_locked), keeping the same [near side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_side_of_the_Moon) facing Earth.

Earth, like most other bodies in the Solar System, [formed about 4.5 billion years ago](wikipedia://en/49256) from gas and dust in the [early Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_and_evolution_of_the_Solar_System). The formation of the ocean and the subsequent [development of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abiogenesis) occurred during the first billion years of [Earth's history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_history). Life spread globally and has been altering Earth's atmosphere and surface, leading to the [Great Oxidation Event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Oxidation_Event) two billion years ago. [Humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human) emerged 300,000 years ago in Africa and have spread across every continent on Earth. Humans depend on Earth's [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere) and natural resources for their survival, but have [increasingly impacted the planet's environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment). Humanity's current impact on Earth's climate and biosphere is unsustainable, threatening the livelihood of humans and many other forms of life, and [causing widespread extinctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_extinction).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The [Modern English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_English) word *Earth* developed, via [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English), from an [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) noun most often spelled *eorðe*.[^o9] It has cognates in every [Germanic language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_language), from which erþō has been reconstructed. In its earliest attestation, the word *eorðe* was used to translate the many senses of Latin *terra* and Greek grc: the ground, its soil, dry land, the human world, the surface of the world (including the sea), and the globe itself. As with [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire) la (or la) and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks) grc-Latn, Earth may have been a [personified goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_goddess) in [Germanic paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_paganism): late [Norse mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norse_mythology) included non ('Earth'), a giantess often given as the mother of [Thor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thor).[^o10]

Historically, *Earth* has been written in lowercase. During the [Early Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_English) period, its [definite sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_article) as "the globe" began being expressed using the phrase *the earth*. By the period of [Early Modern English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Modern_English), [capitalization of nouns began to prevail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalization_in_English#History_of_English_capitalization), and *the earth* was also written *the Earth*, particularly when referenced along with other heavenly bodies. More recently, the name is sometimes simply given as *Earth*, by analogy with the names of the [other planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), though *earth* and forms with *the earth* remain common.[^o9] [House styles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Style_guide) now vary: [Oxford spelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_spelling) recognizes the lowercase form as the more common, with the capitalized form an acceptable variant. Another convention capitalizes *Earth* when appearing as a name, such as a description of the "Earth's atmosphere", but employs the lowercase when it is preceded by *the*, such as "the atmosphere of the earth". It almost always appears in lowercase in colloquial expressions such as "what on earth are you doing?"[^o11]

The name *Terra* is occasionally used in scientific writing; it also sees use in science fiction to distinguish humanity's inhabited planet from others,[^o12] while in poetry *Tellus* has been used to denote personification of the Earth.[^o13] *Terra* is also the name of the planet in some [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages), languages that evolved from Latin, like Italian and Portuguese, while in other Romance languages the word gave rise to names with slightly altered spellings, like the Spanish Tierra and the French Terre. The Latinate form Gaea ( ) of the Greek poetic name grc ( or ) is rare, though the alternative spelling *Gaia* has become common due to the [Gaia hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaia_hypothesis), in which case its pronunciation is rather than the more traditional English .[^o14]

There are a number of adjectives for the planet Earth. The word *earthly* is derived from *Earth*. From the Latin Terra comes *terran* ,[^o15] *terrestrial* ,[^o16] and (via French) *terrene* ,[^o17] and from the Latin Tellus comes *tellurian* [^o18] and *telluric*.[^o19]

## Natural history {#Natural_history}

::: {#o22 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Earth's formation"}

### Formation {#Formation}

*Image: The Mysterious Case of the Disappearing Dust.jpg.* A depiction of the early [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System)'s [protoplanetary disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoplanetary_disk) from which Earth and other Solar System bodies were formed

The oldest material found in the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) is dated to \4.5682 +\0.0002 \0.0004 [Ga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigaannum) (billion years) ago.[^o260] By \4.54 ± \0.04 Ga, the primordial Earth had formed.[^o245] The bodies in [the Solar System formed and evolved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_and_evolution_of_the_Solar_System) with the Sun. In theory, a [solar nebula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_nebula) partitions a volume out of a [molecular cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_cloud) by gravitational collapse, which begins to spin and flatten into a [circumstellar disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumstellar_disk), and then the planets grow out of that disk with the Sun. A nebula contains gas, ice grains, and [dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_dust) (including [primordial nuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_nuclide)). According to [nebular theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebular_theory), [planetesimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetesimal) formed by [accretion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accretion_%28astrophysics%29), with the primordial Earth being estimated as likely taking anywhere from 70 to 100 million years to form.[^o23]

Estimates of the age of the Moon range from 4.5 Ga to significantly younger.[^o24] The [leading hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_impact_hypothesis) is that it was formed by accretion from material loosed from Earth after a [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars)-sized object with about 10% of Earth's mass, named [Theia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theia_%28hypothetical_planet%29), collided with Earth.[^o326] It hit Earth with a glancing blow, and some of its mass merged with Earth.[^o267][^o25] Between approximately 4.0 and \3.8 Ga, numerous [asteroid impacts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event) during the [Late Heavy Bombardment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment) caused significant changes to the greater surface environment of the Moon and, by inference, to that of Earth.[^o26]

:::

### After formation {#After_formation}

[Earth's atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth) and oceans were formed by [volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism) and [outgassing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outgassing).[^o28] Water vapor from these sources [condensed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_water_on_Earth) into the oceans, augmented by water and ice from asteroids, [protoplanets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoplanet), and [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet).[^o353] Sufficient water to fill the oceans may have been on Earth since it formed.[^o29] In this model, atmospheric [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas) kept the oceans from freezing when the newly forming Sun [had only 70%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faint_young_Sun_paradox) of its [current luminosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_luminosity).[^o254] By \3.5 Ga, [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field) was established, which helped prevent the atmosphere from being stripped away by the [solar wind](wikipedia://en/28538).[^o316]

*Image: NASA-EarlyEarth-PaleOrangeDot-20190802.jpg.* *Pale orange dot*, an impression of [Early Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Earth), featuring its tinted orange [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane)-rich [early atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prebiotic_atmosphere)[^o30]

As the molten outer layer of Earth cooled, it [formed](wikipedia://en/54423) the first solid [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust), which is thought to have been [mafic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafic) in composition. The first [continental crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_crust), which was more [felsic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felsic) in composition, formed by the [partial melting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_melting) of this mafic crust.[^o31] [Hadean-aged zircon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadean_zircon) grains found in [Western Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Australia), dating back as far as \4.4 Ga, provide the oldest direct evidence of Earth's surface, indicating that felsic continental crust and liquid water existed within 140–160 million years of the planet's formation.[^o335] There are two main models of how this initial small volume of continental crust evolved to reach its current abundance:[^o356] (1) a relatively steady growth up to the present day,[^o332] which is supported by the radiometric dating of continental crust globally and (2) an initial rapid growth in the volume of continental crust during the [Archean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archean), forming the bulk of the continental crust that now exists,[^o247][^o345] which is supported by isotopic evidence from [hafnium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafnium) in [zircons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zircon) and [neodymium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium) in sedimentary rocks. The two models and the data that support them can be reconciled by large-scale [recycling of the continental crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crustal_recycling), particularly during the early stages of Earth's history.[^o276]

New continental crust forms as a result of [plate tectonics](wikipedia://en/24944), a process ultimately driven by the continuous loss of heat from Earth's interior. Over [the period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale) of hundreds of millions of years, tectonic forces have caused areas of continental crust to group together to form [supercontinents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercontinent) that have subsequently broken apart. At approximately 750 [Ma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaannum), one of the earliest known supercontinents, [Rodinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodinia), began to break apart. The continents later recombined to form [Pannotia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannotia) at 600 +– 540 Ma, then finally [Pangaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangaea), which also began to break apart at 180 Ma.[^o261]

The most recent pattern of [ice ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_age) began about 40 Ma,[^o32] and then intensified during the [Pleistocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleistocene) about 3 Ma.[^o33] [High-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_latitude) and [middle-latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_latitude) regions have since undergone repeated cycles of glaciation and thaw, repeating about every 21,000, 41,000, and 100,000 years.[^o323] The [Last Glacial Period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_Glacial_Period), colloquially called the "last ice age", covered large parts of the continents, to the middle latitudes, in ice and ended about 11,700 years ago.[^o34]

### Origin of life and evolution {#Origin_of_life_and_evolution}

*Image: Archean.png.* An impression of the [Archean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archean), the [eon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_time_scale#Divisions_of_geologic_time) after Earth's formation, featuring round [stromatolites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stromatolite), which are early oxygen-producing forms of life from billions of years ago. After the [Late Heavy Bombardment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment), [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust) had cooled; its water-rich barren [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_surface) is marked by [continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent) and [volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcano), with the Moon still orbiting Earth half as far as it is today, appearing 2.8 times larger and producing strong [tides](wikipedia://en/30718).[^o36]

[Chemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction) led to the first self-replicating molecules about four billion years ago. A half billion years later, the [last common ancestor of all current life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Last_universal_common_ancestor) arose.[^o327] The evolution of [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis) allowed the Sun's energy to be harvested directly by life forms. The resultant [molecular oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_oxygen) (O2) accumulated in the atmosphere and, due to interaction with ultraviolet solar radiation, formed a protective [ozone layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_layer) (O3) in the upper atmosphere.[^o37] The incorporation of smaller cells within larger ones resulted in the [development of complex cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endosymbiotic_theory) called [eukaryotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryote).[^o293] True multicellular organisms formed as cells within [colonies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony_%28biology%29) became increasingly specialized. Aided by the absorption of harmful [ultraviolet radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet_radiation) by the ozone layer, life colonized Earth's surface.[^o265] Among the earliest [fossil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil) evidence for life are [microbial mat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbial_mat) fossils found in 3.48 billion-year-old [sandstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandstone) in [Western Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Australia),[^o38] [biogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogenic_substance) [graphite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphite) found in 3.7 billion-year-old [metasedimentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metasediment) rocks in [Western Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Greenland),[^o39] and remains of [biotic material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotic_material) found in 4.1 billion-year-old rocks in Western Australia.[^o40][^o41] The [earliest direct evidence of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earliest_known_life_forms) on Earth is contained in 3.45 billion-year-old [Australian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia_%28continent%29) rocks showing fossils of [microorganisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microorganism).[^o42][^o43]

During the [Neoproterozoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoproterozoic), 1000 +to 539 Ma, much of Earth might have been covered in ice. This hypothesis has been termed "[Snowball Earth](wikipedia://en/28982)", and it is of particular interest because it preceded the [Cambrian explosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambrian_explosion), when multicellular life forms significantly increased in complexity.[^o44][^o45] Following the Cambrian explosion, 535 Ma, there have been at least five major [mass extinctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction_event) and many minor ones.[^o340] Apart from the proposed current [Holocene extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocene_extinction) event, the [most recent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretaceous–Paleogene_extinction_event) was 66 Ma, when [an asteroid impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicxulub_impactor) triggered the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs and other large reptiles, but largely spared small animals, such as insects, [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammal), lizards, and birds. Mammalian life has diversified over the past 66 million years, and several million years ago, an African [ape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ape) species gained the ability to stand upright.[^o285][^o46] This facilitated tool use and encouraged communication that provided the nutrition and stimulation needed for a larger brain, which led to the [evolution of humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_evolution). The [development of agriculture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_agriculture), and then [civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_ancient_civilizations), led to humans having an [influence on Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment) and the nature and quantity of other life forms that continues to this day.[^o259]

### Future {#Future}

*Image: Red Giant Earth warm.jpg.* A conception of the scorched Earth after the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) has entered the [red giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant) phase, about 5–7 billion years in the future

Earth's expected long-term future is tied to that of the Sun. Over the next \1.1 billion years, solar luminosity will increase by 10%, and over the next \3.5 billion years by 40%.[^o342] Earth's increasing surface temperature will accelerate the [inorganic carbon cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate–silicate_cycle), possibly reducing CO2 concentration to levels lethally low for current plants (10 ppm for [C<sub>4</sub> photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C4_carbon_fixation)) in approximately 100 +– 900 million years.[^o262][^o319] A lack of vegetation would result in the loss of oxygen in the atmosphere, making current animal life impossible.[^o352] Due to the increased luminosity, Earth's mean temperature may reach 100 C in 1.5 billion years, and all ocean water will evaporate and be lost to space, which may trigger a [runaway greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runaway_greenhouse_effect), within an estimated 1.6 to 3 billion years.[^o48] Even if the Sun were stable and eternal, a significant fraction of the water in the modern oceans would descend into the [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_%28geology%29), due to reduced steam venting from mid-ocean ridges as the core of the Earth slowly cools.[^o48][^o289]

The Sun will [evolve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_evolution) to become a [red giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant) in about 5 billion years. Models predict that the Sun will expand to roughly 1 AU, about 250 times its present radius.[^o342][^o343] Earth's fate is less clear. As a red giant, the Sun will lose roughly 30% of its mass, so, without tidal effects, Earth will move to an orbit \1.7 AU from the Sun when the star reaches its maximum radius, otherwise, with tidal effects, it may enter the Sun's atmosphere and be vaporized, with the heavier elements sinking to the core of the dying sun.[^o342] A new study published in 2026 has suggested that the Earth is likely to survive the Sun's [asymptotic giant branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_giant_branch) phase.[^o49]

## Composition and structure {#Composition_and_structure}

*Image: Terrestrial planet size comp 2024.png.* Earth depicted to scale alongside the [planetary-mass objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary-mass_object) of the [Inner Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Solar_System). From left: [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29), [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus), Earth, the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars) and [Ceres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceres_%28dwarf_planet%29).

Earth is a [rocky planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocky_planet) and an [ocean world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_world), the only one in the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) with liquid [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water). Among all rocky [Solar System objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System_object), it is the most massive, 81 times that of Earth's [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon); and among all [planetary-mass objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary-mass_object) of the Solar System, it is the [object with the highest density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Solar_System_objects_by_size).[^o51]

Earth has a rounded shape, through [hydrostatic equilibrium](wikipedia://en/51776),[^o52] with an equatorial diameter of 12756 km, making it the [fifth-largest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Solar_System_objects_by_size) planetary-sized and largest terrestrial object of the Solar System,[^o53] and 3.7 times larger than Earth's Moon.

### Chemical composition {#Chemical_composition}

[Earth's mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mass) is approximately \5.97 × 10<sup>24</sup> kg (5.97). It is composed mostly of iron (32.1% [by mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_fraction_%28chemistry%29)), [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) (30.1%), [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) (15.1%), [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium) (13.9%), [sulfur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfur) (2.9%), [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel) (1.8%), [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium) (1.5%), and [aluminum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) (1.4%), with the remaining 1.2% consisting of trace amounts of other elements. Due to [gravitational separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_differentiation#Gravitational_separation), the core is primarily composed of the denser elements: iron (88.8%), with smaller amounts of nickel (5.8%), sulfur (4.5%), and less than 1% trace elements.[^o320][^o31] The most common rock constituents of the crust are [oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide). Over 99% of the [crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust) is composed of various oxides of 11 elements, principally oxides containing silicon (the [silicate minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_mineral)), aluminum, iron, calcium, magnesium, potassium, or sodium.[^o264][^o320]

Earth's interior, like that of the other terrestrial planets, is divided into layers by their [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical) or physical ([rheological](wikipedia://en/25453)) properties. The outer layer is a chemically distinct [silicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_minerals) solid crust, which is underlain by a highly [viscous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscous) solid mantle. The crust is separated from the mantle by the [Mohorovičić discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohorovičić_discontinuity).[^o284] The thickness of the crust varies from about 6 km under the oceans to 30 \- for the continents. The crust and the cold, rigid top of the [upper mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_mantle) are collectively known as the lithosphere, which is divided into independently moving tectonic plates.[^o62]

Beneath the lithosphere is the [asthenosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthenosphere), a relatively low-viscosity layer on which the lithosphere rides. Important changes in crystal structure within the mantle occur at 410 and below the surface, spanning a [transition zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_zone_%28Earth%29) that separates the upper and lower mantle. Beneath the mantle, an extremely low-viscosity liquid [outer core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_core) lies above a solid [inner core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_inner_core).[^o344] Earth's inner core may be rotating at a slightly higher [angular velocity](wikipedia://en/65927) than the remainder of the planet, advancing by 0.1–0.5° per year, although both somewhat higher and much lower rates have also been proposed.[^o275] The radius of the inner core is about one-fifth of that of Earth. The density increases with depth.

### Internal heat {#Internal_heat}

*Image: Earth heat flow.jpg.* A map of [heat flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_flow) from Earth's interior to the surface of Earth's crust, mostly along the [oceanic ridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_ridge)

The major contributors to Earth's internal heat are primordial heat (heat left over from Earth's formation) and radiogenic heat (heat produced by radioactive decay).[^o64] The major heat-producing [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope) within Earth are [potassium-40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium-40), [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238), and [thorium-232](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorium-232).[^o330] At the center, the temperature may be up to 6000 C,[^o65] and the pressure could reach 360 GPa.[^o324] Because much of the heat is provided by radioactive decay, scientists postulate that early in Earth's history, before isotopes with short half-lives were depleted, Earth's heat production was much higher. At approximately 3 Gyr, twice the present-day heat would have been produced, increasing the rates of [mantle convection](wikipedia://en/7604906) and plate tectonics, and allowing the production of uncommon [igneous rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igneous_rock) such as [komatiites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komatiite) that are rarely formed today.[^o346][^o279]

The mean heat loss from Earth is 87 mW/m<sup>2</sup>, for a global heat loss of \4.42 × 10<sup>13</sup> W.[^o296] A portion of the core's thermal energy is transported toward the crust by [mantle plumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_plume), a form of convection consisting of upwellings of higher-temperature rock. These plumes can produce [hotspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotspot_%28geology%29) and [flood basalts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_basalt).[^o333] More of the heat in Earth is lost through plate tectonics, by mantle upwelling associated with [mid-ocean ridges](wikipedia://en/1821971). The final major mode of heat loss is through conduction through the lithosphere, the majority of which occurs under the oceans.[^o288]

### Tectonic plates {#Tectonic_plates}

*Image: Tectonic plates 2022.svg.* Map of Earth's [16 principal tectonic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tectonic_plates)

Earth's mechanically rigid outer layer of [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust) and [upper mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_mantle_%28Earth%29), the [lithosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithosphere), is divided into [tectonic plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tectonic_plates). These plates are rigid segments that move relative to each other at one of three boundary types: at [convergent boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_boundary), two plates come together; at [divergent boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divergent_boundary), two plates are pulled apart; and at [transform boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transform_fault), two plates slide past one another laterally. Along these plate boundaries, earthquakes, [volcanic activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism), [mountain-building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orogeny), and [oceanic trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_trench) formation can occur.[^o298] The tectonic plates ride on top of the [asthenosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthenosphere), the solid but less-viscous part of the upper mantle that can flow and move along with the plates.[^o338]

As the tectonic plates migrate, oceanic crust is [subducted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subduction) under the leading edges of the plates at convergent boundaries. At the same time, the upwelling of mantle material at divergent boundaries creates mid-ocean ridges. The combination of these processes recycles the oceanic crust back into the mantle. Due to this recycling, most of the ocean floor is less than 100 Myr old. The oldest oceanic crust is located in the Western Pacific and is estimated to be 200 Myr old.[^o277][^o312] By comparison, the oldest dated continental crust is 4030 Myr,[^o272] although zircons have been found preserved as clasts within Eoarchean sedimentary rocks that give ages up to 4400 Myr, indicating that at least some continental crust existed at that time.[^o335]

The seven major plates are the [Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Plate), [North American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Plate), [Eurasian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurasian_Plate), [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Plate), [Antarctic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Plate), [Indo-Australian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indo-Australian_Plate), and [South American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_American_Plate). Other notable plates include the [Arabian Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Plate), the [Caribbean Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caribbean_Plate), the [Nazca Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazca_Plate) off the west coast of South America, and the [Scotia Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotia_Plate) in the southern Atlantic Ocean. The Australian Plate fused with the Indian Plate between 50 +and 55 Ma. The fastest-moving plates are the oceanic plates, with the [Cocos Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocos_Plate) advancing at a rate of 75 mm/year[^o321] and the Pacific Plate moving 52 –. At the other extreme, the slowest-moving plate is the South American Plate, progressing at a typical rate of \10.6 mm/year.[^o67]

### Crust {#Crust}

*Image: AYool topography 15min.png.* [Topographic relief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_relief) map of [Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_crust)

Earth's crust may be divided into [oceanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_crust) and [continental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_crust) crust. The oceanic crust is predominantly [basaltic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt), while the continental crust has more lower-density materials such as [granite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granite), metamorphic rocks, and sediments,[^o300] which—while covering nearly 75% of the continental surfaces—make only about 5% of the mass of the crust.[^o295]

While the oceanic crust is covered by the [ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean), the ocean also covers continental crust, the [continental shelves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_shelf), which makes these parts of the ocean [shelf seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelf_sea). The oceanic crust forms large [oceanic basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_basin) with features like [abyssal plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abyssal_plain), [seamounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamount), [submarine volcanoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_volcano),[^o310] [oceanic trenches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_trench), [submarine canyons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_canyon), [oceanic plateaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_plateau), and a globe-spanning [mid-ocean ridge](wikipedia://en/1821971) system.[^o69] The terrain of the ocean floor has an average depth of roughly 3.8 km.[^o70]

### Surface {#Surface}

*Image: Global View of the Arctic and Antarctic.jpg.* A [composite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compositing) image of Earth with an opaque cloud cover, making the different types of surface discernible: Earth's surface dominating Ocean (blue), Africa with lush (green) to dry (brown) land and Earth's polar ice in the form of [Antarctic sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_sea_ice) (grey) covering the [Antarctic or Southern Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Ocean) and the [Antarctic ice sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_ice_sheet) (white) covering [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica).

Earth's surface is the boundary between the atmosphere and the solid Earth and oceans. The surface is continually being shaped by internal [plate tectonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonic) processes including [earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquakes) and [volcanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanism); by [weathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weathering) and [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion) driven by ice, water, wind and temperature; and by [biological processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_processes) including the growth and decomposition of [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass) into [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil).[^o299][^o72]

Earth's surface has an area of about 510 e6km2[^o317] and can be divided into two [hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemispheres_of_Earth): by [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) into the polar [Northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere) and [Southern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere) hemispheres, by [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) into the continental [Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Hemisphere) and [Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Hemisphere) hemispheres, or roughly as [Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_hemisphere) and [Land hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_hemisphere).

Most of Earth's surface is ocean water: 70.8% or 361 e6km2.[^o317] This vast pool of salty water is often called the *world ocean*,[^o73][^o74] and makes Earth with its dynamic [hydrosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrosphere) a water world[^o75][^o76] or [ocean world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_world).[^o77][^o78] Indeed, in Earth's early history, the ocean may have covered Earth completely.[^o79] The world ocean is commonly divided into the Pacific Ocean, Atlantic Ocean, Indian Ocean, Southern Ocean, and Arctic Ocean, from largest to smallest. At Earth's [polar regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_regions_of_Earth), the [ocean surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface) is covered by seasonally variable amounts of [sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice) that often connects with polar land, [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost), and [ice sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheet), forming [polar ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_ice_cap).[^o80]

Earth's land covers 29.2%, or 149 e6km2 of Earth's surface. The land surface includes many [islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island) around the globe, but most of the land surface is taken up by the four continental [landmasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landmass), which are (in descending order): [Africa-Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afro-Eurasia), [America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas), [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica), and [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainland_Australia).[^o81][^o82][^o83] These landmasses are further broken down and grouped into the [continents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continent). Land can be [covered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_cover) by [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water), snow, ice, artificial structures, or vegetation. Most of Earth's land hosts vegetation,[^o84] but considerable amounts of land are [ice sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheet) (10%,[^o85] not including the equally large area of land under [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost))[^o86] or [deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert) (33%).[^o87]

The [pedosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedosphere) is the outermost layer of Earth's land surface and is composed of [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) and subject to [soil formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_formation) processes. Soil is crucial for land to be arable. Earth's total [arable land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arable_land) is 10.7% of the land surface, with 1.3% being permanent cropland.[^o88][^o89] Earth has an estimated \16.7 e6km2 of cropland and \33.5 e6km2 of pastureland.[^o90]

### Surface topography {#Surface_topography}

Earth's surface [topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography) comprises both the [topography of the ocean surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface_topography) and the [shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypsometry) of Earth's land surface. The [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain) of the land surface varies greatly and consists of mountains, [deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert), [plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain), [plateaus](wikipedia://en/538005), and other [landforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landform). The elevation of the land surface varies from a low point of \-418 m at the [Dead Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea), to a maximum altitude of 8848 m at the top of [Mount Everest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Everest). The mean height of land above sea level is about 797 m.[^o92]

### Shape {#Shape}

Due to Earth's rotation, [its shape](wikipedia://en/233668) is that of an [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/16112368), [bulging at its equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_bulge); its diameter is 43 km longer there than at its [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographical_pole).[^o310][^o304] Earth's shape also has local [topographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topography) variations; the largest local variations, like the [Mariana Trench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_Trench) (10925 m below local sea level),[^o94] shorten Earth's average radius by 0.17%, and [Mount Everest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Everest) (8848 m above local sea level) lengthens it by 0.14%.[^o95] Since Earth's surface is farthest out from its [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) at its equatorial bulge, the summit of the volcano [Chimborazo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimborazo) in Ecuador (\6384.4 km) is its farthest point out.[^o322][^o96] Parallel to the rigid land topography, [the ocean exhibits a more dynamic topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface_topography).[^o97]

To measure the local variation of Earth's topography, [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) employs an idealized Earth producing a [geoid](wikipedia://en/176244) shape. Such a shape is obtained if the ocean is idealized, covering Earth completely and without any perturbations such as tides and winds. The result is a smooth but irregular geoid surface, providing a mean sea level as a reference level for topographic measurements.[^o98]

## Gravity and magnetic field {#Gravity_and_magnetic_field}

### Magnetic field {#Magnetic_field}

*Image: Magnetosphere Levels-en.svg.* A schematic view of Earth's magnetosphere with [solar wind](wikipedia://en/28538) flowing from left to right

The main part of Earth's magnetic field is generated in the core, the site of a [dynamo](wikipedia://en/255217) process that converts the kinetic energy of thermally and compositionally driven convection into electrical and magnetic-field energy. The field extends outwards from the core, through the mantle, and up to Earth's surface, where it is approximately a [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole). The poles of the dipole are located close to Earth's geographic poles. At the equator of the magnetic field, the magnetic-field strength at the surface is 3.05 [T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_%28unit%29), with a [magnetic dipole moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipole_moment) of 7.79 Am${}^{2}$ at epoch 2000, decreasing nearly 6% per century (although it remains stronger than its long-term average).[^o101] The convection movements in the core are chaotic; the magnetic poles drift and periodically change alignment. This causes [secular variation](wikipedia://en/25340789) of the main field and [field reversals](wikipedia://en/2203131) at irregular intervals averaging a few times every million years. The most recent reversal occurred approximately 700,000 years ago.[^o282][^o266]

The extent of Earth's magnetic field in space defines the [magnetosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetosphere). Ions and electrons of the solar wind are deflected by the magnetosphere; solar wind pressure compresses the day-side of the magnetosphere to about 10 Earth radii, and extends the night-side magnetosphere into a long tail.[^o102] Because the velocity of the solar wind is greater than the speed at which waves propagate through the solar wind, a supersonic [bow shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bow_shock) precedes the day-side magnetosphere within the solar wind.[^o103] [Charged particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_particle) are contained within the magnetosphere; the plasmasphere is defined by low-energy particles that essentially follow magnetic-field lines as Earth rotates.[^o104][^o105] The ring current is defined by medium-energy [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle) that drift relative to the geomagnetic field, but with paths that are still dominated by the magnetic field,[^o106] and the [Van Allen radiation belts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Allen_radiation_belt) are formed by high-energy particles whose motion is essentially random, but contained in the magnetosphere.[^o107][^o108] During [magnetic storms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_storm) and [substorms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substorm), charged particles can be deflected from the outer magnetosphere and especially the magnetotail, directed along field lines into Earth's [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere), where atmospheric atoms can be excited and ionized, causing an [aurora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora).[^o341]

### Gravitational field {#Gravitational_field}

The gravity of Earth is the [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) that is imparted to objects due to the distribution of mass within Earth. Near Earth's surface, [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration) is approximately \9.8 m/s2. Local differences in topography, geology, and deeper tectonic structure cause local and broad regional differences in Earth's gravitational field, known as [gravity anomalies](wikipedia://en/937535).[^o110] The [Hill sphere](wikipedia://en/722235), or the [sphere of gravitational influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_of_influence_%28astrodynamics%29), of Earth is about \1.5 e6km in radius.[^o350][^o237] This is the maximum distance at which Earth's gravitational influence is stronger than that of the more distant Sun and planets. Objects must orbit Earth within this radius, or they can become unbound by the gravitational perturbation of the Sun.[^o350]

## Moon and orbital space {#Moon_and_orbital_space}

### Moon {#Moon}

*Image: MarsReconnaissanceOrbiter-Views-EarthMoon-20220422.jpg.* Earth and the Moon as seen from [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars) by the *[Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Reconnaissance_Orbiter)*

The Moon is a relatively large, [terrestrial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_planet), [planet-like natural satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary-mass_moon), with a diameter about one-quarter of Earth's. It is the largest moon in the Solar System relative to the size of its planet, although [Charon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charon_%28moon%29) is larger relative to the dwarf planet [Pluto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluto).[^o113][^o114] The natural satellites of other planets are also referred to as "moons", after Earth's.[^o115] The Moon and Earth orbit a common [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter) every 27.32 days relative to the background stars. When combined with the Earth–Moon system's common orbit around the Sun, the period of the [synodic month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synodic_month), from new moon to new moon, is 29.53 days.

Viewed from the [celestial north pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_pole), the motion of Earth, the Moon, and their axial rotations are all [counterclockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterclockwise). Viewed from Earth, the Moon is just far enough away to have almost the same apparent-sized disk as the Sun. The [angular sizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_size) (or [solid angles](wikipedia://en/94102)) of these two bodies match because, although the Sun's diameter is about 400 times as large as the Moon's, it is also 400 times more distant.[^o251] This allows total and annular solar eclipses to occur on Earth.[^o116] The orbital planes are not precisely aligned: the Earth–Moon plane is tilted up to ±5.1 degrees against the Earth–Sun plane (the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264)). Without this tilt, there would be an eclipse every two weeks, alternating between [lunar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse) and [solar eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse).[^o278][^o305]

The gravitational attraction between Earth and the Moon causes [lunar tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_tide) on Earth.[^o117] The same effect on the Moon has led to its [tidal locking](wikipedia://en/60162): its rotation period is the same as the time it takes to orbit Earth. As a result, it always presents the same face to the planet.[^o118] As the Moon orbits Earth, different parts of its face are illuminated by the Sun, leading to the [lunar phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase).[^o119] Due to their [tidal interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_interactions), the Moon recedes from Earth at the rate of approximately 38 mm/yr. Over millions of years, these tiny modifications—and the lengthening of Earth's day by about 23 [μs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsecond)/yr—add up to significant changes.[^o280] During the [Ediacaran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ediacaran) period (approximately 620 Ma), for example, there were 400±7 days in a year, with each day lasting 21.9±0.4 hours.[^o120]

The Moon may have dramatically affected the development of life by moderating the planet's climate. [Paleontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleontology) evidence and computer simulations show that Earth's axial tilt is stabilized by tidal interactions with the Moon.[^o242] Some theorists think that without this stabilization against the [torques](wikipedia://en/30400) applied by the Sun and planets to Earth's equatorial bulge, the rotational axis might be chaotically unstable, exhibiting large changes over millions of years, as is the case for Mars, though this is disputed.[^o121][^o122]

The most widely accepted theory of the Moon's origin, the [giant-impact hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant-impact_hypothesis), states that it formed from the collision of a Mars-size protoplanet called Theia with the early Earth. This hypothesis explains the Moon's relative lack of iron and volatile elements and the fact that its composition is nearly identical to that of Earth's crust.[^o267] Computer simulations suggest that two blob-like remnants of this protoplanet could be inside the Earth.[^o123][^o124]

### Asteroids and artificial satellites {#Asteroids_and_artificial_satellites}

*Image: Debris-GEO1280.jpg.* A computer-generated image mapping the prevalence of [artificial satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_satellite) and [space debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_debris) around Earth in [geosynchronous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_orbit) and [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit)

Earth's [co-orbital asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-orbital_configuration) population consists of [quasi-satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-satellite), objects with a [horseshoe orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_orbit), and [trojans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trojan_%28celestial_body%29). There are at least seven quasi-satellites, including [469219 Kamoʻoalewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/469219_Kamoʻoalewa), ranging in diameter from 10m to 5000m.[^o270][^o126] A [trojan asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_trojan) companion, 2010 TK, is [librating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration) around the leading [Lagrange triangular point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_point), L4, in [Earth's orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit) around the Sun.[^o269] The tiny [near-Earth asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-Earth_asteroid) 2006 RH makes close approaches to the Earth–Moon system roughly every 20 years. During these approaches, it can orbit Earth for brief periods of time.[^o127]

, there are 7,560 operational, human-made [satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite) orbiting Earth.[^o347] There are also inoperative satellites, including [Vanguard 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanguard_1), the oldest satellite currently in orbit, and over 16,000 pieces of tracked [space debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_debris).[^o240] Earth's largest artificial satellite is the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station) (ISS).[^o128]

## Orbit and rotation {#Orbit_and_rotation}

### Rotation {#Rotation}

*Image: EpicEarth-Globespin-tilt-23.4.gif.* Satellite [time-lapse imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-lapse_photography) of Earth's rotation showing axis tilt

Earth's rotation period relative to the Sun—its mean solar day—is 86,400 seconds of mean [solar time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_time) (86,400.0025 [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) seconds).[^o246] Because Earth's solar day is now slightly longer than it was during the 19th century due to [tidal deceleration](wikipedia://en/30647), each day varies between 0 and 2 [ms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisecond) longer than the mean solar day.[^o349][^o131]

Earth's rotation period relative to the [fixed stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_star), called its *stellar day* by the [International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Earth_Rotation_and_Reference_Systems_Service) (IERS), is 86,164.0989 seconds of mean solar time ([UT1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UT1)), or 23${}^{h}$ 56${}^{m}$ 4.0989${}^{s}$.[^o292][^o234] Earth's rotation period relative to the [precessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precession_%28astronomy%29) or moving mean [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox) (when the Sun is at 90° on the equator) is 86,164.0905 seconds of mean solar time (UT1) (23${}^{h}$ 56${}^{m}$ 4.0905${}^{s}$).[^o292] Thus the sidereal day is shorter than the stellar day by about 8.4 ms.[^o337]

Apart from meteors within the atmosphere and low-orbiting satellites, the main apparent motion of celestial bodies in Earth's sky is to the west at a rate of 15°/h = 15'/min. For bodies near the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681), this is equivalent to an apparent diameter of the Sun or the Moon every two minutes; from Earth's surface, the apparent sizes of the Sun and the Moon are approximately the same.[^o357][^o251]

### Orbit and location {#Orbit_and_location}

*Image: Seasons1.svg.* Exaggerated illustration of Earth's elliptical orbit around the Sun, marking that the orbital extreme points ([apoapsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoapsis) and [periapsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periapsis)) are not the same as the four seasonal extreme points, the [equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) and [solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstice)

Earth orbits the Sun at an average distance of about 150 e6km. This makes Earth the third-closest planet to the Sun and part of the [inner Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_Solar_System).[^o133] Its orbital distance is the basis for the [astronomical unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit) (AU) and is equal to roughly 8.3 [light-minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_minute) or 380 times [Earth's distance to the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_distance). Earth orbits the Sun every 365.2564 mean [solar days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_day), or one [sidereal year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_year), with an apparent movement of the Sun in Earth's sky at a rate of about 1°/day eastward; this is one apparent Sun or Moon diameter every 12 hours. Due to this motion, on average it takes 24 hours—a solar day—for Earth to complete a full rotation about its axis so that the Sun returns to the [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28astronomy%29), slightly longer than the sidereal day.[^o134]

The orbital speed of Earth averages at \29.7827 km/s, which is fast enough to travel a distance equal to Earth's diameter, about 12742 km, in seven minutes, and the distance from Earth to the Moon, 384400 km, in about 3.5 hours.[^o278] Viewed from a vantage point above the Sun's and Earth's north poles, Earth orbits in a counterclockwise direction about the Sun. Earth, along with all of the other planets in the Solar System, orbits roughly in the same flat plane, known as the [invariable plane](wikipedia://en/1673352). Earth's inclination to this plane is about 1.58°.[^o248]

Earth, along with the rest of the Solar System, is situated in the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way) and orbits about 28,000 [light-years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-year) from its center. It is about 20 light-years above the [galactic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactic_plane) in the [Orion Arm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orion_Arm).[^o308]

### Axial tilt and seasons {#Axial_tilt_and_seasons}

*Image: axial tilt vs tropical and polar circles.svg.* Earth's axial tilt causing different angles of seasonal illumination at different orbital positions around the Sun

The axial tilt of Earth is approximately 23.439281°,[^o292] with the axis of the plane of [Earth's orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit) by definition always pointing towards the [Celestial Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_Poles). Due to Earth's axial tilt, the amount of sunlight reaching any given point on the surface varies over the course of the year. This causes the seasonal change in climate, with summer in the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere) occurring when the [Tropic of Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer) is facing the Sun, and in the [Southern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Hemisphere) when the [Tropic of Capricorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn) faces the Sun. In each instance, winter occurs simultaneously in the opposite hemisphere.[^o136]

During the summer, the day lasts longer, and the Sun climbs higher in the sky. In winter, the climate becomes cooler and the days shorter.[^o137] Above the [Arctic Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_Circle) and below the [Antarctic Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Circle), there is no daylight at all for part of the year, causing a [polar night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_night), and this night extends for several months at the poles themselves. These same latitudes also experience a [midnight sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midnight_sun), where the Sun remains visible all day.[^o138][^o139]

By astronomical convention, the four seasons can be determined by the solstices—the points in the orbit of maximum axial tilt toward or away from the Sun—and the [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox), when Earth's rotational axis is aligned with its orbital axis. In the Northern Hemisphere, the [winter solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_solstice) currently occurs around 21 December; the [summer solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_solstice) is near 21 June, the spring equinox is around 20 March, and the [autumnal equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_equinox) is about 22 or 23 September. In the Southern Hemisphere, the situation is reversed, with the summer and winter solstices exchanged and the spring and autumnal equinox dates swapped.[^o263]

The angle of Earth's axial tilt is relatively stable over long periods of time. Its axial tilt does undergo [nutation](wikipedia://en/52297430); a slight, irregular motion with a main period of 18.6 years.[^o301] The orientation (rather than the angle) of Earth's axis also changes over time, [precessing](wikipedia://en/72576) around in a complete circle over each 25,800-year cycle; this precession is the reason for the difference between a sidereal year and a [tropical year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_year). Both of these motions are caused by the varying attraction of the Sun and the Moon on Earth's equatorial bulge. The poles also migrate a few meters across Earth's surface. This [polar motion](wikipedia://en/1475418) has multiple, cyclical components, which collectively are termed [quasiperiodic motion](wikipedia://en/1590390). In addition to an annual component to this motion, there is a 14-month cycle called the [Chandler wobble](wikipedia://en/227314). Earth's rotational velocity also varies in a phenomenon known as length-of-day variation.[^o281]

Earth's orbit is slightly elliptical rather than perfectly circular, and its closest approach to the Sun is called [perihelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perihelion). In modern times, Earth's perihelion occurs around 3 January, and its [aphelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphelion) around 4 July. These dates shift over time due to precession and changes to the orbit, the latter of which follows cyclical patterns known as [Milankovitch cycles](wikipedia://en/372608). The annual change in the Earth–Sun distance causes an increase of about 6.8% in solar energy reaching Earth at perihelion relative to aphelion.[^o140][^o238] Because the Southern Hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun at about the same time that Earth reaches the closest approach to the Sun, the Southern Hemisphere receives slightly more energy from the Sun than does the northern over the course of a year. This effect is much less significant than the total energy change due to the axial tilt, and most of the excess energy is absorbed by the higher proportion of water in the Southern Hemisphere.[^o141]

## Atmosphere and climate {#Atmosphere_and_climate}

*Image: ISS-42 Waning sun.jpg.* A view of the [Southern Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Pacific) under [moonlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonlight) with different layers of Earth's atmosphere visible: the [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere) with its clouds casting shadows, a band of [stratospheric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratospheric) blue sky at the horizon, and a line of green [airglow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airglow) of the lower [thermosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermosphere) around an [altitude of 100 km, at the edge of space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kármán_line)

Earth's atmosphere has no definite boundary, gradually becoming thinner and fading into outer space.[^o143] Three-quarters of the atmosphere's mass is contained within the first 11 km of the surface; this lowest layer is called the troposphere.[^o144] Energy from the Sun heats this layer and the surface below, causing expansion of the air. This lower-density air then rises and is replaced by cooler, higher-density air. The result is [atmospheric circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_circulation) that drives the weather and climate through redistribution of thermal energy.[^o306]

The atmosphere is composed of 78.084% [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen), 20.946% oxygen, 0.934% [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon), and trace amounts of carbon dioxide and other gaseous molecules.[^o149] [Water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) content varies between 0.01% and 4%[^o149] but averages about 1%.[^o278] Earth's [biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosphere) has significantly altered its [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth). [Oxygenic photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_evolution#Oxygen_evolution_in_nature) evolved \2.7 Gya, [forming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_catastrophe) the primarily nitrogen–oxygen atmosphere of today.[^o37] This change enabled the proliferation of [aerobic organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobic_organisms) and, indirectly, the formation of the ozone layer due to the subsequent [conversion of atmospheric O2 into O3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone–oxygen_cycle). The ozone layer blocks ultraviolet [solar radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_radiation), permitting life on land.[^o286] Other atmospheric functions important to life include transporting water vapor, providing useful gases, causing small meteors to burn up before they strike the surface, and moderating temperature.[^o257] This last phenomenon is the [greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect): trace molecules within the atmosphere serve to capture [thermal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_energy) emitted from the surface, thereby raising the average temperature. Water vapor, carbon dioxide, [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane), [nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide), and [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone) are the primary greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. Without this heat-retention effect, the average surface temperature would be \-18 C, in contrast to the current \+15 C,[^o318] and life on Earth probably would not exist in its current form.[^o359]

### Exosphere {#Exosphere}

The [exosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exosphere) is where the atmosphere thins out into the magnetosphere, where the geomagnetic fields interact with the solar wind.[^o336]

Thermal energy causes some of the molecules at the outer edge of the atmosphere to increase their velocity to the point where they can escape from Earth's gravity. This causes a slow but steady [loss of the atmosphere into space](wikipedia://en/2743476). Because unfixed [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) has a low [molecular mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_mass), it can achieve [escape velocity](wikipedia://en/37913) more readily, and it leaks into outer space at a greater rate than other gases.[^o294] The leakage of hydrogen into space contributes to the shifting of Earth's atmosphere and surface from an initially [reducing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) state to its current oxidizing one. Photosynthesis provided a source of free oxygen, but the loss of reducing agents such as hydrogen is thought to have been a necessary precondition for the widespread accumulation of oxygen in the atmosphere.[^o331] Hence the ability of hydrogen to escape from the atmosphere may have influenced the nature of life that developed on Earth.[^o244] In the current oxygen-rich atmosphere, most hydrogen is converted into water before it has an opportunity to escape. Instead, most of the hydrogen loss comes from the destruction of methane in the upper atmosphere.[^o253]

### Upper atmosphere {#Upper_atmosphere}

*Image: Antarctic aurora ESA313457.jpg.* Earth's night-side upper atmosphere appearing from the bottom as bands of [afterglow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afterglow) illuminating the [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere) in orange with silhouettes of clouds, and the [stratosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere) in white and blue. Next, the [mesosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesosphere) (pink area) extends to the orange and faintly green line of the lowest [airglow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airglow), at about one hundred kilometers at the [edge of space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_space#Boundary) and the lower edge of the [thermosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermosphere) (invisible). Continuing with green and red bands of [aurorae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora) stretching over several hundred kilometers.

The upper atmosphere, the atmosphere above the troposphere,[^o147] is usually divided into the [stratosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere), [mesosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesosphere), and [thermosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermosphere).[^o257] Each layer has a different lapse rate, defining the rate of change in temperature with height.

Within the stratosphere is the ozone layer, a component that partially shields the surface from ultraviolet light and thus is important for life on Earth. The [Kármán line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kármán_line), defined as 100 km above Earth's surface, is a working definition for the boundary between the atmosphere and [outer space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_space).[^o273]

### Troposphere {#Troposphere}

The troposphere lies below the stratosphere of the upper atmosphere, and immediately above Earth's surface (including the [peplosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peplosphere)). As such, it is the atmospheric layer of Earth's surface weather and climate. Its height varies with latitude, ranging between 8 km at the poles to 17 km at the equator, with some variation resulting from weather and seasonal factors.[^o283]

The [atmospheric pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_pressure) at Earth's sea level averages \101.325 kPa,[^o149] with a [scale height](wikipedia://en/1994795) of about \8.5 km.[^o278]

### Weather and climate {#Weather_and_climate}

Earth receives 1361 W/m<sup>2</sup> of [solar irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_irradiance).[^o278][^o152] The amount of solar energy that reaches Earth's surface decreases with increasing latitude. At higher latitudes, the sunlight reaches the surface at lower angles, and it must pass through thicker columns of the atmosphere. As a result, the mean annual air temperature at sea level decreases by about \0.4 C-change per degree of latitude from the equator.[^o328]

*Image: IntertropicalConvergenceZone-EO.jpg.* The [Intertropical Convergence Zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone)'s band of clouds over the Eastern Pacific and the Americas as seen from space

Earth's surface can be subdivided into specific latitudinal belts of approximately homogeneous climate. Ranging from the equator to the polar regions, these are the tropical (or equatorial), [subtropical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropics), [temperate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperate), and [polar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_region) climates.[^o271] The primary atmospheric circulation bands consist of the [trade winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_winds) in the equatorial region below 30° latitude and the [westerlies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westerlies) in the mid-latitudes between 30° and 60°.[^o258] [Ocean heat content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_heat_content) and [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current) are also important factors in determining climate, particularly the [thermohaline circulation](wikipedia://en/387457) that distributes thermal energy from the equatorial oceans to the polar regions.[^o325]

Water vapor generated through surface evaporation forms clouds and is transported by circulatory patterns in the atmosphere. [Clouds cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_cover) around two-thirds of Earth's surface, more so over oceans than land.[^o153] When this water condenses and gets heavy enough, it falls to the surface as [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation).[^o306] If not frozen, the resulting surface water slowly evaporates again. This [water cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle) is a vital mechanism for supporting life on land and is a primary factor in the erosion of surface features over geological periods. Precipitation patterns vary widely, ranging from several meters of water per year to less than a millimeter. Atmospheric circulation, topographic features, and temperature differences determine the average precipitation that falls in each region.[^o290]

Major local factors that affect a location's climate are its [proximity to oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continentality), the oceanic and atmospheric circulation, and topology.[^o154] Places close to oceans typically have colder summers and warmer winters, due to the fact that oceans can store large amounts of heat. The wind transports the cold or the heat of the ocean to the land.[^o155] Atmospheric circulation also plays an important role: San Francisco and Washington DC are both coastal cities at about the same latitude. San Francisco's climate is significantly more moderate as the prevailing wind direction is from sea to land.[^o156] Finally, temperatures [decrease with height](wikipedia://en/224312), causing mountainous areas to be colder than low-lying areas.[^o157]

*Image: Koppen classification world map 1991-2020 -3C borderless.png.* Worldwide [Köppen climate classifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Köppen_climate_classification)

The commonly used [Köppen climate classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Köppen_climate_classification) system has five broad groups ([humid tropics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_climate), [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid), [humid middle latitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humid_subtropical_climate), [continental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_climate) and cold [polar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_climate)), which are further divided into more specific subtypes.[^o258] The Köppen system rates regions based on observed temperature and precipitation.[^o158] Surface [air temperature can rise to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_temperature_recorded_on_Earth) around 55 C in [hot deserts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_desert), such as [Death Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_Valley_National_Park), and [can fall as low as](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lowest_temperature_recorded_on_Earth) \-89 C in [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica).[^o159][^o160]

## Hydrosphere {#Hydrosphere}

*Image: Ocean world Earth.jpg.* A view of Earth with its [global ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_ocean) and [cloud cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_cover), which dominate Earth's surface and [hydrosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrosphere); at Earth's [polar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_regions_of_Earth) regions, its hydrosphere forms larger areas of ice cover.

Earth's hydrosphere is the sum of Earth's water and its distribution. Most of Earth's hydrosphere consists of Earth's global ocean. Earth's hydrosphere also consists of water in the atmosphere and on land, including clouds, inland seas, lakes, rivers, and underground waters. The mass of the oceans is approximately 1.35 [metric tons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_ton) or about 1/4400 of Earth's total mass. The oceans cover an area of \361.8 e6km2 with a mean depth of 3682 m, resulting in an estimated volume of \1.332 e9km3.[^o313]

If all of Earth's crustal surface were at the same elevation as a smooth sphere, the depth of the resulting world ocean would be \2.7 to.[^o162] About 97.5% of the water is [saline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saline_water); the remaining 2.5% is [fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fresh_water).[^o163][^o164] Most fresh water, about 68.7%, is present as ice in [ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cap) and [glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacier).[^o165] The remaining 30% is [groundwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater), 1% [surface water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_water) (covering only 2.8% of Earth's land),[^o166] and other small forms of fresh water deposits such as [permafrost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost), [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) in the atmosphere, biological binding, etc.[^o167][^o168]

In Earth's coldest regions, snow survives over the summer and [changes into ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_formation). This accumulated snow and ice eventually forms into [glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glacier), bodies of ice that flow under the influence of their own gravity. [Alpine glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_glaciers) form in mountainous areas, whereas vast [ice sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheets) form over land in polar regions. The flow of glaciers erodes the surface, changing it dramatically, with the formation of [U-shaped valleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-shaped_valley) and other landforms.[^o169] [Sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice) in the Arctic covers an area about as big as the United States, although it is quickly retreating as a consequence of climate change.[^o170]

The average [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) of Earth's oceans is about 35 grams of salt per kilogram of seawater (3.5% salt).[^o297] Most of this salt was released from volcanic activity or extracted from cool igneous rocks.[^o307] The oceans are also a reservoir of dissolved atmospheric gases, which are essential for the survival of many aquatic life forms.[^o309] Sea water has an important influence on the world's climate, with the oceans acting as a large [heat reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_reservoir).[^o303] Shifts in the oceanic temperature distribution can cause significant weather shifts, such as the [El Niño–Southern Oscillation](wikipedia://en/317311).[^o329]

The abundance of water, particularly liquid water, on Earth's surface is a unique feature that distinguishes it from other planets in the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System). Solar System planets with considerable atmospheres do partly host atmospheric water vapor, but they lack surface conditions for stable surface water.[^o171] Despite some [moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite) showing signs of large reservoirs of [extraterrestrial liquid water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_liquid_water), with possibly even more volume than Earth's ocean, all of them are [large bodies of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_largest_lakes_and_seas_in_the_Solar_System) under a kilometers-thick frozen surface layer.[^o172]

## Biosphere {#Biosphere}

*Image: Mollweide Cycle.gif.* An animation of the changing density of [productive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_production) vegetation on land (low in brown; heavy in dark green) and phytoplankton at the [ocean surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface) (low in purple; high in yellow)

Earth is the only known place that has ever been [habitable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_habitability) for life. Earth's [life developed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_life) in Earth's early bodies of water some hundred million years after Earth formed, roughly 4 billion years ago. Earth provides liquid water—an environment where complex [organic molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound) can assemble and interact, and sufficient energy to sustain a [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism).[^o243] Plants and other organisms take up [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) from water, soils, and the atmosphere. These nutrients are constantly recycled between different species.[^o174]

Earth's life has been shaping and inhabiting many particular [ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem) on Earth and has eventually expanded globally, forming an overarching biosphere.[^o175] Earth's life has also over time greatly diversified, allowing the biosphere to have different [biomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biome), which are inhabited by comparatively similar plants and animals.[^o176] The different biomes developed at distinct elevations or [water depths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_depths), planetary temperature [latitudes](wikipedia://en/17616), and on land also with different [humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity). [Earth's species diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latitudinal_gradients_in_species_diversity) and [biomass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomass_%28ecology%29) reach a peak in shallow waters and with [forests, particularly in equatorial, warm and humid conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_rainforest), while freezing [polar regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_regions_of_Earth) and [high altitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpine_tundra), or [extremely arid areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert) are relatively barren of plant and animal life.[^o250]

Therefore, life has impacted Earth, significantly altering Earth's atmosphere and surface over long periods of time, causing changes like the [Great Oxidation Event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Oxidation_Event).[^o177]

### Challenges for life on Earth {#Challenges_for_life_on_Earth}

[Extreme weather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_weather), such as [tropical cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_cyclone), occurs over most of Earth's surface and has a large impact on life in those areas. From 1980 to 2000, these events caused an average of 11,800 human deaths per year.[^o179] Many places are subject to [earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake), [landslides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landslide), [tsunamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsunami), [volcanic eruptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic_eruption), [tornadoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado), [blizzards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blizzard), [floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood), [droughts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drought), [wildfires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildfire), and other calamities and disasters.[^o180] [Human impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment) is felt in many areas due to pollution of the air and water, [acid rain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_rain), loss of vegetation ([overgrazing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overgrazing), [deforestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation), [desertification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desertification)), loss of wildlife, species [extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction),[^o181] [soil degradation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_degradation), [soil depletion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_depletion), and [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion).[^o182] Human activities release greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, which cause [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming).[^o183] This is driving [changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_climate_change) such as the [melting of glaciers and ice sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retreat_of_glaciers_since_1850), a [global rise in average sea levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise), increased risk of drought and wildfires, and migration of species to colder areas.[^o184]

## Human geography {#Human_geography}

*Image: BlackMarble20161km.jpg.* A composite image of [artificial light emissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution) at night on a map of Earth

Humans, who originated from earlier [primates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primate) in Eastern Africa 300,000 years ago, [have since been migrating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_human_migration) around Earth, and with the advent of agriculture in the 10th millennium BCE, have been increasingly [settling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedentism) Earth's land.[^o186] In the 20th century, [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica) became the last continent to be explored and settled by humans, although human presence there remains limited. Since the 19th century, the [human population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_population) has grown exponentially to eight billion in the 2020s,[^o187] and is projected to peak at around ten billion in the second half of the 21st century.[^o188] Most of the growth is expected to take place in [sub-Saharan Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-Saharan_Africa).[^o188]

Distribution and [density of human population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_density#Human_population_density) vary greatly around the world, with the majority living in south to eastern Asia and 90% inhabiting the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere) of Earth,[^o189] partly due to the [hemispherical predominance of the world's land mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_hemisphere), with 68% of the world's land mass being in the Northern Hemisphere.[^o190] Furthermore, since the 19th century, humans have increasingly converged into [urban areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_area), with the majority living in urban areas by the 21st century.[^o315]

Beyond Earth's surface, humans have lived only in a few special-purpose deep [underground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underground_living) and [underwater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_living) presences and a few [space stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_station). The human population remains on Earth's surface, fully dependent on the Earth and the environment it sustains. Since the second half of the 20th century, some hundreds of humans have temporarily [stayed beyond Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_space_travellers_by_first_flight), a tiny fraction of whom have reached another celestial body, the Moon.[^o339][^o191]

*Image: Flag of the United Nations.svg.* The [flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_the_United_Nations) of the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations)

Earth has been subject to extensive human settlement, and humans have developed diverse societies and cultures. Most of Earth's land has been territorially claimed since the 19th century by [sovereign states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereign_state) (countries) separated by [political borders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Border), and [205 such states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_sovereign_states) exist today,[^o192] with only parts of Antarctica and a few small regions [remaining unclaimed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terra_nullius).[^o193] Together, most of these states form the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations), the leading worldwide [intergovernmental organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intergovernmental_organization),[^o194] which extends human governance [over the ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_Sea) and [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Treaty_System), and therefore all of Earth.

### Natural resources and land use {#Natural_resources_and_land_use}

*Image: Global land use for food production, OWID statistic.png.* Earth's land use for human agriculture in 2019

Earth has resources that have been exploited by humans.[^o196] Those termed [non-renewable resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-renewable_resource), such as [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuel), are only replenished over geological timescales.[^o197] Large deposits of fossil fuels are obtained from Earth's crust, consisting of coal, petroleum, and natural gas.[^o198] These deposits are used by humans both for energy production and as feedstock for chemical production.[^o199] Mineral [ore](wikipedia://en/22595) bodies have also been formed within the crust through a process of [ore genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore_genesis), resulting from actions of [magmatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magmatism), erosion, and plate tectonics.[^o200] These metals and other elements are extracted by mining, a process which often causes environmental and health damage.[^o201]

Earth's biosphere produces many useful biological products for humans, including food, wood, [pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical), oxygen, and the recycling of organic waste. The land-based ecosystem depends upon [topsoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topsoil) and fresh water, and the oceanic ecosystem depends on dissolved nutrients washed down from the land.[^o334] In 2019, 39 e6km2 of Earth's land surface consisted of forest and woodlands, 12 e6km2 was shrub and grassland, 40 e6km2 were used for animal feed production and grazing, and 11 e6km2 were cultivated as croplands.[^o314] Of the 1214% of ice-free land that is used for croplands, 2 [percentage points](wikipedia://en/1969007) were irrigated in 2015.[^o202] Humans use natural and manufactured [building materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building_material) to construct shelters and infrastructure.[^o203]

### Environmental impact {#Environmental_impact}

*Image: Global Temperature And Forces With Fahrenheit.svg.* Overall increase in average surface air temperature juxtaposed with natural drivers: human activity has caused increased temperatures, with natural forces adding some variability.[^o205][^o206]

Human activities have impacted Earth's environments. Through activities such as the burning of fossil fuels, humans have been increasing the amount of [greenhouse gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas) in the atmosphere, altering [Earth's energy budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_energy_budget) and climate.[^o183][^o207] It is estimated that global temperatures in the year 2020 were \1.2 C-change warmer than the preindustrial baseline.[^o208] This increase in temperature, known as [global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_warming), has contributed to the [melting of glaciers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retreat_of_glaciers_since_1850), [rising sea levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise), increased risk of drought and wildfires, and migration of species to colder areas.[^o184]

The concept of [planetary boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_boundaries) was introduced to quantify humanity's impact on Earth. Of the nine identified boundaries, five have been crossed: [Biosphere integrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biodiversity_loss), climate change, chemical pollution, destruction of wild habitats, and the [nitrogen cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen_cycle) are thought to have passed the safe threshold.[^o209][^o210] As of 2018, no country meets the basic needs of its population without transgressing planetary boundaries. It is thought possible to provide all basic physical needs globally within sustainable levels of resource use.[^o211]

## In culture {#In_culture}

*Image: Islamic cosmology.jpg.* A combination of different cosmological [Middle Eastern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Eastern) [*axis mundi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_mundi) ideas from the [post-classical age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-classical_age) ([*The Wonders of Creation*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ʿAjā'ib_al-makhlūqāt_wa_gharā'ib_al-mawjūdāt) by [Zakariya al-Qazwini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakariya_al-Qazwini)) showing a flat round Earth surrounded by a range of [Qaf Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Qaf), resting on the back of a giant bull ([Kujata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kujata) or [Behemoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behemoth)), which in turn stands on a vast fish ([Bahamut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bahamut) or [Leviathan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leviathan)) in a [cosmic ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ocean) held up by an angel or [jinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinn)[^o213][^o214]

[Human cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture) have developed many views of the planet.[^o215] Earth is sometimes [personified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personification) as a [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity). In many cultures, it is a [mother goddess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_goddess) that is also the primary [fertility deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertility_deity).[^o216] [Creation myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_myth) in many religions involve the creation of Earth by a supernatural deity or deities.[^o216] The [Gaia hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaia_hypothesis), developed in the mid-20th century, compared Earth's environments and life as a single self-regulating organism, leading to broad stabilization of the conditions of habitability.[^o217][^o218][^o219]

Scientific investigation has resulted in several culturally transformative shifts in people's view of the planet. Initial belief in a [flat Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_Earth) was gradually displaced in [Ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) by the idea of a [spherical Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_Earth), which was attributed to both the philosophers [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) and [Parmenides](wikipedia://en/23575).[^o220][^o221] Earth was generally believed to be [the center of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentrism)[^o222] until the 16th century, when scientists first concluded that it was [a moving object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentrism), one of the planets of the Solar System.[^o252]

It was only during the 19th century that geologists realized [Earth's age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_age) was at least many millions of years.[^o223] [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin) used [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) to estimate the age of Earth to be between 20 million and 400 million years in 1864, sparking a vigorous debate on the subject; it was only when radioactivity and [radioactive dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiometric_dating) were discovered in the late 19th and early 20th centuries that a reliable mechanism for determining Earth's age was established, proving the planet to be billions of years old.[^o224][^o225]

[Images of Earth taken from space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_first_images_of_Earth_from_space), particularly *[Earthrise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthrise)* and *[The Blue Marble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blue_Marble)*, have been credited with altering the way that people viewed the planet that they lived on, called the [overview effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overview_effect), emphasizing its beauty, uniqueness, community, and apparent fragility.[^o226][^o227] In particular, this caused a realization of the scope of effects from human activity on Earth's environment. Enabled by science, particularly [Earth observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_observation),[^o228] humans have started to take [action on environmental issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmentalism) globally,[^o229] acknowledging the impact of humans and the [interconnectedness of Earth's environments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_network).[^o230]
