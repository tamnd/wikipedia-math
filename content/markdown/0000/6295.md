# Chaos theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chaos theory"}

**Chaos theory** is a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and an [interdisciplinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary) area of [scientific study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method). It focuses on underlying patterns and [deterministic](wikipedia://en/522958) laws of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) that are highly sensitive to [initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_conditions). These were once thought to have completely random states of disorder and irregularities.[^o2] The theory states that within the apparent randomness of [chaotic complex systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaotic_complex_system), there are underlying patterns, interconnection, constant feedback loops, repetition, [self-similarity](wikipedia://en/28782), [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals) and [self-organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-organization).[^o3] The butterfly effect, an underlying principle of chaos, describes how a small change in one state of a deterministic [nonlinear system](wikipedia://en/146103) can result in large differences in a later state (meaning there is sensitive dependence on initial conditions).[^o4] A metaphor for this behavior is that a butterfly flapping its wings in [Brazil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazil) can cause or prevent a [tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado) in [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas).[^o5][^o6][^o7]

:::

Small differences in initial conditions, such as those due to errors in measurements or due to rounding errors in [numerical computation](wikipedia://en/21506), can yield widely diverging outcomes for such dynamic systems, rendering long-term prediction of their behavior impossible in general.[^o8] This can happen even though these systems are [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_system_%28mathematics%29), meaning that their future behavior follows a unique evolution[^o9] and is fully determined by their initial conditions, with no [random](wikipedia://en/19196523) elements involved.[^n1] In other words, despite the deterministic nature of these systems, this does not make them predictable.[^n2][^o12] This behavior is known as **deterministic chaos**, or simply **chaos**. The theory was summarized by [Edward Lorenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lorenz) as:[^o13]

Chaos: When the present determines the future but the approximate present does not approximately determine the future.

Chaotic behavior exists in many natural systems, including fluid flow, heartbeat irregularities, weather and climate.[^o102][^o14][^o9] It also occurs spontaneously in some systems with artificial components, such as road traffic.[^o3] This behavior can be studied through the analysis of a chaotic mathematical model or through analytical techniques such as [recurrence plots](wikipedia://en/546120) and [Poincaré maps](wikipedia://en/876732). Chaos theory has applications in a variety of disciplines, including meteorology,[^o9] [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology),[^o15] [sociology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology), [environmental science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_science), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), engineering, [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology), and [pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandemic) [crisis management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_management).[^o16][^o17] The theory formed the basis for such fields of study as complex dynamical systems, edge of chaos theory and [self-assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-assembly) processes.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

Chaos theory concerns deterministic systems which are predictable for some amount of time and then appear to become random. The amount of time for which the behavior of a chaotic system can be effectively predicted depends on three things: How much uncertainty can be tolerated in the forecast, how accurately its current state can be measured, and a time scale depending on the dynamics of the system, called the [Lyapunov time](wikipedia://en/1117315). Some examples of Lyapunov times are: Chaotic electrical circuits, about 1 millisecond; weather systems, a few days (but unproven); the inner solar system, 4 to 5 million years.[^o19] In chaotic systems, the uncertainty in a forecast increases [exponentially](wikipedia://en/191933) with elapsed time. Hence, mathematically, doubling the forecast time more than squares the proportional uncertainty in the forecast. This means, in practice, a meaningful prediction cannot be made over an interval of more than two or three times the Lyapunov time. When meaningful predictions cannot be made, the system appears random.[^n3]

## Chaotic dynamics {#Chaotic_dynamics}

*Image: Chaos Sensitive Dependence.svg.* The [map](wikipedia://en/516931) defined by *x* → 4 *x* (1 – *x*) and *y* → (*x* + *y)* [mod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_operation) 1 displays sensitivity to initial x positions. Here, two series of *x* and *y* values diverge markedly over time from a tiny initial difference.

In common usage, "chaos" means "a state of disorder".[^o22][^o23] However, in chaos theory, the term is defined more precisely. Although no universally accepted mathematical definition of chaos exists, a commonly used definition, originally formulated by [Robert L. Devaney](wikipedia://en/47958305), says that to classify a dynamical system as chaotic, it must have these properties:[^o24]

1. it must be [sensitive to initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitive_dependence_on_initial_conditions),
1. it must be [topologically transitive](wikipedia://en/1308185#Topological_mixing),
1. it must have [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [periodic orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit).

In some cases, the last two properties above have been shown to actually imply sensitivity to initial conditions.[^o25][^o26] In the discrete-time case, this is true for all [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [maps](wikipedia://en/516931) on [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018).[^o27] In these cases, while it is often the most practically significant property, "sensitivity to initial conditions" need not be stated in the definition.

If attention is restricted to [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172), the second property implies the other two.[^o28] An alternative and a generally weaker definition of chaos uses only the first two properties in the above list.[^o29]

### Sensitivity to initial conditions {#Sensitivity_to_initial_conditions}

*Image: SensInitCond.gif.* Lorenz equations used to generate plots for the y variable. The initial conditions for *x* and *z* were kept the same but those for *y* were changed between **\1.001**, **\1.0001** and **\1.00001**. The values for $\rho$, $\sigma$ and $\beta$ were **\45.91**, **16** and **4** respectively. As can be seen from the graph, even the slightest difference in initial values causes significant changes after about 12 seconds of evolution in the three cases. This is an example of sensitive dependence on initial conditions.

**Sensitivity to initial conditions** means that each point in a chaotic system is arbitrarily closely approximated by other points that have significantly different future paths or trajectories. Thus, an arbitrarily small change or perturbation of the current trajectory may lead to significantly different future behavior.[^o3]

Sensitivity to initial conditions is popularly known as the "[butterfly effect](wikipedia://en/4024)", so-called because of the title of a paper given by [Edward Lorenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lorenz) in 1972 to the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science) in Washington, D.C., entitled *Predictability: Does the Flap of a Butterfly's Wings in Brazil set off a Tornado in Texas?*.[^o31] The flapping wing represents a small change in the initial condition of the system, which causes a chain of events that prevents the predictability of large-scale phenomena. Had the butterfly not flapped its wings, the trajectory of the overall system could have been vastly different.

As suggested in Lorenz's book entitled *The Essence of Chaos*, published in 1993,[^o6] "sensitive dependence can serve as an acceptable definition of chaos". In the same book, Lorenz defined the butterfly effect as: "The phenomenon that a small alteration in the state of a dynamical system will cause subsequent states to differ greatly from the states that would have followed without the alteration."[^o6] The above definition is consistent with the sensitive dependence of solutions on initial conditions (SDIC). An idealized skiing model was developed to illustrate the sensitivity of time-varying paths to initial positions.[^o6] A predictability horizon can be determined before the onset of SDIC (i.e., prior to significant separations of initial nearby trajectories).[^o32]

A consequence of sensitivity to initial conditions is that if we start with a limited amount of information about the system (as is usually the case in practice), then beyond a certain time, the system would no longer be predictable. This is most prevalent in the case of weather, which is generally predictable only about a week ahead.[^o33] This does not mean that one cannot assert anything about events far in the future - only that some restrictions on the system are present. For example, we know that the temperature of the surface of the earth will not naturally reach 100 C or fall below \-130 C on earth (during the current [geologic era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geologic_era)), but we cannot predict exactly which day will have the hottest temperature of the year.

In more mathematical terms, the [Lyapunov exponent](wikipedia://en/166441) measures the sensitivity to initial conditions, in the form of rate of exponential divergence from the perturbed initial conditions.[^o34] More specifically, given two starting [trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) in the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) that are infinitesimally close, with initial separation $\delta \mathbf{Z}_0$, the two trajectories end up diverging at a rate given by

$$| \delta\mathbf{Z}(t) | \approx e^{\lambda t} | \delta \mathbf{Z}_0 |,$$

where $t$ is the time and $\lambda$ is the Lyapunov exponent. The rate of separation depends on the orientation of the initial separation vector, so a whole spectrum of Lyapunov exponents can exist. The number of Lyapunov exponents is equal to the number of dimensions of the phase space, though it is common to just refer to the largest one. For example, the maximal Lyapunov exponent (MLE) is most often used, because it determines the overall predictability of the system. A positive MLE, coupled with the solution's boundedness, is usually taken as an indication that the system is chaotic.[^o9]

In addition to the above property, other properties related to sensitivity of initial conditions also exist. These include, for example, [measure-theoretical](wikipedia://en/19873) [mixing](wikipedia://en/1308185) (as discussed in [ergodic](wikipedia://en/5456824) theory) and properties of a [K-system](wikipedia://en/36563911).[^o12]

### Non-periodicity {#Non-periodicity}

A chaotic system may have sequences of values for the evolving variable that exactly repeat themselves, giving periodic behavior starting from any point in that sequence. However, such periodic sequences are repelling rather than attracting, meaning that if the evolving variable is outside the sequence, however close, it will not enter the sequence and in fact, will diverge from it. Thus for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) initial conditions, the variable evolves chaotically with non-periodic behavior.

### Topological mixing {#Topological_mixing}

*Image: LogisticTopMixing1-6.gif.* Six iterations of a set of states $[x,y]$ passed through the logistic map. The first iterate (blue) is the initial condition, which essentially forms a circle. Animation shows the first to the sixth iteration of the circular initial conditions. It can be seen that *mixing* occurs as we progress in iterations. The sixth iteration shows that the points are almost completely scattered in the phase space. Had we progressed further in iterations, the mixing would have been homogeneous and irreversible. The logistic map has equation $x_{k+1} = 4 x_k (1 - x_k )$. To expand the state-space of the logistic map into two dimensions, a second state, $y$, was created as $y_{k+1} = x_k + y_k$, if $x_k + y_k <1$ and $y_{k+1} = x_k + y_k -1$ otherwise.

*Image: Chaos Topological Mixing.png.* This other visualization of the map defined by *x* → 4 *x* (1 – *x*) and *y* → (*x* + *y)* [mod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_operation) 1 also displays [topological mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_mixing). Here, the blue region is transformed by the dynamics first to the purple region, then to the pink and red regions, and eventually to a cloud of vertical lines scattered across the space.

[Topological mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_mixing) (or the weaker condition of topological transitivity) means that the system evolves over time so that any given region or [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) of its [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) eventually overlaps with any other given region. This mathematical concept of "mixing" corresponds to the standard intuition, and the mixing of colored [dyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dye) or fluids is an example of a chaotic system.

Topological mixing is often omitted from popular accounts of chaos, which equate chaos with only sensitivity to initial conditions. However, sensitive dependence on initial conditions alone does not give chaos. For example, consider the simple dynamical system produced by repeatedly doubling an initial value. This system has sensitive dependence on initial conditions everywhere, since any pair of nearby points eventually becomes widely separated. However, this example has no topological mixing, and therefore has no chaos. Indeed, it has extremely simple behavior: all points except 0 tend to positive or negative infinity.

### Topological transitivity {#Topological_transitivity}

A map $f:X \to X$ is said to be topologically transitive if for any pair of non-empty [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) $U, V \subset X$, there exists $k > 0$ such that $f^{k}(U) \cap V \neq \emptyset$. Topological transitivity is a weaker version of [topological mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_mixing). Intuitively, if a map is topologically transitive then given a point *x* and a region *V*, there exists a point *y* near *x* whose orbit passes through *V*. This implies that it is impossible to decompose the system into two open sets.[^n4]

An important related theorem is the Birkhoff Transitivity Theorem. It is easy to see that the existence of a dense orbit implies topological transitivity. The Birkhoff Transitivity Theorem states that if *X* is a [second countable](wikipedia://en/901459), [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832), then topological transitivity implies the existence of a [dense set](wikipedia://en/23523594) of points in *X* that have dense orbits.[^n5]

### Density of periodic orbits {#Density_of_periodic_orbits}

For a chaotic system to have [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [periodic orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbits) means that every point in the space is approached arbitrarily closely by periodic orbits.[^n4] The one-dimensional [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) defined by *x* → 4 *x* (1 – *x*) is one of the simplest systems with density of periodic orbits. For example, $\tfrac{5-\sqrt{5}}{8}$ → $\tfrac{5+\sqrt{5}}{8}$ → $\tfrac{5-\sqrt{5}}{8}$ (or approximately 0.3454915 → 0.9045085 → 0.3454915) is an (unstable) orbit of period 2, and similar orbits exist for periods 4, 8, 16, etc. (indeed, for all the periods specified by [Sharkovskii's theorem](wikipedia://en/87947)).[^n6]

Sharkovskii's theorem is the basis of the Li and Yorke[^o43] (1975) proof that any continuous one-dimensional system that exhibits a regular cycle of period three will also display regular cycles of every other length, as well as completely chaotic orbits.

### Strange attractors {#Strange_attractors}

*Image: TwoLorenzOrbits.jpg.* The [Lorenz attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenz_attractor) displays chaotic behavior. These two plots demonstrate sensitive dependence on initial conditions within the region of phase space occupied by the attractor.

Some dynamical systems, like the one-dimensional [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) defined by *x* → 4 *x* (1 – *x*), are chaotic everywhere, but in many cases chaotic behavior is found only in a subset of phase space. The cases of most interest arise when the chaotic behavior takes place on an [attractor](wikipedia://en/191094), since then a large set of initial conditions leads to orbits that converge to this chaotic region.[^o45]

An easy way to visualize a chaotic attractor is to start with a point in the [basin of attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basin_of_attraction) of the attractor, and then simply plot its subsequent orbit. Because of the topological transitivity condition, this is likely to produce a picture of the entire final attractor, and indeed both orbits shown in the figure on the right give a picture of the general shape of the Lorenz attractor.

Unlike [fixed-point attractors](wikipedia://en/191094#Fixed_point) and [limit cycles](wikipedia://en/452950), the attractors that arise from chaotic systems, known as [strange attractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractor), have great detail and complexity. Strange attractors occur in both [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) dynamical systems (such as the Lorenz system) and in some [discrete](wikipedia://en/8492) systems (such as the [Hénon map](wikipedia://en/485467)). Other discrete dynamical systems have a repelling structure called a [Julia set](wikipedia://en/56434), which forms at the boundary between basins of attraction of fixed points. Julia sets can be thought of as strange repellers. Both strange attractors and Julia sets typically have a [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) structure, and the [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) can be calculated for them.

### Coexisting attractors {#Coexisting_attractors}

*Image: Coexisting Attractors.png.* Coexisting chaotic and non-chaotic attractors within the generalized Lorenz model.[^o59][^o60][^o49] There are 128 orbits in different colors, beginning with different initial conditions for dimensionless time between 0.625 and 5 and a heating parameter r = 680. Chaotic orbits recurrently return close to the saddle point at the origin. Nonchaotic orbits eventually approach one of two stable critical points, as shown with large blue dots. Chaotic and nonchaotic orbits occupy different regions of attraction within the phase space.

In contrast to single type chaotic solutions, studies using Lorenz models[^o47][^o48] have emphasized the importance of considering various types of solutions. For example, coexisting chaotic and non-chaotic may appear within the same model (e.g., the double pendulum system) using the same modeling configurations but different initial conditions. The findings of attractor coexistence, obtained from classical and generalized Lorenz models,[^o59][^o60][^o49] suggested a revised view that "the entirety of weather possesses a dual nature of chaos and order with distinct predictability", in contrast to the conventional view of "weather is chaotic".

### Minimum complexity of a chaotic system {#Minimum_complexity_of_a_chaotic_system}

*Image: Logistic Map Bifurcation Diagram, Matplotlib.svg.* [Bifurcation diagram](wikipedia://en/79173) of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) *x* → *r* *x* (1 – *x*). Each vertical slice shows the attractor for a specific value of *r*. The diagram displays [period-doubling](wikipedia://en/4457659) as *r* increases, eventually producing chaos. Darker points are visited more frequently.

Discrete chaotic systems, such as the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137), can exhibit strange attractors whatever their [dimensionality](wikipedia://en/8398). In contrast, for [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) dynamical systems, the [Poincaré–Bendixson theorem](wikipedia://en/2784317) shows that a strange attractor can only arise in three or more dimensions. [Finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [linear systems](wikipedia://en/722503) are never chaotic; for a dynamical system to display chaotic behavior, it must be either [nonlinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinearity) or infinite-dimensional.

The [Poincaré–Bendixson theorem](wikipedia://en/2784317) states that a two-dimensional differential equation has very regular behavior. The Lorenz attractor discussed below is generated by a system of three [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) such as:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \sigma y - \sigma x, \\ \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \rho x - x z - y, \\ \frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}t} &= x y - \beta z. \end{aligned}$$

where $x$, $y$, and $z$ make up the [system state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_space_representation), $t$ is time, and $\sigma$, $\rho$, $\beta$ are the system [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065). Five of the terms on the right hand side are linear, while two are quadratic; a total of seven terms. Another well-known chaotic attractor is generated by the [Rössler equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rössler_map), which have only one nonlinear term out of seven. Sprott[^o52] found a three-dimensional system with just five terms, that had only one nonlinear term, which exhibits chaos for certain parameter values. Zhang and Heidel[^o53][^o54] showed that, at least for dissipative and conservative quadratic systems, three-dimensional quadratic systems with only three or four terms on the right-hand side cannot exhibit chaotic behavior. The reason is, simply put, that solutions to such systems are [asymptotic](wikipedia://en/641995) to a two-dimensional surface and therefore solutions are well behaved.

While the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem shows that a continuous dynamical system on the Euclidean [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) cannot be chaotic, two-dimensional continuous systems with [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) can still exhibit some chaotic properties.[^o55]

The above set of three ordinary differential equations has been referred to as the three-dimensional Lorenz model.[^o56] Since 1963, higher-dimensional Lorenz models have been developed in numerous studies[^o57][^o58][^o59][^o60] for examining the impact of an increased degree of nonlinearity, as well as its collective effect with heating and dissipations, on solution stability.

:::

### Chaos and linear systems {#Chaos_and_linear_systems}

Perhaps surprisingly, chaos may occur also in linear systems, provided they are infinite dimensional.[^o62] A theory of linear chaos is being developed in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180).[^o63]

[Quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) is also often considered as a prime example of linear non chaotic theory, that dampens out chaotic behaviour in the same manner that [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) dampens out [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664), this is actually not the case for quantum mechanical systems with infinite degrees of freedom, such as strongly correlated systems that do exhibit forms of [nano scale turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_turbulence).[^o64][^o65]

## Other characteristics of chaos {#Other_characteristics_of_chaos}

### Infinite dimensional maps {#Infinite_dimensional_maps}

The straightforward generalization of coupled discrete maps[^o68] is based upon convolution integral which mediates interaction between spatially distributed maps: $\psi_{n+1}(\vec r,t) = \int K(\vec r - \vec r^{,},t) f [\psi_{n}(\vec r^{,},t) ]d {\vec r}^{,}$,

where kernel $K(\vec r - \vec r^{,},t)$ is propagator derived as Green function of a relevant physical system,[^o69]

$f [\psi_{n}(\vec r,t) ]$ might be logistic map alike $\psi \rightarrow G \psi [1 - \tanh (\psi)]$ or [complex map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_map). For examples of complex maps the [Julia set](wikipedia://en/56434) $f[\psi] = \psi^2$ or [Ikeda map](wikipedia://en/3185317) $\psi_{n+1} = A + B \psi_n e^{i (|\psi_n|^2 + C)}$ may serve. When wave propagation problems at distance $L=ct$ with wavelength $\lambda=2\pi/k$ are considered the kernel $K$ may have a form of Green function for [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874):.[^o70][^o71]

$$K(\vec r - \vec r^{,},L) = \frac {ik\exp[ikL]}{2\pi L}\exp[\frac {ik|\vec r-\vec r^{,}|^2}{2 L} ].$$

### Spontaneous order {#Spontaneous_order}

Under the right conditions, chaos spontaneously evolves into a lockstep pattern. In the [Kuramoto model](wikipedia://en/2938694), four conditions suffice to produce synchronization in a chaotic system. Examples include the [coupled oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupled_oscillation) of [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127)' pendulums, fireflies, [neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuron), the [London Millennium Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Millennium_Bridge) resonance, and large arrays of [Josephson junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_junctions).[^n7]

Moreover, from the theoretical physics standpoint, dynamical chaos itself, in its most general manifestation, is a spontaneous order. The essence here is that most orders in nature arise from the [spontaneous breakdown](wikipedia://en/297466) of various symmetries. This large family of phenomena includes elasticity, superconductivity, ferromagnetism, and many others. According to the [supersymmetric theory of stochastic dynamics](wikipedia://en/53961341), chaos, or more precisely, its stochastic generalization, is also part of this family. The corresponding symmetry being broken is the [topological supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/443757) which is hidden in all [stochastic (partial) differential equations](wikipedia://en/1361454), and the corresponding [order parameter](wikipedia://en/54423) is a [field-theoretic](wikipedia://en/25267) embodiment of the butterfly effect.[^o74]

### Combinatorial (or complex) chaos {#Combinatorial_(or_complex)_chaos}

There are also definitions of chaos that don't require the sensitivity on initial conditions property, such as combinatorial chaos (I.e. applying recursively a discrete combinatorial action).[^o76] This is also comparable and similar to chaos generated by [cellular automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_automata). This is important because, as this type of chaos can be equivalent to a [turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403), it is possible to execute computation with such dynamical systems, which is an intuitive way to see that the [halting problem](wikipedia://en/21391870) is not decidable, implying that some computational algorithms may never end. Unpredictability in this type of system is typically of a different nature than that of other types of chaos. [^o77]

## History {#History}

*Image: Barnsley fern plotted with VisSim.PNG.* [Barnsley fern](wikipedia://en/25721564) created using the [chaos game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_game). Natural forms (ferns, clouds, mountains, etc.) may be recreated through an [iterated function system](wikipedia://en/505225) (IFS).

[James Clerk Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) was the first scientist to emphasize the importance of initial conditions, and he is seen as being one of the earliest to discuss chaos theory, with work in the 1860s and 1870s.[^o80][^o81][^o82] In the 1880s, while studying the [three-body problem](wikipedia://en/1208420), [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) found that there can be orbits that are nonperiodic, and yet not forever increasing nor approaching a fixed point.[^o83][^o84][^o85] In 1898, [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675) published an influential study of the motion of a free particle gliding frictionlessly on a surface of constant negative curvature, called "[Hadamard's billiards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard's_billiards)". Hadamard was able to show that all trajectories are unstable,[^o86] in that all particle trajectories diverge exponentially from one another, with a positive [Lyapunov exponent](wikipedia://en/166441).[^o87]

Later studies, also on the topic of nonlinear [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations), were carried out by [George David Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/30871906),[^o88] [Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Nikolaevich_Kolmogorov),[^o89][^o90][^o91] [Mary Lucy Cartwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Lucy_Cartwright) and [John Edensor Littlewood](wikipedia://en/158371),[^o92] and [Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294).[^o93] Experimentalists and mathematicians had encountered turbulence in fluid motion, chaotic behaviour in society and economy, nonperiodic oscillation in radio circuits and fractal patterns in nature without the benefit of a theory to explain what they were seeing.

Despite initial insights in the first half of the twentieth century, chaos theory became formalized as such only after mid-century, when it first became evident to some scientists that [linear theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_theory) which is [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000) and [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122), and which was the prevailing system theory at that time, simply could not explain the observed behavior of certain experiments like that of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) which has [jump](wikipedia://en/2180750) and [erratic](wikipedia://en/292222) behaviours. Both of these observations underline the connection of chaos to either [stochastic](wikipedia://en/292222) or [non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear) dynamical systems, but definitely non-[differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable) and non-[continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) time evolution.

What had been attributed to measure imprecision and simple "[noise](wikipedia://en/41593)" was considered by chaos theorists as a full component of the studied systems. In 1959 [Boris Valerianovich Chirikov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Chirikov) proposed a criterion for the emergence of classical chaos in Hamiltonian systems ([Chirikov criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirikov_criterion)). He applied this criterion to explain some experimental results on [plasma confinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_confinement) in open mirror traps.[^o94][^o95] This is regarded as the very first physical theory of chaos, which succeeded in explaining a concrete experiment. And Boris Chirikov himself is considered as a pioneer in classical and quantum chaos.[^o96][^o97][^o98]

The main catalyst for the development of chaos theory was the electronic computer. Much of the mathematics of chaos theory involves the repeated [iteration](wikipedia://en/68833) of simple mathematical formulas, which would be impractical to do by hand. Electronic computers made these repeated calculations practical, while figures and images made it possible to visualize these systems. As a graduate student in Chihiro Hayashi's laboratory at Kyoto University, Yoshisuke Ueda was experimenting with analog computers and noticed, on November 27, 1961, what he called "randomly transitional phenomena". Yet his advisor did not agree with his conclusions at the time, and did not allow him to report his findings until 1970.[^n8][^n9]

*Image: Airplane vortex edit.jpg.* [Turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664) in the [tip vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingtip_vortices) from an [airplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airplane) wing. Studies of the critical point beyond which a system creates turbulence were important for chaos theory, analyzed for example by the [Soviet physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_physicists) [Lev Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau), who developed the [Landau-Hopf theory of turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau-Hopf_theory_of_turbulence). [David Ruelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ruelle) and [Floris Takens](wikipedia://en/1790146) later predicted, against Landau, that [fluid turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_turbulence) could develop through a [strange attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractor), a main concept of chaos theory.

[Edward Lorenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lorenz) was an early pioneer of the theory. His interest in chaos came about accidentally through his work on [weather prediction](wikipedia://en/19904) in 1961.[^o101][^o102] Lorenz and his collaborator [Ellen Fetter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellen_Fetter) and [Margaret Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Hamilton_%28software_engineer%29)[^o103] were using a simple digital computer, a [Royal McBee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_McBee) [LGP-30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGP-30), to run weather simulations. They wanted to see a sequence of data again, and to save time they started the simulation in the middle of its course. They did this by entering a printout of the data that corresponded to conditions in the middle of the original simulation. To their surprise, the weather the machine began to predict was completely different from the previous calculation. They tracked this down to the computer printout. The computer worked with 6-digit precision, but the printout rounded variables off to a 3-digit number, so a value like 0.506127 printed as 0.506. This difference is tiny, and the consensus at the time would have been that it should have no practical effect. However, Lorenz discovered that small changes in initial conditions produced large changes in long-term outcome.[^o104] Lorenz's discovery, which gave its name to [Lorenz attractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenz_attractor), showed that even detailed atmospheric modeling cannot, in general, make precise long-term weather predictions.

In 1963, [Benoit Mandelbrot](wikipedia://en/3999), studying [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), discovered that noise in many phenomena (including [stock prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_market) and [telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone) circuits) was patterned like a [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172), a set of points with infinite roughness and detail.[^o105] Mandelbrot described both the "Noah effect" (in which sudden discontinuous changes can occur) and the "Joseph effect" (in which persistence of a value can occur for a while, yet suddenly change afterwards).[^o106][^o107] In 1967, he published "[How long is the coast of Britain? Statistical self-similarity and fractional dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_Long_Is_the_Coast_of_Britain?_Statistical_Self-Similarity_and_Fractional_Dimension)", showing that a coastline's length varies with the scale of the measuring instrument, resembles itself at all scales, and is infinite in length for an [infinitesimally](wikipedia://en/160990) small measuring device.[^o108] Arguing that a ball of twine appears as a point when viewed from far away (0-dimensional), a ball when viewed from fairly near (3-dimensional), or a curved strand (1-dimensional), he argued that the dimensions of an object are relative to the observer and may be fractional. An object whose irregularity is constant over different scales ("self-similarity") is a [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) (examples include the [Menger sponge](wikipedia://en/208811), the [Sierpiński gasket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpiński_gasket), and the [Koch curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch_curve) or *snowflake*, which is infinitely long yet encloses a finite space and has a [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) of circa 1.2619). In 1982, Mandelbrot published *[The Fractal Geometry of Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fractal_Geometry_of_Nature)*, which became a classic of chaos theory.[^o109]

In December 1977, the [New York Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Academy_of_Sciences) organized the first symposium on chaos, attended by David Ruelle, [Robert May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_May,_Baron_May_of_Oxford), [James A. Yorke](wikipedia://en/641019) (coiner of the term "chaos" as used in mathematics), [Robert Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Shaw_%28physicist%29), and the meteorologist Edward Lorenz. The following year Pierre Coullet and Charles Tresser published "Itérations d'endomorphismes et groupe de renormalisation", and [Mitchell Feigenbaum](wikipedia://en/1093540)'s article "Quantitative Universality for a Class of Nonlinear Transformations" finally appeared in a journal, after 3 years of referee rejections.[^o110][^o111] Thus Feigenbaum (1975) and Coullet & Tresser (1978) discovered the [universality](wikipedia://en/1752072) in chaos, permitting the application of chaos theory to many different phenomena.

In 1979, [Albert J. Libchaber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_J._Libchaber), during a symposium organized in Aspen by [Pierre Hohenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hohenberg), presented his experimental observation of the [bifurcation](wikipedia://en/2326042) cascade that leads to chaos and turbulence in [Rayleigh–Bénard convection](wikipedia://en/36589154) systems. He was awarded the [Wolf Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Physics) in 1986 along with [Mitchell J. Feigenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_J._Feigenbaum) for their inspiring achievements.[^o112]

In 1986, the New York Academy of Sciences co-organized with the [National Institute of Mental Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Mental_Health) and the [Office of Naval Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Naval_Research) the first important conference on chaos in biology and medicine. There, [Bernardo Huberman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernardo_Huberman) presented a mathematical model of the [eye tracking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eye_movement) dysfunction among people with [schizophrenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schizophrenia).[^o113] This led to a renewal of [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology) in the 1980s through the application of chaos theory, for example, in the study of pathological [cardiac cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_cycle).

In 1987, [Per Bak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_Bak), [Chao Tang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chao_Tang) and [Kurt Wiesenfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Wiesenfeld) published a paper in *[Physical Review Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review_Letters)*[^o114] describing for the first time [self-organized criticality](wikipedia://en/718855) (SOC), considered one of the mechanisms by which [complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexity) arises in nature.

Alongside largely lab-based approaches such as the [Bak–Tang–Wiesenfeld sandpile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bak–Tang–Wiesenfeld_sandpile), many other investigations have focused on large-scale natural or social systems that are known (or suspected) to display [scale-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance) behavior. Although these approaches were not always welcomed (at least initially) by specialists in the subjects examined, SOC has nevertheless become established as a strong candidate for explaining a number of natural phenomena, including [earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake), (which, long before SOC was discovered, were known as a source of scale-invariant behavior such as the [Gutenberg–Richter law](wikipedia://en/4903968) describing the statistical distribution of earthquake sizes, and the [Omori law](wikipedia://en/233529)[^o115] describing the frequency of aftershocks), [solar flares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_flare), fluctuations in economic systems such as [financial markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_market) (references to SOC are common in [econophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econophysics)), landscape formation, [forest fires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_fire), [landslides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landslide), [epidemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemic), and [biological evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_evolution) (where SOC has been invoked, for example, as the dynamical mechanism behind the theory of "[punctuated equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctuated_equilibrium)" put forward by [Niles Eldredge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niles_Eldredge) and [Stephen Jay Gould](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Jay_Gould)). Given the implications of a scale-free distribution of event sizes, some researchers have suggested that another phenomenon that should be considered an example of SOC is the occurrence of [wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War). These investigations of SOC have included both attempts at modelling (either developing new models or adapting existing ones to the specifics of a given natural system), and extensive data analysis to determine the existence and/or characteristics of natural scaling laws.

Also in 1987 [James Gleick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gleick) published *[Chaos: Making a New Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos:_Making_a_New_Science)*, which became a best-seller and introduced the general principles of chaos theory as well as its history to the broad public.[^o116] Initially the domain of a few, isolated individuals, chaos theory progressively emerged as a transdisciplinary and institutional discipline, mainly under the name of [nonlinear systems](wikipedia://en/146103) analysis. Alluding to [Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn)'s concept of a [paradigm shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm_shift) exposed in *[The Structure of Scientific Revolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions)* (1962), many "chaologists" (as some described themselves) claimed that this new theory was an example of such a shift, a thesis upheld by Gleick.

The availability of cheaper, more powerful computers broadens the applicability of chaos theory. Currently, chaos theory remains an active area of research,[^o117] involving many different disciplines such as [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939),[^o118] [social systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_systems),[^o119] [population modeling](wikipedia://en/11397469), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904), [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773), [computational neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_neuroscience), [pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandemic) [crisis management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crisis_management),[^o16][^o17] etc.

## A popular but inaccurate analogy for chaos {#A_popular_but_inaccurate_analogy_for_chaos}

The sensitive dependence on initial conditions (i.e., butterfly effect) has been illustrated using the following folklore:[^o116]

For want of a nail, the shoe was lost. For want of a shoe, the horse was lost. For want of a horse, the rider was lost. For want of a rider, the battle was lost. For want of a battle, the kingdom was lost. And all for the want of a horseshoe nail.

Based on the above, many people mistakenly believe that the impact of a tiny initial perturbation monotonically increases with time and that any tiny perturbation can eventually produce a large impact on numerical integrations. However, in 2008, Lorenz stated that he did not feel that this verse described true chaos but that it better illustrated the simpler phenomenon of instability and that the verse implicitly suggests that subsequent small events will not reverse the outcome.[^o121] Based on the analysis, the verse only indicates divergence, not boundedness.[^o7] Boundedness is important for the finite size of a butterfly pattern.[^o7] The characteristic of the aforementioned verse was described as "finite-time sensitive dependence".[^o122]

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Textile cone.JPG.* A [Conus textile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conus_textile) shell, similar in appearance to [Rule 30](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_30), a [cellular automaton](wikipedia://en/54342) with chaotic behaviour[^o125]

Although chaos theory was born from observing weather patterns, it has become applicable to a variety of other situations. Some areas benefiting from chaos theory today are [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics),[^o126][^o127][^o128] [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering),[^o129][^o130] [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance),[^o131][^o132][^o133][^o134][^o135] [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology),[^o15] [physics](wikipedia://en/22939),[^o136][^o137][^o138] [politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics),[^o139][^o140] [population dynamics](wikipedia://en/501466),[^o141] and [robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BEAM_robotics). A few categories are listed below with examples, but this is by no means a comprehensive list as new applications are appearing.

### Cryptography {#Cryptography}

Chaos theory has been used for many years in [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432). In the past few decades, chaos and nonlinear dynamics have been used in the design of hundreds of [cryptographic primitives](wikipedia://en/4928985). These algorithms include image [encryption algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption_algorithms), [hash functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hash_functions), [secure pseudo-random number generators](wikipedia://en/182249), [stream ciphers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_ciphers), [watermarking](wikipedia://en/1061511), and [steganography](wikipedia://en/28733).[^o143] The majority of these algorithms are based on uni-modal chaotic maps and a big portion of these algorithms use the control parameters and the initial condition of the chaotic maps as their keys.[^o144] From a wider perspective, without loss of generality, the similarities between the chaotic maps and the cryptographic systems is the main motivation for the design of chaos based cryptographic algorithms.[^o143] One type of encryption, secret key or [symmetric key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_key), relies on [diffusion and confusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_and_confusion), which is modeled well by chaos theory.[^o145]

### Robotics {#Robotics}

Robotics is another area that has recently benefited from chaos theory. Instead of robots acting in a trial-and-error type of refinement to interact with their environment, chaos theory has been used to build a [predictive model](wikipedia://en/2538775).[^o147] Chaotic dynamics have been exhibited by [passive walking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_dynamics) biped robots.[^o148]

### Biology {#Biology}

For over a hundred years, biologists have been keeping track of populations of different species with [population models](wikipedia://en/11397469). Most models are [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122), but recently scientists have been able to implement chaotic models in certain populations.[^o150] For example, a study on models of [Canadian lynx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_lynx) showed there was chaotic behavior in the population growth.[^o151] Chaos can also be found in ecological systems, such as [hydrology](wikipedia://en/13435). While a chaotic model for hydrology has its shortcomings, there is still much to learn from looking at the data through the lens of chaos theory.[^o152] Another biological application is found in [cardiotocography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiotocography). Fetal surveillance is a delicate balance of obtaining accurate information while being as noninvasive as possible. Better models of warning signs of [fetal hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrauterine_hypoxia) can be obtained through chaotic modeling.[^o153]

As Perry points out, [modeling](wikipedia://en/20590) of chaotic [time series](wikipedia://en/406624) in [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_ecology) is helped by constraint.[^o154] There is always potential difficulty in distinguishing real chaos from chaos that is only in the model.[^o154] Hence both constraint in the model and or duplicate time series data for comparison will be helpful in constraining the model to something close to the reality, for example Perry & Wall 1984.[^o154] [Gene-for-gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene-for-gene) co-evolution sometimes shows chaotic dynamics in [allele frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele_frequencies).[^o155] Adding variables exaggerates this: Chaos is more common in [models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_model) incorporating additional variables to reflect additional facets of real populations.[^o155] [Robert M. May](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._May) himself did some of these foundational crop co-evolution studies, and this in turn helped shape the entire field.[^o155] Even for a steady environment, merely combining one [crop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop) and one [pathogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathogen) may result in [quasi-periodic-](wikipedia://en/25643591) or [chaotic-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaotic_oscillation) oscillations in pathogen [population](wikipedia://en/27585).[^o156]p. 169

### Economics {#Economics}

It is possible that economic models can also be improved through an application of chaos theory, but predicting the health of an economic system and what factors influence it most is an extremely complex task.[^o158] Economic and financial systems are fundamentally different from those in the classical natural sciences since the former are inherently stochastic in nature, as they result from the interactions of people, and thus pure deterministic models are unlikely to provide accurate representations of the data. The empirical literature that tests for chaos in economics and finance presents very mixed results, in part due to confusion between specific tests for chaos and more general tests for non-linear relationships.[^o159]

Chaos could be found in economics by the means of [recurrence quantification analysis](wikipedia://en/2201538). In fact, Orlando et al.[^o160] by the means of the so-called recurrence quantification correlation index were able to detect hidden changes in time series. Then, the same technique was employed to detect transitions from laminar (regular) to turbulent (chaotic) phases as well as differences between macroeconomic variables and highlight hidden features of economic dynamics.[^o161] Finally, chaos theory could help in modeling how an economy operates as well as in embedding shocks due to external events such as COVID-19.[^o162]

### Finite predictability in weather and climate {#Finite_predictability_in_weather_and_climate}

Due to the sensitive dependence of solutions on initial conditions (SDIC), also known as the butterfly effect, chaotic systems like the Lorenz 1963 model imply a finite predictability horizon. This means that while accurate predictions are possible over a finite time period, they are not feasible over an infinite time span. Considering the nature of Lorenz's chaotic solutions, the committee led by Charney et al. in 1966[^o164] extrapolated a doubling time of five days from a general circulation model, suggesting a predictability limit of two weeks. This connection between the five-day doubling time and the two-week predictability limit was also recorded in a 1969 report by the Global Atmospheric Research Program (GARP).[^o165] To acknowledge the combined direct and indirect influences from the Mintz and Arakawa model and Lorenz's models, as well as the leadership of Charney et al., Shen et al.[^o166] refer to the two-week predictability limit as the "Predictability Limit Hypothesis," drawing an analogy to Moore's Law.

### Other areas {#Other_areas}

In chemistry, predicting gas solubility is essential to manufacturing [polymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymers), but models using [particle swarm optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_swarm_optimization) (PSO) tend to converge to the wrong points. An improved version of PSO has been created by introducing chaos, which keeps the simulations from getting stuck.[^o168] In [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182), especially when observing asteroids, applying chaos theory leads to better predictions about when these objects will approach Earth and other planets.[^o169] Four of the five [moons of Pluto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moons_of_Pluto) rotate chaotically. In [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics) and [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), the study of large arrays of [Josephson junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_junctions) benefitted greatly from chaos theory.[^n7] Closer to home, coal mines have always been dangerous places where frequent natural gas leaks cause many deaths. Until recently, there was no reliable way to predict when they would occur. But these gas leaks have chaotic tendencies that, when properly modeled, can be predicted fairly accurately.[^o171]

Chaos theory can be applied outside of the natural sciences, but historically nearly all such studies have suffered from lack of reproducibility; poor external validity; and/or inattention to cross-validation, resulting in poor predictive accuracy (if out-of-sample prediction has even been attempted). Glass[^o172] and Mandell and Selz[^o173] have found that no EEG study has as yet indicated the presence of strange attractors or other signs of chaotic behavior.

Redington and Reidbord (1992) attempted to demonstrate that the human heart could display chaotic traits. They monitored the changes in between-heartbeat intervals for a single psychotherapy patient as she moved through periods of varying emotional intensity during a therapy session. Results were admittedly inconclusive. Not only were there ambiguities in the various plots the authors produced to purportedly show evidence of chaotic dynamics (spectral analysis, phase trajectory, and autocorrelation plots), but also when they attempted to compute a Lyapunov exponent as more definitive confirmation of chaotic behavior, the authors found they could not reliably do so.[^o174]

In their 1995 paper, Metcalf and Allen[^o175] maintained that they uncovered in animal behavior a pattern of period doubling leading to chaos. The authors examined a well-known response called schedule-induced polydipsia, by which an animal deprived of food for certain lengths of time will drink unusual amounts of water when the food is at last presented. The control parameter (r) operating here was the length of the interval between feedings, once resumed. The authors were careful to test a large number of animals and to include many replications, and they designed their experiment so as to rule out the likelihood that changes in response patterns were caused by different starting places for r.

Time series and first delay plots provide the best support for the claims made, showing a fairly clear march from periodicity to irregularity as the feeding times were increased. The various phase trajectory plots and spectral analyses, on the other hand, do not match up well enough with the other graphs or with the overall theory to lead inexorably to a chaotic diagnosis. For example, the phase trajectories do not show a definite progression towards greater and greater complexity (and away from periodicity); the process seems quite muddied. Also, where Metcalf and Allen saw periods of two and six in their spectral plots, there is room for alternative interpretations. All of this ambiguity necessitate some serpentine, post-hoc explanation to show that results fit a chaotic model.

By adapting a model of career counseling to include a chaotic interpretation of the relationship between employees and the job market, Amundson and Bright found that better suggestions can be made to people struggling with career decisions.[^o176] Modern organizations are increasingly seen as open [complex adaptive systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_adaptive_system) with fundamental natural nonlinear structures, subject to internal and external forces that may contribute chaos. For instance, [team building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Team_building) and [group development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_development) is increasingly being researched as an inherently unpredictable system, as the uncertainty of different individuals meeting for the first time makes the trajectory of the team unknowable.[^o177]

*Image: BML N=200 P=32.png.* The [BML traffic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BML_traffic_model): The red cars and blue cars take turns to move; the red ones only move upwards, and the blue ones move rightwards. Every time, all the cars of the same colour try to move one step if there is no car in front of it. Here, the model has self-organized in a somewhat geometric pattern where there are some traffic jams and some areas where cars can move at top speed.

Traffic forecasting may benefit from applications of chaos theory. Better predictions of when a congestion will occur would allow measures to be taken to disperse it before it would have occurred. Combining chaos theory principles with a few other methods has led to a more accurate short-term prediction model, such as the [BML traffic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BML_traffic_model).[^o178]

Chaos theory has been applied to environmental [water cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_cycle) data (also [hydrological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrological) data), such as rainfall and streamflow.[^o179] These studies have yielded controversial results, because the methods for detecting a chaotic signature are often relatively subjective. Early studies tended to "succeed" in finding chaos, whereas subsequent studies and meta-analyses called those studies into question and provided explanations for why these datasets are not likely to have low-dimension chaotic dynamics.[^o180]
