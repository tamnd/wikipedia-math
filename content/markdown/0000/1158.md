# Algebraic number

*Image: Isosceles right triangle with legs length 1.svg.* The square root of 2 is an algebraic number equal to the length of the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) of a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) with legs of length 1.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **algebraic number** is a number that is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of a non-zero [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in one variable with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) (or, equivalently, [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024)) coefficients. For example, the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) $(1 + \sqrt{5})/2$ is an algebraic number, because it is a root of the polynomial $x^2 - x - 1$, i.e., a solution to the equation $x^2 - x - 1 = 0$, and the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $1 + i$ is algebraic because it is a root of the polynomial $x^4 + 4$. Algebraic numbers include all [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), and [*n*-th roots of integers](wikipedia://en/235029).

:::

Algebraic [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) are closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, and hence form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), denoted $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$. The set of algebraic [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\overline{\mathbb{Q}} \cap \mathbb{R}$ is also a field.

Numbers which are not algebraic are called [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325) and include [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) and $e$. There are [countably infinite](wikipedia://en/6026) algebraic numbers, hence [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) real (or complex) numbers (in the sense of [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)) are transcendental.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- All [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) are algebraic. Any rational number, expressed as the quotient of an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $a$ and a (non-zero) [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $b$, satisfies the above definition, because $x = \tfrac{a}{b}$ is the root of a non-zero polynomial, namely $bx - a$.[^o4]
- [Quadratic irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/305331), irrational solutions of a quadratic polynomial $ax^{2} + bx + c$ with integer coefficients $a$, $b$, and $c$, are algebraic numbers. If the quadratic polynomial is monic ($a = 1$), the roots are further qualified as [quadratic integers](wikipedia://en/21834785).
  - [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628), complex numbers $a + bi$ for which both $a$ and $b$ are integers, are also quadratic integers. This is because $a + bi$ and $a - bi$ are the two roots of the quadratic $x^{2} - 2ax + a^{2} + b^{2}$.
- A [constructible number](wikipedia://en/7439) can be constructed from a given unit length using a straightedge and compass. It includes all quadratic irrational roots, all rational numbers, and all numbers that can be formed from these using the [basic arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operations) and the extraction of square roots. (By designating cardinal directions for +1, −1, +$i$, and −$i$, complex numbers such as $3+i \sqrt{2}$ are considered constructible.)
- Any expression formed from algebraic numbers using any finite combination of the basic arithmetic operations and extraction of [$n$th roots](wikipedia://en/235029) gives another algebraic number.
- Polynomial roots that cannot be expressed in terms of the basic arithmetic operations and extraction of $n$th roots (such as the roots of $x^{5} - x + 1$). [That happens with many](wikipedia://en/152518) but not all polynomials of degree 5 or higher.
- Values of [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) of rational multiples of $\pi$ (except when undefined): for example, $\cos \tfrac{\pi}{7}$, $\cos \tfrac{3\pi}{7}$, and $\cos \tfrac{5\pi}{7}$ satisfy $8x^{3} - 4x^{2} - 4x + 1 = 0$. This polynomial is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) over the rationals and so the three cosines are *conjugate* algebraic numbers. Likewise, $\tan \tfrac{3\pi}{16}$, $\tan \tfrac{7\pi}{16}$, $\tan \tfrac{11\pi}{16}$, and $\tan \tfrac{15\pi}{16}$ satisfy the irreducible polynomial $x^{4} - 4x^{3} - 6x^{2} + 4x + 1 = 0$, and so are conjugate [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981). This is the equivalent of angles which, when measured in degrees, have rational numbers.[^n1]
- Some but not all irrational numbers are algebraic:
  - The numbers $\sqrt{2}$ and $\frac{ \sqrt[3]{3} }{ 2 }$ are algebraic since they are roots of polynomials $x^{2} - 2$ and $8x^{3} - 3$, respectively.
  - The [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) $\varphi$ is algebraic since it is a root of the polynomial $x^{2} - x - 1$.
  - The numbers [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) and [e](wikipedia://en/9633) are not algebraic numbers (see the [Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/348976)).[^o6]

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Algebraicszoom.png.* Algebraic numbers on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) colored by degree (bright orange/red = 1, green = 2, blue = 3, yellow = 4). The larger points come from polynomials with smaller integer coefficients.

- If a polynomial with rational coefficients is multiplied through by the [least common denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_common_denominator), the resulting polynomial with integer coefficients has the same roots. This shows that an algebraic number can be equivalently defined as a root of a polynomial with either integer or rational coefficients.
- Given an algebraic number, there is a unique [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) with rational coefficients of least [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) that has the number as a root. This polynomial is called its [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106). If its minimal polynomial has degree $n$, then the algebraic number is said to be of **degree $n$**. For example, all [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) have degree 1, and an algebraic number of degree 2 is a [quadratic irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_irrational).
- The algebraic numbers are [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [in the reals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Densely_ordered). This follows from the fact they contain the rational numbers, which are dense in the reals themselves.
- The set of algebraic numbers is countable,[^n2][^n3] and therefore its [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) as a subset of the complex numbers is 0 (essentially, the algebraic numbers take up no space in the complex numbers). That is to say, ["almost all"](wikipedia://en/44688) real and complex numbers are transcendental.
- All algebraic numbers are [computable](wikipedia://en/6206) and therefore [definable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definable_number) and [arithmetical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetical_numbers).
- For real numbers $a$ and $b$, the complex number $a + bi$ is algebraic if and only if both $a$ and $b$ are algebraic.[^n4]

### Degree of simple extensions of the rationals as a criterion to algebraicity {#Degree_of_simple_extensions_of_the_rationals_as_a_criterion_to_algebraicity}

For any $\alpha$, the [simple extension](wikipedia://en/4771726) of the rationals by $\alpha$, denoted by $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ (whose elements are the $f(\alpha)$ for $f$ a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) with rational coefficients which is defined at $\alpha$), is of finite [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) if and only if $\alpha$ is an algebraic number.

The condition of finite degree means that there is a fixed set of numbers $\{a_i\}$ of finite [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) $k$ with elements in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ such that $\textstyle \mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^k a_i \mathbb{Q}$; that is, each element of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ can be written as a sum $\textstyle \sum_{i=1}^k a_i q_i$ for some rational coefficients $\{q_i \}$.

Since the $a_i$ are themselves members of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, each can be expressed as sums of products of rational numbers and powers of $\alpha$, and therefore this condition is equivalent to the requirement that for some finite $n$,

$$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha) = \biggl\lbrace \sum_{i=-n}^n \alpha^{i} q_i \mathbin{\bigg|} q_i\in \mathbb{Q}\biggr\rbrace.$$

The latter condition is equivalent to $\alpha^{n+1}$, itself a member of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, being expressible as $\textstyle \sum_{i=-n}^n \alpha^i q_i$ for some rationals $\{q_i\}$, so $\textstyle \alpha^{2n+1} = \sum_{i=0}^{2n} \alpha^i q_{i-n}$ or, equivalently, $\alpha$ is a root of $\textstyle x^{2n+1}-\sum_{i=0}^{2n} x^i q_{i-n}$; that is, an algebraic number with a minimal polynomial of degree not larger than $2n+1$.

It can similarly be proven that for any finite set of algebraic numbers $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$... $\alpha_n$, the field extension $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, ... \alpha_n)$ has a finite degree.

## Field {#Field}

*Image: Algebraic number in the complex plane.png.* Algebraic numbers colored by degree (blue = 4, cyan = 3, red = 2, green = 1). The unit circle is black.

The sum, difference, product, and quotient (if the denominator is nonzero) of two algebraic numbers is again algebraic:

For any two algebraic numbers $\alpha$, $\beta$, this follows directly from the fact that the [simple extension](wikipedia://en/4771726) $\mathbb{Q}(\gamma)$, for $\gamma$ being either $\alpha+\beta$, $\alpha-\beta$, $\alpha\beta$ or (for $\beta\ne 0$) $\alpha/\beta$, is a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of the finite-[degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) field extension $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha,\beta)$, and therefore has a finite degree itself, from which it follows (as shown above) that $\gamma$ is algebraic.

An alternative way of showing this is constructively, by using the [resultant](wikipedia://en/2195020).

Algebraic numbers thus form a [field](wikipedia://en/10603)[^n5] $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ (sometimes denoted by $\mathbb A$, but that usually denotes the [adele ring](wikipedia://en/247978)).

### Algebraic closure {#Algebraic_closure}

Every root of a polynomial equation whose coefficients are *algebraic numbers* is again algebraic. That can be rephrased by saying that the field of algebraic numbers is [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018). In fact, it is the smallest algebraically closed field containing the rationals and so it is called the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) of the rationals.

That the field of algebraic numbers is algebraically closed can be proven as follows: Let $\beta$ be a root of a polynomial $\alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x + \alpha_2 x^2 ... +\alpha_n x^n$ with coefficients that are algebraic numbers $\alpha_0$, $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$... $\alpha_n$. The field extension $\mathbb{Q}^\prime \equiv \mathbb{Q}(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, ... \alpha_n)$ then has a finite degree with respect to $\mathbb{Q}$. The simple extension $\mathbb{Q}^\prime(\beta)$ then has a finite degree with respect to $\mathbb{Q}^\prime$ (since all powers of $\beta$ can be expressed by powers of up to $\beta^{n-1}$). Therefore, $\mathbb{Q}^\prime(\beta) = \mathbb{Q}(\beta, \alpha_1, \alpha_2, ... \alpha_n)$ also has a finite degree with respect to $\mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{Q}(\beta)$ is a linear subspace of $\mathbb{Q}^\prime(\beta)$, it must also have a finite degree with respect to $\mathbb{Q}$, so $\beta$ must be an algebraic number.

## Related fields {#Related_fields}

### Numbers defined by radicals {#Numbers_defined_by_radicals}

Any number that can be obtained from the integers using a [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) number of [additions](wikipedia://en/61338), [subtractions](wikipedia://en/74141), [multiplications](wikipedia://en/20845), [divisions](wikipedia://en/53696), and taking (possibly complex) $n$th roots where $n$ is a positive integer are algebraic. The converse, however, is not true: there are algebraic numbers that cannot be obtained in this manner. These numbers are roots of polynomials of degree 5 or higher, a result of [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) (see [Quintic equations](wikipedia://en/303368) and the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518)). For example, the equation:

$$x^5-x-1=0$$

has a unique real root, ≈ 1.1673, that cannot be expressed in terms of only radicals and arithmetic operations.

### Closed-form number {#Closed-form_number}

Algebraic numbers are all numbers that can be defined explicitly or implicitly in terms of polynomials, starting from the rational numbers. One may generalize this to "[closed-form numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-form_number)", which may be defined in various ways. Most broadly, all numbers that can be defined explicitly or implicitly in terms of polynomials, exponentials, and logarithms are called "[elementary numbers](wikipedia://en/4778133)", and these include the algebraic numbers, plus some transcendental numbers. Most narrowly, one may consider numbers *explicitly* defined in terms of polynomials, exponentials, and logarithms – this does not include all algebraic numbers, but does include some simple transcendental numbers such as $e$ or [ln 2](wikipedia://en/27153398).

## Algebraic integers {#Algebraic_integers}

*Image: Leadingcoeff.png.* Visualisation of the (countable) field of algebraic numbers in the complex plane. Colours indicate the leading integer coefficient of the polynomial the number is a root of (red = 1 i.e. the algebraic integers, green = 2, blue = 3, yellow = 4...). Points becomes smaller as the other coefficients and number of terms in the polynomial become larger. View shows integers 0,1 and 2 at bottom right, +i near top.

An *algebraic integer* is an algebraic number that is a root of a polynomial with integer coefficients with leading coefficient 1 (a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730)). Examples of algebraic integers are $5 + 13 \sqrt{2},$ $2 - 6i,$ and $\frac{1}{2}(1+i\sqrt{3}).$ Therefore, the algebraic integers constitute a proper [superset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superset) of the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), as the latter are the roots of monic polynomials $x - k$ for all $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. In this sense, algebraic integers are to algebraic numbers what [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) are to [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024).

The sum, difference and product of algebraic integers are again algebraic integers, which means that the algebraic integers form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). The name *algebraic integer* comes from the fact that the only rational numbers that are algebraic integers are the integers, and because the algebraic integers in any [number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) are in many ways analogous to the integers. If $K$ is a number field, its [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) is the subring of algebraic integers in $K$, and is frequently denoted as $O_{K}$. These are the prototypical examples of [Dedekind domains](wikipedia://en/145132).
