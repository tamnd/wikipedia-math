# Commutator subgroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commutator subgroup"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **commutator subgroup** or **derived subgroup** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is the [subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgroup_%28mathematics%29) [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by all the [commutators](wikipedia://en/7193) of the group.[^o2][^o3]

:::

The commutator subgroup is important because it is the [smallest](wikipedia://en/32245) [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) such that the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of the original group by this subgroup is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974). In other words, $G/N$ is abelian [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $N$ contains the commutator subgroup of $G$. So in some sense it provides a measure of how far the group is from being abelian; the larger the commutator subgroup is, the "less abelian" the group is.

## Commutators {#Commutators}

For elements $g$ and $h$ of a group *G*, the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) of $g$ and $h$ is $[g,h] = g^{-1}h^{-1}gh$. The commutator $[g,h]$ is equal to the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) *e* if and only if $gh = hg$ , that is, if and only if $g$ and $h$ commute. In general, $gh = hg[g,h]$.

However, the notation is somewhat arbitrary and there is a non-equivalent variant definition for the commutator that has the inverses on the right hand side of the equation: $[g,h] = ghg^{-1}h^{-1}$ in which case $gh \neq hg[g,h]$ but instead $gh = [g,h]hg$.

An element of *G* of the form $[g,h]$ for some *g* and *h* is called a commutator. The identity element *e* = \[*e*,*e*\] is always a commutator, and it is the only commutator if and only if *G* is abelian.

Here are some simple but useful commutator identities, true for any elements *s*, *g*, *h* of a group *G*:

- $$[g,h]^{-1} = [h,g],$$
- $[g,h]^s = [g^s,h^s],$ where $g^s = s^{-1}gs$ (or, respectively, $g^s = sgs^{-1}$) is the [conjugate](wikipedia://en/49176) of $g$ by $s,$
- for any [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $f: G \to H$, $f([g, h]) = [f(g), f(h)].$

The first and second identities imply that the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of commutators in *G* is closed under inversion and conjugation. If in the third identity we take *H* = *G*, we get that the set of commutators is stable under any [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of *G*. This is in fact a generalization of the second identity, since we can take *f* to be the conjugation [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) on *G*, $x \mapsto x^s$, to get the second identity.

However, the product of two or more commutators need not be a commutator. A generic example is \[*a*,*b*\]\[*c*,*d*\] in the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) on *a*,*b*,*c*,*d*. It is known that the least order of a finite group for which there exists two commutators whose product is not a commutator is 96; in fact there are two nonisomorphic groups of order 96 with this property.[^o5]

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

This motivates the definition of the **commutator subgroup** $[G, G]$ (also called the **derived subgroup**, and denoted $G'$ or $G^{(1)}$) of *G*: it is the subgroup [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by all the commutators.

It follows from this definition that any element of $[G, G]$ is of the form

$$[g_1,h_1] \cdots [g_n,h_n]$$

for some [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$, where the *g*<sub>*i*</sub> and *h*<sub>*i*</sub> are elements of *G*. Moreover, since $([g_1,h_1] \cdots [g_n,h_n])^s = [g_1^s,h_1^s] \cdots [g_n^s,h_n^s]$, the commutator subgroup is normal in *G*. For any homomorphism *f*: *G* → *H*,

$$f([g_1,h_1] \cdots [g_n,h_n]) = [f(g_1),f(h_1)] \cdots [f(g_n),f(h_n)],$$

so that $f([G,G]) \subseteq [H,H]$.

This shows that the commutator subgroup can be viewed as a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) on the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), some implications of which are explored below. Moreover, taking *G* = *H* it shows that the commutator subgroup is stable under every endomorphism of *G*: that is, \[*G*,*G*\] is a [fully characteristic subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fully_characteristic_subgroup) of *G*, a property considerably stronger than normality.

The commutator subgroup can also be defined as the set of elements *g* of the group that have an expression as a product *g* = *g*<sub>1</sub> *g*<sub>2</sub> ... *g*<sub>*k*</sub> that can be rearranged to give the identity.

### Derived series {#Derived_series}

This construction can be iterated:

$$G^{(0)} := G$$

$$G^{(n)} := [G^{(n-1)},G^{(n-1)}] \quad n \in \mathbf{N}$$

The groups $G^{(2)}, G^{(3)}, \ldots$ are called the **second derived subgroup**, **third derived subgroup**, and so forth, and the descending [normal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_series)

$$\cdots \triangleleft G^{(2)} \triangleleft G^{(1)} \triangleleft G^{(0)} = G$$

is called the **derived series**. This should not be confused with the [lower central series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_central_series), whose terms are $G_n := [G_{n-1},G]$.

For a finite group, the derived series terminates in a [perfect group](wikipedia://en/585271), which may or may not be trivial. For an infinite group, the derived series need not terminate at a finite stage, and one can continue it to infinite [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932) via [transfinite recursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfinite_recursion), thereby obtaining the **transfinite derived series**, which eventually terminates at the [perfect core](wikipedia://en/4939190) of the group.

:::

### Abelianization {#Abelianization}

Given a group $G$, a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $G/N$ is abelian if and only if $[G, G]\subseteq N$.

The quotient $G/[G, G]$ is an abelian group called the **abelianization** of $G$ or $G$ **made abelian**.[^o12] It is usually denoted by $G^{\operatorname{ab}}$ or $G_{\operatorname{ab}}$.

There is a useful categorical interpretation of the map $\varphi: G \rightarrow G^{\operatorname{ab}}$. Namely $\varphi$ is universal for homomorphisms from $G$ to an abelian group $H$: for any abelian group $H$ and homomorphism of groups $f: G \to H$ there exists a unique homomorphism $F: G^{\operatorname{ab}}\to H$ such that $f = F \circ \varphi$. As usual for objects defined by universal mapping properties, this shows the uniqueness of the abelianization $G^{\operatorname{ab}}$ up to canonical isomorphism, whereas the explicit construction $G\to G/[G, G]$ shows existence.

The abelianization functor is the [left adjoint](wikipedia://en/53991) of the inclusion functor from the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748) to the category of groups. The existence of the abelianization functor **Grp** → **Ab** makes the category **Ab** a [reflective subcategory](wikipedia://en/3967424) of the category of groups, defined as a full subcategory whose inclusion functor has a left adjoint.

Another important interpretation of $G^{\operatorname{ab}}$ is as $H_1(G, \mathbb{Z})$, the first [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homology) of $G$ with integral coefficients.

:::

### Classes of groups {#Classes_of_groups}

A group $G$ is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) if and only if the derived group is trivial: \[*G*,*G*\] = {*e*}. Equivalently, if and only if the group equals its abelianization. See above for the definition of a group's abelianization.

A group $G$ is a [perfect group](wikipedia://en/585271) if and only if the derived group equals the group itself: \[*G*,*G*\] = *G*. Equivalently, if and only if the abelianization of the group is trivial. This is, in a sense, the opposite of being abelian.

A group with $G^{(n)}=\{e\}$ for some *n* in **N** is called a [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012); this is weaker than abelian, which is the case *n* = 1.

A group with $G^{(n)} \neq \{e\}$ for all *n* in **N** is called a non-solvable group.

A group with $G^{(\alpha)}=\{e\}$ for some [ordinal number](wikipedia://en/26547932), possibly infinite, is called a [hypoabelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_radical); this is weaker than solvable, which is the case *α* is finite (a natural number).

### Perfect group {#Perfect_group}

Perfect group Whenever a group $G$ has derived subgroup equal to itself, $G^{(1)} =G$, it is called a **perfect group**. This includes non-abelian [simple groups](wikipedia://en/47600) and the [special linear groups](wikipedia://en/144823) $\operatorname{SL}_n(k)$ for a fixed field $k$.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The commutator subgroup of any [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) is [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059).
- The commutator subgroup of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\operatorname{GL}_n(k)$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) or a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) *k* equals the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\operatorname{SL}_n(k)$ provided that $n \ne 2$ or *k* is not the [field with two elements](wikipedia://en/11615).[^o17]
- The commutator subgroup of the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) *A*<sub>4</sub> is the [Klein four group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein_four_group).
- The commutator subgroup of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) *S<sub>n</sub>* is the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) *A<sub>n</sub>*.
- The commutator subgroup of the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) *Q* = {1, −1, *i*, −*i*, *j*, −*j*, *k*, −*k*} is \[*Q*,*Q*\] = {1, −1}.

### Map from Out {#Map_from_Out}

Since the derived subgroup is [characteristic](wikipedia://en/7198), any automorphism of *G* induces an automorphism of the abelianization. Since the abelianization is abelian, [inner automorphisms](wikipedia://en/61839) act trivially, hence this yields a map

$$\operatorname{Out}(G) \to \operatorname{Aut}(G^{\mbox{ab}})$$

:::
