# Bipyramid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bipyramid"}

In geometry, a **bipyramid**, **dipyramid**, or **double pyramid** is a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) formed by fusing two [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045) together [base](wikipedia://en/9579379)-to-base. The [polygonal](wikipedia://en/23621) base of each pyramid must therefore be the same, and unless otherwise specified the base [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) are usually [coplanar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coplanar) and a bipyramid is usually *symmetric*, meaning the two pyramids are [mirror images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image) across their common base plane. When each [apex](wikipedia://en/6022079) (apices, the off-base vertices) of the bipyramid is on a line perpendicular to the base and passing through its center, it is a *right* bipyramid; otherwise it is *oblique*. When the base is a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306), the bipyramid is also called *regular*.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and properties"}

## Definition and properties {#Definition_and_properties}

A bipyramid is a polyhedron constructed by fusing two [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045) which share the same [polygonal](wikipedia://en/23621) [base](wikipedia://en/9579379); a pyramid is in turn constructed by connecting each vertex of its base to a single new [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) (the [apex](wikipedia://en/6022079)) not lying in the plane of the base, for an $n$-gonal base forming $n$ triangular faces in addition to the base face. An $n$-gonal bipyramid thus has $2n$ faces, $3n$ edges, and $n + 2$ vertices. More generally, a right pyramid is a pyramid where the apices are on the perpendicular line through the [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) of an arbitrary polygon or the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) of a [tangential polygon](wikipedia://en/35520654), depending on the source. Likewise, a *right bipyramid* is a polyhedron constructed by attaching two symmetrical right bipyramid bases; bipyramids whose apices are not on this line are called *oblique bipyramids*.

When the two pyramids are mirror images, the bipyramid is called *symmetric*. It is called *regular* if its base is a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306). When the base is a regular polygon and the apices are on the perpendicular line through its center (a *regular right bipyramid*) then all of its faces are [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428); sometimes the name *bipyramid* refers specifically to symmetric regular right bipyramids, Examples of such bipyramids are the [triangular bipyramid](wikipedia://en/646933), [octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458) (square bipyramid) and [pentagonal bipyramid](wikipedia://en/728038). If all their edges are equal in length, these shapes consist of [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) faces, making them [deltahedra](wikipedia://en/493995); the triangular bipyramid and the pentagonal bipyramid are [Johnson solids](wikipedia://en/16034), and the regular octahedron is a [Platonic solid](wikipedia://en/23905).

*Image: Dual Cube-Octahedron.svg.* The octahedron is dual to the cube

The symmetric regular right bipyramids have [prismatic symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prismatic_symmetry), with [dihedral symmetry group](wikipedia://en/100267) $D_{nh}$ of order $4n$: they are unchanged when rotated $\tfrac{1}{n}$ of a turn around the [axis of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_symmetry), reflected across any plane passing through both apices and a base vertex or both apices and the center of a base edge, or reflected across the mirror plane. Because their faces are transitive under these symmetry transformations, they are [isohedral](wikipedia://en/6286120). They are the [dual polyhedra](wikipedia://en/8815) of [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007) and the prisms are the dual of bipyramids as well; the bipyramids vertices correspond to the faces of the prism, and the edges between pairs of vertices of one correspond to the edges between pairs of faces of the other, and vice versa. The prisms share the same symmetry as the bipyramids. The [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458) is more symmetric still, as its base vertices and apices are indistinguishable and can be exchanged by reflections or [rotations](wikipedia://en/410009); the regular octahedron and its dual, the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), have [octahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2900805).

The [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of a symmetric bipyramid is

$$\frac{2}{3}Bh,$$

where $B$ is the area of the base and $h$ the perpendicular distance from the base plane to either apex. In the case of a regular $n$-sided polygon with side length $s$ and whose altitude is $h$, the volume of such a bipyramid is:

$$\frac{n}{6}hs^2 \cot \frac{\pi}{n}.$$

## Related and other types of bipyramid {#Related_and_other_types_of_bipyramid}

### Concave bipyramids {#Concave_bipyramids}

A *concave bipyramid* has a [concave polygon](wikipedia://en/605156) base, and one example is a concave tetragonal bipyramid or an irregular concave octahedron. A bipyramid with an arbitrary polygonal base could be considered a *right* bipyramid if the apices are on a line perpendicular to the base passing through the base's [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926).

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Asymmetric bipyramid"}

### Asymmetric bipyramids {#Asymmetric_bipyramids}

An *asymmetric bipyramid* has apices which are not mirrored across the base plane; for a right bipyramid this only happens if each apex is a different distance from the base.

The [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) of an asymmetric right $n$-gonal bipyramid is an $n$-gonal [frustum](wikipedia://en/89405).

A regular asymmetric right $n$-gonal bipyramid has symmetry group $C_{nv}$, of order $2n$.

:::

### Scalene triangle bipyramids {#Scalene_triangle_bipyramids}

*Image: EB1911 Crystallography Fig. 46 Ditetragonal Bipyramid.jpg.* Example: ditetragonal bipyramid ($2n = 2\times4$)

An isotoxal right (symmetric) **di-$n$-gonal bipyramid** is a right (symmetric) $\mathbf{2}n$-gonal bipyramid with an [*isotoxal*](wikipedia://en/6289189) flat polygon base: its $2n$ basal vertices are coplanar, but alternate in two [radii](wikipedia://en/1780815).

All its faces are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [scalene triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalene_triangle), and it is [isohedral](wikipedia://en/6286120). It can be seen as another type of a right symmetric di-$n$-gonal *scalenohedron*, with an isotoxal flat polygon base.

An isotoxal right (symmetric) di-$n$-gonal bipyramid has $n$ two-fold rotation axes through opposite basal vertices, $n$ reflection planes through opposite apical edges, an $n$-fold rotation axis through apices, a reflection plane through base, and an $n$-fold [rotation-reflection](wikipedia://en/244324) axis through apices,[^o11] representing symmetry group $D_{nh}, [n,2], (*22n),$ of order $4n$. (The reflection about the base plane corresponds to the $0^{\circ}$ rotation-reflection. If $n$ is even, then there is an [inversion symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_symmetry) about the center, corresponding to the $180^{\circ}$ rotation-reflection.)

Example with $2n = 2\times3$:

An isotoxal right (symmetric) ditrigonal bipyramid has three similar vertical planes of symmetry, intersecting in a (vertical) $3$-fold rotation axis; perpendicular to them is a fourth plane of symmetry (horizontal); at the intersection of the three vertical planes with the horizontal plane are three similar (horizontal) $2$-fold rotation axes; there is no center of inversion symmetry,[^n1] but there is a [center of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_symmetry): the intersection point of the four axes.

Example with $2n = 2\times4$:

An isotoxal right (symmetric) ditetragonal bipyramid has four vertical planes of symmetry of two kinds, intersecting in a (vertical) $4$-fold rotation axis; perpendicular to them is a fifth plane of symmetry (horizontal); at the intersection of the four vertical planes with the horizontal plane are four (horizontal) $2$-fold rotation axes of two kinds, each perpendicular to a plane of symmetry; two vertical planes bisect the angles between two horizontal axes; and there is a centre of inversion symmetry.[^n2]

Double example:

- The bipyramid with isotoxal $2\times2$-gon base vertices $U, U', V, V'$ and right symmetric apices $A, A'$ $$\begin{alignedat}{5} U &= (1,0,0), & \quad V &= (0,2,0), & \quad A &= (0,0,1), \\ U' &= (-1,0,0), & \quad V' &= (0,-2,0), & \quad A' &= (0,0,-1), \end{alignedat}$$ has its faces isosceles. Indeed:
  - Upper apical edge lengths: $$\begin{aligned} \overline{AU} &= \overline{AU'} = \sqrt{2} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{AV} &= \overline{AV'} = \sqrt{5} \,; \end{aligned}$$
  - Base edge lengths: $$\overline{UV} = \overline{VU'} = \overline{U'V'} = \overline{V'U} = \sqrt{5} \,;$$
  - Lower apical edge lengths (equal to upper edge lengths): $$\begin{aligned} \overline{A'U} &= \overline{A'U'} = \sqrt{2} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{A'V} &= \overline{A'V'} = \sqrt{5} \,. \end{aligned}$$
- The bipyramid with same base vertices, but with right symmetric apices $$\begin{aligned} A &= (0,0,2), \\ A' &= (0,0,-2), \end{aligned}$$ also has its faces isosceles. Indeed:
  - Upper apical edge lengths: $$\begin{aligned} \overline{AU} &= \overline{AU'} = \sqrt{5} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{AV} &= \overline{AV'} = 2\sqrt{2} \,; \end{aligned}$$
  - Base edge length (equal to previous example): $$\overline{UV} = \overline{VU'} = \overline{U'V'} = \overline{V'U} = \sqrt{5}\,;$$
  - Lower apical edge lengths (equal to upper edge lengths): $$\begin{aligned} \overline{A'U} &= \overline{A'U'} = \sqrt{5}\,, \\[2pt] \overline{A'V} &= \overline{A'V'} = 2\sqrt{2}\,. \end{aligned}$$

*Image: EB1911 Crystallography Figs. 54 & 55 Orthorhombic Bipyramids.jpg.* Examples of rhombic bipyramids

In [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography), isotoxal right (symmetric) didigonal (8-faced), ditrigonal (12-faced), ditetragonal (16-faced), and dihexagonal (24-faced) bipyramids exist.[^o11][^o14]

### Scalenohedra {#Scalenohedra}

*Image: EB1911 Crystallography Fig. 68.—Scalenohedron.jpg.* Example: ditrigonal scalenohedron ($2n = 2\times3$)

A **scalenohedron** is similar to a bipyramid; the difference is that the scalenohedra has a zig-zag pattern in the middle edges.

It has two apices and $2n$ basal vertices, $4n$ faces, and $6n$ edges; it is topologically identical to a $2n$-gonal bipyramid, but its $2n$ basal vertices alternate in two rings above and below the center.[^o14]

All its faces are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [scalene triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalene_triangle), and it is [isohedral](wikipedia://en/6286120). It can be seen as another type of a right symmetric di-$n$-gonal bipyramid, with a regular zigzag skew polygon base.

A regular right symmetric di-$n$-gonal scalenohedron has $n$ two-fold rotation axes through opposite basal mid-edges, $n$ reflection planes through opposite apical edges, an $n$-fold rotation axis through apices, and a $\mathbf{2}n$-fold [rotation-reflection](wikipedia://en/244324) axis through apices (about which $\mathbf{1}n$ rotations-reflections globally preserve the solid),[^o11] representing symmetry group $D_{nv} = D_{nd}, [2^{+},2n], (2*n),$ of order $4n$. (If $n$ is odd, then there is an [inversion symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inversion_symmetry) about the center, corresponding to the $180^{\circ}$ rotation-reflection.)

Example with $2n = 2\times3$:

A regular right symmetric ditrigonal scalenohedron has three similar vertical planes of symmetry inclined to one another at $60^{\circ}$ and intersecting in a (vertical) $3$-fold rotation axis, three similar horizontal $2$-fold rotation axes, each perpendicular to a plane of symmetry, a center of inversion symmetry,[^n3] and a vertical $\mathbf{6}$-fold rotation-reflection axis.

Example with $2n = 2\times2$:

A regular right symmetric didigonal scalenohedron has only one vertical and two horizontal $2$-fold rotation axes, two vertical planes of symmetry, which bisect the angles between the horizontal pair of axes, and a vertical $\mathbf{4}$-fold rotation-reflection axis;[^n4] it has no center of inversion symmetry.

*Image: EB1911 Crystallography Figs. 50 & 51.jpg.* Examples of disphenoids and of an $8$-faced scalenohedron

For at most two particular values of $z_A = |z_{A'}|,$ the faces of such a **scaleno**hedron may be [**isosceles**](wikipedia://en/315428).

Double example:

- The scalenohedron with regular zigzag skew $2\times2$-gon base vertices $U, U', V, V'$ and right symmetric apices $A, A'$ $$\begin{alignedat}{5} U &= (3,0,2), & \quad V &= (0,3,-2), & \quad A &= (0,0,3), \\ U' &= (-3,0,2), & \quad V' &= (0,-3,-2), & \quad A' &= (0,0,-3), \end{alignedat}$$ has its faces isosceles. Indeed:
  - Upper apical edge lengths: $$\begin{aligned} \overline{AU} &= \overline{AU'} = \sqrt{10} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{AV} &= \overline{AV'} = \sqrt{34} \,; \end{aligned}$$
  - Base edge length: $$\overline{UV} = \overline{VU'} = \overline{U'V'} = \overline{V'U} = \sqrt{34} \,;$$
  - Lower apical edge lengths (equal to upper edge lengths swapped): $$\begin{aligned} \overline{A'U} &= \overline{A'U'} = \sqrt{34} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{A'V} &= \overline{A'V'} = \sqrt{10} \,. \end{aligned}$$
- The scalenohedron with same base vertices, but with right symmetric apices $$\begin{aligned} A &= (0,0,7), \\ A' &= (0,0,-7), \end{aligned}$$ also has its faces isosceles. Indeed:
  - Upper apical edge lengths: $$\begin{aligned} \overline{AU} &= \overline{AU'} = \sqrt{34} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{AV} &= \overline{AV'} = 3\sqrt{10} \,; \end{aligned}$$
  - Base edge length (equal to previous example): $$\overline{UV} = \overline{VU'} = \overline{U'V'} = \overline{V'U} = \sqrt{34} \,;$$
  - Lower apical edge lengths (equal to upper edge lengths swapped): $$\begin{aligned} \overline{A'U} &= \overline{A'U'} = 3\sqrt{10} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{A'V} &= \overline{A'V'} = \sqrt{34} \,. \end{aligned}$$

In [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography), regular right symmetric didigonal ($8$-faced) and ditrigonal ($12$-faced) scalenohedra exist.[^o11][^o14]

The smallest geometric scalenohedra have eight faces, and are topologically identical to the regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635). In this case ($2n = 2\times2$), in crystallography, a regular right symmetric didigonal ($8$-faced) scalenohedron is called a *tetragonal scalenohedron*.[^o11][^o14]

Let us temporarily focus on the regular right symmetric $8$-faced scalenohedra with $h = r,$ i.e.

$$z_{A} = |z_{A'}| = x_{U} = |x_{U'}| = y_{V} = |y_{V'}|.$$

Their two apices can be represented as $A, A'$ and their four basal vertices as $U, U', V, V'$:

$$\begin{alignedat}{5} U &= (1,0,z), & \quad V &= (0,1,-z), & \quad A &= (0,0,1), \\ U' &= (-1,0,z), & \quad V' &= (0,-1,-z), & \quad A' &= (0,0,-1), \end{alignedat}$$

where $z$ is a parameter between $0$ and $1$.

At $z = 0$, it is a regular octahedron; at $z = 1$, it has four pairs of coplanar faces, and merging these into four congruent isosceles triangles makes it a [disphenoid](wikipedia://en/8476166); for $z > 1$, it is concave.

If the $2n$-gon base is both [isotoxal](wikipedia://en/6289189) in-out and [zigzag skew](wikipedia://en/6545461), then **not** all faces of the isotoxal right symmetric scalenohedron are congruent.

Example with five different edge lengths:

- The scalenohedron with isotoxal in-out zigzag skew $2\times2$-gon base vertices $U, U', V, V'$ and right symmetric apices $A, A'$ $$\begin{alignedat}{5} U &= (1,0,1), & \quad V &= (0,2,-1), & \quad A &= (0,0,3), \\ U' &= (-1,0,1), & \quad V' &= (0,-2,-1), & \quad A' &= (0,0,-3), \end{alignedat}$$ has congruent scalene upper faces, and congruent scalene lower faces, but not all its faces are congruent. Indeed:
  - Upper apical edge lengths: $$\begin{aligned} \overline{AU} &= \overline{AU'} = \sqrt{5} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{AV} &= \overline{AV'} = 2\sqrt{5} \,; \end{aligned}$$
  - Base edge length: $$\overline{UV} = \overline{VU'} = \overline{U'V'} = \overline{V'U} = 3;$$
  - Lower apical edge lengths: $$\begin{aligned} \overline{A'U} &= \overline{A'U'} = \sqrt{17} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{A'V} &= \overline{A'V'} = 2\sqrt{2} \,. \end{aligned}$$

For some particular values of $z_{A} = |z_{A'}|$, half the faces of such a **scaleno**hedron may be [**isosceles**](wikipedia://en/315428) or [**equilateral**](wikipedia://en/173285).

Example with three different edge lengths:

- The scalenohedron with isotoxal in-out zigzag skew $2\times2$-gon base vertices $U, U', V, V'$ and right symmetric apices $A, A'$ $$\begin{alignedat}{5} U &= (3,0,2), & \quad V &= \left( 0,\sqrt{65},-2 \right), & \quad A &= (0,0,7), \\ U' &= (-3,0,2), & \quad V' &= \left( 0,-\sqrt{65},-2 \right), & \quad A' &= (0,0,-7), \end{alignedat}$$ has congruent scalene upper faces, and congruent equilateral lower faces; thus not all its faces are congruent. Indeed:
  - Upper apical edge lengths: $$\begin{aligned} \overline{AU} &= \overline{AU'} = \sqrt{34} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{AV} &= \overline{AV'} = \sqrt{146} \,; \end{aligned}$$
  - Base edge length: $$\overline{UV} = \overline{VU'} = \overline{U'V'} = \overline{V'U} = 3\sqrt{10} \,;$$
  - Lower apical edge length(s): $$\begin{aligned} \overline{A'U} &= \overline{A'U'} = 3\sqrt{10} \,, \\[2pt] \overline{A'V} &= \overline{A'V'} = 3\sqrt{10} \,. \end{aligned}$$

### Star bipyramids {#Star_bipyramids}

A ***star* bipyramid** has a [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) base, and is self-intersecting.[^o20]

A regular right symmetric star bipyramid has [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [isosceles](wikipedia://en/315428) triangle faces, and is [isohedral](wikipedia://en/6286120).

A $p/q$-bipyramid has [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) .

## 4-polytopes with bipyramidal cells {#4-polytopes_with_bipyramidal_cells}

The [dual](wikipedia://en/8815#Dual_polytopes_and_tessellations) of the [rectification](wikipedia://en/2192366) of each [convex regular 4-polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_regular_4-polytope) is a [cell-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell-transitive) [4-polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) with bipyramidal cells. In the following:

- $A$ is the apex vertex of the bipyramid;
- $E$ is an equator vertex;
- $\overline{EE}$ is the distance between adjacent vertices on the equator (equal to 1);
- $\overline{AE}$ is the apex-to-equator edge length;
- $\overline{AA}$ is the distance between the apices.

The bipyramid 4-polytope will have $V_{A}$ vertices where the apices of $N_{A}$ bipyramids meet. It will have $V_{E}$ vertices where the type $E$ vertices of $N_{E}$ bipyramids meet.

- $N_\overline{AE}$ bipyramids meet along each type $\overline{AE}$ edge.
- $N_\overline{EE}$ bipyramids meet along each type $\overline{EE}$ edge.
- $C_\overline{AE}$ is the cosine of the [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046) along an $\overline{AE}$ edge.
- $C_\overline{EE}$ is the cosine of the dihedral angle along an $\overline{EE}$ edge.

As cells must fit around an edge,

$$\begin{aligned} N_\overline{EE} \arccos C_\overline{EE} &\le 2\pi, \\[4pt] N_\overline{AE} \arccos C_\overline{AE} &\le 2\pi. \end{aligned}$$

## Other dimensions {#Other_dimensions}

*Image: Romb\_deltoid.svg.* A rhombus is a 2-dimensional analog of a right symmetric bipyramid

A generalized $n$-dimensional "bipyramid" is any $n$-[polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) constructed from an $(n - 1)$-polytope *base* lying in a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862), with every base vertex connected by an edge to two *apex* vertices. If the $(n - 1)$-polytope is a [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693) and the apices are equidistant from its center along the line perpendicular to the base hyperplane, it will have identical [pyramidal](wikipedia://en/3307045) [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647).

A 2-dimensional analog of a right symmetric bipyramid is formed by joining two [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [isosceles triangles](wikipedia://en/315428) base-to-base to form a [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884). More generally, a [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) is a 2-dimensional analog of a (possibly asymmetric) right bipyramid, and any [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) is a 2-dimensional analog of a general bipyramid.
