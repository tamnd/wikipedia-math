# Electrical network

*Image: Ohm's Law with Voltage source TeX.svg.* A simple electric circuit made up of a voltage source and a resistor. Here, $v=iR$, according to [Ohm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm's_law).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Electrical network"}

An **electrical network** is an interconnection of [electrical components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_component) (e.g., [batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_%28electricity%29), [resistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resistor), [inductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor), [capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor), [switches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switch), [transistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor)) or a model of such an interconnection, consisting of [electrical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_element) (e.g., [voltage sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage_source), [current sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_source), [resistances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance_and_conductance), [inductances](wikipedia://en/165146), [capacitances](wikipedia://en/140711)). An **electrical circuit** is a network consisting of a closed loop, giving a return path for the current. Thus all circuits are networks, but not all networks are circuits (although networks without a closed loop are often referred to as **open circuits**).

:::

A *resistive network* is a network containing only resistors and ideal current and voltage sources. [Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_analysis_%28electrical_circuits%29) of resistive networks is less complicated than analysis of networks containing capacitors and inductors. If the sources are constant ([DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_current)) sources, the result is a DC network. The effective resistance and current distribution properties of arbitrary resistor networks can be modeled in terms of their graph measures and geometrical properties.[^o2]

A network that contains [active electronic components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_electronic_component) is known as an *[electronic circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_circuit)*. Such networks are generally nonlinear and require more complex design and analysis tools.

## Classification {#Classification}

### By passivity {#By_passivity}

An active network contains at least one [voltage source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage_source) or [current source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_source) that can supply energy to the network indefinitely. A [passive](wikipedia://en/30874071) network does not contain an active source.

An active network contains one or more sources of [electromotive force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromotive_force). Practical examples of such sources include a [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_battery) or a [generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator). Active elements can inject power to the circuit, provide power gain, and control the current flow within the circuit.

Passive networks do not contain any sources of electromotive force. They consist of passive elements like resistors and capacitors.

### By linearity {#By_linearity}

[Linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_circuit) electrical networks, a special type consisting only of sources (voltage or current), linear lumped elements (resistors, capacitors, inductors), and linear distributed elements (transmission lines), have the property that signals are [linearly superimposable](wikipedia://en/1201321). They are thus more easily analyzed, using powerful [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346) methods such as [Laplace transforms](wikipedia://en/18610), to determine [DC response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_current), [AC response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current), and [transient response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transient_response).

Passive networks are generally taken to be linear, but there are exceptions. For instance, an [inductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor) with an iron core can be driven into [saturation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturation_%28magnetic%29) if driven with a large enough current. In this region, the behaviour of the inductor is very non-linear.

### By lumpiness {#By_lumpiness}

Discrete [passive components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_component) (resistors, capacitors and inductors) are called *lumped elements* because all of their, respectively, resistance, capacitance and inductance is assumed to be located ("lumped") at one place. This design philosophy is called the [lumped-element model](wikipedia://en/301928) and networks so designed are called *lumped-element circuits*. This is the conventional approach to circuit design. At high enough frequencies, or for long enough circuits (such as [power transmission lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_transmission_lines)), the lumped assumption no longer holds because there is a significant fraction of a [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) across the component dimensions. A new design model, called the [distributed-element model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed-element_model), is needed for such cases. Networks designed to this model are called *[distributed-element circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed-element_circuit)*.

A distributed-element circuit that includes some lumped components is called a *semi-lumped* design. An example of a semi-lumped circuit is the [combline filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combline_filter).

## Classification of sources {#Classification_of_sources}

Sources can be classified as independent sources and dependent sources.

### Independent {#Independent}

An ideal independent source maintains the same voltage or current regardless of the other elements present in the circuit. Its value is either constant (DC) or sinusoidal (AC). The strength of voltage or current is not changed by any variation in the connected network.

### Dependent {#Dependent}

[Dependent sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_source) depend upon a particular element of the circuit for delivering the power or voltage or current, depending upon the type of source it is.

## Applying electrical laws {#Applying_electrical_laws}

A number of electrical laws apply to all linear resistive networks. These include:

- [Kirchhoff's current law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff's_current_law): The sum of all currents entering a node is equal to the sum of all currents leaving the node.
- [Kirchhoff's voltage law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff's_voltage_law): The directed sum of the electrical potential differences around a loop must be zero.
- [Ohm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohm's_law): The voltage across a resistor is equal to the product of the resistance and the current flowing through it.
- [Norton's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norton's_theorem): Any network of voltage or current sources and resistors is electrically equivalent to an ideal current source in parallel with a single resistor.
- [Thévenin's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thévenin's_theorem): Any network of voltage or current sources and resistors is electrically equivalent to a single voltage source in series with a single resistor.
- [Superposition theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superposition_theorem): In a linear network with several independent sources, the response in a particular branch when all the sources are acting simultaneously is equal to the linear sum of individual responses calculated by taking one independent source at a time.

Applying these laws results in a set of simultaneous equations that can be solved either algebraically or numerically. The laws can generally be extended to networks containing [reactances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_reactance). They cannot be used in networks that contain nonlinear or time-varying components.

:::

## Design methods {#Design_methods}

To design any electrical circuit, either [analog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogue_electronics) or [digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_circuit), [electrical engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineer) need to be able to predict the voltages and currents at all places within the circuit. Simple [linear circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_circuit) can be analyzed by hand using [complex number theory](wikipedia://en/5826). In more complex cases, the circuit may be analyzed with specialized [computer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program) or estimation techniques such as the piecewise-linear model.

Circuit simulation software, such as [HSPICE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HSPICE) (an analog circuit simulator),[^o13] and languages such as [VHDL-AMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHDL-AMS) and [verilog-AMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verilog-AMS) allow engineers to design circuits without the time, cost and risk of error involved in building circuit prototypes.

::: {#o15 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Electrical network simulation software"}

## Network simulation software {#Network_simulation_software}

More complex circuits can be analyzed numerically with software such as [SPICE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SPICE) or [GNUCAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Circuit_Analysis_Package), or symbolically using software such as [SapWin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SapWin).

### Linearization around operating point {#Linearization_around_operating_point}

When faced with a new circuit, the software first tries to find a [steady state solution](wikipedia://en/1326107), that is, one where all nodes conform to Kirchhoff's current law *and* the voltages across and through each element of the circuit conform to the voltage/current equations governing that element.

Once the steady state solution is found, the *[operating points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operating_point)* of each element in the circuit are known. For a small signal analysis, every non-linear element can be linearized around its operation point to obtain the small-signal estimate of the voltages and currents. This is an application of Ohm's Law. The resulting linear circuit matrix can be solved with [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035).

### Piecewise-linear approximation {#Piecewise-linear_approximation}

Software such as the [PLECS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLECS) interface to [Simulink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulink) uses [piecewise-linear](wikipedia://en/444224) approximation of the equations governing the elements of a circuit. The circuit is treated as a completely linear network of [ideal diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diode_modelling#Mathematically_idealized_diode). Every time a diode switches from on to off or vice versa, the configuration of the linear network changes. Adding more detail to the approximation of equations increases the accuracy of the simulation, but also increases its running time.
