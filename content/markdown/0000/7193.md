# Commutator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commutator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **commutator** gives an indication of the extent to which a certain [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) fails to be [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative). There are different definitions used in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) and [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424).

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Commutator (group theory)"}

## Group theory {#Group_theory}

The **commutator** of two elements, $g$ and $h$, of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$, is the element

$[g, h] = g^{-1}h^{-1}gh$.[^o4]

This element is equal to the group's identity if and only if $g$ and $h$ commute (that is, if and only if $gh = hg$).

The set of all commutators of a group is not in general closed under the group operation, but the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of *G* [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by all commutators is closed and is called the *derived group* or the *[commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847)* of *G*. Commutators are used to define [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) and [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012) groups and the largest [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526).

The definition of the commutator above is used throughout this article, but many group theorists define the commutator as

$[g, h] = ghg^{-1}h^{-1}$.[^o5]

Using the first definition, this can be expressed as $[g^{-1}, h^{-1}]$.

### Identities (group theory) {#Identities_(group_theory)}

Commutator identities are an important tool in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890).[^o7] The expression $a^{x}$ denotes the [conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_%28group_theory%29#Definition) of $a$ by $x$, defined as $x^{-1}ax$.

1. $$x^y = x[x, y].$$
1. $$[y, x] = [x,y]^{-1}.$$
1. $[x, zy] = [x, y]\cdot [x, z]^y$ and $[x z, y] = [x, y]^z \cdot [z, y].$
1. $\left[x, y^{-1}\right] = [y, x]^{y^{-1}}$ and $\left[x^{-1}, y\right] = [y, x]^{x^{-1}}.$
1. $\left[\left[x, y^{-1}\right], z\right]^y \cdot \left[\left[y, z^{-1}\right], x\right]^z \cdot \left[\left[z, x^{-1}\right], y\right]^x = 1$ and $\left[\left[x, y\right], z^x\right] \cdot \left[[z ,x], y^z\right] \cdot \left[[y, z], x^y\right] = 1.$

Identity (5) is also known as the *Hall–Witt identity*, after [Philip Hall](wikipedia://en/1655033) and [Ernst Witt](wikipedia://en/2619911). It is a group-theoretic analogue of the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370) for the ring-theoretic commutator (see next section).

N.B., the above definition of the conjugate of $a$ by $x$ is used by some group theorists.[^o8] Many other group theorists define the conjugate of $a$ by $x$ as $xax^{-1}$.[^o9] This is often written ${}^x a$. Similar identities hold for these conventions.

Many identities that are true modulo certain subgroups are also used. These can be particularly useful in the study of [solvable groups](wikipedia://en/105012) and [nilpotent groups](wikipedia://en/144569). For instance, in any group, second powers behave well:

$$(xy)^2 = x^2 y^2 [y, x][[y, x], y].$$

If the [derived subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_subgroup) is central, then

$$(xy)^n = x^n y^n [y, x]^\binom{n}{2}.$$

## Ring theory {#Ring_theory}

[Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29) often do not support division. Thus, the **commutator** of two elements *a* and *b* of a ring (or any [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112)) is defined differently by

$$[a, b] = ab - ba.$$

The commutator is zero if and only if *a* and *b* commute. In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), if two [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) of a space are represented by commuting matrices in terms of one basis, then they are so represented in terms of every basis. By using the commutator as a [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/17944), every associative algebra can be turned into a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944).

The **anticommutator** of two elements $a$ and $b$ of a ring or associative algebra is defined by

$$\{a, b\} = ab + ba.$$

Sometimes $[a,b]_+$ is used to denote anticommutator, while $[a,b]_-$ is then used for commutator.[^o12] The anticommutator is used less often, but can be used to define [Clifford algebras](wikipedia://en/45305) and [Jordan algebras](wikipedia://en/936371) and in the derivation of the [Dirac equation](wikipedia://en/39407) in [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics).

The commutator of two operators acting on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) is a central concept in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), since it quantifies how well the two [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) described by these operators can be measured simultaneously. The [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883) is ultimately a theorem about such commutators, by virtue of the [Robertson–Schrödinger relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_relation).[^o13] In [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), equivalent commutators of function [star-products](wikipedia://en/1946702) are called [Moyal brackets](wikipedia://en/8875186) and are completely isomorphic to the Hilbert space commutator structures mentioned.

### Identities (ring theory) {#Identities_(ring_theory)}

The commutator has the following properties:

#### Lie-algebra identities {#Lie-algebra_identities}

1. $$[A + B, C] = [A, C] + [B, C]$$
1. $$[A, A] = 0$$
1. $$[A, B] = -[B, A]$$
1. $$[A, [B, C]] + [B, [C, A]] + [C, [A, B]] = 0$$

Relation (3) is called [anticommutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutativity), while (4) is the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370).

#### Additional identities {#Additional_identities}

1. $$[A, BC] = [A, B]C + B[A, C]$$
1. $$[A, BCD] = [A, B]CD + B[A, C]D + BC[A, D]$$
1. $$[A, BCDE] = [A, B]CDE + B[A, C]DE + BC[A, D]E + BCD[A, E]$$
1. $$[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B$$
1. $$[ABC, D] = AB[C, D] + A[B, D]C + [A, D]BC$$
1. $$[ABCD, E] = ABC[D, E] + AB[C, E]D + A[B, E]CD + [A, E]BCD$$
1. $$[A, B + C] = [A, B] + [A, C]$$
1. $$[A + B, C + D] = [A, C] + [A, D] + [B, C] + [B, D]$$
1. $$[AB, CD] = A[B, C]D + [A, C]BD + CA[B, D] + C[A, D]B =A[B, C]D + AC[B,D] + [A,C]DB + C[A, D]B$$
1. $$[[A, C], [B, D]] = [[[A, B], C], D] + [[[B, C], D], A] + [[[C, D], A], B] + [[[D, A], B], C]$$

If $A$ is a fixed element of a ring *R*, identity (1) can be interpreted as a [Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988) for the map $\operatorname{ad}_A: R \rightarrow R$ given by $\operatorname{ad}_A(B) = [A, B]$. In other words, the map ad<sub>*A*</sub> defines a [derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29) on the ring *R*. Identities (2), (3) represent Leibniz rules for more than two factors, and are valid for any derivation. Identities (4)–(6) can also be interpreted as Leibniz rules. Identities (7), (8) express **Z**-[bilinearity](wikipedia://en/4365).

From identity (9), one finds that the commutator of integer powers of ring elements is:

$$[A^N, B^M] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1} A^{n}B^{m} [A,B] B^{M-m-1}A^{N-n-1} = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=0}^{M-1} B^{m}A^{n} [A,B] A^{N-n-1}B^{M-m-1}$$

Some of the above identities can be extended to the anticommutator using the above ± subscript notation.[^o17] For example:

1. $$[AB, C]_\pm = A[B, C]_- + [A, C]_\pm B$$
1. $$[AB, CD]_\pm = A[B, C]_- D + AC[B, D]_- + [A, C]_- DB + C[A, D]_\pm B$$
1. $$[[A,B],[C,D]]=[[[B,C]_+,A]_+,D]-[[[B,D]_+,A]_+,C]+[[[A,D]_+,B]_+,C]-[[[A,C]_+,B]_+,D]$$
1. $$\left[A, [B, C]_\pm\right] + \left[B, [C, A]_\pm\right] + \left[C, [A, B]_\pm\right] = 0$$
1. $$[A,BC]_\pm = [A,B]_- C + B[A,C]_\pm = [A,B]_\pm C \mp B[A,C]_-$$
1. $$[A,BC] = [A,B]_\pm C \mp B[A,C]_\pm$$

#### Exponential identities {#Exponential_identities}

Consider a ring or algebra in which the [exponential](wikipedia://en/9678) $e^A = \exp(A) = 1 + A + \tfrac{1}{2!}A^2 + \cdots$ can be meaningfully defined, such as a [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665) or a ring of [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012).

In such a ring, [Hadamard's lemma](wikipedia://en/15967005) applied to nested commutators gives: $e^A Be^{-A} \ =\ B + [A, B] + \frac{1}{2!}[A, [A, B]] + \frac{1}{3!}[A, [A, [A, B]]] + \cdots \ =\ e^{\operatorname{ad}_A}(B).$ (For the last expression, see *Adjoint derivation* below.) This formula underlies the [Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion](wikipedia://en/285422#An_important_lemma) of log(exp(*A*) exp(*B*)).

A similar expansion expresses the group commutator of expressions $e^A$ (analogous to elements of a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945)) in terms of a series of nested commutators (Lie brackets),

$$e^A e^B e^{-A} e^{-B} = \exp\!\left( [A, B] + \frac{1}{2!}[A{+}B, [A, B]] + \frac{1}{3!} \left(\frac{1}{2} [A, [B, [B, A]]] + [A{+}B, [A{+}B, [A, B]]]\right) + \cdots\right).$$

## Graded rings and algebras {#Graded_rings_and_algebras}

When dealing with [graded algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra), the commutator is usually replaced by the **graded commutator**, defined in homogeneous components as

$$[\omega, \eta]_{gr} := \omega\eta - (-1)^{\deg \omega \deg \eta} \eta\omega.$$

:::

## Adjoint derivation {#Adjoint_derivation}

Especially if one deals with multiple commutators in a ring *R*, another notation turns out to be useful. For an element $x\in R$, we define the [adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) mapping $\mathrm{ad}_x:R\to R$ by:

$$\operatorname{ad}_x(y) = [x, y] = xy-yx.$$

This mapping is a [derivation](wikipedia://en/897658) on the ring *R*:

$$\mathrm{ad}_x\!(yz) \ =\ \mathrm{ad}_x\!(y) \,z \,+\, y\,\mathrm{ad}_x\!(z).$$

By the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370), it is also a derivation over the commutation operation:

$$\mathrm{ad}_x[y,z] \ =\ [\mathrm{ad}_x\!(y),z] \,+\, [y,\mathrm{ad}_x\!(z)] .$$

Composing such mappings, we get for example $\operatorname{ad}_x\operatorname{ad}_y(z) = [x, [y, z]\,]$ and

$$\operatorname{ad}_x^2\!(z) \ =\ \operatorname{ad}_x\!(\operatorname{ad}_x\!(z)) \ =\ [x, [x, z]\,].$$

We may consider $\mathrm{ad}$ itself as a mapping, $\mathrm{ad}: R \to \mathrm{End}(R)$, where $\mathrm{End}(R)$ is the ring of mappings from *R* to itself with composition as the multiplication operation. Then $\mathrm{ad}$ is a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) homomorphism, preserving the commutator:

$$\operatorname{ad}_{[x, y]} = \left[ \operatorname{ad}_x, \operatorname{ad}_y \right].$$

By contrast, it is **not** always a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411): usually $\operatorname{ad}_{xy} \,\neq\, \operatorname{ad}_x\operatorname{ad}_y$.

### General Leibniz rule {#General_Leibniz_rule}

The [general Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/1116115), expanding repeated derivatives of a product, can be written abstractly using the adjoint representation:

$$x^n y = \sum_{k = 0}^n \binom{n}{k} \operatorname{ad}_x^k\!(y)\, x^{n - k}.$$

Replacing $x$ by the differentiation operator $\partial$, and $y$ by the multiplication operator $m_f : g \mapsto fg$, we get $\operatorname{ad}(\partial)(m_f) = m_{\partial(f)}$, and applying both sides to a function *g*, the identity becomes the usual Leibniz rule for the *n*th derivative $\partial^{n}\!(fg)$.
