# Diffeomorphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Diffeomorphism"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **diffeomorphism** is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) of [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219). It is an [invertible function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_function) that maps one differentiable manifold to another such that both the function and its inverse are [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable).

:::

*Image: Diffeomorphism of a square.svg.* The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a rectangular grid on a square under a diffeomorphism from the square onto itself.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given two differentiable manifolds $M$ and $N$, a [continuously differentiable map](wikipedia://en/2119219#Differentiability_of_mappings_between_manifolds) $f \colon M \rightarrow N$ is a **diffeomorphism** if it is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) and its inverse $f^{-1} \colon N \rightarrow M$ is differentiable as well. If these functions are $r$ times continuously differentiable, $f$ is called a $C^r$-diffeomorphism.

Two manifolds $M$ and $N$ are **diffeomorphic** (usually denoted $M \simeq N$) if there is a diffeomorphism $f$ from $M$ to $N$. Two $C^r$-differentiable manifolds are $C^r$-diffeomorphic if there is an $r$ times continuously differentiable bijective map between them whose inverse is also $r$ times continuously differentiable. A $C^1$-diffeomorphism is simply a diffeomorphism, and a $C^0$-diffeomorphism is a homeomorphism.

:::

## Diffeomorphisms of subsets of manifolds {#Diffeomorphisms_of_subsets_of_manifolds}

Given a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $X$ of a manifold $M$ and a subset $Y$ of a manifold $N$, a function $f:X\to Y$ is said to be smooth if for all $p$ in $X$ there is a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) $U\subset M$ of $p$ and a smooth function $g:U\to N$ such that the [restrictions](wikipedia://en/1589032) agree: $g_{|U \cap X} = f_{|U \cap X}$ (note that $g$ is an extension of $f$). The function $f$ is said to be a diffeomorphism if it is bijective, smooth and its inverse is smooth.

## Local description {#Local_description}

Testing whether a differentiable map is a diffeomorphism can be made locally under some mild restrictions. This is the Hadamard-Caccioppoli theorem:[^o6]

If $U$, $V$ are [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [open subsets](wikipedia://en/39358) of $\mathbb{R}^n$ such that $V$ is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected), a differentiable map $f:U\to V$ is a diffeomorphism if it is [proper](wikipedia://en/1190262) and if the [differential](wikipedia://en/1558510) $Df_x:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ is bijective (and hence a [linear isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isomorphism)) at each point $x$ in $U$.

Some remarks:

It is essential for $V$ to be [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) for the function $f$ to be globally invertible (under the sole condition that its derivative be a bijective map at each point). For example, consider the "realification" of the [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) square function

$$\begin{cases} f : \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\} \to \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{(0,0)\} \\ (x,y)\mapsto(x^2-y^2,2xy). \end{cases}$$

Then $f$ is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) and it satisfies

$$\det Df_x = 4(x^2+y^2) \neq 0.$$

Thus, though $Df_x$ is bijective at each point, $f$ is not invertible because it fails to be [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) (e.g. $f(1,0)=(1,0)=f(-1,0)$).

Since the differential at a point (for a differentiable function)

$$Df_x : T_xU \to T_{f(x)}V$$

is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102), it has a well-defined inverse if and only if $Df_x$ is a bijection. The [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) representation of $Df_x$ is the $n\times n$ matrix of first-order [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) whose entry in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column is $\partial f_i / \partial x_j$. This so-called [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) is often used for explicit computations.

Diffeomorphisms are necessarily between manifolds of the same [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398). Imagine $f$ going from dimension $n$ to dimension $k$. If $n<k$ then $Df_x$ could never be surjective, and if $n>k$ then $Df_x$ could never be injective. In both cases, therefore, $Df_x$ fails to be a bijection.

If $Df_x$ is a bijection at $x$ then $f$ is said to be a [local diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/1728483) (since, by continuity, $Df_y$ will also be bijective for all $y$ sufficiently close to $x$).

Given a smooth map from dimension $n$ to dimension $k$, if $Df$ (or, locally, $Df_x$) is surjective, $f$ is said to be a [submersion](wikipedia://en/356413) (or, locally, a "local submersion"); and if $Df$ (or, locally, $Df_x$) is injective, $f$ is said to be an [immersion](wikipedia://en/6993953) (or, locally, a "local immersion").

A differentiable bijection is *not* necessarily a diffeomorphism. $f(x)=x^3$, for example, is not a diffeomorphism from $\mathbb{R}$ to itself because its derivative vanishes at 0 (and hence its inverse is not differentiable at 0). This is an example of a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) that is not a diffeomorphism.

When $f$ is a map between differentiable manifolds, a diffeomorphic $f$ is a stronger condition than a homeomorphic $f$. For a diffeomorphism, $f$ and its inverse need to be [differentiable](wikipedia://en/2119219#Differentiable_functions); for a homeomorphism, $f$ and its inverse need only be [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122). Every diffeomorphism is a homeomorphism, but not every homeomorphism is a diffeomorphism.

$f:M\to N$ is a diffeomorphism if, in [coordinate charts](wikipedia://en/2073470#Differentiable_manifolds), it satisfies the definition above. More precisely: Pick any cover of $M$ by compatible [coordinate charts](wikipedia://en/2073470#Differentiable_manifolds) and do the same for $N$. Let $\phi$ and $\psi$ be charts on, respectively, $M$ and $N$, with $U$ and $V$ as, respectively, the images of $\phi$ and $\psi$. The map $\psi f\phi^{-1}:U\to V$ is then a diffeomorphism as in the definition above, whenever $f(\phi^{-1}(U))\subseteq\psi^{-1}(V)$.

## Examples {#Examples}

Since any manifold can be locally parametrised, we can consider some explicit maps from $\mathbb{R}^2$ into $\mathbb{R}^2$.

- Let

$$f(x,y) = \left (x^2 + y^3, x^2 - y^3 \right ).$$

We can calculate the Jacobian matrix:

$$J_f = \begin{pmatrix} 2x & 3y^2 \\ 2x & -3y^2 \end{pmatrix} .$$

The Jacobian matrix has zero [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) if and only if $xy=0$. We see that $f$ could only be a diffeomorphism away from the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis. However, $f$ is not bijective since $f(x,y)=f(-x,y)$, and thus it cannot be a diffeomorphism.

- Let

$$g(x,y) = \left (a_0 + a_{1,0}x + a_{0,1}y + \cdots, \ b_0 + b_{1,0}x + b_{0,1}y + \cdots \right )$$

where the $a_{i,j}$ and $b_{i,j}$ are arbitrary [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), and the omitted terms are of degree at least two in *x* and *y*. We can calculate the Jacobian matrix at **0**:

$$J_g(0,0) = \begin{pmatrix} a_{1,0} & a_{0,1} \\ b_{1,0} & b_{0,1} \end{pmatrix}.$$

We see that *g* is a local diffeomorphism at **0** if, and only if,

$$a_{1,0}b_{0,1} - a_{0,1}b_{1,0} \neq 0,$$

i.e. the linear terms in the components of *g* are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) as [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000).

- Let

$$h(x,y) = \left (\sin(x^2 + y^2), \cos(x^2 + y^2) \right ).$$

We can calculate the Jacobian matrix:

$$J_h = \begin{pmatrix} 2x\cos(x^2 + y^2) & 2y\cos(x^2 + y^2) \\ -2x\sin(x^2+y^2) & -2y\sin(x^2 + y^2) \end{pmatrix} .$$

The Jacobian matrix has zero determinant everywhere! In fact we see that the image of *h* is the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).

### Surface deformations {#Surface_deformations}

In [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), a stress-induced transformation is called a [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29) and may be described by a diffeomorphism. A diffeomorphism $f:U\to V$ between two [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) $U$ and $V$ has a Jacobian matrix $Df$ that is an [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122). In fact, it is required that for $p$ in $U$, there is a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29) of $p$ in which the Jacobian $Df$ stays [non-singular](wikipedia://en/217122). Suppose that in a chart of the surface, $f(x,y) = (u,v).$

The [total differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_differential) of *u* is

$du = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} dx + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} dy$, and similarly for *v*.

Then the image $(du, dv) = (dx, dy) Df$ is a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation), fixing the origin, and expressible as the action of a complex number of a particular type. When (*dx*, *dy*) is also interpreted as that type of complex number, the action is of complex multiplication in the appropriate complex number plane. As such, there is a type of angle ([Euclidean](wikipedia://en/1196), [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/1139981), or [slope](wikipedia://en/29368)) that is preserved in such a multiplication. Due to *Df* being invertible, the type of complex number is uniform over the surface. Consequently, a surface deformation or diffeomorphism of surfaces has the **conformal property** of preserving (the appropriate type of) angles.

:::

## Diffeomorphism group {#Diffeomorphism_group}

Let $M$ be a differentiable manifold that is [second-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-countable) and [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637). The **diffeomorphism group** of $M$ is the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all $C^r$ diffeomorphisms of $M$ to itself, denoted by $\text{Diff}^r(M)$ or, when $r$ is understood, $\text{Diff}(M)$. This is a "large" group, in the sense that—provided $M$ is not zero-dimensional—it is not [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact).

### Topology {#Topology}

The diffeomorphism group has two natural [topologies](wikipedia://en/30450): *weak* and *strong* (Hirsch 1997). When the manifold is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), these two topologies agree. The weak topology is always [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738). When the manifold is not compact, the strong topology captures the behavior of functions "at infinity" and is not metrizable. It is, however, still [Baire](wikipedia://en/200550).

Fixing a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) on $M$, the weak topology is the topology induced by the family of metrics

$$d_K(f,g) = \sup\nolimits_{x\in K} d(f(x),g(x)) + \sum\nolimits_{1\le p\le r} \sup\nolimits_{x\in K} \left \|D^pf(x) - D^pg(x) \right \|$$

as $K$ varies over compact subsets of $M$. Indeed, since $M$ is $\sigma$-compact, there is a sequence of compact subsets $K_n$ whose [union](wikipedia://en/47949) is $M$. Then:

$$d(f,g) = \sum\nolimits_n 2^{-n}\frac{d_{K_n}(f,g)}{1+d_{K_n}(f,g)}.$$

The diffeomorphism group equipped with its weak topology is locally homeomorphic to the space of $C^r$ vector fields (Leslie 1967). Over a compact subset of $M$, this follows by fixing a Riemannian metric on $M$ and using the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/276548) for that metric. If $r$ is finite and the manifold is compact, the space of vector fields is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989). Moreover, the transition maps from one chart of this atlas to another are smooth, making the diffeomorphism group into a [Banach manifold](wikipedia://en/2504397) with smooth right translations; left translations and inversion are only continuous. If $r=\infty$, the space of vector fields is a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801). Moreover, the transition maps are smooth, making the diffeomorphism group into a [Fréchet manifold](wikipedia://en/3873038) and even into a [regular Fréchet Lie group](wikipedia://en/43466503#Regular_Lie_groups). If the manifold is $\sigma$-compact and not compact the full diffeomorphism group is not locally contractible for any of the two topologies. One has to restrict the group by controlling the deviation from the identity near infinity to obtain a diffeomorphism group which is a manifold; see (Michor Mumford 2013).

### Lie algebra {#Lie_algebra}

The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of the diffeomorphism group of $M$ consists of all [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) on $M$ equipped with the [Lie bracket of vector fields](wikipedia://en/10282799). Somewhat formally, this is seen by making a small change to the coordinate $x$ at each point in space:

$$x^{\mu} \mapsto x^{\mu} + \varepsilon h^{\mu}(x)$$

so the infinitesimal generators are the vector fields

$$L_{h} = h^{\mu}(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}.$$

::: {#o17 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- When $M=G$ is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), there is a natural inclusion of $G$ in its own diffeomorphism group via left-translation. Let $\text{Diff}(G)$ denote the diffeomorphism group of $G$, then there is a splitting $\text{Diff}(G)\simeq G\times\text{Diff}(G,e)$, where $\text{Diff}(G,e)$ is the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $\text{Diff}(G)$ that fixes the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of the group.
- The diffeomorphism group of Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ consists of two components, consisting of the orientation-preserving and orientation-reversing diffeomorphisms. In fact, the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) is a [deformation retract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) of the subgroup $\text{Diff}(\mathbb{R}^n,0)$ of diffeomorphisms fixing the origin under the map $f(x)\to f(tx)/t, t\in(0,1]$. In particular, the general linear group is also a deformation retract of the full diffeomorphism group.
- For a finite [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of points, the diffeomorphism group is simply the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901). Similarly, if $M$ is any manifold there is a [group extension](wikipedia://en/345807) $0\to\text{Diff}_0(M)\to\text{Diff}(M)\to\Sigma(\pi_0(M))$. Here $\text{Diff}_0(M)$ is the subgroup of $\text{Diff}(M)$ that preserves all the components of $M$, and $\Sigma(\pi_0(M))$ is the permutation group of the set $\pi_0(M)$ (the components of $M$). Moreover, the image of the map $\text{Diff}(M)\to\Sigma(\pi_0(M))$ is the bijections of $\pi_0(M)$ that preserve diffeomorphism classes.

:::

### Transitivity {#Transitivity}

For a connected manifold $M$, the diffeomorphism group [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) [transitively](wikipedia://en/12781#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) on $M$. More generally, the diffeomorphism group acts transitively on the [configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596) $C_k M$. If $M$ is at least two-dimensional, the diffeomorphism group acts transitively on the configuration space $F_k M$ and the action on $M$ is [multiply transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Remarkable_properties_of_actions) (Banyaga 1997, 29).

### Extensions of diffeomorphisms {#Extensions_of_diffeomorphisms}

In 1926, [Tibor Radó](wikipedia://en/1490393) asked whether the [harmonic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_integral) of any homeomorphism or diffeomorphism of the unit circle to the [unit disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_disc) yields a diffeomorphism on the open disc. An elegant proof was provided shortly afterwards by [Hellmuth Kneser](wikipedia://en/1368525). In 1945, [Gustave Choquet](wikipedia://en/17301193), apparently unaware of this result, produced a completely different proof.

The (orientation-preserving) diffeomorphism group of the circle is pathwise connected. This can be seen by noting that any such diffeomorphism can be lifted to a diffeomorphism $f$ of the reals satisfying $[f(x+1)=f(x)+1]$; this space is convex and hence path-connected. A smooth, eventually constant path to the identity gives a second more elementary way of extending a diffeomorphism from the circle to the open unit disc (a special case of the [Alexander trick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_trick)). Moreover, the diffeomorphism group of the circle has the homotopy-type of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $O(2)$.

The corresponding extension problem for diffeomorphisms of higher-dimensional spheres $S^{n-1}$ was much studied in the 1950s and 1960s, with notable contributions from [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633), [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) and [Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294). An obstruction to such extensions is given by the finite [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) $\Gamma_n$, the "[group of twisted spheres](wikipedia://en/1114931#Twisted_spheres)", defined as the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) of the abelian [component group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Component_group) of the diffeomorphism group by the subgroup of classes extending to diffeomorphisms of the ball $B^n$.

### Connectedness {#Connectedness}

For manifolds, the diffeomorphism group is usually not connected. Its component group is called the [mapping class group](wikipedia://en/793295). In dimension 2 (i.e. [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865)), the mapping class group is a [finitely presented group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group) generated by [Dehn twists](wikipedia://en/765970); this has been proved by [Max Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602), [W. B. R. Lickorish](wikipedia://en/11177096), and [Allen Hatcher](wikipedia://en/1112366)). Max Dehn and [Jakob Nielsen](wikipedia://en/602516) showed that it can be identified with the [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) of the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the surface.

[William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190) refined this analysis by [classifying elements of the mapping class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nielsen-Thurston_classification) into three types: those equivalent to a [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848#Periodic_mapping) diffeomorphism; those equivalent to a diffeomorphism leaving a simple closed curve invariant; and those equivalent to [pseudo-Anosov diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/2327355). In the case of the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) $S^1\times S^1=\mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}^2$, the mapping class group is simply the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) $\text{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ and the classification becomes classical in terms of [elliptic](wikipedia://en/314493#Elliptic_transforms), [parabolic](wikipedia://en/314493#Parabolic_transforms) and [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/314493#Hyperbolic_transforms) matrices. Thurston accomplished his classification by observing that the mapping class group acted naturally on a [compactification](wikipedia://en/6617) of [Teichmüller space](wikipedia://en/1835661); as this enlarged space was homeomorphic to a closed ball, the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101) became applicable. Smale [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that if $M$ is an [oriented](wikipedia://en/187446#Orientability_of_manifolds) smooth closed manifold, the [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787) of the group of orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms is [simple](wikipedia://en/47600). This had first been proved for a product of circles by [Michel Herman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Herman); it was proved in full generality by Thurston.

### Homotopy types {#Homotopy_types}

- The diffeomorphism group of $S^2$ has the homotopy-type of the subgroup $\mathrm{O}(3)$. This was proven by Stephen Smale.[^o23]
- The diffeomorphism group of the torus has the homotopy-type of its linear [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160): $S^1\times S^1\times\text{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.
- The diffeomorphism groups of orientable surfaces of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) $g>1$ have the homotopy-type of their mapping class groups (i.e. the components are contractible).
- The homotopy-type of the diffeomorphism groups of 3-manifolds are fairly well understood via the work of Ivanov, Hatcher, Gabai and Rubinstein, although there are a few outstanding open cases (primarily 3-manifolds with finite [fundamental groups](wikipedia://en/11004)).
- The homotopy-type of diffeomorphism groups of $n$-manifolds for $n>3$ are poorly understood. For example, it is an open problem whether or not $\mathrm{Diff}(S^4)$ has more than two components. Via Milnor, Kahn and Antonelli, however, it is known that provided $n>6$, $\mathrm{Diff}(S^n)$ does not have the homotopy-type of a finite [CW-complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CW-complex).

## Homeomorphism and diffeomorphism {#Homeomorphism_and_diffeomorphism}

Since every diffeomorphism is a homeomorphism, given a pair of manifolds which are diffeomorphic to each other they are in particular [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to each other. The converse is not true in general.

While it is easy to find homeomorphisms that are not diffeomorphisms, it is more difficult to find a pair of homeomorphic manifolds that are not diffeomorphic. In dimensions 1, 2 and 3, any pair of homeomorphic smooth manifolds are diffeomorphic. In dimension 4 or greater, examples of homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic pairs exist. The first such example was constructed by [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) in dimension 7. He constructed a smooth 7-dimensional manifold (called now [Milnor's sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor's_sphere)) that is homeomorphic to the standard 7-sphere but not diffeomorphic to it. There are, in fact, 28 oriented diffeomorphism classes of manifolds homeomorphic to the 7-sphere (each of them is the total space of a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) over the 4-sphere with the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792) as the fiber).

More unusual phenomena occur for [4-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1362795). In the early 1980s, a combination of results due to [Simon Donaldson](wikipedia://en/976305) and [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970) led to the discovery of [exotic](wikipedia://en/2180637) $\mathbb{R}^4$: there are [uncountably many](wikipedia://en/32248) pairwise non-diffeomorphic open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^4$ each of which is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^4$, and also there are uncountably many pairwise non-diffeomorphic differentiable manifolds homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^4$ that do not [embed smoothly](wikipedia://en/52119#Differential_topology) in $\mathbb{R}^4$.
