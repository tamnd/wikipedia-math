# Atle Selberg

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Atle Selberg"}

**Atle Selberg** (14 June 1917 – 6 August 2007) was a Norwegian [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) known for his work in [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513) and the theory of [automorphic forms](wikipedia://en/782146), and in particular for bringing them into relation with [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713). He was awarded the [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) in 1950 and an honorary [Abel Prize](wikipedia://en/159285) in 2002.

:::

## Early years {#Early_years}

Selberg was born in [Langesund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langesund), Norway, the son of teacher Anna Kristina Selberg and mathematician [Ole Michael Ludvigsen Selberg](wikipedia://en/37564153). Two of his three brothers, [Sigmund](wikipedia://en/37555546) and [Henrik](wikipedia://en/37563752), were also mathematicians. His other brother, [Arne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arne_Selberg), was a professor of engineering.[^o11] While he was still at school, he was influenced by the work of [Srinivasa Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717) and he found an exact analytical formula for the [partition function](wikipedia://en/312250) as suggested by the works of Ramanujan; however, this result was first published by [Hans Rademacher](wikipedia://en/1864916).

He studied at the [University of Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oslo) and completed his [doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in 1943.[^o11]

## World War II {#World_War_II}

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), Selberg worked in isolation due to the [German occupation of Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation_of_Norway). After the war, his accomplishments became known, including a proof that a positive proportion of the zeros of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) lie on the line $\Re(s)=\tfrac{1}{2}$.[^n1]

During the war, he fought against the German invasion of Norway, and was imprisoned several times.

## Post-war in Norway {#Post-war_in_Norway}

After the war, he turned to [sieve theory](wikipedia://en/1035915), a previously neglected topic which Selberg's work brought into prominence. In a 1947 paper he introduced the [Selberg sieve](wikipedia://en/18739942), a method well adapted in particular to providing auxiliary upper bounds, and which contributed to [Chen's theorem](wikipedia://en/1400840), among other important results.

In 1948, Selberg submitted two papers in *[Annals of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/2785)* in which he proved by elementary means the theorems for [primes in arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/11740178) and the [density of primes](wikipedia://en/23692).[^o7][^o8] This challenged the widely held view of his time that certain theorems are only obtainable with the advanced methods of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759). Both results were based on his work on the asymptotic formula

$$\vartheta \left( x \right)\log \left( x \right) + \sum\limits_{p \le x} {\log \left( p \right)} \vartheta \left( {\frac{x}{p}} \right) = 2x\log \left( x \right) + O\left( x \right)$$

where

$$\vartheta \left( x \right) = \sum\limits_{p \le x} {\log \left( p \right)}$$

for primes $p$. He established this result by elementary means in March 1948, and by July of that year, Selberg and [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) each obtained [elementary proofs](wikipedia://en/9318685) of the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692), both using the asymptotic formula above as a starting point.[^o9] Circumstances leading up to the proofs, as well as publication disagreements, led to a bitter [dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priority_dispute) between the two mathematicians.[^o10][^o11]

For his fundamental accomplishments during the 1940s, Selberg received the 1950 [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859).[^o12]

## Institute for Advanced Study {#Institute_for_Advanced_Study}

C.L. Siegel invited Selberg to visit the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey) in 1947. He moved to the United States and was an associate professor at [Syracuse University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse_University) briefly. He returned to the IAS in 1949, where he remained until his death.[^o14][^o15] During the 1950s, he worked on introducing [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) into [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), culminating in his development of the [Selberg trace formula](wikipedia://en/981694), the most famous and influential of his results. In its simplest form, this establishes a duality between the lengths of [closed geodesics](wikipedia://en/19903176) on a [compact Riemann surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Riemann_surface) and the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace-Beltrami_operator), which is analogous to the duality between the [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) and the zeros of the zeta function.

He generally worked alone. His only coauthor was [Sarvadaman Chowla](wikipedia://en/292142).[^o16][^o17]

Selberg was awarded the 1986 [Wolf Prize in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/714278). He was also awarded an honorary [Abel Prize](wikipedia://en/159285) in 2002, its founding year, before the awarding of the regular prizes began.

Selberg received many distinctions for his work, in addition to the Fields Medal, the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize)[^o18] and the [Gunnerus Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunnerus_Medal). He was elected to the [Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Academy_of_Science_and_Letters), the [Royal Danish Academy of Sciences and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Danish_Academy_of_Sciences_and_Letters) and the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences).

In 1972, he was awarded an [honorary degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_degree), doctor philos. honoris causa, at the [Norwegian Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Institute_of_Technology), later part of [Norwegian University of Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_University_of_Science_and_Technology).[^o19]

His first wife, Hedvig Liebermann, who Selberg married in 1947, died in 1995. With her, Selberg had two children: Ingrid Selberg (married to playwright [Mustapha Matura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustapha_Matura)) and Lars Selberg. In 2003, Atle Selberg married Betty Frances "Mickey" Compton (born in 1929).

He died at home in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey) on 6 August 2007 of heart failure. Upon his death, he was survived by his widow, daughter, son, and four grandchildren.[^o20]
