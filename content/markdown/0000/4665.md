# Banach algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Banach algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a **Banach algebra**, named after [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646), is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) $A$ over the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) numbers (or over a [non-Archimedean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonarchimedean_field) complete [normed field](wikipedia://en/990534)) that at the same time is also a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), that is, a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) that is [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) in the [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) induced by the norm. The norm is required to satisfy

$$\|x \, y\| \ \leq \|x\| \, \|y\| \quad \text{ for all } x, y \in A.$$

:::

This ensures that the multiplication operation is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) with respect to the [metric topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_topology).

A Banach algebra is called *unital* if it has an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for the multiplication whose norm is $1,$ and *commutative* if its multiplication is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative). Any Banach algebra $A$ (whether it is unital or not) can be embedded [isometrically](wikipedia://en/254777) into a unital Banach algebra $A_e$ so as to form a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_%28algebra%29) of $A_e$. Often one assumes *a priori* that the algebra under consideration is unital because one can develop much of the theory by considering $A_e$ and then applying the outcome in the original algebra. However, this is not the case all the time. For example, one cannot define all the [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) in a Banach algebra without identity.

The theory of real Banach algebras can be very different from the theory of complex Banach algebras. For example, the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) of an element of a nontrivial complex Banach algebra can never be empty, whereas in a real Banach algebra it could be empty for some elements.

Banach algebras can also be defined over fields of [$p$-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423). This is part of [$p$-adic analysis](wikipedia://en/291327).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The prototypical example of a Banach algebra is $C_0(X)$, the space of (complex-valued) continuous functions, defined on a [locally compact Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact_Hausdorff_space) $X$, that [vanish at infinity](wikipedia://en/461986). $C_0(X)$ is unital if and only if $X$ is [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compactness). The [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation) being an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450), $C_0(X)$ is in fact a [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184). More generally, every C\*-algebra is a Banach algebra by definition.

- The set of real (or complex) numbers is a Banach algebra with norm given by the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991).
- The set of all real or complex $n$-by-$n$ [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) becomes a [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) Banach algebra if we equip it with a sub-multiplicative [matrix norm](wikipedia://en/1543735).
- Take the Banach space $\mathbb{R}^n$ (or $\mathbb{C}^n$) with norm $\|x\| = \max_{} |x_i|$ and define multiplication componentwise: $\left(x_1, \ldots, x_n\right) \left(y_1, \ldots, y_n\right) = \left(x_1 y_1, \ldots, x_n y_n\right).$
- The [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) form a 4-dimensional real Banach algebra, with the norm being given by the absolute value of quaternions.
- The algebra of all bounded real- or complex-valued functions defined on some set (with pointwise multiplication and the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) norm) is a unital Banach algebra.
- The algebra of all bounded [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) real- or complex-valued functions on some [locally compact space](wikipedia://en/48632) (again with pointwise operations and supremum norm) is a Banach algebra.
- The algebra of all [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) operators on a Banach space $E$ (with functional composition as multiplication and the [operator norm](wikipedia://en/246722) as norm) is a unital Banach algebra. The set of all [compact operators](wikipedia://en/577441) on $E$ is a Banach algebra and closed ideal. It is without identity if $\dim E = \infty.$[^o4]
- If $G$ is a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and $\mu$ is its [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645), then the Banach space $L^1(G)$ of all $\mu$-integrable functions on $G$ becomes a Banach algebra under the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) $x y(g) = \int x(h) y\left(h^{-1} g\right) d \mu(h)$ for $x, y \in L^1(G).$[^o5]
- [Uniform algebra](wikipedia://en/4536974): A Banach algebra that is a subalgebra of the complex algebra $C(X)$ with the supremum norm and that contains the constants and separates the points of $X$ (which must be a compact Hausdorff space).
- [Natural Banach function algebra](wikipedia://en/4536974): A uniform algebra all of whose characters are evaluations at points of $X.$
- [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184): A Banach algebra that is a closed \*-subalgebra of the algebra of bounded operators on some [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932).
- [Measure algebra](wikipedia://en/25626903): A Banach algebra consisting of all [Radon measures](wikipedia://en/521933) on some [locally compact group](wikipedia://en/1651204), where the product of two measures is given by [convolution of measures](wikipedia://en/7519#Measures).[^o5]
- The algebra of the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) $\mathbb{H}$ is a real Banach algebra, but it is not a complex algebra (and hence not a complex Banach algebra) for the simple reason that the center of the quaternions is the real numbers, which cannot contain a copy of the complex numbers.
- An [affinoid algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affinoid_algebra) is a certain kind of Banach algebra over a nonarchimedean field. Affinoid algebras are the basic building blocks in [rigid analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/5828941).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Several [elementary functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_functions) that are defined via [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) may be defined in any unital Banach algebra; examples include the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) and the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367), and more generally any [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372). (In particular, the exponential map can be used to define [abstract index groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_index_group).) The formula for the [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) remains valid in general unital Banach algebras. The [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) also holds for two commuting elements of a Banach algebra.

The set of [invertible elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_element) in any unital Banach algebra is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358), and the inversion operation on this set is continuous (and hence is a homeomorphism), so that it forms a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) under multiplication.[^o8]

If a Banach algebra has unit $\mathbf{1},$ then $\mathbf{1}$ cannot be a [commutator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutator_%28ring_theory%29); that is, $xy - yx \neq \mathbf{1}$ for any $x, y \in A.$ This is because $x y$ and $y x$ have the same [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137) except possibly $0.$

The various algebras of functions given in the examples above have very different properties from standard examples of algebras such as the reals. For example:

- Every real Banach algebra that is a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625) is isomorphic to the reals, the complexes, or the quaternions. Hence, the only complex Banach algebra that is a division algebra is the complexes. (This is known as the [Gelfand–Mazur theorem](wikipedia://en/9129699).)
- Every unital real Banach algebra with no [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441), and in which every [principal ideal](wikipedia://en/145126) is [closed](wikipedia://en/47279), is isomorphic to the reals, the complexes, or the quaternions.[^o9]
- Every commutative real unital [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) Banach algebra with no zero divisors is isomorphic to the real or complex numbers.
- Every commutative real unital Noetherian Banach algebra (possibly having zero divisors) is finite-dimensional.
- Permanently singular elements in Banach algebras are [topological divisors of zero](wikipedia://en/2605406), that is, considering extensions $B$ of Banach algebras $A$ some elements that are singular in the given algebra $A$ have a multiplicative inverse element in a Banach algebra extension $B.$ Topological divisors of zero in $A$ are permanently singular in any Banach extension $B$ of $A.$

## Spectral theory {#Spectral_theory}

Unital Banach algebras over the complex field provide a general setting to develop spectral theory. The *spectrum* of an element $x \in A,$ denoted by $\sigma(x)$, consists of all those complex [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) $\lambda$ such that $x - \lambda \mathbf{1}$ is not invertible in $A.$ The spectrum of any element $x$ is a closed subset of the closed disc in $\mathbb{C}$ with radius $\|x\|$ and center $0,$ and thus is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). Moreover, the spectrum $\sigma(x)$ of an element $x$ is [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) and satisfies the [spectral radius](wikipedia://en/386169) formula:

$$\sup \{|\lambda| : \lambda \in \sigma(x)\} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \|x^n\|^{1/n}.$$

Given $x \in A,$ the [holomorphic functional calculus](wikipedia://en/3131706) allows to define $f(x) \in A$ for any function $f$ [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) in a neighborhood of $\sigma(x).$ Furthermore, the spectral mapping theorem holds:[^o12]

$$\sigma(f(x)) = f(\sigma(x)).$$

When the Banach algebra $A$ is the algebra $L(X)$ of bounded linear operators on a complex Banach space $X$ (for example, the algebra of square matrices), the notion of the spectrum in $A$ coincides with the usual one in [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675). For $f \in C(X)$ (with a compact Hausdorff space $X$), one sees that:

$$\sigma(f) = \{f(t) : t \in X\}.$$

The norm of a normal element $x$ of a C\*-algebra coincides with its spectral radius. This generalizes an analogous fact for normal operators.

Let $A$ be a complex unital Banach algebra in which every non-zero element $x$ is invertible (a division algebra). For every $a \in A,$ there is $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ such that $a - \lambda \mathbf{1}$ is not invertible (because the spectrum of $a$ is not empty) hence $a = \lambda \mathbf{1}:$ this algebra $A$ is naturally isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}$ (the complex case of the Gelfand–Mazur theorem).

:::

## Ideals and characters {#Ideals_and_characters}

Let $A$ be a unital *commutative* Banach algebra over $\mathbb{C}.$ Since $A$ is then a commutative ring with unit, every non-invertible element of $A$ belongs to some [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) of $A.$ Since a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ in $A$ is closed, $A / \mathfrak m$ is a Banach algebra that is a field, and it follows from the [Gelfand–Mazur theorem](wikipedia://en/9129699) that there is a bijection between the set of all maximal ideals of $A$ and the set $\Delta(A)$ of all nonzero homomorphisms from $A$ to $\mathbb{C}.$ The set $\Delta(A)$ is called the *structure space* or *character space* of $A$.

A *character* $\chi \in \Delta(A)$ is a linear functional on $A$ that is at the same time multiplicative, $\chi(a b) = \chi(a) \chi(b),$ and satisfies $\chi(\mathbf{1}) = 1.$ Every character is automatically continuous from $A$ to $\mathbb{C},$ since the kernel of a character is a maximal ideal, which is closed. Moreover, the norm (that is, operator norm) of a character is one. Equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence on $A$ (that is, the topology induced by the [weak-\* topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology) of $A^*$), the character space, $\Delta(A),$ is a compact Hausdorff space.

For any $x \in A,$

$$\sigma(x) = \sigma(\hat x)$$

where $\hat x$ is the [Gelfand representation](wikipedia://en/364372) of $x$ defined as follows: $\hat x$ is the continuous function from $\Delta(A)$ to $\mathbb{C}$ given by $\hat x(\chi) = \chi(x).$ The spectrum of $\hat x,$ in the formula above, is the spectrum as element of the algebra $C(\Delta(A))$ of complex continuous functions on the compact space $\Delta(A).$ Explicitly,

$$\sigma(\hat x) = \{\chi(x) : \chi \in \Delta(A)\}.$$

As an algebra, a unital commutative Banach algebra is [semisimple](wikipedia://en/3876151) (that is, its [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) is zero) if and only if its Gelfand representation has trivial kernel. An important example of such an algebra is a commutative C\*-algebra. In fact, when $A$ is a commutative unital C\*-algebra, the Gelfand representation is then an isometric \*-isomorphism between $A$ and $C(\Delta(A)).$Proof: Since every element of a commutative C\*-algebra is normal, the Gelfand representation is isometric; in particular, it is injective and its image is closed. But the image of the Gelfand representation is dense by the [Stone–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858).

## Banach \*-algebras {#Banach_*-algebras}

A Banach \*-algebra $A$ is a Banach algebra over the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), together with a map ${}^* : A \to A$ that has the following properties:

1. $\left(x^*\right)^* = x$ for all $x \in A$ (so the map is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450)).
1. $(x + y)^* = x^* + y^*$ for all $x, y \in A.$
1. $(\lambda x)^* = \bar{\lambda}x^*$ for every $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ and every $x \in A;$ here, $\bar{\lambda}$ denotes the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $\lambda.$
1. $(x y)^* = y^* x^*$ for all $x, y \in A.$

In other words, a Banach \*-algebra is a Banach algebra over $\mathbb{C}$ that is also a [\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/293426).

In most natural examples, one also has that the involution is [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777), that is,

$$\|x^*\| = \|x\| \quad \text{ for all } x \in A.$$

Some authors include this isometric property in the definition of a Banach \*-algebra.

A Banach \*-algebra satisfying $\|x^* x\| = \|x^*\| \|x\|$ is a [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184).

:::
