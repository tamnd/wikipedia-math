# Abelian group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abelian group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **abelian group**, also called a **commutative group**, is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) in which the result of applying the [group operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_operation) to two group elements does not depend on the order in which they are written. That is, the group operation is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative). With addition as an operation, the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) form abelian groups, and the concept of an abelian group may be viewed as a generalization of these examples. Abelian groups are named after the Norwegian mathematician [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573).[^o2]

:::

The concept of an abelian group underlies many fundamental [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364), such as [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), and [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788). The theory of abelian groups is generally simpler than that of their [non-abelian](wikipedia://en/4791400) counterparts, and finite abelian groups are very well understood and fully classified.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An abelian group is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $A$, together with an [operation](wikipedia://en/3948) ･ , that combines any two [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) $a$ and $b$ of $A$ to form another element of $A,$ denoted $a \cdot b$. The symbol ･ is a general placeholder for a concretely given operation. To qualify as an abelian group, the set and operation, $(A, \cdot)$, must satisfy four requirements known as the *abelian group axioms*:

**[Associativity](wikipedia://en/1335)**

For all $a$, $b$, and $c$ in $A$, the equation $(a \cdot b)\cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ holds.

**[Identity element](wikipedia://en/14962)**

There exists an element $e$ in $A$, such that for all elements $a$ in $A$, the equation $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$ holds.

**[Inverse element](wikipedia://en/45199)**

For each $a$ in $A$ there exists an element $b$ in $A$ such that $a \cdot b = b \cdot a = e$, where $e$ is the identity element.

**[Commutativity](wikipedia://en/294390)**

For all $a$, $b$ in $A$, $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$.

Some authors further include as requirements some properties that belong to the definition of an operation: that the operation is *defined* for any ordered pair of elements of $A$, that the result is *[well-defined](wikipedia://en/253456)*, and that the result *[belongs to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29#Notation_and_terminology)* $A$.

A group in which the group operation is not commutative is called a *non-abelian group* or *non-commutative group*.[^o5]

## Facts {#Facts}

### Notation {#Notation}

There are two main notational conventions for abelian groups – additive and multiplicative.

::: {#o10 .table cue=table}

| Convention | Operation | Identity | Powers | Inverse |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Addition | $x + y$ | 0 | $nx$ | $-x$ |
| Multiplication | $x \cdot y$ or $xy$ | 1 | $x^n$ | $x^{-1}$ |

:::

Generally, the multiplicative notation is the usual notation for groups, while the additive notation is the usual notation for [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and [rings](wikipedia://en/48404). The additive notation may also be used to emphasize that a particular group is abelian, whenever both abelian and non-abelian groups are considered, with some notable exceptions being [near-rings](wikipedia://en/839900) and [partially ordered groups](wikipedia://en/172048), where an operation is written additively even when non-abelian.[^o11][^o12]

:::

### Multiplication table {#Multiplication_table}

To verify that a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is abelian, a table (matrix) – known as a [Cayley table](wikipedia://en/1802723) – can be constructed in a similar fashion to a [multiplication table](wikipedia://en/57122).[^o14] If the group is $G = \{g_1 = e, g_2, \dots, g_n \}$ under the operation $\cdot$, the $(i, j)$-th entry of this table contains the product $g_i \cdot g_j$.

The group is abelian [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) this table is [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric) about the main diagonal, which means that for all $i, j = 1, ..., n$, the $(i, j)$ entry of the table equals the $(j, i)$ entry. Indeed, this equality expresses that $g_i \cdot g_j = g_j \cdot g_i.$

## Examples {#Examples}

- The group consisting of only the identity element is abelian.
- For the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and the operation [addition](wikipedia://en/61338) $+$, denoted $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the operation + combines any two integers to form a third integer, addition is associative, zero is the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225), every integer $n$ has an [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312), $-n$, and the addition operation is commutative since $n + m = m + n$ for any two integers $m$ and $n$.
- Every [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $G$ is abelian, because if $x$, $y$ are in $G$, then $xy = a^ma^n = a^{m+n} = a^na^m = yx$. Thus the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), $\mathbb{Z}$, form an abelian group under addition, as do the [integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/20087), $\mathbb{Z}/n \mathbb{Z}$.
- Every [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is an abelian group with respect to its addition operation. In a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) the invertible elements, or [units](wikipedia://en/289666), form an abelian [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742). In particular, the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) are an abelian group under addition, and the nonzero real numbers are an abelian group under multiplication.
- Every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of an abelian group is [normal](wikipedia://en/21918), so each subgroup gives rise to a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526). Subgroups, quotients, and [direct sums](wikipedia://en/106341) of abelian groups are again abelian. The finite [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) abelian groups are exactly the cyclic groups of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) [order](wikipedia://en/493527).[^o17]
- The concepts of abelian group and $\mathbb{Z}$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410) agree. More specifically, every $\mathbb{Z}$-module is an abelian group with its operation of addition, and every abelian group is a module over the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ in a unique way.

In general, [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), even invertible matrices, do not form an abelian group under multiplication because matrix multiplication is generally not commutative. However, some groups of matrices are abelian groups under matrix multiplication – one example is the group of $2 \times 2$ [rotation matrices](wikipedia://en/856005).

:::

## Historical remarks {#Historical_remarks}

[Camille Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931) named abelian groups after the [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573), who had found that the commutativity of the group of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) implies that the roots of the polynomial can be [calculated by using radicals](wikipedia://en/61316#Solvable_groups_and_solution_by_radicals).[^o19][^o20]

## Properties {#Properties}

If $n$ is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) and $x$ is an element of an abelian group $G$ written additively, then $nx$ can be defined as $x + x + \cdots + x$ ($n$ summands) and $(-n)x = -(nx)$. In this way, $G$ becomes a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers. In fact, the modules over $\mathbb{Z}$ can be identified with the abelian groups.[^o23]

Theorems about abelian groups (i.e. [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over the [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) $\mathbb{Z}$) can often be generalized to theorems about modules over an arbitrary principal ideal domain. A typical example is the classification of [finitely generated abelian groups](wikipedia://en/58527) which is a specialization of the [structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/9902787). In the case of finitely generated abelian groups, this theorem guarantees that an abelian group splits as a [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of a [torsion group](wikipedia://en/3568695) and a [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296). The former may be written as a direct sum of finitely many groups of the form $\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$ for $p$ prime, and the latter is a direct sum of finitely many copies of $\mathbb{Z}$.

If $f, g: G \to H$ are two [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) between abelian groups, then their sum $f + g$, defined by $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$, is again a homomorphism. (This is not true if $H$ is a non-abelian group.) The set $\text{Hom}(G,H)$ of all group homomorphisms from $G$ to $H$ is therefore an abelian group in its own right.

Somewhat akin to the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), every abelian group has a *[rank](wikipedia://en/244541)*. It is defined as the maximal [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6173) of a set of [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) (over the integers) elements of the group.[^o24] Finite abelian groups and torsion groups have rank zero, and every abelian group of rank zero is a torsion group. The integers and the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) have rank one, as well as every nonzero [additive subgroup](wikipedia://en/332229) of the rationals. On the other hand, the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of the nonzero rationals has an infinite rank, as it is a free abelian group with the set of the [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) as a basis (this results from the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556)).

The [center](wikipedia://en/7125) $Z(G)$ of a group $G$ is the set of elements that commute with every element of $G$. A group $G$ is abelian if and only if it is equal to its center $Z(G)$. The center of a group $G$ is always a [characteristic](wikipedia://en/7198) abelian subgroup of $G$. If the quotient group $G/Z(G)$ of a group by its center is cyclic then $G$ is abelian.[^o25]

## Finite abelian groups {#Finite_abelian_groups}

Cyclic groups of [integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/20087), $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, were among the first examples of groups. It turns out that an arbitrary finite abelian group is isomorphic to a direct sum of finite cyclic groups of prime power order, and these orders are uniquely determined, forming a complete system of invariants. The [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of a finite abelian group can be described directly in terms of these invariants. The theory had been first developed in the 1879 paper of [Georg Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius) and [Ludwig Stickelberger](wikipedia://en/18870377) and later was both simplified and generalized to finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, forming an important chapter of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).

Any group of prime order is isomorphic to a cyclic group and therefore abelian. Any group whose order is a square of a prime number is also abelian.[^o28] In fact, for every prime number $p$ there are (up to isomorphism) exactly two groups of order $p^2$, namely $\mathbb{Z}_{p^2}$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p\times\mathbb{Z}_p$.

::: {#o30 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups"}

### Classification {#Classification}

The **fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups** states that every finite abelian group $G$ can be expressed as the direct sum of cyclic subgroups of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666)-power order; it is also known as the **basis theorem for finite abelian groups**. Moreover, automorphism groups of cyclic groups are examples of abelian groups.[^o31] This is generalized by the [fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_finitely_generated_abelian_groups), with finite groups being the special case when *G* has zero [rank](wikipedia://en/244541); this in turn admits numerous further generalizations.

The classification was proven by [Leopold Kronecker](wikipedia://en/318450) in 1870, though it was not stated in modern group-theoretic terms until later, and was preceded by a similar classification of quadratic forms by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) in 1801; see [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_finitely_generated_abelian_groups#History) for details.

The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_{mn}$ of order $mn$ is isomorphic to the direct sum of $\mathbb{Z}_m$ and $\mathbb{Z}_n$ if and only if $m$ and $n$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime). It follows that any finite abelian group $G$ is isomorphic to a direct sum of the form

$$\bigoplus_{i=1}^{u}\ \mathbb{Z}_{k_i}$$

in either of the following canonical ways:

- the numbers $k_1, k_2, \dots, k_u$ are powers of (not necessarily distinct) primes,
- or $k_1$ [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) $k_2$, which divides $k_3$, and so on up to $k_u$.

For example, $\mathbb{Z}_{15}$ can be expressed as the direct sum of two cyclic subgroups of order 3 and 5: $\mathbb{Z}_{15} \cong \{0,5,10\} \oplus \{0,3,6,9,12\}$. The same can be said for any abelian group of order 15, leading to the remarkable conclusion that all abelian groups of order 15 are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397).

For another example, every abelian group of order 8 is isomorphic to either $\mathbb{Z}_8$ (the integers 0 to 7 under addition modulo 8), $\mathbb{Z}_4\oplus \mathbb{Z}_2$ (the odd integers 1 to 15 under multiplication modulo 16), or $\mathbb{Z}_2\oplus \mathbb{Z}_2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}_2$.

See also [list of small groups](wikipedia://en/47756) for finite abelian groups of order 30 or less.

:::

### Automorphisms {#Automorphisms}

One can apply the fundamental theorem to count (and sometimes determine) the [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/12397#Automorphisms) of a given finite abelian group $G$. To do this, one uses the fact that if $G$ splits as a direct sum $H\oplus K$ of subgroups of [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) order, then

$$\operatorname{Aut}(H\oplus K) \cong \operatorname{Aut}(H)\oplus \operatorname{Aut}(K).$$

Given this, the fundamental theorem shows that to compute the automorphism group of $G$ it suffices to compute the automorphism groups of the [Sylow](wikipedia://en/53993) $p$-subgroups separately (that is, all direct sums of cyclic subgroups, each with order a power of $p$). Fix a prime $p$ and suppose the exponents $e_i$ of the cyclic factors of the Sylow $p$-subgroup are arranged in increasing order:

$$e_1\leq e_2 \leq\cdots\leq e_n$$

for some $n > 0$. One needs to find the automorphisms of

$$\mathbb{Z}_{p^{e_1}} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}_{p^{e_n}}.$$

One special case is when $n = 1$, so that there is only one cyclic prime-power factor in the Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$. In this case the theory of automorphisms of a finite [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) can be used. Another special case is when $n$ is arbitrary but $e_i = 1$ for $1 \le i \le n$. Here, one is considering $P$ to be of the form

$$\mathbb{Z}_p \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}_p,$$

so elements of this subgroup can be viewed as comprising a vector space of dimension $n$ over the finite field of $p$ elements $\mathbb{F}_p$. The automorphisms of this subgroup are therefore given by the invertible linear transformations, so

$$\operatorname{Aut}(P)\cong\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{F}_p),$$

where $\mathrm{GL}$ is the appropriate [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564). This is easily shown to have order

$$\left|\operatorname{Aut}(P)\right|=(p^n-1)\cdots(p^n-p^{n-1}).$$

In the most general case, where the $e_i$ and $n$ are arbitrary, the automorphism group is more difficult to determine. It is known, however, that if one defines

$$d_k=\max\{r\mid e_r = e_k\}$$

and

$$c_k=\min\{r\mid e_r=e_k\}$$

then one has in particular $k \le d_k$, $c_k \le k$, and

$$\left|\operatorname{Aut}(P)\right| = \prod_{k=1}^n (p^{d_k}-p^{k-1}) \prod_{j=1}^n (p^{e_j})^{n-d_j} \prod_{i=1}^n (p^{e_i-1})^{n-c_i+1}.$$

One can check that this yields the orders in the previous examples as special cases (see Hillar & Rhea).

:::

## Finitely generated abelian groups {#Finitely_generated_abelian_groups}

An abelian group $A$ is finitely generated if it contains a finite set of elements (called *generators*) $G=\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ such that every element of the group is a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) with integer coefficients of elements of $G$.

Let $L$ be a [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) with basis $B=\{b_1, \ldots, b_n\}.$ There is a unique [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $p\colon L \to A,$ such that

$$p(b_i) = x_i\quad \text{for } i=1,\ldots, n.$$

This homomorphism is [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873), and its [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915) is finitely generated (since integers form a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129)). Consider the matrix $M$ with integer entries, such that the entries of its $j$th column are the coefficients of the $j$th generator of the kernel. Then, the abelian group is isomorphic to the [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) of linear map defined by $M$. Conversely every [integer matrix](wikipedia://en/4500787) defines a finitely generated abelian group.

It follows that the study of finitely generated abelian groups is totally equivalent with the study of integer matrices. In particular, changing the generating set of $A$ is equivalent with multiplying $M$ on the left by a [unimodular matrix](wikipedia://en/593773) (that is, an invertible integer matrix whose inverse is also an integer matrix). Changing the generating set of the kernel of $M$ is equivalent with multiplying $M$ on the right by a unimodular matrix.

The [Smith normal form](wikipedia://en/1106564) of $M$ is a matrix

$$S=UMV,$$

where $U$ and $V$ are unimodular, and $S$ is a matrix such that all non-diagonal entries are zero, the non-zero diagonal entries $d_{1,1}, \ldots, d_{k,k}$ are the first ones, and $d_{j,j}$ is a divisor of $d_{i,i}$ for $i > j$. The existence and the shape of the Smith normal form proves that the finitely generated abelian group $A$ is the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/106341)

$$\mathbb{Z}^r \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_{1,1}\mathbb{Z} \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_{k,k}\mathbb{Z},$$

where $r$ is the number of zero rows at the bottom of $S$ (and also the [rank](wikipedia://en/244541) of the group). This is the [fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_finitely_generated_abelian_groups).

The existence of algorithms for Smith normal form shows that the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups is not only a theorem of abstract existence, but provides a way for computing expression of finitely generated abelian groups as direct sums.[^o34]

## Infinite abelian groups {#Infinite_abelian_groups}

The simplest infinite abelian group is the [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) $\mathbb{Z}$. Any [finitely generated abelian group](wikipedia://en/58527) $A$ is isomorphic to the direct sum of $r$ copies of $\mathbb{Z}$ and a finite abelian group, which in turn is decomposable into a direct sum of finitely many [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) of [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) orders. Even though the decomposition is not unique, the number $r$, called the [rank](wikipedia://en/244541) of $A$, and the prime powers giving the orders of finite cyclic summands are uniquely determined.

By contrast, classification of general infinitely generated abelian groups is far from complete. [Divisible groups](wikipedia://en/472147), i.e. abelian groups $A$ in which the equation $nx = a$ admits a solution $x \in A$ for any natural number $n$ and element $a$ of $A$, constitute one important class of infinite abelian groups that can be completely characterized. Every divisible group is isomorphic to a direct sum, with summands isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}$ and [Prüfer groups](wikipedia://en/4885031) $\mathbb{Q}_p/\mathbb{Z}_p$ for various prime numbers $p$, and the cardinality of the set of summands of each type is uniquely determined.[^o36] Moreover, if a divisible group $A$ is a subgroup of an abelian group $G$ then $A$ admits a direct complement: a subgroup $C$ of $G$ such that $G = A \oplus C$. Thus divisible groups are [injective modules](wikipedia://en/473775) in the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748), and conversely, every injective abelian group is divisible ([Baer's criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baer's_criterion)). An abelian group without non-zero divisible subgroups is called **reduced**.

Two important special classes of infinite abelian groups with diametrically opposite properties are *torsion groups* and *torsion-free groups*, exemplified by the groups $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ (periodic) and $\mathbb{Q}$ (torsion-free).

### Torsion groups {#Torsion_groups}

An abelian group is called **[periodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_group)** or **[torsion](wikipedia://en/3039330)**, if every element has finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527). A direct sum of finite cyclic groups is periodic. Although the converse statement is not true in general, some special cases are known. The first and second [Prüfer theorems](wikipedia://en/27970235) state that if $A$ is a periodic group, and it either has a **bounded exponent**, i.e., $nA = 0$ for some natural number $n$, or is countable and the [$p$-heights](wikipedia://en/27972322) of the elements of $A$ are finite for each $p$, then $A$ is isomorphic to a direct sum of finite cyclic groups.[^o38] The cardinality of the set of direct summands isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/p^m\mathbb{Z}$ in such a decomposition is an invariant of $A$.[^o39] These theorems were later subsumed in the **Kulikov criterion**. In a different direction, [Helmut Ulm](wikipedia://en/27973380) found an extension of the second Prüfer theorem to countable abelian $p$-groups with elements of infinite height: those groups are completely classified by means of their [Ulm invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulm_invariant).[^o40]

### Torsion-free and mixed groups {#Torsion-free_and_mixed_groups}

An abelian group is called **torsion-free** if every non-zero element has infinite order. Several classes of [torsion-free abelian groups](wikipedia://en/11630973) have been studied extensively:

- [Free abelian groups](wikipedia://en/247296), i.e. arbitrary direct sums of $\mathbb{Z}$
- [Cotorsion](wikipedia://en/5739241) and [algebraically compact](wikipedia://en/478346) torsion-free groups such as the [$p$-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer)
- [Slender groups](wikipedia://en/5739047)[^o42]

An abelian group that is neither periodic nor torsion-free is called **mixed**. If $A$ is an abelian group and $T(A)$ is its [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052), then the factor group $A/T(A)$ is torsion-free. However, in general the torsion subgroup is not a direct summand of $A$, so $A$ is *not* isomorphic to $T(A) \oplus A/T(A)$. Thus the theory of mixed groups involves more than simply combining the results about periodic and torsion-free groups. The additive group $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers is torsion-free $\mathbb{Z}$-module.[^o43]

### Invariants and classification {#Invariants_and_classification}

One of the most basic invariants of an infinite abelian group $A$ is its [rank](wikipedia://en/244541): the cardinality of the maximal [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) subset of $A$. Abelian groups of rank 0 are precisely the periodic groups, while [torsion-free abelian groups of rank 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion-free_abelian_groups_of_rank_1) are necessarily subgroups of $\mathbb{Q}$ and can be completely described. More generally, a torsion-free abelian group of finite rank $r$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}_r$. On the other hand, the group of [$p$-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer) $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a torsion-free abelian group of infinite $\mathbb{Z}$-rank and the groups $\mathbb{Z}_p^n$ with different $n$ are non-isomorphic, so this invariant does not even fully capture properties of some familiar groups.

The classification theorems for finitely generated, divisible, countable periodic, and rank 1 torsion-free abelian groups explained above were all obtained before 1950 and form a foundation of the classification of more general infinite abelian groups. Important technical tools used in classification of infinite abelian groups are [pure](wikipedia://en/5739585) and [basic](wikipedia://en/27974802) subgroups. Introduction of various invariants of torsion-free abelian groups has been one avenue of further progress. See the books by [Irving Kaplansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Kaplansky), [László Fuchs](wikipedia://en/34809998), [Phillip Griffith](wikipedia://en/34741581), and [David Arnold](wikipedia://en/51256429), as well as the proceedings of the conferences on Abelian Group Theory published in *[Lecture Notes in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/47931235)* for more recent findings.

### Additive groups of rings {#Additive_groups_of_rings}

The additive group of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is an abelian group, but not all abelian groups are additive groups of rings (with nontrivial multiplication). Some important topics in this area of study are:

- [Tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844)
- A.L.S. Corner's results on countable torsion-free groups
- Shelah's work to remove cardinality restrictions
- [Burnside ring](wikipedia://en/4933121)

## Relation to other mathematical topics {#Relation_to_other_mathematical_topics}

Many large abelian groups possess a natural [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), which turns them into [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315).

The collection of all abelian groups, together with the [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) between them, forms the [category](wikipedia://en/454748) $\textbf{Ab}$, the prototype of an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063).

(txt) proved that the first-order theory of abelian groups, unlike its non-abelian counterpart, is decidable. Most [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) other than [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) are [undecidable](wikipedia://en/913118).

There are still many areas of current research:

- Amongst torsion-free abelian groups of finite rank, only the finitely generated case and the [rank 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion-free_abelian_groups_of_rank_1) case are well understood.
- There are many unsolved problems in the theory of infinite-rank torsion-free abelian groups.
- While countable torsion abelian groups are well understood through simple presentations and Ulm invariants, the case of countable mixed groups is much less mature.
- Many mild extensions of the first-order theory of abelian groups are known to be undecidable.
- Finite abelian groups remain a topic of research in [computational group theory](wikipedia://en/618584).

Moreover, abelian groups of infinite order lead, quite surprisingly, to deep questions about the [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) commonly assumed to underlie all of mathematics. Take the [Whitehead problem](wikipedia://en/212619): are all Whitehead groups of infinite order also [free abelian groups](wikipedia://en/247296)? In the 1970s, [Saharon Shelah](wikipedia://en/331211) proved that the Whitehead problem is:

- [Undecidable in ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_statements_undecidable_in_ZFC) ([Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel_axioms)), the conventional [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory) from which nearly all of present-day mathematics can be derived. The Whitehead problem is also the first question in ordinary mathematics proved undecidable in ZFC;
- Undecidable even if [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC) is augmented by taking the [generalized continuum hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_continuum_hypothesis) as an axiom;
- Positively answered if ZFC is augmented with the axiom of [constructibility](wikipedia://en/343335) (see [statements true in L](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statements_true_in_L)).
