# Archimedean solid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Archimedean solid"}

*Image: Archimedian Solids 15.jpg.* The Archimedean solids. Two of them are [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160), with both forms shown, making 15 models in all.

The **Archimedean solids** are a set of thirteen [convex polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedra) whose faces are regular polygons and are [vertex-transitive](wikipedia://en/581560),[^o2] although they are not [face-transitive](wikipedia://en/6286120) and are generally not [edge-transitive](wikipedia://en/6289189). The solids were named after [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844), although he did not claim credit for them. They belong to the class of [uniform polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polyhedra), the polyhedra with regular faces and symmetric vertices. Some Archimedean solids were portrayed in the works of artists and mathematicians during the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance).

:::

The [elongated square gyrobicupola](wikipedia://en/1128551) or *pseudo* is an extra polyhedron with regular faces and congruent vertices. Still, it is not generally counted as an Archimedean solid because it is not [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive).

## The solids {#The_solids}

The Archimedean solids have a single [vertex configuration](wikipedia://en/3603745) and highly symmetric properties. A vertex configuration indicates which regular polygons meet at each vertex. For instance, the configuration $3 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ indicates a polyhedron in which each vertex is met by alternating two triangles and two pentagons. Highly symmetric properties in this case mean the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of each solid was derived from the [Platonic solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_solids), resulting from their construction.[^n1] Some sources say the Archimedean solids are synonymous with the [semiregular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/1548894).[^n2] Yet, the definition of a semiregular polyhedron may also include the infinite families of [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007) and [antiprisms](wikipedia://en/1851), including the [elongated square gyrobicupola](wikipedia://en/1128551).[^o6]

The [skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton_%28topology%29) of Archimedean solids can be drawn in a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806), named [Archimedean graph](wikipedia://en/44969442). Such graphs are [regular](wikipedia://en/85821), [polyhedral](wikipedia://en/24656270) (and therefore by necessity also [3-vertex-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-vertex-connected_graph) [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314)), and also [Hamiltonian graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_graph).[^o7]

The construction of some Archimedean solids begins from the Platonic solids. The [truncation](wikipedia://en/4224429) involves cutting away corners; to preserve symmetry, the cut is in a plane perpendicular to the line joining a corner to the center of the polyhedron and is the same for all corners, and an example can be found in [truncated icosahedron](wikipedia://en/31282) constructed by cutting off all the [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968)'s vertices, having the same symmetry as the icosahedron, the [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844).[^o27] If the truncation is exactly deep enough such that each pair of faces from adjacent vertices shares exactly one point, it is known as a [rectification](wikipedia://en/2192366). [Expansion](wikipedia://en/6341070) involves moving each face away from the center (by the same distance to preserve the symmetry of the Platonic solid) and taking the convex hull. An example is the rhombicuboctahedron, which is constructed by separating the cube or octahedron's faces from their centroids and filling them with squares.[^n6] [Snub](wikipedia://en/9428917) is a construction process of polyhedra by separating the polyhedron faces, twisting their faces in certain angles, and filling them up with [equilateral triangles](wikipedia://en/173285). Examples can be found in [snub cube](wikipedia://en/180249) and [snub dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/287137). The resulting construction of these solids gives the property of [chirality](wikipedia://en/1170160), meaning they are not identical when reflected in a mirror.[^n7] However, not all of them can be constructed in such a way, or they could be constructed alternatively. For example, the [icosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/15023) can be constructed by attaching two [pentagonal rotunda](wikipedia://en/1128491) bases-to-base, or a rhombicuboctahedron that can be constructed alternatively by attaching two [square cupolas](wikipedia://en/1128479) on the bases of an octagonal prism.[^n4]

At least ten of the Archimedean solids have the [Rupert property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_property): each can pass through a copy of itself, of the same size. They are the cuboctahedron, truncated octahedron, truncated cube, rhombicuboctahedron, icosidodecahedron, truncated cuboctahedron, truncated icosahedron, truncated dodecahedron, and the truncated tetrahedron.[^o31]

The [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) of an Archimedean solid is a [Catalan solid](wikipedia://en/564393).[^n1]

## Background of discovery {#Background_of_discovery}

The names of Archimedean solids were taken from the Ancient Greek mathematician [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844), who discussed them in a now-lost work. Although they were not credited to Archimedes originally, [Pappus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/509300) in the fifth section of his titled compendium *Synagoge*, referring to Archimedes, listed thirteen polyhedra and briefly described them in terms of how many faces of each kind these polyhedra have.[^o34]

During the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), artists and mathematicians valued pure forms with high symmetry. Some Archimedean solids appeared in [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca)'s *[De quinque corporibus regularibus](wikipedia://en/65240274)*, in attempting to study and copy the works of Archimedes, as well as include citations to Archimedes.[^n8] Yet, he did not credit those shapes to Archimedes and knew of Archimedes' work, but rather appeared to be an independent rediscovery.[^n9] Other appearance of the solids appeared in the works of [Wenzel Jamnitzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenzel_Jamnitzer)'s *[Perspectiva Corporum Regularium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspectiva_Corporum_Regularium)*, and both *[Summa de arithmetica](wikipedia://en/45351614)* and *[Divina proportione](wikipedia://en/16175342)* by [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473), drawn by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci).[^o37] The [net](wikipedia://en/1148129) of Archimedean solids appeared in [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer)'s *Underweysung der Messung*, copied from the Pacioli's work. By around 1620, [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) in his *[Harmonices Mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonices_Mundi)* had completed the rediscovery of the thirteen polyhedra, as well as defining the [prisms](wikipedia://en/283007), [antiprisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiprisms), and the non-convex solids known as [Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler–Poinsot_polyhedra).[^n10]

Kepler may have also found another solid known as [elongated square gyrobicupola](wikipedia://en/1128551) or *pseudorhombicuboctahedron*. Kepler once stated that there were fourteen Archimedean solids, yet his published enumeration only includes the thirteen uniform polyhedra. The first clear statement of such solid existence was made by [Duncan Sommerville](wikipedia://en/8276753) in 1905.[^n11] The solid appeared when some mathematicians mistakenly constructed the [rhombicuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/26213): two [square cupolas](wikipedia://en/1128479) attached to the [octagonal prism](wikipedia://en/3026569), with one of them rotated forty-five degrees.[^o40] The thirteen solids have the property of [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive), meaning any two vertices of those can be translated onto the other one, but the elongated square gyrobicupola does not. (Grünbaum 2009) observed that it meets a weaker definition of an Archimedean solid, in which "identical vertices" means merely that the parts of the polyhedron near any two vertices look the same (they have the same shapes of faces meeting around each vertex in the same order and forming the same angles). Grünbaum pointed out a frequent error in which authors define Archimedean solids using some form of this local definition but omit the fourteenth polyhedron. If only thirteen polyhedra are to be listed, the definition must use global symmetries of the polyhedron rather than local neighborhoods. In the aftermath, the elongated square gyrobicupola was withdrawn from the Archimedean solids and included in the [Johnson solids](wikipedia://en/16034) instead, a convex polyhedron in which all of the faces are regular polygons.[^n11]
