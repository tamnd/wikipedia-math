# Ecliptic

*Image: Ecliptic with earth and sun animation.gif.* As seen from the orbiting [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) [appears to move](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diurnal_motion) with respect to the [fixed stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_stars), and the ecliptic is the yearly path the Sun follows on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239). This process repeats itself in a cycle lasting a little over [365 days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_year).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ecliptic"}

The **ecliptic** or **ecliptic plane** is the apparent path of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239), resulting from [Earth's orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit) around the Sun.[^o2][^o3] It was a central concept in a number of ancient sciences, providing the framework for key measurements in astronomy, astrology and calendar-making. The ecliptic is so named because the ancients noted that [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse) only occur when the Moon is crossing it.[^o49]

:::

[Sun path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_path) can be traced against the [background of stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_stars), especially the [Zodiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiac) constellations.[^o4] The planets of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) can be seen along the ecliptic because their orbital planes are very close to Earth's. The Moon also appears near the plane, with the [Moon's orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon's_orbit) plane inclined only 5.1°, intersecting the ecliptic at the [lunar nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_node).

The ecliptic is an important [reference plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_of_reference) and is the basis of the [ecliptic coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48387). Ancient scientists were able to calculate Earth's [axial tilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_tilt) by comparing the angle of the ecliptic (about 23.4°) to that of the [equatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) plane.

## Sun's apparent motion {#Sun's_apparent_motion}

The ecliptic is the [apparent path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_path) of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) throughout the course of a [year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year).[^o6]

Because Earth takes one year [to orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit) the Sun, the apparent position of the Sun takes one year to make a complete circuit of the ecliptic. With slightly more than 365 days in one year, the Sun moves a little less than 1° eastward every day. This small difference in the Sun's position against the stars causes any particular spot on Earth's surface to catch up with (and stand directly north or south of) the Sun about four minutes later each day than it would if Earth did not orbit; a day on Earth is therefore 24 hours long rather than the approximately 23-hour 56-minute [sidereal day](wikipedia://en/48837). Again, this is a simplification, based on a hypothetical Earth that orbits at a uniform angular speed around the Sun. The actual speed with which Earth orbits the Sun varies slightly during the year, so the speed with which the Sun seems to move along the ecliptic also varies. For example, the Sun is north of the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) for about 185 days of each year, and south of it for about 180 days.[^o7] The variation of orbital speed accounts for part of the [equation of time](wikipedia://en/438948).[^o8]

Because of the movement of Earth around the Earth–Moon [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961), the apparent path of the Sun wobbles slightly, with a period of about [one month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon). Because of further [perturbations](wikipedia://en/1490148) by the other [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) of the Solar System, the Earth–Moon [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter) wobbles slightly around a mean position in a complex fashion.

## Inclination to the plane of the Solar System {#Inclination_to_the_plane_of_the_Solar_System}

*Image: The Mysterious Case of the Disappearing Dust.jpg.* A depiction of the early [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System)'s [protoplanetary disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoplanetary_disk) from which Earth and other Solar System bodies were formed

Most of the major bodies of the Solar System orbit the Sun in nearly the same plane. This is thought to be due to the planets being formed from a [protoplanetary disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protoplanetary_disk), with all the dust and gas orbiting the sun close to a single plane.[^o10] The *[invariable plane](wikipedia://en/1673352)* of the Solar System is the plane passing through its [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) perpendicular to its [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) vector.[^o11] Earth's orbit, and hence, the ecliptic, is inclined a little more than 1° to the invariable plane, Jupiter's orbit is within a little more than ½° of it, and Mercury, the planet with the highest inclination, lies around 6°.[^n1] Because of this, most Solar System bodies appear very close to the ecliptic in the sky.

The invariable plane is defined by the angular momentum of the entire Solar System, essentially the vector sum of all of the [orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) and [rotational](wikipedia://en/39789#Astronomy) angular momenta of all the bodies of the system; more than 60% of the total comes from the orbit of Jupiter.[^n1] That sum requires precise knowledge of every object in the system, making it a somewhat uncertain value. Because of the uncertainty regarding the exact location of the invariable plane, and because the ecliptic is well defined by the apparent motion of the Sun, the ecliptic is used as the reference plane of the Solar System both for precision and convenience. The only drawback of using the ecliptic instead of the invariable plane is that over geologic time scales, it will move against fixed reference points in the sky's distant background.[^o12][^o13]

## Referencing Earth's equator {#Referencing_Earth's_equator}

*Image: Earths orbit and ecliptic.svg.* The ecliptic plane (gray) projected outward to the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239). The 23.44° tilt of Earth's axis defines its [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) (red). The [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) (red dots) occur biannually, when the Sun appears [directly above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith) the equator.

Because [Earth's rotational axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_rotation) is not perpendicular to its [orbital plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_plane_%28astronomy%29), Earth's [equatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) plane is not [coplanar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coplanar) with the ecliptic plane, but is inclined to it by an angle of about 23.4°, which is known as the *[obliquity of the ecliptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obliquity_of_the_ecliptic)*.[^o19] If the equator is projected outward to the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239), forming the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681), it crosses the ecliptic at two points known as the [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox). The Sun, in its apparent motion along the ecliptic, crosses the celestial equator at these points, one from south to north, the other from north to south. The crossing from south to north is known as the [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox), also known as the *first point of Aries* and the *[ascending node](wikipedia://en/978717) of the ecliptic* on the celestial equator. The crossing from north to south is the [September equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/September_equinox) or [descending node](wikipedia://en/978717).[^o20]

### Celestial reference plane {#Celestial_reference_plane}

*Image: Ecliptic vs equator small.gif.* The apparent motion of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) along the ecliptic (red) as seen on the inside of the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239). [Ecliptic coordinates](wikipedia://en/48387) appear in (red). The [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681) (blue) and the [equatorial coordinates](wikipedia://en/48384) (blue), being inclined to the ecliptic, appear to wobble as the Sun advances.

The ecliptic forms one of the two fundamental [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) used as reference for positions on the celestial sphere, the other being the [celestial equator](wikipedia://en/153681). Perpendicular to the ecliptic are the [ecliptic poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecliptic_pole), the north ecliptic pole being the pole north of the equator. Of the two fundamental planes, the ecliptic is closer to unmoving against the background stars, its motion due to planetary [precession](wikipedia://en/24714) being roughly 1/100 that of the celestial equator.[^o22]

[Spherical coordinates](wikipedia://en/29181), known as ecliptic longitude and latitude or celestial longitude and latitude, are used to specify positions of bodies on the celestial sphere with respect to the ecliptic. Longitude is measured positively eastward 0° to 360° along the ecliptic from the March equinox, the same direction in which the Sun appears to move. Latitude is measured perpendicular to the ecliptic, to +90° northward or −90° southward to the poles of the ecliptic, the ecliptic itself being 0° latitude. For a complete spherical position, a distance parameter is also necessary. Different distance units are used for different objects. Within the Solar System, [astronomical units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit) are used, and for objects near [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), [Earth radii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius) or [kilometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilometre) are used. A corresponding right-handed [rectangular coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) is also used occasionally; the *x*-axis is directed toward the March equinox, the *y*-axis 90° to the east, and the *z*-axis toward the north ecliptic pole; the astronomical unit is the unit of measure. Symbols for ecliptic coordinates are somewhat standardized; see the table.[^o23]

*Summary of notation for ecliptic coordinates[^o25]*

Ecliptic coordinates are convenient for specifying positions of Solar System objects, as most of the planets' orbits have small [inclinations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_inclination) to the ecliptic, and therefore always appear relatively close to it on the sky. Because Earth's orbit, and hence the ecliptic, moves very little, it is a relatively fixed reference with respect to the stars.

Because of the [precessional motion of the equinox](wikipedia://en/24714), the ecliptic coordinates of objects on the celestial sphere are continuously changing. Specifying a position in ecliptic coordinates requires specifying a particular equinox, that is, the equinox of a particular date, known as an [epoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29); the coordinates are referred to the direction of the equinox at that date. For instance, the *Astronomical Almanac* lists the [heliocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric#Modern_use_of_geocentric_and_heliocentric) position of [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars) at 0h [Terrestrial Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_Time), 4 January 2010 as: longitude 118°09′15.8″, latitude +1°43′16.7″, true heliocentric distance 1.6302454 AU, mean equinox and ecliptic of date. This specifies the [mean equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_equinox) of 4 January 2010 0h TT [as above](wikipedia://en/9264#Relationship_to_the_equator), without the addition of nutation.[^o27]

## Change of inclination {#Change_of_inclination}

The obliquity of the ecliptic of 23.4° is currently decreasing 0.013 degrees (47 arcseconds) per hundred years because of planetary perturbations.[^o29]

The angular value of the obliquity is found by observation of the motions of Earth and other planets over many years. Astronomers produce new [fundamental ephemerides](wikipedia://en/34329151) as the accuracy of [observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy) improves and as the understanding of the [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_dynamics) increases, and from these ephemerides various astronomical values, including the obliquity, are derived.[^o34]

Until 1983 the obliquity for any date was calculated from [work of Newcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcomb's_Tables_of_the_Sun), who analyzed positions of the planets until about 1895:

$$\varepsilon = 23^{\circ}27'08.26'' - 46.845'' T - 0.0059'' T^{2} + 0.00181'' T^{3}$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the obliquity and $T$ is [tropical centuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_year) from [B1900.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29) to the date in question.[^o30]

From 1984, the [Jet Propulsion Laboratory's DE series](wikipedia://en/13028634) of computer-generated ephemerides took over as the fundamental ephemeris of the *Astronomical Almanac*. Obliquity based on DE200, which analyzed observations from 1911 to 1979, was calculated:

$$\varepsilon = 23^{\circ}26'21.45'' - 46.815'' T - 0.0006'' T^{2} + 0.00181'' T^{3}$$

where hereafter $T$ is [Julian centuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_year_%28astronomy%29) from [J2000.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch_%28astronomy%29).[^o31]

JPL's fundamental ephemerides have been continually updated. The *Astronomical Almanac* for 2010 specifies:[^o32]

$$\varepsilon = 23^{\circ}26'21.406'' - 46.836769'' T - 0.0001831'' T^{2} + 0.00200340'' T^{3} - 0.576\times10^{-6}{}'' T^{4} - 4.34\times10^{-8}{}'' T^{5}$$

These expressions for the obliquity are intended for high precision over a relatively short time span, perhaps several centuries.[^o33] J. Laskar computed an expression to order $T^{10}$ good to $0.04''$/1000 years over 10,000 years.[^o34]

All of these expressions are for the *mean* obliquity, that is, without the nutation of the equator included. The *true* or instantaneous obliquity includes the nutation.[^o35]

### Precession {#Precession}

The ecliptic itself is not fixed. The gravitational perturbations of the other bodies of the Solar System cause a much smaller motion of the plane of Earth's orbit, and hence of the ecliptic, known as *planetary precession*.

Likewise, the orientation of [Earth's axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_rotation) and equator are not fixed in space, but rotate about the [poles of the ecliptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poles_of_the_ecliptic) with a period of about 26,000 years, a process known as *lunisolar [precession](wikipedia://en/24714)* or [axial precession](wikipedia://en/72576), as it is due mostly to the gravitational effect of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) and Sun on [Earth's equatorial bulge](wikipedia://en/233668).

The combined action of these two motions is called *general precession*, and changes the position of the equinoxes by about 50 [arc seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_of_arc) (about 0.014°) per year.[^o37]

### Nutation {#Nutation}

Once again, this is a simplification. Periodic motions of the Moon and apparent periodic motions of the Sun (actually of Earth in its orbit) cause short-term small-amplitude periodic oscillations of Earth's axis, and hence the celestial equator, known as [*nutation*](wikipedia://en/52297430).[^o39] This adds a periodic component to the position of the equinoxes; the positions of the celestial equator and (March) equinox with fully updated precession and nutation are called the *true equator and equinox*; the positions without nutation are the *mean equator and equinox*.[^o40]

## Eclipses {#Eclipses}

*Image: Eclipse vs new or full moons, annotated.svg.* As the Earth revolves around the Sun, approximate [axial parallelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_parallelism) of the Moon's orbital plane ([tilted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_inclination) five degrees to the ecliptic) results in the revolution of the [lunar nodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_nodes) relative to the Earth. This causes an [eclipse season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse_season) approximately every six months, in which a [solar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse) can occur at the [new moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_moon) phase and a [lunar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_eclipse) can occur at the [full moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_moon) phase.

Because the [orbit of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon) is inclined only about 5.145° to the ecliptic and the Sun is always very near the ecliptic, [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse) always occur on or near it. Because of the inclination of the Moon's orbit, eclipses do not occur at every [conjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_%28astronomy_and_astrology%29) and [opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_%28planets%29) of the Sun and Moon, but only when the Moon is near an [ascending or descending node](wikipedia://en/978717) at the same time it is at conjunction ([new](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_moon)) or opposition ([full](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_moon)). The ecliptic is so named because the ancients noted that eclipses only occur when the Moon is crossing it.[^o49]

## Astrology {#Astrology}

The ecliptic forms the center of the [zodiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiac), a celestial belt about 20° wide in latitude through which the Sun, Moon, and planets always appear to move.[^o56] Traditionally, this region is divided into 12 [signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrological_sign) of 30° longitude, each of which approximates the Sun's motion in one month.[^o57] In ancient times, the signs corresponded roughly to 12 of the constellations that straddle the ecliptic.[^o58] These signs are sometimes still used in modern terminology. The "[First Point of Aries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Point_of_Aries)" was named when the [March equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/March_equinox) Sun was actually in the constellation [Aries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aries_%28constellation%29); it has since moved into [Pisces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisces_%28constellation%29) because of [precession of the equinoxes](wikipedia://en/72576).[^o59]
