# Angle

*Image: Angle.svg.* A green angle formed by two red [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) on the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Angle"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **angle** is formed by two [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) that meet at a point.[^o2] Each line is called a *[side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side_%28plane_geometry%29)* of the angle, and the point they share is called the *[vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417)* of the angle.[^o3][^o4] The term *angle* is used to denote both geometric figures and their [size or magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301) as associated [quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity). **Angular measure** or **measure of angle** are sometimes used to distinguish between the measure of the quantity and figure itself. The measurement of angles is intrinsically linked with circles and rotation, and this is often visualized or defined using the [arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) centered at the vertex and lying between the sides.

:::

## Fundamentals {#Fundamentals}

There is no universally agreed definition of an angle.[^o6] Angles can be conceived of and used in a variety of ways and while valid definitions may be given for specific contexts, it is difficult to give a single formal definition that is completely satisfactory in capturing all aspects of the general concept of *angle*.[^o7]

One standard definition is that an angle is a *figure* consisting of two rays which lie in a plane and share a common endpoint. Alternatively, given such a figure, an angle might be defined as: the *opening* between the rays; the *area of the plane* that lies between the rays; or the *amount of rotation* about the vertex of one ray to the other.

More generally, angles are also formed wherever two [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) come together, such as at the corners of triangles and other polygons,[^o3] or at the intersection of two planes or [curves](wikipedia://en/89246), in which case the rays lying [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to each curve at the point of intersection define the angle.[^n1]

It is common to consider that the sides of the angle divide the plane into two regions called the *interior of the angle* and the *exterior of the angle*. The interior of the angle is also referred to as an **angular sector**.[^o9][^o10]

### Notation and measurement {#Notation_and_measurement}

*Image: Angle diagram.svg.* $\angle \text{BAC}$ is formed by rays $\vec{\text{AB}}$ and $\vec{\text{AC}}$. $\theta$ is the conventional measure of $\angle \text{BAC}$ and $\beta$ is an alternative measure.

An angle symbol ($\angle$ or $\widehat{ \quad }$, read as "angle") together with one or three defining points is used to identify angles in geometric figures. For example, the angle with vertex A formed by the [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) $\vec{\text{AB}}$ and $\vec{\text{AC}}$ is denoted as $\angle \text{A}$ (using the vertex alone) or $\angle \text{BAC}$ (with the vertex always named in the middle). The size or measure of the angle is denoted $m\angle \text{A}$ or $m\angle \text{BAC}$.

In geometric figures and [mathematical expressions](wikipedia://en/609125), it is also common to use [Greek letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_letter) (*α*, *β*, *γ*, *θ*, *φ*, ...) or lower case Roman letters (*a*, *b*, *c*, ...) as [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) to represent the size of an angle.[^n2] Angular measure is commonly a [scalar quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_%28physics%29),[^o13] although in physics and some fields of mathematics, [signed](wikipedia://en/7951270) angles are used by convention to indicate a direction of rotation: positive for anti-clockwise; negative for clockwise.[^o14]

### Units of measurement {#Units_of_measurement}

Angles are measured in various units, the most common being the [degree](wikipedia://en/1195294) (denoted by the symbol **°**), [radian](wikipedia://en/26003) (denoted by the symbol **rad**) and [turn](wikipedia://en/855329). These units differ in the way they divide up a *full angle*, an angle where one ray, initially congruent to the other, performs a compete rotation about the vertex to return back to its starting [position](wikipedia://en/1345771).[^o16]

Degrees and turns are defined directly with reference to a full angle, which measures 1 turn or 360°.[^o17] A measure in turns gives an angle's size as a proportion of a full angle and a degree can be considered as a subdivision of a turn. Radians are not defined directly in relation to a full angle (see *\#Measuring angles*), but in such a way that its measure is 2$\pi$ rad, approximately 6.28 rad.[^o18]

Historically the degree unit was chosen such as the straight angle or half the full angle was attributed the value of 180.

### Addition and subtraction {#Addition_and_subtraction}

*Image: Angle addition.svg.* The angle addition postulate defines addition and subtraction of angles: *θ* + *α* = *φ*; *φ* − *α* = *θ*.

The **angle addition postulate** states that if D is a point lying in the interior of $\angle \text{BAC}$ then:[^o20]

$$m\angle \text{BAC} = m\angle \text{BAD} + m\angle \text{DAC}.$$

This relationship *defines* what it means to add any two angles: their vertices are placed together while sharing a side to create a new larger angle. The measure of the new larger angle is the sum of the measures of the two angles. Subtraction follows from rearrangement of the formula.[^o20]

## Types {#Types}

### Common angles {#Common_angles}

*Image: Angle types.svg.* Common types of angles

- An angle equal to 0° or not turned is called a *zero angle*.[^n3]
- An angle smaller than a right angle (less than 90°) is called an *acute angle*.[^n4]
- An angle equal to $\tfrac{1}{4}$ [turn](wikipedia://en/855329) (90° or $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ rad) is called a *[right angle](wikipedia://en/76956)*. Two lines that form a right angle are said to be *[normal](wikipedia://en/173224)*, *[orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221)*, or *[perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944)*.[^n5]
- An angle larger than a right angle and smaller than a straight angle (between 90° and 180°) is called an *obtuse angle*[^n4] ("obtuse" meaning "blunt").
- An angle equal to $\tfrac{1}{2}$ turn (180° or $\pi$ rad) is called a *straight angle*.[^n3]
- An angle larger than a straight angle but less than 1 turn (between 180° and 360°) is called a *reflex angle*.
- An angle equal to 1 turn (360° or 2$\pi$ rad) is called a *full angle*, *complete angle*, *round angle* or *perigon*.
- An angle that is not a multiple of a right angle is called an *oblique angle*.

::: {#o29 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Adjacent angle"}

### Adjacent and vertical angles {#Adjacent_and_vertical_angles}

***Adjacent angles*** (abbreviated *adj. ∠s*), are angles that share a common vertex and edge but do not share any interior points. In other words, they are angles side by side or adjacent, sharing an "arm".

***Vertical angles*** are formed when two straight lines intersect at a point producing four angles. A pair of angles opposite each other are called *vertical angles*, *opposite angles* or *vertically opposite angles* (abbreviated *vert. opp. ∠s*),[^o30] where "vertical" refers to the sharing of a vertex, rather than an up-down orientation. A theorem states that vertical angles are always congruent or equal to each other.[^n6] A [*transversal*](wikipedia://en/13295107) is a line that intersects a pair of (often parallel) lines and is associated with *exterior angles*, *interior angles*, *alternate exterior angles*, *alternate interior angles*, *corresponding angles*, and *consecutive interior angles*.[^n7]

:::

::: {#o34 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Angular complement"}

### Combining angle pairs {#Combining_angle_pairs}

When summing two angles (either adjacent or separated in space), three special cases are named *complementary*, *supplementary*, and *explementary* angles.

***Complementary angles*** are angle pairs whose measures sum to a right angle ($\tfrac{1}{4}$ turn, 90°, or $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ rad). If the two complementary angles are adjacent, their non-shared sides form a right angle. In a [right-angle triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) the two acute angles are complementary as the sum of the internal angles of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is 180°. The difference between an angle and a right angle is termed the *complement* of the angle.[^n1]

***Supplementary angles*** sum to a straight angle ($\tfrac{1}{2}$ turn, 180°, or $\pi$ rad). If the two supplementary angles are [adjacent](wikipedia://en/1196#adjacent), their non-shared sides form a straight angle or [straight line](wikipedia://en/946975) and are called a *linear pair of angles*.[^n8] The difference between an angle and a straight angle is termed the *supplement* of the angle.[^o37]

***Explementary angles*** or ***conjugate angles*** sum to a full angle (1 turn, 360°, or 2$\pi$ radians).[^o38] The difference between an angle and a full angle is termed the *explement* or *conjugate* of the angle.[^o39][^o40]

Examples of non-adjacent supplementary angles include the consecutive angles of a [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) and opposite angles of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131). For a circle with center O, and [tangent lines](wikipedia://en/19491492) from an exterior point P touching the circle at points T and Q, the resulting angles ∠TPQ and ∠TOQ are supplementary.

:::

### Polygon-related angles {#Polygon-related_angles}

*Image: ExternalAngles.svg.* Internal and external angles

- An angle that is part of a [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530) is called an *[interior angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_angle)* if it lies on the inside of that simple polygon. A simple [concave polygon](wikipedia://en/605156) has at least one interior angle, that is, a reflex angle. In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), the measures of the interior angles of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) add up to $\pi$ radians, 180°, or $\tfrac{1}{2}$ turn; the measures of the interior angles of a simple [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) add up to 2$\pi$ radians, 360°, or 1 turn. In general, the measures of the interior angles of a simple convex [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) with *n* sides add up to (*n* − 2)$\pi$ radians, or (*n* − 2)180 degrees, (*n* − 2)2 right angles, or (*n* − 2)$\tfrac{1}{2}$ turn.
- The supplement of an interior angle is called an *[exterior angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_angle)*; that is, an interior angle and an exterior angle form a linear pair of angles. There are two exterior angles at each vertex of the polygon, each determined by extending one of the two sides of the polygon that meet at the vertex; these two angles are vertical and hence are equal. An exterior angle measures the amount of rotation one must make at a vertex to trace the polygon.[^n9] If the corresponding interior angle is a reflex angle, the exterior angle should be considered [negative](wikipedia://en/154616). Even in a non-simple polygon, it may be possible to define the exterior angle. Still, one will have to pick an [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28space%29) of the [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) (or [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970)) to decide the sign of the exterior angle measure. In Euclidean geometry, the sum of the exterior angles of a simple convex polygon, if only one of the two exterior angles is assumed at each vertex, will be one full turn (360°). The exterior angle here could be called a *supplementary exterior angle*. Exterior angles are commonly used in [Logo Turtle programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logo_%28programming_language%29) when drawing regular polygons.
- In a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654), the [bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547) of two exterior angles and the bisector of the other interior angle are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157) (meet at a single point).[^o43]p. 149
- In a triangle, three intersection points, each of an external angle bisector with the opposite [extended side](wikipedia://en/43958459), are [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581).[^o43]p. 149
- In a triangle, three intersection points, two between an interior angle bisector and the opposite side, and the third between the other exterior angle bisector and the opposite side extended are collinear.[^o43]p. 149
- Some authors use the name *exterior angle* of a simple polygon to mean the *explement exterior angle* (*not* supplement!) of the interior angle.[^o44] This conflicts with the above usage.

### Plane-related angles {#Plane-related_angles}

- The angle between two [planes](wikipedia://en/19469852) (such as two adjacent faces of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470)) is called a *[dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046)*.[^n1] It may be defined as the acute angle between two lines [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) to the planes.
- The angle between a plane and an intersecting straight line is complementary to the angle between the intersecting line and the [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) to the plane.

## Measuring angles {#Measuring_angles}

Angle measurement encompasses both direct physical measurement using a measuring instrument such as a [protractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protractor), as well as the theoretical calculation of angle size from other known quantities. While the measurement of angles is intrinsically linked with rotation and circles, there are various perspectives as to exactly *what* is being measured, including amongst others: the amount of rotation about the vertex of one ray to the other;[^o48] the amount of opening between the rays;[^o49] or the length of the arc that subtends the angle at the centre of a unit circle.[^o54]

The measurement of angles is inherently different from the measurement of other physical quantities such as length.[^o50] Angles of special significance (such as the right angle) inform the systems and units of angular measurement, which is not the case for length where the units of measurement (metres, feet) are arbitrary.

Broadly there are two approaches to measuring angles: relative to a reference angle (such as a right angle); and circular measurement.

### Reference angle {#Reference_angle}

A chosen reference angle (right angle, straight angle or full angle) can be divided into equal parts, and the size of one part used as a unit for measurement of other angles.

In the most common method of practical angular measurement a right angle is divided into 90 equal parts called [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294), while in the rarely used *centesimal* system, a right angle is divided into 100 equal parts called [gradians](wikipedia://en/201629).[^o52][^o56]

### Circular measurement {#Circular_measurement}

*Image: Basic angle in circle.svg.* The angle size $\theta$ can be measured as *s*/*r* radians or *s*/*C* turns

With circular measurement an angle is placed within a circle of any size, with the vertex at the circle's centre and the sides intersecting the perimeter.

An [arc](wikipedia://en/5578920) with length s is formed as the perimeter between the two points of intersection, which is said to [subtend](wikipedia://en/1999527) the angle. The length *s* can be used to measure the angle's size *θ*, however as *s* is dependent on the size of the circle chosen, the measure must be scaled. This can be done by taking the ratio of *s* to either the radius *r* or circumference *C* of the circle.

The ratio of the length s by the radius r is the number of [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) in the angle,[^o54] while the ratio of length s by the circumference C is the number of [turns](wikipedia://en/855329):

$$\theta = \frac{s}{r} \, \mathrm{rad} = \frac{s}{C} \, \mathrm{turn} = \frac{s}{2\pi r} \, \mathrm{turn}$$

*Image: Radian measurement.svg.* The radian measure of $\theta$$\theta = \frac{s}{r} = \frac{s}{s_N}$

The value of $\theta$ thus defined is independent of the size of the circle: if the length of the radius is changed, then both the circumference and the arc length change in the same proportion, so the ratios $\tfrac{s}{r}$ and $\tfrac{s}{C}$ are unaltered.

The ratio $\tfrac{s}{r}$ is called the "radian measure"[^o20] or "circular measure"[^o55][^o56][^o57] of an angle, but is also used to define a [unit of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement) called a radian, which is defined as an angle for which the ratio $\tfrac{s}{r}$ = 1.[^o55] Thus, the measurement of an angle given by $\tfrac{s}{r}$ can be thought of in two ways: firstly as a measure in terms of the angle's own proportions (ratio of arc length to radius), or secondly as the quantity of units in the angle (ratio of arc length of measured angle to arc length of unit angle).[^o58][^o56]

### Dimensional analysis {#Dimensional_analysis}

In mathematics and the [International System of Quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Quantities), an angle is defined as a [dimensionless quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantity),[^o69] and in particular, the [radian](wikipedia://en/26003) is defined as dimensionless in the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units). This convention prevents angles providing information for [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267). For example, when one measures an angle in radians by dividing the arc length by the radius, one is essentially dividing a length by another length, and the units of length cancel each other out. Therefore the result—the angle—doesn't have a physical "dimension" like meters or seconds.[^o71] This holds true with all angle units, such as radians, degrees, or turns—they all represent a pure number quantifying how much something has turned.[^o72] This is why, in many equations, angle units seem to "disappear" during calculations, which feels inconsistent and can lead to mixing up angle units.[^o73][^o74]

This has led to significant discussion among scientists and educators. Some scientists have suggested treating the angle as having its own fundamental dimension, similar to length or time.[^o75] This would mean that angle units like radians would always be explicitly present in calculations, facilitating dimensional analysis. However, this approach would also require changing many well-known mathematical and physics formulas, making them longer and perhaps a bit less familiar.[^o76] For now, the established practice is to write angle units where appropriate but consider them dimensionless, understanding that these units are important but behave differently from meters or kilograms.[^o77]

### Signed angles {#Signed_angles}

*Image: Angles on the unit circle.svg.* Measuring from the [x-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-axis), angles on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) count as positive in the [counterclockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterclockwise) direction, and negative in the [clockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clockwise) direction.

An angle denoted as $\angle\operatorname{BAC}$ might refer to any of four angles: the clockwise angle from B to C about A, the anticlockwise angle from B to C about A, the clockwise angle from C to B about A, or the anticlockwise angle from C to B about A, It is therefore frequently helpful to impose a convention that allows positive and negative angular values to represent [orientations](wikipedia://en/2544098) and/or [rotations](wikipedia://en/410009) in opposite directions or "sense" relative to some reference.

In a two-dimensional [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), an angle is typically defined by its two sides, with its vertex at the origin. The *initial side* is on the positive [x-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-axis), while the other side or *terminal side* is defined by the measure from the initial side in radians, degrees, or turns, with *positive angles* representing rotations toward the positive [y-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-axis) and *negative angles* representing rotations toward the negative *y*-axis. When Cartesian coordinates are represented by *standard position*, defined by the *x*-axis rightward and the *y*-axis upward, positive rotations are [anticlockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticlockwise), and negative cycles are [clockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clockwise).

In many contexts, an angle of −*θ* is effectively equivalent to an angle of "one full turn minus *θ*". For example, an orientation represented as −45° is effectively equal to an orientation defined as 360° − 45° or 315°. Although the final position is the same, a physical rotation (movement) of −45° is not the same as a rotation of 315° (for example, the rotation of a person holding a broom resting on a dusty floor would leave visually different traces of swept regions on the floor).

In three-dimensional geometry, "clockwise" and "anticlockwise" have no absolute meaning, so the direction of positive and negative angles must be defined in terms of an [orientation](wikipedia://en/187446), which is typically determined by a [normal vector](wikipedia://en/173224) passing through the angle's vertex and perpendicular to the plane in which the rays of the angle lie.

In [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation), [bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearing_%28navigation%29) or [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) are measured relative to north. By convention, viewed from above, bearing angles are positive clockwise, so a bearing of 45° corresponds to a north-east orientation. Negative bearings are not used in navigation, so a north-west orientation corresponds to a bearing of 315°.

:::

### Equivalent angles {#Equivalent_angles}

- Angles that have the same measure (i.e., the same magnitude) are said to be *equal* or *[congruent](wikipedia://en/39330)*. An angle is defined by its measure and is not dependent upon the lengths of the sides of the angle (e.g., all *right angles* are equal in measure).
- Two angles that share terminal sides, but differ in size by an integer multiple of a turn, are called *coterminal angles*.
- The *reference angle* (sometimes called *related angle*) for any angle *θ* in standard position is the positive acute angle between the terminal side of *θ* and the x-axis (positive or negative).[^o81] Procedurally, the magnitude of the reference angle for a given angle may determined by taking the angle's magnitude [modulo](wikipedia://en/1352428) $\tfrac{1}{2}$ turn, 180°, or $\pi$ radians, then stopping if the angle is acute, otherwise taking the supplementary angle, 180° minus the reduced magnitude. For example, an angle of 30 degrees is already a reference angle, and an angle of 150 degrees also has a reference angle of 30 degrees (180° − 150°). Angles of 210° and 510° correspond to a reference angle of 30 degrees as well (210° mod 180° = 30°, 510° mod 180° = 150° whose supplementary angle is 30°).

### Related quantities {#Related_quantities}

For an angular unit, it is definitional that the [angle addition postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_addition_postulate) holds, however some measurements or quantities related to angles are in use that do not satisfy this postulate:

- The *[slope](wikipedia://en/29368)* or *gradient* is equal to the [tangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_%28trigonometric_function%29) of the angle and is often expressed as a percentage ("rise" over "run"). For very small values (less than 5%), the slope of a line is approximately the measure in radians of its angle with the horizontal direction. An [elevation grade](wikipedia://en/259162) is a slope used to indicate the steepness of roads, paths and railway lines.
- The *[spread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_%28rational_trigonometry%29)* between two lines is defined in [rational geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_geometry) as the square of the sine of the angle between the lines. As the sine of an angle and the sine of its supplementary angle are the same, any angle of rotation that maps one of the lines into the other leads to the same value for the spread between the lines.
- Although done rarely, one can report the direct results of [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367), such as the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) of the angle.

## Angles between curves {#Angles_between_curves}

*Image: Curve angles.svg.* The angle between the two curves at P is defined as the angle between the tangents A and B at P.

The angle between a line and a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) (mixed angle) or between two intersecting curves (curvilinear angle) is defined to be the angle between the [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482) at the point of intersection. Various names (now rarely, if ever, used) have been given to particular cases:—*amphicyrtic* (Gr. ἀμφί, on both sides, κυρτός, convex) or *cissoidal* (Gr. κισσός, ivy), biconvex; *xystroidal* or *sistroidal* (Gr. ξυστρίς, a tool for scraping), concavo-convex; *amphicoelic* (Gr. κοίλη, a hollow) or *angulus lunularis*, biconcave.[^o85]

## Bisecting and trisecting angles {#Bisecting_and_trisecting_angles}

The [ancient Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) knew how to bisect an angle (divide it into two angles of equal measure) using only a [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge) but could only trisect certain angles. In 1837, [Pierre Wantzel](wikipedia://en/2610953) showed that this construction could not be performed for most angles.

## Dot product and generalisations {#Dot_product_and_generalisations}

In the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the angle *θ* between two [Euclidean vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) **u** and **v** is related to their [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) and their lengths by the formula

$$\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \cos(\theta) \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| \left\| \mathbf{v} \right\| .$$

This formula supplies an easy method to find the angle between two planes (or curved surfaces) from their [normal vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector) and between [skew lines](wikipedia://en/1975821) from their vector equations.

### Inner product {#Inner_product}

To define angles in an abstract real [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856), we replace the Euclidean dot product ( **·** ) by the inner product $\langle \cdot , \cdot \rangle$, i.e.

$$\langle \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v} \rangle = \cos(\theta)\ \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| \left\| \mathbf{v} \right\| .$$

In a complex [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856), the expression for the cosine above may give non-real values, so it is replaced with

$$\operatorname{Re} \left( \langle \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v} \rangle \right) = \cos(\theta) \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| \left\| \mathbf{v} \right\| .$$

or, more commonly, using the absolute value, with

$$\left| \langle \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v} \rangle \right| = \left| \cos(\theta) \right| \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| \left\| \mathbf{v} \right\| .$$

The latter definition ignores the direction of the vectors. It thus describes the angle between one-dimensional subspaces $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{u})$ and $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v})$ spanned by the vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ correspondingly.

### Angles between subspaces {#Angles_between_subspaces}

The definition of the angle between one-dimensional subspaces $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{u})$ and $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v})$ given by

$$\left| \langle \mathbf{u} , \mathbf{v} \rangle \right| = \left| \cos(\theta) \right| \left\| \mathbf{u} \right\| \left\| \mathbf{v} \right\|$$

in a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) can be extended to subspaces of finite number of dimensions. Given two subspaces $\mathcal{U}$, $\mathcal{W}$ with $\dim ( \mathcal{U}) := k \leq \dim ( \mathcal{W}) := l$, this leads to a definition of $k$ angles called canonical or [principal angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_angles) between subspaces.

### Angles in Riemannian geometry {#Angles_in_Riemannian_geometry}

In [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) is used to define the angle between two [tangents](wikipedia://en/31482). Where *U* and *V* are tangent vectors and *g*<sub>*ij*</sub> are the components of the metric tensor *G*,

$$\cos \theta = \frac{g_{ij} U^i V^j}{\sqrt{ \left| g_{ij} U^i U^j \right| \left| g_{ij} V^i V^j \right|}}.$$

### Hyperbolic angle {#Hyperbolic_angle}

*Image: Hyperbolic\_rotation.gif.* A constant hyperbolic angle (0.2), under [hyperbolic rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_rotation), corresponds to a variable circular angle.

A [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) is an [argument](wikipedia://en/32842052) of a [hyperbolic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) just as the *circular angle* is the argument of a [circular function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_function). The comparison can be visualized as the size of the openings of a [hyperbolic sector](wikipedia://en/1139926) and a [circular sector](wikipedia://en/1967249) since the [areas](wikipedia://en/1209) of these sectors correspond to the angle magnitudes in each case.[^o92] Unlike the circular angle, the hyperbolic angle is unbounded. When the circular and hyperbolic functions are viewed as [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) in their angle argument, the circular ones are just [alternating series](wikipedia://en/494308) forms of the hyperbolic functions. This comparison of the two series corresponding to functions of angles was described by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in *[Introduction to the Analysis of the Infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduction_to_the_Analysis_of_the_Infinite)* (1748).

## History and etymology {#History_and_etymology}

The word *angle* comes from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) word angulus, meaning "corner". [Cognate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognate) words include the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) ἀγκύλος (ankylοs) meaning "crooked, curved" and the [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language) word "[ankle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankle)". Both are connected with the [Proto-Indo-European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language) root *\*ank-*, meaning "to bend" or "bow".[^o94]

Philosophers have debated the nature of angles for millennia, with some arguing that angles are a measure (quantity), and others saying they are a kind of shape defined by the lines that bound it (qualitative relation), and still others saying an angle is both.[^o95] Pedagogically, the accepted answer is that angles are defined as figures, and the measure of an angle is defined as the number of congruent non-overlapping copies of a unit angle necessary to cover the angle and its interior. Angles are said to be equal in measure and [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) or [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) in shape.[^o96]

[Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) defines a plane angle as the inclination to each other, in a plane, of two lines that meet each other and do not lie straight with respect to each other. According to the Neoplatonic metaphysician [Proclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus), an angle must be either a quality, a quantity, or a relationship. The first concept, angle as quality, was used by [Eudemus of Rhodes](wikipedia://en/3454204), who regarded an angle as a deviation from a [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line); the second, angle as quantity, by [Carpus of Antioch](wikipedia://en/16302250), who regarded it as the interval or space between the intersecting lines; Euclid adopted the third: angle as a relationship.[^o97]

::: {#o99 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Vertical angle theorem"}

### Vertical angle theorem {#Vertical_angle_theorem}

The equality of vertically opposite angles is called the *vertical angle theorem*. [Eudemus of Rhodes](wikipedia://en/3454204) attributed the proof to [Thales of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales).[^o100][^n6] The proposition showed that since both of a pair of vertical angles are supplementary to both of the adjacent angles, the vertical angles are equal in measure. According to a historical note,[^n6] when Thales visited Egypt, he observed that whenever the Egyptians drew two intersecting lines, they would measure the vertical angles to make sure that they were equal. Thales concluded that one could prove that all vertical angles are equal if one accepted some general notions such as:

- All straight angles are equal.
- Equals added to equals are equal.
- Equals subtracted from equals are equal.

When two adjacent angles form a straight line, they are supplementary. Therefore, if we assume that the measure of angle *A* equals *x*, the measure of angle *C* would be 180° − *x*. Similarly, the measure of angle *D* would be 180° − *x*. Both angle *C* and angle *D* have measures equal to 180° − *x* and are congruent. Since angle *B* is supplementary to both angles *C* and *D*, either of these angle measures may be used to determine the measure of Angle *B*. Using the measure of either angle *C* or angle *D*, we find the measure of angle *B* to be 180° − (180° − *x*) = *x*. Therefore, both angle *A* and angle *B* have measures equal to *x* and are equal in measure.

:::

## Angles in geography and astronomy {#Angles_in_geography_and_astronomy}

In [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography), the location of any point on the Earth can be identified using a *[geographic coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_system)*. This system specifies the [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) and [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) of any location in terms of angles subtended at the center of the Earth, using the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) and (usually) the [Greenwich meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwich_meridian) as references.

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), a given point on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) (that is, the apparent position of an astronomical object) can be identified using any of several *[astronomical coordinate systems](wikipedia://en/48381)*, where the references vary according to the particular system. Astronomers measure the *[angular separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_separation)* of two [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) by imagining two lines through the center of the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), each intersecting one of the stars. The angle between those lines and the angular separation between the two stars can be measured.

In both geography and astronomy, a sighting direction can be specified in terms of a [vertical angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_angle) such as [altitude angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude_angle) or [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation_angle) with respect to the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) as well as the [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) with respect to [north](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North).

Astronomers also measure objects' *apparent size* as an [angular diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_diameter). For example, the [full moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_moon) has an angular diameter of approximately 0.5°, or 30 arcminutes, when viewed from Earth. One could say, "The Moon's diameter subtends an angle of half a degree." The [small-angle formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small-angle_formula) can convert such an angular measurement into a distance/size ratio.

Other astronomical approximations include:

- \0.5° is the approximate angular diameter of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) and of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) as viewed from Earth.
- 1° is the approximate angular width of the [little finger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_finger) at arm's length.
- 10° is the approximate angular width of a closed fist at arm's length.
- 20° is the approximate angular width of a handspan at arm's length.

These measurements depend on the individual subject, and the above should be treated as rough [rule of thumb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thumb) approximations only.

In astronomy, [right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073) is usually measured in angular units that are expressed in terms of time based on a 24-hour day.[^o104]
