# Disjunction introduction

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Disjunction introduction"}

**Disjunction introduction.**

If $P$ is true, then $P$ or $Q$ must be true.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Disjunction introduction"}

**Disjunction introduction** or **addition** (also called **or introduction**)[^o3][^o4][^o5] is a [rule of inference](wikipedia://en/252311) of [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) and almost every other [deduction system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deduction_system). The rule makes it possible to introduce [disjunctions](wikipedia://en/7962) to [logical proofs](wikipedia://en/1197184). It is the [inference](wikipedia://en/317465) that if *P* is true, then *P or Q* must be true.

:::

An example in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language):

Socrates is a man.

Therefore, Socrates is a man or pigs are flying in formation over the English Channel.

The rule can be expressed as:

$$\frac{P}{\therefore P \lor Q}$$

where the rule is that whenever instances of "$P$" appear on lines of a proof, "$P \lor Q$" can be placed on a subsequent line.

More generally it's also a simple [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [argument form](wikipedia://en/3331706), this means that if the premise is true, then the conclusion is also true as any rule of inference should be, and an [immediate inference](wikipedia://en/25379366), as it has a single proposition in its premises.

Disjunction introduction is not a rule in some [paraconsistent logics](wikipedia://en/421085) because in combination with other rules of logic, it leads to [explosion](wikipedia://en/591394) (i.e. everything becomes provable) and paraconsistent logic tries to avoid explosion and to be able to reason with contradictions. One of the solutions is to introduce disjunction with over rules. See Paraconsistent logic.

## Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

The *disjunction introduction* rule may be written in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation:

$$P \vdash (P \lor Q)$$

where $\vdash$ is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) symbol meaning that $P \lor Q$ is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) of $P$ in some [logical system](wikipedia://en/396102);

and expressed as a truth-functional [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) or [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of propositional logic:

$$P \to (P \lor Q)$$

where $P$ and $Q$ are propositions expressed in some [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102).
