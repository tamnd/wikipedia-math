# Analytic geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Analytic geometry"}

branches In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **analytic geometry**, also known as **coordinate geometry** or **Cartesian geometry**, is the study of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) using a [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931). This contrasts with [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484).

:::

Analytic geometry is used in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), and also in [aviation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation), [rocketry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_engineering), [space science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_science), [spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaceflight), [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), and the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science). It is the foundation of most modern fields of geometry, including [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1997), [differential](wikipedia://en/8625), [discrete](wikipedia://en/386468) and [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927).

Usually the Cartesian coordinate system is applied to manipulate equations for planes, straight lines, and circles, often in two and sometimes three dimensions. Geometrically, one studies the Euclidean plane (two dimensions) and Euclidean space. As taught in school books, analytic geometry can be explained more simply: it is concerned with defining and representing geometric shapes in a numerical way and extracting numerical information from shapes' numerical definitions and representations. That the algebra of the real numbers can be employed to yield results about the linear continuum of geometry relies on the [Cantor–Dedekind axiom](wikipedia://en/1595873).

## History {#History}

### Ancient Greece {#Ancient_Greece}

The [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) mathematician [Menaechmus](wikipedia://en/1944319) solved problems and proved theorems by using a method that had a strong resemblance to the use of coordinates and it has sometimes been maintained that he had introduced analytic geometry.[^o5]

[Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242), in *[On Determinate Section](wikipedia://en/257242#De_Sectione_Determinata)*, dealt with problems in a manner that may be called an analytic geometry of one dimension; with the question of finding points on a line that were in a ratio to the others.[^o6] Apollonius in the *Conics* further developed a method that is so similar to analytic geometry that his work is sometimes thought to have anticipated the work of [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes) by some 1800 years. His application of reference lines, a diameter and a tangent is essentially no different from our modern use of a coordinate frame, where the distances measured along the diameter from the point of tangency are the abscissas, and the segments parallel to the tangent and intercepted between the axis and the curve are the ordinates. He further developed relations between the abscissas and the corresponding ordinates that are equivalent to rhetorical equations (expressed in words) of curves. However, although Apollonius came close to developing analytic geometry, he did not manage to do so since he did not take into account negative magnitudes and in every case the coordinate system was superimposed upon a given curve *a posteriori* instead of *a priori*. That is, equations were determined by curves, but curves were not determined by equations. Coordinates, variables, and equations were subsidiary notions applied to a specific geometric situation.[^o7]

### Persia {#Persia}

The 11th-century Persian mathematician [Omar Khayyam](wikipedia://en/92550) saw a strong relationship between geometry and algebra and was moving in the right direction when he helped close the gap between numerical and [geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/12939)[^o10] with his geometric solution of the general [cubic equations](wikipedia://en/180787),[^o9] but the decisive step came later with Descartes.[^o10] Omar Khayyam is credited with identifying the foundations of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), and his book *Treatise on Demonstrations of Problems of Algebra* (1070), which laid down the principles of analytic geometry, is part of the body of Persian mathematics that was eventually transmitted to Europe.[^o11] Because of his thoroughgoing geometrical approach to algebraic equations, Khayyam can be considered a precursor to Descartes in the invention of analytic geometry.[^o12]

### Western Europe {#Western_Europe}

Analytic geometry was independently invented by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) and [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966),[^o14][^o15] although Descartes is sometimes given sole credit.[^o16][^o17] *Cartesian geometry*, the alternative term used for analytic geometry, is named after Descartes.

Descartes made significant progress with the methods in an essay titled *[La Géométrie](wikipedia://en/239981) (Geometry)*, one of the three accompanying essays (appendices) published in 1637 together with his *Discourse on the Method for Rightly Directing One's Reason and Searching for Truth in the Sciences*, commonly referred to as *[Discourse on Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_on_Method)*. *La Geometrie*, written in his native [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) tongue, and its philosophical principles, provided a foundation for [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) in Europe. Initially the work was not well received, due, in part, to the many gaps in arguments and complicated equations. Only after the translation into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) and the addition of commentary by [van Schooten](wikipedia://en/66858) in 1649 (and further work thereafter) did Descartes's masterpiece receive due recognition.[^o18]

[Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) also pioneered the development of analytic geometry. Although not published in his lifetime, a manuscript form of *Ad locos planos et solidos isagoge* (Introduction to Plane and Solid Loci) was circulating in Paris in 1637, just prior to the publication of Descartes' *Discourse*.[^o19][^o20][^o21] Clearly written and well received, the *Introduction* also laid the groundwork for analytical geometry. The key difference between Fermat's and Descartes' treatments is a matter of viewpoint: Fermat always started with an algebraic equation and then described the geometric curve that satisfied it, whereas Descartes started with geometric curves and produced their equations as one of several properties of the curves.[^o18] As a consequence of this approach, Descartes had to deal with more complicated equations and he had to develop the methods to work with polynomial equations of higher degree. It was [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) who first applied the coordinate method in a systematic study of space curves and surfaces.

## Coordinates {#Coordinates}

*Image: Cartesian-coordinate-system.svg.* Illustration of a Cartesian coordinate plane. Four points are marked and labeled with their coordinates: (2,3) in green, (−3,1) in red, (−1.5,−2.5) in blue, and the origin (0,0) in purple.

In analytic geometry, the [plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) is given a coordinate system, by which every [point](wikipedia://en/593693) has a pair of [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) coordinates. Similarly, [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is given coordinates where every point has three coordinates. The value of the coordinates depends on the choice of the initial point of origin. There are a variety of coordinate systems used, but the most common are the following:[^o23]

### Cartesian coordinates (in a plane or space) {#Cartesian_coordinates_(in_a_plane_or_space)}

The most common coordinate system to use is the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), where each point has an *x*-coordinate representing its horizontal position, and a *y*-coordinate representing its vertical position. These are typically written as an [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362) (*x*, *y*). This system can also be used for three-dimensional geometry, where every point in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is represented by an [ordered triple](wikipedia://en/132729) of coordinates (*x*, *y*, *z*).

### Polar coordinates (in a plane) {#Polar_coordinates_(in_a_plane)}

*Image: Polära\_koordinater.svg.* Three [vectors in the polar coordinate system](wikipedia://en/25120#Vector_calculus) all originating at the origin with separate angles $\theta$ passing through the points $(x_{1},y_{1}), (x_{2},y_{2}), (x_{3},y_{3})$.

In [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates), every point of the plane is represented by its distance *r* from the origin and its [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) *θ*, with *θ* normally measured counterclockwise from the positive *x*-axis. Using this notation, points are typically written as an ordered pair (*r*, *θ*). One may transform back and forth between two-dimensional Cartesian and polar coordinates by using these formulae:[^o26]

$$x = r\, \cos\theta,\, y = r\, \sin\theta; \, r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\, \theta = \arctan(y/x).$$

This system may be generalized to three-dimensional space through the use of [cylindrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates) or [spherical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) coordinates.[^o26]

### Cylindrical coordinates (in a space) {#Cylindrical_coordinates_(in_a_space)}

In [cylindrical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_coordinates), every point of space is represented by its height *z*, its [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) *r* from the *z*-axis and the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) *θ* its projection on the *xy*-plane makes with respect to the horizontal axis.

### Spherical coordinates (in a space) {#Spherical_coordinates_(in_a_space)}

In spherical coordinates, every point in space is represented by its distance *ρ* from the origin, the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) *θ* its projection on the *xy*-plane makes with respect to the horizontal axis, and the angle *φ* that it makes with respect to the *z*-axis. The names of the angles are often reversed in physics.[^o23]

## Equations and curves {#Equations_and_curves}

In analytic geometry, any [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) involving the coordinates specifies a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the plane, namely the [solution set](wikipedia://en/567840) for the equation, or [locus](wikipedia://en/542465). For example, the equation *y* = *x* corresponds to the set of all the points on the plane whose *x*-coordinate and *y*-coordinate are equal. These points form a [line](wikipedia://en/946975), and *y* = *x* is said to be the equation for this line. In general, linear equations involving *x* and *y* specify lines, [quadratic equations](wikipedia://en/25175) specify [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673), and more complicated equations describe more complicated figures.[^o31]

Usually, a single equation corresponds to a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) on the plane. This is not always the case: the trivial equation *x* = *x* specifies the entire plane, and the equation *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = 0 specifies only the single point (0, 0). In three dimensions, a single equation usually gives a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970), and a curve must be specified as the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of two surfaces (see below), or as a system of [parametric equations](wikipedia://en/576108).[^o32] The equation *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = *r*<sup>2</sup> is the equation for any circle centered at the origin (0, 0) with a radius of r.

### Lines and planes {#Lines_and_planes}

Lines in a [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane), or more generally, in [affine coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_coordinates), can be described algebraically by *linear* equations. In two dimensions, the equation for non-vertical lines is often given in the *[slope-intercept form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope-intercept_form)*:

$$y = mx + b$$

where:

- *m* is the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) or [gradient](wikipedia://en/29368) of the line.
- *b* is the [y-intercept](wikipedia://en/464331) of the line.
- *x* is the [independent variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_variable) of the function *y* = *f*(*x*).

In a manner analogous to the way lines in a two-dimensional space are described using a point-slope form for their equations, planes in a three dimensional space have a natural description using a point in the plane and a vector orthogonal to it (the [normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector)) to indicate its "inclination".

Specifically, let $\mathbf{r}_0$ be the position vector of some point $P_0 = (x_0,y_0,z_0)$, and let $\mathbf{n} = (a,b,c)$ be a nonzero vector. The plane determined by this point and vector consists of those points $P$, with position vector $\mathbf{r}$, such that the vector drawn from $P_0$ to $P$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{n}$. Recalling that two vectors are perpendicular if and only if their dot product is zero, it follows that the desired plane can be described as the set of all points $\mathbf{r}$ such that

$$\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0) =0.$$

(The dot here means a [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093), not scalar multiplication.) Expanded this becomes

$$a (x-x_0)+ b(y-y_0)+ c(z-z_0)=0,$$

This is just a [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570):

$$ax + by + cz + d = 0, \text{ where } d = -(ax_0 + by_0 + cz_0).$$

Conversely, it is easily shown that if *a*, *b*, *c* and *d* are constants and *a*, *b*, and *c* are not all zero, then the graph of the equation

$$ax + by + cz + d = 0,$$

This familiar equation for a plane is called the *general form* of the equation of the plane.[^o34]

In three dimensions, lines can *not* be described by a single linear equation, so they are frequently described by [parametric equations](wikipedia://en/576108):

$$x = x_0 + at$$

$$y = y_0 + bt$$

$$z = z_0 + ct$$

where:

- *x*, *y*, and *z* are all functions of the independent variable *t* which ranges over the real numbers.
- (*x*<sub>0</sub>, *y*<sub>0</sub>, *z*<sub>0</sub>) is any point on the line.
- *a*, *b*, and *c* are related to the slope of the line, such that the [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) (*a*, *b*, *c*) is parallel to the line.

### Conic sections {#Conic_sections}

*Image: Hyperbler\_konjugerte.png.* A hyperbola and its [conjugate hyperbola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola)

In the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706), the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of a [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175) in two variables is always a conic section – though it may be degenerate, and all conic sections arise in this way. The equation will be of the form

$$Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 +Dx + Ey + F = 0\text{ with }A, B, C\text{ not all zero.}$$

As scaling all six constants yields the same locus of zeros, one can consider conics as points in the five-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbf{P}^5.$

The conic sections described by this equation can be classified using the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607)[^o36]

$$B^2 - 4AC .$$

If the conic is non-degenerate, then:

- if $B^2 - 4AC < 0$, the equation represents an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277);
  - if $A = C$ and $B = 0$, the equation represents a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), which is a special case of an ellipse;
- if $B^2 - 4AC = 0$, the equation represents a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231);
- if $B^2 - 4AC > 0$, the equation represents a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052);
  - if we also have $A + C = 0$, the equation represents a [rectangular hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052).

### Quadric surfaces {#Quadric_surfaces}

A **quadric**, or **quadric surface**, is a *2*-dimensional [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) in 3-dimensional space defined as the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of [zeros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of a [quadratic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial). In coordinates *x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>,*x*<sub>3</sub>, the general quadric is defined by the [algebraic equation](wikipedia://en/404001)[^o38]

$$\sum_{i,j=1}^{3} x_i Q_{ij} x_j + \sum_{i=1}^{3} P_i x_i + R = 0.$$

Quadric surfaces include [ellipsoids](wikipedia://en/145381) (including the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859)), [paraboloids](wikipedia://en/145845), [hyperboloids](wikipedia://en/145844), [cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29), [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cones), and [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29).

## Distance and angle {#Distance_and_angle}

*Image: Distance Formula.svg.* The distance formula on the plane follows from the Pythagorean theorem.

In analytic geometry, geometric notions such as [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) and [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) measure are defined using [formulas](wikipedia://en/164040). These definitions are designed to be consistent with the underlying [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417). For example, using [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) on the plane, the distance between two points (*x*<sub>1</sub>, *y*<sub>1</sub>) and (*x*<sub>2</sub>, *y*<sub>2</sub>) is defined by the formula

$$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2},$$

which can be viewed as a version of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034). Similarly, the angle that a line makes with the horizontal can be defined by the formula

$$\theta = \arctan(m),$$

where *m* is the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of the line.

In three dimensions, distance is given by the generalization of the Pythagorean theorem:

$$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2+ (z_2 - z_1)^2},$$

while the angle between two vectors is given by the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093). The dot product of two Euclidean vectors **A** and **B** is defined by[^o40]

$$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} \left\|\mathbf A\right\| \left\|\mathbf B\right\| \cos\theta,$$

where *θ* is the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between **A** and **B**.

## Transformations {#Transformations}

*Image: FourGeometryTransformations.svg.* a) y = f(x) = |x| 5 b) y = f(x+3) 5 c) y = f(x)-3 5 d) y = 1/2 f(x)

Transformations are applied to a parent function to turn it into a new function with similar characteristics.

The graph of $R(x,y)$ is changed by standard transformations as follows:

- Changing $x$ to $x-h$ moves the graph to the right $h$ units.
- Changing $y$ to $y-k$ moves the graph up $k$ units.
- Changing $x$ to $x/b$ stretches the graph horizontally by a factor of $b$. (think of the $x$ as being dilated)
- Changing $y$ to $y/a$ stretches the graph vertically.
- Changing $x$ to $x\cos A+ y\sin A$ and changing $y$ to $-x\sin A + y\cos A$ rotates the graph by an angle $A$.

There are other standard transformation not typically studied in elementary analytic geometry because the transformations change the shape of objects in ways not usually considered. Skewing is an example of a transformation not usually considered. For more information, consult the Wikipedia article on [affine transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformations).

For example, the parent function $y=1/x$ has a horizontal and a vertical asymptote, and occupies the first and third quadrant, and all of its transformed forms have one horizontal and vertical asymptote, and occupies either the 1st and 3rd or 2nd and 4th quadrant. In general, if $y=f(x)$, then it can be transformed into $y=af(b(x-k))+h$. In the new transformed function, $a$ is the factor that vertically stretches the function if it is greater than 1 or vertically compresses the function if it is less than 1, and for negative $a$ values, the function is reflected in the $x$-axis. The $b$ value compresses the graph of the function horizontally if greater than 1 and stretches the function horizontally if less than 1, and like $a$, reflects the function in the $y$-axis when it is negative. The $k$ and $h$ values introduce translations, $h$, vertical, and $k$ horizontal. Positive $h$ and $k$ values mean the function is translated to the positive end of its axis and negative meaning translation towards the negative end.

Transformations can be applied to any geometric equation whether or not the equation represents a function. Transformations can be considered as individual transactions or in combinations.

Suppose that $R(x,y)$ is a relation in the $xy$ plane. For example,

$$x^2+y^2-1=0$$

is the relation that describes the unit circle.

## Finding intersections of geometric objects {#Finding_intersections_of_geometric_objects}

For two geometric objects P and Q represented by the relations $P(x,y)$ and $Q(x,y)$ the intersection is the collection of all points $(x,y)$ which are in both relations.[^o43]

For example, $P$ might be the circle with radius 1 and center $(0,0)$: $P = \{(x,y) | x^2+y^2=1\}$ and $Q$ might be the circle with radius 1 and center $(1,0): Q = \{(x,y) | (x-1)^2+y^2=1\}$. The intersection of these two circles is the collection of points which make both equations true. Does the point $(0,0)$ make both equations true? Using $(0,0)$ for $(x,y)$, the equation for $Q$ becomes $(0-1)^2+0^2=1$ or $(-1)^2=1$ which is true, so $(0,0)$ is in the relation $Q$. On the other hand, still using $(0,0)$ for $(x,y)$ the equation for $P$ becomes $0^2+0^2=1$ or $0=1$ which is false. $(0,0)$ is not in $P$ so it is not in the intersection.

The intersection of $P$ and $Q$ can be found by solving the simultaneous equations:

$$x^2+y^2 = 1$$

$$(x-1)^2+y^2 = 1.$$

Traditional methods for finding intersections include substitution and elimination.

**Substitution:** Solve the first equation for $y$ in terms of $x$ and then substitute the expression for $y$ into the second equation:

$$x^2+y^2 = 1$$

$$y^2=1-x^2.$$

We then substitute this value for $y^2$ into the other equation and proceed to solve for $x$:

$$(x-1)^2+(1-x^2)=1$$

$$x^2 -2x +1 +1 -x^2 =1$$

$$-2x = -1$$

$$x=1/2.$$

Next, we place this value of $x$ in either of the original equations and solve for $y$:

$$(1/2)^2+y^2 = 1$$

$$y^2 =3/4$$

$$y = \frac{\pm \sqrt{3}}{2}.$$

So our intersection has two points:

$$\left(1/2,\frac{+ \sqrt{3}}{2}\right) \;\; \text{and} \;\; \left(1/2,\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{2}\right).$$

**Elimination**: Add (or subtract) a multiple of one equation to the other equation so that one of the variables is eliminated. For our current example, if we subtract the first equation from the second we get $(x-1)^2-x^2=0$. The $y^2$ in the first equation is subtracted from the $y^2$ in the second equation leaving no $y$ term. The variable $y$ has been eliminated. We then solve the remaining equation for $x$, in the same way as in the substitution method:

$$x^2 -2x +1 -x^2 =0$$

$$-2x = -1$$

$$x=1/2.$$

We then place this value of $x$ in either of the original equations and solve for $y$:

$$(1/2)^2+y^2 = 1$$

$$y^2 = 3/4$$

$$y = \frac{\pm \sqrt{3}}{2}.$$

So our intersection has two points:

$$\left(1/2,\frac{+ \sqrt{3}}{2}\right) \;\; \text{and} \;\; \left(1/2,\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{2}\right).$$

For conic sections, as many as 4 points might be in the intersection.

### Finding intercepts {#Finding_intercepts}

One type of intersection which is widely studied is the intersection of a geometric object with the $x$ and $y$ coordinate axes.

The intersection of a geometric object and the $y$-axis is called the $y$-intercept of the object. The intersection of a geometric object and the $x$-axis is called the $x$-intercept of the object.

For the line $y=mx+b$, the parameter $b$ specifies the point where the line crosses the $y$ axis. Depending on the context, either $b$ or the point $(0,b)$ is called the $y$-intercept.

## Geometric axis {#Geometric_axis}

Axis in geometry is the perpendicular line to any line, object or a surface.

Also for this may be used the common language use as a: normal (perpendicular) line, otherwise in engineering as *axial line*.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **normal** is an object such as a line or vector that is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to a given object. For example, in the two-dimensional case, the **normal line** to a curve at a given point is the line perpendicular to the [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) to the curve at the point.

In the three-dimensional case a **surface normal**, or simply **normal**, to a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) at a point *P* is a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) that is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to the [tangent plane](wikipedia://en/30807) to that surface at *P*. The word "normal" is also used as an adjective: a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) normal to a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), the normal component of a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force), the **normal vector**, etc. The concept of **normality** generalizes to [orthogonality](wikipedia://en/102221).

## Spherical and nonlinear planes and their tangents {#Spherical_and_nonlinear_planes_and_their_tangents}

Tangent is the linear approximation of a spherical or other curved or twisted line of a function.

### Tangent lines and planes {#Tangent_lines_and_planes}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **tangent line** (or simply **tangent**) to a plane [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) at a given [point](wikipedia://en/593693) is the [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line) that "just touches" the curve at that point. Informally, it is a line through a pair of [infinitely close](wikipedia://en/160990) points on the curve. More precisely, a straight line is said to be a tangent of a curve *y* = *f*(*x*) at a point *x* = *c* on the curve if the line passes through the point (*c*, *f*(*c*)) on the curve and has slope *f*'(*c*) where *f*' is the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of *f*. A similar definition applies to [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve) and curves in *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697).

As it passes through the point where the tangent line and the curve meet, called the **point of tangency**, the tangent line is "going in the same direction" as the curve, and is thus the best straight-line approximation to the curve at that point.

Similarly, the **tangent plane** to a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) at a given point is the [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) that "just touches" the surface at that point. The concept of a tangent is one of the most fundamental notions in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and has been extensively generalized; see [Tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807).
