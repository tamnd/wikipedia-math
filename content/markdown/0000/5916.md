# Circumference

*Image: Circle-withsegments.svg.* black solid 3px blue solid 2px red solid 2px green solid 2px Circumference = $\pi$ × diameter = 2$\pi$ × radius.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Circumference"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **circumference** (la) is the [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) or [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277). The circumference is the [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) of the circle, as if it were opened up and straightened out to a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860).[^o2] More generally, the perimeter is the [curve length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve_length) around any closed figure. Circumference may also refer to the circle itself, that is, the [locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locus_%28geometry%29) corresponding to the [edge](wikipedia://en/12158034) of a [disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disk_%28geometry%29). The circumference of a sphere is the circumference, or length, of any one of its [great circles](wikipedia://en/48082).

:::

## Circle {#Circle}

The circumference of a circle is the distance around it, but if, as in many elementary treatments, distance is defined in terms of straight lines, this cannot be used as a definition. Under these circumstances, the circumference of a circle may be defined as the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of the perimeters of inscribed [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) as the number of sides increases without bound.[^o5] The term circumference is used when measuring physical objects, as well as when considering abstract geometric forms.

*Image: Pi-unrolled-720.gif.* When a circle's [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) is 1, its circumference is $\pi.$

*Image: 2pi-unrolled.gif.* When a circle's [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) is 1—called a [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071)—its circumference is $2\pi.$

[Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) described how the circumference can be defined and computed using the [method of exhaustion](wikipedia://en/1721284). In modern terms, one considers a set $A$ of all perimeters of [inscribed polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon), a set $B$ of all perimeters of polygons circumscribing the circle. Every element of $A$ is less than every element of $B$, and there is no positive gap: no number that is less than all differences of elements of $A$ from elements of $B$. Therefore the circumference can be defined, and calculated, as the [unique number](wikipedia://en/45569) $x$ such that $a<x<b$ for every inscribed perimeter $a$ and every circumscribed perimeter $b$.

### Relationship with $\pi$ {#Relationship_with}

The circumference of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is related to one of the most important [mathematical constants](wikipedia://en/48779551). This [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132), [pi](wikipedia://en/23601), is represented by the [Greek letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_letter) [$\pi.$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_%28letter%29) Its first few decimal digits are 3.141592653589793... Pi is defined as the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of a circle's circumference $C$ to its [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) $d:$[^o8]

$$\pi = \frac{C}{d}.$$

Or, equivalently, as the ratio of the circumference to twice the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815). The above formula can be rearranged to solve for the circumference:

$${C} = \pi \cdot{d} = 2\pi \cdot{r}.\!$$

The ratio of the circle's circumference to its radius is equivalent to $2\pi$. This is also the number of [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) in one [turn](wikipedia://en/855329). The use of the mathematical constant $\pi$ is ubiquitous in mathematics, engineering, and science.

In *[Measurement of a Circle](wikipedia://en/18203202)* written circa 250 BCE, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) showed that this ratio (written as $C/d,$ since he did not use the name $\pi$) was greater than 3$\tfrac{10}{71}$ but less than 3$\tfrac{1}{7}$ by calculating the perimeters of an inscribed and a circumscribed regular polygon of 96 sides.[^o9] This method for approximating $\pi$ was used for centuries, obtaining more accuracy by using polygons of larger and larger number of sides. The last such calculation was performed in 1630 by [Christoph Grienberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Grienberger) who used polygons with 10<sup>40</sup> sides.

### Circumference in analysis {#Circumference_in_analysis}

In modern [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the circumference of a circle can be defined as its one-dimensional [measure](wikipedia://en/19873). One common intrinsic definition is by [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109). If $E$ is a subset of the Euclidean plane, its one-dimensional Hausdorff measure is obtained by covering $E$ with sets of small diameter and taking the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of the sums of those diameters.

For sufficiently regular curves, this measure agrees with the usual notion of arc length. Equivalently, if $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^2$ is a [rectifiable curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifiable_curve), its length may be defined as

$$L(\gamma)=\sup\sum_{i=1}^n |\gamma(t_i)-\gamma(t_{i-1})|,$$

where the supremum is taken over all partitions $a=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_n=b.$ For continuously differentiable curves this length is equal to

$$L(\gamma)=\int_a^b |\gamma'(t)|,dt.$$

Applying this to the circle of radius (r), with parametrization

$$\gamma(t)=(r\cos t,r\sin t),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 2\pi,$$

gives $|\gamma'(t)|=r$, and hence

$$L(\gamma)=\int_0^{2\pi}r,dt=2\pi r.$$

Thus, in analysis, the formula for circumference follows from a general definition of one-dimensional length rather than from assuming in advance that the circle has a perimeter given by polygonal exhaustion.

## Ellipse {#Ellipse}

*Image: Ellipses same circumference.png.* Circle, and ellipses with the same circumference

Some authors use circumference to denote the perimeter of an ellipse. There is no general formula for the circumference of an ellipse in terms of the [semi-major and semi-minor axes](wikipedia://en/23409699) of the ellipse that uses only elementary functions. However, there are approximate formulas in terms of these parameters. One such approximation, due to Euler (1773), for the [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) ellipse,

$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1,$$

is

$$C_{\rm{ellipse}} \sim \pi \sqrt{2\left(a^2 + b^2\right)}.$$

Some lower and upper bounds on the circumference of the canonical ellipse with $a\geq b$ are:[^o12]

$$2\pi b \leq C \leq 2\pi a,$$

$$\pi (a+b) \leq C \leq 4(a+b),$$

$$4\sqrt{a^2+b^2} \leq C \leq \pi \sqrt{2\left(a^2+b^2\right)}.$$

Here the upper bound $2\pi a$ is the circumference of a [circumscribed](wikipedia://en/161262) [concentric circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric_circle) passing through the endpoints of the ellipse's major axis, and the lower bound $4\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ is the [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) of an [inscribed](wikipedia://en/4470203) [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884) with [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) at the endpoints of the major and minor axes.

The circumference of an ellipse can be expressed exactly in terms of the [complete elliptic integral of the second kind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_elliptic_integral_of_the_second_kind).[^o13] More precisely,

$$C_{\rm{ellipse}} = 4a \int_0^{\pi/2} \sqrt{1 - e^2 \sin^2\theta}\ d\theta,$$

where $a$ is the length of the semi-major axis and $e$ is the eccentricity $\sqrt{1 - b^2/a^2}.$
