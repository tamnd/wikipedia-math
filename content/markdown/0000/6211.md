# Context-sensitive grammar

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Context-sensitive grammar"}

A **context-sensitive grammar** (**CSG**) is a [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) in which the left-hand sides and right-hand sides of any [production rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Production_%28computer_science%29) may be surrounded by a context of [terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_symbol) and [nonterminal symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonterminal_symbol). Context-sensitive grammars are more general than [context-free grammars](wikipedia://en/6759), in the sense that there are languages that can be described by a CSG but not by a context-free grammar. Context-sensitive grammars are less general (in the same sense) than [unrestricted grammars](wikipedia://en/4906174). Thus, CSGs are positioned between context-free and unrestricted grammars in the [Chomsky hierarchy](wikipedia://en/6011).[^o2]

:::

A [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939) that can be described by a context-sensitive grammar, or, equivalently, by a [noncontracting grammar](wikipedia://en/12441085) or a [linear bounded automaton](wikipedia://en/1811962), is called a [context-sensitive language](wikipedia://en/6212). Some textbooks actually define CSGs as non-contracting,[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6] although this is not how [Noam Chomsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noam_Chomsky) defined them in 1959.[^o7][^o8] This choice of definition makes no difference in terms of the languages generated (i.e. the two definitions are [weakly equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_equivalence_%28formal_languages%29)), but it does make a difference in terms of what grammars are structurally considered context-sensitive; the latter issue was analyzed by Chomsky in 1963.[^o9][^o10]

Chomsky introduced context-sensitive grammars as a way to describe the syntax of [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173) where it is often the case that a word may or may not be appropriate in a certain place depending on the context. [Walter Savitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Savitch) has criticized the terminology "context-sensitive" as misleading and proposed "non-erasing" as better explaining the distinction between a CSG and an [unrestricted grammar](wikipedia://en/4906174).[^o11]

Although it is well known that certain features of languages (e.g. [cross-serial dependency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-serial_dependency)) are not context-free, it is an [open question](wikipedia://en/1333335) how much of CSGs' expressive power is needed to capture the context sensitivity found in natural languages. Subsequent research in this area has focused on the more computationally tractable [mildly context-sensitive languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mildly_context-sensitive_language). The syntaxes of some [visual programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_programming_language) can be described by context-sensitive [graph grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_grammar).[^o12]

::: {#o14 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

### Formal grammar {#Formal_grammar}

Let us notate a [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) as $G = (N, \Sigma, P, S)$, with $N$ a set of nonterminal symbols, $\Sigma$ a set of terminal symbols, $P$ a set of production rules, and $S \in N$ the start symbol.

A string $u \in (N \cup \Sigma)^*$ *directly yields*, or *directly derives to*, a string $v \in (N \cup \Sigma)^*$, denoted as $u \Rightarrow v$, if *v* can be obtained from *u* by an application of some production rule in *P*, that is, if $u = \gamma L \delta$ and $v = \gamma R \delta$, where $(L \to R) \in P$ is a production rule, and $\gamma, \delta \in (N \cup \Sigma)^*$ is the unaffected left and right part of the string, respectively. More generally, *u* is said to *yield*, or *derive to*, *v*, denoted as $u \Rightarrow^* v$, if *v* can be obtained from *u* by repeated application of production rules, that is, if $u = u_0 \Rightarrow ... \Rightarrow u_n = v$ for some *n* ≥ 0 and some strings $u_1, ..., u_{n-1} \in (N \cup \Sigma)^*$. In other words, the relation $\Rightarrow^*$ is the [reflexive transitive closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_transitive_closure) of the relation $\Rightarrow$.

The **language** of the grammar *G* is the set of all terminal-symbol strings derivable from its start symbol, formally: $L(G) = \{ w \in \Sigma^* \mid S \Rightarrow^* w \}$. Derivations that do not end in a string composed of terminal symbols only are possible, but do not contribute to *L*(*G*).

### Context-sensitive grammar {#Context-sensitive_grammar}

A formal grammar is **context-sensitive** if each rule in *P* is of either of the forms

α*A*β → αγβ

A → ε

with $\varepsilon$ being the [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827), $A \in N$,[^o17] $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in (N \cup \Sigma )^*, \gamma \neq \varepsilon$, i.e., α, β and γ are strings of nonterminals and [terminals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_symbol), but γ is not allowed to be empty. Without this restriction, the resulting grammars become equal in power to [unrestricted grammars](wikipedia://en/4906174).[^o11]

The name *context-sensitive* is explained by the α and β that form the context of *A* and determine whether *A* can be replaced with γ or not. By contrast, in a [context-free grammar](wikipedia://en/6759), no context is present: the left hand side of every production rule is just a nonterminal.

### (Weakly) equivalent definitions {#(Weakly)_equivalent_definitions}

A [noncontracting grammar](wikipedia://en/12441085) is a grammar in which for any production rule, of the form *u* → *v*, the length of *u* is less than or equal to the length of *v*.

Every context-sensitive grammar is noncontracting, while every noncontracting grammar can be converted into an equivalent context-sensitive grammar; the two classes are [weakly equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_equivalence_%28formal_languages%29).[^o19]

Some authors use the term *context-sensitive grammar* to refer to noncontracting grammars in general.

The **left-context**- and **right-context**-sensitive grammars are defined by restricting the rules to just the form α*A* → αγ and to just *A*β → γβ, respectively. The languages generated by these grammars are also the full class of context-sensitive languages.[^o20] The equivalence was established by [Penttonen normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penttonen_normal_form).[^o21]

### *a<sup>n</sup>b<sup>n</sup>c<sup>n</sup>* {#anbncn}

The following context-sensitive grammar, with start symbol *S*, generates the canonical non-[context-free language](wikipedia://en/6867) { *a<sup>n</sup>b<sup>n</sup>c<sup>n</sup>* | *n* ≥ 1 } :

| \1. |  | *S* | → | *a* | *B* | *C* |  |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| \2. |  | *S* | → | *a* | *S* | *B* | *C* |
| \3. | *C* | *B* | → | *C* | *Z* |  |  |
| \4. | *C* | *Z* | → | *W* | *Z* |  |  |
| \5. | *W* | *Z* | → | *W* | *C* |  |  |
| \6. | *W* | *C* | → | *B* | *C* |  |  |
| \7. | *a* | *B* | → | *a* | *b* |  |  |
| \8. | *b* | *B* | → | *b* | *b* |  |  |
| \9. | *b* | *C* | → | *b* | *c* |  |  |
| \10. | *c* | *C* | → | *c* | *c* |  |  |

Rules 1 and 2 allow for blowing-up *S* to *a*<sup>*n*</sup>*BC*(*BC*)<sup>*n*−1</sup>; rules 3 to 6 allow for successively exchanging each *CB* to *BC* ([four rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revesz'_trick) are needed for that since a rule *CB* → *BC* wouldn't fit into the scheme α*A*β → αγβ); rules 7–10 allow replacing a non-terminal *B* or *C* with its corresponding terminal *b* or *c*, respectively, provided it is in the right place. ### *a<sup>n</sup>b<sup>n</sup>c<sup>n</sup>d<sup>n</sup>*, etc. {#anbncndn,_etc.}

More complicated grammars can be used to parse { *a<sup>n</sup>b<sup>n</sup>c<sup>n</sup>d<sup>n</sup>* | *n* ≥ 1 }, and other languages with even more letters. Here we show a simpler approach using non-contracting grammars: Start with a kernel of regular productions generating the sentential forms $(ABCD)^{n}abcd$ and then include the non contracting productions $p_{Da} : Da\rightarrow aD$, $p_{Db} : Db\rightarrow bD$, $p_{Dc} : Dc\rightarrow cD$, $p_{Dd} : Dd\rightarrow dd$, $p_{Ca} : Ca\rightarrow aC$, $p_{Cb} : Cb\rightarrow bC$, $p_{Cc} : Cc\rightarrow cc$, $p_{Ba} : Ba\rightarrow aB$, $p_{Bb} : Bb\rightarrow bb$, $p_{Aa} : Aa\rightarrow aa$.

### *a<sup>m</sup>b<sup>n</sup>c<sup>m</sup>d<sup>n</sup>* {#ambncmdn}

A non contracting grammar (for which there is an equivalent CSG) for the language $L_{Cross} = \{ a^mb^nc^{m}d^{n} \mid m \ge 1, n \ge 1 \}$ is defined by

$$p_0 : S \rightarrow RT,$$

$$p_1 : R\rightarrow aRC | aC,$$

$$p_3 : T\rightarrow BTd | Bd,$$

$$p_5 : CB\rightarrow BC,$$

$$p_6 : aB\rightarrow ab,$$

$$p_7 : bB\rightarrow bb,$$

$p_8 : Cd\rightarrow cd$, and

$$p_9 : Cc\rightarrow cc.$$

With these definitions, a derivation for $a^3b^2c^3d^2$ is: $S \Rightarrow_{p_0} RT \Rightarrow_{p^{2}_{1}p_{2}} a^3C^3T \Rightarrow_{p_{3}p_{4} } a^3C^3B^2d^2 \Rightarrow_{p^{6}_{5} } a^3B^2C^3d^2 \Rightarrow_{p_{6}p_{7} } a^3b^2C^3d^2 \Rightarrow_{p_{8}p^{2}_{9}} a^3b^2c^3d^2$.

### *a*<sup>2<sup>*i*</sup></sup> {#a2i}

A noncontracting grammar for the language { *a*<sup>2<sup>*i*</sup></sup> | *i* ≥ 1 } is constructed in Example 9.5 (p. 224) of (Hopcroft, Ullman, 1979):[^o28]

1. $$S\rightarrow [ACaB]$$
1. $$\begin{cases} \ [Ca]a\rightarrow aa[Ca] \\ \ [Ca][aB]\rightarrow aa[CaB] \\ \ [ACa]a\rightarrow [Aa]a[Ca] \\ \ [ACa][aB]\rightarrow [Aa]a[CaB] \\ \ [ACaB]\rightarrow [Aa][aCB] \\ \ [CaB]\rightarrow a[aCB] \end{cases}$$
1. $$[aCB]\rightarrow [aDB]$$
1. $$[aCB]\rightarrow [aE]$$
1. $$\begin{cases} \ a[Da]\rightarrow [Da]a \\ \ [aDB]\rightarrow [DaB] \\ \ [Aa][Da]\rightarrow [ADa]a \\ \ a[DaB]\rightarrow [Da][aB] \\ \ [Aa][DaB]\rightarrow [ADa][aB] \end{cases}$$
1. $$[ADa]\rightarrow [ACa]$$
1. $$\begin{cases} \ a[Ea]\rightarrow [Ea]a \\ \ [aE]\rightarrow [Ea] \\ \ [Aa][Ea]\rightarrow [AEa]a \end{cases}$$
1. $$[AEa]\rightarrow a$$

## Kuroda normal form {#Kuroda_normal_form}

Every context-sensitive grammar which does not generate the empty string can be transformed into a [weakly equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_equivalence_%28formal_languages%29) one in [Kuroda normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuroda_normal_form). "Weakly equivalent" here means that the two grammars generate the same language. The normal form will not in general be context-sensitive, but will be a [noncontracting grammar](wikipedia://en/12441085).[^o30][^o31]

The Kuroda normal form is an actual normal form for non-contracting grammars.

## Properties and uses {#Properties_and_uses}

### Equivalence to linear bounded automaton {#Equivalence_to_linear_bounded_automaton}

A formal language can be described by a context-sensitive grammar if and only if it is accepted by some [linear bounded automaton](wikipedia://en/1811962) (LBA).[^o34] In some textbooks this result is attributed solely to Landweber and [Kuroda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S.-Y._Kuroda).[^o8] Others call it the [Myhill](wikipedia://en/2611636)–Landweber–Kuroda theorem.[^o35] (Myhill introduced the concept of deterministic LBA in 1960. Peter S. Landweber published in 1963 that the language accepted by a deterministic LBA is context sensitive.[^o36] Kuroda introduced the notion of non-deterministic LBA and the equivalence between LBA and CSGs in 1964.[^o37][^o38])

it is still an open question whether every context-sensitive language can be accepted by a *deterministic* LBA.[^o39]

### Closure properties {#Closure_properties}

Context-sensitive languages are closed under [complement](wikipedia://en/54347). This 1988 result is known as the [Immerman–Szelepcsényi theorem](wikipedia://en/2827371).[^o35] Moreover, they are closed under [union](wikipedia://en/47949), [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429), [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474#Concatenation_of_sets_of_strings), [substitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_substitution),[^o41] [inverse homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_string_homomorphism), and [Kleene plus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleene_plus).[^o42]

Every [recursively enumerable language](wikipedia://en/54789) *L* can be written as *h*(*L*) for some context-sensitive language *L* and some [string homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_homomorphism) *h*.[^o43]

### Computational problems {#Computational_problems}

The [decision problem](wikipedia://en/8336) that asks whether a certain string *s* belongs to the language of a given context-sensitive grammar *G*, is [PSPACE-complete](wikipedia://en/54685). Moreover, there are context-sensitive grammars whose languages are PSPACE-complete. In other words, there is a context-sensitive grammar *G* such that deciding whether a certain string *s* belongs to the language of *G* is PSPACE-complete (so *G* is fixed and only *s* is part of the input of the problem).[^o45]

The [emptiness problem](wikipedia://en/57342784) for context-sensitive grammars (given a context-sensitive grammar *G*, is *L*(*G*)=∅ ?) is [undecidable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undecidable_language).[^o46][^o47]

### As model of natural languages {#As_model_of_natural_languages}

Savitch has [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) the following theoretical result, on which he bases his criticism of CSGs as basis for natural language: for any [recursively enumerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable) set *R*, there exists a context-sensitive language/grammar *G* which can be used as a sort of proxy to test membership in *R* in the following way: given a string *s*, *s* is in *R* if and only if there exists a positive integer *n* for which *sc<sup>n</sup>* is in G, where *c* is an arbitrary symbol not part of *R*.[^o11]

It has been shown that nearly all [natural languages](wikipedia://en/21173) may in general be characterized by context-sensitive grammars, but the whole class of CSGs seems to be much bigger than natural languages. Worse yet, since the aforementioned decision problem for CSGs is PSPACE-complete, that makes them totally unworkable for practical use, as a polynomial-time algorithm for a PSPACE-complete problem would imply [P=NP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P=NP_problem).

It was proven that some natural languages are not context-free, based on identifying so-called [cross-serial dependencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-serial_dependency) and [unbounded scrambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbounded_scrambling) phenomena. However this does not necessarily imply that the class of CSGs is necessary to capture "context sensitivity" in the colloquial sense of these terms in natural languages. For example, [linear context-free rewriting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_context-free_rewriting_system) (LCFRSs) are strictly weaker than CSGs but can account for the phenomenon of cross-serial dependencies; one can write a LCFRS grammar for {*a<sup>n</sup>b<sup>n</sup>c<sup>n</sup>d<sup>n</sup>* | *n* ≥ 1} for example.[^o49][^o50][^o51]

Ongoing research on [computational linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_linguistics) has focused on formulating other classes of languages that are "[mildly context-sensitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mildly_context-sensitive_language)" whose decision problems are feasible, such as [tree-adjoining grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree-adjoining_grammar), [combinatory categorial grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatory_categorial_grammar), [coupled context-free languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupled_context-free_language), and [linear context-free rewriting systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_context-free_grammar#Linear_Context-free_Rewriting_Systems_%28LCFRSs%29). The languages generated by these formalisms properly lie between the context-free and context-sensitive languages.

More recently, the class [PTIME](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PTIME) has been identified with [range concatenation grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_concatenation_grammar), which are now considered to be the most expressive of the mild-context sensitive language classes.[^o51]
