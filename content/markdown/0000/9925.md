# Eubulides

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Eubulides"}

**Eubulides** (Εὐβουλίδης; fl. 4th century BCE) of [Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miletus) was a [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) of the [Megarian school](wikipedia://en/1219340) who is famous for his [paradoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradoxes).

:::

## Life {#Life}

According to [Diogenes Laërtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_Laërtius), Eubulides was a pupil of [Euclid of Megara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid_of_Megara),[^n1] the founder of the [Megarian school](wikipedia://en/1219340). He was a contemporary of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), against whom he wrote with great bitterness.[^n2][^o6] He taught logic to [Demosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosthenes),[^o7] and he is also said to have taught [Apollonius Cronus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonius_Cronus), the teacher of [Diodorus Cronus](wikipedia://en/2437676), and the historian [Euphantus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphantus).[^n3][^n4]

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Falakros"}

## Paradoxes of Eubulides {#Paradoxes_of_Eubulides}

Eubulides is most famous for inventing the forms of seven famous [paradoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradoxes),[^n1] some of which, however, are also ascribed to [Diodorus Cronus](wikipedia://en/2437676):[^n5]

1. [The Liar (*pseudomenos*) paradox](wikipedia://en/17967): A man says: "What I am saying now is a [lie](wikipedia://en/229723)." If the statement is true, then he is lying, even though the statement is true. If the statement is a lie, then he is not actually lying, even though the statement is a lie. Thus, if the speaker is lying, he tells the truth, and vice versa.
1. The Masked Man (*enkekalymmenos*) paradox: "Do you know this masked man?" "No." "But he is your father. So – do you not know your own father?"
1. The Electra (*Elektra*) paradox: [Electra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electra) does not know that the man approaching her is her brother, [Orestes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orestes). Electra knows her brother. Does Electra know the man who is approaching?
1. The Overlooked Man (*dialanthanôn*) paradox: Alpha ignored the man approaching him and treated him as a stranger. The man was his father. Did Alpha ignore his own father and treat him as a stranger?
1. [The Heap (*sôritês*) paradox](wikipedia://en/231687): A single grain of sand is certainly not a heap. Nor is the addition of a single grain of sand enough to transform a non-heap into a heap: when we have a collection of grains of sand that is not a heap, then adding but one single grain will not create a heap. And yet we know that at some point we will have a heap.
1. The Bald Man (*phalakros*) paradox: A man with a full head of hair is obviously not bald. Now the removal of a single hair will not turn a non-bald man into a bald one. And yet it is obvious that a continuation of that process must eventually result in baldness.
1. The Horns (*keratinês*) paradox: What you have not lost, you have. But you have not lost horns. Therefore, you have horns.

The first paradox ([the Liar](wikipedia://en/17967)) is probably the most famous, and is similar to the famous paradox of [Epimenides the Cretan](wikipedia://en/9638). The second, third and fourth paradoxes are variants of a single paradox and relate to the problem of what it means to "know" something and the identity of objects involved in an affirmation (compare the [masked-man fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masked-man_fallacy)). The fifth and sixth paradoxes are also a single paradox and is usually thought to relate to the vagueness of language.[^n6] The final paradox, the horns, is a paradox related to [presupposition](wikipedia://en/1965727).[^n7]

:::

## Legacy {#Legacy}

These paradoxes were very well known in ancient times, some are alluded to by Eubulides' contemporary [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)[^o17] and even partially by [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954).[^o18][^n4] [Chrysippus](wikipedia://en/37143), the [Stoic](wikipedia://en/19224834) philosopher wrote about the paradoxes developed by Eubulides and characterized the Horns paradox as an intractable problem (aporoi logoi).[^n7] [Aulus Gellius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aulus_Gellius) mentions how the discussion of such paradoxes was considered (for him) after-dinner entertainment at the [Saturnalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturnalia),[^o21] but [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger), on the other hand, considered them a waste of time: "Not to know them does no harm, and mastering them does no good."[^o22]
