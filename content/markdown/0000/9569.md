# Endomorphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Endomorphism"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), an **endomorphism** is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from a mathematical object to itself.[^o2] More generally in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), an endomorphism is a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) from an object in some [category](wikipedia://en/53759) to itself.[^o3] An endomorphism that is also an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160). For example, an endomorphism of a vector space V is a linear map f: V → V, and an endomorphism of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) G is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) f: G → G.

*Image: Orthogonal projection.svg.* [Orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) onto a line, $m$, is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on the plane. This is an example of an endomorphism that is not an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160).

:::

In general, we can talk about endomorphisms in any [category](wikipedia://en/53759). In the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), endomorphisms are [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) from a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *S* to itself.

In any category, the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of any two endomorphisms of $X$ is again an endomorphism of $X$. It follows that the set of all endomorphisms of $X$ forms a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652), the [full transformation monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_transformation_monoid), and denoted $\operatorname{End}(X)$ (or $\operatorname{End}_{C}(X)$ to emphasize the category $C$).

## Automorphisms {#Automorphisms}

An [invertible](wikipedia://en/14907) endomorphism of $X$ is called an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160). The set of all automorphisms is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\operatorname{End}(X)$ with a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) structure, called the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of $X$ and denoted $\operatorname{Aut}(X)$. ## Endomorphism rings {#Endomorphism_rings}

Any two endomorphisms of an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), $A$, can be added together by the rule $(f + g)(a) = f(a) + g(a)$. Under this addition, and with multiplication being defined as function composition, the endomorphisms of an abelian group form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) (the [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623)). For example, the set of endomorphisms of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is the ring of all $n \times n$ [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) entries. The endomorphisms of a vector space or [module](wikipedia://en/276410) also form a ring, as do the endomorphisms of any object in a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521). The endomorphisms of a nonabelian group generate an algebraic structure known as a [near-ring](wikipedia://en/839900). Every ring with one is the endomorphism ring of its [regular module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_module), and so is a subring of an endomorphism ring of an abelian group;[^o6] however there are rings that are not the endomorphism ring of any abelian group.

## Operator theory {#Operator_theory}

In any [concrete category](wikipedia://en/167001), especially for [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), endomorphisms are maps from a set into itself, and may be interpreted as [unary operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unary_operator) on that set, [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_%28group_theory%29) on the elements, and allowing the notion of element [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) to be defined, etc.

Depending on the additional structure defined for the category at hand ([topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29), ...), such operators can have properties like [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29), [boundedness](wikipedia://en/311509), and so on. More details should be found in the article about [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675).

## Endofunctions {#Endofunctions}

An **endofunction** is a function whose [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is equal to its [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264). A [homomorphic](wikipedia://en/13564) endofunction is an endomorphism.

Let $S$ be an arbitrary set. Among endofunctions on $S$ one finds [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of $S$ and constant functions associating to every $x$ in $S$ the same element $c$ in $S$. Every permutation of $S$ has the codomain equal to its domain and is [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942) and invertible. If $S$ has more than one element, a constant function on $S$ has an [image](wikipedia://en/579311) that is a proper subset of its codomain, and thus is not bijective (and hence not invertible). The function associating to each [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$ the floor of $n/2$ has its image equal to its codomain and is not invertible.

Finite endofunctions are equivalent to [directed pseudoforests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_pseudoforest). For sets of size $n$ there are $n^{n}$ endofunctions on the set.

Particular examples of bijective endofunctions are the [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450); i.e., the functions coinciding with their inverses.
