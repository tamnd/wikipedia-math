# Aerodynamics

*Image: Airplane vortex edit.jpg.* A NASA [wake turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wake_turbulence) study at [Wallops Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallops_Flight_Facility) is portrayed, 1990. A [vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex) is created by passage of an aircraft wing, revealed by smoke. Vortices are one of the many phenomena associated with the study of aerodynamics.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Aerodynamics"}

**Aerodynamics** (grc) is the study of the motion of [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth), particularly when affected by a [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) object, such as an [airplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airplane) wing.[^o2] It involves topics covered in the field of [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034) and its subfield of [gas dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_dynamics), and is an important domain of study in [aeronautics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeronautics). The term *aerodynamics* is often used synonymously with gas dynamics, the difference being that "gas dynamics" applies to the study of the motion of all gases, and is not limited to air. The formal study of aerodynamics began in the modern sense in the eighteenth century, although observations of fundamental concepts such as [aerodynamic drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_drag) were recorded much earlier. Most of the early efforts in aerodynamics were directed toward achieving [heavier-than-air flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft#Heavier-than-air_–_aerodynes), which was first demonstrated by [Otto Lilienthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Lilienthal) in 1891.[^o3] Since then, the use of aerodynamics through [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) analysis, empirical approximations, [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel) experimentation, and [computer simulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_simulation) has formed a rational basis for the development of heavier-than-air flight and a number of other technologies. Recent work in aerodynamics has focused on issues related to [compressible flow](wikipedia://en/219144), [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664), and [boundary layers](wikipedia://en/224301). **Computational aerodynamics**[^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7] is an interdisciplinary field and a specialization of [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924) that developes [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) to solve problems related to aerodynamics.

:::

## History {#History}

Modern aerodynamics only dates back to the seventeenth century, but aerodynamic forces have been harnessed by humans for thousands of years in sailboats and windmills,[^o9] and images and stories of flight appear throughout recorded history[^o10] including the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) legend of [Icarus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icarus) and [Daedalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daedalus).[^o11] Fundamental concepts of [continuum](wikipedia://en/5918), [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_drag), and [pressure gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_gradient) appear in the work of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) and [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844).[^o28]

In 1726, Sir [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) became the first person to develop a theory of air resistance,[^o12] making him one of the first aerodynamicists. [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_people)-[Swiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_people) mathematician [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784) followed in 1738 with *Hydrodynamica* in which he described a fundamental relationship between pressure, density, and flow velocity for [incompressible flow](wikipedia://en/461477) known today as [Bernoulli's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli's_principle), which provides one method for calculating aerodynamic lift.[^o13] In 1757, [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) published the more general [Euler equations](wikipedia://en/396022) which could be applied to both compressible and incompressible flows. The Euler equations were extended to incorporate the effects of viscosity in the first half of the 1800s, resulting in the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395).[^o14][^o15] The Navier–Stokes equations are the most general governing equations of fluid flow but are difficult to solve for the flow around all but the simplest of shapes.

*Image: WB Wind Tunnel.jpg.* A replica of the [Wright brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_brothers)' [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel) is on display at the [Virginia Air and Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Air_and_Space_Science_Center) in [Hampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampton,_Virginia). Wind tunnels were key in the development and validation of the laws of aerodynamics.

In 1799, [Sir George Cayley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Cayley) became the first person to identify the four aerodynamic forces of flight ([weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight), [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29), [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_drag), and [thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust)), as well as the relationships between them,[^o16][^o17] and in doing so outlined the path toward achieving heavier-than-air flight for the next century. In 1871, [Francis Herbert Wenham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Herbert_Wenham) constructed the first [wind tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_tunnel), allowing precise measurements of aerodynamic forces. Drag theories were developed by [Jean Le Rond d'Alembert](wikipedia://en/51182),[^o18] [Gustav Kirchhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff),[^o19] and [Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Strutt,_3rd_Baron_Rayleigh).[^o20] In 1889, [Charles Renard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Renard), a [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_people) aeronautical engineer, became the first person to reasonably predict the power needed for sustained flight.[^o21] [Otto Lilienthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Lilienthal), the first person to become highly successful with glider flights, was also the first to propose thin, curved [airfoils](wikipedia://en/290053) that would produce high lift and low drag. Building on these developments as well as research carried out in their own wind tunnel, the [Wright brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wright_brothers) flew the first powered airplane on December 17, 1903.

During the time of the first flights, [Frederick W. Lanchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_W._Lanchester),[^o22] [Martin Kutta](wikipedia://en/551234), and [Nikolai Zhukovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Zhukovsky_%28scientist%29) independently created theories which connected [circulation](wikipedia://en/753962) of a fluid flow to lift. Kutta and Zhukovsky then developed a two-dimensional wing theory. Expanding upon the work of Lanchester, [Ludwig Prandtl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Prandtl) is credited with developing the mathematics[^o23] behind thin-airfoil and lifting-line theories as well as working with [boundary layers](wikipedia://en/224301).

*Image: Smooth Finish Makes Smooth Flying Art.IWMPST14268.jpg.* A [Ministry of Aircraft Production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Aircraft_Production) poster on aerodynamics

As aircraft speed increased designers began to encounter challenges associated with air [compressibility](wikipedia://en/166084) at speeds near the speed of sound. The differences in airflow under such conditions lead to problems in aircraft control, increased drag due to [shock waves](wikipedia://en/146253) and the threat of structural failure due to [aeroelastic flutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroelasticity). The ratio of the flow speed to the speed of sound was named the [Mach number](wikipedia://en/20051) after [Ernst Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Mach) who was one of the first to investigate the properties of the [supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic) flow. [Macquorn Rankine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._J._M._Rankine) and [Pierre Henri Hugoniot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Henri_Hugoniot) independently developed the theory for flow properties before and after a [shock wave](wikipedia://en/146253), while [Jakob Ackeret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakob_Ackeret) led the initial work of calculating the lift and drag of supersonic airfoils.[^o24] [Theodore von Kármán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_von_Kármán) and [Hugh Latimer Dryden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Latimer_Dryden) introduced the term [transonic](wikipedia://en/247737) to describe flow speeds between the [critical Mach number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_Mach_number) and Mach 1 where drag increases rapidly. The rapid increase in drag led aerodynamicists and aviators to disagree on whether supersonic flight was achievable until the [sound barrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_barrier) was broken in 1947 using the [Bell X-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_X-1) aircraft.

By the time the sound barrier was broken, aerodynamicists' understanding of the subsonic and low supersonic flow had matured. The [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War) prompted the design of an ever-evolving line of high-performance aircraft. [Computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924) began as an effort to solve for flow properties around complex objects and has rapidly grown to the point where entire aircraft can be designed using computer software, with wind-tunnel tests followed by flight tests to confirm the computer predictions. Understanding of [supersonic](wikipedia://en/61273) and [hypersonic](wikipedia://en/55244) aerodynamics has matured since the 1960s, and the goals of aerodynamicists have shifted from the behaviour of fluid flow to the engineering of a vehicle such that it interacts predictably with the fluid flow. Designing aircraft for supersonic and hypersonic conditions, as well as the desire to improve the aerodynamic efficiency of current aircraft and propulsion systems, continues to motivate new research in aerodynamics, while work continues to be done on important problems in basic aerodynamic theory related to flow turbulence and the existence and uniqueness of analytical solutions to the Navier–Stokes equations.

## Fundamental concepts {#Fundamental_concepts}

*Image: aeroforces.svg.* Forces on a powered aircraft in unaccelerated level flight

Understanding the motion of air around an object (often called a flow field) enables the calculation of forces and [moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_%28physics%29) acting on the object. In many aerodynamics problems, the forces of interest are the fundamental forces of flight: [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29), [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamic_drag), [thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust), and [weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight). Of these, lift and drag are aerodynamic forces, i.e. forces due to air flow over a solid body. Calculation of these quantities is often founded upon the assumption that the flow field behaves as a continuum. Continuum flow fields are characterized by properties such as [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity), [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure), [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density), and [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050), which may be functions of position and time. These properties may be directly or indirectly measured in aerodynamics experiments or calculated starting with the equations for conservation of mass, [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431), and energy in air flows. Density, flow velocity, and an additional property, [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754), are used to classify flow fields.

### Flow classification {#Flow_classification}

Flow velocity is used to classify flows according to speed regime. Subsonic flows are flow fields in which the air speed field is always below the local speed of sound. Transonic flows include both regions of subsonic flow and regions in which the local flow speed is greater than the local speed of sound. Supersonic flows are defined to be flows in which the flow speed is greater than the speed of sound everywhere. A fourth classification, hypersonic flow, refers to flows where the flow speed is much greater than the speed of sound. Aerodynamicists disagree on the precise definition of hypersonic flow.

[Compressible flow](wikipedia://en/166084) accounts for varying density within the flow. Subsonic flows are often idealized as incompressible, i.e. the density is assumed to be constant. Transonic and supersonic flows are compressible, and calculations that neglect the changes of density in these flow fields will yield inaccurate results.

Viscosity is associated with the frictional forces in a flow. In some flow fields, viscous effects are very small, and approximate solutions may safely neglect viscous effects. These approximations are called inviscid flows. Flows for which viscosity is not neglected are called viscous flows. Finally, aerodynamic problems may also be classified by the flow environment. External aerodynamics is the study of flow around solid objects of various shapes (e.g. around an airplane wing), while internal aerodynamics is the study of flow through passages inside solid objects (e.g. through a jet engine).

#### Continuum assumption {#Continuum_assumption}

Unlike liquids and solids, gases are composed of discrete [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) which occupy only a small fraction of the volume filled by the gas. On a molecular level, flow fields are made up of the collisions of many individual gas molecules among themselves, and their collisions with solid surfaces. However, in most aerodynamics applications, the discrete molecular nature of gases is ignored, and the flow field is assumed to behave as a [continuum](wikipedia://en/5918). This assumption allows fluid properties such as density and flow velocity to be defined everywhere within the flow.

The validity of the [continuum assumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_assumption) is dependent on the density of the gas and the application in question. For the continuum assumption to be valid, the [mean free path](wikipedia://en/170097) length must be much smaller than the length scale of the application in question. For example, many aerodynamics applications deal with aircraft flying in atmospheric conditions, where the mean free path length is on the order of micrometers and where the body is orders of magnitude larger. In these cases, the length scale of the aircraft ranges from a few meters to a few tens of meters, which is much larger than the mean free path length. For such applications, the continuum assumption is reasonable. The continuum assumption is less valid for extremely low-density flows like those encountered by vehicles at very high altitudes (e.g. 300,000 ft/90 km)[^o28] or satellites in [Low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit). In these cases, [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) is a more accurate method of solving the problem than is continuum aerodynamics. The [Knudsen number](wikipedia://en/401314) can be used to guide the choice between statistical mechanics and the continuous formulation of aerodynamics.

::: {#o30 .statement cue=named-heading name="Conservation laws"}

### Conservation laws {#Conservation_laws}

The assumption of a [fluid continuum](wikipedia://en/5918) allows problems in aerodynamics to be solved using [fluid dynamics conservation laws](wikipedia://en/11034#Conservation_laws). Three conservation principles are used:

**[Conservation of mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_mass)**

Conservation of mass requires that mass is neither created nor destroyed within a flow; the mathematical formulation of this principle is known as the [mass continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793#Fluid_dynamics).

**[Conservation of momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_momentum)**

The mathematical formulation of this principle can be considered an application of [Newton's second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_second_law). Momentum within a flow is only changed by external forces, which may include both [surface forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_force) such as viscous ([frictional](wikipedia://en/11062)) forces, and [body forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_force), such as [weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity). The momentum conservation principle may be expressed as either a [vector](wikipedia://en/32370) equation or separated into a set of three [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) equations (x,y,z components).

**[Conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088)**

The energy conservation equation states that energy is neither created nor destroyed within a flow, and that any addition or subtraction of energy to a volume in the flow is caused by [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726), or by [work](wikipedia://en/149861) into and out of the region of interest.

Together, the equations are known as [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395), although some authors define the term to only include the momentum equation(s). Navier–Stokes equations have no known analytical solution and are solved in modern aerodynamics using [computational techniques](wikipedia://en/305924). Because computational methods using high speed computers were not historically available and the high computational cost of solving these complex equations now that they are available, simplifications of the Navier–Stokes equations have been and continue to be employed. [Euler equations](wikipedia://en/396022) are a set of similar conservation equations which neglect viscosity and may be used in cases where the effect of viscosity is expected to be small. Further simplifications lead to [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) and [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) theory. Additionally, [Bernoulli's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli's_principle) is a solution in one dimension to both the momentum and energy conservation equations.

The [ideal gas law](wikipedia://en/59881) or another such [equation of state](wikipedia://en/9908) is often used in conjunction with these equations to form a determined system which allows the solution for the unknown variables.[^o31]

:::

## Branches of aerodynamics {#Branches_of_aerodynamics}

*Image: 3840x1080\_F16\_OpenFOAM.jpg.* Computational modelling of airflow over a jet

Aerodynamic problems are classified by the flow environment or properties of the flow, including [flow speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_speed), [compressibility](wikipedia://en/166084), and viscosity. *External* aerodynamics is the study of flow around solid objects of various shapes. Evaluating the [lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29) and [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_%28physics%29) on an [airplane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airplane) or the [shock waves](wikipedia://en/146253) that form in front of the nose of a [rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket) are examples of external aerodynamics. *Internal* aerodynamics is the study of flow through passages in solid objects. For instance, internal aerodynamics encompasses the study of the airflow through a [jet engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_engine) or through an [air conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_conditioning) pipe.

Aerodynamic problems can also be classified according to whether the [flow speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_speed) is below, near or above the [speed of sound](wikipedia://en/147853). A problem is called subsonic if all the speeds in the problem are less than the speed of sound, [transonic](wikipedia://en/247737) if speeds both below and above the speed of sound are present (normally when the characteristic speed is approximately the speed of sound), supersonic when the characteristic flow speed is greater than the speed of sound, and hypersonic when the flow speed is much greater than the speed of sound. Aerodynamicists disagree over the precise definition of hypersonic flow; a rough definition considers flows with [Mach numbers](wikipedia://en/20051) above 5 to be hypersonic.[^o28]

The influence of viscosity on the flow dictates a third classification. Some problems may encounter only very small viscous effects, in that case viscosity can be considered to be negligible. The approximations to these problems are called [inviscid flows](wikipedia://en/5877457). Flows for which viscosity cannot be neglected are called viscous flows.

### Incompressible aerodynamics {#Incompressible_aerodynamics}

An incompressible flow is a flow in which density is constant in both time and space. Although all real fluids are compressible, a flow is often approximated as incompressible if the effect of the density changes cause only small changes to the calculated results. This is more likely to be true when the flow speeds are significantly lower than the speed of sound. Effects of compressibility are more significant at speeds close to or above the speed of sound. The Mach number is used to evaluate whether the incompressibility can be assumed, otherwise the effects of compressibility must be included.

::: {#o35 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Subsonic flight"}

#### Subsonic flow {#Subsonic_flow}

Subsonic (or low-speed) aerodynamics describes fluid motion in flows which are much lower than the speed of sound everywhere in the flow. There are several branches of subsonic flow but one special case arises when the flow is [inviscid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inviscid), [incompressible](wikipedia://en/166084) and [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational). This case is called [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) and allows the [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) which describe the flow to be a simplified version of the equations of [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), thus making available to the aerodynamicist a range of quick and easy solutions.[^o36]

In solving a subsonic problem, one decision to be made by the aerodynamicist is whether to incorporate the effects of compressibility. Compressibility is a description of the amount of change of [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) in the flow. When the effects of compressibility on the solution are small, the assumption that density is constant may be made. The problem is then an incompressible low-speed aerodynamics problem. When the density is allowed to vary, the flow is called compressible. In air, compressibility effects are usually ignored when the Mach number in the flow does not exceed 0.3 (about 335 feet (102 m) per second or 228 miles (366 km) per hour at 60 °F (16 °C)). Above Mach 0.3, the problem flow should be described using compressible aerodynamics.

:::

::: {#o38 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Low-speed aerodynamics"}

### Compressible aerodynamics {#Compressible_aerodynamics}

According to the theory of aerodynamics, a flow is considered to be compressible if the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) changes along a [streamline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streamlines,_streaklines_and_pathlines). This means that–unlike incompressible flow–changes in density are considered. In general, this is the case where the Mach number in part or all of the flow exceeds 0.3. The Mach 0.3 value is rather arbitrary, but it is used because gas flows with a Mach number below that value demonstrate changes in density of less than 5%. Furthermore, that maximum 5% density change occurs at the [stagnation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stagnation_point) (the point on the object where flow speed is zero), while the density changes around the rest of the object will be significantly lower. Transonic, supersonic, and hypersonic flows are all compressible flows.

#### Transonic flow {#Transonic_flow}

The term Transonic refers to a range of flow velocities just below and above the local [speed of sound](wikipedia://en/147853) (generally taken as [Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mach_Number) 0.8–1.2). It is defined as the range of speeds between the [critical Mach number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_mach), when some parts of the airflow over an aircraft become [supersonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersonic), and a higher speed, typically near [Mach 1.2](wikipedia://en/20051), when all of the airflow is supersonic. Between these speeds, some of the airflow is supersonic, while some of the airflow is not supersonic.

#### Supersonic flow {#Supersonic_flow}

Supersonic aerodynamic problems are those involving flow speeds greater than the speed of sound. Calculating the lift on the [Concorde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concorde) during cruise can be an example of a supersonic aerodynamic problem.

Supersonic flow behaves very differently from subsonic flow. Fluids react to differences in pressure; pressure changes are how a fluid is "told" to respond to its environment. Therefore, since [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) is in fact, an infinitesimal pressure difference propagating through a fluid, the [speed of sound](wikipedia://en/147853) in that fluid can be considered the fastest speed that "information" can travel in the flow. This difference most obviously manifests itself in the case of a fluid striking an object. In front of that object, the fluid builds up a [stagnation pressure](wikipedia://en/2953344) as impact with the object brings the moving fluid to rest. In fluid traveling at subsonic speed, this pressure disturbance can propagate upstream, changing the flow pattern ahead of the object and giving the impression that the fluid "knows" the object is there by seemingly adjusting its movement and is flowing around it. In a supersonic flow, however, the pressure disturbance cannot propagate upstream. Thus, when the fluid finally reaches the object it strikes it and the fluid is forced to change its properties – [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050), [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density), [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure), and [Mach number](wikipedia://en/20051)—in an extremely violent and [irreversible](wikipedia://en/416681) fashion called a [shock wave](wikipedia://en/146253). The presence of shock waves, along with the compressibility effects of high-flow velocity (see [Reynolds number](wikipedia://en/23868856)) fluids, is the central difference between the supersonic and subsonic aerodynamics regimes.

#### Hypersonic flow {#Hypersonic_flow}

In aerodynamics, hypersonic speeds are speeds that are highly supersonic. In the 1970s, the term generally came to refer to speeds of Mach 5 (5 times the speed of sound) and above. The hypersonic regime is a subset of the supersonic regime. Hypersonic flow is characterized by high temperature flow behind a shock wave, viscous interaction, and chemical dissociation of gas.

:::

## Associated terminology {#Associated_terminology}

*Image: Types of flow analysis in fluid mechanics.svg.* Different types flow analysis around an airfoil: \#f3f3fd \#ff9665 \#3b3bde

The incompressible and compressible flow regimes produce many associated phenomena, such as boundary layers and turbulence.

### Boundary layers {#Boundary_layers}

The concept of a [boundary layer](wikipedia://en/224301) is important in many problems in aerodynamics. The viscosity and fluid friction in the air is approximated as being significant only in this thin layer. This assumption makes the description of such aerodynamics much more tractable mathematically.

### Turbulence {#Turbulence}

In aerodynamics, turbulence is characterized by chaotic property changes in the flow. These include low momentum diffusion, high momentum convection, and rapid variation of pressure and flow velocity in space and time. Flow that is not turbulent is called [laminar flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminar_flow).

## Aerodynamics in other fields {#Aerodynamics_in_other_fields}

### Engineering design {#Engineering_design}

Aerodynamics is a significant element of [vehicle design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive_engineering), including [road cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Car) and [trucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truck) where the main goal is to reduce the vehicle [drag coefficient](wikipedia://en/172291), and [racing cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto_racing), where in addition to reducing drag the goal is also to increase the overall level of [downforce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downforce).[^o36] Aerodynamics is also important in the prediction of forces and moments acting on [sailing vessels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sailing). It is used in the design of mechanical components such as [hard drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_drive) heads. [Structural engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineering) resort to aerodynamics, and particularly [aeroelasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeroelasticity), when calculating [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind) loads in the design of large buildings, [bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridge), and [wind turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_turbine_design).

The aerodynamics of internal passages is important in [heating/ventilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVAC), [gas piping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duct_%28HVAC%29), and in [automotive engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_combustion_engine) where detailed flow patterns strongly affect the performance of the engine.

### Environmental design {#Environmental_design}

Urban aerodynamics are studied by [town planners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_planning) and designers seeking to improve [amenity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amenity) in outdoor spaces, or in creating urban microclimates to reduce the effects of urban pollution. The field of environmental aerodynamics describes ways in which [atmospheric circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_circulation) and flight mechanics affect ecosystems. Aerodynamic equations are used in [numerical weather prediction](wikipedia://en/1505381).

### Ball-control in sports {#Ball-control_in_sports}

Sports in which aerodynamics are of crucial importance include [soccer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football), [table tennis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_tennis), [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket), [baseball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball), and [golf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf), in which most players can control the trajectory of the ball using the "[Magnus effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_effect#In_sport)".
