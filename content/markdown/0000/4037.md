# Bletchley Park

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Bletchley Park"}

**Bletchley Park** is an [English country house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_country_house) and estate in [Bletchley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bletchley), [Milton Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Keynes) (Buckinghamshire), that became the principal centre of [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II) [code-breaking during the Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II_cryptography). During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), the estate housed the [Government Code and Cypher School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Code_and_Cypher_School) (GC&CS), which regularly penetrated the secret communications of the [Axis powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers) – most importantly the German [Enigma](wikipedia://en/9256) and [Lorenz](wikipedia://en/301173) ciphers. The GC&CS team of codebreakers included [John Tiltman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tiltman), [Dilwyn Knox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilwyn_Knox), [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208), [Margaret Rock](wikipedia://en/40910655), [Mavis Lever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mavis_Lever), [Harry Golombek](wikipedia://en/1820134), [Gordon Welchman](wikipedia://en/2385693), [Hugh Alexander](wikipedia://en/2784711), [Donald Michie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Michie), [Bill Tutte](wikipedia://en/52021) and [Stuart Milner-Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Milner-Barry).

:::

The team at Bletchley Park, [75% women](wikipedia://en/54254691), devised automatic machinery to help with decryption, culminating in the development of [Colossus](wikipedia://en/6229), the world's first programmable digital electronic computer. Codebreaking operations at Bletchley Park ended in 1946 and all information about the wartime operations was classified until the mid-1970s. After the war it had various uses and now houses the Bletchley Park museum.

## House and pre-war history {#House_and_pre-war_history}

It was first known as Bletchley Park after its purchase in 1877 by the architect [Samuel Lipscomb Seckham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Lipscomb_Seckham),[^o4] who built a house there.[^o5] The estate of 581 acres was bought in 1883 by Sir [Herbert Samuel Leon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Samuel_Leon), who expanded the house[^o6] into what architect [Landis Gores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landis_Gores) called a "maudlin and monstrous pile",[^o7][^o8] combining [Victorian Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_Gothic), [Tudor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_Revival_architecture), and [Dutch Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Baroque_architecture) styles.[^o9] After the death of Herbert Leon in 1926, the estate continued to be occupied by his widow Fanny Leon (née Higham) until her death in 1937.[^o10] In 1938, the mansion and much of the site was bought by a builder for a housing estate, but in May 1938 Admiral Sir [Hugh Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Sinclair), head of the [Secret Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Intelligence_Service) (SIS or [MI6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI6)), bought the site for use in the event of war.[^o11]

## World War II {#World_War_II}

Government Code and Cypher School

### Preparation for War {#Preparation_for_War}

*Image: bp-polish-codebreakers-plaque.jpg.* Bletchley's Polish Memorial, commemorating "the \[prewar\] work of [Marian Rejewski](wikipedia://en/4349420), [Jerzy Różycki](wikipedia://en/197851) and [Henryk Zygalski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henryk_Zygalski), mathematicians of the [Polish intelligence service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biuro_Szyfrów), in first breaking the Enigma code. Their work greatly assisted the Bletchley Park code breakers and contributed to the Allied victory in World War II."

Sinclair bought the mansion and 58 acre of land for use by SIS. A key advantage seen by Sinclair and his colleagues (inspecting the site under the cover of "Captain Ridley's shooting party")[^o14] was Bletchley's geographical centrality. It was almost immediately adjacent to [Bletchley railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bletchley_railway_station), where the "[Varsity Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varsity_Line)" between [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford) and [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge) – whose universities were expected to supply many of the code-breakers – met the main [West Coast railway line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Coast_Main_Line) connecting London, [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham), [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester), [Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool), [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow) and [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh). [Watling Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watling_Street), the main road linking London to the north-west (subsequently the [A5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A5_road_%28Great_Britain%29)) was close by, and high-volume communication links were available at the telegraph and telephone repeater station in nearby [Fenny Stratford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenny_Stratford).[^o15] Sinclair bought the park using £6,000 (£UK today) of his own money as the Government said they did not have the budget to do so.[^o11]

### Section D and the explosives school {#Section_D_and_the_explosives_school}

Before the site was considered for use by GC&CS, it had been initially selected by [Laurence Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Grand) and established to be a school for his own section of the SIS called the [Section for Destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Section_for_Destruction), or Section D, a paramilitary outfit specialising in [irregular warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irregular_warfare), [guerrilla warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guerrilla_warfare), and [sabotage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabotage). This site was selected to be an experimental [research and development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_and_development) station and [explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive) schoolhouse for a rather pyrotechnical team belonging to Section D. Modern sabotage from a scientific approach was a brand new field of science, and Bletchley was only one of many such early locations where new ingenious devices were studied.[^o17] Grand was asked once to explain the conception behind this outfit, to which his response was: "We just want to blow off people's hats."[^o18]

They began their programs here on 15 June 1939. The chemical engineer Colin Meek (D/X1), a recent graduate from [Manchester University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_University), employee of [Imperial Chemical Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Chemical_Industries) and one of the early members of the team to invent [plastic explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_explosive), was selected to train recruits in the composition and usage of [demolitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demolitions) and [explosives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosives) under the explosives program schoolmaster, Dr. Drane (D/X).[^o17]

However, when the war officially began, and the codebreakers finally moved in, they constantly complained that the noise of the explosives being tested by Section D made them lose their concentrations. The explosives testing of Section D was then moved to [Aston House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aston_House) in November, several months after the codebreakers had set up shop.[^o17]

### Early work {#Early_work}

Five weeks before the [outbreak of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland), Warsaw's [Cipher Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biuro_Szyfrów) revealed [its achievements](wikipedia://en/872175) in breaking Enigma to astonished French and British personnel.[^o67] The British used the Poles' information and techniques, and the [Enigma clone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Enigma_doubles) sent to them in August 1939, which greatly increased their (previously very limited) success in decrypting Enigma messages.[^o20]

*Image: Information Flow Bletchley Park Enigma Messages.png.* Flow of information from an intercepted Enigma message[^o21]

The first personnel of the [Government Code and Cypher School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Code_and_Cypher_School) (GC&CS) moved to Bletchley Park on 15 August 1939. The Naval, Military, and Air Sections were on the ground floor of the mansion, together with a telephone exchange, teleprinter room, kitchen, and dining room; the top floor was allocated to [MI6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI6). Construction of the wooden huts began in late 1939, and Elmers School, a neighbouring boys' boarding school in a Victorian Gothic redbrick building by a church, was acquired for the Commercial and Diplomatic Sections.[^o22]

The only direct enemy damage to the site was done 20–21 November 1940 by three bombs probably intended for [Bletchley railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bletchley_railway_station); Hut 4, shifted two feet off its foundation, was winched back into place as work inside continued.[^o23]

### Action This Day {#Action_This_Day}

During a morale-boosting visit on 9 September 1941, [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) reportedly remarked to Denniston or Menzies: "I told you to leave no stone unturned to get staff, but I had no idea you had taken me so literally."[^o25] Six weeks later, having failed to get sufficient typing and unskilled staff to achieve the productivity that was possible, Turing, Welchman, Alexander and Milner-Barry wrote directly to Churchill. His response was "Action this day make sure they have all they want on extreme priority and report to me that this has been done."[^o26]

### Allied involvement {#Allied_involvement}

After the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) joined World War II, a number of American [cryptographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographer) were posted to [Hut 3](wikipedia://en/31026380), and from May 1943 onwards there was close co-operation between British and American intelligence[^o28] leading to the [1943 BRUSA Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1943_BRUSA_Agreement) which was the forerunner of the [Five Eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Eyes) partnership.[^o29]

In contrast, the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) was never officially told of Bletchley Park and its activities, a reflection of Churchill's distrust of the Soviets even during the US-UK-USSR alliance imposed by the Nazi threat.[^o30] However Bletchley Park was infiltrated by the Soviet mole [John Cairncross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cairncross), a member of the [Cambridge Spy Ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Spy_Ring), who leaked Ultra material to Moscow.[^n1]

### Personnel {#Personnel}

*Image: AlanTuring-Bletchley.jpg.* [Stephen Kettle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Kettle)'s 2007 [*Alan Turing* statue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statue_of_Alan_Turing,_Bletchley_Park)

Admiral [Hugh Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Sinclair) was the founder and head of GC&CS between 1919 and 1938 with Commander [Alastair Denniston](wikipedia://en/2634891) being operational head of the organisation from 1919 to 1942, beginning with its formation from the [Admiralty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Admiralty)'s [Room 40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room_40) (NID25) and the [War Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_Office)'s [MI1b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI1).[^o33] Key GC&CS [cryptanalysts](wikipedia://en/5715) who moved from London to Bletchley Park included [John Tiltman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tiltman), [Dillwyn "Dilly" Knox](wikipedia://en/905870), [Josh Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Cooper_%28cryptographer%29), [Oliver Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Strachey) and [Nigel de Grey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_de_Grey). These people had a variety of backgrounds – linguists and chess champions were common, and Knox's field was [papyrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrology). The British War Office recruited top solvers of [cryptic crossword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptic_crossword) puzzles, as these individuals had strong [lateral thinking](wikipedia://en/298235) skills.[^o34]

On [the day Britain declared war on Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom_declaration_of_war_on_Germany_%281939%29), Denniston wrote to the [Foreign Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Office) about recruiting "men of the professor type".[^o35] Personal networking drove early recruitments, particularly of men from the universities of Cambridge and Oxford. Trustworthy women were similarly recruited for administrative and clerical jobs.[^o36] In one 1941 recruiting stratagem, *[The Daily Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Telegraph)* was asked to organise a crossword competition, after which promising contestants were discreetly approached about "a particular type of work as a contribution to the war effort".[^o37]

Denniston recognised, however, that the enemy's use of electromechanical cipher machines meant that formally trained mathematicians would also be needed;[^o38] Oxford's [Peter Twinn](wikipedia://en/1180404) joined GC&CS in February 1939;[^o39] Cambridge's [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208)[^o40] and [Gordon Welchman](wikipedia://en/2385693)[^o41] began training in 1938 and reported to Bletchley the day after war was declared, along with [John Jeffreys](wikipedia://en/8315517). Later-recruited cryptanalysts included the mathematicians [Derek Taunt](wikipedia://en/906145),[^o42] [Jack Good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.J._Good), [Bill Tutte](wikipedia://en/52021),[^o43] and [Max Newman](wikipedia://en/19867); historian [Harry Hinsley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Hinsley), and chess champions [Hugh Alexander](wikipedia://en/2784711) and [Stuart Milner-Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Milner-Barry).[^o44] [Joan Clarke](wikipedia://en/40962833) was one of the few women employed at Bletchley as a full-fledged cryptanalyst.[^o45][^o46]

When seeking to recruit more suitably advanced linguists, [John Tiltman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tiltman) turned to [Patrick Wilkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Wilkinson_%28scholar%29) of the Italian section for advice, and he suggested asking [Lord Lindsay of Birker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandie_Lindsay,_1st_Baron_Lindsay_of_Birker), of [Balliol College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balliol_College,_Oxford), S. W. Grose, and [Martin Charlesworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Charlesworth), of [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge), to recommend classical scholars or applicants to their colleges.[^o47]

The staff of "[Boffins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boffin) and [Debs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deb_%28society%29)" (scientists and young women of high society)[^o48] nicknamed GC&CS as the "Golf, Cheese and Chess Society".[^o49] Among those who worked there and later became famous in other fields were historian [Asa Briggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asa_Briggs), politician [Roy Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Jenkins) and novelist [Angus Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angus_Wilson).[^o50]

After initial training at the Inter-Service Special Intelligence School set up by [John Tiltman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tiltman) (initially at an RAF depot in Buckingham and later in [Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedford) – where it was known locally as "the Spy School")[^o51] staff worked a six-day week, rotating through three shifts: 4 p.m. to midnight, midnight to 8 a.m. (the most disliked shift), and 8 a.m. to 4 p.m., each with a half-hour meal break. At the end of the third week, a worker went off at 8 a.m. and came back at 4 p.m., thus putting in 16 hours on that last day. The irregular hours affected workers' health and social life, as well as the routines of the nearby homes at which most staff lodged. The work was tedious and demanded intense concentration; staff got one week's leave four times a year, but some "girls" collapsed and required extended rest.[^o52] Recruitment took place to combat a shortage of experts in Morse code and German.[^o53]

In January 1945, at the peak of codebreaking efforts, 8,995 personnel were working at Bletchley and its outstations.[^n2] About three-quarters of these were women.[^o55] Many of the women came from middle-class backgrounds and held degrees in the areas of mathematics, physics and engineering; they were given the chance due to the lack of men, who had been sent to war. They performed calculations and coding and hence were integral to the computing processes.[^o56] Among them were [Eleanor Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleanor_Ireland), who worked on the [Colossus computers](wikipedia://en/6229)[^o57] and [Ruth Briggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audrey_Ruth_Briggs), a German scholar, who worked within the Naval Section.[^o58][^o59]

The female staff in Dilwyn Knox's section were sometimes termed "Dilly's Fillies".[^o60] Knox's methods enabled [Mavis Lever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mavis_Lever) (who married mathematician and fellow code-breaker [Keith Batey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Batey)) and [Margaret Rock](wikipedia://en/40910655) to solve a German code, the [Abwehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abwehr) cipher.[^o61][^o62]

Many of the women had backgrounds in languages, particularly French, German and Italian. Among them were [Rozanne Colchester](wikipedia://en/50156020), a translator who worked mainly for the Italian air forces Section,[^o63] and [Cicely Mayhew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicely_Mayhew), recruited straight from university, who worked in Hut 8, translating decoded German Navy signals,[^o64] as did [Jane Fawcett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Fawcett) (née Hughes) who decrypted a vital message concerning the [German battleship *Bismarck*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_battleship_Bismarck) and after the war became an opera singer and buildings conservationist.[^o50]

[Alan Brooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Brooke) (CIGS) in his secret wartime diary frequently refers to "intercepts":[^n3]

16 April 1942: *Took lunch in car and went to see the organization for breaking down ciphers \[Bletchley Park\] – a wonderful set of professors and genii! I marvel at the work they succeed in doing.*

28 June 1945: *After lunch (with Andrew Cunningham (RN) and Sinclair (RAF) we went to "The Park" … I began by addressing some 400 of the workers who consist of all 3 services, both sexes, and civilians, They come from every sort of walk of life, professors, students, actors, dancers, mathematicians, electricians signallers, etc. I thanked them on behalf of the Chiefs of Staff and congratulated them on the results of their work. We then toured round the establishment and had tea before returning.*

### Secrecy {#Secrecy}

Properly used, the German Enigma and [Lorenz ciphers](wikipedia://en/301173) should have been virtually unbreakable, but flaws in German cryptographic procedures, and poor discipline among the personnel carrying them out, created vulnerabilities that made Bletchley's attacks just barely feasible. These vulnerabilities, however, could have been remedied by relatively simple improvements in enemy procedures,[^o67] and such changes would certainly have been implemented had Germany had any hint of Bletchley's success. Thus the intelligence Bletchley produced was considered wartime Britain's "[Ultra](wikipedia://en/31748) secret" – higher even than the normally highest classification *Most Secret* – and security was paramount.[^o68]

All staff signed the [Official Secrets Act (1939)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Official_Secrets_Act_1939) and a 1942 security warning emphasised the importance of discretion even within Bletchley itself: "Do not talk at meals. Do not talk in the transport. Do not talk travelling. Do not talk in the billet. Do not talk by your own fireside. Be careful even in your Hut ..."[^o69]

Nevertheless, there were security leaks. [Jock Colville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jock_Colville), the Assistant Private Secretary to [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill), recorded in his diary on 31 July 1941, that the newspaper proprietor [Lord Camrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Camrose) had discovered Ultra and that security leaks "increase in number and seriousness".[^n4]

Despite the high degree of secrecy surrounding Bletchley Park during the Second World War, unique and hitherto unknown amateur film footage of the outstation at nearby [Whaddon Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaddon_Hall) came to light in 2020, after being anonymously donated to the Bletchley Park Trust.[^o71][^o72] A spokesman for the Trust noted the film's existence was all the more incredible because it was "very, very rare even to have \[still\] photographs" of the park and its associated sites.[^o73]

Bletchley Park was known as "B.P." to those who worked there.[^o74] "[Station X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_SOE_establishments)" (X = [Roman numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numeral) ten), "London Signals Intelligence Centre", and "[Government Communications Headquarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Headquarters)" were all cover names used during the war.[^o75] The formal posting of the many "Wrens" – members of the [Women's Royal Naval Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Royal_Naval_Service) – working there, was to [HMS *Pembroke V*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Eastcote). Royal Air Force names of Bletchley Park and its outstations included [RAF Eastcote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Eastcote), RAF Lime Grove and RAF Church Green.[^o76] The postal address that staff had to use was "Room 47, Foreign Office".[^o77]

### Intelligence reporting {#Intelligence_reporting}

*Image: Ultra Hut3 Graph.png.* Number of signals despatched daily from Bletchley Park Hut 3 during the Second World War[^o79]

Initially, when only a very limited amount of Enigma traffic was being read,[^o80] deciphered non-Naval Enigma messages were sent from [Hut&nbsp;6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut&nbsp;6) to [Hut 3](wikipedia://en/31026380) which handled their translation and onward transmission. Subsequently, under [Group Captain Eric Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Malcolm_Jones), Hut 3 expanded to become the heart of Bletchley Park's intelligence effort, with input from decrypts of "[Tunny](wikipedia://en/301173)" (Lorenz SZ42) traffic and many other sources. Early in 1942 it moved into Block D, but its functions were still referred to as Hut 3.[^n5]

Hut 3 contained a number of sections: Air Section "3A", Military Section "3M", a small Naval Section "3N", a multi-service Research Section "3G" and a large liaison section "3L".[^n6] It also housed the Traffic Analysis Section, SIXTA.[^n7] An important function that allowed the synthesis of raw messages into valuable [Military intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_intelligence) was the indexing and cross-referencing of information in a number of different filing systems.[^o84] Intelligence reports were sent out to the Secret Intelligence Service, the intelligence chiefs in the relevant ministries, and later on to high-level commanders in the field.[^o85]

Naval Enigma deciphering was in [Hut&nbsp;8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut&nbsp;8), with translation in [Hut&nbsp;4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut&nbsp;4). Verbatim translations were sent to the [Naval Intelligence Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Intelligence_Division_%28United_Kingdom%29) (NID) of the Admiralty's Operational Intelligence Centre (OIC), supplemented by information from indexes as to the meaning of technical terms and cross-references from a knowledge store of German naval technology.[^o86] Where relevant to non-naval matters, they would also be passed to Hut 3. Hut 4 also decoded a manual system known as the dockyard cipher, which sometimes carried messages that were also sent on an Enigma network. Feeding these back to Hut 8 provided excellent "cribs" for [Known-plaintext attacks](wikipedia://en/517019) on the daily naval Enigma key.[^o87]

### Listening stations {#Listening_stations}

Initially, a [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio) room was established at Bletchley Park. It was set up in the mansion's water tower under the code name "Station X",[^o89] a term now sometimes applied to the codebreaking efforts at Bletchley as a whole. The "X" is the [Roman numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numeral) "ten", this being the Secret Intelligence Service's tenth such station. Due to the long radio aerials stretching from the wireless room, the radio station was moved from Bletchley Park to nearby [Whaddon Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whaddon_Hall) to avoid drawing attention to the site.[^o90][^o91]

Subsequently, other listening stations – the [Y-stations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-stations), such as the ones at [Chicksands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Chicksands) in Bedfordshire, [Beaumanor Hall](wikipedia://en/831408), Leicestershire (where the headquarters of the War Office "Y" Group was located) and [Beeston Hill Y Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beeston_Hill_Y_Station) in Norfolk – gathered raw signals for processing at Bletchley. Coded messages were taken down by hand and sent to Bletchley on paper by motorcycle [despatch riders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Despatch_rider) or (later) by teleprinter.[^o92]

### Additional buildings {#Additional_buildings}

The wartime needs required the building of additional accommodation.[^o95]

#### Huts {#Huts}

*Image: Hut-1.jpg.* Hut 1

*Image: BletchleyPark Hut4 01.JPG.* Hut 4, adjacent to the mansion, is now a bar and restaurant for the museum.

*Image: Hut6.jpg.* Hut 6 in 2004

Often a hut's number became so strongly associated with the work performed inside that even when the work was moved to another building it was still referred to by the original "Hut" designation.[^o97][^o98]

- *Hut 1*: The first hut, built in 1939[^o99] used to house the Wireless Station for a short time,[^o89] later administrative functions such as transport, typing, and Bombe maintenance. The first Bombe, "Victory", was initially housed here.[^o100]
- *Hut 2*: A recreational hut for "beer, tea, and relaxation".[^o101]
- *[Hut 3](wikipedia://en/31026380)*: Intelligence: translation and analysis of Army and Air Force decrypts[^o102]
- *[Hut 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_4)*: Naval intelligence: analysis of Naval Enigma and [Hagelin](wikipedia://en/3165135) decrypts[^o103]
- *Hut 5*: Military intelligence including Italian, Spanish, and Portuguese ciphers and German police codes.[^o104]
- *[Hut 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_6)*: Cryptanalysis of Army and Air Force Enigma[^o105]
- *[Hut 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_7)*: Cryptanalysis of [Japanese naval codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_naval_codes) and intelligence.[^o106][^o107]
- *[Hut 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_8)*: Cryptanalysis of Naval Enigma.[^o86]
- *Hut 9*: ISOS (Intelligence Section Oliver Strachey).
- *Hut 10*: [Secret Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Intelligence_Service) (SIS or MI6) codes, Air and Meteorological sections.[^o108]
- *Hut 11*: Bombe building.[^o109]
- *Hut 14*: Communications centre.[^o110]
- *Hut 15*: SIXTA (Signals Intelligence and Traffic Analysis).
- *Hut 16*: ISK (Intelligence Service [Knox](wikipedia://en/905870)) [Abwehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abwehr) ciphers.
- *Hut 18*: ISOS (Intelligence Section Oliver Strachey).
- *Hut 23*: Primarily used to house the engineering department. After February 1943, Hut 3 was renamed Hut 23.

#### Blocks {#Blocks}

In addition to the wooden huts, there were a number of brick-built "blocks".

- *Block A*: Naval Intelligence.
- *Block B*: Italian Air and Naval, and Japanese code breaking.
- *Block C*: Stored the substantial punch-card indexes.
- *Block D*: From February 1943 it housed those from Hut 3, who synthesised intelligence from multiple sources, Huts 6 and 8 and SIXTA.[^n8]
- *Block E*: Incoming and outgoing Radio Transmission and TypeX.
- *Block F*: Included the [Newmanry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newmanry) and [Testery](wikipedia://en/9285787), and Japanese Military Air Section. It has since been demolished.
- *Block G*: Traffic analysis and deception operations.
- *Block H*: *Tunny* and Colossus (now [The National Museum of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Museum_of_Computing)).

### Work on specific countries' signals {#Work_on_specific_countries'_signals}

#### German signals {#German_signals}

Most German messages decrypted at Bletchley were produced by one or another version of the [Enigma](wikipedia://en/9256) cipher machine, but an important minority were produced by the even more complicated twelve-rotor [Lorenz SZ42 on-line teleprinter cipher machine](wikipedia://en/301173) used for high command messages, known as [Fish](wikipedia://en/294139).[^o115]

*Image: RebuiltBombeFrontView.jpg.* The working rebuilt [bombe](wikipedia://en/615354), built by a team led by [John Harper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harper_%28computer_engineer%29)[^o116] and switched on by [the Duke of Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward,_Duke_of_Kent), patron of the [British Computer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Computer_Society), on 17 July 2008. This is now located at [The National Museum of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Museum_of_Computing) in Block H on Bletchley Park.

The [bombe](wikipedia://en/615354) was an electromechanical device whose function was to discover some of the daily settings of the Enigma machines on the various German military [networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_network).[^o117][^o118][^o119] Its pioneering design was developed by [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) (with an important contribution from Gordon Welchman) and the machine was engineered by [Harold 'Doc' Keen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Keen) of the [British Tabulating Machine Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Tabulating_Machine_Company). Each machine was about 7 ft high and wide, 2 ft deep and weighed about a ton.[^o120]

At its peak, GC&CS was reading approximately 4,000 messages per day.[^o121] As a hedge against enemy attack[^o122] most bombes were dispersed to installations at [Adstock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adstock) and [Wavendon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavendon) (both later supplanted by installations at [Stanmore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanmore) and [Eastcote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastcote)), and [Gayhurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gayhurst).[^o123][^o124]

[Luftwaffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luftwaffe) messages were the first to be read in quantity. The German navy had much tighter procedures, and the capture of code books was needed before they could be broken. When, in February 1942, the German navy introduced the four-rotor Enigma for communications with its Atlantic U-boats, this traffic became unreadable for a period of ten months.[^o125] Britain produced modified bombes, but it was the success of the [US Navy Bombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Navy_Bombe) that was the main source of reading messages from this version of Enigma for the rest of the war. Messages were sent to and fro across the Atlantic by enciphered teleprinter links.[^o92]

Bletchley's work was essential to defeating the [U-boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat) in the [Battle of the Atlantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Atlantic), and to the British naval victories in the [Battle of Cape Matapan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cape_Matapan) and the [Battle of North Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_North_Cape). In 1941, Ultra exerted a powerful effect on the [North African desert campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_African_Campaign) against German forces under General [Erwin Rommel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Rommel). General Sir [Claude Auchinleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Auchinleck) wrote that were it not for Ultra, "Rommel would have certainly got through to Cairo". While not changing the events, "[Ultra](wikipedia://en/31748)" decrypts featured prominently in the story of [Operation SALAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Salaam), [László Almásy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/László_Almásy)'s mission across [the desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Desert_%28Egypt%29) behind Allied lines in 1942.[^o126] Prior to the [Normandy landings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normandy_landings) on D-Day in June 1944, the Allies knew the locations of all but two of Germany's fifty-eight Western-front divisions.[^o127]

*Image: Colossus.jpg.* A Mark 2 Colossus computer. The ten Colossi were the world's first (semi-) programmable electronic computers, the first having been built in 1943.

The [Lorenz messages](wikipedia://en/33647361) were codenamed *Tunny* at Bletchley Park. They were only sent in quantity from mid-1942. The Tunny networks were used for high-level messages between German High Command and field commanders. With the help of German operator errors, the cryptanalysts in the [Testery](wikipedia://en/9285787) (named after [Ralph Tester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Tester), its head) worked out the logical structure of the machine despite not knowing its physical form. They devised automatic machinery to help with decryption, which culminated in [Colossus](wikipedia://en/6229), the world's first programmable digital electronic computer. This was designed and built by [Tommy Flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommy_Flowers) and his team at the [Post Office Research Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_Office_Research_Station) at [Dollis Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollis_Hill). The prototype first worked in December 1943, was delivered to Bletchley Park in January and first worked operationally on 5 February 1944. Enhancements were developed for the Mark 2 Colossus, the first of which was working at Bletchley Park on the morning of 1 June in time for [D-day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normandy_landings). Flowers then produced one Colossus a month for the rest of the war, making a total of ten with an eleventh part-built. The machines were operated mainly by Wrens in a section named the [Newmanry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newmanry) after its head [Max Newman](wikipedia://en/19867).[^o128]

#### Italian signals {#Italian_signals}

Italian signals had been of interest since Italy's attack on Abyssinia in 1935. During the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War) the [Italian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Italian_Navy) used the K model of the commercial Enigma without a plugboard; this was solved by Knox in 1937. When Italy entered the war in 1940 an improved version of the machine was used, though little traffic was sent by it and there were "wholesale changes" in Italian codes and cyphers.[^o130]

Knox was given a new section for work on Enigma variations, which he staffed with women ("Dilly's girls"), who included [Margaret Rock](wikipedia://en/40910655), Jean Perrin, Clare Harding, Rachel Ronald, Elisabeth Granger; and [Mavis Lever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mavis_Batey).[^o131] Mavis Lever solved the signals revealing the Italian Navy's operational plans before the [Battle of Cape Matapan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cape_Matapan) in 1941, leading to a British victory.[^o132]

Although most Bletchley staff did not know the results of their work, Admiral [Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Cunningham,_1st_Viscount_Cunningham_of_Hyndhope) visited Bletchley in person a few weeks later to congratulate them.[^o132]

On entering World War II in June 1940, the [Italians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy) were using book codes for most of their military messages. The exception was the [Italian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Navy), which after the Battle of Cape Matapan started using the [C-38](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-38_%28cipher_machine%29) version of the [Boris Hagelin](wikipedia://en/578033) [rotor-based](wikipedia://en/196223) [cipher machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cipher_machine), particularly to route their navy and merchant marine convoys to the conflict in North Africa.[^o133] As a consequence, [JRM Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Ramsay_Montagu_Butler) recruited his former student [Bernard Willson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Willson) to join a team with two others in Hut 4.[^o103][^o134] In June 1941, Willson became the first of the team to decode the Hagelin system, thus enabling military commanders to direct the [Royal Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Navy) and [Royal Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Air_Force) to sink enemy ships carrying supplies from Europe to Rommel's [Afrika Korps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrika_Korps). This led to increased shipping losses and, from reading the intercepted traffic, the team learnt that between May and September 1941 the stock of fuel for the [Luftwaffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luftwaffe) in North Africa reduced by 90 per cent.[^o135] After an intensive language course, in March 1944 Willson switched to Japanese language-based codes.

A Middle East Intelligence Centre (MEIC) was set up in Cairo in 1939. When Italy entered the war in June 1940, delays in forwarding intercepts to Bletchley via congested radio links resulted in cryptanalysts being sent to Cairo. A Combined Bureau Middle East (CBME) was set up in November, though the Middle East authorities made "increasingly bitter complaints" that GC&CS was giving too little priority to work on Italian cyphers. However, the principle of concentrating high-grade cryptanalysis at Bletchley was maintained.[^o137] [John Chadwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chadwick) started cryptanalysis work in 1942 on Italian signals at the naval base 'HMS Nile' in Alexandria. Later, he was with GC&CS; in the Heliopolis Museum, Cairo and then in the Villa Laurens, Alexandria.[^o138]

#### Soviet signals {#Soviet_signals}

Soviet signals had been studied since the 1920s. In 1939–40, [John Tiltman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tiltman) (who had worked on Russian Army traffic from 1930) set up two Russian sections at Wavendon (a country house near Bletchley) and at [Sarafand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarafand_al-Amar) in Palestine. Two Russian high-grade army and navy systems were broken early in 1940. Tiltman spent two weeks in Finland, where he obtained Russian traffic from Finland and Estonia in exchange for radio equipment. In June 1941, when the Soviet Union became an ally, Churchill ordered a halt to intelligence operations against it. In December 1941, the Russian section was closed down, but in late summer 1943 or late 1944, a small GC&CS Russian cypher section was set up in London overlooking Park Lane, then in Sloane Square.[^o140]

#### Japanese signals {#Japanese_signals}

An outpost of the Government Code and Cypher School had been set up in Hong Kong in 1935, the [Far East Combined Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_East_Combined_Bureau) (FECB). The FECB naval staff moved in 1940 to Singapore, then [Colombo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombo), [Ceylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka), then [Kilindini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilindini_Harbour), [Mombasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mombasa), Kenya. They succeeded in deciphering Japanese codes with a mixture of skill and good fortune.[^o142] The Army and Air Force staff went from Singapore to the [Wireless Experimental Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_Experimental_Centre) at [Delhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delhi), India.[^o143]

In early 1942, a six-month crash course in Japanese, for 20 undergraduates from Oxford and Cambridge, was started by the Inter-Services Special Intelligence School in Bedford, in a building across from the main Post Office. This course was repeated every six months until war's end. Most of those completing these courses worked on decoding Japanese naval messages in [Hut&nbsp;7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut&nbsp;7), under [John Tiltman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tiltman).[^o143]

By mid-1945, well over 100 personnel were involved with this operation, which co-operated closely with the FECB and the US Signal intelligence Service at [Arlington Hall](wikipedia://en/340801), Virginia. In 1999, Michael Smith wrote that: "Only now are the British codebreakers (like [John Tiltman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tiltman), [Hugh Foss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Foss), and [Eric Nave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Nave)) beginning to receive the recognition they deserve for breaking Japanese codes and cyphers".[^o144]

## Post-war uses and legacy {#Post-war_uses_and_legacy}

The Government Code & Cypher School became the Government Communications Headquarters ([GCHQ](wikipedia://en/12884)), moving to [Eastcote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastcote) in 1946 and to [Cheltenham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheltenham) in 1951.[^o146]

### Continued secrecy and official recognition {#Continued_secrecy_and_official_recognition}

*Image: Commemorative medal for those working at Bletchley Park, awarded to Joyce Gladys Dickson (nee Day).jpg.* Commemorative medal for those working at Bletchley Park

Until the mid 1970s the [thirty year rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirty_year_rule) meant that there was no official mention of the work done at Bletchley Park. This meant that there were many operations where codes broken by Bletchley Park played an important role, but this was not present in the history of those events.[^o148]

After the War, the secrecy imposed on Bletchley staff remained in force, so that most relatives never knew more than that a child, spouse, or parent had done some kind of secret war work.[^o149] Churchill referred to the Bletchley staff as "the geese that laid the golden eggs and never cackled".[^o150] That said, occasional mentions of the work performed at Bletchley Park slipped the censor's net and appeared in print.[^o151]

With the publication of [F. W. Winterbotham's](wikipedia://en/2918883) *The Ultra Secret* in 1974[^o152] public discussion of Bletchley Park's work in the English speaking world finally became accepted, although some former staff considered themselves bound to silence forever.[^o153] Winterbotham's book was written from memory and although officially allowed, there was no access to archives.[^n9]

Not until July 2009 did the British government fully acknowledge the contribution of the many people working at Bletchley Park.[^o155] Only then was a commemorative medal struck to be presented to those involved.[^o156] The gilded medal bears the inscription *GC&CS 1939–1945 Bletchley Park and its Outstations*.[^o157]

### Decline and partial demolition {#Decline_and_partial_demolition}

The site passed through a succession of hands and saw a number of uses, including as a teacher-training college, and by various government agencies, including the [GPO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Post_Office) and the [Civil Aviation Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Aviation_Authority). One large building, block F, was demolished in 1987 by which time the site was being run down with tenants leaving.[^o159]

### Bletchley Park museums {#Bletchley_Park_museums}

*Image: Turing flat.jpg.* The stableyard cottages, where Alan Turing worked

Bletchley Park Trust was set up in 1991 by a group of people who recognised the site's importance,[^n10] as it was at risk of being sold off for housing.[^o162] and in February 1992, the [Milton Keynes Borough Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Keynes_Borough_Council) declared most of the Park a [conservation area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_area).[^o163]

The site opened to visitors in 1993, and was formally inaugurated by the [Duke of Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Edward,_Duke_of_Kent) as Chief Patron in July 1994[^o164] and [Tony Sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Sale) became the first director of the Bletchley Park Museums in 1994.[^o165]

In 1999 the land owners, the [Property Advisors to the Civil Estate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Government_Commerce) and [BT Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BT_Group), granted a lease to the Trust giving it control over most of the site.[^o166]

June 2014 saw the completion of an £8 million restoration project by museum design specialist, [Event Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_Communications), which was marked by a visit from [Catherine, Duchess of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine,_Duchess_of_Cambridge).[^o167] The Duchess' paternal grandmother, [Valerie Middleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middleton_family#Capt._Peter_and_Valerie_Middleton), and Valerie's twin sister, Mary (*née* Glassborow), both worked at Bletchley Park during the war. The twin sisters worked as Foreign Office Civilians in [Hut 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_6), where they managed the interception of enemy and neutral diplomatic signals for decryption. Valerie married Catherine's grandfather, Captain [Peter Middleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middleton_family#Capt._Peter_and_Valerie_Middleton).[^o168][^o169][^o170] A [memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorial) at Bletchley Park commemorates Mary and Valerie Middleton's work as [code-breakers](wikipedia://en/5715).[^o171]

*Image: StoryOfEnigma.jpg.* The Story of Enigma workshop with [Middlesex University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex_University) students

The Bletchley Park Learning Department offers educational group visits with active learning activities for schools and universities. Visits can be booked in advance during term time, where students can engage with the history of Bletchley Park and understand its wider relevance for computer history and national security. Their workshops cover introductions to codebreaking, cyber security and the story of [Enigma](wikipedia://en/9256) and [Lorenz](wikipedia://en/301173).[^o172]

### Funding {#Funding}

In October 2005, American billionaire [Sidney Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Frank) donated £500,000 to Bletchley Park Trust to fund a new Science Centre dedicated to [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208).[^o174] [Simon Greenish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Greenish) joined as Director in 2006 to lead the fund-raising effort[^o175] in a post he held until 2012 when Iain Standen took over the leadership role.[^o176] In July 2008, a letter to *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times)* from more than a hundred academics condemned the neglect of the site.[^o177][^o178] In September 2008, [PGP](wikipedia://en/23080), [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) and other technology firms announced a fund-raising campaign to repair the facility.[^o179] On 6 November 2008, [English Heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Heritage) announced that it would donate £300,000 to help maintain the buildings at Bletchley Park, and that they were in discussions regarding the donation of a further £600,000.[^o180]

In October 2011, the Bletchley Park Trust received a £4.6 million [Heritage Lottery Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritage_Lottery_Fund) grant to be used "to complete the restoration of the site, and to tell its story to the highest modern standards" on the condition that £1.7 million of match funding is raised by the Bletchley Park Trust.[^o181][^o182] Just weeks later, Google contributed £550,000[^o183] and by June 2012 the trust had successfully raised £2.4 million to unlock the grants to restore Huts 3 and 6, as well as develop its exhibition centre in Block C.[^o184]

Additional income is raised by renting Block H to the National Museum of Computing, and some office space in various parts of the park to private firms.[^o185][^o186][^o187]

Due to the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic) the Trust expected to lose more than £2 million in 2020 and be required to cut a third of its workforce. Former MP [John Leech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Leech_%28politician%29) asked [Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon_%28company%29), [Apple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc.), [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google), [Facebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook) and [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft) to donate £400,000 each to secure the future of the Trust. Leech had led the successful campaign to pardon [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) and implement [Turing's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing_law).[^o188]

## Other organisations sharing the campus {#Other_organisations_sharing_the_campus}

### The National Museum of Computing {#The_National_Museum_of_Computing}

*Image: Bombe-rebuild.jpg.* Rear of the rebuilt [Bombe](wikipedia://en/615354), now located at [the National Museum of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Museum_of_Computing), a separate museum on the Bletchley Park site in Block H[^o191]

*Image: ColossusRebuild 11.jpg.* [Tony Sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Sale) supervising the breaking of an enciphered message with the completed [Colossus computer](wikipedia://en/6229) rebuild in 2006 at [The National Museum of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Museum_of_Computing)

The National Museum of Computing is housed in Block H, which is rented from the Bletchley Park Trust. Its Colossus and Tunny galleries tell an important part of allied breaking of German codes during World War II. There is a working reconstruction of a Bombe and a rebuilt [Colossus computer](wikipedia://en/6229) which was used on the high-level [Lorenz cipher](wikipedia://en/301173), codenamed *Tunny* by the British.[^o192][^o193]

The museum, which opened in 2007, is an independent voluntary organisation that is governed by its own board of trustees. Its aim is "To collect and restore computer systems particularly those developed in Britain and to enable people to explore that collection for inspiration, learning and enjoyment."[^o194] Through its many exhibits, the museum displays the story of computing through the mainframes of the 1960s and 1970s, and the rise of personal computing in the 1980s. It has a policy of having as many of the exhibits as possible in full working order.[^o195]

### Science and Innovation Centre {#Science_and_Innovation_Centre}

This consisted of serviced office accommodation housed in Bletchley Park's Blocks A and E, and the upper floors of the Mansion. Its aim was to foster the growth and development of dynamic knowledge-based start-ups and other businesses.[^o197] It closed in 2021 and blocks A and E were taken into use as part of the museum.[^o198]

### Proposed National College of Cyber Security {#Proposed_National_College_of_Cyber_Security}

In April 2020 Bletchley Park Capital Partners, a private company run by Tim Reynolds, Deputy Chairman of the National Museum of Computing, announced plans to sell off the [freehold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freehold_%28law%29) to part of the site containing former Block G for commercial development. Offers of between £4 million and £6 million were reportedly being sought for the 3 [acre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acre) plot, for which planning permission for employment purposes was granted in 2005.[^o200][^o201] Previously, the construction of a [National College of Cyber Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_College_of_Cyber_Security) for students aged from 16 to 19 years old had been envisaged on the site, to be housed in Block G after renovation with funds supplied by the Bletchley Park Science and Innovation Centre.[^o202][^o203][^o204][^o205]

### RSGB National Radio Centre {#RSGB_National_Radio_Centre}

The [Radio Society of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Society_of_Great_Britain)'s National Radio Centre (including a library, radio station, museum and bookshop) are in a newly constructed building close to the main Bletchley Park entrance.[^o207][^o208]
