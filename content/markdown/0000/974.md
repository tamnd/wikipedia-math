# Ada Lovelace

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Ada Lovelace"}

**Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace** (**Byron**; 10 December 1815 – 27 November 1852), also known as **Ada Lovelace**, was an English [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [writer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writer) chiefly known for work on [Charles Babbage](wikipedia://en/5698)'s proposed mechanical general-purpose [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer), the [analytical engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_engine). She was the first to recognise the machine had applications beyond pure calculation. Lovelace is often considered the first [computer programmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programmer).

:::

Lovelace was the only [legitimate child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimate_child) of poet [Lord Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron) and reformer [Anne Isabella Milbanke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Byron).[^o4] Lord Byron separated from his wife a month after Ada was born, and died when she was eight. Although often ill in childhood, Lovelace pursued her studies [assiduously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assiduous). She married [William King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_King-Noel,_1st_Earl_of_Lovelace) in 1835. King was a [Baron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron), and was created Viscount Ockham and 1st [Earl of Lovelace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Lovelace) in 1838. The name Lovelace was chosen because Ada was descended from the extinct [Baron Lovelaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Lovelace).[^o5] The title given to her husband thus made Ada the Countess of Lovelace.

Lovelace's educational and social exploits brought her into contact with scientists such as [Andrew Crosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Crosse), [Charles Babbage](wikipedia://en/5698), [David Brewster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Brewster), [Charles Wheatstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Wheatstone) and [Michael Faraday](wikipedia://en/19727), and the author [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens), contacts which she used to further her education. Lovelace described her approach as "poetical science"[^n1] and herself as an "Analyst (& [Metaphysician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics))".[^n2]

At age 18, Lovelace's mathematical talents led her to a long working relationship and friendship with fellow British mathematician Charles Babbage. She was particularly interested in Babbage's work on the analytical engine. Lovelace first met him on 5 June 1833, when she and her mother attended one of [Charles Babbage's Saturday night soirées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Babbage's_Saturday_night_soirées)[^o8] with their mutual friend, and Lovelace's private tutor, [Mary Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Somerville). Though Babbage's analytical engine was never constructed and did not influence the invention of electronic computers, it has been recognised as a [Turing-complete](wikipedia://en/30621) general-purpose computer, which anticipated the essential features of a modern electronic computer. Babbage is therefore known as the "father of computers," and Lovelace is credited with several computing "firsts" for her collaboration with him. Lovelace translated an article by the military engineer [Luigi Menabrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Menabrea) about the analytical engine, supplementing it with seven long explanatory notes. These described a method of using the machine to calculate [Bernoulli numbers](wikipedia://en/4964) which is often called the first published [computer program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_program).

She developed a vision of the capability of computers to go beyond mere [calculating](wikipedia://en/239121) or number-crunching, while many others, including Babbage, focused only on those capabilities.[^n3] Lovelace was the first to point out the possibility of encoding information besides mere [arithmetical](wikipedia://en/3118) figures, such as [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), and manipulating it with such a machine. Her mindset of "poetical science" led her to ask questions about the analytical engine, examining how individuals and society relate to technology as a collaborative tool.[^o10] Ada is widely commemorated, including in the names of a [programming language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29), roads, buildings and institutes, as well as programmes, lectures and courses. There are plaques, statues, paintings, literary and non-fiction works about her.

## Biography {#Biography}

### Childhood {#Childhood}

[Lord Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron) expected his child to be a "glorious boy" and was disappointed when [Lady Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Byron) gave birth to a girl.[^n4] The child was named after Byron's half-sister, [Augusta Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusta_Leigh), and was called "Ada" by Byron himself.[^n5] On 16 January 1816, at Lord Byron's command, Lady Byron left for her parents' home at [Kirkby Mallory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirkby_Mallory), taking their five-week-old daughter with her.[^n4] Although English law at the time granted full custody of children to the father in cases of separation, Lord Byron made no attempt to claim his parental rights,[^n6] but did request that his sister keep him informed of Ada's welfare.[^n7]

*Image: Miniature of Ada Byron.jpg.* Ada Byron, aged four

On 21 April, Lord Byron signed the [deed of separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deed_of_separation), although very reluctantly, and left England for good a few days later.[^n8] Aside from an acrimonious separation, Lady Byron continued throughout her life to make allegations about her husband's immoral behaviour.[^n9] This set of events made Lovelace infamous in Victorian society. Ada did not have a relationship with her father. He died in April 1824 when she was eight years old. Her mother was the only significant parental figure in her life.[^o20] Lovelace was not shown the family portrait of her father until her 20th birthday.[^n10]

*Image: Ada Lovelace child portrait Somerville College.jpg.* Ada Byron, aged seven, by [Alfred d'Orsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_d'Orsay), 1822, [Somerville College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_College,_Oxford)

Lovelace did not have a close relationship with her mother. She was often left in the care of her maternal grandmother Judith, Hon. Lady Milbanke, who doted on her. However, because of societal attitudes of the time—which favoured the husband in any separation, with the welfare of any child acting as mitigation—Lady Byron had to present herself as a loving mother to the rest of society. This included writing anxious letters to Lady Milbanke about her daughter's welfare, with a cover note saying to retain the letters in case she had to use them to show maternal concern.[^n11] In one letter to Lady Milbanke, she referred to her daughter as "it": "I talk to it for your satisfaction, not my own, and shall be very glad when you have it under your own."[^n12] Lady Byron had her teenage daughter watched by close friends for any sign of moral deviation. Lovelace dubbed these observers the "Furies" and later complained they exaggerated and invented stories about her.[^n13]

*Image: Ada Byron aged seventeen (1832).jpg.* Ada Byron, aged seventeen, 1832

Lovelace was often ill, beginning in early childhood. At the age of eight, she experienced headaches that obscured her vision.[^n5] In June 1829, she was paralyzed after a bout of [measles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measles). She was subjected to continuous bed rest for nearly a year, something which may have extended her period of disability. By 1831, she was able to walk with crutches. Despite the illnesses, she developed her mathematical and technological skills.

Ada Byron had an affair with a tutor in early 1833. She tried to elope with him after she was caught, but the tutor's relatives recognised her and contacted her mother. Lady Byron and her friends covered the incident up to prevent a public scandal.[^n14] Lovelace never met her younger half-sister, [Allegra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegra_Byron), the daughter of Lord Byron and [Claire Clairmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claire_Clairmont). Allegra died in 1822 at the age of five. Lovelace did have some contact with [Elizabeth Medora Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Medora_Leigh), the daughter of Byron's half-sister Augusta Leigh, who purposely avoided Lovelace as much as possible when introduced at court.[^n15]

### Adult years {#Adult_years}

*Image: Ada-byron.png.* Watercolor portrait of Ada Byron, by [Alfred Edward Chalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Edward_Chalon), c. 1835.

*Image: Ada Lovelace portrait.jpg.* Watercolour portrait of Ada King, Countess of Lovelace, c. 1840, possibly by Alfred Edward Chalon

Lovelace became close friends with her tutor [Mary Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Somerville), who introduced her to Charles Babbage in 1833. She had a strong respect and affection for Somerville,[^n16] and they corresponded for many years. Other acquaintances included the scientists [Andrew Crosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Crosse), [Sir David Brewster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Brewster), [Charles Wheatstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Wheatstone), [Michael Faraday](wikipedia://en/19727) and the author [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens). She was [presented at Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debutante) at the age of seventeen "and became a popular belle of the season" in part because of her "brilliant mind".[^n17] By 1834 Ada was a regular at Court and started attending various events. She danced often and was able to charm many people, and was described by most people as being dainty, although [John Hobhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hobhouse,_1st_Baron_Broughton), Byron's friend, described her as "a large, coarse-skinned young woman but with something of my friend's features, particularly the mouth".[^n18] This description followed their meeting on 24 February 1834 in which Ada made it clear to Hobhouse that she did not like him, probably due to her mother's influence, which led her to dislike all of her father's friends. This first impression was not to last, and they later became friends.[^n19]

*Image: Ada Lovelace's husband William King in uniform.webp.* William King, 1st Earl of Lovelace in uniform

On 8 July 1835, she married [William, 8th Baron King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_King,_1st_Earl_of_Lovelace), becoming Lady King. They had three homes: [Ockham Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ockham_Park), Surrey; a Scottish estate on [Loch Torridon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torridon) in [Ross-shire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross-shire); and a house in London. They spent their honeymoon at Ashley Combe near [Porlock Weir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porlock_Weir), Somerset, which had been built as a hunting lodge in 1799 and was improved by King in preparation for their honeymoon. It later became their summer retreat and was further improved during this time. From 1845, the family's main house was [Horsley Towers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsley_Towers), built in the [Tudorbethan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_Revival_architecture) fashion by the architect of the Houses of Parliament, [Charles Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Barry),[^o33][^o34] and later greatly enlarged to Lovelace's own designs.

They had three children: [Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byron_King-Noel,_Viscount_Ockham) (born 1836); [Anne Isabella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Anne_Blunt) (called Annabella, born 1837); and [Ralph Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_King-Milbanke,_2nd_Earl_of_Lovelace) (born 1839). Immediately after the birth of Annabella, Lady King experienced "a tedious and suffering illness, which took months to cure".[^n19] Ada was a descendant of the extinct [Barons Lovelace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baron_Lovelace) and in 1838, her husband was made [Earl of Lovelace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_of_Lovelace) and Viscount Ockham, meaning Ada became the Countess of Lovelace.[^o36] In 1843–44, Ada's mother assigned [William Benjamin Carpenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Benjamin_Carpenter) to teach Ada's children and to act as a "moral" instructor for Ada.[^n20] He quickly fell for her and encouraged her to express any frustrated affections, claiming that his marriage meant he would never act in an "unbecoming" manner. When it became clear that Carpenter was trying to start an affair, Ada cut it off.[^n21]

In 1841, Lovelace and [Medora Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Medora_Leigh) (the daughter of Lord Byron's half-sister Augusta Leigh) were told by Ada's mother that Ada's father was also Medora's father.[^n22] On 27 February 1841, Ada wrote to her mother: "I am not in the least *astonished*. In fact, you merely *confirm* what I have for *years and years* felt scarcely a doubt about, but should have considered it most improper in me to hint to you that I in any way suspected."[^n23] She did not blame the incestuous relationship on Byron, but instead blamed Augusta Leigh: "I fear she is more inherently wicked than he ever was."[^n24] In the 1840s, Ada flirted with scandals: firstly, from a relaxed approach to extra-marital relationships with men, leading to rumours of affairs;[^n25] and secondly, from her love of gambling. She apparently lost more than £3,000 on the horses during the later 1840s.[^o43] The gambling led to her forming a syndicate with male friends, and an ambitious attempt in 1851 to create a mathematical model for successful large bets. This went disastrously wrong, leaving her thousands of pounds in debt to the syndicate, forcing her to admit it all to her husband.[^n26] She had a shadowy relationship with Andrew Crosse's son John from 1844 onwards. John Crosse destroyed most of their correspondence after her death as part of a legal agreement. She bequeathed him the only heirlooms her father had personally left to her.[^n27] During her final illness, she would panic at the idea of the younger Crosse being kept from visiting her.[^n28]

### Education {#Education}

*Image: Ada Lovelace sonnet The Rainbow Somerville College.JPG.* Sonnet titled *The Rainbow* in Lovelace's own hand ([Somerville College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_College_Library))

From 1832, when she was seventeen, her mathematical abilities began to emerge,[^n17] and her interest in mathematics dominated the majority of her adult life.[^n29] Her mother's obsession with rooting out any of the insanity of which she accused Byron was one of the reasons that Ada was taught mathematics from an early age. She was privately educated in mathematics and science by [William Frend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Frend_%28social_reformer%29), [William King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_King_%28physician%29),[^o50] and [Mary Somerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Somerville), the noted 19th-century researcher and scientific author. In the 1840s, the mathematician [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289) extended her "much help in her mathematical studies" including study of advanced calculus topics including the "[numbers of Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/4964)" (that formed her celebrated algorithm for Babbage's Analytical Engine).[^o51] In a letter to Lady Byron, De Morgan suggested that Ada's skill in mathematics might lead her to become "an original mathematical investigator, perhaps of first-rate eminence".[^n30]

Lovelace often questioned basic assumptions through integrating poetry and science. Whilst studying [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416), she wrote to De Morgan:

> I may remark that the curious transformations many formulae can undergo, the unsuspected and to a beginner apparently impossible identity of forms exceedingly dissimilar at first sight, is I think one of the chief difficulties in the early part of mathematical studies. I am often reminded of certain sprites and fairies one reads of, who are at one's elbows in *one* shape now, and the next minute in a form most dissimilar.[^n31]

Lovelace believed that intuition and imagination were critical to effectively applying mathematical and scientific concepts. She valued [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) as much as mathematics, viewing both as tools for exploring "the unseen worlds around us".[^n32]

### Death {#Death}

*Image: Ada Lovelace in 1852.jpg.* Painting of Lovelace seated at a piano, by [Henry Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wyndham_Phillips) (1852). Although in great pain at the time, she agreed to sit for the painting as her father, [Lord Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron), had been painted by Phillips' father, [Thomas Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Phillips).

Lovelace died at the age of 36 on 27 November 1852[^o56] from [cervical cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cervical_cancer) (which contemporary accounts called uterine cancer, since a distinction between the two was not made at that time).[^n33][^o58] The illness lasted several months, in which time Lady Byron took command over whom Ada saw, and excluded all of her friends and confidants. Under her mother's influence, Ada had a religious transformation and was coaxed into repenting of her previous conduct and making Lady Byron her [executor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executor#Executor_of_will).[^n34] She lost contact with her husband after confessing something to him on 30 August which caused him to abandon her bedside. It is not known what she told him.[^n35] She was buried, at her request, next to her father at the [Church of St. Mary Magdalene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_St._Mary_Magdalene,_Hucknall) in Hucknall, Nottinghamshire.[^o61]

## Work {#Work}

Throughout her life, Lovelace was strongly interested in scientific developments and fads of the day, including [phrenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrenology)[^n36] and [mesmerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesmerism).[^n37] After her work with Babbage, Lovelace continued to work on other projects. In 1844, she commented to a friend Woronzow Greig about her desire to create a mathematical model for how the brain gives rise to thoughts and nerves to feelings ("a calculus of the nervous system").[^n38] She never achieved this, however. In part, her interest in the brain came from a long-running preoccupation, inherited from her mother, about her "potential" madness. As part of her research into this project, she visited the electrical engineer Andrew Crosse in 1844 to learn how to carry out electrical experiments.[^n39] In the same year, she wrote a review of a paper by Baron [Karl von Reichenbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_von_Reichenbach), *Researches on Magnetism*, but this was not published and does not appear to have progressed past the first draft.[^n40] In 1851, the year before her cancer struck, she wrote to her mother mentioning "certain productions" she was working on regarding the relation of maths and music.[^n41]

*Image: Ada Lovelace.jpg.* *[Portrait of Ada Lovelace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portrait_of_Ada_Lovelace)* by the British painter [Margaret Sarah Carpenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Sarah_Carpenter) (1836)

Lovelace first met [Charles Babbage](wikipedia://en/5698) in June 1833, through their mutual friend Mary Somerville. Later that month, Babbage invited Lovelace to see the prototype for his [difference engine](wikipedia://en/8324).[^n42] She became fascinated with the machine and used her relationship with Somerville to visit Babbage as often as she could. Babbage was impressed by Lovelace's intellect and analytic skills. He called her "The Enchantress of Number".[^o70][^o71] In 1843, he wrote to her:

Forget this world and all its troubles and if possible its multitudinous Charlatans—every thing in short but the Enchantress of Number.[^o70]

[^o72]

### Babbage's lecture, Menabrea's French transcription, Lovelace's translation and Notes A-G {#Babbage's_lecture,_Menabrea's_French_transcription,_Lovelace's_translation_and_Notes_A-G}

In 1840, Babbage was invited to give a seminar at the [University of Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Turin) about his Analytical Engine. [Luigi Menabrea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Menabrea), a young Italian engineer and the future [Prime Minister of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Italy), transcribed Babbage's lecture into French, and this transcript was subsequently published in the *[Bibliothèque universelle de Genève](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliothèque_universelle_de_Genève)* in October 1842. Babbage's friend [Charles Wheatstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Wheatstone) commissioned Lovelace to translate Menabrea's paper into English.

During a nine-month period in 1842–43, Lovelace translated Menabrea's article.[^o74] She augmented the paper with seven notes, A to G, about three times longer than the translation.[^n43] The translation and notes were then published in the September 1843 edition of Taylor's *[Scientific Memoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Memoirs)* under her initials *AAL*.[^o76]

Explaining the Analytical Engine's function was a difficult task; many other scientists did not grasp the concept and the British establishment had shown little interest in it.[^n44] Lovelace's notes even had to explain how the Analytical Engine differed from the original Difference Engine.[^n45] Her work was well received at the time; the scientist [Michael Faraday](wikipedia://en/19727) described himself as a supporter of her writing.[^n46]

### Babbage's preface {#Babbage's_preface}

Lovelace and Babbage had a minor falling out when the papers were published, when he tried to leave his own statement (criticising the government's treatment of his Engine) as an unsigned preface, which could have been mistakenly interpreted as a joint declaration. When [Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Taylor_%28editor%29)'s *[Scientific Memoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_Memoirs)* ruled that the statement should be signed, Babbage wrote to Lovelace asking her to withdraw the paper. This was the first that she knew he was leaving it unsigned, and she wrote back refusing to withdraw the paper. The historian [Benjamin Woolley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Woolley) theorised that "His actions suggested he had so enthusiastically sought Ada's involvement, and so happily indulged her ... because of her 'celebrated name'."[^n47] Their friendship recovered, and they continued to correspond. On 12 August 1851, when she was dying of cancer, Lovelace wrote to him asking him to be her executor, though this letter did not give him the necessary legal authority. Part of the terrace at Worthy Manor was known as *Philosopher's Walk;* it was there that Lovelace and Babbage were reputed to have walked while discussing mathematical principles.[^n46]

### First published computer program {#First_published_computer_program}

*Image: Diagram for the computation of Bernoulli numbers.jpg.* Lovelace's diagram from "[Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G)", the first published computer [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775)

The notes are important in the early [history of computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_computers), especially since [Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G)[^o84] described, in complete detail, a method for calculating a sequence of [Bernoulli numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_numbers) using the Analytical Engine, which might have run correctly had it ever been built.[^o90] Though Babbage's personal notes from 1837 to 1840 contain the first programs for the engine,[^n48][^n49][^o87] the [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) in Note G is often called the first published computer program. The engine was never completed and so the program was never tested.[^n50]

In 1953, more than a century after her death, Ada Lovelace's notes on Babbage's Analytical Engine were republished as an appendix to [B. V. Bowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._V._Bowden)'s *Faster than Thought: A Symposium on Digital Computing Machines*.[^o89] The engine has now been recognised as an early model for a computer and her notes as a description of a computer and software.[^o90]

#### Controversy over contribution {#Controversy_over_contribution}

Based on this work, Lovelace is often called the first computer programmer[^o92][^o93] and her method has been called the world's first computer program.[^o94][^o95][^o96]

Eugene Eric Kim and Lovelace biographer, Betty Alexandra Toole, argued it was "incorrect" to regard Lovelace as the first computer programmer in an article for [Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American).[^n50] Babbage claims credit in his autobiography for the algorithm in Note G,[^o98] and regardless of the extent of Lovelace's contribution to it, she was not the very first person to write a program for the Analytical Engine, as Babbage had written the initial programs for it, although the majority were never published.[^n50] Bromley notes several dozen sample programs prepared by Babbage between 1837 and 1840, all substantially predating Lovelace's notes.[^o100] [Dorothy K. Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_K._Stein) regards Lovelace's notes as "more a reflection of the mathematical uncertainty of the author, the political purposes of the inventor, and, above all, of the social and cultural context in which it was written, than a blueprint for a scientific development".[^o101]

[Allan G. Bromley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_Bromley_%28historian%29), in the 1990 article *Difference and Analytical Engines*: All but one of the programs cited in her notes had been prepared by Babbage from three to seven years earlier. The exception was prepared by Babbage for her, although she did detect a "bug" in it. Not only is there no evidence that Ada ever prepared a program for the Analytical Engine, but her correspondence with Babbage shows that she did not have the knowledge to do so.[^o102]

Bruce Collier wrote that Lovelace "made a considerable contribution to publicizing the Analytical Engine, but there is no evidence that she advanced the design or theory of it in any way".[^o103]

[Doron Swade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doron_Swade) has said that Ada only published the first computer program instead of actually writing it, but agrees that she was the only person to see the potential of the analytical engine as a machine capable of expressing entities other than quantities.[^o104]

In his book, *Idea Makers*, [Stephen Wolfram](wikipedia://en/52469) defends Lovelace's contributions. While acknowledging that Babbage wrote several unpublished algorithms for the Analytical Engine prior to Lovelace's notes, Wolfram argues that "there's nothing as sophisticated—or as clean—as Ada's computation of the Bernoulli numbers. Babbage certainly helped and commented on Ada's work, but she was definitely the driver of it." Wolfram then suggests that Lovelace's main achievement was to distill from Babbage's correspondence "a clear exposition of the abstract operation of the machine—something which Babbage never did".[^o105]

### Insight into potential of computing devices {#Insight_into_potential_of_computing_devices}

In her notes, Ada Lovelace emphasised the difference between the Analytical Engine and previous calculating machines, particularly its ability to be programmed to solve problems of any complexity.[^n51] She realised the potential of the device extended far beyond mere number crunching. In her notes, she wrote:

\[The Analytical Engine\] might act upon other things besides *number*, were objects found whose mutual fundamental relations could be expressed by those of the abstract science of operations, and which should be also susceptible of adaptations to the action of the operating notation and mechanism of the engine...Supposing, for instance, that the fundamental relations of pitched sounds in the science of harmony and of musical composition were susceptible of such expression and adaptations, the engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent.[^o108][^o109]

This analysis was an important development from previous ideas about the capabilities of computing devices and anticipated the implications of modern computing one hundred years before they were realised. [Walter Isaacson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Isaacson) ascribes Ada's insight regarding the application of computing to *any* process based on logical symbols to an observation about textiles: "When she saw some [mechanical looms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacquard_machine) that used [punchcards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punched_cards) to direct the weaving of beautiful [patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern), it reminded her of how Babbage's engine used punched cards to make calculations."[^o110] This insight is seen as significant by writers such as Betty Toole and Benjamin Woolley, as well as the programmer [John Graham-Cumming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Graham-Cumming), whose project Plan 28 has the aim of constructing the first complete Analytical Engine.[^n52][^n53][^o113]

According to the historian of computing and Babbage specialist [Doron Swade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doron_Swade):

> Ada saw something that Babbage in some sense failed to see. In Babbage's world his engines were bound by number...What Lovelace saw...was that number could represent entities other than quantity. So once you had a machine for manipulating numbers, if those numbers represented other things, letters, musical notes, then the machine could manipulate symbols of which number was one instance, according to rules. It is this fundamental transition from a machine which is a number cruncher to a machine for manipulating symbols according to rules that is the fundamental transition from calculation to computation—to general-purpose computation—and looking back from the present high ground of modern computing, if we are looking and sifting history for that transition, then that transition was made explicitly by Ada in that 1843 paper.[^n54]

*[Note G](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Note_G)* also contains what came to be known as "Lady Lovelace's Objection" to the concept of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence), so named by [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) in his paper "[Computing Machinery and Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing_Machinery_and_Intelligence)"[^o115][^o116] The objection is Lovelace's statement that:

> The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to *originate* anything. It can do *whatever we know how to order it* to perform. It can follow analysis; but it has no power of anticipating any analytical relations or truths.

This objection has been the subject of much debate and rebuttal, including in the paper that originally coined it.[^o116]

### Distinction between mechanism and logical structure {#Distinction_between_mechanism_and_logical_structure}

Lovelace recognized the difference between the details of the computing mechanism, as covered in an 1834 article on the Difference Engine,[^o118] and the logical structure of the Analytical Engine, on which the article she was reviewing dwelt. She noted that different specialists might be required in each area.

> The \[1834 article\] chiefly treats it under its mechanical aspect, entering but slightly into the mathematical principles of which that engine is the representative, but giving, in considerable length, many details of the mechanism and contrivances by means of which it tabulates the various orders of differences. M. Menabrea, on the contrary, exclusively develops the analytical view; taking it for granted that mechanism is able to perform certain processes, but without attempting to explain how; and devoting his whole attention to explanations and illustrations of the manner in which analytical laws can be so arranged and combined as to bring every branch of that vast subject within the grasp of the assumed powers of mechanism. It is obvious that, in the invention of a calculating engine, these two branches of the subject are equally essential fields of investigation... They are indissolubly connected, though so different in their intrinsic nature, that perhaps the same mind might not be likely to prove equally profound or successful in both.[^o84]

## Commemoration {#Commemoration}

*Image: AdaLovelaceplaque.JPG.* [Blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque) to Ada Lovelace in [St James's Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_James's_Square), London

The computer language [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29), created on behalf of the [United States Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense), was named after Lovelace.[^o120] The reference manual for the language was approved on 10 December 1980 and the [Department of Defense Military Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Standard) for the language, *MIL-STD-1815*, was given the number of the year of her birth.[^o121]

In 1981, the [Association for Women in Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Women_in_Computing) inaugurated its [Ada Lovelace Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace_Award).[^o122][^o123] , the [British Computer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Computer_Society) (BCS) has awarded the [Lovelace Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lovelace_Medal),[^o124] and in 2008 initiated an annual competition for women students.[^o125] [BCSWomen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCSWomen) sponsors the Lovelace Colloquium, an annual conference for women undergraduates.[^o125] In 2013, the [University of Deusto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Deusto) in Spain established the [Ada Byron Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Byron_Award) for women in technology, which was subsequently expanded to several Latin American countries.[^o126]

[Ada, the National College for Digital Skills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada,_the_National_College_for_Digital_Skills), is a specialist [college of further education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Further_education) and [higher education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_education) in England, focused on digital skills. The college has campuses in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London) ([Pimlico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pimlico)) and [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester) ([Ancoats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancoats)). The college teaches degree-level apprenticeship, as well as a sixth-form college for students aged 16 to 19.[^o127][^o128]

[Ada Lovelace Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace_Day) is an annual event celebrated on the second Tuesday of October,[^o129] which began in 2009.[^o130] Its goal is to "... raise the profile of women in science, technology, engineering, and maths," and to "create new role models for girls and women" in these fields.[^o130] Events have included Wikipedia [edit-a-thons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edit-a-thons) with the aim of improving the representation of women on Wikipedia in terms of articles and editors to reduce unintended [gender bias on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_bias_on_Wikipedia).

The [Ada Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Initiative) was a non-profit organisation dedicated to increasing the involvement of women in the [free culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_culture_movement) and [open source movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_software_movement).[^o131]

The building of the department of Engineering Mathematics at the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol) is called the Ada Lovelace Building.[^o132]

The Engineering in Computer Science and Telecommunications College building in [Zaragoza University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zaragoza_University) is called the Ada Byron Building.[^o133]

The computer centre in the village of [Porlock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porlock), near where Lovelace lived, is named after her.

Ada Lovelace House is a council-owned building in [Kirkby-in-Ashfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirkby-in-Ashfield), Nottinghamshire, near where Lovelace spent her infancy.[^o134][^o135]

In 2012, a [Google Doodle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Doodle) and blog post honoured her on her birthday.[^o136][^o137] In 2013, [Ada Developers Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Developers_Academy) was founded and named after her. Its mission is to diversify tech by providing women and gender-diverse people the skills, experience, and community support to become professional software developers to change the face of tech.[^o138] On 17 September 2013, the [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4) biography programme *[Great Lives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Lives)* devoted an episode to Ada Lovelace; she was sponsored by TV presenter [Konnie Huq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konnie_Huq).[^o139]

*Image: Ada Lovelace statue 2024-10-03.jpg.* Lovelace statue in Millbank, City of Westminster, London

As of November 2015, all new [British passports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_passport) have included an illustration of Lovelace and Babbage.[^o140][^o141] In 2017, a Google Doodle honoured her with other women on [International Women's Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Women's_Day).[^o142] On 2 February 2018, [Satellogic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellogic), a high-resolution [Earth observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_observation) imaging and analytics company, launched a [ÑuSat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ÑuSat) type [micro-satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_satellite) named in honour of Ada Lovelace.[^o143] In March 2018, *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times)* published a belated obituary for Ada Lovelace.[^o144]

On 27 July 2018, [Senator Ron Wyden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Wyden) submitted, in the [United States Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Senate), the designation of 9 October 2018 as National Ada Lovelace Day: "To honor the life and contributions of Ada Lovelace as a leading woman in science and mathematics". The resolution (S.Res.592)[^o145] was considered, and agreed to without amendment and with a preamble by unanimous consent. In November 2020 it was announced that [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin), whose library had previously held forty busts, all of them of men, was commissioning four new busts of women, one of whom was to be Lovelace. The four new busts (Ada Lovelace, Mary Wollstonecraft, Augusta Gregory and Rosalind Franklin) were unveiled on February 1, 2023 [^o146]

In March 2022, a statue of Ada Lovelace was installed at the site of the former Ergon House in the [City of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Westminster), London, honouring its scientific history. The redevelopment was part of a complex with [Imperial Chemical House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Chemical_House).[^o147] The statue was sculpted by Etienne and Mary Millner and based on the portrait by [Margaret Sarah Carpenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Sarah_Carpenter). The sculpture was unveiled on International Women's Day, 2022. It stands on the 7th floor of Millbank Quarter overlooking the junction of Dean Bradley Street and [Horseferry Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseferry_Road).[^o148]

The 2022 [graphics processor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphics_processing_unit) [microarchitecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microarchitecture) from [Nvidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia) is named [Ada Lovelace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace_%28microarchitecture%29).[^o149] In July 2023, the [Royal Mint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mint) issued four commemorative [£2 coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commemorative_coins_of_the_United_Kingdom#Two_pounds) in various metals to "honour the innovative contributions of computer science visionary Ada Lovelace and her legacy as a female trailblazer."[^o150]

In 2025, the [National Portrait Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Portrait_Gallery,_London) acquired three images of Lovelace–the only known photographs of Lovelace.[^o151] Two were daguerreotypes by [Antoine Claudet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_Claudet) and were taken around 1843.[^o152][^o153] The third was by an unknown photographer and is of [Henry Wyndham Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wyndham_Phillips)’ portrait of Lovelace.[^o154]

In February 2026, a statue of Lovelace was unveiled on the [Hinckley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinckley) campus of the North Warwickshire and South Leicestershire College.[^o155]

Special exhibitions were displayed by the [Science Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Museum,_London) in London, England[^o166] and the [Weston Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weston_Library) (part of the [Bodleian Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodleian_Library)) in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford), England.[^o167]

## In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

*Image: Ada Lovelace color.svg.* An illustration inspired by the [A. E. Chalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._E._Chalon) portrait created for the [Ada Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Initiative), which supported open technology and women

### Novels, plays and poetry {#Novels,_plays_and_poetry}

Lovelace is portrayed in [Romulus Linney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romulus_Linney_%28playwright%29)'s 1977 play *[Childe Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Childe_Byron)*.[^o170] In [Tom Stoppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Stoppard)'s 1993 play *[Arcadia](wikipedia://en/58379)*, the precocious teenage genius Thomasina Coverly—a character "apparently based" on Ada Lovelace (the play also involves [Lord Byron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Byron))—comes to understand [chaos theory](wikipedia://en/6295), and theorises the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/133017), before either is officially recognised.[^o171][^o172]

In the 1990 [steampunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steampunk) novel *[The Difference Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Difference_Engine)* by [William Gibson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gibson) and [Bruce Sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Sterling),[^o173] Lovelace delivers a lecture on the "punched cards" programme which proves [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](wikipedia://en/58863) decades before their actual discovery. Lovelace and [Mary Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Shelley) as teenagers are the central characters in [Jordan Stratford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_Stratford)'s steampunk series, *The Wollstonecraft Detective Agency*.[^o174]

Lovelace features in [John Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Crowley_%28author%29)'s 2005 novel, *Lord Byron's Novel: The Evening Land*, as an unseen character whose personality is forcefully depicted in her annotations and anti-heroic efforts to archive her father's lost novel.[^o175]

The 2015 play *Ada and the Engine* by [Lauren Gunderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauren_Gunderson) portrays Lovelace and Charles Babbage in unrequited love, and it imagines a post-death meeting between Lovelace and her father.[^o176][^o177] Lovelace and Babbage are also the main characters in [Sydney Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Padua)'s webcomic and graphic novel *[The Thrilling Adventures of Lovelace and Babbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thrilling_Adventures_of_Lovelace_and_Babbage)*. The comic features extensive footnotes on the history of Ada Lovelace, and many lines of dialogue are drawn from actual correspondence.[^o178]

Poet [Jessy Randall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessy_Randall) included a tribute to Lovelace in her 2025 collection of poems about women scientists, *The Path of Most Resistance*.[^o179]

### Film and television {#Film_and_television}

In the 1997 film *[Conceiving Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceiving_Ada)*, a computer scientist obsessed with Ada finds a way of communicating with her in the past by means of "undying information waves".[^o181]

Lovelace, identified as Ada Augusta Byron, is portrayed by Lily Lesser in the second series of *[The Frankenstein Chronicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Frankenstein_Chronicles)* aired on ITV in 2017.[^o182] She is employed as an "analyst" to provide the workings of a life-sized humanoid automaton. The brass workings of the machine are reminiscent of Babbage's analytical engine. Her employment is described as keeping her occupied until she returns to her studies in advanced mathematics.[^o183]

"Lovelace" is the name of the operating system designed by the character Cameron Howe in *[Halt and Catch Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halt_and_Catch_Fire_%28TV_series%29)*, which aired on [AMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMC_%28TV_channel%29) in the US in 2015.[^o184]

In the documentary *Calculating Ada: The Countess of Computing* (2015), Dr Hannah Fry covers the life of Ada Lovelace.[^o185]

Lovelace and Babbage appear as characters in the [second season](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_%28British_TV_series%29) of the ITV series *[Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_%28UK_TV_series%29)* (2017). [Emerald Fennell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald_Fennell) portrays Lovelace in the episode, "The Green-Eyed Monster."[^o186]

Lovelace features as a character in "[Spyfall, Part 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spyfall_%28Doctor_Who%29)", the second episode of [*Doctor Who*, series 12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who_%28series_12%29), which first aired on [BBC One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_One) on 5 January 2020.[^o187] The character was portrayed by Sylvie Briggs, alongside characterisations of Charles Babbage and [Noor Inayat Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noor_Inayat_Khan).[^o187]

### Publication history {#Publication_history}

Six copies of the 1843 first edition of *Sketch of the Analytical Engine* with Ada Lovelace's "Notes"[^o84] have been located. Three are held at Harvard University, one at the University of Oklahoma, and one at the United States Air Force Academy.[^o222] On 20 July 2018, the sixth copy was sold at auction to an anonymous buyer for £95,000.[^o223] A digital facsimile of one of the copies in the Harvard University Library is available online.[^n43]
