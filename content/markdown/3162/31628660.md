# Generality of algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generality of algebra"}

In the [history of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220), the **generality of algebra** was a phrase used by [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) to describe a method of argument that was used in the 18th century by mathematicians such as [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) and [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793),[^o2] particularly in manipulating [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series). According to Koetsier,[^o3] the generality of algebra principle assumed, roughly, that the [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) rules that hold for a certain class of expressions can be extended to hold more generally on a larger class of objects, even if the rules are no longer obviously valid. As a consequence, 18th century mathematicians believed that they could derive meaningful results by applying the usual rules of algebra and [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) that hold for finite expansions even when manipulating infinite expansions.

:::

In works such as *[Cours d'Analyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cours_d'Analyse)*, Cauchy rejected the use of "generality of algebra" methods and sought a more [rigorous](wikipedia://en/655974#Mathematical_proof) foundation for [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396).

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

An example[^o3] is Euler's derivation of the series

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\frac{\pi - x}{2} = \sin x + \frac{1}{2}\sin 2x + \frac{1}{3}\sin 3x+\cdots \tag{1}$$

:::

for $0<x<\pi$. He first evaluated the identity

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\frac{1-r\cos x}{1-2r\cos x + r^2} = 1 + r\cos x + r^2\cos2x+r^3\cos 3x+\cdots \tag{2}$$

:::

at $r=1$ to obtain

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$0 = \frac{1}{2} + \cos x + \cos 2x + \cos 3x + \cdots. \tag{3}$$

:::

The infinite series on the right hand side of ((3)) diverges for all [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x$. But nevertheless [integrating](wikipedia://en/15532) this term-by-term gives ((1)), an identity which is known to be true by [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659).

:::
