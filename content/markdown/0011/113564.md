# General linear group

classical

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="General linear group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **general linear group** of degree $n$ is the set of $n\times n$ [invertible matrices](wikipedia://en/217122), together with the operation of ordinary [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). This forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), because the product of two invertible matrices is again invertible, and the inverse of an invertible matrix is invertible, with the identity matrix as the identity element of the group. The group is so named because the columns (and also the rows) of an invertible matrix are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent), hence the vectors/points they define are in [general linear position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_linear_position), and matrices in the general linear group take points in general linear position to points in general linear position.

:::

To be more precise, it is necessary to specify what kind of objects may appear in the entries of the matrix. For example, the general linear group over $\mathbb{R}$ (the set of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers)) is the group of $n\times n$ invertible matrices of real numbers, and is denoted by $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ or $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$.

More generally, the general linear group of degree $n$ over any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$ (such as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)), or a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ (such as the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563)), is the set of $n\times n$ invertible matrices with entries from $F$ (or $R$), again with matrix multiplication as the group operation.[^o2] Typical notation is $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ or $\operatorname{GL}_n(F)$, or simply $\operatorname{GL}(n)$ if the field is understood.

More generally still, the general linear group of a vector space $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ is the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772), not necessarily written as matrices.

The [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823), written $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$ or $\operatorname{SL}_n(F)$, is the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ consisting of matrices with a [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of 1.

The group $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ and its [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) are often called **linear groups** or **matrix groups** (the automorphism group $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ is a linear group but not a matrix group). These groups are important in the theory of [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695), and also arise in the study of spatial [symmetries](wikipedia://en/53741) and symmetries of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) in general, as well as the study of [polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomials). The [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019) may be realised as a quotient of the special linear group $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.

If $n\geq 2$, then the group $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ is not [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974).

## General linear group of a vector space {#General_linear_group_of_a_vector_space}

If $V$ is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the field $F$, the general linear group of $V$, written $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ or $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, is the group of all [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of $V$, i.e. the set of all [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) $V\to V$, together with functional composition as group operation. If $V$ has finite [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_dimension) $n$, then $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ and $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397). The isomorphism is not canonical; it depends on a choice of [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) in $V$. Given a basis $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ of $V$ and an automorphism $T$ in $\operatorname{GL}(V)$, we have then for every basis vector *e*<sub>*i*</sub> that

$$T(e_i) = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ji} e_j$$

for some constants $a_{ij}$ in $F$; the matrix corresponding to $T$ is then just the matrix with entries given by the $a_{ji}$.

In a similar way, for a commutative ring $R$ the group $\operatorname{GL}(n,R)$ may be interpreted as the group of automorphisms of a *[free](wikipedia://en/326454)* $R$-module $M$ of rank $n$. One can also define GL(*M*) for any $R$-module, but in general this is not isomorphic to $\operatorname{GL}(n,R)$ (for any $n$).

## In terms of determinants {#In_terms_of_determinants}

Over a field $F$, a matrix is [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible) if and only if its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) is nonzero. Therefore, an alternative definition of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ is as the group of matrices with nonzero determinant.

Over a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $R$, more care is needed: a matrix over $R$ is invertible if and only if its determinant is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in $R$, that is, if its determinant is invertible in $R$. Therefore, $\operatorname{GL}(n,R)$ may be defined as the group of matrices whose determinants are units.

Over a non-commutative ring $R$, determinants are not at all well behaved. In this case, $\operatorname{GL}(n,R)$ may be defined as the [unit group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) of the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) $M(n,R)$.

## As a Lie group/algebra {#As_a_Lie_group/algebra}

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="GL+"}

### Real case {#Real_case}

The general linear group $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ over the field of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) is a real [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of dimension $n^2$. To see this, note that the set of all $n\times n$ real matrices, $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, forms a [real vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) of dimension $n^2$. The subset $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ consists of those matrices whose [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) is non-zero. The determinant is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) map, and hence $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is an [open affine subvariety](wikipedia://en/248808) of $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ (a [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744)), and therefore[^o8] a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) of the same dimension.

The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, denoted $\mathfrak{gl}_n,$ consists of all $n\times n$ real matrices with the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) serving as the Lie bracket.

As a manifold, $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is not [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) but rather has two [connected components](wikipedia://en/6233): the matrices with positive determinant and the ones with negative determinant. The [identity component](wikipedia://en/1088787), denoted by $\operatorname{GL}^+(n,\mathbb{R})$, consists of the real $n\times n$ matrices with positive determinant. This is also a Lie group of dimension $n^2$; it has the same Lie algebra as $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$.

The [polar decomposition](wikipedia://en/1369241), which is unique for invertible matrices, shows that there is a homeomorphism between $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ and the Cartesian product of $\operatorname{O}(n)$ with the set of positive-definite symmetric matrices. Similarly, it shows that there is a homeomorphism between $\operatorname{GL}^+(n,\mathbb{R})$ and the Cartesian product of $\operatorname{SO}(n)$ with the set of positive-definite symmetric matrices. Because the latter is contractible, the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of $\operatorname{GL}^+(n,\mathbb{R})$ is isomorphic to that of $\operatorname{SO}(n)$.

The homeomorphism also shows that the group $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is [noncompact](wikipedia://en/6042). “The” [^o9] [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $\operatorname{O}(n)$, while "the" maximal compact subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}^+(n,\mathbb{R})$ is the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) $\operatorname{SO}(n)$. As for $\operatorname{SO}(n)$, the group $\operatorname{GL}^+(n,\mathbb{R})$ is not [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) (except when $n=1$, but rather has a [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ for $n=2$ or $\mathbb{Z}_2$ for $n>2$.

:::

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Complex general linear group"}

### Complex case {#Complex_case}

The general linear group over the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})$, is a *complex* [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of complex dimension $n^2$. As a real Lie group (through realification) it has dimension $2n^2$. The set of all real matrices forms a real Lie subgroup. These correspond to the inclusions

$$\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})<\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})<\operatorname{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R}),$$

which have real dimensions $n^2$, $2n^2$, and $(2n)^2=4n^2$. Complex $n$-dimensional matrices can be characterized as real $2n$-dimensional matrices that preserve a [linear complex structure](wikipedia://en/1549805); that is, matrices that commute with a matrix $J$ such that $J^2=-I$, where $J$ corresponds to multiplying by the imaginary unit $i$.

The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) corresponding to $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})$ consists of all $n\times n$ complex matrices with the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) serving as the Lie bracket.

Unlike the real case, $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233). This follows, in part, since the multiplicative group of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}^\times$ is connected. The group manifold $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is not compact; rather its [maximal compact subgroup](wikipedia://en/1496726) is the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $\operatorname{U}(n)$. As for $\operatorname{U}(n)$, the group manifold $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is not [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) but has a [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$.

:::

## Over finite fields {#Over_finite_fields}

*Image: Symmetric group 3; Cayley table; GL(2,2).svg.* [Cayley table](wikipedia://en/1802723) of GL(2, 2), which is isomorphic to [S<sub>3</sub>](wikipedia://en/2375824).

If $F$ is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with $q$ elements, then we sometimes write $\operatorname{GL}(n,q)$ instead of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$. When *p* is prime, $\operatorname{GL}(n,p)$ is the [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) of the group $\mathbb{Z}_p^n$, and also the [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) group, because $\mathbb{Z}_p^n$ is abelian, so the [inner automorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_automorphism_group) is trivial.

The order of $\operatorname{GL}(n,q)$ is:

$$\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(q^n-q^k)=(q^n - 1)(q^n - q)(q^n - q^2)\ \cdots\ (q^n - q^{n-1}).$$

This can be shown by counting the possible columns of the matrix: the first column can be anything but the zero vector; the second column can be anything but the multiples of the first column; and in general, the $k$th column can be any vector not in the [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of the first $k-1$ columns. In [*q*-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396) notation, this is $[n]_q!(q-1)^n q^{n \choose 2}$.

For example, GL(3, 2) has order (8 − 1)(8 − 2)(8 − 4) = 168. It is the automorphism group of the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404) and of the group $\mathbb{Z}_2^3$. This group is also isomorphic to [PSL(2, 7)](wikipedia://en/390426).

More generally, one can count points of [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810) over $F$: in other words the number of subspaces of a given dimension $k$. This requires only finding the order of the [stabilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_%28group_theory%29) subgroup of one such subspace and dividing into the formula just given, by the [orbit-stabilizer theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit-stabilizer_theorem).

These formulas are connected to the [Schubert decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schubert_decomposition) of the Grassmannian, and are [*q*-analogs](wikipedia://en/1619396) of the [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262) of complex Grassmannians. This was one of the clues leading to the [Weil conjectures](wikipedia://en/244705).

Note that in the limit $q\to 1$ the order of $\operatorname{GL}(n,q)$ goes to 0! – but under the correct procedure (dividing by $(q-1)^n$) we see that it is the order of the symmetric group (see Lorscheid's article). In the philosophy of the [field with one element](wikipedia://en/13798040), one thus interprets the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) as the general linear group over the field with one element: $S_n\cong \operatorname{GL}(n,1)$.

### History {#History}

The general linear group over a prime field, $\operatorname{GL}(\nu,p)$, was constructed and its order computed by [Évariste Galois](wikipedia://en/9815) in 1832, in his last letter (to Chevalier) and second (of three) attached manuscripts, which he used in the context of studying the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of the general equation of order $p^\nu$.[^o14]

## Special linear group {#Special_linear_group}

The *special linear group*, $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$, is the group of all matrices with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) 1. These matrices are special in that they lie on a [subvariety](wikipedia://en/248808): they satisfy a polynomial equation (as the determinant is a polynomial in the entries). Matrices of this type form a group as the determinant of the product of two matrices is the product of the determinants of each matrix.

If we write $F^\times$ for the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of $F$ (that is, $F$ excluding 0), then the determinant is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396)

$$\det : \operatorname{GL}(n,F) \to F^\times$$

that is surjective and its [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is the special linear group. Thus, $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$ is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$, and by the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem), $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)/\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $F^\times$. In fact, $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ can be written as a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246):

$$\operatorname{GL}(n,F)=\operatorname{SL}(n,F)\rtimes F^\times.$$

The special linear group is also the [derived group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_group) (also known as commutator subgroup) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ (for a field or a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) $F$), provided that $n \neq 2$ or $F$ is not the [field with two elements](wikipedia://en/11615).[^o16]

When $F$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$ is a [Lie subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_subgroup) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ of dimension $n^2-1$. The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$ consists of all $n\times n$ matrices over $F$ with vanishing [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_%28matrix%29). The Lie bracket is given by the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193).

The special linear group $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ can be characterized as the group of *[volume](wikipedia://en/32498) and [orientation-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation-preserving)* linear transformations of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

The group $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{C})$ is simply connected, while $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is not. $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ has the same fundamental group as $\operatorname{GL}^+(n,\mathbb{R})$, that is, $\mathbb{Z}$ for $n=2$ and $\mathbb{Z}_2$ for $n>2$.

## Other subgroups {#Other_subgroups}

### Diagonal subgroups {#Diagonal_subgroups}

The set of all invertible [diagonal matrices](wikipedia://en/174080) forms a subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ isomorphic to $(F^\times)^n$. In fields like $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$, these correspond to rescaling the space; the so-called dilations and contractions.

A **scalar matrix** is a diagonal matrix which is a constant times the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). The set of all nonzero scalar matrices forms a subgroup of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ isomorphic to $F^\times$. This group is the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$. In particular, it is a normal, abelian subgroup.

The center of $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$ is simply the set of all scalar matrices with unit determinant, and is isomorphic to the group of $n$th [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) in the field $F$.

### Classical groups {#Classical_groups}

The so-called [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878) are subgroups of $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ which preserve some sort of [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) on a vector space $V$. These include the

- [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954), $\operatorname{O}(V)$, which preserves a [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate) [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) on $V$,
- [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977), $\operatorname{Sp}(V)$, which preserves a [symplectic form](wikipedia://en/292852) on $V$ (a non-degenerate [alternating form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_form)),
- [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993), $\operatorname{U}(V)$, which, when $F=\mathbb{C}$, preserves a non-degenerate [hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) on $V$.

These groups provide important examples of Lie groups.

## Related groups and monoids {#Related_groups_and_monoids}

### Projective linear group {#Projective_linear_group}

The [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) $\operatorname{PGL}(n,F)$ and the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) $\operatorname{PSL}(n,F)$ are the [quotients](wikipedia://en/11526) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ and $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)$ by their [centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_center) (which consist of the multiples of the identity matrix therein); they are the induced [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on the associated [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135).

### Affine group {#Affine_group}

The [affine group](wikipedia://en/298828) $\operatorname{Aff}(n,F)$ is an [extension](wikipedia://en/345807) of $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ by the group of translations in $F^n$. It can be written as a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246):

$$\operatorname{Aff}(n,F)=\operatorname{GL}(n,F)\ltimes F^n$$

where $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ acts on $F^n$ in the natural manner. The affine group can be viewed as the group of all [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449) of the [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) underlying the vector space $F^n$.

One has analogous constructions for other subgroups of the general linear group: for instance, the [special affine group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_affine_group) is the subgroup defined by the semidirect product, $\operatorname{SL}(n,F)\ltimes F^n$, and the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) is the affine group associated to the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489), $\operatorname{O}(1,3,F)\ltimes F^n$.

### General semilinear group {#General_semilinear_group}

The [general semilinear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_semilinear_group) $\operatorname{\Gamma L}(n,F)$ is the group of all invertible [semilinear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semilinear_transformation), and contains $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$. A semilinear transformation is a transformation which is linear “up to a twist”, meaning “up to a [field automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_automorphism) under scalar multiplication”. It can be written as a semidirect product:

$$\operatorname{\Gamma L}(n,F)=\operatorname{Gal}(F)\ltimes \operatorname{GL}(n,F)$$

where $\operatorname{Gal}(F)$ is the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of $F$ (over its [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field)), which acts on $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)$ by the Galois action on the entries.

The main interest of $\operatorname{\Gamma L}(n,F)$ is that the associated [projective semilinear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_semilinear_group) $\operatorname{P\Gamma L}(n,F)$, which contains $\operatorname{PGL}(n,F)$, is the [collineation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collineation_group) of [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135), for $n>2$, and thus semilinear maps are of interest in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849).

::: {#o25 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Full linear monoid"}

### Full linear monoid {#Full_linear_monoid}

If one removes the restriction of the determinant being non-zero, the resulting algebraic structure is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652), usually called the *full linear monoid*,[^o26][^o27][^o28] but occasionally also *full linear semigroup*,[^o29] *general linear monoid*[^o30][^o31] etc. It is actually a [regular semigroup](wikipedia://en/6797677).[^o27]

:::

::: {#o33 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Infinite general linear group"}

## Infinite general linear group {#Infinite_general_linear_group}

The **infinite general linear group** or **stable general linear group** is the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of the inclusions $\operatorname{GL}(n,F)\to \operatorname{GL}(n+1,F)$ as [block diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_diagonal_matrix) with an added $1$ entry in the lower right. It is denoted by either $\operatorname{GL}(F)$ or $\operatorname{GL}(\infty,F)$, and can also be interpreted as invertible infinite matrices which differ from the identity matrix in only finitely many places.[^o34]

It is used in [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) to define [K<sub>1</sub>](wikipedia://en/598500#K1), and over the reals has a well-understood topology, thanks to [Bott periodicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bott_periodicity).

It should not be confused with the space of (bounded) invertible operators on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), which is a larger group, and topologically much simpler, namely contractible – see [Kuiper's theorem](wikipedia://en/2993692).

:::
