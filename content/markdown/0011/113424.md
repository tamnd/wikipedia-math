# Chi-squared distribution

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Chi-squared distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **$\chi^2$-distribution** with $k$ [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606) is the distribution of a sum of the squares of $k$ [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) random variables.[^o3]

:::

The chi-squared distribution $\chi^2_k$ is a special case of the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) and the univariate [Wishart distribution](wikipedia://en/364996). Specifically if $X \sim \chi^2_k$ then $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha=\frac{k}{2}, \theta=2)$ (where $\alpha$ is the shape parameter and $\theta$ the scale parameter of the gamma distribution) and $X \sim \text{W}_1(1,k)$.

The **scaled chi-squared distribution** $s^2 \chi^2_k$ is a reparametrization of the gamma distribution and the univariate [Wishart distribution](wikipedia://en/364996). Specifically if $X \sim s^2 \chi^2_k$ then $X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha=\frac{k}{2}, \theta=2 s^2)$ and $X \sim \text{W}_1(s^2,k)$.

The chi-squared distribution is one of the most widely used [probability distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) in [inferential statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferential_statistics), notably in [hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing) and in construction of [confidence intervals](wikipedia://en/280911).[^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7] This distribution is sometimes called the **central chi-squared distribution**, a special case of the more general [noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749).[^o8]

The chi-squared distribution is used in the common [chi-squared tests](wikipedia://en/226680) for [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821) of an observed distribution to a theoretical one, the [independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) of two criteria of classification of [qualitative data](wikipedia://en/2720954), and in finding the confidence interval for estimating the population [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) of a normal distribution from a sample standard deviation. Many other statistical tests also use this distribution, such as [Friedman's analysis of variance by ranks](wikipedia://en/3175261).

::: {#o10 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

If $Z_{1}, ..., Z_{k}$ are [independent](wikipedia://en/27593), [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) random variables, then the sum of their squares,

$$X\ = \sum_{i=1}^k Z_i^2,$$

is distributed according to the chi-squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom.

This is usually denoted as

$$X\ \sim\ \chi^2(k)\ \ \text{or}\ \ X\ \sim\ \chi^2_k.$$

The chi-squared distribution has one parameter: a positive integer $k$ that specifies the number of [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606) (the number of random variables *Z*<sub>*i*</sub> being summed).

### Introduction {#Introduction}

The chi-squared distribution is used primarily in hypothesis testing, and to a lesser extent for confidence intervals for population variance when the underlying distribution is normal. Unlike more widely known distributions such as the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) and the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906), the chi-squared distribution is not as often applied in the direct modeling of natural phenomena. It arises in the following hypothesis tests, among others:

- [Chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226676) of independence in [contingency tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingency_tables)
- [Chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226676) of goodness of fit of observed data to hypothetical distributions
- [Likelihood-ratio test](wikipedia://en/45035) for nested models
- [Log-rank test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log-rank_test) in survival analysis
- [Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel_test) for stratified contingency tables
- [Wald test](wikipedia://en/1600352)
- [Score test](wikipedia://en/1599160)

It is also a component of the definition of the [*t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) and the [*F*-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116) used in *t*-tests, analysis of variance, and regression analysis.

The primary reason for which the chi-squared distribution is extensively used in hypothesis testing is its relationship to the normal distribution. Many hypothesis tests use a test statistic, such as the [*t*-statistic](wikipedia://en/22474343) in a *t*-test. For these hypothesis tests, as the sample size, $n$, increases, the [sampling distribution](wikipedia://en/520670) of the test statistic approaches the normal distribution ([central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406)). Because the test statistic (such as $t$) is asymptotically normally distributed, provided the sample size is sufficiently large, the distribution used for hypothesis testing may be approximated by a normal distribution. Testing hypotheses using a normal distribution is well understood and relatively easy. The simplest chi-squared distribution is the square of a standard normal distribution. So wherever a normal distribution could be used for a hypothesis test, a chi-squared distribution could be used.

Suppose that $Z$ is a random variable sampled from the standard normal distribution, where the mean is $0$ and the variance is $1$: $Z \sim N(0,1)$. Now, consider the random variable $X = Z^2$. The distribution of the random variable $X$ is an example of a chi-squared distribution: $\ X\ \sim\ \chi^2_1$. The subscript 1 indicates that this particular chi-squared distribution is constructed from only 1 standard normal distribution. A chi-squared distribution constructed by squaring a single standard normal distribution is said to have 1 degree of freedom. Thus, as the sample size for a hypothesis test increases, the distribution of the test statistic approaches a normal distribution. Just as extreme values of the normal distribution have low probability (and give small p-values), extreme values of the chi-squared distribution have low probability.

An additional reason that the chi-squared distribution is widely used is that it turns up as the large sample distribution of generalized [likelihood ratio tests](wikipedia://en/45035) (LRT).[^o12] LRTs have several desirable properties; in particular, simple LRTs commonly provide the highest power to reject the null hypothesis ([Neyman–Pearson lemma](wikipedia://en/685158)) and this leads also to optimality properties of generalised LRTs. However, the normal and chi-squared approximations are only valid asymptotically. For this reason, it is preferable to use the *t* distribution rather than the normal approximation or the chi-squared approximation for a small sample size. Similarly, in analyses of contingency tables, the chi-squared approximation will be poor for a small sample size, and it is preferable to use [Fisher's exact test](wikipedia://en/819467). Ramsey shows that the exact [binomial test](wikipedia://en/935655) is always more powerful than the normal approximation.[^o13]

Lancaster shows the connections among the binomial, normal, and chi-squared distributions, as follows.[^o14] De Moivre and Laplace established that a binomial distribution could be approximated by a normal distribution. Specifically they showed the asymptotic normality of the random variable

$$\chi = \frac{m - Np}{\sqrt{Npq}}$$

where $m$ is the observed number of successes in $N$ trials, where the probability of success is $p$, and $q = 1 - p$.

Squaring both sides of the equation gives

$$\chi^2 = \frac{\left(m - Np\right)^2}{Npq}$$

Using $N = Np + N(1 - p)$, $N = m + (N - m)$, and $q = 1 - p$, this equation can be rewritten as

$$\chi^2 = \frac{\left(m - Np\right)^2}{Np} + \frac{\left(N - m - Nq\right)^2}{Nq}$$

The expression on the right is of the form that [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695) would generalize to the form

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\left(O_i - E_i\right)^2}{E_i}$$

where

- $\chi^2$ = Pearson's cumulative test statistic, which asymptotically approaches a $\chi^2$ distribution;
- $O_i$ = the number of observations of type $i$;
- $E_i = N p_i$ = the expected (theoretical) frequency of type $i$, asserted by the null hypothesis that the fraction of type $i$ in the population is $p_i$; and
- $n$ = the number of cells in the table[^o15]

In the case of a binomial outcome (flipping a coin), the binomial distribution may be approximated by a normal distribution (for sufficiently large $n$). Because the square of a standard normal distribution is the chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom, the probability of a result such as 1 heads in 10 trials can be approximated either by using the normal distribution directly, or the chi-squared distribution for the normalised, squared difference between observed and expected value. However, many problems involve more than the two possible outcomes of a binomial, and instead require 3 or more categories, which leads to the multinomial distribution. Just as de Moivre and Laplace sought for and found the normal approximation to the binomial, Pearson sought for and found a degenerate multivariate normal approximation to the multinomial distribution (the numbers in each category add up to the total sample size, which is considered fixed). Pearson showed that the chi-squared distribution arose from such a multivariate normal approximation to the multinomial distribution, taking careful account of the statistical dependence (negative correlations) between numbers of observations in different categories.[^o14]

### Probability density function {#Probability_density_function}

The [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) (pdf) of the chi-squared distribution is

$$f(x;\,k) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x^{k/2 -1} e^{-x/2}}{2^{k/2} \, \Gamma{\left(\frac k 2 \right)}}, & x > 0; \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

where $\Gamma(k/2)$ denotes the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316), which has [closed-form values for integer $k$](wikipedia://en/4239318).

For derivations of the pdf in the cases of one, two and $k$ degrees of freedom, see [Proofs related to chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/24510992).

### Cumulative distribution function {#Cumulative_distribution_function}

*Image: Chernoff-bound.svg.* Chernoff bound for the [CDF](wikipedia://en/5793) and tail (1-CDF) of a chi-squared random variable with ten degrees of freedom ($k = 10$)

Its [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) is:

$$F(x;\,k) = \frac{\gamma{\left(\frac{k}{2},\,\frac{x}{2}\right)}}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)}} = P{\left(\frac{k}{2},\,\frac{x}{2}\right)},$$

where $\gamma(s,t)$ is the [lower incomplete gamma function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_incomplete_gamma_function) and $P(s,t)$ is the [regularized gamma function](wikipedia://en/456050#Regularized_gamma_functions_and_Poisson_random_variables).

In a special case of $k = 2$ this function has the simple form:

$$F(x;\,2) = 1 - e^{-x/2}$$

which can be easily derived by integrating $f(x;\,2) = \frac{1}{2}e^{-x/2}$ directly. The integer recurrence of the gamma function makes it easy to compute $F(x;\,k)$ for other small, even $k$.

Tables of the chi-squared cumulative distribution function are widely available and the function is included in many [spreadsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spreadsheet) and all [statistical packages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_packages).

Letting $z \equiv x/k$, [Chernoff bounds](wikipedia://en/741759#The_first_step_in_the_proof_of_Chernoff_bounds) on the lower and upper tails of the CDF may be obtained.[^o18] For the cases when $0 < z < 1$ (which include all of the cases when this CDF is less than half): $F(z k;\,k) \leq (z e^{1-z})^{k/2}.$

The tail bound for the cases when $z > 1$, similarly, is

$$1-F(z k;\,k) \leq (z e^{1-z})^{k/2}.$$

For another [approximation](wikipedia://en/336271) for the CDF modeled after the cube of a Gaussian, see under [Noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749#Approximation).

### Cochran's theorem {#Cochran's_theorem}

The following is a special case of Cochran's theorem.

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.** If $Z_1,...,Z_n$ are [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) identically distributed (i.i.d.), [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) random variables, then $\sum_{t=1}^n \left(Z_t - \bar Z\right)^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$ where $\bar Z = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^n Z_t.$

::: {#o24 .proof cue=collapse name="[Proof]"}

**Proof.** Let $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar 0,1\!\!1)$ be a vector of $n$ independent normally distributed random variables, and $\bar Z$ their average. Then $\sum_{t=1}^n(Z_t-\bar Z)^2 ~=~ \sum_{t=1}^n Z_t^2 -n\bar Z^2 ~=~ Z^\top[1\!\!1 -{\textstyle\frac1n}\bar 1\bar 1^\top]Z ~=:~ Z^\top\!M Z$ where $1\!\!1$ is the identity matrix and $\bar 1$ the all ones vector. $M$ has one eigenvector $b_1:={\textstyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}} \bar 1$ with eigenvalue $0$, and $n-1$ eigenvectors $b_2,...,b_n$ (all orthogonal to $b_1$) with eigenvalue $1$, which can be chosen so that $Q:=(b_1,...,b_n)$ is an orthogonal matrix. Since also $X:=Q^\top\!Z\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar 0,Q^\top\!1\!\!1 Q) =\mathcal{N}(\bar 0,1\!\!1)$, we have $\sum_{t=1}^n(Z_t-\bar Z)^2 ~=~ Z^\top\!M Z ~=~ X^\top\!Q^\top\!M Q X ~=~ X_2^2+...+X_n^2 ~\sim~ \chi^2_{n-1},$ which proves the claim.

:::

### Additivity {#Additivity}

It follows from the definition of the chi-squared distribution that the sum of independent chi-squared variables is also chi-squared distributed. Specifically, if $X_i,i=\overline{1,n}$ are independent chi-squared variables with $k_i$, $i=\overline{1,n}$ degrees of freedom, respectively, then $Y = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$ is chi-squared distributed with $k_1 + \cdots + k_n$ degrees of freedom.

### Sample mean {#Sample_mean}

The sample mean of $n$ [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d.) chi-squared variables of degree $k$ is distributed according to a gamma distribution with shape $\alpha$ and scale $\theta$ parameters:

$$\overline X = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \sim \operatorname{Gamma}\left(\alpha{=}\tfrac{n k}{2}, \,\theta{=}\tfrac{2}{n} \right) \qquad \text{where } X_i \sim \chi^2(k)$$

Asymptotically, given that for a shape parameter $\alpha$ going to infinity, a Gamma distribution converges towards a normal distribution with expectation $\mu = \alpha \theta$ and variance $\sigma^2 = \alpha \theta^2$, the sample mean converges towards:

$$\overline X \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} N{\left(\mu{=}k, \, \sigma^2{=}\tfrac{2k}{n} \right)}$$

Note that we would have obtained the same result invoking instead the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406), noting that for each chi-squared variable of degree $k$ the expectation is $k$, and its variance $2k$ (and hence the variance of the sample mean $\overline{X}$ being $\sigma^2 = \tfrac{2k}{n}$).

### Entropy {#Entropy}

The [differential entropy](wikipedia://en/3504168) is given by

$$\begin{aligned} h &= \int_0^\infty f(x;\,k) \ln f(x;\,k) \, dx \\ &= \frac k 2 + \ln \left[2\,\Gamma{\left(\frac k 2 \right)}\right] + \left(1-\frac k 2 \right) \psi\!\left(\frac k 2 \right), \end{aligned}$$

where $\psi(x)$ is the [Digamma function](wikipedia://en/406935).

The chi-squared distribution is the [maximum entropy probability distribution](wikipedia://en/1813193) for a random variate $X$ for which $\operatorname{E}(X)=k$ and $\operatorname{E}(\ln(X))=\psi(k/2)+\ln(2)$ are fixed. Since the chi-squared is in the family of gamma distributions, this can be derived by substituting appropriate values in the [Expectation of the log moment of gamma](wikipedia://en/207079#Logarithmic_expectation_and_variance). For derivation from more basic principles, see the derivation in [moment-generating function of the sufficient statistic](wikipedia://en/339174#Moment-generating_function_of_the_sufficient_statistic).

### Noncentral moments {#Noncentral_moments}

The noncentral moments (raw moments) of a chi-squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom are given by[^o29][^o30]

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}(X^m) &= k (k+2) (k+4) \cdots (k+2m-2) \\[1ex] &= 2^m \frac{\Gamma{\left(m+\frac{k}{2}\right)}}{\Gamma{\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)}}. \end{aligned}$$

### Cumulants {#Cumulants}

The [cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684) are readily obtained by a [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) expansion of the logarithm of the characteristic function:

$$\kappa_n = 2^{n-1}(n-1)!\,k$$

with [cumulant generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulant_generating_function) $\ln \operatorname{E}[e^{tX}] = - \frac{k}{2} \ln(1-2t)$.

### Concentration {#Concentration}

The chi-squared distribution exhibits strong concentration around its mean. The standard Laurent-Massart[^o33] bounds are:

$$\Pr(X - k \ge 2 \sqrt{k x} + 2x) \le e^{-x}$$

$$\Pr(k - X \ge 2 \sqrt{k x}) \le e^{-x}$$

One consequence is that, if $Z \sim N(0, 1)^k$ is a Gaussian random vector in $\mathbb{R}^k$, then as the dimension $k$ grows, the squared length of the vector is concentrated tightly around $k$ with a width $k^{1/2 + \alpha}$:

$$\Pr\left(\left\|Z\right\|^2 \in \left[k - 2k^{1/2+\alpha}, \; k + 2k^{1/2+\alpha} + 2k^{\alpha}\right]\right) \geq 1-e^{-k^\alpha}$$

where the exponent $\alpha$ can be chosen as any value in $\mathbb{R}$.

Since the cumulant generating function for $\chi^2(k)$ is $K(t) = -\frac k2 \ln(1-2t)$, and its [convex dual](wikipedia://en/1001490) is $K^*(q) = \frac{1}{2} \left(q - k + k\ln\frac{k}{q}\right)$, the standard [Chernoff bound](wikipedia://en/741759) yields

$$\begin{aligned} \ln \Pr(X \geq (1 + \varepsilon) k) &\leq -\frac{k}{2} \left( \varepsilon - \ln(1+\varepsilon)\right) \\ \ln \Pr(X \leq (1 - \varepsilon) k) &\leq -\frac{k}{2} \left(-\varepsilon - \ln(1-\varepsilon)\right) \end{aligned}$$

where $0 < \varepsilon < 1$. By the union bound,

$$Pr(X \in (1\pm \varepsilon ) k ) \geq 1 - 2 e^{-\frac k2 (\frac{1}{2} \varepsilon^2 - \frac 13 \varepsilon^3)}$$

This result is used in proving the [Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma](wikipedia://en/14855952).[^o34]

### Asymptotic properties {#Asymptotic_properties}

*Image: Chi-square median approx.png.* Approximate formula for median (from the Wilson–Hilferty transformation) compared with numerical quantile (top); and difference (blue) and relative difference (red) between numerical quantile and approximate formula (bottom). For the chi-squared distribution, only the positive integer numbers of degrees of freedom (circles) are meaningful.

By the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406), because the chi-squared distribution is the sum of $k$ independent random variables with finite mean and variance, it converges to a normal distribution for large $k$. For many practical purposes, for $k>50$ the distribution is sufficiently close to a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), so the difference is ignorable.[^o37] Specifically, if $X \sim \chi^2(k)$, then as $k$ tends to infinity, the distribution of $(X-k)/\sqrt{2k}$ [tends](wikipedia://en/50723#Convergence_in_distribution) to a standard normal distribution. However, convergence is slow as the [skewness](wikipedia://en/28212) is $\sqrt{8/k}$ and the [excess kurtosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excess_kurtosis) is $12/k$.

The sampling distribution of $\ln(\chi^2)$ converges to normality much faster than the sampling distribution of $\chi^2$,[^o38] as the [logarithmic transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_transformation) removes much of the asymmetry.[^o39]

Other functions of the chi-squared distribution converge more rapidly to a normal distribution. Some examples are:

- If $X \sim \chi^2(k)$ then $\sqrt{2X}$ is approximately normally distributed with mean $\sqrt{2k-1}$ and unit variance (1922, by [R. A. Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._A._Fisher), see (18.23), p. 426 of Johnson).[^o6]
- If $X \sim \chi^2(k)$ then $\sqrt[3]{X/k}$ is approximately normally distributed with mean $1-\frac{2}{9k}$ and variance $\frac{2}{9k} .$[^o40] This is known as the **Wilson–Hilferty transformation**, see (18.24), p. 426 of Johnson.[^o6]
  - This [normalizing transformation](wikipedia://en/10056274#Transforming_to_normality) leads directly to the commonly used median approximation $k\bigg(1-\frac{2}{9k}\bigg)^3\;$ by back-transforming from the mean, which is also the median, of the normal distribution.

## Related distributions {#Related_distributions}

- As $k\to\infty$, $(\chi^2_k-k)/\sqrt{2k} ~ \xrightarrow{d}\ N(0,1) \,$ ([normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462))[^o42]
- $\chi_k^2 \sim {\chi'}^2_k(0)$ ([noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749) with non-centrality parameter $\lambda = 0$)
- If $Y \sim \mathrm{F}(\nu_1, \nu_2)$ then $X = \lim_{\nu_2 \to \infty} \nu_1 Y$ has the chi-squared distribution $\chi^2_{\nu_{1}}$[^o42]
  - As a special case, if $Y \sim \mathrm{F}(1, \nu_2)\,$ then $X = \lim_{\nu_2 \to \infty} Y\,$ has the chi-squared distribution $\chi^2_{1}$
- $\left\|\boldsymbol{N}_{i=1,\ldots,k} (0,1) \right\|^2 \sim \chi^2_k$ (The squared [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) of *k* standard normally distributed variables is a chi-squared distribution with *k* [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606))
- If $X \sim \chi^2_\nu\,$ and $c>0 \,$, then $cX \sim \Gamma(k=\nu/2, \theta=2c)\,$. ([gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079))
- If $X \sim \chi^2_k$ then $\sqrt{X} \sim \chi_k$ ([chi distribution](wikipedia://en/1901933))
- If $X \sim \chi^2_2$, then $X \sim \operatorname{exp}(1/2)$ is an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906). (See [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) for more.)[^o43]
- If $X \sim \chi^2_{2k}$, then $X \sim \operatorname{Erlang}(k, 1/2)$ is an [Erlang distribution](wikipedia://en/199028).
- If $X \sim \operatorname{Erlang}(k,\lambda)$, then $2\lambda X\sim \chi^2_{2k}$
- If $X \sim \operatorname{Rayleigh}(1)\,$ ([Rayleigh distribution](wikipedia://en/262296)) then $X^2 \sim \chi^2_2\,$
- If $X \sim \operatorname{Maxwell}(1)\,$ ([Maxwell distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_distribution)) then $X^2 \sim \chi^2_3\,$
- If $X \sim \chi^2_\nu$ then $\tfrac{1}{X} \sim \operatorname{Inv-}\chi^2_\nu\,$ ([Inverse-chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/1600924))
- The chi-squared distribution is a special case of type III [Pearson distribution](wikipedia://en/1804023)
- If $X \sim \chi^2_{\nu_1}\,$ and $Y \sim \chi^2_{\nu_2}\,$ are independent then $\tfrac{X}{X+Y} \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\tfrac{\nu_1}{2}, \tfrac{\nu_2}{2})\,$ ([beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074))
- If $X \sim \operatorname{U}(0,1)\,$ ([uniform distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29)) then $-2\log(X) \sim \chi^2_2\,$
- If $X_i \sim \operatorname{Laplace}(\mu,\beta)\,$ then $\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{2 |X_i-\mu|}{\beta} \sim \chi^2_{2n}\,$
- If $X_i$ follows the [generalized normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21736981) (version 1) with parameters $\mu,\alpha,\beta$ then $\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{2 |X_i-\mu|^\beta}{\alpha} \sim \chi^2_{2n/\beta}\,$[^o44]
- The chi-squared distribution is a transformation of [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057)
- [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) is a transformation of chi-squared distribution
- [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) can be obtained from chi-squared distribution and [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462)
- The [noncentral beta distribution](wikipedia://en/32632892) can be obtained as a transformation of chi-squared distribution and [noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749)
- The [noncentral t-distribution](wikipedia://en/3287836) can be obtained from normal distribution and chi-squared distribution

A chi-squared variable with $k$ degrees of freedom is defined as the sum of the squares of $k$ independent [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) random variables.

If $Y$ is a $k$-dimensional Gaussian random vector with mean vector $\mu$ and rank $k$ covariance matrix $C$, then $X = (Y-\mu )^\mathsf{T}C^{-1}(Y-\mu)$ is chi-squared distributed with $k$ degrees of freedom.

The sum of squares of [statistically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistically_independent) unit-variance Gaussian variables which do *not* have mean zero yields a generalization of the chi-squared distribution called the [noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749).

If $Y$ is a vector of $k$ [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d.) standard normal random variables and $A$ is a $k\times k$ [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474), [idempotent matrix](wikipedia://en/2974863) with [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) $k-n$, then the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) $Y^\mathsf{T}\!AY$ is chi-square distributed with $k-n$ degrees of freedom.

If $\Sigma$ is a $p\times p$ positive-semidefinite covariance matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries, then for $X\sim N(0,\Sigma)$ and $w$ a random $p$-vector independent of $X$ such that $w_1+\cdots+w_p=1$ and $w_i\geq 0, i=1,\ldots,p,$ then[^o39]

$$\frac{1}{\tilde{w}^\mathsf{T} \Sigma \tilde{w}} \sim \chi_1^2.$$

where $\tilde{w} = (w_1/X_1, \dots, w_p/X_p)$.

The chi-squared distribution is also naturally related to other distributions arising from the Gaussian. In particular,

- $Y$ is [F-distributed](wikipedia://en/332116), $Y \sim F(k_1, k_2)$ if $Y = \frac{ {X_1}/{k_1} }{ {X_2}/{k_2} }$, where $X_1 \sim \chi^2_{k_1}$ and $X_2 \sim \chi^2_{k_2}$ are statistically independent.
- If $X_1 \sim \chi^2_{k_1}$ and $X_2 \sim \chi^2_{k_2}$ are statistically independent, then $X_1 + X_2\sim \chi^2_{k_1+k_2}$. If $X_1$ and $X_2$ are not independent, then $X_1+X_2$ is not chi-square distributed.

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The chi-squared distribution is obtained as the sum of the squares of $k$ independent, zero-mean, unit-variance Gaussian random variables. Generalizations of this distribution can be obtained by summing the squares of other types of Gaussian random variables. Several such distributions are described below.

:::

### Linear combination {#Linear_combination}

If $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are chi square random variables and $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, then the distribution of $X = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i X_i$ is a special case of the [generalized chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/24899099). A closed expression for this distribution is not known. It may be, however, approximated efficiently using the [property of characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401#Properties) of chi-square random variables.[^o48]

### Chi-squared distributions {#Chi-squared_distributions}

#### Noncentral chi-squared distribution {#Noncentral_chi-squared_distribution}

The noncentral chi-squared distribution is obtained from the sum of the squares of independent Gaussian random variables having unit variance and *nonzero* means.

#### Generalized chi-squared distribution {#Generalized_chi-squared_distribution}

The generalized chi-squared distribution is obtained from the quadratic form $z'\operatorname{Az}$ where $z$ is a zero-mean Gaussian vector having an arbitrary covariance matrix, and $A$ is an arbitrary matrix.

### Gamma, exponential, and related distributions {#Gamma,_exponential,_and_related_distributions}

The chi-squared distribution $X \sim \chi_k^2$ is a special case of the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079), in that $X \sim \Gamma{\left(\tfrac{k}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}$ using the rate parameterization of the gamma distribution (or $X \sim \Gamma {\left(\tfrac{k}{2},2 \right)}$ using the scale parameterization of the gamma distribution) where $k$ is an integer.

Because the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) is also a special case of the gamma distribution, we also have that if $X \sim \chi_2^2$, then $X\sim \operatorname{exp}\left(\tfrac 1 2\right)$ is an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906).

The [Erlang distribution](wikipedia://en/199028) is also a special case of the gamma distribution and thus we also have that if $X \sim\chi_k^2$ with even $k$, then $X$ is Erlang distributed with shape parameter $k/2$ and scale parameter $1/2$.

## Occurrence and applications {#Occurrence_and_applications}

The chi-squared distribution has numerous applications in inferential [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), for instance in [chi-squared tests](wikipedia://en/226680) and in estimating [variances](wikipedia://en/32344). It enters the problem of estimating the mean of a normally distributed population and the problem of estimating the slope of a [regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) line via its role in [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375). It enters all [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634) problems via its role in the [F-distribution](wikipedia://en/332116), which is the distribution of the ratio of two independent chi-squared [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), each divided by their respective degrees of freedom.

Following are some of the most common situations in which the chi-squared distribution arises from a Gaussian-distributed sample.

- if $X_1, ..., X_n$ are [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d.) $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), then $\sum_{i=1}^n \left(X_i - \bar{X}\right)^2 \sim \sigma^2 \chi^2_{n-1}$ where $\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$.
- The box below shows some [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) based on $X_i \sim N(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i), i= 1, \ldots, k$ independent random variables that have probability distributions related to the chi-squared distribution:

::: {#o54 .table cue=table}

| Name | Statistic |
|---|---|
| chi-squared distribution | $\sum_{i=1}^k \left(\frac{X_i-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2$ |
| [noncentral chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/2601749) | $\sum_{i=1}^k \left(\frac{X_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2$ |
| [chi distribution](wikipedia://en/1901933) | $\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k \left(\frac{X_i-\mu_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2}$ |
| [noncentral chi distribution](wikipedia://en/2858062) | $\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k \left(\frac{X_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2}$ |

:::

The chi-squared distribution is also often encountered in [magnetic resonance imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging).[^o58]

## Computational methods {#Computational_methods}

### Table of $\chi^{2}$ values vs $p$-values {#Table_of_values_vs_-values}

The [$p$-value](wikipedia://en/554994) is the probability of observing a test statistic *at least* as extreme in a chi-squared distribution. Accordingly, since the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) (CDF) for the appropriate degrees of freedom *(df)* gives the probability of having obtained a value *less extreme* than this point, subtracting the CDF value from 1 gives the *p*-value. A low *p*-value, below the chosen significance level, indicates [statistical significance](wikipedia://en/160995), i.e., sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis. A significance level of 0.05 is often used as the cutoff between significant and non-significant results.

## History {#History}

This distribution was first described by the German geodesist and statistician [Friedrich Robert Helmert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Robert_Helmert) in papers of 1875–6,[^n1][^o66] where he computed the sampling distribution of the sample variance of a normal population. Thus in German this was traditionally known as the *Helmert'sche* ("Helmertian") or "Helmert distribution".

The distribution was independently rediscovered by the English mathematician [Karl Pearson](wikipedia://en/164695) in the context of [goodness of fit](wikipedia://en/2474821), for which he developed his [Pearson's chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226676), published in 1900, with computed table of values published in (Elderton 1902), collected in (Pearson 1914, xxxi–xxxiii, 26–28). The name "chi-square" ultimately derives from Pearson's shorthand for the exponent in a [multivariate normal distribution](wikipedia://en/50347) with the Greek letter [Chi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi_%28letter%29), writing $-\chi^{2}$ for what would appear in modern notation as $-\mathbf{x}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ ($\Sigma$ being the [covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/191752)).[^o69] The idea of a family of "chi-squared distributions", however, is not due to Pearson but arose as a further development due to Fisher in the 1920s.[^n1]
