# Birational invariant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Birational invariant"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **birational invariant** is a property that is preserved under [birational equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_equivalence).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A **birational invariant** is a quantity or object that is [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined) on a [birational equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_equivalence) class of [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties). In other words, it depends only on the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843) of the variety.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The first example is given by the grounding work of [Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann) himself: in his thesis, he shows that one can define a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) to each [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260); every Riemann surface comes from an algebraic curve, well defined up to birational equivalence and two birational equivalent curves give the same surface. Therefore, the Riemann surface, or more simply its [Geometric genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) is a birational invariant.

A more complicated example is given by [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590): in the case of an [algebraic surface](wikipedia://en/648311), the [Hodge numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_number) *h*<sup>0,1</sup> and *h*<sup>0,2</sup> of a [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities) projective complex surface are birational invariants. The Hodge number *h*<sup>1,1</sup> is not, since the process of [blowing up](wikipedia://en/1817790) a point to a curve on the surface can augment it.

:::
