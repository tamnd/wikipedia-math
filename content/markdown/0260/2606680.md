# Interval exchange transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Interval exchange transformation"}

*Image: Interval exchange.svg.* Graph of interval exchange transformation (in black) with $\lambda = (1/15,2/15,3/15,4/15,5/15)$ and $\pi=(3,5,2,4,1)$. In blue, the orbit generated starting from $1/2$.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **interval exchange transformation**[^o2] is a kind of [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087) that generalises [circle rotation](wikipedia://en/9445837). The phase space consists of the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324), and the transformation acts by cutting the interval into several subintervals, and then permuting these subintervals. They arise naturally in the study of [polygonal billiards](wikipedia://en/2160429) and in [area-preserving flows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Area-preserving_flow).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let $n > 0$ and let $\pi$ be a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) on $1, \dots, n$. Consider a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) $\lambda = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ of positive real numbers (the widths of the subintervals), satisfying

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = 1.$$

Define a map $T_{\pi,\lambda}:[0,1]\rightarrow [0,1],$ called the **interval exchange transformation associated with the pair $(\pi,\lambda)$** as follows. For $1 \leq i \leq n$ let

$$a_i = \sum_{1 \leq j < i} \lambda_j \quad \text{and} \quad a'_i = \sum_{1 \leq j < \pi(i)} \lambda_{\pi^{-1}(j)}.$$

Then for $x \in [0,1]$, define

$$T_{\pi,\lambda}(x) = x - a_i + a'_i$$

if $x$ lies in the subinterval $[a_i,a_i+\lambda_i)$. Thus $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ acts on each subinterval of the form $[a_i,a_i+\lambda_i)$ by a [translation](wikipedia://en/204682), and it rearranges these subintervals so that the subinterval at position $i$ is moved to position $\pi(i)$.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Any interval exchange transformation $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) of $[0,1]$ to itself that preserves the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198). It is continuous except at a finite number of points.

The [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of the interval exchange transformation $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ is again an interval exchange transformation. In fact, it is the transformation $T_{\pi^{-1}, \lambda'}$ where $\lambda'_i = \lambda_{\pi^{-1}(i)}$ for all $1 \leq i \leq n$.

If $n=2$ and $\pi = (12)$ (in [cycle notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_notation)), and if we join up the ends of the interval to make a circle, then $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ is just a [circle rotation](wikipedia://en/9445837). The [Weyl equidistribution theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_equidistribution_theorem) then asserts that if the length $\lambda_1$ is [irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational), then $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ is [uniquely ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniquely_ergodic). Roughly speaking, this means that the orbits of points of $[0,1]$ are uniformly evenly distributed. On the other hand, if $\lambda_1$ is rational then each point of the interval is [periodic](wikipedia://en/10779), and the period is the denominator of $\lambda_1$ (written in lowest terms).

If $n>2$, and provided $\pi$ satisfies certain non-degeneracy conditions (namely there is no integer $0 < k < n$ such that $\pi(\{1,\dots,k\}) = \{1,\dots,k\}$), a deep theorem which was a conjecture of M.Keane and due independently to [William A. Veech](wikipedia://en/45548896)[^o7] and to [Howard Masur](wikipedia://en/46960501)[^o8] asserts that for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) choices of $\lambda$ in the unit simplex $\{(t_1, \dots, t_n) : \sum t_i = 1\}$ the interval exchange transformation $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ is again [uniquely ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniquely_ergodic). However, for $n \geq 4$ there also exist choices of $(\pi,\lambda)$ so that $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ is [ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic) but not [uniquely ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniquely_ergodic). Even in these cases, the number of ergodic [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) of $T_{\pi,\lambda}$ is finite, and is at most $n$.

Interval maps have a [topological entropy](wikipedia://en/5463978) of zero.[^o9]

:::

## Odometers {#Odometers}

*Image: Dyadic odometer.svg.* Dyadic odometer $T$

*Image: Dyadic odometer, twice iterated.svg.* Dyadic odometer iterated twice; that is $T^2.$

*Image: Dyadic odometer thrice iterated.svg.* Dyadic odometer thrice iterated; that is $T^3.$

*Image: Dyadic odometer iterated four times.svg.* Dyadic odometer iterated four times; that is $T^4.$

The [dyadic odometer](wikipedia://en/56880139) can be understood as an interval exchange transformation of a countable number of intervals. The dyadic odometer is most easily written as the transformation

$$T\left(1,\dots,1,0,b_{k+1},b_{k+2},\dots\right) = \left(0,\dots,0,1,b_{k+1},b_{k+2},\dots \right)$$

defined on the [Cantor space](wikipedia://en/352376) $\{0,1\}^\mathbb{N}.$ The standard mapping from Cantor space into the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) is given by

$$(b_0,b_1,b_2,\cdots)\mapsto x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty b_n2^{-n-1}$$

This mapping is a measure-preserving [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) from the Cantor set to the unit interval, in that it maps the standard [Bernoulli measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_measure) on the Cantor set to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on the unit interval. A visualization of the odometer and its first three iterates appear on the right.

## Higher dimensions {#Higher_dimensions}

Two and higher-dimensional generalizations include polygon exchanges, polyhedral exchanges and [piecewise isometries](wikipedia://en/82990915).[^o12]
