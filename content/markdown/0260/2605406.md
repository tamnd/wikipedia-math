# Topological divisor of zero

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Topological divisor of zero"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an element $z$ of a [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665) $A$ is called a **topological divisor of zero** if there exists a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $x_1,x_2,x_3,...$ of elements of $A$ such that

:::

1. The sequence $zx_n$ converges to the zero element, but
1. The sequence $x_n$ does not converge to the zero element.

If such a sequence exists, then one may assume that $\left \Vert \ x_n \right \| = 1$ for all $n$.

If $A$ is not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity), then $z$ is called a "left" topological divisor of zero, and one may define "right" topological divisors of zero similarly.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- If $A$ has a unit element, then the invertible elements of $A$ form an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of $A$, while the non-invertible elements are the complementary [closed subset](wikipedia://en/47279). Any point on the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) between these two sets is both a left and right topological divisor of zero.
- In particular, any [quasinilpotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasinilpotent) element is a topological divisor of zero (e.g. the [Volterra operator](wikipedia://en/3654902)).
- An operator on a Banach space $X$, which is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), not [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), but whose image is dense in $X$, is a left topological divisor of zero.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

The notion of a topological divisor of zero may be generalized to any [topological algebra](wikipedia://en/2688895). If the algebra in question is not [first-countable](wikipedia://en/904940), one must substitute [nets](wikipedia://en/22170) for the sequences used in the definition.

:::
