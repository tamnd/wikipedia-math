# Asymptotic theory (statistics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Asymptotic theory"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **asymptotic theory**, or **large sample theory**, is a framework for assessing properties of [estimators](wikipedia://en/10043) and [statistical tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test). Within this framework, it is often assumed that the [sample size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size) $n$ may grow indefinitely; the properties of estimators and tests are then evaluated under the limit of $n \to \infty$. In practice, a limit evaluation is considered to be approximately valid for large finite sample sizes too.[^o2]

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Most statistical problems begin with a dataset of [size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size) $n$. The asymptotic theory proceeds by assuming that it is possible (in principle) to keep collecting additional data, thus that the sample size grows infinitely, i.e. $n \to \infty$. Under the assumption, many results can be obtained that are unavailable for samples of finite size. An example is the [weak law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055). The law states that for a sequence of [independent and identically distributed](wikipedia://en/30876902) (IID) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) $X_{1}, X_{2}, ...$, if one value is drawn from each random variable and the average of the first $n$ values is computed as $\overline{X}_{n}$, then the $\overline{X}_{n}$ [converge in probability](wikipedia://en/50723#Convergence_in_probability) to the population mean $E[X_{i}]$ as $n \to \infty$.[^o4]

In asymptotic theory, the standard approach is $n \to \infty$. For some [statistical models](wikipedia://en/27576), slightly different approaches of asymptotics may be used. For example, with [panel data](wikipedia://en/2499813), it is commonly assumed that one dimension in the data remains fixed, whereas the other dimension grows: $T = \operatorname{constant}$ and $N \to \infty$, or vice versa.[^o4]

Besides the standard approach to asymptotics, other alternative approaches exist:

- Within the [local asymptotic normality](wikipedia://en/27260158) framework, it is assumed that the value of the "true parameter" in the model varies slightly with $n$, such that the $n$-th model corresponds to $\theta_{n} = \theta + h/\sqrt{n}$. This approach lets us study the [regularity of estimators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_estimator).
- When [statistical tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test) are studied for their power to distinguish against the alternatives that are close to the null hypothesis, it is done within the so-called "local alternatives" framework: the null hypothesis is $H_{0}: \theta = \theta_{0}$ and the alternative is $H_{1}: \theta = \theta_{0} + h/\sqrt{n}$. This approach is especially popular for the [unit root tests](wikipedia://en/2473482).
- There are models where the dimension of the parameter space $\Theta_{n}$ slowly expands with $n$, reflecting the fact that the more observations there are, the more structural effects can be feasibly incorporated in the model.
- In [kernel density estimation](wikipedia://en/2090057) and [kernel regression](wikipedia://en/8771825), an additional parameter is assumed—the bandwidth $h$. In those models, it is typically taken that $h \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$. The rate of convergence must be chosen carefully, though, usually $h \propto n^{-1/5}$.

In many cases, highly accurate results for finite samples can be obtained via numerical methods (i.e. computers); even in such cases, though, asymptotic analysis can be useful. This point was made by (Small 2010, §1.4), as follows.

## Asymptotic properties {#Asymptotic_properties}

### Point estimators {#Point_estimators}

In the following, $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ will be an arbitrary sequence of estimators.

#### [Consistency](wikipedia://en/1497569) {#Consistency}

The sequence $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is said to be *(weakly)* *consistent*, if it [converges in probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converges_in_probability) to the true value of the parameter being estimated:

$$\hat\theta_n\ \xrightarrow{\overset{}p}\ \theta_0.$$

This means that the probability that the estimated value gets arbitrarily close to $\theta_0$ converges to zero as $n$ tends to infinity.[^o4] More specifically, if the convergence of $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ to $\theta_0$ is of [stochastic order](wikipedia://en/22284600) $1/a_n$ (also written $\textit O_p(1/a_n)$) for some sequence $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of real numbers diverging to infinity, that is,

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb P(|a_n(\hat\theta_n-\theta_0)|>K)=0 \quad \text{for all }K>0,$$

then $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is called *$a_n$-consistent*.[^o10]

The sequence of estimates is said be *strongly consistent*, if it even [converges almost surely](wikipedia://en/50723#Almost_sure_convergence) to the true parameter:

$$\hat\theta_n\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}\ \theta_0.$$

The subtle difference to a weakly consistent estimator is that a strongly consistent estimator is guaranteed to get arbitrarily close to $\theta_0$ as $n$ tends to infinity, eventually.

#### [Asymptotic distribution](wikipedia://en/3001287) {#Asymptotic_distribution}

If it is possible to find sequences of non-random constants $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$, $(b_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ (possibly depending on the value $\theta_0$), and a non-degenerate distribution $\mu$ such that

$$b_n(\hat\theta_n - a_n)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \mu,$$

then the sequence of estimators $\hat\theta_n$ is said to have the *asymptotic distribution* $\mu$.

Most often, the asymptotic distribution of estimators encountered in practice is the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) $\mu=\mathcal N(0,V)$ for some covariance matrix $V$, in which case the estimator is called *asymptotically normal*.[^o12] Some authors require specifically $a_n=\theta_0$ and $b_n=\sqrt{n}$ and then call the sequence of estimators *asymptotically normal for* $\theta_0$.[^o13]

#### Unbiasedness in the limit {#Unbiasedness_in_the_limit}

The sequence is called *unbiased in the limit*, if $\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n]=\theta_0$.[^o15] An estimator with this property is also sometimes called *approximately unbiased*.[^o16]

#### Asymptotic unbiasedness {#Asymptotic_unbiasedness}

The sequence is called *asymptotically unbiased* for the parameter $\theta_0$, if $\sigma_n(\hat\theta_n-\theta_0)$ [converges in distribution](wikipedia://en/50723#Convergence_in_distribution) to a random variable $G\neq 0$ with $\mathbb{E}[G]=0$ for some sequence $(\sigma_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of real numbers.[^o18]

#### Limiting variance {#Limiting_variance}

If there exists a sequence $(\tau_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of real numbers such that $\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_n\mathrm{Var}({\hat\theta_n})=\sigma^2$, then $\sigma^2$ is called the *limiting variance* of $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$. Typically, it is chosen $\tau_n=n$.[^o20]

#### Asymptotic variance {#Asymptotic_variance}

If there exists a sequence $(b_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ such that

$$b_n(\hat\theta_n - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} G$$

with $0<\mathbb{E}[G^2]<\infty$, then $V=\mathrm{Var}(G)$ is called the *asymptotic variance* of $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$.[^o22] In particular, if $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is asymptotically unbiased, then $V=\mathbb{E}[G^2]$.

#### [Asymptotic efficiency](wikipedia://en/27250866#Asymptotic_efficiency) {#Asymptotic_efficiency}

The sequence is called *asymptotically efficient*, if it is asymptotically normal for $\theta_0$ and its asymptotic variance is $V=I(\theta_0)^{-1}$ with the [Fisher information](wikipedia://en/598971) $I(\theta_0)$, that is, the asymptotic variance achieves the [Cramér–Rao bound](wikipedia://en/581124).[^o24][^o25]

## General relations between asymptotic properties {#General_relations_between_asymptotic_properties}

As above, let $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ be an arbitrary sequence of estimators.

- If $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is asymptotically normal for $\theta_0$, then it is consistent (by [Slutsky's theorem](wikipedia://en/5213747)).
- If $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is consistent, then it is asymptotically unbiased.[^o29]
- If $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is asymptotically unbiased and [uniformly integrable](wikipedia://en/9610107), then it is unbiased in the limit.[^o30] This is the case, for example, when the second moments are uniformly bounded, i.e., $\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb E[|\hat\theta_n|^2]<\infty$.
- In the univariate case, if an estimator is asymptotically unbiased and has asymptotic variance $\tau^2$, and if the limiting variance $\sigma^2$ exists, then $\tau^2\leq\sigma^2$.[^o24] Equility holds if and only if the normalized sequence of estimators, that is $b_n(\hat\theta_n-\theta_0)$ for suitable $b_n$, is uniformly integrable (which is often the case in practice).[^o22][^o24]

## Asymptotic theorems {#Asymptotic_theorems}

Asymptotic properties of a sequence of estimators $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ (for a parameter $\theta$) often result from general theorems from [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). In the following, let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be [independent and identically distributed](wikipedia://en/30876902) observations from the true distribution $\mathbb{P}_\theta$.

### For consistency {#For_consistency}

::: {#o35 .statement cue=named-heading name="Strong law of large numbers"}

#### Strong law of large numbers {#Strong_law_of_large_numbers}

If the estimators are of the form $\hat\theta_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i)$ for some measurable function $f$ such that $\mathbb{E}[|f(X_1)|]<\infty$, then by the strong [law of large numbers](wikipedia://en/157055)

$$\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mathbb{E}[f(X_1)].$$

Thus, if $\theta=\mathbb{E}[f(X_1)]$, then $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is strongly consistent.

:::

::: {#o37 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Continuous mapping theorem"}

#### Continuous mapping theorem {#Continuous_mapping_theorem}

Suppose $\theta=f(\tau)$ for some other parameter $\tau$ and a continuous function $f$. Given a (strongly) consistent sequence of estimators $(\hat\tau_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ for the parameter $\tau$, then by the [continuous mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/20813286), the sequence of estimators $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(f(\hat\tau_n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is (strongly) consistent for the parameter $\theta$.[^o38]

:::

### For asymptotic distribution {#For_asymptotic_distribution}

::: {#o41 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Central limit theorem"}

#### Central limit theorem {#Central_limit_theorem}

If the estimators are of the form $\hat\theta_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i)$ for some real-valued measurable function $f$ such that $\mathbb{E}[|f(X_1)|^2]<\infty$, then by the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406), $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is asymptotically normal in the sense

$$\sqrt n\frac{\hat\theta_n-\mu}{\sigma}\ \overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\ \mathcal N(0,1)$$

with the definitions $\mathbb E[f(X_1)]=\mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(f(X_1))=\sigma^2$.

:::

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fisher–Tippet–Gnedenko theorem"}

#### Fisher–Tippet–Gnedenko theorem {#Fisher–Tippet–Gnedenko_theorem}

If the estimators are of the form $\hat\theta_n=\max_{i=1,\dots,n}f(X_i)$ for some real-valued measurable function $f$ and if there exists sequences $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ and $(b_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ and a non-[degenerate distribution](wikipedia://en/101843) $G$ such that

$$\frac{\hat\theta_n-a_n}{b_n}\ \overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\ G,$$

then, by the [Fisher–Tippet–Gnedenko theorem](wikipedia://en/20962073), the asymptotic distribution $G$ of $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a [generalized extreme value distribution](wikipedia://en/1494479).

:::

#### Delta method {#Delta_method}

Suppose $\theta=f(\tau)$ for some other parameter $\tau$ and a differentiable function $f$ with $\nabla f(\tau)\neq 0$. Given a sequence of estimators $(\hat\tau_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ for the parameter $\tau$ having asymptotic distribution $G$ in the sense

$$\frac{\hat\tau_n-\tau}{\sigma_n}\ \overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\ Z\sim G$$

for some sequence of real numbers $(\sigma_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$, then by the [delta method](wikipedia://en/2826026), the sequence of estimators $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(f(\hat\tau_n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ has asymptotically the distribution of $\nabla f(\tau)^\top Z$.

In the special case, where $(\hat\tau_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is asymptotically normal for $\tau$ with asymptotic variance $V$, the sequence $(\hat\theta_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is asymptotically normal with asymptotic variance $\nabla f(\tau)^\top V\nabla f(\tau)$.

:::
