# Invariance of domain

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Invariance of domain"}

**Invariance of domain** is a theorem in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) about [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$. It states:

:::

If $U$ is an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $f : U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ is an [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map), then $V := f(U)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $f$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) between $U$ and $V$.

The theorem and its proof are due to [L. E. J. Brouwer](wikipedia://en/172896), published in 1912.[^o2] The proof uses tools of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), notably the [Brouwer fixed point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brouwer_fixed_point_theorem).

## Notes {#Notes}

The conclusion of the theorem can equivalently be formulated as: "$f$ is an [open map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map)".

Normally, to check that $f$ is a homeomorphism, one would have to verify that both $f$ and its [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) $f^{-1}$ are continuous; the theorem says that if the domain is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the image is also in $\mathbb{R}^n,$ then continuity of $f^{-1}$ is automatic. Furthermore, the theorem says that if two subsets $U$ and $V$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are homeomorphic, and $U$ is open, then $V$ must be open as well. (Note that $V$ is open as a subset of $\mathbb{R}^n,$ and not just in the subspace topology. Openness of $V$ in the subspace topology is automatic.) Both of these statements are not at all obvious and are not generally true if one leaves Euclidean space.

*Image: A map which is not a homeomorphism onto its image.png.* An injective map which is not a homeomorphism onto its image: $g : (-1.1, 1) \to \mathbb{R}^2$ with $g(t) = \left(t^2 - 1, t^3 - t\right).$

It is of crucial importance that both [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and [image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_of_a_function) of $f$ are contained in Euclidean space of the same dimension. Consider for instance the map $f : (0, 1) \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $f(t) = (t, 0).$ This map is injective and continuous, the domain is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}$, but the image is not open in $\mathbb{R}^2.$ A more extreme example is the map $g : (-1.1, 1) \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $g(t) = \left(t^2 - 1, t^3 - t\right)$ because here $g$ is injective and continuous but does not even yield a homeomorphism onto its image.

The theorem is also not generally true in infinitely many dimensions. Consider for instance the [Banach](wikipedia://en/3989) [$L^{p}$ space](wikipedia://en/45194) $\ell^{\infty}$ of all bounded real [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838). Define $f : \ell^\infty \to \ell^\infty$ as the shift $f\left(x_1, x_2, \ldots\right) = \left(0, x_1, x_2, \ldots\right).$ Then $f$ is injective and continuous, the domain is open in $\ell^{\infty}$, but the image is not.

## Consequences {#Consequences}

If $n>m$, there exists no continuous injective map $f:U\to\mathbb{R}^m$ for a nonempty open set $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$. To see this, suppose there exists such a map $f.$ Composing $f$ with the standard inclusion of $\mathbb{R}^m$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$ would give a continuous injection from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to itself, but with an image with empty interior in $\mathbb{R}^n$. This would contradict invariance of domain.

In particular, if $n\ne m$, no nonempty open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ can be homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^m$.

And $\mathbb{R}^n$ is not homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^m$ if $n\ne m.$

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The domain invariance theorem may be generalized to [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470): if $M$ and $N$ are topological $n$-manifolds without boundary and $f : M \to N$ is a continuous map which is [locally one-to-one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_injective_function) (meaning that every point in $M$ has a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29) such that $f$ restricted to this neighborhood is injective), then $f$ is an [open map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map) (meaning that $f(U)$ is open in $N$ whenever $U$ is an open subset of $M$) and a [local homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/248582).

There are also generalizations to certain types of continuous maps from a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) to itself.[^o7]
