# Henry Briggs (mathematician)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Henry Briggs"}

**Henry Briggs** (1 February 1561 – 26 January 1630) was an [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) notable for changing the [original logarithms](wikipedia://en/1477110) invented by [John Napier](wikipedia://en/15993) into [common (base 10) logarithms](wikipedia://en/174482), which are sometimes known as Briggsian logarithms in his honor. The specific [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for [long division](wikipedia://en/313384) in modern use was introduced by Briggs c. 1600 AD.[^o3]

:::

Briggs was a committed [Puritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puritan)[^o4][^o5] and an influential professor in his time.[^o6]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

*Image: (The North Part Of America by Henry Briggs 1625) (1906).jpg.* Map by Henry Briggs

Briggs was born at Daisy Bank, Sowerby Bridge, near [Halifax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halifax,_West_Yorkshire), in [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire), England. After studying [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) and [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) at a local grammar school, he entered [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge), in 1577, and graduated in 1581. In 1588, he was elected a Fellow of St John's. In 1592, he was made reader of the physical lecture founded by [Thomas Linacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Linacre); he also read some of the mathematical lectures. During this period, he took an interest in navigation and astronomy, collaborating with [Edward Wright](wikipedia://en/17214829).

In 1596, he became first professor of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) in the recently founded [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College), [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London), where he also taught astronomy and navigation. He lectured there for nearly 23 years, and made Gresham College a centre of English mathematics, from which he supported the new ideas of [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736).

He was a friend of [Christopher Heydon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Heydon), a writer on astrology, though Briggs himself rejected [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) for religious reasons.[^o9] He once called astrology, "a mere system of groundless conceits".[^o10]

At this time, Briggs obtained a copy of *[Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio](wikipedia://en/26291704)*, in which Napier introduced the idea of logarithms. It has also been suggested that he knew of the method outlined in *[Fundamentum Astronomiae](wikipedia://en/49672578)* published by the Swiss clockmaker [Jost Bürgi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jost_Bürgi), through [John Dee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dee).[^o11] [Napier's formulation](wikipedia://en/17860#From_Napier_to_Euler) was awkward to work with, but the book fired Briggs' imagination – in his lectures at Gresham College he proposed the idea of base 10 logarithms in which the logarithm of 10 would be 1; and soon afterwards he wrote to the inventor on the subject. Briggs was active in many areas, and his advice in astronomy, surveying, navigation, and other activities like mining was frequently sought. Briggs in 1619 invested in the [London Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Company), and he had two sons: Henry, who later emigrated to Virginia, and Thomas, who remained in England.[^o12]

Briggs died on 26 January 1630, and was buried in the chapel of [Merton College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College,_Oxford). Dr Smith, in his *Lives of the Gresham Professors*, characterises him as a man of great probity, a condemner of riches, and contented with his own station, preferring a studious retirement to all the splendid circumstances of life. The lunar crater [Briggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Briggs_%28crater%29) is named in his honour.

## Mathematical contributions {#Mathematical_contributions}

*Image: Logarithmorum Chilias Prima page 0-67.jpg.* A page from Henry Briggs' 1617 *Logarithmorum Chilias Prima* showing the base-10 (common) logarithm of the integers 0 to 67 to fourteen decimal places.

In 1616 Briggs visited Napier at [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh) in order to discuss the suggested change to Napier's logarithms. The following year he again visited for a similar purpose. During these conferences the alteration proposed by Briggs was agreed upon; and on his return from his second visit to Edinburgh, in 1617, he published the first [chiliad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiliad) of his logarithms,[^o13] giving 14-digit common logarithms of the integers from 1 to 1000.

In 1619 he was appointed [Savilian Professor of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Geometry) at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), and resigned his professorship of Gresham College in July 1620. Soon after his settlement at Oxford he was incorporated Master of Arts.[^o13]

In 1622 he published a small tract on the *[Northwest Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwest_Passage) to the South Seas, through the Continent of Virginia and [Hudson Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_Bay)*. The tract is notorious today as the origin of the cartographic myth of the [Island of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_of_California). In it Briggs stated he had seen a map that had been brought from Holland that showed the Island of California. The tract was republished three years later (1625) in [Pvrchas His Pilgrimes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Purchas) (vol 3, p848).

In 1624 his *Arithmetica Logarithmica* was published, in folio, a work containing the logarithms of thirty thousand [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) to fourteen decimal places[^o13] (1-20,000 and 90,001 to 100,000). The remaining logarithms of the numbers 20,001 to 90,000 were later calculated by [Adriaan Vlacq](wikipedia://en/4292863) in his table of logarithms of the numbers 1 to 100,000 being accurate to 10 places. [Alexander John Thompson](wikipedia://en/30059434) published a table of logarithms of the numbers 1 to 100,000 accurate to 20 places in 1952. Briggs was one of the first to use [finite-difference methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-difference_method) to compute tables of functions.[^o15][^o16]

He also completed a table of [logarithmic sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_sine) and [tangents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_tangent) for the hundredth part of every [degree](wikipedia://en/1195294) to fourteen decimal places, with a table of [natural sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) to fifteen places, and the [tangents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_%28trigonometric_function%29) and [secants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secant_%28trigonometric_function%29) for the same to ten places; all of which were printed at Gouda in 1631 and published in 1633 under the title of *Trigonometria Britannica*;[^o13] this work was probably a successor to his 1617 *Logarithmorum Chilias Prima* ("The First Thousand Logarithms"), which gave a brief account of logarithms and a long table of the first 1000 integers calculated to the 14th decimal place.

English translations of Briggs's *Arithmetica* and the first part of his *Trigonometria Britannica* are available on the web.[^o17]
