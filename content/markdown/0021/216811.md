# Tarski's circle-squaring problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tarski's circle-squaring problem"}

**Tarski's circle-squaring problem** is the challenge, posed by [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) in 1925, to take a [disc](wikipedia://en/329542) in the plane, cut it into finitely many pieces, and reassemble the pieces so as to get a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) of equal [area](wikipedia://en/1209). It is possible, using pieces that are [Borel sets](wikipedia://en/43327), but not with pieces cut by [Jordan curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve).

:::

## Solutions {#Solutions}

Tarski's circle-squaring problem was [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) to be solvable by [Miklós Laczkovich](wikipedia://en/6021299) in 1990. The decomposition makes heavy use of the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) and is therefore [non-constructive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconstructive_proof). Laczkovich estimated the number of pieces in his decomposition at roughly 10<sup>50</sup>. The pieces used in his decomposition are [non-measurable subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-measurable_subset) of the plane.

Laczkovich actually proved the reassembly can be done *using translations only*; rotations are not required. Along the way, he also proved that any [simple polygon](wikipedia://en/1059530) in the plane can be decomposed into finitely many pieces and reassembled using translations only to form a square of equal area.

It follows from a result of (Wilson 2005) that it is possible to choose the pieces in such a way that they can be moved continuously while remaining [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) to yield the square. Moreover, this stronger statement can be proved as well to be accomplished by means of translations only.

A constructive solution was given by Łukasz Grabowski, András Máthé and Oleg Pikhurko in 2016 which worked everywhere except for a set of [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero). In 2017, Andrew Marks and Spencer Unger gave a completely constructive solution using [Borel sets](wikipedia://en/43327). In an interview, Marks has claimed it is possible to use about $10^{200}$ Borel pieces.

## Limitations {#Limitations}

[Lester Dubins](wikipedia://en/36027272), [Morris W. Hirsch](wikipedia://en/32523108) & Jack Karush proved it is impossible to dissect a circle and make a square using pieces that could be cut with an [idealized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealization_%28science_philosophy%29) pair of scissors (that is, having [Jordan curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve) boundary).

## Related problems {#Related_problems}

The [Bolyai–Gerwien theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolyai–Gerwien_theorem) is a related but much simpler result: it states that one can accomplish such a decomposition of a simple polygon with finitely many *polygonal pieces* if both translations and rotations are allowed for the reassembly.

These results should be compared with the much more [paradoxical decompositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradoxical_decomposition) in three dimensions provided by the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220); those decompositions can even change the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of a set. However, in the plane, a decomposition into finitely many pieces must preserve the sum of the [Banach measures](wikipedia://en/1339598) of the pieces, and therefore cannot change the total area of a set.
