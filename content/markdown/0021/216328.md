# H. S. M. Coxeter

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="H. S. M. Coxeter"}

**Harold Scott MacDonald** "**Donald**" **Coxeter** () (9 February 1907 – 31 March 2003)[^o4] was a British-Canadian [geometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometer) and mathematician. He is regarded as one of the greatest geometers of the 20th century.[^o5]

:::

Coxeter was born in England and educated at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), with student visits to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University). He worked for 60 years at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) in Canada, from 1936 until his retirement in 1996, becoming a full professor there in 1948. His many honours included membership in the [Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Canada), the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society), and the [Order of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Canada).

He was an author of 12 books, including *[The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra](wikipedia://en/13181245)* (1938) and *[Regular Polytopes](wikipedia://en/8629753)* (1947). Many concepts in geometry and [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) are named after him, including the [Coxeter graph](wikipedia://en/5358570), [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004), [Coxeter's loxodromic sequence of tangent circles](wikipedia://en/16159670), [Coxeter–Dynkin diagrams](wikipedia://en/5358554), and the [Todd–Coxeter algorithm](wikipedia://en/894779).

## Biography {#Biography}

Coxeter was born in [Kensington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensington), England, to Harold Samuel Coxeter and Lucy (Gee). His father had taken over the family business of Coxeter & Son, manufacturers of surgical instruments and compressed gases (including a mechanism for anaesthetising surgical patients with [nitrous oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrous_oxide)), but was able to retire early and focus on sculpting and baritone singing; Lucy Coxeter was a portrait and landscape painter who had attended the [Royal Academy of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Arts). A maternal cousin was the architect Sir [Giles Gilbert Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giles_Gilbert_Scott).[^o7][^o4]

In his youth, Coxeter composed music and was an accomplished pianist at the age of 10.[^o8] He felt that [mathematics and music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_and_music) were intimately related, outlining his ideas in a 1962 article on "Music and Mathematics" in the *Canadian Music Journal*.[^o8]

He was educated at [King Alfred School, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Alfred_School,_London), and then attended [St George's School, Harpenden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_George's_School,_Harpenden) with the goal of becoming a composer, instead deciding he had more interest in mathematics. While recovering from [chickenpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chickenpox) at St George's, he met [John Flinders Petrie](wikipedia://en/23915649), later a mathematician for whom [Petrie polygons](wikipedia://en/18571869) were named.[^o7] He was accepted at [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge), in 1925, but decided to spend a year studying in hopes of gaining admittance to [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge), where the standard of mathematics was higher.[^o4] Coxeter won an entrance scholarship and went to Trinity in 1926 to read mathematics. There he earned his BA (as [Senior Wrangler](wikipedia://en/19355609)) in 1928, and his doctorate in 1931.[^o8] In 1932 he went to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) for a year as a [Rockefeller Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Fellow), where he worked with [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen), and [Solomon Lefschetz](wikipedia://en/384384).[^o9] Returning to Trinity for a year, he attended [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741)'s seminars on the [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439).[^o8] Wittgenstein selected Coxeter and others to take notes of his lectures, the collection of which later became *[The Blue Book](wikipedia://en/28341040)*.[^o10] In 1934 he spent a further year at Princeton as a Procter Fellow.[^o9]

In 1936 Coxeter moved to the University of Toronto. In 1938 he and [P. Du Val](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._Du_Val), H. T. Flather, and [John Flinders Petrie](wikipedia://en/23915649) published *[The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra](wikipedia://en/13181245)* with [University of Toronto Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto_Press). In 1940 Coxeter edited the eleventh edition of *Mathematical Recreations and Essays*,[^o11] originally published by [W. W. Rouse Ball](wikipedia://en/308290) in 1892. He was elevated to [professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor) in 1948. He was elected a Fellow of the [Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Canada) in 1948 and a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) in 1950. He met [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) in 1954 and the two became lifelong friends; his work on geometric figures helped inspire some of Escher's works, particularly the [*Circle Limit*](wikipedia://en/39847439) series based on [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291) [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671). He also inspired some of the innovations of [Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminster_Fuller).[^o9] Coxeter, [M. S. Longuet-Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._S._Longuet-Higgins) and [J. C. P. Miller](wikipedia://en/20706909) were the first to publish the full [list of uniform polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2850448) (1954).P54

He worked for 60 years at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) and published twelve books.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Coxeter was a [vegetarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetarianism). He attributed his longevity to his vegetarian diet, daily exercise such as fifty press-ups and standing on his head for fifteen minutes each morning, and consuming a nightly cocktail made from [Kahlúa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahlúa) (a coffee liqueur), [peach schnapps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peach_schnapps), and [soy milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soy_milk).[^o7]

His wife, Rien died in 1999, after which Coxeter travelled with his daughter, Susan. He died on 31 March 2003, in Toronto.

## Mathematical contributions {#Mathematical_contributions}

### Coxeter group {#Coxeter_group}

Coxeter invented a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) to apply the description of the [set of reflections](wikipedia://en/2422023) in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), hence it is named the [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004).P34 It is defined as the [presentation of a group](wikipedia://en/99494)

$$\left\langle r_1,r_2,\ldots,r_n \mid (r_ir_j)^{m_{ij}}=1\right\rangle$$

where $m_{ii}=1$ and $m_{ij} = m_{ji} \ge 2$ is either an integer or $\infty$ for $i\neq j$. Here, the condition $m_{i j}=\infty$ means that no relation of the form $(r_ir_j)^m = 1$ for any integer $m \ge 2$ should be imposed.[^o15]

*Image: Finite coxeter.svg.* [Coxeter&ndash;Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter&ndash;Dynkin_diagram) for the fundamental finite [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004)

Coxeter classified the finite Coxeter groups in 1935.P35 These groups were associated with diagrams consisting of dots and segment lines connecting them. These diagrams are known as the [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109), named for Soviet–American mathematician [Eugene Dynkin](wikipedia://en/1351540), describing the families of finite-dimensional [simple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/59500649) of [root systems](wikipedia://en/277087) $A_n$, $B_n$, $C_n$, $D_n$, $E_6$, $E_7$, $E_8$, $F_4$, $G_2$, $H_4$, $H_5$, $I_2$. The amalgamation is now known as [Coxeter&ndash;Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter&ndash;Dynkin_diagram) or Coxeter graph.[^o16] Coxeter denotes these groups and their diagram structures in bracket notations, named as [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502) or Coxeter symbol.

### Polyhedra and polytopes {#Polyhedra_and_polytopes}

The book *[The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra](wikipedia://en/13181245)* by Coxeter, [Patrick du Val](wikipedia://en/10637467), H. T. Flather, and [John Flinders Petrie](wikipedia://en/23915649),B73 published by the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) in 1938, describes the 59 [stellations](wikipedia://en/180253) of a [regular icosahedron](wikipedia://en/14968), made by extending its faces to form a star-shaped solid.[^o18]

Coxeter's *[Regular Polytopes](wikipedia://en/8629753)* was published in numerous editions:B63 by Methuen in 1947, by Pitman Publishing in 1948,[^o19][^o20] the second edition by Macmillan in 1963,[^o21][^o22] and the third edition by Dover Publications in 1973.[^o23][^o24]

English mathematician John Flinders Petrie, in 1926, took the concept of [regular skew polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon)—where the vertices are not in the same plane—to three-dimensional polyhedra. He found two [regular skew apeirohedra](wikipedia://en/45592828), constructed of [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285) and [truncated octahedra](wikipedia://en/279654) by removing the shared square faces of adjacent polyhedra to form a tunnels. Coxeter discovered the third regular skew polyhedron, constructed of multiple [truncated tetrahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_tetrahedra) under Petrie's procedure.B68[^o25] Respectively, these polyhedra were later named by English mathematician [John Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Conway) as mucube, muoctahedron, and mutetrahedron.[^o26]

[Michael Goldberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Goldberg_%28mathematician%29) introduced the [Goldberg polyhedron](wikipedia://en/39781140) family, whose faces are pentagons and hexagons.[^o27] The dual of such a polyhedron is [geodesic polyhedron](wikipedia://en/53024913), described as an almost spherical shape with triangular faces, which [Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminster_Fuller) called "geodesic domes". These polyhedra were extended and applied in [Donald Caspar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Caspar) and [Aaron Klug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Klug)'s article on the geometry of [viral capsids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_capsid).[^o28] CoxeterB71 covered much of the information without referring to biological explanations. Caspar and Klug were the first to publish the most general correct construction of a geodesic polyhedron, making the name "[Goldberg–Coxeter construction](wikipedia://en/56092947)" an instance of [Stigler's law of eponymy](wikipedia://en/682348).[^o29]

### Spirals and helices {#Spirals_and_helices}

Coxeter studied the infinite [circles](wikipedia://en/6220) tangent to each other [arranged loxodromically, so that any four consecutive circles in a sequence](wikipedia://en/16159670) are pairwise mutually tangent.P68[^o31] This was later generalized to the case of [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859) and [hyperspheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) in higher-dimensional space.P97

Coxeter and Arie Hendrick Boerdijk constructed an assemblage of stacked regular tetrahedra linearly, such that the edges form three [helices](wikipedia://en/179924), thereby what is nowadays called [Boerdijk–Coxeter helix](wikipedia://en/19172682).P85b[^o32][^o33]

### Graph theory {#Graph_theory}

Coxeter in 1983's paper *My Graph*P83 showed a [3-regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821) with 28 vertices and 42 edges.[^o35] A graph with 30 vertices and 45 edges was discovered by [William Thomas Tutte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomas_Tutte),[^o36] and the study of its connection to geometric configurations was investigated by Tutte[^o37] and Coxeter,P58 now known as [Tutte–Coxeter graph](wikipedia://en/1396880).

Coxeter, along with [Robert Frucht](wikipedia://en/27069553), extended [LCF notation](wikipedia://en/24186720) devised by [Joshua Lederberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Lederberg) for the representation of [cubic graphs](wikipedia://en/980508) that contain a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle).B81[^o38]

## Awards {#Awards}

Since 1978, the [Canadian Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/906055) has awarded the [Coxeter–James Prize](wikipedia://en/18739444) in his honor.

He was made a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) in 1950 and in 1997 he was awarded their [Sylvester Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvester_Medal).[^o9] In 1990, he became a Foreign Member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences)[^o40] and in 1997 was made a [Companion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Canada#Grades) of the [Order of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Canada).

In 1973 he received the [Jeffery–Williams Prize](wikipedia://en/18739069).[^o9]

A [festschrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festschrift) in his honour, *The Geometric Vein*, was published in 1982. It contained 41 essays on geometry, based on a symposium for Coxeter held at Toronto in 1979.[^o42] A second such volume, *The Coxeter Legacy*, was published in 2006 based on a Toronto Coxeter symposium held in 2004.[^o43]
